## 28. What Sera Paid

The moon-blessing left her the way a fever breaks.

Sera Vane had spent her whole adult life with it under her skin—a cool silver thread running from sternum to crown, humming faintly when she prayed, flaring bright when she fought. She'd known, holding the line on the Glass Steppe while Rosalind ran, that she was spending it. Not lending. Spending. Every ward she threw at the Hunt's hounds was a coin from a purse that did not refill.

She hadn't told him that either.

Now she lay in the lee of a fallen waystone at the Steppe's edge, where the mirror-flat glass gave way to honest dirt and scraggly pine, and felt—for the first time in nineteen years—nothing underneath her skin but skin.

It was terrifying.

It was also, absurdly, quiet. The blessing had always hummed. She had forgotten what silence inside her own body sounded like. It sounded like Greyhollow, she thought. Like a valley where nothing answers.

Her hands shook. Not from cold, though the night was cold enough. From emptiness. She was thirty-four years old and mortal as a milkmaid and everything she had built her life on—the order, the oath, the light—was spent on a farrier who hadn't even asked her to do it.

He was crouched ten feet away, feeding sticks to a small fire. He'd carried her off the glass. She remembered that much: his arms under her shoulders, his storm-scars glowing like cracked lanterns, Wren's soul flickering somewhere in them like a moth in a jar. He'd walked three miles with her weight on him and never once complained about the weight.

"Sera." He didn't look up from the fire. "You've been staring at me for an hour."

"I'm taking inventory," she said.

"Of what?"

"Of how much of me is left."

That got his attention. He looked over, and in the firelight his face did the thing it always did—that careful farrier's stillness, the face of a man who had learned that every expression might be evidence. But his eyes were kind. Twelve years of exile had not managed to burn the kindness out of him. The Undercourt hadn't managed it either. She found this both admirable and infuriating.

"The blessing's gone," she said. Because if she was going to be honest, she might as well be thorough about it. "All of it. I held the Hunt as long as I could and then there wasn't any more to hold. I'm just—" she flexed her fingers, watched them tremble, "—a woman with a sword and bad knees."

"You have excellent knees."

"I have *spent* knees. There's a difference."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, because he was looking at her hands too, and he understood what he was seeing. He was a man who knew prices. He'd paid one for twelve years without complaint.

"Will it come back?" he asked.

"No." She said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've rehearsed saying so it won't crack you open when it leaves your mouth. "Moon-blessings don't refill. They're not wells, they're... candles. You burn them down or you don't. Mine's burned." She looked at the fire instead of at him. "I'll be slower now. I'll sleep more. In twenty years I'll be an old woman while everyone we know is still complaining about their taxes. That's the arithmetic."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, a crow called once—not Corvass, just a crow, just a bird—and Sera thought about how strange it was that she could no longer feel the difference.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Rosalind asked quietly. "Before. On the glass. When you decided to hold the line."

"Because you'd have argued."

"Yes."

"And I'd have won, because I'm better at arguing than you are, and then we'd both be dead and Wren would be a jar-moth forever." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her something in her shoulder, and she winced. "Some lies are load-bearing, Rosalind. You take one out and the whole house comes down."

"That's not a lie," he said. "That's a choice you made for me."

"Yes. And here's the thing about choices made for other people." She sat up, slowly, joints announcing themselves like they were billing her by the hour. "They're never free. There's always a bill. And I owe you one that has nothing to do with the blessing."

The words were coming now, and she found she couldn't stop them, the way she hadn't been able to stop the wards on the glass. Honesty, it turned out, was also a thing you spent all at once or not at all.

* * *

"In Saltmere," she said, "when the Debt Collector came to us—you remember. The man with the ledger-skin. The voice like a clerk reading your own eulogy."

Rosalind went very still. Farrier-still. Evidence-still.

"He offered me my disgrace back. My *un*-disgrace. Full restoration. The Order of the Silver Moon reinstated, rank restored, my name struck from the rolls of shame. All I had to do was deliver you to the court." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I refused. I want that on the record first, because the rest is worse. I refused. But I didn't tell you. For weeks I carried it alone, and when I finally confessed—on the corpse-road, after the wraiths—I told you the shape of it but not the size. I said he *offered*. I didn't say what the offer actually was. I didn't say why refusing it broke something in me that hasn't healed."

The firelight moved on his face. He waited. He was good at waiting; exile teaches patience the way the sea teaches salt.

"The Order fell eleven years ago," Sera said. "You know the official story—the cult grew proud, the gods went silent, the paladins scattered. What you don't know is the last mission. The one I was on. We were sent to a village called Emberfen, in the hill country south of the Steppe. Forty families. And the Inquisitor-General had received word that the village was 'god-touched.' Which meant the children. Nineteen children born in the year the old gods last stirred, before the silence—born with marks, with gifts, with prayers that *answered back*. The Order's doctrine by then was that god-touched children were doors, and doors let things in, and things that come in must be burned out."

She heard her own voice go flat, clerical, the voice of reports she'd read and reports she'd written.

"I was twenty-three. Second-rank, full blessing, certain of everything. We rode into Emberfen at dusk and the Inquisitor-General gave the order for the Rite of Cleansing at dawn. And I stood in that square with nineteen children locked in the meetinghouse behind me, and I looked at my commander, and I said no."

Rosalind's breath caught. Just barely. A farrier's sound, small and controlled.

"They gave me the night to reconsider. I spent it moving children out through the fen on foot, one family at a time, waist-deep in black water, carrying toddlers on my back. By dawn I'd gotten out eleven. The Rite proceeded anyway. The meetinghouse burned with nine children inside, because nine sets of parents wouldn't run, wouldn't believe, wouldn't leave their neighbors' homes undefended." Her voice did not shake. She had spent all its shaking years ago, in the fen water, in the dark. "And the Order stripped my blessing to half, struck my name, and called it mercy, because they let me live. The Inquisitor-General himself told me: *You chose the door over the wall, Vane. One day a storm will come through it, and you will understand.*"

She finally looked at Rosalind directly.

"You're the storm, Rosalind. You understand that? When the Debt Collector showed me your file—your actual file, the court keeps files—he said, *Here is the storm through the door.* Deliver him, and everything you lost for disobedience is returned. The Order vindicated. The children avenged, retroactively, in the only currency power ever pays in." She laughed, short and ugly. "He was good. He didn't threaten me. He *validated* me. He said the word *righteousness* like it was a debt instrument."

"And you refused," Rosalind said.

"I refused. And here is what refusing cost, which is the part I never told you." She pulled her cloak tighter, though the cold wasn't the problem. "Refusing didn't restore anything. Refusing just confirmed the loss. The Order stayed fallen. The nine children stayed dead. My name stayed struck. And I understood, standing in that Saltmere alley with the ledger-man smiling at me, that there was no version of my life where the burning was undone. I could keep my honor or I could get my order back, and I had already spent my honor's price at Emberfen, and the world does not give refunds." She swallowed. "So when I stood on the glass yesterday and poured out the last of the blessing to buy you time, I wasn't making a noble sacrifice. I was finishing a payment plan. I've been liquidating myself since I was twenty-three, Rosalind. Emberfen took half. Saltmere took my secret. The glass took the rest. And the terrible truth—the one I've been choking on since the valley—is that I don't regret any of it, and I don't know who I am without it, and I am lying here feeling nothing under my skin for the first time in nineteen years and I am *frightened*, because I was a paladin for so long that I forgot how to be a person."

The silence after that was long. The fire settled. Pine trees creaked in a wind that was just wind—or maybe wasn't; maybe it was him, listening, the way weather listens.

* * *

When Rosalind finally spoke, his voice was rough.

"Do you know what I did with my guilt for twelve years?" he said. "I shoe horses. That's it. That's the whole strategy. Every morning I did the ritual to keep the storm down, and every morning I hammered iron, and I told myself: *this is penance, this is smallness, this is what I deserve.* And you know what I learned?" He poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "Smallness isn't penance. It's just another way of hiding. I hid in a forge. You hid in an order. Wren hid in a bargain. Everyone I love hides somewhere, and the hiding always costs more than the thing we're hiding from."

"I'm not asking for absolution—"

"I know. You're asking for something harder. You're asking someone to hear it and stay." He moved around the fire and sat beside her, close enough that she could feel the faint warmth that still lived in his scars, the residue of the storm he carried. "Sera. At Emberfen, you saved eleven children. Eleven. Do you know what I would have given, at thirteen years old, for one person to look at the storm in me and say *no* to the people who wanted to burn it out?"

She stared at him.

"You think your disgrace is a failure of duty," he said. "I think it's the only duty you ever kept. Everything else—the Order, the blessing, the rank—that was costume. The vow underneath was *protect the god-touched*, and you kept it. You kept it at the cost of everything the costume required. And then the Debt Collector offered to sell you back the costume, and you said no, and you've been calling that refusal a wound ever since." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't a wound. It was the truest thing you've ever done. You just did it alone, and unreported, and unpaid, which is the only way true things ever get done."

Sera found that her eyes were wet, which was mortifying, and that she didn't care, which was unprecedented.

"I let you walk toward the Undercourt carrying my secret," she said. "For weeks. If I'd fallen—if I'd died on the glass—you'd have never known why I flinched every time the court's name came up."

"But I didn't die. And now I know. And I'm still here." He put his hand over hers—her shaking, emptied, mortal hand—and his grip was warm and steady and absolutely human. "You want to know what I forgive? Everything. The secret, the silence, the spending yourself without asking. All of it. Because here's my arithmetic, paladin: you gave your god for mine. Nobody's ever paid that rate for me before. Not even Wren, and Wren sold herself for me."

"She'd argue the accounting."

"Wren argues everything. It's genetic." He almost smiled, and this time the smile stayed. "You're not less without the blessing, Sera. I know that sounds like comfort-talk, so here's proof: everything you did that mattered, you did *against* the blessing. Against the Order. Against the doctrine. The blessing never made you good. You made the blessing mean something, right up until it ran out. Whatever you are now—mortal, slow, bad-kneed—you were already that person. The candle was never the light. You were."

She laughed despite herself, wet and cracked. "Did you practice that speech?"

"Twelve years in a valley with no one to talk to. I practiced *everything*."

And then she was laughing properly, and then crying properly, and then doing some undignified combination of both against his shoulder while the fire burned down and the ordinary stars came out over the edge of the Steppe, and none of it was dignified at all, and all of it was true.

After a long time she said, into his collar, "What do I do now? I've been an instrument my whole life. An instrument of the Order, then of your quest, then of the glass. I don't know how to be a purpose of my own."

"You start small," Rosalind said. "You start the way everything starts. With a vow you actually choose." He pulled back and looked at her, and in his eyes was the same thing she'd seen the moment she first recognized the gods' fingerprints on him, back in Greyhollow—except now it wasn't suspicion. It was recognition. "Not an oath handed down. Not a debt inherited. A vow. Small and chosen and yours."

"Like what?"

"Like: *I will not hide again.*" He shrugged. "Start there. Amend later."

Sera sat with it. Around them the night deepened, and far off across the dark glass of the Steppe, something glimmered—faint, rhythmic, wrong. Not starlight. Starlight doesn't pulse.

She felt it in her chest before she understood it with her mind: the place where the blessing used to live, the empty socket of it, aching like a tooth gone bad. Mortal now, yes. Blind to the moon's currents, deaf to the silver thread.

But not deaf to everything.

Because the ache had a direction. And the direction was the mountain.

"Rosalind," she said quietly. "The blessing's gone, but the scar tissue where it lived—it's still moon-attuned. Residual. Like a sailor's bones remembering the sea." She was already reaching for her sword, already pushing herself upright on legs that shook and held. "And something over there is ringing it like a bell."

He rose beside her, and his storm-scars began, softly, to glow.

Across the Steppe, pulsing on the horizon beneath the thirteenth moon's last sliver, the lights of the Undercourt were going out—one hall at a time, like candles being snuffed by a hand that had finally read the ledger all the way to the bottom.

Something in the court beneath the mountain had just discovered what Wren had done.

And it was waking up angry.
