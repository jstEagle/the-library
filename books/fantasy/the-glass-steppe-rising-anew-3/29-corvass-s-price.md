## 29. Corvass's Price

The storm broke over us an hour after we cleared the Hunt's last marker, and I stood in it like a man standing in his own heartbeat.

That was the trouble. It *was* my heartbeat now—or close enough that I couldn't tell where the weather ended and I began. Sera had noticed it first, back on the corpse-road: the way lightning leaned toward me like a dog hearing its name. She'd said nothing for two days. Then, last night, watching me sleep with my fists clenched around thunder, she'd finally spoken.

"It's getting louder in you," she'd said. "The storm. Every day it's a little less yours and a little more you."

I hadn't answered. There wasn't an answer that wasn't terrifying.

Now we crouched beneath a shelf of black stone on the Steppe's northern rim, Wren's soul banked safe in the storm-scar across my ribs—a warmth there, a small stubborn coal of her—and the rain came down in sheets that parted politely around our shelter without being asked. The sky was doing that more and more. Asking less. Acting as if we were already the same thing.

Sera built a fire from wet gorse and sheer paladin stubbornness. Her moon-blessing was nearly spent; I could see it in the gray at her temples that hadn't been there a month ago, in the way she favored her left arm since the Hunt's riders had caught her shield-edge. She was spending herself in installments and pretending not to count.

The crow arrived before the fire did.

It didn't land so much as arrive—there was empty air above the stones, and then Corvass was there, feathers slick with rain he wasn't actually touching, one eye gold and one eye human and both of them looking at me with an attention that made my skin prickle.

"You're brooding," the crow said. "Storms shouldn't brood. Storms should *act*. It's why you lot make such poor gods."

"We're not doing this tonight," Sera said flatly.

"Oh, I think we are." He hopped down onto a flat rock, folded his wings with the crisp finality of a magistrate closing a ledger. "The Recall is coming. You know that. You felt the gates seal behind you and you felt them *reopen*—the court doesn't forgive, it litigates. Somewhere under that mountain, a clerk is reading your sister's line in the Ledger of Forfeit, and when he finishes reading it, something with too many joints will come to collect."

Wren's soul stirred against my ribs. Not words. Just a tightening, the way a sleeping person curls smaller when they hear a door open downstairs.

"How long?" I asked.

"Days. Perhaps fewer. The thirteenth moon is past, but the contract predates moons—it was written when the sky was younger and more obedient. The clause is simple: the soul of Wren Whitlock, held as collateral against the exile-price of Rosalind Whitlock, returnable upon demand to the holder of record." The crow's human eye glittered. "They hold the record. They will make the demand. And no wall you build, no storm you summon, no paladin's broken oath will stop what comes, because it won't come as force. It will come as *paperwork* made flesh, and paperwork, my dear farrier, cannot be struck by lightning."

Sera swore softly. I understood the sentiment exactly.

"So we run," I said. "North, past Saltmere, into the sea if we have to—"

"And then what? Live running forever? Your sister deserves better than a life spent as contraband." Corvass preened once, unhurried, letting the silence do its work. He was good at that. Gods usually are. "Or," he said, "we end it."

*

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the only currency I have left that nobody's claimed yet.

I wanted to say yes before he even told me the price. That's the thing about loving someone the way I love Wren—the way you love someone you sold twelve years of their life to save—I would have paid anything, signed anything, knelt in front of anything with wings and a voice. When Corvass said *we end it*, something in my chest went loose and grateful, and gratitude is the most dangerous thing a desperate man can feel.

"What's the offer?" I asked.

"The gods are awake," Corvass said. "You know why, now. Prayers need debtors. Souls need somewhere to go. The old courts are falling, and the new order—" he spread a wing at the rain, at the whole howling dark, "—is being written right now, by whatever stands in the middle of it. We need a vessel. Not a servant. Servants we have plenty of; half of them are crows. A *vessel*. Something mortal enough to remember what prayer feels like, powerful enough to carry what the prayers become."

"No," Sera said immediately.

"I haven't named the price yet," Corvass said mildly.

"You don't have to. I've read enough contracts under that mountain to know the price is always the same shape: everything."

Corvass turned his gold eye to her, and for a moment the crow-god looked very old and very tired, the way mountains look tired. "Paladin. I watched your order burn a village of god-touched children because a king found their prayers inconvenient. I watched you refuse, and I watched them break you for it, and I have thought, these many years, that yours was the only clean vow left in the world." His head tilted. "So believe me when I tell you: what I ask of him is not a trick. It is not a ledger-clause. It is a price, paid in full, in the only coin the gods can never mint for themselves."

"Which is?"

"A name."

The rain filled the pause. Against my ribs, Wren's soul went very still, listening.

"Surrendered," Corvass said. "Not hidden, not changed, not traded away and bought back later. Given. Freely, wholly, the way he gave nothing all these years in Greyhollow while he hoarded himself behind a false name and a hammer and a careful, careful silence. Rosalind Whitlock would cease to exist—not die, *cease*. What remains would be the storm that remembers being a man. Ours. Vessel, herald, guardian of the covenant between the waking gods and the small praying world. And in exchange—" the human eye fixed on me, gentle and merciless, "—your sister's soul becomes weather too. Claimed by the sky itself. No ledger holds her. No contract reaches her. No hunter, however many joints it has, can collect what heaven owns."

"That's it?" I heard myself say. "She goes free, and I—"

"You stop being anyone," Sera said. Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "Rosalind. Listen to what he's actually saying. You don't get to come back from this. There's no last page where you hand the name back and walk home. This isn't the Undercourt's bargain with a loophole for clever sisters to find. This is a god asking for the only thing you can't take back."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" She grabbed my wrist—her grip still strong, her blessing guttering under her skin like a lamp low on oil. "Because I've watched you for a month. You flinch every time someone says your name out loud. You've spent twelve years teaching yourself that who you are is a debt waiting to be collected. And now a god shows up offering to erase the debt by erasing the debtor, and you're going to call that freedom?"

"Yes," I said.

And I meant it. That was the terrible part. I meant it down to the marrow.

*

Here is what twelve years in Greyhollow taught me, though I never said it aloud, not even to myself, not even in the ritual whispers before dawn:

A name is a door. And mine had been nailed shut from the outside.

Every morning I pressed my palm to the cold forge-stone and whispered the binding-words the valley elders gave me, and every morning I felt Rosalind Whitlock—storm-caller, kingslayer, the boy whose grief cracked the sky open over a royal procession—sit down inside me like a prisoner told to stay quiet. I became the farrier. I learned horses' feet and neighbors' names and which widow took sugar in her tea. I was *good* at it. I was so good at it that some nights I almost believed it.

But a man who has surrendered his name doesn't get to choose a new one. He just gets to be unnamed. And unnamed men don't belong anywhere—they only get tolerated places. Greyhollow tolerated me. The valley elders branded me traitor when I crossed the boundary, but understand: they'd been waiting twelve years for an excuse. Tolerance is just exile with better manners.

And now a god sat on a wet rock in the rain and offered me the opposite of tolerance. Not a place I was permitted to stand. A *place I was made of*. The storm doesn't tolerate the valley. The storm is the valley's weather, its rain on the barley, its snow on the passes, its thunder telling children stories through the shutters. If I gave up my name, I wouldn't belong to Greyhollow the way a fugitive belongs to a hiding place.

I'd belong the way the sky belongs.

"Say the rest of it," I told Corvass. "There's always a rest of it."

"There is." He didn't preen this time. "You will feel everything. That is what a vessel is. Every prayer said under open sky will pass through you—every grief, every gratitude, every small cruel wish and small kind one. Mortals pray terribly, Rosalind. They pray for rain on their fields and drought on their neighbors'. They pray thanks for bread they stole. You will carry all of it, and you will not be able to put any of it down, because you will be the carrying."

"For how long?"

"Storms don't keep calendars."

Sera made a sound low in her throat. "And Wren? What does she get? A brother-shaped wind? Is that your idea of mercy, crow?"

"Wren gets her soul back and a body of rain and light, and a lifetime—however long she wants it—to live without ledgers, without courts, without a brother's guilt chained to her ankle." Corvass's voice softened, and that was somehow worse than the hardness. "And yes. She gets a brother who is weather. She will speak and the rain will answer. She will laugh and the thunder will laugh back. I have watched souls for longer than your kingdoms have existed, paladin, and I will tell you what I know: love does not require a name. It requires attention. He will have nothing *but* attention to give her."

Against my ribs, Wren's soul burned.

Not words. She couldn't form words yet, not from inside the scar—but I'd learned her language these weeks, the way you learn a fire's language, by heat and flicker. And what she was saying, steady and furious and terrified and certain, was: *don't you dare do this for me alone.*

But that was the thing she'd never understood, my sister, my brilliant reckless girl who'd sold herself to a court of shadows to pay my blood-price without asking my leave. It was never *for her alone*. It had never been for anyone alone. The storm that killed the king's heir hadn't been for Wren either—it had been grief with nowhere to go, power with no name to answer to, a boy's whole self bursting out sideways because there was no door for it.

I had spent twelve years learning to live without doors.

Corvass was offering me every door in the sky.

*

Sera walked away from the fire. Ten paces into the rain, arms crossed, shoulders rigid—the posture of a woman arguing with a god and losing and refusing to admit it. I let her. Some arguments a person has to lose privately.

I looked at the crow.

"If I do this," I said, "it's on my terms. One term."

"Names aren't negotiated, farrier."

"Not the name. The *keeping*. When I'm gone—when Rosalind Whitlock is gone—you keep what's left of him. The memory. The forge-smell, the lullabies, the way he loved his sister badly and completely. Don't let the storm forget it was ever a man. Because a storm that forgets being a man is just weather. And weather doesn't guard anything. Weather doesn't *choose*."

For a long moment the crow was silent. Rain ran off his feathers without wetting them.

"You understand," he said at last, quietly, "that this is precisely why it must be you. Any soul in the world could carry the storms. Only one would think to ask that the man be kept inside the god." He dipped his head—an old gesture, older than crow, older than bird. "It will be kept. I swear it by the first shadow ever cast. You will not be lost, Rosalind. You will be *remembered so thoroughly* that forgetting you becomes impossible. Ask any valley that's ever cursed the rain and blessed it in the same breath."

From across the clearing, Sera spoke without turning around. "If you do this, I'm staying. In Greyhollow. Someone has to teach the weather its manners, and apparently the gods have none."

Something in my chest cracked open—grief and laughter in the same breath, which is, I think, the only honest sound a person can make at a moment like this.

"When?" I asked Corvass.

"Now," he said. "Before the Recall comes. Before the fear makes the choice for you. A name given freely is worth ten given desperately, and the gods can tell the difference." He lifted off the rock and hovered, wings beating slow, rain bending around him. "Kneel in the water. Speak your true name. All of it—the one your mother gave you, the one the court stripped, the one the bridge-warden wrung out of you. Say it like you're handing over a key you've carried so long it's grown into your palm. And then let go."

I looked at Sera's back. At the fire drowning. At the north, where Greyhollow lay beyond three hundred miles of glass-flat Steppe, where a forge stood cold and a valley full of people who'd branded me traitor slept under roofs that had never once been struck by lightning, because I'd spent twelve years making sure the sky stayed polite.

They didn't deserve me. Neither did I deserve them. Belonging, I was beginning to understand, has nothing to do with deserving.

I pulled off my shirt. The storm-mark across my ribs glowed faintly, Wren's soul pulsing inside it like a second heart, and I laid my palm over it one last time.

"Hold on, little wren," I murmured. "One more turn of the wheel."

Then I knelt in the flooding rain, tipped my head back to the boiling sky, and opened my mouth to give away the only thing I had left—

—and the horizon lit white, and the ground shook, and something with far too many joints crested the ridge behind us, dragging a scroll the length of a river.

The Recall had not waited for my answer after all.

"*Now*, Rosalind," Corvass screamed, in a voice like every roost in the world taking wing at once. "*Speak!*"

And I did.
