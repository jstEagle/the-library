## 31. The Name Given

The hunter came out of the mountain's throat like a debt coming due.

It wore the shape of a man the way a ledger wears ink—something written over something else. Its face was smooth where a face should have been, and where its eyes should have been there were two small black seals, wax-stamped, official. The Undercourt had sent its last argument up the corpse-road, and it did not run. It walked. Things that are certain do not need to hurry.

Behind me, Wren's soul flickered in my storm-mark like a candle in a doorway—there, then almost not there, then there again. The Recall was already pulling at her. I could feel it as a tug under my ribs, a hook set deep in the scar tissue where I'd hidden her, reeling line in hand after hand.

"Rosalind." Sera was on her knees in the snow, drained to gray, her moon-blessing spent holding the Hunt on the glass below. She couldn't stand. She tried anyway. "Rosalind, whatever you're thinking—"

"You know what I'm thinking."

"That's why I'm telling you not to."

Corvass landed on my shoulder, and his weight was wrong for a crow—heavy as a verdict. *You have heard the price,* he said, and his voice was every crow that had ever watched a funeral from a fence post. *I will say it once more, plainly, because gods lie less than courts but we are not gentle. Give me your name. Not your life. Your name. The word your mother used when you were small. The word the bridge-warden made you speak. Every prayer you have ever answered will answer back through you, and you will not be a man who commands weather. You will be weather that remembers being a man.*

"And if I don't?"

*Then the hunter takes her back at moonrise, and she sits in the dark for another twelve years, and then forever.* A pause. The crow's single human eye regarded me without pity. Pity would have been an insult. *There is no third door, storm-child. There never was. You have been walking toward this since the night you called the lightning down on a prince.*

The hunter stopped ten paces away. It didn't draw a weapon. Weapons were for things that could be killed. It simply raised one hand, palm open, and in the palm lay a contract so old the vellum had gone the color of bone—the original bargain, signed in a smokehouse by a dying woman's trembling hand, the blood-price of a king's heir paid in a sister's soul.

"The debt stands," it said. Its voice was the sound of a stamp coming down. "The Recall is invoked. Surrender the vessel."

Wren flared inside my scars. Not words. Just *no*, white-hot, a sister's whole vocabulary compressed into one syllable.

I looked at my hands. They were shaking—not with fear. With weather. Twelve years of holding it down, of the dawn ritual, of the cold iron of horseshoes and the quiet of a valley where magic couldn't follow, and now all of it stood up inside me at once like a congregation rising.

"Sera," I said.

"Don't." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Don't you dare make this noble. There's nothing noble about it. It's just loss wearing a crown."

"Maybe. But it's mine to lose." I crouched beside her. Her eyes were wet and furious and I memorized them, because I suspected I was about to stop having eyes. "When they offer to give it back—and they will, someday, some court or god or well-meaning fool—you tell them I said no. Tell them I finally belonged somewhere, and it wasn't to a name."

"I hate you," she said, which is what love sounds like when it has run out of arguments.

"I know." I kissed her forehead, snow-cold, salt-wet. Then I stood, and turned, and walked past the hunter—past it, because it wasn't who I'd come here to face—to the edge of the mountain's shoulder, where the sky opened over the Glass Steppe like a held breath waiting to be released.

*

The storm was already there.

Of course it was. It had always been there. Every dawn ritual, every swallowed thunderclap, every year of quiet—I hadn't been suppressing my power. I'd been storing it. Greyhollow's magicless silence had been a jar with a lid, and I had filled it to the brim and beyond, and now the lid was off and the sky knew my address.

Lightning moved in the clouds overhead like veins behind skin. The wind came up the mountainside and did not howl; it waited. Even storms, it turns out, can recognize a threshold.

Corvass lifted from my shoulder and hung in the air before me, wings beating slow, a hundred other crows assembling out of nothing around him—a parliament, a jury, a choir. *Speak it,* he said. *Not to me. To the sky. Names belong to whoever hears them true.*

And here is the thing no one tells you about surrendering your name: you have to mean it. A false gift is just a lie with wrapping paper. If I spoke those two syllables with even a shred of holding-back, the gods would taste it and refuse me, and Wren would go into the dark, and I would live long enough to hate myself for a coward's half-measure.

So I reached for the name the way you reach for a splinter. Knowing it will hurt. Knowing the hurt is the point.

My mother used it in the kitchen of our house above the Steppe, flour on her hands, calling me in from the rain. My father used it once, angry, the only time, and I had never let anyone use it since. The bridge-warden had dragged it out of me on the toll-bridge, and the bridge itself had shuddered to hear it, because names are currency and mine had been hoarded twelve years in a vault of silence.

I opened my mouth. The wind went still. The whole Steppe leaned in.

"Rosalind Whitlock," I said.

Just that. No thunder under it, no grandeur. A man's name, spoken plainly into the weather, the way you'd hand someone your last coin.

And the sky took it.

*

I want to describe what it feels like to be unmade, and I can't, because description requires a describer, and the describer went first.

The name left me like a tooth leaving the jaw—shock, then a strange lightness, then the tongue going again and again to the gap where something essential used to be. My memories stayed. That was Corvass's mercy, or maybe just physics: the storm took the word, not the man. I remembered my mother's flour-white hands. I remembered Wren teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass. I remembered Sera saying *I hate you* with tears freezing on her lashes. I remembered everything.

But the *I* that remembered was coming apart at the seams, and what poured through the seams was not grief.

It was pressure. It was charge. It was the smell of rain three valleys away and the static that lifts the hair on your arms before the world breaks open. I felt myself spread. That's the closest word. I spread—up into the cloud deck, down into the frozen ground, out across the Steppe in fingers of wind, and everywhere I went I was still *me*, still watching, still Rosalind-shaped in the way a riverbed shapes water without containing it.

The hunter stamped its seal-hand down on empty air where a man had been standing, and found nothing to seize.

For the first time in its long bureaucratic existence, it hesitated.

Then the storm hesitated too—which is to say, I did—and looked at the thing that had hunted my sister across twelve years and four hundred miles, and understood, with the simple clarity of weather, what needed to happen next.

I came down.

Not lightning. Lightning is a letter; this was the author. The whole storm descended on that mountain shoulder at once, every drop of rain arriving simultaneously, every volt finding ground, and the hunter stood in the middle of it with its wax-seal eyes and its bone-old contract and discovered that paper does not survive water, and neither do debts when the debtor's collateral has been struck from the ledger by a force older than ledgers.

It didn't scream. Courts don't scream. It *unstamped*—the seal-eyes cracking, the written-over shape peeling away from whatever had been underneath, which was nothing, which had always been nothing, because the Undercourt's servants are just obligations given legs. The contract in its hand dissolved into pulp and ran off the mountainside in gray rivulets, and somewhere far beneath us, in a hall of obsidian, I felt a ledger close with a sound like a coffin lid, and felt ten thousand clerks look up from their desks in the same instant, and feel afraid.

Good, I thought, in my new way of thinking, which was less a thought than a change in barometric pressure. *Be afraid. You taught us how.*

*

And then—Wren.

Her soul had been tucked in my storm-mark, riding my scars like a bird in a cupped hand, and when I became the storm, the mark became the storm, and the storm became everywhere, and suddenly she was nowhere to hide and everywhere to be found.

I felt her wake inside the rain.

Not gradually. All at once, the way spring happens—one day the ice is solid, and the next day the whole valley is running with meltwater and nobody saw it begin. Her soul unfolded into the weather around me like a hand opening, and where she passed, the rain changed. It fell upward in small silver threads. It fell sideways in spirals. It gathered itself, drop by drop, into a shape.

A body. Of a sort.

She stood on the mountain shoulder in the aftermath-light, and she was made of rain and radiance—water that held its shape because she wanted it to, lit from within like a lantern with a soul for a flame. She was seventeen in the way souls stay seventeen, the age she'd been when she sold herself for me, and she looked down at her own luminous hands and turned them over, and over, and over.

Then she laughed.

I know her laugh. I'd know it anywhere—in thunder, in rainfall, in the particular way hail bounces. And she laughed now, high and disbelieving and cracked right down the middle with joy, and the sound rolled out across the Steppe as actual thunder, and every crow on the mountain took wing at once.

"It worked," she said, and her voice was rain on a roof, rain on a forge, rain on the tin pail we used to catch drips in our childhood kitchen. "You impossible, stubborn, *idiot*—it actually worked—" She spun, taking in her own body of light and water, and then she stopped, because she realized what it had cost, and her face—that beautiful borrowed face—did something complicated and breaking.

"Rosalind?" she said. Quietly. Testing.

The wind stirred. That was me. Trying to answer.

Her chin came up. Twelve years in the Undercourt's vaults had taught her things, and one of them, apparently, was how to listen to weather. She closed her eyes. She stood very still in the rain that was her own substance and mine mingled together, and she listened the way you listen for a heartbeat under a pillow.

"...You're still in there," she whispered. "Aren't you. You gave them the *name*, not the—you absolute mule, you found the loophole." Another laugh, wetter this time. "Say something. Say anything. Come on. You used to whistle through grass. Whistle."

I gathered a thread of wind and pushed it through a crack in the rocks near her feet, and it made a thin, wavering note. Terrible pitch. Perfect recognition.

She put her glowing hands over her mouth, and the rain fell harder everywhere within a mile, because grief and joy are both just pressure systems, and hers were colliding directly over my heart—or where my heart used to be, or wherever hearts go when they become the sky.

Sera was crying. I registered it distantly, the way you register a change in humidity. She knelt in the slush with Wren's light falling on her face, and she was laughing too, helplessly, the laugh of a woman whose entire belief system has just been demolished and replaced with something better. "He's the *weather*," she kept saying. "He's actually the weather. I swore an oath to the moon and the moon has a sense of humor."

Corvass settled on Wren's luminous shoulder, and his weight was right, this time—light as a feather, because feathers are what crows keep and what they give. *The account is settled,* he said, to all of us, to the mountain, to the listening dark beneath it. *One name, surrendered freely. One soul, returned. One hunter, unmade. The gods accept payment, storm-child, and the gods remember their debtors.*

*And the court?* I asked, in the only voice I had left—a gust rattling the scree slopes.

The crow's human eye glinted. Somewhere far below us, deep in the mountain, I could feel the Undercourt scrambling—ledgers burning, gates grinding, ten thousand hoarded souls beginning to stir in their vaults like embers feeling a draft. The thirteenth moon was turning overhead, full and fat and cheated of its deadline.

*The court,* said Corvass, *is about to learn what happens when the weather holds a grudge.*

Wren looked up at the turning moon, then down at her own shining hands, then out across the Glass Steppe where the reflections of a thousand stolen souls were beginning, very faintly, to rise off the mirror-flat glass like steam off morning water.

"Well," she said, and her voice was every rainstorm I'd ever called and every one I'd ever been, "we'd better get home before it starts raining clerks."

The thunder laughed. It took me a moment to realize the thunder was me.
