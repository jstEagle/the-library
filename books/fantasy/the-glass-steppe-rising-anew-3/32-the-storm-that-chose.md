## 32. The Storm That Chose

The first thing I learned about having a body again was that it *ached*.

Not the old ache of the vaults—twelve years of being a ledger entry, a number in a column, a weight in a drawer that never opened. This was new ache. Living ache. My feet hurt from walking on scree. My throat was raw from screaming my brother's name into a snowstorm. My eyes streamed and stung with wind.

I had forgotten that bodies were such *inconvenient* things. I had also forgotten how much I'd missed them.

I stood on the shoulder of the mountain where the Undercourt's gates had been—or where they had been before my brother unmade them, before he unmade the hunter, before he unmade, as far as I could tell, himself—and I looked out at a world that was doing something impossible.

It was snowing upward.

Flakes rose off the black glass of the Steppe in slow spirals, climbing against every law of weather I'd ever known, gathering into a column that swayed like a reed and did not fall. Lightning moved inside it—not striking down but threading sideways, stitching the column together like seams. And when the wind turned, just for a moment, the whole storm leaned toward me.

Like it was listening.

"Wren," I said aloud, to myself, because someone had to say something. "You're alive. Start there."

Alive was generous. What Corvass had done in the last hour of the court's collapse—what the freed souls had done, rising like embers through the shattering halls—I still didn't fully understand. I knew only this: somewhere between the thirteenth moon turning and the mountain coming apart, my soul had slipped its ledger, crossed the space between the vaults and my brother's scars, and found shelter there. And when Rosalind spoke his true name into the storm and let the gods take it—when he became the thing that had torn the hunter out of existence—the shelter went with him. I had been carried inside the storm. Born out the other side in a rush of rain and light onto the frozen Steppe, gasping, solid, wearing a body the crows had woven for me out of weather and memory.

I hadn't seen him since.

I started walking.

* * *

The wreckage of the Hunt stretched across the glass for half a mile. Shards of obsidian gate. Fragments of hunters that had been contracts given legs, now crumbling into black sand that hissed when the wind touched it. Somewhere under all of it, Sera Vane lay bleeding, having spent her moon-blessing to buy us time—but two of Corvass's crows had already led me to her, and she was breathing, and a remnant paladin named Osk had her wrapped in furs by the time I finished panicking over her. She would live. She'd lost the blessing, not the life. "Go," she'd said, gripping my wrist with a hand that shook. "Find him. Storms don't wander, Wren. They *go* somewhere."

So I walked the line of the wreckage while the snow fell up, calling his name.

"Rosalind!"

The word came out cracked and small. Twelve years I'd spent without a mouth, and the first things I did with one were scream and cry and call for my brother, and I couldn't even feel embarrassed about it because embarrassment required a future, and right then I wasn't sure I had one that included him.

Because here is what I knew, what I had known since the moment I woke on the glass with rain running down my face: names don't come back. In the court I had read ledgers until the script crawled into my dreams. A surrendered name doesn't sit in a vault waiting to be returned. It gets spent. It becomes part of whatever bought it. The gods are made of surrendered names—that's what Corvass told me once, through a crow's crooked beak, back when I was still a dream-voice whispering warnings across the dark. *We are what mortals give up, little ledger-keeper. Every god is a debt that chose to keep paying.*

My brother had paid himself to the sky. There was no clause for refund. I'd have found it if there were. I read *everything.*

"Rosalind!" I shouted again, and my voice broke on the second syllable, and that was when the thunder answered.

Not nearby thunder. Not the random grumbling of a mountain shrugging off its ruins. This came low and deliberate, rolling out of the standing column of storm a quarter mile east—one long peal, soft at the edges, almost gentle.

Almost like a reply.

I stopped walking. My breath hung in front of me in ragged clouds.

"That's not funny," I said to the sky.

Thunder rolled again. Closer. The snow-column began to move—not drifting, not blowing, but *walking*, in the way that a thing with no legs can walk when it has decided where it wants to be. It dragged its base along the glass like a skirt hem, shedding spirals of white, and lightning flickered through it in patterns I almost recognized. Two long flashes, high up. One short flash, low down.

Two-and-one. The signal we'd invented as children, tapping on the wall between our rooms when neither of us could sleep. *Still awake? Still here.*

"No," I whispered. "No, no, no—don't do this to me. Don't you dare do this to me."

The storm stopped ten paces away. Close enough that its edge ruffled my hair. Close enough that the cold coming off it felt less like winter and more like the forge room where I'd sat as a girl, watching him work, the heat of coals on one side and the draft under the door on the other.

"Rosie?" I said. The childhood nickname. The one only I had ever been allowed to use. "Rosie, if that's you—if any part of that is you—tap twice."

Lightning flickered. High, high. Then again, high, high.

Then low. Once.

Two-and-one. Still awake. Still here.

I sat down on the glass right there, in the ruin of everything, and I laughed until it turned into sobbing and then kept going until both ran out and left me hollowed out and light as an empty cup. The storm waited through all of it. Patient. The way he used to wait through my worst tempers when we were young, arms crossed, one eyebrow up, until I burned myself out and he could hand me bread.

"You absolute *idiot*," I said, wiping my face with hands that were mine, warm and clumsy and gloriously mortal. "You gave up your *name*. Do you know what that means? I read those contracts for twelve years. Names don't come back. They don't—there's no provision, there's no—" My voice cracked again. "You're supposed to come home. You promised. Twelve years ago you promised me we'd both get out."

The storm made a sound. Not thunder exactly—a pressure change, a deep shifting hum, like a vast throat clearing. If I hadn't spent a childhood learning to translate my brother's silences, I'd have missed it entirely. But silence was his first language, and apparently the sky had learned it from him.

It sounded like: *I know.*

"It's not fair," I said. "You hear me? Of all the bargains in that rotten court, all the ledgers and clauses and interest rates—they finally wrote one that's *airtight*, and it's yours. Yours. Because you couldn't just steal her back like a normal criminal brother. You had to go and be *noble* about it."

The storm shifted. Snow spiraled off its shoulders. And then, slowly, deliberately, it extended a tendril of cloud and wind down toward me—hesitant, the way a hand hesitates—and brushed the hair back from my face.

Cold. Soft. Careful.

The same gesture. The exact same gesture he'd made the night before the storm, twelve years ago, when I was crying over the blood-price letter and he tucked my hair behind my ear and said, *Whatever happens, Wren, none of it is yours to carry.* I had spent twelve years in a vault replaying that moment, because it was the last time anyone had touched me like I mattered.

Now the sky was doing it.

"Okay," I managed. "Okay." I laughed, wet and helpless. "New rule. Nobody in this family makes any more promises. We clearly can't be trusted with them."

* * *

We spent the rest of the day learning each other again.

That's the only word for it. He was still in there—him, Rosalind, my brother—but stretched thin across miles of air and pressure and light, poured into a shape with no edges. Speaking cost him effort. Every answer came as weather: the double-tap of lightning, the low hum of assent, a sudden warm gust when I said something that pleased him (he liked hearing about the ledgers; my brother always did love knowing how systems worked), a crackle of static that raised the hair on my arms when I mentioned the Understeward, whom he evidently still hated on principle.

I talked. Gods, did I talk. Twelve years of stored words came out of me like water through a broken dam. I told him about the vaults, the other souls, the clerk-spirits who shuffled papers with hands made of ink. I told him how I'd mapped the court's fear of the waking gods the way he used to map escape routes through the valley hills. I told him about the dream-warnings, how I'd screamed the clause at him through Corvass's feathers, hoping some fragment reached him in time.

He answered in his way. And slowly, as afternoon slanted gold across the glass, I realized something extraordinary:

He wasn't sad.

Oh, there was grief in him—when I asked whether he missed having hands, the storm went very quiet, and a single thread of rain fell from its underside like a leak in heaven. But underneath it, threaded through every gust, was something else. Something I'd never heard in my brother's voice in all his years of exile, all his careful gray days of hiding.

Relief.

"Do you know what the strangest thing is?" I said, walking beside the storm's slow drift, my boots crunching on glass. "In the court, I thought about freedom every day. Getting out. Getting a body. Getting back to you. But I never once imagined what it would feel like when it happened. And now I'm here, and you're—" I gestured at all of it, the towering column, the upward snow, the patient lightning. "And you're free too. Maybe freer than either of us planned. No crown hunting you. No court holding a contract. No valley elders with their rules. You're just... weather."

The storm rippled. A gust spun a little eddy of snowflakes in front of me, arranging them—for one absurd moment—into something almost like a shrug.

"Don't you shrug at me. I spent twelve years reading fine print. I've earned the right to lecture." I kicked a shard of obsidian; it skittered away ringing. "Sera's going to wake up soon and start organizing everyone within reach, so we should figure out what we tell her. And the crows will want a report. And Greyhollow—" I stopped. The word landed differently than I expected. Home. The valley where magic couldn't follow, which meant the valley where my brother had been unable to be himself for twelve years. "What happens when we go back?"

The storm went still. All its motion paused except a slow rotation at the top, thoughtful, like a man looking at the horizon.

Then, gently, it pushed a breeze against my back—pointing me west. Toward the valley. Toward home.

And beneath the breeze, so faint I might have imagined it, came a sound I will carry for the rest of my life, however long this strange reborn life turns out to be. A rhythm in the wind. Four notes, rising and falling.

Our mother's lullaby. The one she sang before the fever took her, before the king's tax collectors, before everything. He remembered it. Whatever the gods had taken when they took his name, they had not taken that.

I cried again. Sue me. I'd earned it.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a fallen gate-shard, Osk and the waking Sera and me, with three crows roosting on the obsidian above us like disapproving gargoyles. Sera was upright by dusk, gray-faced and furious at her own weakness, demanding reports in a voice roughened by smoke and blood loss. When I told her what the storm was—who it was—she looked at the distant column glowing faintly against the stars for a long, long time.

"He gave his name," she said at last. "To the gods. Freely."

"Freely," I confirmed. "Idiotically. Beautifully. Pick your adverb."

Something moved across her face—grief and awe wrestling for the same ground. "That's not how the old stories go," she said quietly. "In the stories, the gods *take*. People bargain, and lose, and curse heaven afterward." Her hand drifted to her chest, where her moon-blessing used to live, now just an ache where a warmth had been. "But he chose it. To save you."

"He's always chosen wrong by the world's arithmetic," I said. "And right by ours."

Above us, the crow with the human eye—Corvass's favorite herald—ruffled its feathers and croaked, and the sound carried an odd harmonic underneath it, a voice beneath the voice: *"The gods are listening, little ledger-keeper. They heard what you called him. Idiotically. Beautifully. They may quote you."*

"They'd better not," I said. "I hold copyright."

The crow made a sound suspiciously close to laughter and flew off into the dark.

Later, when Sera slept and Osk kept watch, I stepped outside the camp into the cold and looked up. The storm had drawn closer in the night hours, keeping its distance from the fire but near enough that I could see the slow pulse of lightning at its heart, steady as a heartbeat. Two-and-one. Over and over. Still awake. Still here.

"I'm going to teach you to talk properly," I told it, breath fogging. "Weather words. A whole vocabulary. We'll start with yes and no and *stop worrying about me*, because I know you're doing it."

A warm gust touched my cheek.

"And someday," I said, and my voice went soft, "if the gods ever find a spare name lying around—you know, one nobody's using—we'll talk about that too."

The lightning pulsed. Twice high, once low. But slower this time. Almost wistful.

Far to the west, beyond the edge of the Steppe, past the salt flats and the corpse-road and the border of the valley where magic could not follow—something was changing. I felt it before I understood it, the way I'd once felt prayers moving through the ledgers: a tremor in the air, a pressure behind the stars.

Corvass landed on the gate-shard beside me, folding his wings, his hundred eyes reflecting our fire and the storm and something else besides.

"Little ledger-keeper," the crow-god said, and his voice was grave. "You should know. The elders of Greyhollow have lit their beacon fires tonight. They've seen the storm coming home—and they remember who they exiled. Your valley is deciding, right now, whether to welcome your brother or bar their doors against him."

The storm's lightning pulsed once—low, uncertain.

"And," Corvass added, tilting his head, "they have sent for a covenant-mediator. A woman of the broken order, they said. Someone who understands oaths."

Sera stirred in her sleep, and frowned, as if she already knew.
