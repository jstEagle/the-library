## 33. The Court Beneath the Mountain Falls

Sera Vane had stood in the presence of the moon at its full rising. She had knelt in the Hall of Silver while her order's high cantor sang the dawn-blessing, and she had felt that vast cold attention settle over her like snowfall. She had thought, then, that nothing could be more beautiful than being witnessed by something enormous.

She was wrong.

The moment Rosalind Whitlock gave up his name, every candle in the Undercourt went out.

Not guttered. Not dimmed. *Out*—all at once, ten thousand black tapers snuffed by a single breath, and in the dark there was a sound Sera had no word for. It was like a bell rung underwater. It was like a ledger's spine cracking open. It was like a lock, older than kingdoms, finally turning.

Then the obsidian floor began to glow.

Not with firelight. With *script*. The lines of the great Ledger of Forfeit—the one Wren's soul had been written into, the one Rosalind had nearly signed his life away on—blazed up through the stone itself, every clause and covenant and blood-price burning gold, and then, line by line, the gold began to *lift*. Words peeling off the page of the world like ash off a log.

"It's unbinding," Sera breathed. "Corvass—it's all unbinding."

Above them, perched on a shattered pillar, the crow-god laughed. It was not a kind sound, exactly, but it was not unkind either. It was the laugh of an old thing watching a debt collector get robbed.

"Of course it is," Corvass said. His voice came from everywhere now—from every crow, from the walls, from the storm still roaring somewhere above the mountain's roots. "A court is only as strong as its contracts, little paladin. And its chief hunter has just been *unmade* by a man who no longer exists to be bound. Tell me: what happens to a promise when both the maker and the made-to are gone?"

Sera didn't answer. She was watching the first of the ledger-crats flee.

They came pouring out of their alcoves and archive-cells like beetles when a stone is lifted—small grey figures in robes the color of dust, clutching armfuls of scrolls and seals and stamping-irons, scuttling toward service corridors Sera hadn't known existed. One of them dropped a ledger as he ran. It hit the floor, fell open, and every name on its page rose off the vellum like sparks off a struck flint.

"Oh," Sera said softly. "Oh, look at them."

The souls came up out of the books like embers off a bonfire.

They were small lights, most of them. Dim, worn thin by years—or decades, or centuries—of storage, of being *collateral*, of sitting in dark vaults as interest against debts no living person remembered taking on. But they were rising. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, drifting up through the vaulted dark of the Undercourt like a slow golden snowfall running in reverse, and where they passed, the obsidian walls wept condensation and the dead candles tried, futilely, to relight.

"Where do they go?" Rosalind's sister asked. Wren stood at Sera's side, newly made, her body a weave of rain and pale light, her feet not quite touching the floor. Her voice was the strangest and loveliest thing Sera had ever heard—like rain heard through a window, like someone laughing in another room. "Where do they all go?"

"Home," Sera said. Her throat was tight. "I think they go home."

* * *

They followed the embers upward.

It was not a decision anyone made so much as a gravity they surrendered to. The Undercourt's shifting halls, which had hunted them for two days and nights, which had rearranged themselves into mazes and traps and closing jaws of stone, now seemed to *want* them gone. Corridors opened ahead of them like hands unclenching. Staircases that had spiraled down now obligingly spiraled up. The whole architecture of the place was a bureaucracy, and bureaucracies, Sera understood suddenly, had only one true instinct: self-preservation.

Behind them, deeper in the mountain, something enormous was collapsing. She could hear it—not as sound but as pressure, a series of vast soft implosions, like rooms forgetting they had ever been furnished.

"The court isn't dying," Corvass said, gliding beside her on wings that shed small darknesses like sparks shed light. "Courts don't die. They *dissolve*. Watch. Every contract voided, every office abandoned, every seal broken—the mountain is forgetting what it was for."

"And the crats?"

"Some will make it out. Some won't." The crow-god cocked his head, one human eye catching the ember-light. "Does that trouble you, moon-paladin? They fed on souls for a thousand years."

Sera thought about it honestly, because she had learned—through disgrace, through exile, through a burned oath and a long road—that honesty was the only discipline worth keeping.

"A little," she said. "They were monsters who did monstrous work. But some of them were probably just clerks. Somebody's daughter who took a job under the mountain because it was steady." She watched a grey-robed figure vanish down a corridor, arms full of stamping-irons, moving with the desperate energy of a woman late for something. "My order taught me that evil is a choice you make loudly. Nobody told me how much of it is just paperwork done carefully for a very long time."

Corvass regarded her for a long moment.

"You would have made a terrible hunter," he said, "and an excellent god."

"I'll take the first one. It pays worse but the hours are better."

Ahead of them, Wren laughed—and the laugh moved through the air like weather. The nearest embers brightened at the sound, swarming toward her briefly, moths to a sister-shaped lantern, before continuing their long ascent. Sera saw Wren reach out and let one pass through her palm, and saw her face as it did: grief and wonder braided so tightly together they were indistinguishable.

"That one's going to a farmer," Wren said quietly. "In Lowmark. He's been asleep eleven years. His wife never stopped setting two plates." She looked up at Sera, and her eyes were full of reflected light. "I know things like this now. Twelve years in the vaults—you hear everything. Every soul hums its own story if you're quiet enough to listen."

"What else do you hear?" Sera asked.

Wren turned her face upward, toward the distant ceiling of the world.

"I hear my brother," she said. "He's very loud right now."

* * *

They broke the surface at dawn.

Not the mountain's peak—the Undercourt had no peak; it had *depths*, plural, and the way out was through the old mine-shafts the first soul-debtors had dug with their bare hands—but a cleft in the mountainside high above the Glass Steppe, and the dawn that met them was the most violent, gorgeous thing Sera had ever seen.

Because the sky was full of souls.

They poured out of the cleft behind them in a river of gold, streaming up past the snowline, spreading across the morning like a flock too large to have a shape—and then they scattered. That was the part that undid her. Each ember found its heading and *went*: south over the Steppe, east toward the lowlands, north into the hill country, each one a small sun traveling home to a body waiting in a bed or a grave or a chair by a window where someone still kept watch.

Sera sank down onto a rock and put her face in her hands and cried like she hadn't cried since the night she refused to burn a village of children.

She wasn't even sure what the tears were for. Joy, maybe. Or grief for all the years the joy had been owed. Or the sheer animal relief of watching a thousand-year theft be reversed in a single morning. Somewhere behind her, Wren drifted close and settled beside her—not touching, because Wren's touch was rain and Sera was still flesh, but near, near enough to share warmth that wasn't warmth.

"My brother used to say," Wren said, "that the worst thing about exile is that you stop believing the world can be fixed. You start thinking the brokenness is just... the size of the world." A pause, and then, wryly: "He said it less eloquently. He was holding a horseshoe at the time."

"He held a lot of horseshoes," Sera managed. "I'm given to understand it was his entire personality."

"It really was."

And then, because the moment demanded it and because twelve years underground had earned her the right, Wren laughed again—and the sound rolled down the mountainside and across the Glass Steppe below, where the mirror-flat expanse caught it and threw it back multiplied, a hundred laughing reflections, until the whole plain rang with it like a struck bowl.

Below, far off, the Steppe's surface shimmered. And in the shimmer, Sera saw the reflections moving freely now—no longer stalkers, no longer the court's eyes, just reflections again, doing what reflections do. Somewhere in that glass, a copy of a farrier who no longer existed was dissolving into ordinary light.

Good, she thought. Let him rest.

* * *

They stayed on the mountainside three days, because the world required witnessing.

Ember after ember rose past them. Some paused—Sera swore some of them *paused*—circling Wren once before continuing on, as if paying respects to the soul who had spent twelve years learning all their names. On the second day, a light no bigger than a candleflame hovered before Sera herself for a long moment, flickering in a pattern she almost recognized, and then darted away westward, fast, urgent, joyful.

"The moon-touched children," she whispered. "From the village. From—" She couldn't finish.

"They're going back," Wren said gently. "All of them. The ones the court took as infants, as payment against your order's old debts. They're going home to grow up."

Sera closed her eyes. *Her order had fallen*—had been declared heretical, disbanded, hunted—for refusing to burn those same children. And all along, the children hadn't even been *there* anymore. Their bodies slept in the village and their souls sat in a vault beneath a mountain, filed under *assets*.

She had lost everything defending a truth she'd never fully known. Now the truth was streaming past her in gold, going home.

On the third day, the last of the embers rose, and the sky over the Steppe was empty and clean and blue, and Corvass landed on a boulder in front of them and folded his wings with the finality of a judge closing a book.

"So," the crow-god said. "The court beneath the mountain is dissolved. Its hunter is unmade. Its ledgers are ash. Its hoard is returned." He tilted his head, human eye gleaming. "Which leaves the question of what comes next. And the question of Greyhollow."

Sera stiffened. "Greyhollow?"

"The valley where magic cannot follow," Corvass said. "Where a certain farrier spent twelve years keeping himself small. The valley that branded him traitor when he left." The crow hopped once along the boulder, feathers rustling. "The gods are awake now, moon-paladin. Truly awake, and hungry, and *grateful*. And gratitude, among our kind, wants form. It wants covenant. It wants a place." His eye fixed on hers. "There is a valley that has lived without magic for a very long time. I think the gods mean to offer it a gift—and I think someone should be there to help the valley decide whether to accept. Someone the valley might actually listen to."

"Not me. I'm nobody to them."

"You're a paladin of the moon who chose mercy over law," Corvass said. "In a world just freed from a court that confused the two, that makes you the rarest creature alive." He spread his wings. "Besides. Your friend's sister cannot cross that boundary alone. Rain does not do well in a rainless land. But a sworn keeper of covenants..." The crow's beak clicked, almost amused. "...might negotiate passage."

Sera looked at Wren. Wren, bodiless and radiant and twelve years stolen, was looking east with an expression Sera recognized from mirrors—homesickness aimed at a place that had hurt you.

"She wants to go home," Sera said.

"She wants to find what's left of her brother," Corvass corrected. "And I suspect what's left of him is waiting exactly where he always wanted to be."

Sera stood. Her legs ached. Her blessing was spent, her order was ash, her name was still disgraced in every capital from here to the sea. None of it mattered. She slung her pack, and it weighed nothing at all.

"All right," she said. "Greyhollow. But if the elders give me any trouble, I'm invoking the moon."

"On what grounds?"

"On grounds that I'm tired, armed, and right."

Corvass laughed—a real one this time, crackling like static before a storm—and lifted off into the clean blue morning, wheeling east. Wren rose beside him, trailing light. And Sera Vane, disgraced paladin of a broken order, started down the mountainside toward a magicless valley that had no idea what was coming home to it.

Behind them, deep in the mountain, the last light went out of the last ledger, and the Undercourt was only stone.
