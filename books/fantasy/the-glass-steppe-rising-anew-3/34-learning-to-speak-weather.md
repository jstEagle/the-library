## 34. Learning to Speak Weather

The first thing you have to understand about my brother is that he is not gone.

Everyone else in the world would look at the rain over Greyhollow and see weather. They'd feel the wind shift and say *storm coming*, the way people said it for twelve years while I sat in a vault beneath a mountain made of ledgers, and they'd be right, technically, the way a ledger is technically true. But I spent three days after the mountain fell sitting on a wet hillside with my knees pulled to my chest, watching a thundercloud refuse to leave, and I can tell you: that is not weather. That is Rosalind Whitlock, doing what he has always done, which is standing between me and whatever's coming.

He just doesn't have a face anymore.

We're working on it.

* * *

Communication started badly.

I want that on the record. Someday, when this all becomes one of those stories the valley tells its children—the ones Sera is already drafting in her careful paladin's hand—someone will smooth it into something dignified. *And Wren spoke to her brother, and he answered.* No. What actually happened was that I stood in the ruins of the Undercourt's gates, soaked through, screaming his name at the sky because I didn't know yet that he didn't have one anymore, and the sky did nothing at all.

That was the worst hour of my life, and I include the twelve years of being a ledger entry in that count. Because I had been a soul in a jar. I had been collateral. I had watched the crats of the Undercourt file past me like I was grain in a silo, and none of it had hurt as much as shouting *Rosalind* at an empty sky and getting silence back.

Then Corvass landed on a fencepost beside me—a crow the size of a cat now, or maybe I was still small from being dead—and said, in his voice like a drawer full of knives:

"He can't hear that name. You gave it away for him. It belongs to us now."

"You *took* it," I said.

"We were given it." The crow preened, unbothered. "There is a difference. Gods are made of given things. Ask him yourself."

"How?"

Corvass cocked his head—one eye human-brown, one eye black as a coin. "You're his sister. Figure it out."

Which was, I would learn, extremely characteristic of the old gods. They wake up after centuries of silence and their entire philosophy is *you try.*

* * *

Here's what I knew about my brother that nobody else alive knew:

He hums when he works. Not songs—he doesn't remember whole songs—but the shape of them, the first four notes of a lullaby our mother sang about a boat on grey water, looping over and over while the hammer falls. He does it when he's content. He did it the night before the storm, the last ordinary night, hammering a plowshare in the forge while I sat on the woodpile and pretended I wasn't afraid of what he was becoming.

He taps twice before he knocks. Always twice, always the same rhythm, since we were children sharing a room and he wanted me to know it was him and not Father.

He lies badly and jokes well, and both get worse when he's scared.

So on the second day, instead of screaming, I sat down on the hillside in the drizzle and I hummed. Four notes. Boat on grey water. Over and over, until my voice cracked, until the drizzle stopped falling around me in a circle maybe ten feet wide—not raining elsewhere, just here, just holding, the way a person goes quiet when they're listening so hard it hurts.

Then, very softly, thunder rolled. Four beats.

Not the same notes. Thunder can't do pitch, not really—it's all boom and rumble—but the *rhythm* was there. The cadence. The shape of the tune, carried in timing instead of tone, and I laughed and cried at the same time which is a thing bodies do apparently, and the thunder stuttered, embarrassed, and I said, out loud, to the cloud:

"There you are."

It rained gently on my head for the rest of the afternoon. Like a hand. Like the way he used to put his palm on my hair when I was small and couldn't sleep.

That was the first word. Not a word, exactly. But the first sentence: *I'm here. Are you?*

Yes, I told him, in every way I had. Yes.

* * *

We built a language out of almost nothing, and I'm proud of it, and I'm going to describe how it works because someday someone will need to talk to a storm and no one will have written it down.

Thunder does rhythm. One long roll means yes. Two short raps—his knock, always two, even now—means *it's me.* A low continuous grumble means he's thinking. Silence, real silence, the kind where even the wind stops, means he's frightened, and I learned to sit with him through those without pushing, the same as you'd sit with anyone in the dark.

Wind does mood. A warm gust against your face is affection—I am not making this up; the first time it happened I was mid-sentence telling him something stupid about a guard in the Undercourt who used to sneeze whenever he lied, and a gust hit me square in the chest and nearly knocked me over, and Corvass, watching from the fence, said "He's laughing" in a tone of profound scholarly disgust. Cold air sliding over your shoulders is worry. Wind that circles you once, slow, checking every side—that's him looking at you. Making sure you're whole. He does it constantly. He cannot stop doing it. Twelve years of guilt condensed into weather that patrols its little sister like a dog that's been left too many times.

Rain does everything else. Rain is his hands. Rain writes—well, sort of. It can't spell. But if you ask him a question with two answers, he'll rain on your left or your right. Three answers took some negotiation. We eventually worked out a system where I'd hold up fingers and he'd gust the number back, and I'll tell you, watching a storm do arithmetic by blowing my hair eastward three times is one of the strangest and most tender things I have ever witnessed.

Lightning he saves. Lightning costs him something—I can feel it in the air pressure, a kind of held breath—and he only spends it for big truths. When I asked him once, carefully, whether he regretted it, all of it, the name and the storms and the twelve years, the lightning split the sky so bright I saw the bones of my own hands, and then thunder came in a pattern I hadn't taught him but understood anyway, because it was Mother's lullaby again, the part where the boat comes home.

No. The answer was no. He'd do it all again.

I didn't ask him anything hard for three days after that. Some truths you need to digest.

* * *

Grief has its own dialect.

I learned this in the fourth week, when the valley elders sent word—they wouldn't come themselves, cowards with good posture—that the forge could be reclaimed if any family remained to claim it. Our family. Our father's forge, cold for thirteen years, the tools probably rusted, the house above it probably fallen in.

I went down to look. Sera came with me, silent, armored in that plain grey she wears now that her order is ash and her oath is new. The door stuck. The hinges screamed. Inside, everything was exactly where a life had been interrupted: the kettle on the hook, the boots by the door, and over the cold hearth, the little shelf where Mother had kept her figurines—three clay birds, chipped, that she swore were worth more than the house.

Two birds had survived. The third lay in pieces on the hearthstone, and I don't know why that was the thing that broke me, after everything—after the vault and the ledgers and the mountain and my brother dissolving into weather—but I picked up the broken bird and sat down on our father's floor and grieved like a child, loudly, ugly, with no language at all.

Outside, the sky went strange.

Not violent. That's what I want people to understand. It didn't rage. The clouds gathered close and low, and the light went the color of deep water, and the rain came down soft and steady and *stayed*, hours longer than any spring shower should, and underneath it the thunder kept doing the two-knock pattern, over and over, gentle, patient, the way you'd tap on a door of a room where someone you love is crying, saying: *it's me. It's me. It's me.*

He couldn't hold me. So he held the sky around me instead.

When I finally came outside, wrung out and red-eyed, Sera looked up at the low grey ceiling and said, quietly, "How long can he keep that up?"

"I don't know," I said.

"It's been six hours."

I looked up. The rain fell on my face, and I felt—for the first time since the mountain—the shape of what my brother had become, and what it cost. A body ends its grieving. It sleeps, it eats, it forgets for merciful minutes. But a storm that loves someone has no eyelids to close. He had been crouched over my sadness all afternoon with no way to set it down.

*I'm all right,* I told the sky, and patted the doorframe, twice. *Go rest. Go be weather somewhere.*

The clouds thinned. Reluctantly. Like a dog told to lie down who keeps one eye open.

Sera watched it go, and then she said something I've thought about every day since. She said: "Your brother spent twelve years practicing how to give things away. I think he got too good at it."

She wasn't wrong. Which is why the next part of this chapter exists, and why I fought him about it, and why I won.

* * *

Because here is the thing about a storm with no name: he keeps trying to apologize himself out of existing.

It started small. I'd be talking to him—telling him about the Undercourt, about Wren-things he'd missed, filling thirteen years back into him like water into a dry well—and I'd notice the rain drifting off, thinning, going to go *be useful* somewhere. Watering the barley fields. Filling the creek. Putting out a chimney fire two valleys over. And I'd have to whistle him back, literally whistle, like a falconer, except the falcon is the sky.

"You don't have to earn staying," I told him one evening. Two knocks, sheepish, from the treeline. "I mean it. You don't have to be *useful* to be allowed."

Another two knocks. Less sheepish. More stubborn.

"Oh, don't you take that tone with me, I invented that tone."

A gust of wind knocked my hood over my eyes. My brother, ladies and gentlemen. Dead, renamed, de-bodied, and still capable of being an insufferable older sibling via atmospheric pressure.

But the real fight came later, on the night I caught him trying to leave.

I'd woken past midnight to a wrongness in the air—the particular hush of a valley holding its breath—and run out barefoot into the yard in time to see the clouds pulling away northward, gathering themselves up, condensing into something traveling-fast and traveling-far, and I knew, I *knew* with the bone-knowledge of twins-or-close-enough, that he'd decided the valley would be safer without a rogue storm living overhead. That the elders' fear, the court's hunters, whatever might come looking—all of it would follow *him*, and if he left, Greyhollow could go back to its magicless sleep and everyone would be safe, and that is the arithmetic of a man who has spent thirteen years believing his existence is a debt other people pay.

So I stood in the middle of the yard in my nightgown, freezing, furious, and I sang.

Mother's lullaby. All of it, every verse, including the one about the grey water and the far shore that we never sang because it made her cry. My voice cracked on the high part. I didn't care. And the storm stopped moving north, hung there over the ridge like a held breath, and then it came *back*, fast, and the rain came down around me in a ring and the thunder said *it's me, it's me, it's me* and I shouted at the sky, at my brother, at the idiot weather he'd become:

"THEN STAY BEING YOU! HERE! WHERE I CAN FIND YOU!"

Silence. Total, terrified silence.

And then, so soft it was barely weather at all: rain on my upturned face, one drop at a time, deliberate, like counting. Like a promise being signed.

He stayed.

* * *

We're not finished. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in this valley wants to pretend the story ended on the mountain and everything since has been epilogue. It hasn't. We are two people—or one person and one meteorological event—relearning each other from scratch, and some nights it's clumsy and some nights I say something and the silence that follows is so long I wonder if he heard, and some mornings the dawn comes up gold and clean and I stand in the yard and laugh for no reason and the wind laughs with me, spinning the laundry line, rattling the shutters like knuckles on a door: *up, lazy, up.*

Yesterday I taught him a joke. Well—I told him a joke, about the Understeward's filing system, and there was a pause, and then hail. Tiny, pea-sized, perfectly harmless hail, bouncing off the roof in a burst, and Corvass nearly fell off the fencepost laughing, and I decided that counted. My brother's laugh is hail now. I can live with that. I can live with a great many things, it turns out, if they knock twice.

Last night, though, something changed.

I was half-asleep in the loft of the rebuilt forge—we reclaimed it, the two birds sit on the mantel, the third I keep meaning to mend—when the wind came through the window gap and did something new. Not a knock. Not the lullaby-rhythm. It was slower, deliberate, shaped, and it took me three passes to catch it, and when I did I sat bolt upright in the dark with my heart going like a smith's hammer.

Because the wind was humming.

Not thunder-rhythm. Humming. Actual pitch, actual melody—thin and strange, a sound like the air itself remembering having a throat—and it was playing the fourth verse of Mother's song. The far-shore verse. The one that made her cry. And under the melody, so faint I had to press my ear to the window-gap to be sure, there was something else: a voice-shaped absence, a place in the wind where a voice used to be, straining toward words the way a man underwater strains toward light.

He was trying to speak. Actually speak. Not knock, not gust, not rain-on-left-for-yes. Words.

The melody trailed off. The wind settled, embarrassed, and went back to being ordinary night air, and I lay awake until dawn staring at the rafters, thinking: he found a piece of his name. Or a piece of himself the name left behind. Something with a throat in it.

At sunrise, Corvass landed on the windowsill with a feather in his beak—one of his own, black shot through with silver, and when I took it, it crackled faintly against my skin like static before lightning.

"The storm is growing a voice," the crow-god said. "Gods are made of surrendered names, little ledger-girl. And surrendered things don't vanish." His coin-black eye fixed on mine. "They gather."

"Gather into what?"

"That," said Corvass, "depends on what he gathers them *for*. And whether the valley that exiled him is prepared to welcome what comes home wearing his shape." He hopped to the sill's edge, wings unfolding. "Because the elders are meeting today, girl. And they've sent for a moon-paladin to attend. They intend to decide what to do about the storm over their heads."

I was already pulling on my boots.

"They can decide," I said, "right after they decide what to do about me."

Corvass made a sound I think was approval, and flew ahead of me down the road toward the elder-hall, where Sera Vane stood waiting at the doors with her arms crossed and an expression I'd learned to read as *this is going to be a disaster, and I will be standing next to you when it happens.*
