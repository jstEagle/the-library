## 35. The Return to Greyhollow

The valley announced itself before I saw it.

We were two days out from the corpse-road, Wren walking beside me with her new-made feet leaving prints in the frost like anyone else's, when the wind changed. It came down off the northern ridge carrying woodsmoke and wet stone and the particular green smell of Greyhollow in late autumn—and it carried something else too. A pressure. A hush. The feeling of walking into a room where everyone has stopped talking about you.

Wren felt it first. She stopped on the trail, tilted her head, and frowned at nothing.

"It's quiet," she said. "Wrong-quiet."

"That's the valley." I adjusted my pack and kept walking, because if I stopped I would have to think about what we were walking into, and I had decided somewhere on the Glass Steppe that thinking about Greyhollow was a luxury for after. "Twelve years of nothing living in that air. Magic doesn't cross the ridgeline. Never has."

"And him?"

I didn't pretend not to know who she meant.

"The storm got in anyway," I said. "It's been getting in for twelve years. Rain still falls here, Wren. Wind still blows. Whatever your brother is now, he's been here the whole time, wearing weather like a coat."

She walked faster after that. I let her.

* * *

Greyhollow hadn't changed, which was somehow worse than if it had.

Same crooked lanes. Same slate roofs furred with moss. Same smokehouse where old Tam Vessel had prayed himself into a grave and started all of this—the gods waking, the crows talking, the whole rotten beautiful machinery of it. I looked at its sagging door as we passed and felt twelve years of my own disgrace look back at me through the boards.

The villagers came out of their houses the way people come out during an earthquake—slowly, cautiously, checking each other's faces before committing to a direction. Word travels fast in a valley of four hundred souls, and word had clearly traveled ahead of us: the exiled farrier's sister, returned from the dead. And with her, a paladin in a moon-cult tabard so patched and faded it was more memory than cloth.

An elder stepped into our path at the well. Magda Sorrel, oldest of the nine, hands knotted around a walking stick she'd never needed until this year. Behind her stood the others: Bram, hollow-eyed since his son had gone south and never written; little Ilse, who'd been a child when I arrived and now had grey in her beard; the rest, arranged behind them like a wall built of doubt.

"Sera Vane," Magda said. She knew my name. Of course she did—I'd spent three days in this valley asking questions before Rosalind Whitlock ever trusted me, and elders remember questions. "They said you'd died on the Steppe."

"Reports exaggerated."

"They say a great many things are exaggerated lately." Her eyes went past me to Wren and stayed there, and something moved across her face that I couldn't name—not fear exactly, not grief. Recognition, maybe. "You have her eyes."

"He talked about you," Wren said. Her voice was steady but her hands weren't. "Every night by the fire, near enough. He never said your names, but he described you. Magda who kept the ledgers. Bram who taught him horseshoes. Ilse who—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He remembered everything. Twelve years, and he remembered everything, and he loved every one of you, and you threw him away."

The lane went very silent.

"Child," Magda began.

"I'm older than you were when you signed the exile," Wren said. "I did the arithmetic in the Undercourt. There wasn't much else to do in there. So don't *child* me."

Bram made a sound like a man taking a blow. Ilse looked at the ground.

"We didn't know what they'd do to her," Magda said, and now she was speaking to me, or past me, or to the air where a farrier used to stand. "The crown's men gave us a choice. Give up the storm-caller or lose the tithe and the protection both. He was already gone by then—we thought. We thought the storm took him."

"You thought wrong," I said. "He walked out of that storm alive and came back here and shod your horses for twelve years under a false name because this valley was the only place magic couldn't follow him. You fed him. You paid him. You watched him grow old before his time and none of you ever once asked why a man that young had eyes like that."

"We were afraid."

"Yes," I said. "That's what I keep coming back to. Everyone I've met on this road was afraid, and everyone's fear cost someone else everything. The court was afraid of the gods. The crown was afraid of the court. You were afraid of the crown. And the bill landed on a boy who called one storm to save his sister from drowning."

Wren made a small sound beside me. I kept my eyes on Magda.

"So no, I'm not here for absolution, and neither is she. We're here because the gods sent us. And the gods have terms."

* * *

We convened in the meeting-house that evening, all nine elders, plus half the valley crowded at the windows and doors because privacy in Greyhollow is a rumor nobody's ever confirmed. Candles everywhere—people had started lighting them again, ever since Tam Vessel's prayer, ever since word spread that prayers were being answered. Little shrines had sprung up on doorsteps. I counted seven different kinds on the walk over.

Magda sat at the head of the table and looked like a woman preparing to negotiate with a flood.

"Terms," she said.

I laid Corvass's feather on the table. It was black at the base and silver at the tip, and it moved slightly in air that wasn't moving, and everyone in the room pretended not to stare at it except the children at the windows, who stared openly, because children haven't yet learned which things are too frightening to look at directly.

"The old gods wake," I said. "Not just here—everywhere. But here is special. This valley pushed magic out for centuries, and that made it a wound in the world, and wounds draw attention. The Undercourt used it as a blind spot to hide its ledger-work. The crown used it as a dumping ground for people it feared. And underneath all of it, the land itself has been waiting. Holding its breath. The gods want to make an offer, and they asked me to carry it, because—" I paused, because this part still caught in my throat like a fishbone. "Because the god of crows says I have credibility. I refused the court's bargains, I broke my order rather than burn innocent people, and I spent my blessing defending a storm. Apparently that counts for something in the accounting up there."

"What kind of offer?" Bram asked. His voice was rough.

"A covenant. Greyhollow becomes the first covenant-ground in the world—a place where the old gods and mortals hold each other to terms. Not worship. Not servitude. Terms. The valley keeps the shrines, keeps the old days, teaches the god-touched children instead of hiding them. In exchange, the gods ward the valley—no court, no crown, no hunter crosses the ridgeline uninvited. Ever again."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a crow called twice, and every adult in that room flinched.

"And the price?" Magda said. Elders always ask about the price. It's the only question they respect.

"The price was paid twelve years ago," I said. "By a boy you exiled. The gods consider the account settled." I let that sit for a moment. "But there's a second matter. The guardian."

Ilse spoke for the first time. "The storm."

"The storm. It's already here. It's been here since before the covenant was offered—it's the reason the rain comes gentler over the fields than it used to, the reason the forge-fire never spread last spring, the reason—" I stopped, because Bram had gone white, and I realized he'd lost barns to fire last spring, and I realized the fire had stopped at his fence line for no reason anyone could name. "Yes. That. The gods offer to bind the storm to the valley formally. A guardian, not a haunting. But the choice isn't mine or the gods'. It belongs to the man the storm used to be. And he'll want to hear it from you. All of you. What you're prepared to say to Rosalind Whitlock."

Nobody spoke. The candles guttered in a draft, and for one absurd moment I thought—hoped—that he was already listening, that the draft was him leaning in.

Then Magda Sorrel stood up, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and turned to face the crowded doorway and the whole valley beyond it.

"Bring him, then," she said. "Whatever's left of him. Bring him to the forge, where he worked. And we'll say it where he can hear."

* * *

They gathered at the old forge two nights later, under a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain.

The whole valley came. Four hundred people standing in the mud of the smithy yard, holding lanterns, holding candles, holding each other's elbows the way crowds do when they're afraid but determined to be afraid together. Wren stood at the front with me, dressed in a borrowed coat, looking so much like her brother at that age that several people visibly struggled with it.

The elders had debated for two days about what to do with the forge itself. Someone had wanted to dismantle it—the site of the deception, the liar's anvil. Bram had put a stop to it. "He kept it clean," Bram told me afterward, in the voice of a man confessing. "All those years. I used to think he was just particular. Now I think—" He hadn't finished. He didn't need to.

Magda stood at the anvil with the ledger of Greyhollow open in front of her, the actual book, the one with the exile writ recorded in ink that was brown now instead of black.

"Rosalind Whitlock," she read aloud, and saying the name aloud seemed to cost her something physical. "Called to account for the storm of the ninth of Frostmoon, in the year the king's heir perished. Sentenced to exile without name, without return, without—" She closed the book. "This writ is void. We void it. Before witnesses, before whatever gods are listening, and before him, if he can hear us. We were wrong. Not frightened—wrong. There's a difference, and it matters, and we spent twelve years pretending it didn't."

The wind picked up.

It came down off the ridge in a long slow exhale, and the lanterns bent but didn't go out, and the rain—which had been sulking at the edges of the sky all evening—came on soft, deliberate, falling only inside the yard and nowhere else, a column of water with four hundred people standing dry-eyed in the middle of it. Except they weren't dry-eyed. Nobody was.

The rain moved. That's the only way I can describe what I saw. It circled the forge once, slow as a procession, and touched the anvil, and touched the doorframe, and touched Bram's shoulder, and touched the step where a young man had sat and eaten his supper alone for twelve years. And everywhere it touched, the crowd bowed their heads, and Wren laughed and wept at the same time and said, "That's him. That's how he says hello. He always touched the doorframe."

Magda raised her hand and the crowd quieted, though the rain went on circling.

"Guardian of Greyhollow," she said, loudly, formally, the way she'd read proclamations for forty years. "The valley offers covenant. Shelter, remembrance, and a home that cannot cast you out, because there will be no power here greater than yours. Ask your terms."

The rain stopped circling.

For a long moment there was nothing but the drip of eaves and four hundred people holding one breath between them. Then the wind rose—not violent, not threatening, just present, the way a voice is present—and it moved through the yard and through the crowd and it smelled like ozone and hot iron and something older than either, and I swear on my broken oath that I heard it, heard *him*, not in words but in the way weather means things:

*No chains.*

"No chains," Wren repeated instantly, translating, her hand pressed flat against her sternum where a soul had been kept for twelve years. "He says no chains. No binding, no summoning, no calling him down to punish anyone. If he guards, he guards free."

*No altar,* the wind said, or the rain, or the space between them. *No sacrifices. No kneeling.*

"No altars, no sacrifice, no kneeling," Wren echoed, and then stopped, and her face crumpled and reassembled itself. "And—he says—" She pressed her fist to her mouth. When she lowered it she was smiling. "He says someone should finally fix the south pasture fence. Twelve years. He's been looking at that fence for twelve years."

A ragged laugh went through the crowd, the specific laugh of people discovering they're allowed to be happy, and old Ilse shouted back at the sky, "I'll get Bram on it tomorrow!" and the rain fell harder for a moment, and I would testify anywhere, before any court, that the rain was laughing.

Magda waited out the laughter, then raised the ledger one final time.

"Covenant accepted, then. Witnessed and sealed." She tore the exile writ from the book—tore it, in front of everyone—and held it up, and the wind took it, lifted it, spun it once above the crowd like a gull riding a thermal, and carried it up into the dark where nobody would ever find it again. "Welcome home, Rosalind."

And the whole valley said it back. Four hundred voices, ragged and off-key and utterly certain: "*Welcome home.*"

The storm broke open overhead—all at once, joyously, the way summer storms break—and washed the yard and the lanterns and the mud and the grief and the twelve years clean, and nobody ran for shelter. They stood in it. Children danced in it. Wren stood in the middle of it with her arms out and her face turned up, laughing, talking, arguing with her brother about the fence again while the thunder answered her in low rolling syllables that might have been grumbling and might have been laughter and were almost certainly both.

I stayed at the edge, under the eaves of the forge, because some part of me is always at the edge, under the eaves, watching. That's what I'm for, I suppose. Watching, remembering, writing it down.

But as I stood there, a crow dropped onto the anvil beside me. Black feathers, silver-tipped. One eye human, one eye older than kingdoms.

"Well," Corvass said, in the voice of a creditor closing a satisfactory account. "The valley has its storm, the girl has her brother, and the writ is confetti. Everything balances." He cocked his head at the dancing crowd, at Wren, at the rain. "Almost everything. There's a matter outstanding, paladin. A small thing. A feather-weight thing. You'll want to hear it before you found your little order here."

I looked at him. He looked at me with the patience of a creature who has outwaited empires.

"Tell me," I said.

And the crow-god of small prayers leaned close, and beneath the distant thunder, told me what the gods had never yet asked of anyone—

and what they intended to ask of me.
