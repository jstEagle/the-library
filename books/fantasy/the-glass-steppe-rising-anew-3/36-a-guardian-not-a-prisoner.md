## 36. A Guardian, Not a Prisoner

I had forgotten what it was like to have hands.

Not the shape of them—I remembered that, callused palms and a burn scar on the left thumb from my first year at the forge—but the *having*. The weight of a thing held. The decision of where to put yourself in a room. A body is a series of small choices, and I had made none of them for a season now.

Instead I was everywhere at once, which meant I was nowhere in particular.

The rain moved over Greyhollow in the long gray light of late autumn, and I moved with it. I fell on the terraced barley fields in a fine, patient drizzle because old Marta's crop needed a slow drink, not a drowning. I pooled in the cattle troughs. I ran off the slate roofs of houses whose names I knew and whose doors I had once stood outside of, hat in hand, waiting to be paid for shoes I'd nailed in silence. I slid down the gully behind the forge—*my* forge, though someone else worked it now—and gathered in the millpond, and sat there, if sitting could be said of water, watching the moon come up doubled on the surface.

This was my life. This was, against every expectation I had carried into the storm, a good one.

Then the crows came, and the gods made their offer, and I learned that even weather can be tempted.

* * *

Corvass arrived the way he always did: without announcement, as if he had been there all along and merely decided to be noticed. Three crows dropped out of the dark onto the millpond's rim of stones, and the middle one had the human eye, amber and knowing, that I would know anywhere now.

"You're brooding," Corvass said. "Water can't brood. It's undignified."

"I'm *raining*," I said, and lifted a gust to ruffle his feathers, which he tolerated with the weary patience of an uncle at a wedding.

"Brooding," he repeated. "The whole valley feels it. You've been sulking over the north ridge for three days. The shepherds are calling it an ill wind."

"The shepherds are right." I let myself thin out across the pond, a mist on the water, because it was easier to say hard things when I wasn't concentrated anywhere. "Winter's coming. Wren dances in me when it rains and talks to me when it doesn't, and Sera has started building something on the temple hill that looks suspiciously like an order, and the elders have stopped flinching when thunder rolls. It's good, Corvass. That's the problem. It's good, and I keep waiting for it to be taken away, because everything else was."

The crow-god preened once, twice. When gods are deciding how honest to be with you, they make you wait.

"There's a matter," he said finally. "The others sent me. They've been arguing about it since the court fell, and they argue the way glaciers argue, so forgive the delay."

"The others being—"

"The waking ones. The old gods. Your colleagues, I suppose you'd call them, though 'colleague' implies wages." He hopped closer to the water's edge. "They owe you, Rosalind Whitlock. More than they owe anyone living. You gave them your name freely, and a name given freely is the only currency gods respect. So they've voted—such as voting means among beings who predate the concept—to give something back."

The pond went very still around me. Even the rain held itself.

"They can restore it," Corvass said. "Your name. Not just speak it back to you—they can *make* it yours again, unburned, unwritten in any ledger, theirs no longer. And with the name comes the rest. Flesh. Bone. Hands." He tapped the stone with his beak. "A body, farrier. Human form, whole and mortal, standing in this valley by dawn if you want it. The storm goes back to being weather, and you go back to being a man. No court holds a claim on you. No contract names you. You'd be free in the oldest sense—the sense where free just means *yours*."

I did not answer immediately, which for a storm is a long time. The moon crossed a quarter of the sky. Somewhere up-valley a dog barked at nothing, or at me, or at the idea of me.

"Why," I said at last. "Gods don't give refunds."

"This isn't a refund. It's—" He stopped. Started again, and I heard something rare in him: uncertainty. "When you gave us your name, you gave it into a storm that was already ours. We thought we were taking a tool. What we got was—" His feathers ruffled, and not from my wind. "You've been raining on barley fields for a season. Do you know what the other gods see when they look at you? They see a power that could level mountains choosing, every single hour, to water turnips. They see mercy with no name attached to it. Mercy that isn't *for* anything. They've never seen that before. Names make gods selfish, Rosalind. Every god you've ever heard of is a name that wanted something. You're the first thing we've ever had that wants nothing, and gives anyway."

"And so they want to fix that," I said. "Give me a name again so I'll want things like a proper divine instrument."

"So they want to *reward* you," Corvass snapped, and then caught himself, and was quiet a moment. "And yes. Perhaps also that. Gods are not complicated, whatever the theologians say. We see a good thing and we want to keep it. A body keeps you. A name keeps you. Right now you're weather, and weather belongs to everyone and no one, and some of the older ones find that... unsettling."

There it was. Underneath the gratitude and the amber eye and the careful phrasing: they wanted me *held*. Filed. Made legible. A guardian with papers.

I thought about hands.

* * *

I went to see Wren before I answered. Some questions you don't ask a crow; you ask your sister.

She lived now in the small house below the temple hill, the one with the crooked chimney, and she kept her window open at night no matter the cold, because she said closed windows felt like held breath. I came to her as fog off the pond, thin and silver, and curled through the gap and settled in the corner of her room like a cat made of cold.

She was awake. She was always awake when I came; twelve years in the Undercourt's vaults had burned the habit of easy sleep out of her, and she kept midnight hours the way I used to keep the forge. She was mending a shirt by candlelight, badly, and talking to herself in the low running commentary of people who spent too long alone.

"Wren," I said, in the voice I'd learned to make—a pressure in the room, a warmth in the candleflame, thunder held so far away it was only a suggestion.

Her needle stopped. Her face did the thing it always did, the quick grief-and-joy scramble, because hearing me was still hearing me and never quite not hurting. "Rosalind. You're dripping on my floor."

"I'm always dripping on your floor."

"One of us should own a bucket." She set down the shirt. "What is it? You do your thinking-out-loud rain when something's wrong. Marta told Sera the north ridge has been miserable for days."

"Marta talks to Sera now?"

"Marta brings Sera pies. It's terrifying. Everyone's becoming friends. Answer the question."

So I told her. The offer, the name, the body, the hands. I told her the way storms tell things, which is honestly but all at once, and when I finished the candle had guttered twice and she had gone very still, the mending forgotten in her lap.

"They can give you back to yourself," she said. "Flesh and blood. A face. Rosalind—you could *hold things*. You could sit at a table. You could—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and pushed through. "Do you know what I would give for one hour of you with arms? One hour. To be hugged by my brother instead of hugged by *humidity*?"

"Yes," I said. "And I would give anything for it too. That's why I have to say no."

The silence stretched. Outside, real wind moved through the valley, ordinary wind, and I envied it its simplicity.

"Tell me why," she said.

"Because I've been owned my whole life, Wren. By the crown, when my blood was useful. By the court, when my soul was collateral. By my own guilt, for twelve years, which is the worst owner of all—it never sleeps and never pays." The candleflame bent toward me as I spoke; I couldn't help it; feeling moves weather. "If I take the name back, I'm theirs again. Not in chains—worse. In *gratitude*. Every kindness they ever show me becomes a debt I'm paying forever. A named thing can be bargained with. A named thing can be threatened. You taught me that yourself, in the vaults—one clause, one line of fine print, and I nearly fed their war on the gods. Names are handles, Wren. Handles are for pulling."

"And weather has no handle?" she asked softly.

"Weather has no *ledger*. That's different." I gathered myself closer to her, the fog thickening, taking the chill off the room the way I'd learned to, the way she'd shown me without ever saying it—she'd leave the window open, and I'd learn what the open window was for. "Listen. Today I rained on Marta's fields because Marta's fields needed rain. Nobody commanded it. Nobody paid for it. Nobody will thank me, and if they did, I wouldn't owe them back. Tomorrow I might snow, just to see the children's faces. That's not exile anymore. That's not a prison. That's—" I searched for it, the word, and found it where I least expected: in the memory of the forge. "That's craft. A smith doesn't resent the iron for not being finished. I'm not unfinished, Wren. I'm just not *made*."

She was crying a little, quietly, the way she'd learned to cry in the vaults where sound carried. "You'll never hold anything again."

"I hold the whole valley down when the winds come off the Steppe. I hold the frost off the orchards in late spring. Last week I held a roof on during the gale—did Sera tell you? The Harrow place, the old beam cracked, and I leaned on it until morning." A rumble moved through me, almost a laugh. "It's not hands. But it's not nothing."

"It's not nothing," she agreed. Then, fierce, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand: "But you tell those gods, you tell Corvass with his smug amber eye, that if they ever try to *take* you—if being unnamed stops being your choice and starts being their arrangement—I don't care what they are. I spent twelve years learning where they keep their ledgers. I'll burn every one."

The thunder that rolled through the valley that night was, the elders later agreed, unusually warm.

* * *

I gave my answer at dawn, on the temple hill, where the new order's foundations were going in—Sera's doing, stone and timber rising over the bones of the old moon-cult chapel, though she hadn't named it yet and bristled whenever anyone tried.

All three of them were there, which I suspected was not coincidence. Corvass on the fence rail. Wren wrapped in two shawls, arms crossed, ready to fight heaven if required. Sera Vane standing with her hands loose at her sides in the way she stood when she expected trouble and intended to be polite about it.

The presence of the gods came with the sunrise—not bodies, not voices, but a pressure like the moment before lightning, a green taste in the air, the sense of enormous attention turned politely toward one small valley.

*I am here,* I said, in the rolling voice, and every blade of grass on the hill stood up straight. *I have considered the gift.*

"We hoped," said Corvass, for the others, "you would consider accepting it."

*I have.* I let the wind move through the scaffolding, gentle, testing nothing. *Here is my answer. Keep the name.*

Wren's breath went out slow. Sera's eyebrows rose a fraction—an earthquake, on her face.

*A name is a door,* I said, *and every door I ever had was kicked open by someone with a claim on me. The crown claimed my blood. The court claimed my debt. My guilt claimed my years. I am done being claimed. If you want to honor what I gave you, honor it by not giving anything back.*

The pressure in the air shifted—surprise, among beings who predated surprise.

"But a body," Corvass pressed, and there was real care under it now, real regret. "Hands. A life. You understand what you're refusing."

*I understand exactly what I'm refusing.* I let myself rise, a little whirlwind of leaf and mist, so I could be—at least in metaphor—at eye level with them. *I spent twelve years being a man the world had no place for. Now I am the weather, and the world cannot help but have a place for me. It complains about me, mostly. It also plants according to me. It leaves its windows open.*

"That last one is Wren's fault," Sera said dryly.

*Everything good here is Wren's fault.* The thunder laughed, low and warm. *Hear my terms, then, since you came expecting a bargain and I intend to disappoint you. I will not be your vessel. I will not be your instrument, your example, or your mercy-with-a-purpose. I belong to no court, no crown, no ledger, and no god—including, Corvass, especially, you. What I will be is this: the valley's weather. Its guardian, if it wants one—which the elders may confirm at their leisure, and may rescind at their leisure too, because guardianship that can't be refused is just occupation with better manners.*

Silence on the hill. The sunrise came up red through my drifting edges.

*And one more thing,* I said. *If any of you—any power, any court, any clever thing with a contract—comes for this valley, for these people, for my sister, they will find out what a storm is when it has nothing left to lose and nowhere it would rather be. That is not a threat. Threats are for negotiations. This is a forecast.*

The pressure broke like a held breath released. And then—gods are not complicated, whatever the theologians say—I felt it roll over me from all directions at once: something that in a person would have been laughter, and in a god was the sudden warming of the whole sky.

*"Guardian, not prisoner,"* Corvass repeated, and shook his feathers flat. "Well. They'll hate it. They'll also repeat it for a thousand years. That's how myths start, you know—not with agreements but with refusals good enough to sing about." He cocked the amber eye at me. "You're certain? There's no revisiting this. Names, once declined—"

*Corvass. I'm weather. Everything I do, I do again tomorrow.*

He laughed, a croaking bark, and took wing, and the great attention withdrew with him like a tide going out, leaving the hill ordinary and golden and mine.

Sera watched the crows shrink over the ridge, then turned to where I hung in the brightening air. "For the record," she said, "the elders will want terms. Boundaries. Probably a schedule. They're already arguing about whether you're allowed to hail in harvest season."

*Let them argue. I'll listen while it rains.*

"And the order—" She glanced at the foundations, and something moved through her face, quick and unresolved, a weight she'd been carrying since the Steppe. "Someone has to keep the covenant on the human side. Write it down. Hold the valley to its half, and the gods to theirs. Someone with a name, and a vow, and—" she exhaled, "—a history of breaking the wrong oaths for the right reasons. I think I know where to find one."

Above us, Wren—who had been silent through all of it, watching her brother refuse heaven with her chin up and her eyes shining—threw back her head and shouted at the sky, wordless, triumphant, twelve years of vault-darkness burned off her in a single sound.

I answered the only way I could.

It began, gently, to rain—and the rain, everyone in the valley agreed afterward, sounded exactly like applause.
