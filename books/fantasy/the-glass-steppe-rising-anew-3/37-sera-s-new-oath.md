## 37. Sera's New Oath

The forge was still warm when I came to it.

Not from fire—there had been no fire in that hearth for a year now, since Rosalind stopped being a man and started being weather—but warm anyway. The rain came down through the broken roof in thin silver threads and struck the anvil, and where each drop landed there was a faint bloom of heat, like a coal remembered. The valley said the forge had become a shrine. The valley said a lot of things. What I knew was this: when I pressed my palm to the anvil's face, something on the other side of the sky pressed back.

"Sera," Wren called from the doorway. She had learned to move quietly for someone who'd spent twelve years bodiless; she still startled easily at her own shadow, her own hunger, the astonishing fact of her own hands. "They're waiting."

"Let them wait."

"They've been waiting two hours."

"Then they're well practiced." I pulled my cloak off its peg—the grey one, moon-pale at the hem, the last garment I owned that bore the sigil of the Order of the Silver Veil. I had been meaning to burn it. I kept not burning it.

Wren watched me buckle it on with the expression she'd developed lately, the one that meant she saw more than she said. Her brother's sister to the bone. "You could just tell them no," she offered. "The elders. You've earned telling people no."

"I'm not going to tell them no." I checked the blade at my hip out of twelve years of habit, though Greyhollow hadn't seen violence since before the exile, maybe ever. "I'm going to tell them something worse. I'm going to tell them yes—on my terms."

She grinned. It was strange and wonderful how fast she'd gotten her grin back, as if joy had been folded up inside her soul all those years in the vaults beneath the mountain, creased but unbroken, waiting only for a body to unfold into. "Rosalind would like that," she said.

The rain on the anvil quickened, just slightly. A drum-tap. An opinion.

"He already does," I said, and went out into the wet.

* * *

The moot-hall of Greyhollow smelled of tallow and old timber and the particular sourness of frightened respectability. The elders sat along the high bench: Maren Ashby with her chain of office gone green at the links, old Tobbin who'd never forgiven magic for existing, the widow Corrigan whose prayers had started all of this two years ago in her smokehouse, though she didn't know it and I wasn't the one to tell her. Behind them, the whole valley crowded the benches—farmers and smiths and the god-touched children Wren had begun gathering like stray cats, sitting in the front row because children always do.

Maren rose. "Sera Vane. You asked for this council."

"I did."

"The covenant is three weeks old. The gods have given us their terms. The storm-guardian watches our borders. And yet the valley has no voice in any of it except yours." She said it without accusation, which was somehow heavier. "You speak for the gods here. You speak for the guardian. You spoke for the girl when she came home. Who speaks for you?"

"That," I said, "is exactly what I've come to fix."

I walked the length of the hall while I talked, because my teachers in the Silver Veil had taught me that a speech delivered standing still is a lecture, and a speech delivered in motion is a march. Old habits. Dead order. Time, I decided, to bury them properly.

"You know what I was," I said. "Paladin of the moon-cult, Order of the Silver Veil, sworn to the Crown's faith and the Crown's sword. You know why I was cast out. I refused to burn a village of god-touched children, and my brothers and sisters in silver did not refuse, and so the order died—not in battle, but in obedience. That is what duty looks like when it serves a throne. It looks like ash on the wind and everyone agreeing it was necessary."

Tobbin shifted. The children did not shift. Children know a true thing when they hear one.

"For twelve years I wandered with a broken oath and a name I couldn't use, hunting rumors of a storm-witch because hunting was the only prayer I had left. And then I came to this valley, and I found the storm-witch shoeing horses and keeping his head down, and I found something else too. I found a place where the quiet wasn't emptiness. Where people survived without magic by leaning on each other instead of on power. I thought I'd come here to make an arrest." I let that land. Half the hall still flinched at the memory. "Instead I made a choice. First real one of my life. Everything since has grown from it."

"So you say the gods chose well," Tobbin said flatly. "A storm for a shepherd."

"No. I say the gods chose nothing. That's the secret nobody wants spoken aloud." I stopped at the foot of the high bench. "Corvass told me once—gods are made of surrendered names. They don't command. They *answer*. Every miracle since the waking began has been an answer to something a person already wanted, already prayed, already grieved for. The widow Corrigan prayed over a dying friend and the frost bloomed. Rosalind gave up his name to save his sister and became the rain. The gods are not kings. They're *listeners*. And I have spent my whole life serving kings, and I am done."

"Then what," Maren said quietly, "are you proposing?"

"An order." The word came out steadier than I felt. "New. Small. Sworn to no crown, no court, no throne under any mountain. Sworn instead to the covenant itself—to hearing prayers, and answering them honestly, and standing between this valley and anything that would bargain away its souls again. Not paladins. We'll call ourselves keepers. Covenant-keepers. We will hold the terms the gods offered, and we will hold the valley to them, and we will hold *ourselves* to both—and when crown or court or god asks us to break faith with the people, we refuse, and we accept the price of refusing, because I have paid that price and I can tell you it is survivable and shame is not."

Silence. Rain on the roof, faint and rhythmic, like fingers drumming approval—or impatience. With Rosalind it was hard to tell. It had always been hard to tell.

"And who joins this order?" Tobbin asked. "You? Alone in a grey cloak?"

"No," said a voice from the front row.

It was one of the children—the Ashford boy, Tam, eleven years old, born with frost in his breath and a mother who'd hidden him in a root cellar every solstice until the gods woke. He stood up among the other children as if he'd been waiting his whole short life for permission.

"I'll join," he said. "If you teach me to fight like you fought the hunters on the Steppe. My ma says you held the line alone."

"I didn't win that fight," I said. "I lost slowly enough for someone else to win it."

"That counts," Tam said, with the terrible logic of children. "My da says holding is winning slow."

Behind him, others stood. Not many. Three. Then five. A farmwoman named Ilsabet with burn-scars up her arms and lightning caught somewhere behind her eyes. A drover. The widow Corrigan herself, rising on legs that shook, saying only, "Someone answered me when I prayed. Someone should be there when others pray."

I looked at Maren Ashby. "Seven," I said. "We begin with seven."

Maren studied me for a long moment. Then she unpinned the green-linked chain of office from her own shoulders—no, I realized, not her office; it was older than her office, some ancestral thing—and set it on the bench between us. "Greyhollow doesn't crown anyone," she said. "But it recognizes. This council recognizes the Order of the Covenant, seated at the forge, under the guardianship of the storm. On one condition."

"Name it."

"That your first vow is sworn publicly, today, where we can all hear what we're getting."

I almost smiled. Almost. "Elder," I said, "I was hoping you'd ask."

* * *

We swore the oaths at dusk, at the forge, because I insisted the order have no temple. Temples accrue thrones the way wounds accrue infection. The forge would do: a broken roof, a warm anvil, rain coming down through the gap in threads of silver, and above it all—if you knew how to listen, and I was teaching myself—a presence like pressure before a storm, vast and patient and fond.

Tam stood first. Then Ilsabet, the drover Harl, the widow Corrigan, and the other two—a shy stonemason's daughter and a boy who could make candles gutter just by sulking near them. Seven candles on the anvil's edge, lit from a single flame, because symbolism matters even when you're renouncing hierarchy.

I had written the oath myself, three drafts, burned two. The surviving one was short. Short vows get kept; long ones get recited.

"Lift your right hands," I said, "and repeat after me.

"*I swear by my own name, which is mine to give—*

"*to hear the small prayers and the great ones alike;*

"*to answer honestly, or to say plainly that I cannot;*

"*to serve no crown, no court, no throne beneath any mountain;*

"*and when faith and love come into conflict,*

"*to choose love,*

"*and pay whatever the choosing costs.*

"*This I swear, witnessed by the rain, the moon, and the crows.*"

They repeated it. Their voices wobbled and overlapped and came together anyway, and above us the rain thickened against the broken roof, drumming out a rhythm I had learned to read on a corpse-road full of soul-wraiths: *yes. yes. yes.*

And then, because I had saved this part, I took the grey cloak from my shoulders—the last garment bearing the sigil of the Silver Veil—and laid it across the anvil, and drew my knife, and cut the sigil free, and fed it to the cold hearth.

The hearth was not cold.

Flame leapt up around the sigil, blue-white and eager, and the rain through the roof bent sideways, all of it at once, curving around the seven of us in a slow revolving wall so that not one drop touched our candles. In the heart of the water-wall something shaped itself briefly—a suggestion of shoulders, a suggestion of a smile, a pressure of attention like a hand on the back of the neck, gentle as a blessing.

*You cut away everything,* the rain seemed to say, in the way it says things, which is to say in no words at all, only certainty. *Look what's left.*

"What's left," I said aloud, to the storm, to the order, to myself, "is the work."

The rain laughed. I'd have testified to that before any council: storms laugh. It sounds like hail on a tin roof and feels like forgiveness.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, and gave up trying around the second bell, and walked out onto the ridge above the valley because sleepless nights belong to the ridge, that's the arrangement we have.

The moon was three days past new, a nail-paring of light, and beneath it the Glass Steppe stretched away eastward, mirror-flat, catching the starlight like a second sky lying down. Somewhere out there was the mountain that no longer had a court beneath it—just empty halls and scattered ledger-pages, and souls long since flown home like embers on the wind. Somewhere out there was a border town where a debt collector had once offered me my disgrace back, and I had said no, and never told Rosalind until a hunt nearly killed us both.

I touched the scar on my palm where the moon-blessing used to pool. It was quiet now. Spent on the Steppe, buying seconds. Whatever divinity I'd carried, I'd carried it as far as it would go and poured it out, and what remained was just Sera Vane, mortal, middle-aged, tired in the bones—and freer than I had ever been in armor.

"Counsel," said a voice above me.

I didn't flinch. Twelve years of discipline, plus a year of knowing better. The crow dropped out of the dark and landed on the fence post beside me, folding black wings, tilting its head so its single human eye caught the moonlight. The other eye stayed a crow's eye, black and bead-bright, watching the Steppe the way birds watch things.

"You're late," I said. "The oath-taking was hours ago."

"I attended," Corvass said. His voice came from everywhere crows are, which is nowhere in particular. "I listened. I approve of the clause about love. It will cause you enormous trouble."

"It's caused me enormous trouble already."

"Yes. That's how you know it's working." The crow ruffled its feathers against the wind coming down off the ridge. For a while neither of us spoke. Crows are good at silence. Gods made of surrendered names are better. "Do you understand what you founded tonight?"

"An order of covenant-keepers. Seven members and a forge."

"Do you understand what it *is*?" His human eye fixed on me, patient, ancient, amused. "The old orders were swords the thrones held. Your new one is a hinge. Do you know what happens in a world where the gods wake and the courts fall and every village must learn to pray without a king to license the praying? Someone must stand in the doorway between the small human want and the enormous listening answer, and check the arithmetic. Check that no prayer buys a soul. Check that no god's generosity becomes another kind of tax. That is the work you swore to. You've made yourself the door."

"I noticed," I said dryly. "I've been a door before. Paladins are doors. We just used to open for the wrong side."

The crow made a sound that might have been laughter, if laughter were gravel. Then it went still, that particular stillness I'd learned to dread, the stillness of a god consulting distances I couldn't see.

"There is a petition," Corvass said at last. "Traveling toward your valley. Slowly. On foot."

"From whom?"

"A woman. Older. Moon-marked, though her mark is faded almost to nothing—an initiate of the old Silver Veil, from the western houses. She carries a question she has carried for thirty years." The crow turned its human eye fully on me. "She heard that the Silver Veil died, and that something new stands in its place, and she has come to ask whether the new thing burns god-touched children."

The wind moved along the ridge. Far below, in the valley, the forge-light glimmered through rain, and I thought of Tam asleep in the loft above it, frost-breath and stubborn, and of the oath we'd sworn with our own names, which are ours to give.

"What do I tell her?" I asked.

"That," said Corvass, spreading his wings, "is the first test of your new order. Choose well, Keeper Vane. She arrives within the week—and she is not the only one asking. Word travels faster than crows, these days. Everyone is wondering what replaces the things that fell." He lifted off into the dark, and his voice came back once, thin and pleased, like a knife being wrapped in silk: "Everyone is watching what you build next."

I stood a long time on the ridge after he was gone, the nail-paring moon overhead, the Steppe gleaming below like a road made of sky.

Within the week, then. A woman with an old wound and a true question, and after her—who knew? The fallen leave gaps, and gaps draw pilgrims, and pilgrims draw everything else.

I turned toward the valley, toward the forge-light and the sleeping children and the rain that loved us, and I started walking down.

Whatever came up the road, the Covenant would meet it standing—with open hands, and honest answers, and love chosen first, whatever it cost.
