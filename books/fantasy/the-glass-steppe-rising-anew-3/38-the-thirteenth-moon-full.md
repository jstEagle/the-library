## 38. The Thirteenth Moon, Full

The lanterns went up at dusk, all along the valley road, and Wren stood in the middle of the forge square and watched Greyhollow hang its light like a promise.

Twelve years of her life had been spent in the dark beneath the mountain, in the vaulted cold of the Ledger Hall, where nothing glowed unless some clerk-soul wanted it to. She had learned to count light there. To ration it. A candle's worth was an hour of hope; a window's worth, a day. So when the whole valley lit itself at once—every doorway, every sill, every child carrying a paper lamp on a stick down the lane toward the square—the sheer abundance of it made her throat close.

*This is yours now,* she told herself. *You're allowed.*

She still wasn't sure she believed it.

"You'll want to eat something before the dancing starts," said Marta Hale, who had appointed herself Wren's keeper within a week of the homecoming and had never been asked to step down. Marta pressed a honey-bun into Wren's hands, warm from the oven, sticky enough to require both. "You get that look. The one where you're counting things."

"I'm not counting."

"You're always counting. Twelve years under that mountain, you learned to count." Marta said it plainly, without pity, which was why Wren could bear her. "Tonight you don't have to. That's the whole point of the night."

Wren bit into the bun to keep from saying anything foolish. Around them, the square filled. Farmers came down from the terraces with their families; the god-touched children arrived in a shrieking pack, led by old Tam Whitlock—no relation, though he claimed one loudly and often—who had taken charge of them as if he'd been born for it. Sera Vane stood near the well in her new grey-and-white vestments, the refounded order's colors, accepting blessings and returning them, looking faintly embarrassed by the whole enterprise and hiding it behind perfect posture.

And overhead, in the sky above the eastern ridge, the clouds were gathering.

They gathered the way they always did now, on this night: slowly, deliberately, like a guest who knew he was expected and refused to hurry. The valley had learned his schedule. The bakers planned around it. The children bet on it.

"Storm's late," said a boy near the well.

"Storm's never late," said his sister. "Storm's dramatic. There's a difference."

"He's not *a storm,*" Wren said, before she could stop herself, and then laughed at herself, because she'd been having that argument for three years now and would be having it forever. "He's weather. Singular. And he has excellent timing."

As if he'd heard—as if he always heard—the first drop fell.

It landed on the back of Wren's hand, cool and precise, right over the old scar-line where the ledger had once held her. Then another, on her shoulder. Then the sky opened, soft and warm and utterly unlike any natural rain, falling only inside the valley's rim while the peaks beyond stayed dry as bone.

The crowd cheered. Not politely. They cheered the way people cheer a friend coming up the road after a long absence, and somewhere inside the rain—inside the vast slow turning of pressure and vapor and lightning held gently leashed—Wren felt him feel it. She'd learned to sense that feeling the way you learn someone's footsteps on a stair. A brightening. A gathering-in. Her brother, twelve years a fugitive and one terrible bargain a nameless thing of storm, hearing himself cheered by the people who had once branded him traitor at the boundary stones.

*Hello,* she thought at him, the way she did.

The rain tapped twice against the forge roof across the square. *Tap-tap.* Their oldest signal. *I'm here. Are you?*

"I'm here," she said aloud, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and let Marta pretend not to notice.

* * *

The Night of Returned Souls had started, three years ago, as an act of defiance disguised as a festival.

The elders had not wanted it. Old Aldous Marsh had stood in the moot-hall and said, in his dry way, that celebrating the return of hoarded souls was "inviting the Undercourt to take notice," and half the hall had murmured agreement, because Greyhollow had survived twelve years of quiet by believing quiet was safety. It had been Sera who turned them—not with argument but with arithmetic. She'd read out the names from the court's stolen ledgers, the ones Wren had memorized in the vaults: forty-one souls from the valley and its surrounding farms, taken across two centuries in bargains the desperate had struck when no other help existed. Forty-one. And of those, thirty-three had living kin still in Greyhollow.

"Every one of those souls came home when the court fell," Sera had said. "You can spend tonight deciding what you owe them, or you can spend it welcoming them. But they're your neighbors' daughters and fathers, and they are standing in this square right now, wondering which kind of town they came back to."

The vote wasn't even close.

So the festival had grown its rituals quickly, the way young things grow. At moonrise, each family that had lost someone to the courts set a second lantern at their door, unlit, and walked to the square, and the returned soul—or the family of a returned soul, or anyone at all who wished—would light it from the great bonfire, and speak the name aloud, and the name would go up with the smoke. Names as currency, the bridge-warden had demanded once. Names as toll. Tonight names were free. Tonight they were gifts.

Wren carried four lanterns herself.

She always did. Three for the souls she'd known in the vaults whose families were gone or scattered—a miller's boy named Oskar who had spent sixty years as collateral and cried for a week straight when he woke in a body that had aged without him; a midwife called Ivetta; a child too young to have a story, whom the others simply called Little Lantern because none of them ever learned her name. And one for Wren herself, because in the first year Sera had handed it to her and said, "You were forfeit too. Light your own. No one else is going to do it right."

She lit them one at a time from the bonfire, and spoke each name into the smoke, and the crowd echoed each one back the way a congregation does—and the last name she gave was her own, and her voice cracked on the *W*, and three hundred people said it back to her anyway, gentle as rain.

That was the part that undid her every year. Not the fire. The echo.

Under the mountain, her name had been a line item. *Forfeit: one soul, female, against the exile-price of Rosalind Whitlock.* Here, it was a sound people made with love in their mouths, and every year it taught her again the difference between being recorded and being known.

The rain slowed while the lanterns burned, holding itself back out of courtesy, a hush of silver mist instead of proper drops. He always did that during the naming. Even storms can be polite, when they've spent years learning how to be people.

* * *

Then the drums started, and the mist thickened just so, and the dancing began.

It began the way everything in Greyhollow began: imperfectly, enthusiastically, with Tam Whitlock calling steps nobody followed and the god-touched children inventing better ones. The square was packed earth and lamplight and the bonfire throwing sparks at the low clouds, and Wren danced because she had promised herself she would, the first year, and had kept the promise every year since. Dancing was a body-thing. She'd gone twelve years without a body, and she had decided—with the particular ferocity of someone who has been a line item—that she would use hers extravagantly, wastefully, gloriously, until the day she died.

She spun. She stomped. She taught a farmer's toddler to clap off-beat. She danced with Sera, who danced like a paladin—precise, rhythmic, secretly delighted—and with Marta, who danced like a landslide, and with Oskar the miller's boy, who wept and grinned at the same time because he'd missed sixty years of festivals and intended to make up ground.

And through it all, the rain kept time.

That was the miracle nobody in Greyhollow remarked on anymore, because miracles stop being miraculous when they become habits: the rain fell in rhythm. Not on the dancers—around them, between them, threading the lanes of the crowd like a partner who knew every figure. When the drums quickened, the rain quickened. When the fiddle slid into the old slow tune the grandmothers insisted on, the rain went soft and long, drumming the rooftops in waltz-time. Children ran through it shrieking and came up dry-eyed and laughing, and their parents let them, because everyone knew whose rain it was, and everyone trusted it more than they trusted most weather and most people.

Wren danced to the edge of the square, where the eaves of the old forge threw a shadow, and stopped, and tilted her face up.

The rain gathered there, over her, and nowhere else. A column of falling water shaped like attention.

"Dance with me," she said.

He couldn't, exactly. She knew that. He was weather now—vast, diffuse, bound to the valley's skies the way roots are bound to soil. But he could *approximate*. He could pull a gust into a swirl and spin fallen leaves in a circle; he could make the rain leap and scatter in bursts that looked, if you squinted, if you loved someone enough, like feet moving. And he did it now, awkwardly, hugely, a storm trying to waltz, and Wren laughed—really laughed, head thrown back, the laugh of a woman with a body and a home and a brother—and the thunder answered.

Not distant thunder. Close thunder, rolling once, twice, in a pattern she'd taught him three years ago on a gray afternoon when grief had sat heavy on both of them:

Two beats. Pause. Three beats. The rhythm of the lullaby their mother had sung in the kitchen in another life, in a house that no longer existed, in a language of taps and hums they'd invented between siblings because their mother sang badly and they'd needed a joke.

*Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap.*

The nearest dancers stopped. Turned. And then old Tam Whitlock, seventy years old and deaf in one ear, began humming the lullaby's tune, because everyone in Greyhollow knew it now—it had spread the way songs spread, from Wren's humming to the children to everyone—and the fiddler picked it up, and the drums softened to fit it, and the whole square was suddenly dancing to a song that had begun as a private joke between a dead woman's two surviving children.

The rain fell harder, joyful now, and the lightning came green-gold and harmless behind the clouds, heat-lightning painting the ridge in flashes like applause, and Wren danced in the center of it with her arms open and her face wet and didn't care whether it was rain or tears, because it was both, it was always both, and that was the whole secret of joy: it's grief that got to come home.

* * *

Late—past midnight, when the fires burned low and the children slept in piles under borrowed coats—Wren climbed the path to the forge alone.

She always ended the night here. It wasn't the forge he'd owned; that building was gone, or rather it was everywhere, dissolved into cloud and valley air. This forge belonged to a farrier named Dunmore who had bought the plot cheap after the exile and never quite shaken the feeling that the property came haunted. But the old anvil remained—heavy, useless, too costly to move—and the rain always found it.

She sat on the mounting block beside it and let the storm settle around her. Closer now. Thicker. The air took on that charged weight she'd learned meant he was gathering himself small, condensing attention into the space where she sat, the way a man pulls up a chair.

"Good festival?" she asked.

Rain drummed the anvil: *tap-tap. tap-tap-tap.* Yes. Very good.

"The children were showing off for you. I saw the little Vasquez girl making lightning-faces at the sky."

A rumble that was almost, almost a chuckle. Thunder had a register now, a range she'd mapped like a language—low rumbles for contentment, sharp cracks for alarm, that particular double-crack he used for laughter, which had frightened half the valley the first time he'd done it during a harvest dance and sent three farmers diving under a cart.

She talked. She always talked; he mostly listened, and answered in taps and rolls, and sometimes—in wind—if the message mattered enough, in actual words, syllables shaped from gusts, expensive for him and reserved for the important things. *Yes. Stay. Careful. Home.* His vocabulary was small and it was the truest vocabulary she'd ever heard.

"Corvass came by today," she said. "While you were doing your dramatic entrance over the east ridge. He landed on my windowsill with that smug one-eye look and wouldn't say what he wanted. Just left this."

She reached into her coat and drew out a feather.

It wasn't a crow feather. Crows don't have feathers like this—grey shot with silver, edged faintly in blue-white, the exact color of heat-lightning frozen mid-flash. It weighed nothing and slightly less than nothing, and when she turned it in the moonlight, the barbs moved like rain running down glass.

"It's yours," she said. "Or it's about you. Or—" she turned it again, and the unease she'd been carrying all evening finally put itself into words. "Ros. When gods give you a piece of themselves, it's not a gift. You know that better than anybody. It's an invitation. Or a summons. He keeps a feather of you in his nest, he told me once, and I thought that was sweet, and tonight I'm thinking maybe I should have thought harder about it."

The rain had gone very quiet. Not absent—present, attentive, the held-breath quiet of a storm reading the sky ahead of itself.

Above the eastern ridge, past the valley's rim, beyond the boundary stones where the magicless quiet ended, the night sky was doing something it should not do.

The stars were going out. Not all of them. One thin line of them, arcing northward over the Steppe, winking out in sequence, left to right, deliberate as a clerk drawing a rule beneath an entry in a ledger.

And out of the dark where they'd been, wings. Hundreds of wings. A flight of crows rising against the last starlight, streaming toward Greyhollow in a long black river, silent as spilled ink, led by a single bird with one human eye that caught the bonfire's glow from miles away and held it like a lamp.

Wren stood up slowly, the feather burning cold in her palm, and the storm gathered around her like a brother putting an arm around his sister's shoulders.

"Told me what he wanted," she whispered. "He wanted to see if I'd notice."
