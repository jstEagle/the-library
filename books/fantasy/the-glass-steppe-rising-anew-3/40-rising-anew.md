## 40. Rising Anew

The valley had learned to listen.

That was the first thing travelers noticed, the ones who still came down the mountain road in the years after—how quiet Greyhollow was, and how the quiet was not empty. A listening quiet. The kind of quiet a room makes when it is waiting for someone you love to come home.

Maren Ashfoot felt it most mornings as she walked her goats up the terraced path above the village. She was seventy-one now and had lived in Greyhollow all her life, through the lean years and the frightened ones, through the twelve silent years when the sky sat on the valley like a lid, and through the night the lid came off.

She remembered that night better than she pretended to.

She remembered the elders' faces when the storm came down the mountain—not raging, not tearing, but *arriving*, the way a son arrives at his mother's door after too long away. She remembered old Hesper Tull, who had voted for the exile, standing in the square with rain running down her face, saying over and over, "It's him. It's the Whitlock boy. He's forgiven us," until her daughter led her inside. Maren had never been sure whether Hesper meant the storm had forgiven them or that they ought to forgive the storm, and by then it no longer mattered. Both were true. Both had always been true.

Now the goats knew the rhythm of the valley's weather the way they knew grass. When the rain came gentle across the high pasture, tapping the stones in threes—*tap-tap-tap, pause, tap-tap-tap*—they grazed easy. When it drummed quick and bright along the fence line, they moved toward the low meadow without being told. Nobody in Greyhollow needed a forecast anymore. They just listened, and the valley told them what it meant to do.

Maren paused at the top of the terrace, as she did every morning, and looked at the forge below.

The farrier's forge still stood at the edge of the village square, though no fire had burned in it for six years now. Nobody would tear it down. Nobody would live in it. The elders had tried once to make it a shrine, and the rain had come down so hard on the roof for three days straight—playful, insistent, almost laughing—that they'd taken the candles back out and left it be. It was not a shrine. It was a house someone might come home to.

Some evenings, when the light went long and gold across the Steppe beyond the mountains, the rain came to the forge. It tapped on the iron chimney cap. It traced the window frames. It gathered in the eaves and dripped, slow and deliberate, onto the anvil stone outside—*drip, drip, drip*—like knuckles on a door.

Knock knock, said the rain. I'm home. Are you home?

And the village, without being asked, lit the lanterns in its windows early on those nights, so the forge would not sit dark.

* * *

Wren taught the children in the old smokehouse, which seemed right to everyone even if nobody could have explained why.

There were eleven of them this season, god-touched in one way or another—the first generation born after the waking, when the old gods came back into the world like water into cracked ground. A boy named Tam who could make bread rise with a hummed note. Twin girls who spoke to the crows, badly, the way children speak any second language. A solemn little girl called Nettle whose shadow moved a half-second ahead of her, and who had learned, with Wren's patient help, to stop being frightened of it.

"Again," Wren said, kneeling in the sawdust. "From the beginning."

Eleven children stood in a circle around a clay bowl of water. This was the hardest lesson, harder than prayers, harder than names: the lesson of *asking*. The gods woke because prayers needed answering, Corvass said, but there was asking and there was demanding, and only one of them opened doors.

"Please," they said together, raggedly, the way children say anything. "Please."

Nothing happened. The water sat flat.

Nettle frowned at it. Then, instead of repeating the word, she leaned close to the bowl and whispered something none of the others caught—and the water shivered, rose, and shaped itself into a small perfect crown that hovered a finger's width above the surface before collapsing back.

The other children gasped. Wren laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh of a woman who had spent twelve years as a soul in a vault and had decided somewhere along the way that she would never again ration out her joy.

"Yes," she said, ruffling Nettle's hair. "That. That's how it works. Not *please, give me*. Just—tell it what you love. The gods are made of gifts. Speak gift-language."

"Is that how your brother does it?" asked Tam. The children asked about the storm-guardian constantly, the way children ask about anything enormous: casually, relentlessly, with total sincerity.

Wren considered the question the way she considered everything now—with the unhurried attention of someone who had missed twelve years and refused to miss another hour.

"My brother doesn't *do* it," she said. "My brother *is* it. There's a difference." She picked up the bowl and carried it to the window. "When I want to talk to him, I don't pray. I just go outside and get rained on and complain about my hair. He loves that. Drives him crazy. Well—" she smiled, "he doesn't have a self to drive crazy anymore. But you know what I mean."

They didn't, quite. But they laughed anyway, because Wren's laughter was contagious the way weather was.

After lessons she walked the boundary of the valley, as she did every day, out past the last field to the place where Greyhollow ended and magic began. Her brother's storm could not cross that line—it was bound to the valley now, woven into it, root and rain—and so every day the weather came to the border to meet her, the way a dog comes to the gate. Wind moving against wind. A pressure like a hand resting on her shoulder.

*I'm here,* said the air.

*I know,* said Wren. *I know.*

Twelve years in the Undercourt's vaults had taught her the shape of loss so thoroughly that she had sworn, walking out of that falling court with embers rising around her like reversed snowfall, never to leave a day ungreeted again. So she greeted the wind. Every day. Even in winter. Especially in winter.

Some days she brought gossip. Some days she sang the lullaby their mother used to sing, the one about the moon who lost her name to the sea, and the thunder would roll in soft under her voice like a second singer finding the harmony. Some days she brought complaints—about the twins feeding the crows her entire stores of dried fish, about Sera's new acolytes drilling prayer-forms at dawn outside her window—and the rain would come down extra hard that evening on Sera's roof specifically, and Wren would pretend not to notice, and Sera would pretend not to notice her pretending, and both of them would be smiling.

He had given up his name for her. Everyone knew the story; Corvass had made sure of that, telling it to every crow from here to the coast until it wore grooves in the world like a river wears stone. But Wren knew the part the crows didn't tell: he hadn't given it up *for* her, exactly. He'd given it up *as* her—as the person she had become in the vault, the one who learned the court's secrets, who slipped her own soul into his scars, who had taught him in dreams the difference between a bargain and a gift. He had become the storm because becoming the storm was the first thing he had ever chosen freely, and choosing it had felt, he told her once in a dream of warm rain, like finally exhaling after twelve held breaths.

Belonging, he'd learned, wasn't a place that let you in. It was a thing you became.

* * *

The Order of the Covenant kept its hall where the moon-cult's Saltmere remnants had once hidden—a modest stone building beside the meetinghouse, its door always unlocked, its hearth always lit. Over the door hung a carved sign: *Neither Crown Nor Court.* Beneath it, smaller, in Sera Vane's own hand: *Ask Honestly.*

Sera was fifty-four and grey at the temples and still moved like a soldier, though the order she commanded bore no swords. Twenty-three members now. They came from everywhere—farmers, midwives, a former Undercourt clerk who had fled the falling bureaucracy with three ledgers clutched to his chest and wept for two days straight before anyone could get soup into him. They studied bargains. That was the heart of the training: learning to read contracts, spoken and written and cosmic, learning to find the clause that damns, learning the difference between a debt freely owed and a debt extracted. The world was full of courts still—lesser ones, hungrier ones, things that woke alongside the old gods and wanted worship on their terms. Somebody had to teach people to say no beautifully.

"You're drilling them too hard," Wren said, leaning in the doorway of the training hall one autumn afternoon while Sera put a row of novices through recitation drills.

"I'm drilling them exactly hard enough," Sera said. "The last time we drilled them softly, Brother Aldous signed away his Tuesdays for a year to a hedge-spirit. Do you know what a hedge-spirit wants with Tuesdays?"

"What did it want with them?"

"No idea. That's the point." She dismissed the novices and came to stand beside Wren, arms folded, watching the young people scatter toward supper. For a moment neither woman spoke. Theirs had become an old friendship, worn smooth like a river stone, built in the crossing of the Glass Steppe and tempered in the fall of the court beneath the mountain.

"He came to the forge again last night," Wren said.

"I know. My roof leaked."

"That wasn't leakage. That was commentary. You've got a novice, Pell, who's been skipping his covenant-study to court the cooper's daughter, and my brother has *opinions* about neglected duties."

"Your brother has opinions about everything. He once flooded the entire eastern pasture because Elder Hargrave suggested building a drainage ditch without consulting anyone."

"And was he wrong?"

"The ditch was badly sited," Sera admitted. "We rebuilt it. With consultation." She glanced sideways. "You know what I miss? Arguing with him. Proper arguing. He used to be so stubborn. Now he's just—weather. Weather agrees with everyone eventually."

Wren laughed. "You could ask Corvass for a feather."

"No."

"A feather of the storm. One question, truly answered. He keeps them in his nest—"

"I know what he keeps." Sera's voice went quiet, not angry—something older than anger. "I spent my whole life believing duty meant giving things up. My order. My blessing. Nearly my life on the Glass, holding that line while you two ran." She shook her head slowly. "I won't take shortcuts to his voice. If he wants to tell me something, he knows where my roof is."

Above them, unprompted, a single drop of rain fell out of a clear blue sky and struck the top of Sera's head with the precision of long practice.

"There," Wren said. "He heard you."

"That," said Sera, wiping her head with dignity, "was unnecessary."

But she was smiling when she said it, and later that evening she left the shutters of the training hall open, and the rain came in small polite gusts and turned the pages of the covenant ledger for her, gently, like an old friend reading over her shoulder.

* * *

Corvass remembered everything. That was the burden and the glory of being a god made of memory and surrendered names.

He sat that evening in the great pine above the village square, in a nest the size of a wagon bed, lined with shed antler velvet and ribbon and one stolen wedding ring he fully intended to keep. Around him roosted his descendants—hundreds of crows now, the flock grown fat and clever on the offerings of a grateful valley—and each of them carried a piece of his sight out into the world each morning and brought pieces of the world home each dusk. Through them he watched the whole waking land: the Glass Steppe green-edged now, reflections walking peacefully alongside their originals; the ruins of the Undercourt a honeycomb of empty vaults where freed souls had left their warmth behind like banked fires; the distant capital, where a new king had quietly repealed the witch-laws and pretended they had never mattered.

He watched, and he remembered, and in remembering he kept the story alive. That was a god's true work, he had decided—not power, not dominion. Curation.

In the nest, beneath his feet, lay a single feather. It was grey and silver and faintly luminous, and it was not a crow's feather, and it had never molted from any bird. It had simply appeared in the nest one morning, six years ago, coiled among the twigs like a gift—which is exactly what it was. A feather of the storm. A piece of Rosalind Whitlock's choice, given freely to the god who had carried messages between worlds when no other messenger would.

Corvass touched it sometimes with his beak, the way an old man touches a letter from a friend. Through it he could feel the vast slow mind of the storm-guardian—not thoughts, exactly; storms did not think in words—but moods. Seasons. Today's mood was the good one: the deep contentment of rain on plowed fields, of full cisterns, of lanterns lit early in windows.

*You did well,* Corvass told him, as he often did, though the feather never answered and he never expected it to. *You gave the finest gift I ever received. Names are what gods are made of, little storm-caller. Did you know that? Every god is a name somebody loved enough to surrender. And yours—*

Below, a child shrieked with laughter as she ran through the evening drizzle, arms out, mouth open to catch the rain.

Corvass paused. Listened.

The rain was doing it again—the thing it did some evenings, the pattern that had made even the eldest villagers stop and press their hands to their hearts the first time they heard it. Tap-tap-tap on the forge's chimney. Drip-drip-drip on the anvil stone. Knock-knock-knock on the door of the darkened smithy.

Not a haunting. Not a ghost. A greeting. The greeting of a friend who has traveled very far and changed very much, coming home to the place that made him, checking—as he checked every evening, and would check, Corvass suspected, for as long as the valley stood—whether anyone was home.

The lanterns bloomed in the windows, one by one. The forge stayed dark and beloved. And the rain knocked softly, patiently, joyfully at the door, like a friend come home—

—when, high on the mountain pass where no road had gone in six years, a crow landed on a cairn of stones and cocked its head at something new.

Footprints. Human ones, ascending the Hollow King's Road from the far side of the Steppe, fresh in the evening frost, dozens of them, walking toward Greyhollow in careful single file. And at their head, wrapped in travel-stained robes, carrying a staff crowned with a shard of obsidian pried from the gates of a fallen court, came a figure whose face Corvass knew from a thousand memories—and whose lips were moving, shaping, over and over, a single word that drifted up to the pine tree on the wind:

*A bargain. A bargain. I've come to offer a bargain.*

Corvass spread his wings, and every crow in Greyhollow lifted screaming into the darkening sky at once.

The valley, sensing nothing yet, slept warm in its guardian's rain.
