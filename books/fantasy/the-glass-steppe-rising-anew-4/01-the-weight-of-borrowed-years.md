## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The smuggler's road through Thornfell was the kind of road that apologized for existing. It wound along the mountain's flank like a scar someone had tried to hide under moss, narrow enough that Rosalind could touch the rock wall with his left hand and empty air with his right, and it smelled perpetually of pine resin and other people's secrets.

He had been walking it for three days in the company of a wool merchant named Doss Halloway, who talked the way rivers run — constantly, and mostly downhill. Doss talked about wool prices, about his wife's opinions on shutters, about a cousin who had married badly, and about, at length and with a tenderness that made Rosalind's teeth ache, a vault.

Not *a* vault, actually. *His* vault.

"You can tell a man anything on a smuggling road," Doss had confided on the first evening, around a fire built small because fires were taxed now too, or soon would be, if the criers were to be believed. "That's what I like about this road. No clerks. No ledger-men. Just honest criminals."

"Is that what we are?" Rosalind asked, warming his hands. He was very good at asking questions that sounded like agreement. It was, in its way, the whole of his profession.

"Smugglers? Aye." Doss leaned close, breath smelling of cheap plum brandy. "And I'll tell you something I don't tell my wife, because she'd want it spent on shutters." And out it came — the location, the lock, the combination hidden inside a memory so precious Doss kept it polished like a heirloom: the night his father had shown him the vault beneath the old mill at Corrow's Bend, the numbers whispered over a boy's head like a blessing.

Rosalind had not even needed to work for it. That was the trouble with the wealthy, he'd long ago decided. They carried their treasures loose in open pockets of the mind, and they were lonely, and loneliness made them generous to anyone who listened.

The lifting itself took less time than pouring tea.

It was not theft, exactly — or rather, it was theft of a kind no court had ever managed to legislate against, though the crown was certainly trying. Rosalind did not take the memory. Taking left holes, and holes got people noticed, and noticed people got dragged before magistrates who had recently developed an interest in the gifted. No — Rosalind *sipped*. He dipped a finger into the warm surface of another mind and lifted away only the shimmer on top, the way you might skim cream without disturbing the milk. The merchant would keep his vault, his father's voice, his whole treasured evening. Rosalind would simply have a copy. And in a week's time, when a certain unremarkable man in a certain unremarkable coat sold that copy to parties interested in unguarded fortunes, Doss Halloway would never know why the thieves had known where to look.

Everyone won. Well. Almost everyone.

He performed the sipping that third morning while Doss paused to adjust a strap on his mule, standing at the rail with his head bowed and his thoughts conveniently pooled. Rosalind rested two fingers lightly against the back of the merchant's neck — a gesture any traveling companion might make, steadying, friendly — and slipped into the shallow waters.

The vault came up easily. Stone steps behind the mill, a door of black iron, the father's gruff voice reciting numbers. Good. Solid merchandise. He was already composing the note to Old Bram — *vault, Corrow's Bend, iron door, family concern, will fetch forty crowns at least* — when the memory shivered at its edges and something else surfaced beneath it, the way a drowned thing rises when you disturb still water.

A throne.

Rosalind almost let go. Sipping was delicate work; you took what floated near the top and you did not go diving after things that belonged to deeper water, because deep water in a stranger's mind could hold anything. But the image had already caught him — vast, carved from living rock, lit by lamps the color of bruises — and kneeling before it, small and straight-backed in a way he knew the way you know your own heartbeat—

Wren.

His hands went cold on Doss's neck. The memory was secondhand, borrowed by the merchant from some traveler in a tavern, thin as smoke, but the girl was unmistakable. Eight years old, maybe nine. Dark hair cut bluntly at the jaw. Kneeling on stone before that bruise-lit throne with her chin lifted, and her eyes —

Her eyes were open, and they were empty as winter windows, and she was alive.

The world tilted. Rosalind withdrew so fast he nearly staggered into the rock wall, and Doss turned, mule strap forgotten, and found his companion white as whey and gripping the mountainside like a man holding on to the world itself.

"You all right there, Halloran?"

"Altitude," Rosalind managed. "Thin air. Happens on this stretch."

"Take a sweetmeat." Doss pressed a wrinkled candied plum into his hand with the expansive kindness of a man whose vault remained safely his own. "My wife makes them. Don't tell her I gave one away."

Rosalind ate it without tasting anything at all.

*Dead*, Wren was dead. Thirteen years dead. He had buried the knowledge so deep it had grown its own geology — grief layered on guilt layered on the terrible arithmetic of a boy who understood that his sister had been taken by the fever winter because he'd had nothing, nothing, nothing to trade for a physician. He had built a whole self on top of that grave. Every stolen memory, every sly transaction, every coin skimmed from the minds of the comfortable had been, in some way he rarely examined, a payment against that old debt.

And here she knelt, alive, in the dark beneath Thornfell, before a throne that should not exist outside madmen's tales.

He walked the rest of that day in silence, which worried Doss considerably, since silence from a chatty man is more alarming than shouting from a quiet one. Rosalind turned the fragment over and over in his mind's hands. Secondhand. Tainted by distance. Tavern-talk wearing a memory's clothes. There were a hundred innocent explanations — a story someone told, a nightmare passed along, a play he'd seen once. People's minds were full of rubbish; he knew better than anyone.

But her eyes had looked at him. Not at the tavern, not at the fire, not at the storyteller. In the strange grammar of memory, she had looked directly out of Doss Halloway's borrowed thought and found him there, and her empty eyes had seemed, for one unbearable instant, to be waiting.

By dusk he could no longer pretend to himself, and pretending to himself was, frankly, one of his principal talents.

"I have to leave you here," he told Doss at the fork above Greyhollow, where the smuggler's road split — one path down to the city's honest gates, one winding toward the river district where nothing was honest and everything was available.

"Now? We're an hour from hot food and a bed that doesn't move!"

"Family business," Rosalind said, and found the words tasted strange, like coins held too long in the mouth.

Doss clapped him on the shoulder, pressed three more sweetmeats into his pocket, and went on down toward the lights of Greyhollow, talking to his mule. Rosalind watched him go, then turned toward the river district, where the lamps burned low and greasy and everyone you met was either buying something or being something for sale.

Old Bram kept his shop behind a tannery, on the theory that no magistrate's nose could survive the approach. It was less a shop than an avalanche of paper — ledgers, broadsheets, letters of credit, love notes purchased and unsent — piled in towers that leaned together conspiratorially overhead. Bram himself sat amid it like a spider who had grown fond of the flies, a vast old man with milky eyes and fingers stained black with ink, sorting secrets the way other men sorted buttons.

"Well," Bram said, without looking up. "The river district smells of tallow and regret, and one of those has just walked through my door. Sit down before you fall down, Halloran. You look like a man who's seen a ghost, and in my experience men who've seen ghosts are bad customers. They pay in sentiment."

"Bram." Rosalind sat on a stool that groaned. "What do you know about the court beneath Thornfell?"

The ink-stained fingers stopped moving.

For a long moment the only sound was the tannery next door, breathing its chemical breath through the walls. Then Bram raised his cloudy eyes, and Rosalind saw something in them he had never seen in ten years of profitable acquaintance: fear.

"Nothing," Bram said. "I know nothing about it, and neither do you, and if you're wise we'll both continue knowing nothing until we die comfortably of old age and cheese."

"There's a soul down there, Bram. A child's soul, bought and kept. Thirteen years kept."

"Thirteen years." Bram set down his pen with exaggerated care. "And how does a wool merchant come to be carrying that particular piece of filth in his head?"

"It doesn't matter how I—"

"It matters entirely. Listen to me." The old man's voice dropped, and the fear in it was worse than shouting. "Three days ago the crown posted hunters on every road into Greyhollow. Proclamation comes tomorrow, if the criers are paid on time. The hunt for memory-thieves, they're calling it — politely. What it means is they're sweeping the province for the gifted, and no one knows why, and when no one knows why, it's because the reason is ugly." He leaned forward. "They took a girl from Tanner's Row last week for remembering too much. Her own mother reported her. Do you understand what season this is, Rosalind? This is the season when clever men burn their stock and become farmers."

"The crown doesn't care about a vault in Corrow's Bend."

"The crown doesn't care about anything except what it wants, and right now I don't know what it wants, and that frightens me more than anything I've learned in fifty years of knowing everything." Bram pushed back from his desk, and the tower of papers behind him swayed and settled, sighing. "Go home. Sleep. Forget whatever face you saw kneeling in the dark."

"She's my sister."

The words came out smaller than he intended. Bram stared at him, and slowly, terribly, the old fence's face changed — the merchant's mask slipping to show the man underneath, the one who had once been young in a harder century.

"Oh," Bram said quietly. "Oh, lad. That's not merchandise. That's grief wearing a price tag, and grief is the one buyer that always overpays."

"Then sell me something anyway. Names. Routes. Who trades below the mountain, who survived coming back, who takes coin and asks nothing—"

"No."

"Bram—"

"*No.*" The old man stood, which he did perhaps twice a year, and the room seemed to rearrange itself around his bulk. "You'll get yourself killed, or worse, you'll get me killed, and I am too old and too comfortable to be martyred for your dead sister who is apparently not dead, which raises theological questions I decline to entertain." He jabbed an ink-black finger. "There are thirteen moons in the turning, Halloran. Everyone knows that much about the undercourt, if they know anything. Whatever bargain holds her — it holds until the thirteenth moon turns. You have time. Be sensible. Be afraid. Be a farmer."

"And if I'm not?"

Bram looked at him for a long moment, milky eyes unreadable. Then he sighed, the sigh of a man watching a younger version of himself walk off a cliff with excellent posture, and shuffled to a shelf buried under a stuffed heron. From behind the heron he produced a scrap of paper no larger than a playing card, worn soft with handling.

"Sefa," he said. "Cast out of the undercourt, if half of what I hear is true, which it usually isn't. Lives past the tannery, keeps goats, hates visitors. Tell her Old Bram sent you and she'll hate you slightly less." He thrust the paper into Rosalind's hand. "This isn't help. This is evidence of my stupidity. Go home tonight. Sleep on it. If you still mean to be a fool at dawn, at least be a fool who knows the way."

Rosalind folded the paper into his coat, over his heart, where he kept everything that mattered and most things that would ruin him.

At the door he paused. "Bram. The hunters the crown posted. What do they hunt with?"

The old man settled back into his chair and picked up his pen, and for a moment he was only a fat old dealer in other people's pasts again.

"Needles," he said. "So I'm told. Long ones."

Outside, Greyhollow's bells began to ring the hour, and somewhere across the dark city, a crier was counting out coin for a proclamation. Rosalind pulled his collar up against the river damp and started walking, the memory of his sister's empty eyes burning behind them like a lamp the color of bruises.

Twelve moons, then. He intended to spend them well.
