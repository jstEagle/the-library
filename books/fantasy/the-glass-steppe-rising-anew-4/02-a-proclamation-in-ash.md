## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

Greyhollow woke the way it always did: badly, loudly, and smelling of fish and wet stone. Rosalind Halloran had lived in the city long enough to find the smell almost comforting, in the way a man finds an old boot comforting — not because it's good, but because it's his.

He was on his third cup of bitter tea at the Broken Compass when the criers began.

The first one stood on the fountain steps in Copperlane Square, unrolling a scroll with the flourish of a man who believed himself important. He was not important. He was, however, loud, which in Greyhollow amounted to nearly the same thing.

"By decree of the Crown!" he bellowed. "A hunt is declared for those who traffic in the memories of others! Thieves of thought! Pickers of minds! Let all such be named, shamed, and taken!"

Rosalind lowered his cup very slowly, the way one lowers a hand from a sleeping dog.

"Well," said Tam Wickerby beside him, a fishmonger whose gout was as legendary as his gossip, "that'll thin out the taverns."

"Mm," said Rosalind.

"My cousin's barber swears there's a fellow in Saltmarket who can pull a memory clean out of your head like drawing a thread from cloth. Never met him myself." Tam squinted. "You'd know, though, wouldn't you? You know everyone."

"I know no one," Rosalind said pleasantly. "I'm famously forgettable."

Which was, all things considered, the truest sentence he'd spoken that week.

The crier moved on to the second proclamation, which was less theatrical and considerably worse. Anyone harboring the gifted was to be fined. Anyone *aiding* them — buying their wares, sheltering them, so much as tipping their hats — would be considered complicit. And any citizen found to possess memories they could not account for would be detained for questioning by the Office of Inquiry.

Questioning. Rosalind had heard what questioning meant when the crown's men did it. There were needles involved. He finished his tea, paid with exact coins, tipped Tam nothing at all — a kindness, really, under the circumstances — and went home to burn his life down.

* * *

His lodgings above the chandler's shop on Bellwether Lane were modest, which was the word he preferred over *cramped* and considerably more accurate than *cozy*. One room, one window overlooking the lane, one floorboard near the hearth that squeaked in E minor if you knew where to step. Rosalind stepped elsewhere, knelt, and pried the board up.

Beneath lay a tin box, and inside the tin box lay his fortune.

Not coin. Coin could be stolen; these were better hidden and worse to lose. Thirty-one vials of grey glass, each no longer than his thumb, each holding a memory drawn from some wealthy skull or other: a magistrate's confession of bribery, a shipping baron's map of his smugglers' routes, a duchess's recollection of exactly which minister she'd bribed with exactly which favor. Years of careful work. Years of leverage. Each vial a small knife he could hold to somebody's throat without ever breaking skin.

And now every one of them was evidence.

He sat back on his heels and looked at the little grey glimmers and felt something in his chest do an ugly, complicated turn. A man's whole trade, boxed up like buttons.

"You're being dramatic," he told himself aloud, because the room was empty and someone ought to keep him company. "It's just money."

It wasn't just money, and he knew it. Half those vials held pieces of himself too — the residue that clung to every extraction, the way flour dusts a baker. His first theft, clumsily done, still laced through a merchant's memory of a harvest festival. The taste of his mother's plum cake lived in three separate vials because he'd been careless in his early twenties and sentimental besides. Burning them meant burning those fragments too.

But keeping them meant the gallows, or worse — the needle, and whatever came after the needle.

Downstairs, in the chandler's cellar, there was a second cache behind the tallow barrels. Old Marta the chandler owed him for the year he'd quietly lifted her grief over her dead husband and sold it back to her softened at the edges, dulled just enough that she could sleep. She didn't know that was why she slept well now. She only knew Rosalind paid his rent on time and never complained about the smell.

He knocked the particular knock. She opened the door, took one look at his face, and put the kettle on without asking questions, which was why he loved her.

"They took the Fenwick boy this morning," she said, pouring.

Rosalind's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Tam?"

"Remembering too much, they said." Marta's hands were steady but her voice wasn't quite. "His mother swears he never stole anything. But he remembered his grandfather's stories word for word, all the old ballads, every battle — things nobody living taught him. The soldiers said no mind should hold that much that isn't its own. They dragged him down the lane past the bakery. He was calling for his mum."

Tam Wickerby's grandson. Eight years old. Rosalind had met him once on the stairs, a round-faced child who'd asked him a hundred questions about the ships in the harbor and forgotten none of the answers, because that was simply how Tam was made — a little vessel that couldn't stop filling.

"He doesn't have my gift," Rosalind said quietly. "He just *remembers*."

"That's enough for them now." Marta set down his cup. Her eyes, red-rimmed, fixed on him. "Rosalind. Whatever it is you keep down here behind my barrels—"

"There's nothing behind your barrels."

"—whatever it is," she repeated, with the patience of a woman who had buried a husband and outlived two kings' tax collectors, "you should decide quickly whether it's worth more than your neck. Because I like having you upstairs, and I'd rather not learn what you are from a proclamation."

She turned back to her kettle. Rosalind drank his tea, thanked her, and went down to the cellar alone.

* * *

The chandler's cellar smelled of tallow and salt and thirty years of candles waiting to happen. Rosalind pulled the barrels aside, worked loose the loose brick, and drew out the second box: forty-odd vials more, plus a ledger written in his own private shorthand — names, prices, dates, debts. The architecture of a decade.

He carried both boxes up to his room, locked the door, shuttered the window, and built a fire in the hearth hotter than any sensible person needed on a spring morning.

Then he sat down in front of it and did nothing for a long time.

This was the part nobody warned you about, he thought. Not the danger. Danger was easy; danger got the blood moving and made a man clever. It was this — the sitting, the looking, the arithmetic of what a life weighed when you sold it piece by piece. Every vial was a door he'd once opened into somebody else's head. Every door had left a smudge of him on the frame. Burn the evidence and you burned the smudges, and the smudges, it turned out, were most of what he had of certain years.

He picked up the first vial. A shipping baron's routes — worthless now, the man had rerouted everything after Rosalind squeezed him two winters back. Easy. He uncorked it over the flames and watched the grey shimmer rise, twist, and come apart like breath on cold glass. Somewhere in the city a fat man would sleep soundly tonight and never know why he felt lighter.

The second was harder. The duchess. Inside it, tangled in her court intrigue like a fly in amber, was a fragment of Rosalind himself: twenty-three years old, dancing badly at some lord's gala, laughing. He hadn't thought about that night in years. Now, knowing he was about to destroy the only place it still existed, he held it to the light and looked.

*He is young and thin and his coat is borrowed and Wren is there, somehow, visiting the city — his sister, sixteen, all elbows and opinions, scolding him for stepping on her feet while secretly delighted. The music is dreadful. Neither of them cares.*

Rosalind's hands shook.

He hadn't known that was in there. That was the cruelty of the trade: you never got to inventory yourself. Pieces of you stuck to other people's memories and drifted off, and you found them again only at moments like this, when burning them was the price of breathing free.

Wren. Dead thirteen years — drowned, they'd told him, in the Thornfell river, though they'd never shown him a body. And yesterday he'd seen her kneeling before a throne beneath a mountain, alive and hollow-eyed, in a stranger's stolen memory.

He had a vault to find. A debt-broker to squeeze. A sister to steal back from the underworld itself. None of which he could do as a wanted man carrying a decade of contraband.

"Burning bridges," he murmured, "so the bridge can't be traced back to the arsonist."

One by one, he fed the fire.

Some vials he opened and let the memory go gently, a grey sigh into the smoke. Some he crushed, because anger was easier than ceremony. The magistrate's confession went last, and when it flared he fancied the flame burned briefly green, though he was fairly sure that was wishful thinking.

When it was done, the tin boxes were empty and the ledger was ash and Rosalind Halloran sat in a room that smelled like a burnt offering, lighter by seventy-one vials and several irreplaceable scraps of his own soul.

He felt terrible. He also felt, distantly, underneath the terrible, *free* — the way a man feels after cutting off his own coat-tails to slip a snare. Lighter. Uglier at the hem. Alive.

Someone pounded on the street door below.

Rosalind went very still. Through the window-shutters he saw torchlight on Bellwether Lane, boots, the grey-and-silver of crown livery. Voices. Marta's voice, sharp and unhurried, saying something he couldn't make out.

They were going door to door. Of course they were. First day of the hunt, and they'd start with the poor streets, because crowns always did.

He looked around his room — the narrow bed, the chipped mirror, the hearth full of the ashes of everything he'd built. Nothing here said *memory-thief* anymore. Nothing here said much of anything. He was, officially, nobody.

There was a knock at the street door. Then Marta's voice again, clearer now, pitched to carry up through the floorboards in a way he was quite sure she'd practiced:

"No lodger! Just me and my candles, officer, and my knees, which are none of your business!"

Boots retreated. Torchlight slid off down the lane toward the next house, the next name, the next neighbor who remembered too much.

Rosalind let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since morning. Then he packed a single bag — spare shirt, knife, the last of his honest coin — and paused at the door.

Behind him, in the cold hearth, the ashes of his old life were already going grey. Ahead of him lay Thornfell mountain, a debt signed with his own name, and a throne that held his sister's soul on a leash of a single memory.

First, though, he had a broker to visit. A man called Quill, down in Saltmarket, who traded in older, uglier paper than Rosalind had ever touched — and who, thirteen years ago, had handled a sale that Rosalind was beginning to fear had been signed by *him*.

He pulled his hood up, stepped over the silent floorboard, and slipped out into the dark.
