## 3. The Inquisitor's Needle

The interrogation room in Greyhollow's Crownhouse had one window, and Veyra Crane had petitioned twice to have it bricked over. Light, in her experience, only made liars squint more convincingly.

The thief currently chained to the table was called Petch Marlowe, though he had offered three other names in the first hour and seemed to be workshopping a fourth. He was a small man with ink-stained fingers — not the ink of letters, but of the cheap tattoo parlors along Dredge Street, where for two coppers they would draw a memory-sigil on your wrist that did nothing at all except make you feel mysterious.

"Master Marlowe," she said. "Let us try again."

"I told you everything."

"You told me three versions of everything, each less plausible than the last. The third involved a talking mule." She set her case on the table. It was made of black lacquer and iron hinges, and it opened with a sound like a held breath released. "I am going to show you what I keep in here. You will find it clarifying."

Inside, nested in grey velvet, lay the needle.

It was longer than a sewing needle by half a hand, silver-bright, with an eye that did not hold thread. The court smiths who forged such things called the eye a *receptacle*. Old thieves called them soul-hooks. Veyra called it *the needle*, because naming things plainly kept them from growing teeth in the dark.

Petch Marlowe looked at it the way men look at snakes.

"That's illegal," he said.

"Yes," said Veyra. "For you."

* * *

She had done this perhaps four hundred times, and she could count the pleasant ones without removing her boots. The needle slid into the soft hollow behind the ear — the crown's physicians insisted there was no pain, and technically they were right; what came was worse than pain and quieter. A pulling sensation. A coldness behind the eyes, like winter arriving early through a window left open.

Petch whimpered anyway. They always did.

"Hold still," she murmured, almost kindly. "Think about the thing you're hiding. That helps it come loose. Like picking a splinter."

"What happens to what you take?"

"It goes into the needle." She turned the silver length so the eye caught the lamplight. "And then into me, briefly, when I read it. And then into wax, when I record it. And then into the fire, if it proves useless. Most things prove useless."

"That's — that's my *life*—"

"Parts of it," Veyra agreed. "The parts worth burning."

She pressed her thumb to the eye of the needle and closed her own eyes, and let the stolen thing pour in.

* * *

Memory arrived the way weather arrives: all at once, already everywhere.

A cellar under a tannery. Rain-smell through floorboards. Petch Marlowe, younger and thinner, kneeling across from a man whose face the memory refused to hold — not blurred, exactly, but *turned aside*, like a portrait hung crooked so the viewer keeps looking away.

*"You want to sell or buy?"* the crooked-faced man asked. His voice was warm. Younger than his hands. *"Because buying's easy. Selling's the hard trade. You'd be amazed how little some people's lives fetch."*

*"Selling,"* Petch said. *"My mother died last spring and I can't stop seeing the sickroom. Every night. I just want it out."*

A pause. The warm voice grew gentler, which was somehow the most frightening part of the whole memory.

*"That's not how it works, friend. I can't take a memory from you. I can only take one that was given freely — truly freely — or one that's already loose, already dead in someone else's head. What I can do is this: I'll take your worst night, and I'll give you back someone else's best day. Fair weight. You'll remember a wedding you never attended instead of a funeral you can't forget."*

*"Whose wedding?"*

*"Does it matter? It was a good one. There was dancing."*

In the memory, Petch wept and nodded. In the memory, hands changed something invisible between them, and the sickroom lifted off him like fog burning off a field, and a stranger's joy settled into its place — secondhand sunlight, borrowed years.

Veyra opened her eyes.

The needle was warm against her thumb. Petch Marlowe sat slumped in his chains, looking vaguely lighter around the shoulders, as though a coat had been removed that he hadn't known he was wearing.

"Well?" he whispered.

"The man you met," she said. "His name."

"He never gave one. But the tanner's boys called him—" Petch frowned, groping. "Halloran. Rosalind Halloran. He works the smuggler's road through Thornfell. Sells memories like fishmonger sells herring. Everyone knows him. Nobody knows him."

"And the exchange he performed on you. Did he do it often? For many people?"

Petch laughed, wet and disbelieving. "Madam, half of Dredge Street has slept better because of Halloran. Half of *Greyhollow*. He takes the nights people can't carry and trades them for days somebody else didn't need. He's not a thief. He's a—" the man searched, found the word, and offered it up like evidence for the defense: "—a pawnshop. For souls."

Veyra wrote the name down. Her hand, she noticed distantly, was very steady, which was fortunate, because the rest of her was not.

* * *

Afterward she dismissed the guards, which she never did, and sat alone in the interrogation room with the window's thin light lying across the table like something spilled.

The gift — she hated calling it that, but the word had teeth and would not be shaken — ran in her family the way red hair ran in others. Her grandmother could taste a lie before it finished being spoken. Her mother could stand in a market square and know which griefs belonged to which baskets of onions. And Veyra, at seven years old, had once put her hand on her father's knee and told him, precisely, what he dreamed the night her brother was born: a river, a boat, and a door standing open on the water.

He had gone white. Within the month there had been priests, and a procedure the Church called *the Quiet Blessing* and her mother called, once, drunk, *the cutting*.

Veyra remembered none of it. That was the point. Whatever they had taken from her mind, they had taken cleanly — and what remained was a shape of absence, the way a missing tooth leaves a hole the tongue returns to obsessively, at night, in the dark.

She had become an inquisitor, she sometimes suspected, for the same reason a starving man becomes a cook.

And now this. This *Halloran*. A man who dealt openly in the very substance the crown had cut out of her, trading griefs like a grocer, and — according to a weeping tattooed thief — doing it *kindly*. Taking the unbearable and giving back the beautiful. Charging, she'd wager, barely enough to eat.

It should have disgusted her. It did disgust her. It also produced, somewhere beneath the disgust, in the toothless socket of her mind, a feeling she refused to name and named anyway: envy. Clean, cold, structural envy. Envy the way architecture is structure.

Someone knocked. Her lieutenant, Ossler — a large, honest man with the imagination of a fence post, which is why she kept him.

"Inquisitor. Dispatch from Thornfell. The hunters posted on the mountain road report activity near the old vault roads. Smugglers moving at night. One of them, per description—" he checked the paper, unnecessarily; he had clearly memorized it for effect, "—matches your Halloran."

"Description of whom?"

"A man. Dark-haired. Youngish. Witnesses say he looks at you like he's reading you."

"They always say that. It means nothing."

"Also," Ossler added, with the delicacy of a man dropping a boot down a well, "there's a rumor from the debt-quarter. Thirteen years back, a child's soul sold to the undercourt. Signed bill of sale. The name on it, they say, is Halloran."

Veyra looked at him for a long moment.

"Say that again."

"A child's soul. Sold to the court beneath Thornfell. Bill signed—"

"No, I heard you." She rose, took her case, clicked it shut — the breath held, released. "Thirteen years ago. Who brokered the debt?"

Ossler blinked. "There's a broker in Greyhollow still holding the paper, they say. A moneylender. Fennick, of Candle Row."

"Fennick." She tested the name, found it familiar, filed it. "Prepare horses. Two days' ride to Greyhollow's edge first — I want Fennick before the rumor grows legs. Then Thornfell."

"And the prisoner?"

"Sell him back his mother's sickroom for a confession in writing. Full price." At Ossler's expression, she added, dryly, "We are not monsters, Lieutenant. We are merely employed by them."

He left. Veyra stood alone again with the window's spilled light.

She took out the needle. The eye still held the ghost of Petch Marlowe's memory — the tannery cellar, the warm voice, the fair weight of traded sorrow. She pressed her thumb to it one more time, not to read it, but to feel it sitting there: proof that a person's worst night could be lifted out like a splinter and life could go on lighter for it.

*Somebody cut mine out too,* she thought. *Somebody decided for me.*

The difference between herself and Halloran, she understood suddenly, was not method. It was consent. He waited for the freely given. The crown had never waited for anything in its life.

She wrapped the needle in its velvet, and thought, with something adjacent to dread: *If I catch this man, I am afraid of what I will recognize.*

Then she snuffed the lamp, left the window to its useless light, and went to saddle for Candle Row.
