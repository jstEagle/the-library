## 4. Old Debts, Older Names

The debt-broker's shop sat at the crooked end of Coil Street, where Greyhollow's buildings leaned together like conspirators sharing a secret. Rosalind had passed it a hundred times in his years of trade — *Marrow & Quill, Debts of Every Nature Settled Discreetly* — and had always assumed the name was a joke. Nobody named Marrow could be trusted with anything, and nobody honest needed a quill for the kind of debts that required discretion.

He pushed open the door anyway.

The bell above it didn't ring so much as sigh, a small brass exhaustion. Inside, the shop smelled of tallow, dust, and old paper — the smell of promises that had outlived the people who made them. Shelves climbed the walls, stuffed with ledgers bound in leather gone soft as ear skin. A single lamp burned behind the counter, and beneath it sat an old man so thin his collarbones looked like they were trying to escape through his shirt.

"Closed," the old man said without looking up.

"It's midmorning."

"Then I'm closed early." He turned a page. "Come back never."

Rosalind set the merchant's memory on the counter — not the whole thing, just a sliver of it, pressed into a drop of amber resin the way he'd learned to do before Bram taught him better methods. The resin caught the lamplight and held it, and inside the light, faint as a face seen through frosted glass, was the image: a girl kneeling before a throne of black glass, her red hair bright as a struck match.

The old man looked up.

His name, Rosalind knew from three separate whispers on three separate streets, was Fennick Marrow, and he was the last broker in Greyhollow who dealt in the old kind of debts — the kind measured in more than coin. He'd survived the purges of two decades ago by being too useful to hang and too dull to envy, which was, Rosalind reflected, a career strategy with real merit.

"That's undercourt work," Fennick said quietly.

"I know."

"You don't. Nobody who knows comes knocking at my door asking about it. They come running." He peered at Rosalind over half-moon spectacles. "You've got the look of a man who came running and then remembered to walk before he arrived."

That was exactly right, and Rosalind resented him for it. He had, in fact, run — out of Bram's back room, down four alleys, past a fishwife who'd asked if he was well and received no answer — before pride had caught up with panic and insisted he arrive anywhere looking like something other than a madman.

"A girl," Rosalind said. "Red hair. Thirteen, maybe fourteen when this was made. Kneeling before the throne. I need to know whose debt bought her, and when, and why."

Fennick took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which gave him time to look at Rosalind properly. "You knew her," he said. Not a question.

"She's my sister."

Something moved behind the broker's eyes — pity, or its close cousin, embarrassment. "Ah," he said softly. "Then you'd best sit down."

* * *

Rosalind did not sit down. He stood by the counter with his knuckles whitening against its edge while Fennick climbed a ladder older than both of them combined and pulled down a ledger bound in grey calfskin, the spine stamped with a year thirteen turns gone.

"The crown hunts us now," Rosalind said, watching him. "Gifted folk. Memory-thieves. You deal in souls' worth of paper. Why aren't you running?"

"Because I'm ninety-one years old, boy, and I've been running since before your father was a disappointment. I've decided to die at my desk. It saves everyone the trouble of a eulogy." He dropped the ledger onto the counter with a thump that raised a small ghost of dust. "Besides, the hunt won't reach Coil Street. The criers skip this end. Too depressing."

He opened the ledger and ran a cracked thumbnail down columns of entries written in ink faded to the color of weak tea. Rosalind read upside down as best he could — names, sums, collateral — a census of desperation. Farmers who'd pledged their harvests twice over. A widow who'd signed away her house for her husband's medicine and lost both. And there, near the bottom of the page, an entry that stopped his breath:

*Received: one soul, minor, female, approx. 11 yrs.*
*Condition: sound.*
*Consignor: R. Halloran.*
*Consideration rendered: full discharge of consignor's obligations; further consideration pending.*

Below it, in different ink, darker and newer-looking, someone had added: *Delivered to the court beneath Thornfell. Consideration paid in full.*

Rosalind read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

"This is a forgery," he said. His voice came out flat and strange, like someone else speaking through him.

"It is not."

"My sister died. She drowned. I was there — I—" He stopped, because the memory, when he reached for it, slid like wet rope. He had a river. He had cold. He had Wren's shoe on the bank, one small boot, mud-stained, and he had grief so large it had eaten everything around itself, including, apparently, the details. He could not remember pulling her from the water. He could not remember a body. He could not remember burying her, or seeing her buried, or standing at any grave at all.

Thirteen years, and he had never once noticed the hole where those memories should have been. Grief, he'd told himself. Grief blurs things. But grief doesn't erase. It only smudges. Something had taken a cloth to the page of him.

"What did they take?" he whispered.

Fennick's expression gentled. "I couldn't say. I'm a bookkeeper, not a surgeon. But consignments like this — souls sold whole — usually come with a trimming. The court takes the anchor memories first. Makes the rest easier to carry." He tapped the ledger. "And they pay the consignor to forget the transaction. Clean conscience, clean sale. Very tidy business. The undercourt prides itself on tidiness."

"Who signed this?"

"You can read."

"*My name*, Fennick. I was fourteen. Eleven-year-olds don't sell their sisters. Someone used my name."

Fennick was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, it was with the careful kindness of a man handling broken glass. "Names are currency here, same as anything. Anyone could have borrowed yours." He hesitated. "Anyone with access to you."

The shop seemed very still. Outside, a cart rattled past, and the sound came from far away.

"Our parents were dead," Rosalind said slowly, assembling the arithmetic he'd spent thirteen years refusing to do. "It was winter. We were starving. There was a man — a gentleman — who offered to take Wren. Said she'd be fed, schooled, given a place in some great house in the south. I said no. I said we'd manage." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "We managed. Barely. And she drowned in the spring."

"Or," Fennick said gently, "she went south in the winter, and the river was the story you were left holding."

Rosalind gripped the counter. The wood bit into his palms and he was grateful for it, grateful for any pain that wasn't this one.

"Someone took my refusal," he said, "and sold it anyway. Sold *her*. And wrote my name at the bottom so that when they trimmed me—" his voice cracked— "so that I'd grieve clean and never ask questions. Paid in full. That's what it says. *Paid in full.* My survival. My debts — I owed money even then, I remember owing — all of it cleared, all of it bought with—" 

He couldn't finish. He turned away, because a ninety-one-year-old bookkeeper should not have to watch a grown man come apart at his counter, and because there was nothing on the dusty shelves that could help him anyway.

Fennick let him have his moment. Then, briskly, the way kindness often sounds when it's wearing work clothes: "There's more. Sit down now. Truly."

Rosalind sat.

Fennick turned the page. "A consignment isn't finished until the court confirms receipt. Confirmation comes back through channels. Look."

There, at the top of the next leaf, a notation in a fine, precise hand:

*Confirmed by the Office of the Throne. Soul entered into service. Term: twelve turnings of the moon, renewable at Her Majesty's discretion. Anchor retained by the Crown Regent below. Redemption clause per the Old Bargain: original consignor may reclaim within term by payment of equivalent weight.*

"Twelve turnings," Rosalind repeated. "From when?"

"From confirmation. Which is dated here." Fennick touched the margin, where a small numeral sat in faded brown ink. "Thirteen years minus eleven months."

Rosalind did the arithmetic and felt the floor tilt. One month. Give or take a handful of days, depending on how the moons counted — and the moons under the mountain counted differently than the moons above, Sefa had said, or would say, or — no. He hadn't met Sefa yet. That was ahead of him, though he already knew it the way you know the shape of a road you haven't walked.

One moon. Maybe less.

"And the redemption clause. 'Equivalent weight.' Equivalent to what?"

"To what was purchased." Fennick closed the ledger with terrible gentleness. "A soul, boy. You put in a soul, you get a soul out. That's the Old Bargain, and the court keeps its bargains — it's the one thing they're honest about. It's why they're worse than liars."

Rosalind stared at the ledger. At his own name, written in a stranger's hand but wearing his face. Somewhere beneath the Thornfell mountains, in a court that traded in people the way Fennick traded in paper, his sister knelt before a black-glass throne, kept alive and hollowed by a leash of memory, and the price of her freedom was a soul of equal weight, tendered before the thirteenth moon turned.

He thought of Bram's warning — *hunters on the roads, boy, and you're the quarry now* — and found, to his surprise, that fear had gone quiet somewhere between the ledger and the floor. In its place sat something colder and much more patient.

"They'll be hunting me either way," he said aloud. "So I may as well be guilty of something worth the gallows."

Fennick snorted. "Spoken like a Halloran."

Rosalind's head snapped up. "What did you say?"

The old man paused, spectacles halfway to his nose, and for the first time since Rosalind had walked in, he looked genuinely uncomfortable. "I said, spoken like a—"

"No. After that. The name." Rosalind leaned across the counter. "You knew the name Halloran before I gave it to you. You flinched when I said *sister*. You pulled that ledger down like you knew exactly which year to reach for." He stood. "Who else signed, Fennick? Whose hand is on that line? Because I've been assuming a stranger — a moneylender, a gentleman, anyone — but you looked at me just now like you were looking at a ghost you'd helped bury."

For a long moment, the only sound was the lamp's small flame eating its wick. Then Fennick sighed, the sigh of a man who has kept secrets for ninety-one years and finds the last one the heaviest, and reached beneath the counter for a slim bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

"There's a second signature," he said. "On the consideration page. The person who paid your debts — who paid *for* you, boy, body and soul, and arranged the whole tidy tragedy." He set the bundle down but did not unwrap it. "I never met him. But he left instructions, in case anyone ever came asking. Instructions and payment, thirteen years in advance. He said someone with your gift would come eventually. Gifted always find their way home to their own wounds."

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. "Who?"

"He called himself your uncle," Fennick said, and began, slowly, to untie the knot. "But between you and me — and I've been between you and me a long time — I checked the parish rolls afterward, and your father was an only child."
