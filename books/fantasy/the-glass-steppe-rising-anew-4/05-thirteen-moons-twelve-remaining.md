## 5. Thirteen Moons, Twelve Remaining

The priestess lived above a dye-shop in the Tanners' Quarter, which meant Rosalind spent a quarter hour climbing stairs that smelled of vinegar and old mutton before he reached her door. It was painted blue — aggressively blue, the blue of a bruise that had ambitions — and someone had scratched a sigil into the paint: a circle crossed by thirteen lines.

He knocked. Nothing. He knocked again, and a voice like dry leaves said, "If you're from the crown, I'm dead. If you're selling fish, leave it on the step. If you're neither, you'd better be interesting."

"My name is Rosalind Halloran," he said, "and I'm looking for the court beneath Thornfell."

A long silence. Then, muffled, almost to herself: "Well. That's interesting." Bolts slid back one by one, each with the reluctant clack of a woman who had rehearsed this moment for years and hoped it would never come.

Sefa was small and brown as a walnut, with white braids wound tight to her skull and eyes the color of pond water — green going gray at the edges, the way water does when it has seen too much settle to the bottom. She looked him up and down twice.

"Halloran," she repeated. "You have your sister's chin."

The floor tilted gently under Rosalind's feet. He gripped the doorframe. "You knew Wren?"

"I knew of her. Come in before the whole quarter hears you say that name out loud." She yanked him inside and bolted the door behind them with the thoroughness of a woman who expected the walls themselves to inform on her.

* * *

Her rooms were narrow and crammed with things that refused to explain themselves: jars of gray sand labeled in no alphabet Rosalind knew, a cracked bell that hummed instead of ringing, thirteen candles burned down to thirteen different heights on a shelf above the hearth. She put a kettle on without asking whether he wanted tea, which he found both presumptuous and deeply comforting.

"Thirteen years ago," she said, settling into a chair that groaned like an old ship, "I kept the ledger at the second gate. Souls in, souls out, terms of custody. It was dull work, mostly. The court beneath the mountain is not dramatic, whatever the ballads tell you. It is *clerical*. Souls are filed. Debts are stamped. Grief is processed in triplicate." She poured tea into two cups chipped at the rim. "And then a child came through, and the paperwork had your name on it."

"Tell me it wasn't my name."

"It was your name. Signed in your own hand, or something close enough that the gates accepted it. That's how soul-contracts work — they don't care whose blood signed, only whose *name*. A name is a kind of memory, you understand. The oldest kind. You give someone your name and you've given them power over every story told about you afterward." She sipped. "Someone sold Wren Halloran's soul to the throne for the price of one boy's survival. Thirteen years of it. The debt comes due when the thirteenth moon turns full."

Rosalind set his cup down because his hands had begun to embarrass him. "When?"

Sefa crossed to the shelf of candles and touched the tallest stub. "Count with me. Twelve moons remain. The thirteenth rises on the night of the Long Dark, four hundred days from now, give or take what the mountain does with time — and the mountain does *things* with time, so don't quote me to the day." Her pond-water eyes fixed on him. "After that moon turns, the contract closes. Permanently. Her soul belongs to the throne, body or no body, and there is no theft in any world — above or below — that can unmake a closed contract."

"And before that?"

"Before that, she can be reclaimed. By right of kinship, if the claimant comes himself." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of someone handing over a bill. "Which brings us to the part you're not going to like."

"I haven't liked anything since chapter one of this conversation."

"The gates." Sefa held up one finger. "There are three between the surface and the throne vault. Each takes a toll. Not coin — the court has no use for coin, it prints its own out of other people's longing. Each gate takes a memory. Freely given. That last part matters more than you'd think." She leaned forward. "A memory taken is theft, and the gates are built to catch thieves. But a memory *given* — offered up willingly, gladly even — that's payment. Clean payment. The gates drink it and let you pass."

"What kind of memory?"

"Yours to choose. That's the cruelty of it. The court doesn't want your worst moments — those are cheap, everyone's drowning in those. It wants weight. Joy weighs more than grief, did you know that? Grief is heavy but hollow, like lead. Joy is dense all the way through. The happiest memory you own will buy you further than ten of your worst." She refilled his cup unasked. "So you'll stand at each gate and choose what to feed it, knowing you'll never get it back. Most people who go down don't come back up whole. Some come back up as strangers wearing their own faces. And some come back up *empty*, hollowed out and hired on as servants, because after three gates there isn't enough left of them to want anything else."

Rosalind thought about that. He thought about it the way a man thinks about a knife balanced point-down on his palm.

"You're telling me the price of my sister is everything I am."

"I'm telling you the price is negotiable," Sefa said, "but only in the currency of yourself. Choose carefully what you spend. Spend the small bright things first — a good meal, a kind stranger, a dog you loved. Hoard the ones that made you *you* until you absolutely must." She paused. "Though I'll warn you: the gates can tell. They taste hesitation. If you offer them something you're glad to lose, they may refuse it and take something else entirely. You have to mean the giving. You have to hand over your treasure and feel, honestly, that it was worth it."

"That's monstrous."

"That's *accounting*," Sefa said, with the mild pride of a former professional. "The court beneath Thornfell was founded by bookkeepers, dear. Everything monstrous about it is just arithmetic wearing a crown."

* * *

He stayed while the light died. She fed him stew that tasted mostly of pepper and stubbornness, and talked, and he let her talk, because every word was a rope thrown across a gorge.

She told him about the hollowed servants, the ones who had paid too much at the gates and now swept the endless stairways with smiles fixed on their faces like paint. She told him about the throne itself — not a chair, she said, but a *keeping*, a vast slow mind of stone and stored souls, and the throne held each purchased soul anchored by a single memory, the strongest one it could find. "Cut the anchor and the soul slips free. But the anchors are kept in the vault, and the vault opens once a year, on the night the thirteenth moon wanes — the same night the contract closes." Her voice dropped. "Which means your window and your deadline are the same night. You will be stealing her back in the last hour she can legally be stolen."

"Naturally," Rosalind said. "Why would anything ever be simple."

"Simple is for people without debts." Sefa gathered the bowls. Then, quieter, not looking at him: "One more thing, Halloran. The throne knows your name already. It's been holding it thirteen years, waiting for the man who signed it to come collect. When you walk through those gates, you won't be sneaking in. You'll be *expected*."

The candle flames all leaned sideways at once, though there was no draft. Somewhere below the floorboards, below the dye-shop, below the bedrock of Greyhollow itself, Rosalind felt — or imagined he felt — something vast turn its attention toward him like a page being marked.

Twelve moons. Four hundred days. Three gates. One sister.

He stood, and found his legs had decided to be brave without consulting him. "Then I'd best start choosing what I can live without."

"No," Sefa said softly, seeing him to the door. "Start choosing what you can't. The rest is just luggage."

He was halfway down the vinegar-smelling stairs when he heard it — boots on the cobbles outside, several pairs, moving in the unhurried rhythm of people who were searching rather than walking. Through the dyeshop's grimy window he caught a flash of gray cloaks and the cold glint of a long silver needle-case at one searcher's belt.

They weren't looking for Sefa.

They were asking after a man named Halloran.
