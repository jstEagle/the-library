## 6. What the Needle Keeps

The safehouse smelled of dust and old decisions.

Veyra Crane stood in the doorway of the room above the chandler's shop on Weft Street and let that smell tell her everything it could. Dust meant abandonment, not flight — no one had swept here in weeks, but nothing had been knocked over in a hurry either. Old decisions meant a man who had lived carefully. She approved of careful men. They were easier to catch.

"Light," she said, and Corporal Dunmore raised his lantern obediently. He was a good boy, Dunmore. Young enough to still believe the hunt was about justice. She intended to keep him believing that as long as possible, mostly because his belief made him useful, and partly because she remembered — distantly, the way one remembers a coat outgrown — what it was like to believe things herself.

The room was spare. A cot with military corners. A washbasin gone dry, a ring of mineral scale at its bottom like a pale eye. One shelf. One chair. And on the desk, arranged with a curator's precision, a collection of small glass vials, each stoppered with wax, each labeled in a fine slanted hand.

Empty vials. Every last one of them.

"He burned them," Dunmore said. "The criers said he would. The Halloran man."

"He burned some." Veyra crossed to the desk and picked up a vial, turning it so the lantern light ran through the glass. "He burned what he could carry evidence of. But memory doesn't always live where you store it, Corporal. It soaks. Into walls. Into floors. Into objects a man touched every day." She set the vial down. "A house remembers its tenant the way a glove remembers a hand."

She drew the needle from the case at her hip.

It was a slender thing, silver-dark, no longer than her forefinger, and it hummed against her palm the way it always did — faintly, hungrily, like a cat waking. The court smiths had forged it for her, or rather had reforged it, since needles of this kind were older than the crown that now claimed their use. She tried not to think about where the originals had come from. She thought about it anyway, most nights.

"Dunmore. Wait outside."

"But, Inquisitor—"

"If there's residue in this room, I need quiet to read it. If there isn't, I need you outside so I can be disappointed in private." She smiled, and he flushed and went, and the door clicked shut behind him, and Veyra Crane stood alone in a dead man's room with a needle that wanted feeding.

She knelt at the center of the floor, where the wear in the boards was deepest, and pressed the needle's point to the wood.

*Drink,* she thought, and it drank.

The room went white.

---

She was small, and the world was enormous, which is the natural condition of being six years old.

A kitchen. Not this kitchen — a kitchen in Greyhollow's lower terraces, where the ceilings sweated in summer and the bread smelled like it had been argued over. A woman stood at the stove with her back turned, singing something tuneless and half-remembered, and a girl sat on the table swinging her legs, and a boy —

The boy was Rosalind Halloran. Of course he was. Veyra knew this the way one knows things in dreams: without process, without doubt. He was perhaps nine, all elbows and dark hair, and he was holding a chipped cup of water out to the girl with the gravity of a priest offering communion.

"You have to *want* to remember it properly," he was saying. "That's the whole trick. If you just sort of want it, you get the sort-of version."

"I want it properly," the girl said. She had a gap where a tooth had gone missing and a stubbornness about the mouth that Veyra found, absurdly, familiar. "Do the festival again. The part with the paper fish."

"The paper fish is the best part."

"It's the *only* part I remember and I want it *properly.*"

Rosalind closed his eyes. And Veyra, standing invisible at the edge of her own extraction, felt the memory reach for itself — felt the boy gather something up the way other children gathered marbles — and pour it into his sister.

And the kitchen *changed.*

Lanterns everywhere, strung between the stalls of the harvest market, hundreds of them, and paper fish on strings turning in the night wind, orange and green and one magnificent impossible violet, and Wren — the name arrived whole, *Wren*, like a bird let out of a cage — Wren shrieking with laughter as her brother lifted her onto his shoulders so she could bite the head off a honeycomb sweet shaped like a sun. Music from somewhere. A mother's voice calling from a doorway. The smell of hot sugar and river water and woodsmoke, all of it so vivid that Veyra's throat ached around a sweetness she had never tasted.

Then the kitchen again. Wren blinking, tears standing in her eyes, laughing.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's *exactly* it. Ro, how do you *do* that?"

"I don't know." The boy looked frightened of his own gift, briefly, before pride swallowed the fear. "I just — borrow it. From myself. Better."

*From himself.* Veyra watched the boy give away his own best hours like a man handing out coins, and understood two things at once, the way one understands things in memories: that this child was generous beyond sense, and that generosity of this kind has a bill attached.

The memory shivered. Thinned. This was old residue, soaked deep, and even a needle could only draw so much from floorboards. But it held long enough for one more scene —

— a road, years later, gray morning, and the boy older now, fourteen maybe, walking beside a man in a broker's coat whose face the memory refused to hold. Papers passed between them. The broker's voice came through flat and wrong, like speech heard underwater:

*"Thirteen years' deferment, signed against the estate. The court below is generous with time, Master Halloran. It is not generous twice."*

And the boy saying, in a voice that cracked in the middle: *"Take mine instead. Whatever the ledger says she owes — take it from me. I'm the gifted one. I'm worth more."*

Silence. Then the scratch of a quill.

The memory ended.

---

Veyra came back to herself kneeling on the floorboards of the safehouse, the needle trembling in her grip, her face wet.

She wiped it with the heel of her hand, furious at it. Crying during an extraction was amateur. Crying during an extraction of *floorboards* was unforgivable.

But she had seen the sister. Wren. Dead thirteen years by every record the crown kept — drowned, the file said, a debt-child written off in the standard manner — and yet alive in her brother's oldest memories with a gap-toothed grin and a hunger for paper fish. And Veyra had seen something else besides. Something the needle had shown her without asking permission:

Her own face. Younger. Seven, perhaps eight. Standing at the edge of the harvest festival crowd while a dark-haired boy lifted his sister onto his shoulders, and the girl on his shoulders had leaned down and said, *You look sad. Do you want to see the violet fish? There's only one.*

And Veyra — Veyra who did not remember any of this, Veyra whose childhood was a corridor of locked doors with the handles removed — had said yes. Had followed a stranger's sister through lantern light to see a paper fish the color of dusk, and had laughed, she was almost certain she had laughed, though there was no memory left of the laughing. Only the shape of it, like a footprint in snow.

They had taken it from her. The crown had taken it, when they took everything else, when they scraped her clean and gave her the needle and told her that emptiness was discipline. She had believed them. Emptiness had been easier than grief.

Now she knelt in a hunted man's rooms holding proof that the things removed from her had not been destroyed. They were *somewhere.* Things soaked. Things kept. The needle kept what it drank; the crown kept what the needle drank; and somewhere in the vaults beneath the Citadel there might be a vial with her name on it containing a festival and a violet fish and the sound of her own young laugh.

And beneath that thought, colder, darker, coiling up from the transcript she'd pulled from the thief in the Iron cells three nights ago: *the court below is generous with time.* A soul sold to the undercourt. A debt-child written off. Thirteen years ago, the same year the crown's records showed a sudden, unexplained surplus in the undercourt tithes — the year the hunt's founding charter was drafted, in a hand no archivist would admit to.

The hunt wasn't for thieves. It had never been for thieves. The hunt was a *harvest.* The crown flushed the gifted out of the gutters and shipped them down-mountain, and called it justice, and called the payments tithes, and she had been the needle at the end of it all — drinking souls one drop at a time and delivering them to men who fed them to a throne.

Dunmore knocked. "Inquisitor? Anything?"

Veyra looked at the needle. It lay in her palm, sated, faintly warm, keeping what it kept.

"Nothing useful," she said. "The site's cold."

"And Halloran?"

She rose. Her knees ached. When had her knees started aching?

"Halloran is going down the mountain," she said quietly. "Anyone selling that much of himself is buying passage somewhere. Seal the Thornfell roads behind us, Corporal. We ride within the hour."

"Down into the deeps?" Dunmore's face went pale. "Inquisitor, the charter forbids—"

"The charter," Veyra said, pocketing the needle, "was drafted by liars. I intend to read it very closely at the first gate."

She took one last look around the safehouse — at the empty vials, the careful corners, the ghost of a boy who paid his sister's debts with his own brightest hours — and thought, with a clarity that felt like the first honest thing in twenty years:

*I met you once, Rosalind Halloran. You don't know it. I don't remember it. But I am going to follow you all the way down, and one of us is going to come back up knowing the truth.*

Outside, the moon was rising — thin, silver, eleven days from full.

Twelve remained after that. She didn't know yet why that number mattered. She only knew she was running out of it.
