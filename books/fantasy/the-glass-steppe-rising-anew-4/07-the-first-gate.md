## 7. The First Gate

The entrance to the Thornfell deeps was not a door. It was a wound.

Rosalind stood at the lip of it at dusk, where the smuggler's road gave up pretending to be a road and simply fell into the mountain's mouth — a fissure in the rock wide enough to swallow a cart and dark enough to swallow everything after it. Cold air breathed up from below, smelling of wet stone and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in a cellar.

"Right," he said to no one. "Lovely holiday destination."

Sefa had given him instructions the way a surgeon gives instructions: precisely, and without any comfort whatsoever. *Three gates between you and the court. At each one, the gate will ask for a memory. It must be given freely — held out, not clutched. If you bargain, if you hesitate past the third asking, the gate takes what it likes instead, and the gate has excellent taste.*

He'd asked her what happened to the memories afterward. She'd looked at him with the particular pity of someone who has already paid this toll and said, *They are spent. The way a coin is spent. You do not go looking for your coin in another man's purse.*

He had spent three days deciding what to bring. That was the cruelty of it — he could not choose what the gates would ask for, but he could choose what he carried down, and whatever he carried was fair game. So he had left his caches behind, all of them, every stolen secret and purchased recollection that made Rosalind Halloran Rosalind Halloran. He came to the mountain carrying almost nothing.

Almost.

In his head, wrapped in careful folds of will like linen around porcelain, he carried the festival night. Harvestfire, eleven years ago. Wren at seventeen, flour still in her hair from the morning's baking because she'd refused on principle to wash it out before the dancing. The fiddlers tuning badly. The smell of scorched sugar and woodsmoke. Wren laughing so hard at his terrible attempt at the reel that she snorted, and then laughed harder because she'd snorted, and then he'd laughed until his ribs ached, and neither of them had danced a single step of the reel they'd argued about for a week beforehand.

That memory was the load-bearing wall of his entire self. He knew it. Sefa knew it. He suspected the mountain knew it too, mountains being what they were.

He stepped into the wound.

* * *

The descent was longer than the mountain should have allowed. He walked for hours by the light of veins of pale mineral in the walls — glowstone, or the undercourt's version of it, a light that didn't so much illuminate as *remember* illumination, a secondhand brightness. His boots found stairs where there had been no stairs, cut by hands or by patience, spiraling down through chambers where the air grew warmer and the sweet smell thickened.

He passed carvings. That was the first wonder. Whole galleries of them, floor to ceiling: faces, thousands upon thousands, each one different, each one rendered with the tenderness of a portraitist who loved their subjects. He stopped before one — a round-faced woman mid-laugh, dimples carved deep — and felt an absurd urge to apologize for staring.

"They're the given ones," said a voice near his elbow.

He did not jump. He was proud of that. He turned with the unhurried grace of a man who had absolutely not just been startled out of his skin, and found a small figure beside him — a child, or shaped like one, with eyes the color of the glowstone and an expression of vast professional boredom.

"The given ones," Rosalind repeated.

"The memories people hand over at the gates." The child shrugged. "They get carved. Everything the mountain takes, the mountain keeps. It doesn't throw anything away." A pause, and then, with the gravity of a much older creature: "You're going down to the throne."

"I'm going down to the market," Rosalind said smoothly. "I hear the prices are to die for."

The child looked at him for a long moment. Then it smiled, and the smile was wrong in a way he couldn't name, and it said, "They always joke at the first gate," and walked backward into the rock as though the rock were a curtain.

Rosalind stood alone in the gallery of laughing faces and decided, firmly, not to think about that.

* * *

The first gate announced itself with music.

He heard it before he saw it — a thin, sweet sound like a glass harmonica played underwater, drifting up the stairwell. Then the stairwell opened into a cavern so vast that the glowstone veins above hung like constellations, and across its floor ran a wall.

Not built. *Grown*, perhaps, or woven — a barrier of silver filaments stretching from floor to distant ceiling, dense as cloth, and set into its center an archway of black stone. The music came from the filaments themselves, humming as the air moved through them. And along the wall, at intervals, stood figures.

They looked like people. That was the horror of them, though he didn't know it yet. They stood perfectly still, facing outward, and their faces were pleasant and vacant as unfurnished rooms.

"Hollowed," Sefa had said. "Servants of the court. Don't speak to them. Don't let them touch you. And whatever you do, don't look too long into their eyes — some of them still have guests."

As Rosalind approached, the nearest hollowed one turned its head. Smoothly, all at once, like a weathervane finding wind. Its face was a middle-aged man's, kind-looking, well-fed, the face of someone who had once owned things.

"Welcome, traveler," it said. The voice was warm and slightly blurred, like speech heard through a wall. "The gate asks its question."

From the archway itself, the music shifted, resolved, became — unmistakably — a voice. Not cruel. That was the worst part. It sounded patient, the way winter is patient.

***What will you give?***

Rosalind had prepared for this. He had rehearsed on the road above, offering up small things in his mind like a man testing which floorboards creak. The taste of bad wine in Greyhollow. The name of his first fence. The gray afternoon he'd learned to pick pockets, rain on the awning, the fat merchant's oblivious laugh.

The archway declined all of them. He felt the refusals like a host pushing back a plate. *No. No. No.* The hollowed servant watched him with its kind, empty face, waiting without impatience, and the music waited, and the whole cavern waited, and Rosalind understood with dawning dread that the gate knew exactly what it wanted.

It wanted the best thing he had.

"You can't have—" he started, and stopped, because Sefa's voice was in his head: *held out, not clutched*. Clutching was how the gate took what it liked. And what it liked, presumably, was everything.

He thought about turning back. He actually thought about it, honestly, for the length of ten heartbeats. He could climb back into the world where Wren was dead and grief was at least a familiar coat. Or he could stand here and let the mountain take the single warmest room in his house.

Wren's face rose in his mind — flour in her hair, mid-snort, alive.

"That's the point, isn't it," he said quietly. "You're pricing the door in love."

***The gate does not price,*** said the music, gently. ***The traveler chooses what the door is worth to him.***

"Oh, that's *worse*," Rosalind told it. "I want you to know that's worse."

But he was already doing it. He gathered the festival night up in both hands of his mind — the fiddles, the sugar-smoke, the snort, the ache in his ribs, Wren's hand sticky with cider honey gripping his — and instead of clutching, he opened.

Holding it out hurt more than he'd imagined. It was like holding a bird with open fingers, feeling it weigh its options. For one suspended moment the whole memory hovered between them, golden and complete, and he had time to think *I could close my fist, I could keep it, I could—*

He let go.

The archway drank it. There was no other word. The music swelled, rich and satisfied, and the memory went out of him the way light goes out of a window at dusk — gradually, then entirely. He felt the shape of where it had been, a Wren-shaped warmth, and then even the shape smoothed over, and he was standing in a cavern holding nothing, reaching for something that had been there a moment ago and finding only a smooth blank wall where the room used to be.

He knew he had had a sister. That fact remained, dry as a ledger entry. *Had a sister. Dead, he'd believed; sold, he now knew.* But the night itself — the flour, the laughter, the terrible reel — was gone. Spent. A coin in another man's purse.

"Well," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle, and he cleared his throat and tried again. "Well. Lovely acoustics."

The hollowed servant inclined its head. "The gate is pleased. Pass, traveler." And then, as Rosalind moved toward the archway, the servant added — softly, in a voice suddenly less blurred, suddenly terribly present:

"She snorted when she laughed. Yours did. I heard it echo down just now. It was a good sound to carry."

Rosalind froze. "You—"

But the servant's face had already gone vacant again, pleasant as an unfurnished room, and the moment was gone, and Rosalind walked through the black archway with his eyes burning and his jaw set, hating the mountain with a completeness that felt almost holy.

* * *

Beyond the first gate, the deeps opened into the undercourt's outer markets, and Rosalind forgot his grief for exactly as long as it took to see what was being sold.

Stalls. Actual stalls, in caverns lit by chandeliers of glowstone, run by merchants of every description — and the goods were souls. Not metaphorically. Vials, lanterns, loaves of dark bread with a soul baked in for leavening. Memories by the pound. Years of somebody's childhood decanted into stoppered bottles, labeled in a fine spidery hand: *SUMMER, AGE NINE. GENTLY USED.*

And everywhere, moving among the stalls, the hollowed ones. Serving. Carrying. Smiling their unfurnished smiles. Some wore livery; some wore rags; all of them moved with the same smooth wrongness, and all of them, Rosalind noticed with a creeping cold, occasionally paused mid-task to stare at nothing with an expression like someone listening through a wall.

A merchant caught his eye — a woman with a jeweled collar and sharp, fully-present eyes — and beckoned. "Fresh from above? You've got the look of a man with inventory. I pay well for—"

"I'm not selling," Rosalind said.

"Everyone sells eventually, dear. The mountain's very persuasive." She smiled. "Mind the servants, won't you? They do so love new arrivals. Sometimes they remember pieces of what they were, and they get *homesick*, and homesick servants grab."

She turned back to her vials. Rosalind stood in the glowstone light, surrounded by the commerce of souls, and realized he had been standing still too long — that a hollowed servant had drifted near, a young woman in servant's gray, watching him with those listening-through-a-wall eyes.

"Don't," he warned her, low.

She tilted her head. And then, in a whisper so faint he might have imagined it, she said:

"Careful at the second gate, brother. It takes faces."

Then her eyes emptied again, and she curtsied, and glided away into the market crowd — leaving Rosalind Halloran standing very still, with one memory fewer than he'd arrived with, and the sudden, chilling knowledge that somewhere down here, the hollowed ones talked to each other.

And that they already knew his name.
