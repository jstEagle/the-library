## 8. The Throne That Remembers

The second gate was not a gate at all.

Rosalind had expected doors — something grand and terrible, iron and bone, the sort of thing a court beneath a mountain would commission when it wanted to frighten pilgrims. Instead he found an old woman sitting on a stool beside a candle stub, darning a sock that had no foot in it. She looked up at him with eyes like wells.

"Payment," she said.

"I gave at the first gate." His voice came out steadier than he felt. He'd been rehearsing this argument for six hours of descending dark, and now that it was time to deliver it, all his rehearsals evaporated like breath on glass.

"The first gate took your happiest day." The woman threaded her needle with thread that wasn't there. "The second takes something you carry every waking hour. Something you cannot set down. Your mother's face."

"No."

"You may turn back." She gestured behind him with the empty sock. The passage he'd climbed down through had sealed itself sometime during the last mile — he'd heard the stone knit shut with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "Though I notice you haven't turned around."

Rosalind looked at her. She looked back, patient as sediment.

"That's cheating," he said.

"It's arithmetic," said the old woman. "Everything below is arithmetic. Souls in, souls out, memories weighed against memories. The throne keeps very good books." She held up the needleless needle. "So. Do we do this the easy way, or do I fetch the arithmetic?"

He thought about Wren kneeling before the throne in a merchant's stolen memory, thirteen years old forever, her hands folded like a good girl waiting to be told what came next. He thought about Sefa's warning: *the deeper gates take what defines you, and they don't give receipts.*

His mother had died when he was nine, in a fever season that took half of Greyhollow's riverside quarter. What remained of her in his head was mostly warmth and flour dust and one specific afternoon — kneading bread on the scarred table while rain came down like the sky was apologizing — but her *face*, God, her face was the first beautiful thing he'd ever memorized. He had built a career on taking other people's treasures, and hers was the one thing he'd never once tried to sell.

"If I give it," he said slowly, "will I forget her?"

"You'll remember that you loved her. You'll remember she existed. You'll remember every story you ever tell about her — the fever, the bread, the way she sang off-key on purpose to make Wren laugh." The old woman smiled, and it was almost kind. "You simply won't be able to see her anymore. Faces are the crown jewels of a memory. We take the crown and leave the kingdom standing."

"And if I ever get them back? If someone were to steal from *this* place—"

The old woman laughed. It echoed strangely, as though the laugh were traveling down corridors he couldn't see. "Then you'd be the first thief in history to rob the throne twice. Now hold still."

She didn't touch him. She didn't need to. She simply looked at him the way he looked at merchants' minds — gently, professionally, the way a pickpocket regards a fat purse — and Rosalind felt the memory rise in him like a bubble through honey.

He fought it. Of course he fought it. But there was nothing to grip; grief without a face is like trying to punch fog. The last thing he saw of his mother was her mouth, smiling, mid-word, some sentence he'd never hear finished —

— and then it was gone, and he was kneeling on cold stone with wet cheeks and the peculiar lightness of a man who has lost weight he didn't know he was carrying.

"There," said the old woman, satisfied. "You'll want to lean on the wall a moment. Everyone does." She paused, then added, with the air of someone bestowing a great courtesy: "For what it's worth, it was a lovely face. Freckles. She bit her lip when she was worried."

"You *looked* at it."

"I look at everything that passes my gate. Occupational hazard." She resumed darning the sock that had no foot. "Go on, then. The throne's been expecting you longer than you know."

* * *

The court beneath Thornfell did not look like a court. It looked like a harvest.

Rosalind walked out of the gate-passage into a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor, and everywhere he looked there were souls — or the shells that souls leave behind when they've been sold and spent. They stood in rows in fields of pale lichen-light: men and women in fine clothes grown threadbare, children who weren't children anymore, their faces smooth and pleasant and utterly vacant, like houses with all the lamps lit but nobody home. A few moved among them wearing the gray robes of undercourt stewards, feeding them, guiding their hands, murmuring instructions. The hollowed obeyed beautifully. They had nothing left with which to disobey.

A steward approached Rosalind — a narrow man with the careful smile of a shopkeeper greeting a customer he suspects can't afford anything. "Name?"

"I'm expected."

"Everyone is expected. Names?"

Rosalind hesitated. Somewhere between the first gate and this one, Sefa's parting advice had lodged in him like a splinter: *down here, a name is a leash-ring.* "Halloran," he said finally, because lying to the arithmetic seemed unwise, and because some stubborn scrap of pride insisted that if he was going to walk into hell, he'd walk in under his own banner.

The steward consulted nothing — there was no ledger in his hands — and yet his smile widened by a precise, unsettling degree. "Ah. *The* Halloran. Yes. Thirteen years of interest, all come due at once. The throne does enjoy an anniversary." He beckoned. "Come. You're granted audience. Very few living guests are granted audience. Try to look grateful."

"What happens to them?" Rosalind asked, nodding at the rows of empty faces as he fell into step. "The ones in the field."

"They serve. Gently. Forever." The steward said it the way a farmer might say *the wheat grows*. "Souls are labor, friend. Memories are currency. The throne runs the whole exchange. Up above they call us myth. Down here we call upstairs 'accounts receivable.'"

It was meant as humor. Rosalind didn't laugh. The steward didn't seem to mind; he'd probably forgotten what laughter sounded like, or perhaps he was saving his own somewhere, in a jar, against a rainy century.

They walked through the harvest-field, and Rosalind made himself look. Made himself meet the vacant eyes. One woman turned her head as he passed, tracking him with the slow, aimless attention of a sunflower following a cloud. Her lips moved. No sound came out. He found himself wondering — horribly, helplessly — whether she'd been somebody's mother too.

Then the cavern opened into the throne hall, and every other thought he owned fell off a cliff.

The throne was not a chair. It was a *memory* — the largest single memory Rosalind had ever sensed, a vast slow ocean of recollection coiled into the shape of a seat, glowing faintly at its depths like a lamp seen through deep water. It remembered things older than Greyhollow, older than the crown, older, possibly, than mountains. Sitting in it — or being worn by it, which was closer to the truth — was a girl.

Wren.

Thirteen years underground, and she hadn't aged a day past the image in the merchant's memory: small, straight-backed, hands folded in her lap, wearing a gown of silver-gray that might have been woven from moonlight or moth-wing. She was radiant. That was the word his heart kept reaching for even as his stomach curdled — she glowed, softly, steadily, the way a soul glows when it's been polished by ownership instead of worn by living.

And her eyes were empty.

Not vacant like the field-souls — worse. Hers were *anchored* eyes. Eyes fixed on something only she could see, somewhere behind the world, some single bright thread the court had left her so she'd stay stitched to the throne. Everything else had been taken. This one thing remained, and because it remained, she stayed. A bird tethered by one golden string, perched on the hand that held it.

"Wren," Rosalind said.

Nothing. Not a blink.

"Wren. It's Rosalind. It's—" His voice cracked on his own name, absurdly, as though *he* were the fiction here. He took a step forward and a dozen gray-robed stewards flowed between them like water finding its level. "We used to share a bed in winter because the stove was broken and neither of us would admit we were cold. You bit your nails until you were seven. You named a one-legged pigeon Admiral. Wren, you *named a pigeon*, you cried for three days when it flew off, you—"

Her head tilted. Just slightly. For one wild heartbeat Rosalind thought — hoped — that some brother-shaped echo had reached her through thirteen years of stone and silence.

Then she spoke, and her voice was lovely and light and had absolutely no one home behind it.

"You are expected," she said, in the tone of a door announcing a visitor. "The throne bids you welcome, Rosalind Halloran, debtor of record. Your account stands settled. Your presence is... noted."

"Noted?" He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Thirteen years ago someone signed *my name* to sell you, and I'm *noted*?"

"You are noted." A pause. Then, softer, with something flickering far down in those anchored eyes — a fish glimpsed under ice, there and gone: "...Rosalind?"

"Yes," he breathed. "Yes, Wren, it's me—"

But the flicker guttered out. Whatever the court allowed her — whatever single memory they'd left dangling as her leash — it wasn't him. Or it was only a sliver of him, a fragment kept deliberately out of reach, close enough to keep her soul from wandering off entirely, far enough that she could never pull herself free with it. He watched her hands unfold and refold in her lap, restless, a body remembering an itch the mind had been forbidden to name.

*They left her enough of me to hurt,* he realized. *Just enough to hurt.*

Something in Rosalind Halloran went very quiet and very cold, the way a forge goes quiet right before the hammer falls.

He had spent his whole life stealing small things: afternoons, secrets, the taste of other people's weddings. Petty theft. Pocket change. But the throne before him ran on theft the way cities ran on water, and it had committed, over centuries, the greatest heist imaginable — it had stolen a girl and called it accounting.

Fine, then. Two could play at arithmetic.

Somewhere in that coiled ocean of memory sat the anchor — the one recollection holding Wren to the throne like a hook through a fish's jaw. Steal that, and the whole account collapsed. He didn't know yet where the vault was, or how it was guarded, or what a soul cost when the books came due. He knew only that Sefa's thirteenth moon was turning overhead, beyond miles of rock, and that he had twelve of its sisters left to work with.

"Is there anything else?" asked Wren's lovely empty voice. "Audience is concluded."

"Yeah," said Rosalind quietly, meeting those anchored eyes with eyes of his own that had learned, over years of professional practice, exactly where people kept the things they loved most. "There's one more item on the agenda."

He smiled at the throne — at the vast, slow, ancient memory wearing a little girl like a glove — and let it see the smile.

"I've come to rob you."

Far down in the throne's luminous depths, something stirred. And high above, through stone and silence, the twelfth moon began its long climb toward fullness.
