## 9. The Theft of a Soul

Rosalind had stolen a great many things in his life — purses, secrets, the occasional reputation — but he had never before stolen anything while standing inside a cathedral made of moonlight and bone.

The throne vault lay beneath the undercourt's great hall, where tonight the thirteenth-moon ceremony would turn. Above him, through layers of living stone, he could hear the court assembling: the hollowed servants shuffling to their places, the nobles of the deep places rustling like moths in velvet, and at the center of it all, Wren — radiant, enthroned, empty-eyed — being carried down the processional stair on a litter of silver birch.

*Twelve moons I counted,* he thought. *Thirteen was always going to be a thief's number.*

He crouched behind a pillar of veined quartz and did what he did best: he listened.

Two vault-wardens stood at the door ahead. They were not men, exactly. They had been men once, perhaps, before the court wore them down to their duties. One of them was complaining about his feet.

"My feet," it said to the other, "have been aching for four hundred years."

"Have you tried sitting?"

"I am a warden of the eternal vault, Grel. Sitting is not in my nature."

"Well," said Grel, "neither is walking, from the look of you."

Rosalind allowed himself a small, guilty smile. Even here, even now, there was something almost homey about two old souls grumbling at each other in the dark. It reminded him of Bram. It reminded him of every fence and smuggler and cutpurse who'd ever complained about their feet on a long night's work.

Then he remembered what he was here to do, and the smile went out of him like a snuffed candle.

The anchor memory was kept in the heart of the vault, suspended in a lantern-glass no bigger than an egg. Sefa had described it to him: *the court does not chain her body, Rosalind. Chains rust. They keep one memory — the memory that makes her *hers* — and as long as they hold it, she will sit on any throne they point her at and call it home.* He had asked which memory. Sefa had shaken her head. *That is the cruelty of it. You'll know it when you see it, because it will be the one that breaks your heart.*

The wardens' argument about feet reached a natural pause. Rosalind slipped from the pillar, crossed the vault floor on the balls of his feet — a trick he'd learned at nine years old, fleeing a baker with a rolling pin — and pressed himself into the shadow of the inner door just as the wardens turned to greet the procession above.

The lock was a knot of black iron grown into the stone, and it was not locked with a key. It was locked with a price. He could feel it humming against his palm, patient and enormous: *something given, freely.*

He'd paid at both gates already. His happiest night. His mother's face. He was running low on treasures; that was rather the point of a life like his. But he still had things. Small things. The smell of rain on Greyhollow rooftops. The sound of Bram calling him an idiot, fondly, over cards.

He gave the lock the smell of rain.

It wasn't enough — he felt it weigh the gift and find it wanting — but it opened anyway, slowly, grudgingly, the way Bram opened his door when Rosalind came asking for loans. *A down payment,* the lock seemed to say. *We will discuss the balance later.*

Rosalind stepped into the heart of the vault.

And there it was. A lantern of glass on a pedestal of salt-white stone, and inside it, glowing softly, a memory. He didn't need Sefa's warning. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

A kitchen. Morning light. A girl of six years standing on a chair so she could reach the counter, flour on her nose, laughing at something — laughing at *him*, at some joke he couldn't hear anymore because the memory belonged to her, not him, and this was her version of it. In her version, he was tall and safe and funny, and the world was a kitchen with bread rising, and nothing bad had ever happened to anyone anywhere.

*Wren's version of me,* he thought. *That's what they took. That's what they've been holding all these years.*

Not a leash made of fear. A leash made of *this*. Of the belief that someone loved her enough to come back for her.

His hands were shaking when he lifted the lantern off its pedestal.

* * *

Above, in the world of air and weather, Veyra Crane stood in the mouth of the Thornfell pass with a crown warrant in one hand and a conscience in open revolt.

Behind her: the hunt. Twelve inquisitors, forty soldiers, and Master Quillon of the Office of Records, who smelled like ink and self-satisfaction and who had been explaining the protocol for soul-retrieval to her for the better part of an hour.

"—and of course the vessel must be delivered intact," Quillon was saying. "The throne prefers them breathing. Though frankly, Crane, after the Halloran business, I'd have thought you'd be relieved to hand the whole mess upward. You've been *odd* lately. Distracted. One might say—"

"One might say many things," Veyra said. "One might also fall down this mountain. Accidents happen in the passes."

Quillon shut up. He was a records man; he knew when an entry was about to be altered.

Veyra looked out at the darkening sky. The thirteenth moon was rising, fat and wrong-colored, the color of a bruise healing. Somewhere below her feet, a man she had hunted across half the kingdom was doing something either very brave or very stupid, and she had decided — insofar as she could point to the exact moment of deciding, which she could not, because the decision had apparently been making itself in her for weeks — that she was going to help him do it.

She thought about the needle. She thought about the memory she had pulled from his safehouse: a girl named Wren, gap-toothed, daring him to jump off the mill wall. And she thought about the other thing she'd found in that house, the thing she had told no one: a child's ledger page, thirteen years old, recording a sale. A soul purchased by the undercourt. Paid for with the name *Halloran*.

The crown hadn't been hunting memory-thieves. The crown had been hunting *inventory*.

"Crane," said one of her inquisitors, a young woman called Pell, whose talent was finding lies. Pell was looking at her with an expression Veyra recognized, because she'd worn it herself for twenty years: the look of someone who has noticed the cage and is pretending it's a coat. "The order says we seal the pass behind us. No one comes out until the delivery is made."

"Yes," Veyra said. "The order says that."

"And are we... going to follow it?"

Veyra considered the question honestly, which was more than the order deserved.

"Pell," she said, "how would you feel about a career change?"

* * *

In the vault, Rosalind got exactly three steps toward the door with the lantern before the throne noticed.

He felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. The whole mountain drew breath. The salt-stone pedestal began to glow. And a voice filled the vault — not loud, worse than loud, *reasonable*, the voice of a creditor who has all the time in the world.

**THAT IS NOT YOURS.**

"No," Rosalind agreed, hugging the lantern to his chest. "It's hers. That's rather the point."

**THE BARGAIN WAS STRUCK. THE SOUL WAS SOLD. THIRTEEN MOONS HAVE TURNED.**

"Twelve," Rosalind said. "You've been rounding up. Very like a creditor." He started backing toward the door. "I've read your contract, your holiness. Sefa read it to me twice. The soul returns if reclaimed *before* the thirteenth moon turns full. And look—" he tipped his head toward the distant ceiling, beyond which the wrong-colored moon was still climbing, "—still turning. I've stolen from faster clocks than you."

Silence. Then, horribly, the voice grew warmer.

**A DEBT IS A DEBT. BUT THE COURT IS GENEROUS. RETURN WHAT YOU CARRY, AND WE WILL FORGET YOUR TRESPASS. OR—** the warmth curdled, **—REPLACEMENT. A SOUL OF EQUAL WEIGHT MAY STAND IN HER PLACE. THAT IS ALSO IN THE CONTRACT. YOU READ IT TWICE.**

Rosalind stopped walking.

He had read it twice. He had hoped, in the way you hope about the fine print of your own life, that the clause wouldn't apply. But the throne was right, and the throne knew it, and somewhere beneath his fear a cold clear part of him — the part that had survived Greyhollow, the part that counted coins in the dark — did the arithmetic.

Equal weight. A soul of equal weight.

There was only one soul in this vault besides Wren's.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, you clever, patient horror. This is why you let me get this far."

**WE LET ALL DEBTORS COME HOME,** said the throne. **IT IS ONLY FAIR.**

Rosalind looked down at the lantern in his arms. Inside it, a six-year-old girl laughed in a sunlit kitchen, flour on her nose, certain the world was good because her brother had never once let her down.

He thought: *if I put it back, she sits on that throne forever, empty-eyed, and I walk out alive, and I will spend every remaining day of my life knowing exactly what I chose.*

He thought: *if I don't put it back—*

Well. There wasn't really an *if*. There was only the heartbeat between the question and the answer, and Sefa had warned him about that too: *the throne counts on hesitation, Rosalind. Debtors hesitate. Thieves don't.*

He was, whatever else could be said of him, a very good thief.

"Done," said Rosalind Halloran, and set the lantern down on the pedestal with a tenderness that surprised him, and stepped back, and spread his empty hands. "Take the replacement. But I want it noted — loudly, in writing, wherever you keep your contracts — that the debt is *paid*. All of it. Hers and mine. She walks out of this mountain owing you nothing, and neither does anyone whose name ends in Halloran."

**NOTED,** said the throne, and the vault filled with light.

It didn't hurt, which was the strangest part. He had expected pain. Instead it felt like being *read* — like the most thorough, patient, merciless reader in the world opening him cover to cover. His name went first. Then his debts, which were many. Then Greyhollow, and Bram's card table, and the smell of rain, and the mill wall, and—

Wait. Not everything. Something was being left. Something small and stubborn, tucked away where even a throne's reading eye skimmed past — a splinter of himself, unclaimed, unpriced. He felt it the way you feel a coin left forgotten in a winter coat.

*Rosalind,* he thought, grasping at his own name as it went. *My name is Rosalind. My sister is Wren. My sister is free. My sister is free. My sister is—*

The light took the rest.

* * *

Far above, at the moment the thirteenth moon reached its fullness, Veyra Crane drew her sword, turned to face the hunt she had led for a hundred days, and said, in the flat tone she reserved for verdicts:

"Gentlemen. The warrant is void. The debt is paid. Anyone who disagrees can take it up with me."

Pell, standing beside her, found no lie in it anywhere.

And down in the deeps, in a kitchen-memory newly returned to her, a girl of nineteen woke on a throne with flour on her ghost-nose and one word burning in her chest like a coal she refused to drop:

*Rosalind.*

She stood up. The hollowed servants flinched. The throne, sated and silent, did not stop her.

Wren Halloran walked out of the vault carrying her brother's last unclaimed splinter in the palm of her hand — and began, step by step, to climb.
