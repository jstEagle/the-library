## 10. A Stranger at the Door

The mountain let them go grudgingly, the way a miser lets go of a coin — one reluctant inch at a time.

Wren climbed with Veyra Crane a half-step ahead of her, because Veyra walked like someone who had spent her whole life descending and hadn't yet learned how to come up. The inquisitor's coat was scorched at the hem. Her hands were bandaged where the needle had finally turned on its mistress. She did not complain once, which Wren decided was either courage or habit, and she suspected habit.

"You're limping," Veyra said, without turning around.

"I'm *ascending dramatically*. There's a difference."

"Hm." A pause, stones shifting under boots. "Your brother dramatized too. It's in the memories."

Wren stopped walking.

She had been carrying them for three days now — Rosalind's remaining memories, folded into her the way you'd carry a stranger's letters through hostile country. They sat behind her ribs like birds that hadn't decided whether to sing. Most of the time she could ignore them. Sometimes one would flutter, and she'd be suddenly, violently *somewhere else*: a rooftop in Greyhollow at fourteen, laughing so hard she couldn't breathe; a cellar full of candle stubs; a hand smoothing hair back from her forehead while fever burned.

"He does," she said quietly. "Did. He *did*."

Veyra turned then. In the grey morning light her face was all angles and old decisions, but her eyes — Wren had learned to read those eyes on the climb, the way you learn to read weather — her eyes were careful.

"We'll find him," Veyra said. "Men who trade in memory leave traces. Even men who've traded away themselves."

"Especially," Wren said, "men who've traded away themselves. Bram told me once — well, he told *him*, but I have it now — that a memory never really leaves the world. It just changes pockets."

They climbed on.

* * *

The thirteenth moon had turned two nights ago, full and white over the mouth of Thornfell, and Wren had stood in the snow and watched it set with her brother's memories pressed against her heart like contraband. Which, she supposed, they were.

She remembered almost nothing of herself before the throne. Thirteen years of being *Wren-the-enthroned*, radiant and hollow, a lamp with no candle in it. What came back when Rosalind pulled the anchor-memory free was not her childhood — that was gone, sold or eaten or worn smooth by the court's long handling — but something better and worse: his memories *of* her. She knew herself now only as he had known her. Every piece of Wren Halloran that existed in the world had passed through her brother first.

And then he had given away the rest.

She had screamed at him. She wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the undercourt kept of debts and payments: *she screamed*. She had pounded her fists on his chest and called him every name their mother — whose face he no longer had — might have used, and he had held her by the shoulders and smiled with an expression she recognized from a hundred borrowed moments, and said:

"Somebody has to walk out. You're the one with the soul."

As if it were arithmetic. As if she wouldn't spend the rest of her life doing the sums.

* * *

They found him on the fourth day, on the old smuggler's road below Thornfell, sitting on a milestone and eating an apple with the concentration of a man performing surgery.

He looked up when they approached, and Wren felt the memories behind her ribs go still — every bird holding its breath.

He was Rosalind. Of course he was Rosalind; the throne had taken his name and his years and his mother's face out of his head, but it could not take the shape of him, the way he tilted his chin when he was assessing a stranger for weaknesses, the crooked half-smile already forming because here were two strangers and strangers meant opportunities. His eyes went from Veyra's scorched coat to Wren's face and settled there, kind and curious and entirely empty of recognition.

"Afternoon," he said. "You look like people having a very bad week. I'd offer you the rest of this apple, but I'm fairly sure it's the only thing I own."

Wren laughed. It came out broken in the middle, and she didn't try to fix it.

"Do you know your name?" she asked.

He considered the apple. "No. Do you? Because if you do, I should warn you I'm prepared to negotiate. I have," he patted his pockets, "half an apple."

"It's Rosalind. Rosalind Halloran."

Something moved across his face — not recognition, nothing so generous, but the shadow of a door trying to open in a wall that had been bricked over. He frowned at the middle distance.

"Halloran," he repeated. "That feels... expensive. Like a word I used to be able to afford."

"You could always afford it," Wren said. "It was everyone else who couldn't."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the milestone, because Veyra refused — *refused*, with the full authority of a woman who had recently committed treason against a crown and an underworld simultaneously — to travel the pass in the dark. Rosalind built the fire without being asked, arranging the wood with a thief's economy, and Wren watched him do it and grieved and rejoiced in the same breath, which she was learning was simply what love cost now.

"So," he said, settling across the flames. "I don't want to alarm anyone, but I have the distinct impression you two know me and I don't know you, and one of you keeps looking at me like I'm a letter she's afraid to open."

"That's accurate," Veyra said.

"And the other one?"

Wren hugged her knees. "I'm your sister."

The fire popped. He looked at her for a long time — and here was the thing, the terrible, unbearable, wonderful thing: even empty, even stripped, he looked at her *gently*. Whatever the throne had taken, it had left the kindness in, like a house gutted by fire that somehow spares the hearth.

"I believe you," he said at last. "Isn't that strange? I have no reason to. But something in me says: yes, her, obviously, her before anyone." He rubbed his chest, right over the sternum, where the memories used to live. "There's a hole here shaped like thirteen years. Did I give them away?"

"You gave away everything," Wren said. "To buy me back."

"Was it worth it?"

"Yes."

"Well then." He nodded, satisfied, as if she'd confirmed the price of turnips. Then the humor faded and he looked into the fire. "What do I do now? I keep reaching for things that aren't there. Names. Faces. There was a fence — I can feel him, gray beard, smelled like pipe smoke and bad decisions — but I can't find his name. There was a festival. There was—" his hand pressed flat against his chest again, harder, "—there was a night, I think, that mattered more than all the others, and it's just *gone*, and I hate it, I hate having lost something I can't even miss properly—"

"Rosalind." Wren moved around the fire and took his hand off his chest and held it. "You didn't lose it. You *spent* it. And I was carrying it, and I brought it home."

* * *

She gave him pieces slowly, over the following days, because Veyra advised it — "a man who swallows a river drowns; give him a cup at a time" — and because Wren needed the time too. Each memory she returned was a small resurrection and a small funeral. She got to watch his face light up with their mother's laugh, and then she had to admit that she herself had no memory of that laugh except through him. She gave him Old Bram and the cellar of candles, and he wept, and she wept, and Veyra sat apart sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, which was her way of weeping.

Some things she kept back. Not out of selfishness — or not only. The festival night, the happiest one, the one he'd paid at the first gate: she kept that a while longer, turning it over in the dark like a coin she wasn't ready to spend. If she gave him everything at once, he'd be whole and she'd be the hollow one, a girl built entirely out of other people's recollections. She wasn't proud of the thought. But she was honest about it, at least, and she suspected honesty counted for something in a family that had traded in secrets.

On the sixth day he caught her at it.

They were walking down through the foothills toward Greyhollow, toward smoke and criers and a city that had recently learned to fear its gifted, and he stopped on the road and said, "You're rationing me."

"I'm *preserving* you."

"Wren." He said her name carefully, tasting it, and she realized he'd been practicing. "I may be mostly an empty house, but I know furniture when I see it. You're keeping something. Something big. You get this look — I apparently taught you that look, by the way, you do it exactly like me — when you're deciding whether to lie to a mark."

"You're not a mark."

"Everyone's a mark," he said cheerfully. "The question is only what you're selling." Then, softer: "Whatever it is. Give it to me when you're ready. Or don't. I'm told I'm the sort of man who builds himself out of whatever's at hand." He glanced back up the mountain, at Thornfell's jagged teeth against the sky, and his jaw tightened. "But tell me one thing. The thing I bought you back *with*. Was it good? The memory I paid. Was it worth a life?"

Wren thought of the festival: lanterns on the canal, Wren twelve years old and certain the world was made of light, Rosalind winning her a paper crown from a rigged ring-toss and then picking the barker's pocket out of principle.

"It was the best night of either of our lives," she said. "Twice, now. Once yours, once mine."

He smiled — the crooked half-smile, the real one, the one no throne could mint or melt — and offered her his arm like a gentleman, which he had absolutely never been.

"Then walk me into the city," he said, "and start from the beginning. I want to hear about this Rosalind Halloran. By all accounts he owed a great many debts."

"He did," Wren said, taking his arm. "And I intend to collect every one."

Behind them, far up the pass, something in the mountain's dark mouth glimmered — faint, patient, like an eye opening. Veyra saw it too, and her hand went to the needle at her belt, and none of them spoke of it.

But the debt-brokers of Greyhollow would soon learn that the Hallorans were back in business.
