## 1. The Weight of Entries

The trouble with impossible things, Isandro Zhou had learned in their first year at the Glass Steppe, was not that they existed. It was that they arrived without receipts.

Isandro sat in the counting room above the gatehouse with the autumn ledger open before them and a pot of ink gone the color of old bruises. Outside, wind moved through the grass of the Steppe in long silver waves, and somewhere below, a client was arguing with the door-warden about tariffs. Isandro could hear it faintly through the floor — a merchant's voice climbing toward outrage, the warden's flat replies. They did not go down to intervene. The ledgers came first. The ledgers always came first.

*Entry 4,112,* they wrote. *One storm, bottled. Seller: the Weatherwright of Callowmere. Buyer: withheld by request. Condition: agitated.*

The bottle itself sat on the shelf behind them, third from the left, wrapped in wool and chained twice because the Weatherwright had insisted once was an insult. It had been raining inside the glass since Tuesday. Isandro had checked. They checked everything.

That was the whole of their office, if you asked the guild masters: keeper of the books, keeper of the gate, a person made of small careful marks. But Isandro knew better. A ledger was not a record. A ledger was a promise that things could be found again — that nothing traded, nothing lost, nothing *hidden* would ever fully escape the account. The Glass Steppe dealt in impossibilities precisely because someone here believed impossibilities deserved bookkeeping. Otherwise you got the capital's way of doing things, which was to call impossible things treason and burn them.

Isandro turned back two hundred pages, to spring, and ran a finger down the entries until they found the one that still bothered them.

*Entry 2,907. One year of life, borrowed. Lender: unnamed. Borrower: a woman of Vhalen, aged sixty-one. Term: one year, repayment deferred to her heirs. Interest: to be negotiated at collection.*

No one had signed as lender. That alone should have stopped the trade. But the guild master then had been Master Orenn, who was soft in his last months and had waved it through, and now the entry sat there like a splinter. Somewhere in Vhalen a woman was living a year she had not earned, and somewhere — nowhere Isandro could point to — something was waiting for its interest. Isandro had written *collection pending* in the margin three times, each time pressing harder, as though ink could hurry fate.

Then there was the door.

*Entry 3,551. One door, blue, sold to Lord Haskin of the Marches.* And beneath it, in different ink, dated four months later: *Entry 3,990. One door, blue, sold to the Widow Sarel of Dunlow.*

Same door. Isandro had measured the description against itself a dozen times — the iron hinges shaped like herons, the crack running from the keyhole to the lower left corner like a river delta. Sold twice. Doors were not supposed to do that. Doors were supposed to be singular, obedient things, standing where you put them. But this one kept turning up in two places at once, and both buyers swore theirs was the original, and when Isandro had sent a runner to verify, the runner had come back pale and said he'd knocked on Lord Haskin's door and heard knocking answer from the other side.

Isandro had logged that too. *Note: door may be aware.*

Below them, the argument ended. Coins, probably. Arguments at the Steppe always ended in coins or silence, never in blood. That was the covenant: the Steppe took no sides, sold no weapons, opened no gates. Neutrality was the mortar between every stone of the place. Isandro had been raised on it the way other children were raised on prayers.

They had been a foundling, after all. Left at the gatehouse steps one frozen morning with no name but the one stitched into their blanket — *Zhou*, which meant nothing in any language the guild masters knew, so they kept it, the way you keep a coat that fits. The guild had weighed them like everything else. *One child, unpriced. Asset: meticulous. Disposition: keep.* Isandro had read their own entry at age nine and understood, with a clarity that never quite left, that they were a line item who had learned to hold the pen.

They did not resent it. Mostly.

The bell over the counting room door rang once — the small bell, meaning a servant, not a client. Tam, the youngest archivist, put his head in. He was fifteen and afraid of everything except the ledgers, which is why Isandro liked him.

"Keeper. There's dust on the east shelves again. And—" he hesitated, "the gate hummed last night."

"The gate always hums."

"It hummed *differently*." Tam said it the way people report a dog growling at an empty corner. "Low. Like it was clearing its throat."

"Log it," Isandro said. "Time, duration, pitch if you can manage it."

"You think I should log a *hum*?"

"I think," Isandro said, dipping the pen, "that the day we stop logging hums is the day a hum becomes something worse and we have no record of when it started."

Tam went away looking like a boy who'd been handed a philosophy instead of an answer. Isandro returned to Entry 4,113 — a crate of bottled storms' *echoes*, whatever those were; the seller had been vague and the buyer vaguer — and worked until the light through the west window went amber and then gray. This was the shape of their days, and they loved it, and they trusted it, and if you had told Isandro Zhou that evening what the evening actually held, they would have checked your claim against three sources and found it wanting.

* * *

The stranger came at dusk, when the Steppe's lamps were being lit and the grass had turned the color of tarnished silver.

Isandro was in the gatehouse, performing the closing audit — the ritual they had performed every night for eleven years, without variation, because variation was how gates got opened. Count the wards. Check the seals. Confirm the bar. The gate itself was a black slab of some stone that wasn't stone, taller than three men, set into the gatehouse wall, and it had been shut for as long as the guild had kept records. Longer. The founding charter said only: *This gate opens inward, and must not.* No reason given. Founders rarely gave reasons; they gave rules and expected gratitude.

Isandro laid a palm flat against the seal plate, as required, and felt — nothing, as always. Cold. Dumb. Safe.

Then the watch-horn sounded from the outer road. Two notes. Then a third, ragged, wrong — the signal no one used, the one that meant *traveler in distress, judgment to the warden's discretion.*

Isandro went out into the wind.

The man had fallen twice before reaching the lamp-line; Isandro could see the dark streaks he'd left on the pale road-stones. He was maybe fifty, dressed in the mud-colored clothes of a carter, and he was holding his left arm against his chest at an angle arms don't go. Two wardens flanked him with staves leveled, uncertain, because the covenant said no traveler was refused and also said wounded strangers were risks, and the covenant did not reconcile the two.

"Please," the man said. His voice was shredded. "I need — I was told — the keeper of the ledgers. Only the keeper."

"I'm the keeper," Isandro said.

He looked at them then, and Isandro saw that his eyes were the problem. Not wild, not mad. *Certain*. He looked at Isandro the way a drowning man looks at a rope, and that was worse than madness.

"They said you'd know what it means." He fumbled at his collar with his good hand, and drew out something folded small and flat, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire. "I carried it from the capital. Eleven days. They killed the others who carried it. I didn't know why I was carrying it. I still don't. Please. You have to see it. It has your name in it."

"My name," Isandro repeated.

"In the hand," the man whispered. "It's in your hand."

The wardens glanced at each other. Isandro took the package. The oilcloth was stiff with old sweat and something darker. Inside, folded twice, was a page of ledger paper — the heavy cream stock, the double rule at the head, the guild watermark that only the Steppe's own presses made. Torn roughly along the spine-edge. Torn from a bound volume.

Torn from *their* volumes.

Isandro knew the paper the way they knew their own pulse. And the hand writing across it was their own — the slight forward slant, the way the crossbar of the *t* hooked left, the numeral fours made with the closed top that Master Orenn had spent a year beating out of them and failed. Every habit of their pen, present. Every entry they had ever written began the same way, and so did this:

*Entry —*

But the entry number was blank. And the date was not any date Isandro had lived.

*Entry — . Dated: the third night of the Frost Moon, three years hence.*

Three years hence. Isandro stood under the lamp with the wind pulling at their coat and read the rest, and the world went very quiet, the way it does before a storm decides.

The handwriting that was theirs recorded, in their own meticulous voice, with their own marginal notes and their own habit of pressing harder on words that mattered:

*Entry — . Tonight the gate opens. I am the one who opens it. I have weighed it every way the guild taught me, and it cannot be balanced, and I will do it anyway. Forgive me the entry number. I will not live to file it.*

At the bottom, in the same hand, smaller, almost an afterthought — the way Isandro annotated everything, the way they had annotated *door may be aware* — were four words:

*Do not let me.*

The carter was watching their face. "What does it say?" he breathed. "Eleven days I've carried it. What does it say?"

Isandro did not answer. They were looking past him, up the dark road toward the capital, and then — slowly, because some part of them already knew, because some part of them had known since Tam reported the hum — they turned and looked at the gate.

The black slab stood silent in its wall. Sealed. Barred. Cold as it had been for three hundred years.

And beneath the wind and the lamps and the carter's ragged breathing, Isandro heard it — low, patient, unmistakable.

The gate was humming.

And it was humming in time with their own heartbeat.
