## 2. What the Crown Proclaims

The ink was still wet when Maren Voss understood that the kingdom had ended, though the proclamation announcing it would not reach the streets until morning.

She stood in the Scriptorium of the Royal Archive, third gallery, at a desk assigned to her eleven years ago and never reassigned despite three petitions for a window. The lamps were trimmed low — wax was rationed now, another small sign nobody wished to read — and she copied by their amber light with the steady hand that had made her, at thirty-one, the youngest principal transcriber in the archive's history.

*By order of His Majesty Aldric IV, rightful sovereign of the Nine Thrones and Protector of the Marches: let it be proclaimed throughout the realm that all persons possessed of the Gift, howsoever manifested, shall present themselves before the King's assessors within forty days.*

Her nib paused at *possessed*. The draft that had come up from the Chancery two days ago had said *blessed*. Someone below her had changed it, or someone above her had changed it back, and either way the word told her more than the whole first paragraph. You registered what you blessed. You hunted what you possessed.

She kept copying.

*...and upon registration, each such person shall be rendered unto the Crown's service as the Crown shall determine, without appeal, petition, or delay.*

There it was again. *Rendered.* Not conscripted. Not enrolled. Rendered, which in the old liturgical registers meant one thing only: the reduction of a living thing into its usable parts. Tallow from flesh. Ink from gall. She had transcribed enough sacrificial accounts from the Third Dynasty to know the word did not wander into proclamations by accident.

Maren set down her pen, flexed her cramping fingers, and allowed herself the luxury of looking at the decree the way an archivist looks at anything — not as a text but as an artifact. Where had it come from? Who had drafted it? The fair copy she'd received bore the Chancery seal and the signature of Lord Chancellor Hesse, whose handwriting she knew well enough to forge, a skill she kept private. But beneath the Chancery's tidy modern script, the decree's structure was wrong. Wrong for this reign. Wrong, in fact, for any reign she had catalogued.

It began with an invocation. Modern decrees began with the king's style and titles, because modern kingship was a legal fiction and legal fictions required preamble. This began: *Before the Throne and behind it, before the Crown and within it.* That was not law. That was liturgy. And it was liturgy she had seen before, in exactly one place.

The Sealed Stacks.

No one entered the Sealed Stacks. That was not quite true, in the way such statements are never quite true; what was true was that no one *admitted* to entering them, and the keys lived on a ring in the Archon's private cabinet, and the ring had not turned a lock in the Archon's lifetime so far as anyone knew. The catalogue of the Sealed Stacks existed in a single handwritten volume that Maren had been permitted to read — read, only read, standing, gloves on, supervised — during her certification, because the examiners believed an archivist should know what she did not know.

She remembered the entries anyway. She remembered everything; it was her gift, in the small unregistered sense of the word, the sense that had gotten scholarship girls mocked and promoted in equal measure. Entry after entry of folios designated with the old sigla: *Veth*, *Corunne*, the black *Amaranthus*. And one line she had thought about perhaps twice in nine years and was thinking about now with her heart going hard against her ribs:

*Amaranthus VII: Rites of Succession and Substitution. Liturgical fragments, pre-dynastic. Language archaic Vhalori. Access: none.*

*Pre-dynastic.* Before Aldric the First raised the capital out of the river marsh. Before there were Nine Thrones to be rightful sovereign of. The invocation in tonight's decree — *before the Throne and behind it, before the Crown and within it* — was four hundred years older than the crown that now claimed it.

Someone had opened the Amaranthus. Someone had taken rites out of the sealed dark and fed them through the Chancery like ordinary law, trusting that no clerk alive could recognize the bones under the skin.

No clerk except her.

Maren sat very still in the lamplight and listened to the building breathe. The Royal Archive breathed at night; every old building does, stone expanding and contracting its slow sighs, pipes ticking, the settling groans of a structure carrying more paper than people. Somewhere below, the night porters were playing dice. Above her, three floors of catalogued history slept in their cases, and beneath everything, down stairs that officially did not exist, the Sealed Stacks held their breath along with her.

She should finish the copy. Forty-one copies were required by dawn — one for each district crier, one for each temple, one for the university gates where students would read it and understand that half their classmates had become criminals overnight. Her section had drawn the task because the decree's language was too archaic for junior clerks. She had laughed at that, two days ago, when the assignment came down. *They think we need scholars for a manhunt.*

She was not laughing now. She dipped her pen, and her hand — her precise, celebrated, incorruptible hand — wrote something that was not on the original.

In the margin, small, in the shorthand cipher she had invented at nineteen and shared with no one: *Invocation matches Amaranthus VII. Pre-dynastic rite. "Render" = liturgical reduction. Source: sealed. Query: who has access?*

Answer: the Archon. The Archon's shadow. And whoever the Archon answered to, which, in the last year, seemed increasingly to be no one at court she could name. The Archon had stopped attending council. The Archon's windows stayed lit past terce. Twice she had passed his door and heard him speaking to someone in a room she knew was empty — speaking in cadences, rising and falling, like a man reading aloud, or reciting.

Or like a man being dictated to.

Maren looked at the marginal note for a long moment. Then she took up her penknife and scraped the page clean, because she was careful, because she had survived eleven years in the capital's serpent pit by being careful — and because scraping it away changed nothing. She already knew. Knowledge is the one entry you cannot strike from your own ledger.

* * *

She worked until the fourth bell, copying the decree with flawless fidelity, and then she did the thing she had decided to do somewhere around the second bell without ever quite deciding it, the way you fall asleep.

She went down.

The night stairs to the sublevels were locked; she had the lock's measure from years of passing it, and a bent wire from the binding of a damaged folio served where courage alone would not. The porter's dice game was audible through the wall, which meant the porter was not at his post, which meant the corridor beyond was hers.

The Sealed Stacks did not look like much. That was the horror of them, she thought later: they looked like every other gallery, iron shelving, oilcloth covers, the same smell of dust and leather — except colder, cold enough that her breath showed, and quieter, quiet in the way of a place where sound had been paid for and none remained. No rats. She realized that only afterward. Eleven years in the oldest building in the capital, and she had never once seen a rat below the third stair. Whatever was kept here, even the vermin declined it.

The catalogue had given her a bay and a case. *Amaranthus VII.* She found the case empty.

Not misplaced. Not borrowed-without-leave. Empty, and recently — the shelf dust showed a clean rectangle where the folio had rested, sharp-edged, undisturbed around its borders, the way a print shows when a book is lifted straight up and nowhere else. Taken within days. Perhaps within hours.

Maren stood in the cold with her lamp shaking slightly in her grip and made herself think. If the Amaranthus was gone upstairs into the Chancery's machinery, then the source was spent, used, burned through like fuel — and the decree downstairs was its ash. But the Amaranthus was seven volumes. The catalogue said so. *Amaranthus I through IX,* with gaps at III and VI that even the sealed catalogue marked *lost prior to record.*

Seven volumes minus one left six, and six volumes of pre-dynastic succession rites did not go missing. They went *somewhere*, carried by someone, and if the person who took them had also written the decree —

Then whoever wrote the decree was not finished.

Her eye caught the edge of something beneath the empty case, wedged where the shelf met the frame. A scrap of vellum, thin as onion skin. She drew it out with tweezers from her sleeve — always tweezers, always — and held it to the light.

It was a fragment of a flyleaf, and on it, in a hand far older than the dynasty, ran a single legible line amid the decay:

*...the crown drinks and the bearer pours, until the vessel is dry and another is chosen...*

Below it, in a different ink entirely — brown-black, recent, unmistakably human — someone had written a date. Not next year. Not five years hence.

Next month.

Maren put the fragment inside her shirt, against her skin, where it felt less like paper than like a coal. Then she stood in the cold dark of a room that officially did not exist and confronted, with the clarity that was her curse and her craft, the shape of her life.

She could go upstairs. She could finish her forty-one copies. She could report the empty case to the Archon, who would thank her gravely and have her dead by week's end, or have her worse than dead — registered, assessed, *rendered*, because surely the Hunt would not stop at hedge-witches and gifted children once it learned efficiency. A memory like hers, in a kingdom about to start harvesting gifts? She was not a person anymore. She was inventory awaiting appraisal.

Or she could take the other six volumes.

The idea arrived whole, absurd, suicidal, and perfect. Six folios of forbidden rites, stolen from the most guarded room in the realm, carried out of the capital by a woman whose entire criminal experience consisted of returning books late. She almost laughed. What saved the plan from laughter was the arithmetic: the thing wearing the Chancery's authority had needed the Amaranthus once. It would need it again — to check its work, to advance the rite, to feed whatever came *after* the Hunt. Every volume she removed was a volume it could not consult. Every month of confusion was a village of gifted children not yet rendered.

An archivist's whole training reduced to this: control the sources, and you control what can be known. She could not fight the crown. She could bankrupt its library.

She took the volumes. All six, wrapped in her spare shift and the oilcloth from the empty case, packed into a scribe's satchel built for tax rolls, heavy as sin and heavier than her future. The lock relocked behind her. The dice game went on. On the third stair she paused, looked back down into the breathing dark, and whispered — why, she never knew, except that some rooms deserve a eulogy — "Catalogued as lost."

* * *

Dawn found her at the west postern with a travel pass she had forged the night before in the Chancellor's own hand, a skill finally earning its keep, and a story prepared for the gate sergeant involving her mother's illness in the river towns. The sergeant barely looked at her. The city behind her was waking to criers' bells, and she could hear, faintly, the first ragged public reading of the decree rolling down the processional avenue — *all persons possessed of the Gift... rendered unto the Crown's service* — and the particular silence that follows when a crowd hears a word it doesn't understand and understands it anyway.

Forty days. The gifted of the Nine Thrones had forty days to register, and she had perhaps ten before the Archon discovered the gap in his dark and started asking which subordinate had keys, and ambition, and a memory too good for her own safety.

The road north ran toward the river provinces and, beyond them, the free cities. And beyond those — Maren walked and did not let herself hope too loudly — lay the Glass Steppe, that impossible market she had filed long ago under *trade routes, exotic* and never under *sanctuary*. But the ledgers said the Steppe traded in anything. Anything included, presumably, passage. Anything included, surely, a keeper of books who had finally read one worth keeping.

At the first milestone she stopped, took out the flyleaf fragment, and read the recent ink again. The date. Next month. Whatever the crown-rite required, it required soon, and it would require the volumes she had just made the rarest objects in the world.

Somewhere behind her, she knew, riders were already being dispatched. Somewhere ahead, a gate that should stay shut waited for people with no other door.

Maren Voss, principal transcriber of the Royal Archive, thief of the Amaranthus, walked north with six stolen centuries on her back and did not look at the capital again.
