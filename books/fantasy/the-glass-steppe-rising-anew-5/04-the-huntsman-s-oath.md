## 4. The Huntsman's Oath

The girl was fast. That was the first thing Dain Corvale noted about her, and the thing he would remember longest.

She ran through the barley fields outside Mirefen like a fox with hounds behind it, bare feet throwing up dust, and when his riders closed on her left flank she simply *wasn't there* — one stride in the open, the next thirty feet ahead, as though the world had agreed to look away for a moment. A blink-step. Third-rank gift, maybe second. Useful. Trainable, if you believed the manuals he'd memorized at fourteen.

"Net her before the treeline!" Sergeant Hallow barked from the right, and Dain put his heels to his horse because if she reached the trees she'd be gone until autumn, and if she was gone until autumn the Hunt would burn three farms looking for her, and Dain had grown tired of writing *structure damaged, no casualties* in reports that were lies stacked on lies.

He cut the angle. He was still the best horseman in the company — ten years of warrants and nobody had taken that from him yet — and he came out of a drainage ditch at a gallop with the weighted net already loose in his hand. She saw him. He watched her see him, watched her eyes go wide and white, and something in his chest did what it always did lately: flinched, and then obeyed.

The net took her across the shoulders. She went down rolling, screaming words he didn't catch, and by the time the horses circled her she was wrapped to the chin in lead-thread mesh, small hands pressed flat against the weave like she could push the whole world away if she tried hard enough.

"Good throw," Hallow said, riding past without looking at him.

Dain dismounted and knelt beside her. Protocol said keep your distance from the gifted until rendering. Protocol said a lot of things.

"What's your name?" he asked.

She spat at him. Missed. Her hair was full of barley chaff and her ankle was bleeding where the net's weights had caught her, and she looked up at him with the particular fury of a child who has not yet learned that fury changes nothing.

"Dain," Hallow called. "Leave it. Wagon's coming."

He stood. The girl kept her eyes on him the whole time, and he made the mistake — the mistake he would count later, when counting mattered — of looking back.

Her name, he learned from the village reeve they questioned afterward, was Pell. Nine years old. Daughter of a cooper. First in her family ever flagged, which meant her parents would pay a visitation fine and sign a silence bond, and that would be the end of them in the crown's ledgers, a single line: *one rendered, none retained.*

Dain wrote it down himself, because that was his job. The finest huntsman in the king's service, writing children into columns.

* * *

The rendering station at Coldharbour Keep had been a granary once. Dain remembered hauling grain sacks there as a boy on his father's farm two valleys over, back when Coldharbour was just a place with a good roof and bad smells. Now it had iron doors and no windows on the ground floor, and the wagons went in through the south arch and nothing came out but paperwork.

They arrived at dusk. There were eleven captured in the wagon train that week — Pell and an old woman who hummed while she prayed, and twin brothers from the coast whose gift, the manifest said, was *finding things*, which struck Dain as the most harmless talent he'd ever logged, and a fisherman who could call eels, and six more whose crimes were written in the same clerk's hand, tidy and identical: *gifted, unregistered, apprehended.*

Captain Vess met them at the arch. She was new — transferred from the southern command eight months ago, after the old captain retired, or died, or whatever the word was for men who asked too many questions about Coldharbour. She had gray eyes and a voice like a ledger being opened.

"The girl goes first," Vess said. "The spire-master requested a young subject."

Dain heard the word *subject* and felt it land somewhere below his ribs.

"Requested," he repeated. "Since when do spire-masters request?"

"Since the new protocols." Vess handed him a slate, already chalked. "You'll witness. Huntsmen of your rank witness all renderings now. Crown order."

"I've witnessed four renderings," Dain said. "They're transfers. Assessment, classification, transport to the holding houses."

"These aren't transfers anymore, Corvale."

He should have asked then. He wants to believe, in the long accounting he does of himself later — lying awake on the salt roads with his ribs taped and his warrant ash — that there was a version of him who would have asked, right there, in the doorway, and turned his horse around before the worst of it. But the truth is uglier. He read the slate. He signed the witness line. He walked down into the granary floor because he was told to, and because ten years of obedience fits a man like armor, and armor doesn't come off just because you notice it's cold.

* * *

They held Pell in a side chamber overnight, chained to a ring in the floor, under lamp-light so she wouldn't sleep well. Dain brought her water and bread around midnight, against protocol, and sat outside her reach on an overturned crate.

"You should eat," he said.

"They're going to hurt me."

"No. They're going to assess you. Classify you. Then—"

"Then what? You know. I can tell you know." Her voice wasn't furious now. That was worse. "My mam said the Hunt takes you somewhere and gives you back wrong. She said my cousin Tam went with them two winters ago and came home and stood in the yard all night staring at the well, and when his mother shook him he looked at her like a stranger wearing Tam's face." Pell pulled her knees up inside the chain's circle. "Is that true?"

Dain thought about Tam. He'd processed a boy named Tam from a coastal village two winters ago. Finding-gift, minor. Transferred to a holding house in the east. He'd never checked the follow-up. Nobody checked follow-ups. The paperwork went to the spire and stopped.

"I don't know," he said, and it was the most honest sentence he'd spoken in a year.

"My mam said if they ever catch me, I should bite and run and never stop running." She looked at him over her knees. "Why didn't you let me go? In the field. You saw me. I saw you seeing me."

Because protocol. Because the net was in his hand. Because he was the crown's finest huntsman and the finest huntsman catches what he's sent to catch.

"I don't know," he said again.

She ate the bread eventually. She left the water. In the morning they came for her with a wheeled chair and leather straps and a hood, gentle as nurses, and Dain walked behind them down the corridor to the granary floor, and the last thing she said before the hood went on — said quietly, only to him, because she'd decided something about him in the night and he would spend the rest of his life wondering if she'd been right — was:

"You could still open the door."

* * *

The rendering chamber was the granary's old threshing floor, and someone had spent a great deal of crown money making it wrong. Rings of silvered metal were inlaid in concentric circles, and between them ran channels of some dark glassy substance Dain didn't recognize, and above it all hung a framework of iron shaped like a ribcage, big enough to hold a person upright. Chalk marks covered every surface — not the tidy script of clerks but dense, angular glyphs, older than any alphabet Dain knew, older than the dynasty's own heraldry, if the spire-master was to be believed.

The spire-master himself was a narrow man named Ossen Ferris who wore gray robes and spoke to the guards as if they were furniture. He had a case of instruments laid out on a trestle table, and Dain — huntsman, tracker, a man who knew blades the way farmers knew weather — could not identify a single tool in it. Some were needles. One looked like a tuning fork made from finger bones lashed with wire. One was a shallow bowl of black glass that seemed to hold shadow even in lamplight.

"Witness positions," Ferris said, without looking up. Four chalk crosses marked the floor near the wall. Vess took one. Two officers Dain didn't know took others. Dain took the last.

They strapped Pell into the ribcage standing. She had stopped crying, which frightened Dain more than screaming would have. She watched Ferris work with the flat, animal patience of prey that has exhausted its options, and when her eyes found Dain at his chalk cross, he could not make himself look away this time either, and hated himself cleanly, thoroughly, for both the watching and the cowardice braided together in it.

"Subject: female, approximately nine years," Ferris recited to a clerk. "Gift class: kinetic displacement, minor. Yield estimate—" he consulted the black glass bowl, into which he had poured a few drops of her blood, "—adequate. Begin."

"Begin what?" Dain said.

No one answered him. Ferris drew the bone fork along the innermost silver ring and struck it, and the note that came off it wasn't sound. It went through Dain's teeth and the fillings of his soul, a vibration in the floor's deep grain, and the channels of dark glass began to *run*, light moving through them like oil finding its way downhill, and Pell screamed.

Not the scream of the netted girl in the barley. This was thinner, longer, a sound with no bottom to it, and as it stretched the color went out of her. Dain watched it happen and could not stop watching: the brown leaving her hair, the blood leaving her cheeks, the life leaving her eyes — not death, nothing so merciful as death, but a draining, a pouring-out, her small body going pale as skimmed milk from the feet upward while the glass channels brightened and the black bowl filled with something that glowed faintly, softly, like a lamp seen through fog.

"*Stop,*" Dain said.

Ferris struck the fork again.

"She's a *child*—"

"Yield confirmed," Ferris said to the clerk, serene as a man reading rainfall totals. "Kinetic gift, fully expressed. Note the purity — untrained subjects render cleanest. Log the sample for transfer to the capital. Priority seal."

Pell's head dropped forward. The straps held her up. What looked out from under her pale hair when the guards unhooded her afterward was not the girl from the barley field. It was quiet. It was obedient. It was a face like Tam's, like a stranger wearing a child, and when Ferris snapped his fingers she stepped down from the frame without stumbling and stood where she was pointed and waited.

"Remarkable," Vess murmured beside him. Dain hadn't heard her come close. Her gray eyes were fixed on the black bowl with something that was not horror. "Ten years we were told the holding houses were for containment."

"Containment of what?" Dain asked, and his voice came out strange.

Vess finally looked at him, and smiled very slightly, the way a knife smiles. "Of waste, Corvale. Nothing is wasted anymore. Every gift the Hunt harvests feeds the binding. Why do you think the quotas tripled this season?" She turned back toward the frame, toward the pale thing that had been Pell. "The king has great need."

The word *harvest* went through Dain like the fork's note had gone through the floor. He thought of eleven years of warrants. Of the twins who found things, waiting in their cell for their turn on the threshing floor. Of the old woman humming over her prayers. Of columns in his own handwriting: *one rendered, none retained.*

Rendered. Not transferred. Not contained. Poured out like wine into a cup, and the cup carried to the capital, to a king who had great need.

He walked out of Coldharbour Keep on legs that belonged to someone else. In the stable yard the night was enormous and ordinary — stars, wind off the barley, a horse stamping — and Dain Corvale stood in the middle of it and understood that everything he had been for ten years was an instrument, and the instrument had been used, and the hand on it was not the hand he'd sworn it to.

His warrant was in his breast pocket. His name, his rank, the king's seal in red wax. He took it out and read it by starlight — *Dain Corvale, licensed huntsman, empowered to apprehend and deliver* — and the word *deliver* sat on the page like a maggot.

There was a lantern hanging by the tack room. He lit it, and fed the warrant into its flame, and held it until the fire reached his gloves, because letting go too early would have been a mercy he hadn't earned. The wax bubbled. The king's seal curled and went black. Ash drifted off toward the barley.

Behind him, in the keep, the pale thing that had been Pell was sleeping in a locked room, and tomorrow it would ride east in a wagon with a priority seal, and Dain could not open that door. He knew that with a clarity that felt like breaking. One man, one horse, no plan. If he drew steel tonight he'd die in a corridor and the wagons would roll anyway, and the only difference would be one less man riding west to say what he'd seen.

So he said it instead. To whoever might listen. To the stars, to the barley, to the ghost of a girl who had asked him why he didn't let her go.

"It's a harvest," he said aloud. "God forgive us. It's a harvest."

* * *

He rode west through the night and did not stop until the sun was up and Coldharbour was a smudge behind him. By noon he had a plan of sorts, which was to say a direction: the free cities lay beyond the Glass Steppe, past the salt roads, and the free cities did not answer to the crown. If anywhere would take a deserter's testimony, it was there.

By dusk of the second day, the horn sounded.

It came off the hills behind him — two notes, high and low, the company's recall-and-pursue, blown by men who knew his habits, his routes, his water stops. They'd found the ash of his warrant in the lantern, or a groom had seen him ride, or Vess had simply known — she'd smiled at him in that chamber like she'd been waiting for exactly this. It didn't matter. What mattered was the hoofbeats under the horn, many horses, spread wide to pin him against the river.

Dain turned his lathered mare off the road and into the gorse, and rode the way he'd hunted fugitives for a decade: low ground, dead ground, the folds of the land that a pursuer's eye slides over. He knew their craft because it was his craft. He used it now against them with a cold, precise bitterness that surprised him — every trick he'd learned in the crown's service turned inside out and pointed home.

They nearly had him at the ford anyway. Lanterns swept the shallows as he came down the far bank, and a crossbow bolt took the air where his shoulder had been a heartbeat earlier, and he heard Hallow's voice, flat and carrying across the water:

"He's got nowhere to go, Corvale! Free cities won't have you! Steppe won't have you! Come back and it's a hearing, not a rope!"

Dain crouched in the reeds with water to his waist and thought about Pell's face under the hood, saying *you could still open the door.* He had not opened it. He would not get a second chance to. But he could carry the door's existence out into the world. He could stand in front of people who didn't know and tell them what happened on threshing floors behind iron doors, and make them unable to unknow it.

"Hearing," he muttered, and waded north into the dark.

Three days he ran. They took his horse at a burned waystation — his own company's work, a raid he'd have led once, torching a Steppe outpost to the stones — and after that he went on foot, and the salt roads took him. Hard country, white-crusted flats and thorn scrub, villages that appeared on no map he'd ever been issued, and everywhere the same signature: shuttered homes, cold hearths, doors nailed shut from the *outside*. Villages emptied of their gifted, and of everyone stubborn enough to protest. Once he found a child's shoe in the middle of the road and could not make himself leave it there, and so he carried it, absurdly, forty miles, until grief and exhaustion blurred together and he couldn't have said why he still had it.

On the sixth day, fever found him where the pursuit couldn't — a wound in his side from the ford's crossbow graze, gone hot and angry. He collapsed within sight of a caravan track, in the shadow of a rock formation like a leaning tooth, and the last thing he saw before the dark took him was lantern-light swinging toward him out of the dusk, and a woman's voice, dry as salt, saying:

"Well. Look what the crown dragged in and threw away."

Then the hooks of the next chapter close: fever, canvas overhead, the smell of tar and spice, and a face leaning over him — sharp-cheeked, grinning, entirely unimpressed by his warrant-less self.

"If you're dying," Captain Sefa Ilun said, "do it quietly. If you're useful, start talking. And if you're neither—" she patted the knife at her belt conversationally, "—we're equidistant from several very deep holes."

Dain Corvale, the crown's finest huntsman, opened his mouth to bargain for his life.
