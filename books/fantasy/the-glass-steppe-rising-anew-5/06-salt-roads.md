## 6. Salt Roads

Dain Corvale had been a huntsman for nineteen years, and in all that time he had never once run from anything. Now he was running from his own shadow.

The wound in his side had stopped bleeding sometime before dawn, which he chose to read as a good sign. The fever hadn't started yet, or if it had, the cold was hiding it. He moved through the scrub pine north of the Salt Road with one hand pressed to his ribs and the other on his knife, and every time a branch cracked behind him he told himself it was a deer, a bird, the wind.

It was never the wind. He knew pursuit the way a hound knew scent. Third Company ran their quarry in relays — two riders on the trail, four flanking wide, and a handler with a sniffer-hound at the rear to keep the line honest. Dain had trained half of them. He knew exactly how good they were, and that knowledge sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He'd burned his warrant three nights ago, in a waystation hearth, and watched the royal seal curl and blacken. It should have felt like freedom. Mostly it felt like amputating a limb he'd forgotten was attached to him.

Behind him, somewhere south, a gifted child named Wren was walking around with pale eyes and no dreams, carrying water for the men who had emptied her.

Dain kept moving.

* * *

The village of Harrow's Bend appeared out of the gray morning like something drowned surfacing. Twelve houses, a well, a shrine to some river god whose name had worn off the stone. No smoke from any chimney. No dog barking. That was the first wrong thing. Villages always had dogs, and dogs always had opinions about strangers.

The second wrong thing was the door.

It hung open on the largest house, and nailed to it was a sheet of vellum bearing the crown's seal. Dain had written enough of those notices himself that he didn't need to get close, but he got close anyway, because some part of him needed to see the words in his own government's hand:

*By order of the Gift Hunt: this household rendered in compliance with the King's Edict. Gratitude is owed by all citizens for their cooperation.*

Gratitude is owed. He stood there reading it twice, three times, his blood ticking slow and hot behind his eyes. Inside the house, through the open door, he could see a table set for breakfast. Three bowls. One of them still had a spoon standing upright in cold porridge. Whatever had come for this family had not given them time to eat, and whatever came after had not bothered to close the door.

He'd done this. Not this door, maybe, but doors like it. Forty of them. Sixty. He'd knocked, he'd been polite, he'd said *it will be easier if you come quietly*, and he'd believed it, because believing it was the only way to keep doing the work, and doing the work was the only thing he knew how to be.

Wren had come quietly too. She'd held his hand in the wagon because she was scared, and he'd let her, because he was a kind man, everyone said so, the kindest of the hunters. And then the handlers had taken her into the rendering tent, and when she came out she hadn't known his name, or her own, or why her hands were shaking, and the officer had clapped Dain on the shoulder and said, *Good work, Corvale. She'll make a fine servant.*

Dain turned away from the notice and vomited into the well-adjacent weeds, which cost him something, because bending over pulled at the stitch in his side and the world went white at the edges. When he could stand again, he made himself walk through the rest of the village. Counting. A huntsman counted. Eleven households. Every door marked. In the last house, an old woman lay on the floor where she'd fallen, and she was not marked, because the Hunt did not render the old unless they were gifted, and she wasn't — she'd simply died of being left alone, or of fear, or of a heart that finally refused.

He closed her eyes. It took longer than it should have, because his hands wouldn't stop shaking.

"They're taking whole villages now," he said aloud, to nobody, to the empty road. "It was never containment."

He had known. Some part of him had known since the first year, when the orders changed from *register* to *assess*, and then from *assess* to *render*, and the word *render* itself arrived without anyone ever explaining what it meant, as though everyone had agreed not to ask. You don't render a prisoner. You render fat. You render a carcass down to what's useful.

He wiped his mouth and walked north, toward the salt flats, because north was away from the capital and away from Third Company, and because a smuggler contact he'd once arrested — and then, in a moment of corruption he'd never regretted, released with a warning — had told him, laughing, that if Dain ever needed to disappear, the salt roads carried everything the crown couldn't weigh.

* * *

He found them at dusk, or they found him.

One moment he was stumbling along the edge of the flats with his vision tunneling and the fever finally winning its argument; the next there were lanterns swinging in a ring around him and crossbows leveled, and a woman was looking down at him from a wagon bench with the expression of someone appraising a horse with bad teeth.

"Well," she said. "If it isn't the crown's own wolfhound, bleeding in my road."

Dain squinted up at her. Small, brown-skinned, hair cropped short and silvered at the temples, wearing a captain's coat with the buttons replaced by coins from six different kingdoms. He placed her after a long, feverish second. "Sefa Ilun."

"Captain Sefa Ilun," she corrected. "You arrested me in Vhalen four years ago. Confiscated my cargo." She leaned forward. "What was it? Say it back to me."

"Salted eels," Dain said. "And forty pounds of bottled lightning under the eels."

"And what did you do?"

"You talked for two hours. I let you go."

"I talked for two hours," Sefa agreed, "and you let me go, which means either you're corrupt or you're smart, and I've spent four years deciding it was the second one. So." She spread her hands. "Here you are, hunted by your own hounds, dying on my route. Talk fast, wolfhound. My people are nervous and my bolts are cheap."

Dain tried to stand. His legs informed him this was not happening. He settled for kneeling, which had the added benefit of making him less threatening to eleven crossbows.

"The Hunt has changed," he said. "You've seen it. Your routes are emptier every season. Whole villages gone. It's not registration anymore, it's harvest. They're rendering the gifted into — " his voice caught on it, even now — "into servants. Pale ones. Obedient. And there's a plan underneath it bigger than servants, and I don't know all of it, but I know the routes. Every patrol line, every relay station, every safehouse and checkpoint from here to the capital. Third Company's rotation schedule for the next month, because I wrote half of it."

Sefa chewed on something — he could see her jaw working, a habitual thing, though her mouth was empty. Her crew exchanged glances.

"You're offering to sell out your own company," she said.

"I burned my warrant. There's no company anymore. There's just me and what I know, and I'd rather it buy passage than die with me in a ditch." He looked up at her, and the fever made it easy to say the true thing plainly. "I put a child in a rendering tent, Captain. I'm not asking you to forgive that. I'm asking you to spend me. Point me at the worst job you have and use me until I'm used up."

The silence went on long enough that Dain heard the wind moving across the salt, a sound like a vast dry whisper.

Then Sefa laughed — one sharp bark, no humor in it. "Gods. They really did build you people sincere." She swung down off the bench and crouched in front of him, close enough that he could smell tar and cloves. "Here's my problem, wolfhound. Everything you just said is exactly what a clever trap would say. Crown catches a huntsman, wounds him plausibly, points him at a caravan full of contraband and refugees, lets him lead us into a net. I've seen it done."

"It's not — "

"I know." She held up a hand. "I know, because a trap would have been brought in clean, and you look like something a swamp coughed up. Also your wound's real; Tamis can tell a blade cut from a staged one at ten paces, and she says yours is three days old and badly dressed, which means you did it yourself or nobody loved you enough to do it right." She stood. "So here's the bargain. You ride in the muck cart. You give us the patrol lines as we need them, not all at once, so I know you're not feeding me a script. And the first time I smell crown on you, I leave you on the salt for the birds. Nod if we're agreed."

Dain nodded. It cost him more than the wound did.

"Sefa," said one of the crossbowmen, a big woman with a scarred jaw, "the Steppe won't take him. Guild neutrality cuts both ways. We bring a crown hunter to their gate, they'll turn the whole caravan away."

"He's not a crown hunter," Sefa said. "He's cargo. Dangerous, possibly cursed cargo, which is the only kind that pays." She jerked her thumb at the muck cart. "In. And wolfhound — if you die back there, try to do it quietly. The muck's load-bearing."

* * *

They traveled by night and hid by day, tucked into hollows and dead groves, wagons draped in gray canvas that made them look, from any distance, like nothing at all. Dain rode in the muck cart as promised, wedged between barrels, drifting in and out of fever-dreams in which Wren's pale eyes opened in every face he'd ever known.

On the fourth night, Sefa woke him by throwing a strip of dried fish at his head.

"Eat. Then talk. You said villages. Show me."

They walked to the crest of a low rise while the caravan rested below. South and east, in the blue dark, a scatter of lights marked a town on the Salt Road — too many lights, moving lights, torchlight in a column.

"That's Marrow Ford," Sefa said. "Or it was. Look at the roads."

Daiin looked. Even at this distance, a huntsman's eye sorted what civilians saw as noise: carts in convoy, riders spaced at military intervals, foot traffic herded rather than flowing. And at the town's edge, a corral of wagons ringed with rope, the distinctive shape of a holding pen.

"They're clearing the whole valley," he said. "That's not a sweep, that's a cull. They must be racing to fill quotas before — " He stopped.

"Before what?" Sefa's voice had lost its flippancy entirely.

"I don't know. Before winter. Before some date. The officers started counting weeks ago — everything measured against a deadline nobody would name." He thought of the sealed briefings, the handlers' new equipment, the way the rendering tents had doubled in size. "Something's coming. Something that needs a lot of them. A lot of *gifted*."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. Below them, the caravan's night-fires were banked to embers, and the sentries moved soft along the wagon line.

"My niece is gifted," she said at last, conversationally, which was how Dain was learning she delivered the things that mattered most. "Little spark in her fingertips. Lights candles when she laughs. My sister hides her in a root cellar every time a crown rider passes, and tells her it's a game." She turned to look at him, and the lantern light caught her eyes flat and hard. "Four years ago you were the reason my sister hides her child in a hole in the ground. Tonight you're the reason I know which patrols to dodge. I haven't decided what that makes you. Neither have you, I'd guess."

"No," Dain said. "I haven't."

"Good. Honest. Keep that." She tossed him the waterskin. "There's a place ahead called the Glass Steppe. Guild territory. Neutral ground, supposedly. We're running medicine and iron to them, and a handful of passengers who paid in coin I didn't ask about." Her mouth twisted. "Gifted families. Fleeing. Toward a gate that hasn't opened in a hundred years, because hope is the one cargo that never makes sense and never stops shipping."

Dain stared south, at the torch-column swallowing Marrow Ford, and felt the guilt in his chest compress into something denser, harder, more useful. Guilt was a stone you could carry or a stone that crushed you, and he had decided, somewhere between the well at Harrow's Bend and the muck cart, to carry it.

"When we get there," he said, "the guild won't want me. Send me in first. If they turn me away, fine. But if the crown comes for that gate — and it will, Captain, it's the one door in the kingdom they don't control — then they'll need someone who knows how the Hunt thinks. How *I* thought." He flexed his bandaged hand, testing the healing. "Let me spend myself where it counts."

Sefa studied him for a long moment, chewing on nothing.

"You know what I do with dangerous cargo?" she said. "I deliver it. Carefully. To whoever's fool enough to sign for it." She turned back down the slope. "Sleep, wolfhound. Two days to the Steppe, and the roads are about to get very interesting."

She was right. They got interesting before dawn.

Because it was Dain who heard it first — the far-off belling of a hound on the wind, high and eager, coming from the northeast. Not one voice. Three. And under the hounds' cry, faint but unmistakable across the salt flats, the rhythm of hoofbeats riding hard on the caravan's trail.

Third Company had found them.

Sefa stood beside him in the dark, listening, and Dain watched her face shift from irritation to calculation to something almost like delight.

"Well," Captain Ilun said, cracking her knuckles. "Now it's a smuggling run."
