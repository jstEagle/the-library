## 7. The Council of Weights

They summoned me at the third bell after dawn, which meant they had been arguing about me since the first.

I knew because I kept the ledgers, and the ledgers told me things. The ink requisitions for the council chamber had been drawn up two days early. A runner had been paid — entry 4,417, *one copper, courier services, urgent* — to fetch Master Ovelin from his sickbed. And someone had ordered the good tea, the black leaf from the coast that we only brewed when we wanted a meeting to feel like a funeral.

I dressed carefully. If I was going to be weighed, I would at least arrive with my buttons straight.

The Council of Weights met in a round room at the heart of the Steppe's great house, beneath a ceiling carved into a scale so vast its pans held nothing but sky through two oculus windows. Nine chairs for nine masters. Eight were filled when I entered. The ninth — the First Chair, old Master Corvane — sat empty, and no one looked at it, which is how I knew he would not be coming.

"Isandro Zhou," said Master Hessa, who handled our contracts in salt and silence. "Sit."

There was no tenth chair. I stood. It seemed appropriate.

"You know why you're here," said Master Drenn. He was the youngest of them, forty perhaps, with a merchant's soft hands and a magistrate's flat eyes. He slid a page across the table toward me. I did not need to look at it. I knew it by heart — the expulsion order, dated three nights past, ordering every gifted refugee sheltering in the Steppe's lower halls to be delivered to the crown's road by week's end.

An order I had written. An order that had never been voted. An order that existed nowhere except in the ledger where truth was supposed to live.

"The record says the council passed this," Drenn said. "The record is wrong."

"The record," I said, "is what I wrote."

A silence. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the oculus and made the empty scale creak.

Hessa leaned forward. She was the oldest of them now, with a voice like paper tearing slowly. "Do you understand what you've done? Not morally. I don't care about morally. Do you understand *mechanically* what you've done?"

"You've falsified the ledger," said Master Quill — yes, that was truly her name; she'd bought it herself, entry 12, *one name, self-selected, price: everything prior*. "In three hundred years, no keeper has falsified the ledger. Not under threat of fire. Not under threat of kings. The Steppe survives because our books are the one thing in this world that cannot lie. Every treaty, every truce, every impossible trade ever brokered rests on that. You have put a crack in the foundation of everything."

"I put a lie in the ledger," I said, "to keep thirty-one people from being rendered."

"And there it is." Drenn spread his hands as if presenting me to an audience. "Some things cannot be weighed. Isn't that your argument? I've heard you say it twice already this morning through the door."

"Then let me say it a third time." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "You weigh everything. That's the guild's genius, I don't deny it. Bottled storms, borrowed years, a door sold twice — all of it priced, all of it balanced, all of it *fair*. But those people downstairs aren't inventory. They're not a line item. They are a glassblower named Tam who can make glass sing, and his daughter who lights candles by looking at them, and twenty-nine others whose only crime is being born with something the crown has decided to harvest. There is no weight you can put on one side of a scale that balances a child on the other. Some things cannot be weighed. That isn't sentiment. That's arithmetic."

"It's arithmetic that gets us burned," said Master Vosk, who ran our arms dealings and had the shoulders to prove it. "You think the crown cares about your arithmetic? The envoy arrives within the week. I've seen the outriders on the north ridge. When the usurper wants something from the Steppe, history tells us exactly how these conversations end. Ask the Salt Guild. Oh — you can't. There's no one left to ask."

"The Salt Guild opened their gate," Hessa said quietly. "That was their crime. Not defiance. *Hospitality.*"

"And we are not opening ours," Vosk said. "That I'll grant the boy. The gate stays shut. But the refugees —"

"The refugees are inside our walls," I said. "Under our roof. Logged in our books, even if I had to hide their names inside trade entries to do it. Entry 4,402: *one bolt of grey wool, received, quality: frightened.* Entry 4,403: *three jars of honey, one of which weeps at night.* You want to expel them? Then do it honestly. Vote it. Put your names to it. Don't hide behind a forgery you're horrified I made, when the only difference between my forgery and your intention is that mine hasn't killed anyone yet."

Quill laughed — one short, sharp note, quickly strangled. Hessa shot her a look.

"Young keeper," Hessa said, "you speak as though the Steppe is a fortress. It is not. It is a *marketplace*, and marketplaces survive by selling to everyone and threatening no one. The day we take a side, we stop being neutral ground and start being territory. The free cities can afford to be territories. They have walls and fleets. We have books and a door that must never open. Neutrality is not our policy. It is our *architecture*."

"I know," I said. "I audit the architecture. I've read every charter back to the Founding. I can recite the neutrality clauses in my sleep — I probably have." I looked around the ring of faces. "But I've also been reading something else lately. A page torn from my own ledger, written in my own hand, dated three years from now. Maren Voss has spent two weeks deciphering it. Would you like to hear what it says?"

"No," said Drenn.

"Yes," said Quill, at the same moment.

Hessa closed her eyes briefly, then opened them. "Say it."

"It says the gate opens on a night three years hence. It says I open it. And it says that what comes through — or what is already behind it — decides whether any of us have a world left to be neutral in." I let that sit. "I'm not asking you to believe in prophecy. I'm a bookkeeper; I don't believe in anything I can't count. But I am telling you that the ground under our neutrality may already be moving, and pretending otherwise is not caution. It's just a slower way of falling."

For a long moment, the only sound was the wind in the great scale overhead.

"Deliberation," Hessa said at last. "Closed session. Isandro — wait outside."

* * *

I waited outside for four hours, which gave me ample time to catalog everything I might lose.

The antechamber key on its chain against my sternum. The small room over the archive with its single window facing east. The smell of the ledgers — vellum and iron-gall ink and, faintly, the storm-bottles stored one floor below, which lent everything a taste of ozone. The foundling's name stitched into my collar when I was too young to know what stitching meant. I had come to the Steppe with nothing, which meant I could leave with nothing, which meant — I tried to make myself believe — I was unweighable. Free.

It didn't quite work. Fear is a poor accountant. It rounds everything up.

At the second hour, Maren came and sat beside me on the bench outside the council doors. She still carried her stolen folios everywhere, wrapped in oilcloth, hugged to her chest like a nursing infant.

"They'll expel you too, if this goes badly," I said.

"They can't expel me. I was never admitted. I'm a fugitive sleeping in your archive and eating your bread, which makes me either a guest or a ghost, depending on the vote." She smoothed the oilcloth. "Isandro. Whatever they decide — the forged entry was right."

"You don't know that."

"I know the ritual texts better than anyone alive, and I know what the crown-thing needs. It needs an anchor. Gates anchor things. Your gate anchors *something*, and it has been breathing behind that door since before the dynasty, and every instinct I have as an archivist says the prophecy page and the binding rites are describing the same event from opposite ends." She looked at me. "Three years. Maybe less, now that the usurper knows the free cities are harboring the gifted. Timelines accelerate when tyrants get hungry."

"Cheerful."

"I'm an archivist. Cheerfulness is above my pay grade, which is currently nothing."

The doors opened at the fourth hour. Drenn summoned me back in alone.

"The council has deliberated," he said. His face gave away nothing, which told me everything; merchants' faces always give away something unless they're working very hard. "The forgery stands unreversed. Reversing it would require admitting it exists, and the council has elected to treat the ledger as... continuous."

Meaning: they would let my lie stand rather than confess it. Meaning: they hated me, and they needed me.

"The refugees remain," Hessa said, "for now. Under quarantine in the lower halls. No contact with clients. And you, Keeper Zhou, are suspended from the First Chair's duties pending — "

"Pending what?" I asked. "Corvane's chair has been empty for six years. Who suspends me?"

"*Pending*," she repeated, with the patience of a woman setting down something very heavy very carefully, "the arrival of the crown's envoy, at which point the council will reassess all outstanding questions." Her eyes flicked to mine, and for half a heartbeat the old broker behind them looked almost kind. "Go. Audit something. It's what you're good at."

* * *

The envoy arrived that night, three days ahead of any reasonable schedule, which told me he wanted us off balance. It worked.

I watched from the gallery above the receiving hall as the guild masters assembled to meet him. He came with twelve soldiers in the usurper's new livery — black and bone-white, colors that hadn't existed a year ago — and he himself was a small, neat man with a clerk's posture and a voice like poured honey. His name was Envoy Serell, and before he said a single word he asked to see the receiving ledger, and initialled the page recording his own arrival, and thanked the registrar for the accuracy of the entry.

He was, I realized with a cold turn in my stomach, a man who understood exactly what the Steppe was. He had done his reading.

"Honored masters." He spread his hands. "I bring greetings from the throne, and an offer so simple I can state it in the time it takes to pour wine. The crown seeks thirty-one individuals currently residing within your hospitality. In exchange, His Majesty offers — " he produced a document sealed in black wax — "a perpetual trade charter. Full exemption from the Gift Hunt's levies. Recognition of Steppe sovereignty in perpetuity, signed and countersigned. And gold." He smiled. "Ten thousand crowns. Paid in advance. Honestly, the gold is the least interesting part of the offer, and I suspect you know that."

Vosk's jaw tightened. Hessa's face was stone. Quill was looking at the charter the way a starving woman looks at bread.

"There is one further item," Serell continued pleasantly. "The crown also seeks the individual known as Isandro Zhou, keeper of the gate. The charge is forgery of official instruments and harboring of unregistered gifted persons. The reward for the keeper is separate, and — forgive me — substantial. The crown is aware the Steppe does not surrender its officers. Therefore the offer is structured thus: deliver the refugees and the keeper to the road outside your walls, and the charter stands. Refuse, and the charter is withdrawn, the Gift Hunt's levies apply to your own staff — and I am authorized to inform you that we have already identified eleven gifted individuals among your permanent guildsmen. Their names are in my dispatch case. I would prefer not to need them."

The hall went very quiet. Above us, the great carved scale creaked in the wind, pans swinging empty, weighing nothing, weighing everything.

Serell waited. He was good at waiting. Waiting was clearly most of his job.

It was Hessa who finally spoke, and I will give her this: she did not waver where he could see it. "The council will consider your offer, Envoy. You will have our answer in the morning."

"Of course." He bowed, precise as a ledger line. "One courtesy, before I withdraw. Might I speak with the keeper? Briefly. Professional courtesy between people who understand records."

Every master stiffened. But refusing would tell him fear, and fear would tell him leverage, and so they sent for me, and I came down the gallery stairs with my buttons straight and my heart going like a stamping-press.

Serell dismissed his soldiers with a flick of two fingers. When we were alone — or as alone as one ever is in the Steppe's receiving hall, with eight masters watching from the dais — he studied me with frank, unhurried interest.

"So you're the one who falsified the ledger." He sounded delighted. "Three hundred years of incorruptible books, and you cracked them for thirty-one strangers. Do you know what His Majesty would give for a man with your particular talents? Not the bounty. The *salary*."

"I'm not for sale."

"Everyone is for sale. Most people simply haven't seen the correct price." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to something almost intimate. "Keeper, let me save us both some theater. I've read the intelligence reports. I know about the torn page. I know about the archivist and her folios, and the prophecy, and the date. Three years, yes? Here is what your council doesn't grasp, because councils never grasp anything until it's chewing on them: His Majesty does not intend to wait three years. The Hunt accelerates next month. Ten thousand souls, gathered and rendered, and the binding completed while your precious gate is still shut and your precious neutrality still means something. You are going to open that gate eventually — the page says so, and pages written in your own hand have a way of coming true — and the only question is whether you open it for a world that still exists, or a world that's already been eaten." He smiled his honey smile. "Think about which side of that equation the crown is offering to be on."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow the council weighs ten thousand crowns against eleven of their own people, and we discover together what the Glass Steppe is actually made of." He collected his dispatch case and bowed again, crisp and correct. "Good evening, Keeper Zhou. Or — forgive me — whatever you end up calling yourself."

He walked out into the dark with his soldiers, and the doors boomed shut behind him, and the sound rolled through the great house like the first stone of an avalanche.

I stood alone in the middle of the receiving hall. From the dais, eight masters watched me, and none of them would meet my eyes — not Hessa, not Quill, not even Vosk. The charter lay on the table where Serell had left it, black seal gleaming, worth more than the Steppe had earned in a decade.

Above me, the great scale swung in the wind, empty pans creaking, and for the first time in my life I understood that it wasn't decoration. It was a warning. Everything in this house was built to be weighed, and tonight the thing on the scales was us.

And far below, down past the archive, past the vaults, past the door that must never open — something breathed, slow and patient, waiting to see what we would decide.

I turned toward the stairs. Tomorrow the council would vote, and I already knew which way the weights would tip, and there was exactly one person in this house who could stop it, and she was asleep in the archive with a stolen kingdom's secrets hugged to her chest.

I went to wake her.
