## 8. A Door Sold Twice

The stolen folios weighed less than a child. Maren had carried heavier ledgers down the archive stairs a hundred times, and yet these three bundles of vellum and cord pressed against her ribs like something alive, something with a heartbeat she could feel through the oilcloth wrapping.

She had been running for nine days. Nine days since she walked out of the Royal Archive of Carreth with the folios hidden in the false bottom of her document satchel, past the door-wardens who checked every clerk's seal twice, past the heralds nailing the Gift Hunt proclamations to the gates of every district. Nine days of hired carts, river barges, and one terrifying stretch on foot across the salt flats where the only cover was the long grass and her own stillness. Nine days, and now she sat in a rented room above a dyer's shop in the free city of Vhalen, with rain hammering the shutters and a single tallow candle burning low, and finally — *finally* — she could open what she had stolen.

Her hands were not steady. She noticed this the way she noticed everything: precisely, uselessly, like a clerk cataloguing her own ruin. *Item: one archivist, former. Item: hands trembling. Item: no career to return to. Item: no country that would not hang her.* She breathed until the shaking slowed, and she untied the first cord.

The folios were old. That was the word the catalogue had used, in the sealed annex where Maren was one of four people in the capital permitted to walk: *old, provenance uncertain, do not accession.* The Royal Archive's solution to documents it did not understand was to pretend they did not exist. Maren had spent eleven years pretending alongside them, shelving the inexplicable in the dark of the annex and telling herself that some questions were above her station.

Then the proclamation had come down from the palace, and she had read its language, and pretending had become impossible.

*Every person gifted beyond the common measure shall present themselves for registry within sixty days. Those registered shall be conveyed to places appointed for their examination and rendering.*

*Rendering.* A clean administrative word. Maren had copied proclamations for a decade; she knew how language laundered violence. But it wasn't the word itself that had frozen her at her copying desk. It was the grammar beneath it — the peculiar, antique constructions, the subjunctives that hadn't been spoken aloud in two centuries, the rhythm of clauses that belonged to a formulary older than the dynasty itself. Proclamations written by chancellors echoed other proclamations. This one echoed something else entirely. Something that predated the crown it claimed to speak for.

She had gone looking for its source in the annex, and she had found these folios, and now, in Vhalen, in the rain, she found the truth.

* * *

The first folio was a household ledger. That was all it appeared to be at first — entries of grain and wax, of stipends paid, of robes mended. But the hand that kept it grew stranger as the pages turned, and the entries began to record things no steward should count.

*Paid to the sorcerer Vauntis, by his own instruction: one year of my daughter Liss's sleep. To be held in trust. He swears the king will wake.*

*Paid: my father's last winter, taken whole. He consented gladly. The king coughs blood no longer.*

*Paid: the memory of the sea, from a sailor of the southern fleet. He wept when he signed. The king remembered his coronation today, which he had lost entire.*

Maren read by candlelight with the patience of her trade, cross-referencing dates against the dynastic rolls she knew by heart. The ledger spanned forty years. Forty years of small payments — a year here, a memory there, a season of someone's joy — all flowing in one direction. All flowing toward the throne.

Vauntis, court sorcerer. She found him in the second folio, in his own hand this time, and his handwriting was beautiful in a way that made her skin crawl: each letter formed with the same perfect pressure, page after page, decade after decade, as though the man writing never aged and never hurried and never, ever hesitated.

He had come to the court of King Aldous the Second as a healer. The royal line was sick — that much the official histories admitted, a vague chronic ailment passed from heir to heir like a family curse, blamed on bad blood or bad luck. The histories did not say what Vauntis's private papers said: that the sickness had a shape. That it was not in the blood but *on* it, wrapped around it, a binding laid over the royal line generations before Aldous the Second's grandfather was born.

And Vauntis had not removed it. He had studied it. Then he had done something worse.

Maren set down the folio and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw gold sparks. The candle had burned down another finger's width. Below her, the dyer's shop was silent; the family slept. Rain ticked against the shutter like a patient clerk counting entries.

*Keep reading,* she told herself. *You are an archivist. Read.*

The second folio held Vauntis's working notes, and they were the worst thing she had ever read, and she had catalogued the interrogation records of the Purge of the Eastern Guilds. Because Vauntis wrote clearly. He wrote the way Maren herself wrote, methodically, with marginal glosses and cross-references, as if he were describing the repair of a roof rather than the consumption of a royal house.

*The binding is elegant beyond my skill to equal and beyond my courage to attempt removing. Whoever laid it understood that a thing anchored in blood renews itself. Each heir strengthens it; each heir feeds it. I have traced its structure for thirty years and I can say this with certainty: it was not made to protect the crown, whatever the founders believed. It was made to be fed.*

*The king does not dream anymore. He asked me yesterday whether his wife had been beautiful, and I lied to him, and he thanked me. The payments keep him animate. I have become very good at finding people willing to sell pieces of themselves. Poverty is a deep well; there is always more to draw.*

*Note: the prince shows early aptitude. The binding has begun to reach into him. I must decide, soon, whether I am this family's physician or its farmer.*

There the entry stopped. No decision recorded. Maren turned the pages with fingers gone cold despite the close air of the room, searching for the answer, and found instead a gap — six missing leaves, cut out cleanly with a blade, the stubs remaining like pulled teeth. Someone had wanted those pages unread.

But Vauntis's later entries answered it anyway, in their terrible oblique way. The payments changed character. Where once the ledger recorded sleep and memory and small seasons of feeling, it began recording larger things. Years of a life, sold by desperate families during the famine of Aldous Third's reign. And then — Maren made herself look directly at it, the way one looks at a wound — entries marked with a sigil she recognized from the proclamation's watermark, the crowned spiral that stamped every decree of the current reign.

*Conveyed to the appointed place: fourteen of the gifted, per arrangement. Their rendering proceeds. The king sleeps deeply now, and wakes rested, and remembers nothing of what sustains him. Perhaps it is mercy that he does not know. I have decided it is mercy. I have decided many things.*

The gifted. Rendered. Fed to the binding, through Vauntis's arrangements, for — she counted the regnal years against the entries — nearly a century. Four generations of court sorcerers, because Vauntis himself had stopped aging somewhere along the way, or had taught successors so thoroughly that their hands became his hands. The handwriting never changed. That was the detail that broke something open in Maren's chest: across ninety years of vellum, the letters stayed identical. Either one man had served four kings without dying, or the office had consumed everyone who held it and worn their bodies the way it wore the crown.

And then the final sequence of entries, dated within the last two years, in that same unaging hand:

*The king is nearly empty. There is little left to take. What remains behind the eyes is not him. I write his decrees now; he signs whatever is placed before him, and the signature is still legally his, and the binding drinks the authority of his name. The Gift Hunt was my design. We require more than the trickle of volunteers now. We require harvest.*

*The thing that wears him is patient. It has waited generations inside its own binding. When the last of Aldous's line is consumed, it will step forward and put on the kingdom as one puts on a coat. The armies gather already. The free cities will be brought to heel, for their guilds shelter the gifted, and the gifted are the last great store of fuel.*

*I record this here because some part of me — the part that came to court to heal a coughing young king — needs it written somewhere outside myself. If you are reading this, reader, know that I understood. Know that I chose. Do not forgive me. Forgiveness is a payment too, and I have taken enough.*

Maren closed the folio.

She sat for a long time without moving. The candle guttered, recovered, guttered again. Somewhere below, a dog barked once at the rain and thought better of it.

* * *

An archivist's mind, trained for eleven years in the Royal Archive, does not simply absorb horror. It organizes it. Maren lay on the narrow rented bed in the dark and felt her own thoughts arranging themselves into columns, cross-references, marginalia, because organization was the only prayer she knew.

First column: facts established. A binding older than the dynasty, anchored in the royal bloodline. A court sorcery office that had tended it like an orchard for a century. A king hollowed out so gradually that history itself had mistaken the process for illness. And now a thing wearing the crown, issuing decrees in the true king's name — decrees whose antique grammar she had recognized on sight, because the thing wrote the way Vauntis had written, in forms learned before the dynasty had words of its own.

Second column: implications. The Gift Hunt was not policy. It was provisioning. Every gifted person registered and rendered was a payment into the binding, fuel for something preparing to finish consuming the throne and turn outward. The free cities were not targets of conquest. They were the next course.

Third column — and this one she resisted, turning her face to the wall, pressing her palm flat against the cool plaster as if she could push the thought through it and out of her head:

Third column: the torn page. The page the bloodied stranger had carried to the Glass Steppe, the page Isandro Zhou had shown her on the night she arrived, the entry written in Isandro's own hand and dated three years from now. *The gate opens. The keeper opens it.* She had told herself it was a curiosity, an anomaly, perhaps a forgery, perhaps a madness. She had filed it under *unresolved* the way the archive filed everything it feared.

It was not unresolved. It was a thread, and she had been pulling it for weeks without knowing, and now the whole weave was coming apart in her hands, and she could see what was stitched underneath: the gate at the Glass Steppe, the gate the guild swore was only a door between markets, standing at the center of a pattern that included a bound king and a hungry crown and a prophecy written backward through time by the only keeper who had never opened it.

The Steppe's neutrality was famous. The guild traded in impossible things and took no sides — that was its law and its pride. But the crown-thing did not care about laws it had not written. The envoy's offer of gold for refugees' lives had reached the council chamber; Maren had heard the masters debating weights and measures while she waited in the anteroom with her forged travel seals sweating in her palm. Gold against lives. The council would weigh them, because weighing was all the guild knew how to do.

Some things cannot be weighed. She had thought it then, standing in that marble anteroom, and she thought it now in the dark above the dye shop, and the thought had changed since the capital — it had grown teeth. In Carreth it had been a scholar's discomfort. Here it was becoming a plan.

Because here was the fourth column, the one she had been circling all night: the truth was useless in her satchel. Truth in an archive was a preserved specimen, pinned and labeled and admired by four people with clearance. Truth in the world was a weapon, but only if someone swung it. The merchant councils of the free cities trusted paper and seals and provenance; she had all three, and the seals were forged, and none of it mattered if she stood alone in a council chamber reading from stolen folios that any chancellor's advocate could dismiss as forgery. She needed witnesses. She needed weight behind her voice.

She needed, specifically, the keeper of the ledgers who had shown her a page from a future no one had written yet, and the huntsman rumored to have burned his warrant and fled east, and the smuggler captain whose caravan routes ran through every village the Hunt had emptied. She needed the broken, scattered, improbable company that circumstance had already begun assembling around the Glass Steppe, as though the pattern itself were doing the recruiting.

Or — she corrected herself, archivist to the last — as though someone had written the assembly down in advance, in a hand identical to Isandro Zhou's, and the world were merely catching up to its own records.

Maren rose. The rain had softened to a whisper. Grey light stood in the seams of the shutters; she had spent the whole night reading, and the city was waking below her, carts rattling, the dyer's apprentices calling to one another in the yard. She dressed, bound the folios back in their oilcloth, and tucked them into the satchel against her spine, where she had carried them through nine days of flight and would carry them through whatever came next.

Before she left, she took out her travel journal — the small honest book she kept apart from her forged papers, the one thing about her life that remained true — and she wrote, in the neat hand of a woman who had copied ten thousand proclamations and finally understood what she was copying for:

*A door was sold twice. First the crown sold its king, payment by payment, and called it healing. Now the thing wearing him means to buy the world the same way. I have the receipts. It is time someone read them aloud.*

She blew out the candle, shouldered the satchel, and went down the stairs into the wet grey morning, toward the harbor quarter, where rumor said a smuggler named Sefa Ilun was drinking away the profits of her last run and taking on passengers foolish enough to pay for passage toward the Steppe.

Behind her, in the empty room, the three folios' absence left a shape in the air like a door newly opened — and far away, in a palace built on a hollowed man, something wearing a crown paused over its correspondence, and felt, for the first time in a hundred years, a draft.
