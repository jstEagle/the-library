## 9. Night of Knives

The lamps guttered at the same hour they always did — the third bell, when Isandro trimmed the wicks in the Long Archive and the Steppe outside went quiet enough to hear the glass grass ticking against itself. Routine was a kind of armor. Isandro had worn it for twenty years.

They were re-inking the day's entries when the first lamp died for good.

Not guttered. *Died* — snuffed as if a hand had closed around its flame, and the dark that rushed into its place had weight, had intent. Isandro set down the pen without a sound and listened. Below, in the counting hall, someone coughed. Then stopped mid-cough.

They moved the way the guild had trained them: along the spine of shelves, one hand on the ledgers' cases, breathing shallow. The archive smelled of vellum and iron-gall ink and, faintly now, of something else. Wet stone. The smell of the antechamber door, which no one opened.

A shadow crossed the aisle's far end. Then another. They wore no guild colors. Their blades were black from hilt to point — not painted, but *treated*, some alchemy that drank lamplight, and Isandro knew in one cold instant what they'd come for. Not coin. The Steppe kept no coin worth this. They'd come for the ledger with the false entry in it. For Maren. For the names hidden inside trade lines like seeds inside fruit.

For the refugees sleeping in the guesting hall, forty-one souls logged as shipments of dye and dried fish.

Isandro did exactly the wrong thing, and did it deliberately: they knocked a lamp off the shelf.

It burst on the flagstones, oil and flame, and the whole aisle lit up orange, and every black blade in the building turned toward the light. Toward Isandro.

"There," someone said. "The keeper."

* * *

They ran. There was no dignity in it — Isandro took the maintenance stair behind the cartography cases, the narrow one that smelled of mouse droppings and old glue, and came out sideways into the scriptorium where Maren Voss slept curled under her own stolen folios like a hen over eggs.

She woke before they touched her. Archivists sleep light; the archives teach you that anything can be lost between one breath and the next.

"Knives," Isandro said. "Six I've seen. Maybe more. They want the folios and the ledger."

Maren was already moving, stuffing folios into her shirt, her satchel, anywhere they'd fit. "The refugees?"

"Guesting hall's on the other side of the counting hall. Where they came in."

"Then we go through them." She said it the way she read decrees — flatly, having already accepted the cost. "Isandro. The council."

"Gone to Vhalen. All of them. Master Oren stayed." Isandro's chest tightened. "He said someone should mind the books."

They went out through the scriptorium's window onto the lower roof, glass grass crunching under the tiles, and dropped into the colonnade — and found the counting hall already burning.

Not all of it. One brazier overturned, flames climbing the tribute-tapestries, and by that light Isandro saw the fight: three assassins in the doorway of the guesting hall, and against them a wall of refugees armed with kitchen knives and tent poles and a smith's hammer. A gifted girl — ten years old, storm-touched, her hair standing on end like a dandelion gone to seed — stood at the back with lightning crawling down her arms because she didn't know yet how to make it stop.

One assassin fell. Another. The third drove his black blade through an old man's chest and came on.

Isandro hit him with a stool. It was not elegant. It was effective. The man went down and Isandro stamped the wrist that held the knife until something cracked, and then there was no time for anything but the next moment and the next: Maren hauling children through the smoke, the smith roaring, the guesting hall emptying into the night side of the Steppe where the grass grew tall and cut like razored glass if you ran through it wrong.

Forty-one souls. Isandro counted them twice, out loud, the way they counted everything, and came up two short both times.

"The old man," Maren said quietly. "And the boy who went back for him."

Grief later. Grief always later. Isandro herded the living toward the salt cellar — deep, windowless, door of green bronze — and left them with the smith, and turned back toward the burning counting hall, because one thing remained unaccounted for, and it was not a soul.

It was the master ledger. And Master Oren, who had stayed to mind the books.

* * *

Isandro found him in the Long Archive, at the heart of the fire's edge, doing the most Oren thing imaginable: he had dragged the master ledger case away from the flames and was sitting on it, an old man of seventy years using his own body as a paperweight, while two assassins tried to pry him loose.

"You're robbing a guild house," Oren was saying, in the tone he used for apprentices who misfiled things. "There are procedures. There are *penalties*—"

The blade went in under his ribs.

Isandro screamed — actually screamed, no keeper's composure left — and the second assassin turned toward the sound, and Maren's stolen folio, flung like a brick, caught the side of his head. He staggered. Isandro reached them both in four strides and put the trimming-knife — a bookbinder's tool, never meant for this — into the man's throat.

The other assassin fled. Later Isandro would understand why: they had gotten a look at the ledger, or enough of one, and one life was not worth lingering for when the prize could be taken another way.

Oren slid down against the case. His hands, ever careful with pages, pressed against his own wound as though he could file the bleeding under pending.

"Master—" Isandro knelt, pressing their own hands over his. Warmth everywhere. Too much warmth.

"Isandro Zhou." He smiled, and blood showed on his teeth. "You falsified the expulsion record. Terrible hand. You loop your f's now. You never used to loop your f's."

"I had to."

"I know." His eyes drifted to the case beneath him, to the ledgers within, decades of impossible trades, bottled storms, borrowed years, a door sold twice. "I signed off on your apprenticeship because you understood something the other candidates didn't. That a ledger isn't a record. It's a *promise*. Everything written down is a promise that it happened. That it mattered."

"Save your breath—"

"Hush. Keeper's privilege. I'm speaking." Blood bubbled at the corner of his mouth. "You've been asking the wrong question about the gate. Everyone has. We keep it shut so nothing gets *in*, yes? That's what the charter says. What I told the council. What my own master told me."

Isandro's hands were shaking. "Then what does it keep?"

Oren gripped their wrist with the last strength of a man who had spent sixty years lifting heavy books. He pulled them close, close enough that his whisper was only for them, and the fire painted his face gold and terrible.

"The gate was never meant to keep things out. It keeps one thing *in.*"

"What thing? Master — *what thing?*"

But his eyes had gone past them, past the fire, to somewhere very far away, perhaps to a first page of a first ledger no one had ever shown them. His grip loosened. His last breath came out almost as a laugh.

"And you," he murmured, "were always going to be the key."

* * *

Dawn came gray over the Glass Steppe, and the fires burned themselves out, and Isandro sat in the ashes of the counting hall with the master ledger open across their knees, reading by the light of a lamp they'd relit themselves, because routine was armor and right now they needed all they could get.

Maren crouched nearby, soot-streaked, holding a wet cloth she hadn't yet offered. The refugees were in the salt cellar, alive, and would be moved before noon to Sefa Ilun's caravan route, because Isandro had spent the small hours composing a message and a smuggler captain was the only name left to send it to.

"They'll come again," Maren said. "In force. Assassins fail; armies don't."

"I know."

"And Oren's words. *Keeps one thing in.* Do you believe him?"

Isandro turned a page. Turned another. Stopped.

Three hundred years ago — before the dynasty, before the binding anyone had recorded — there was an entry in a hand much older than any current guild script. An expenditure. Materials purchased for the construction of the gate. And beneath it, in the margin, in faded iron-gall brown:

*Built outward, sealed inward. Let no keeper open what holds the sleeper.*

Isandro looked up at Maren, and heard their own voice come out steady, which surprised them, since everything inside had gone quiet and vast, like the pause between lightning and thunder.

"He wasn't just telling me how to die well. He was handing me the rest of the entry." They closed the ledger gently, the way Oren had taught them to close every book. "The gate doesn't lead out of the Steppe, Maren. It leads *down.* And whatever the crown-thing is hunting — whatever it needs to finish becoming unkillable —"

"—is asleep behind it," Maren finished. Her face had gone the color of the ash falling around them. "And the assassins weren't sent to steal your ledger at all. They were sent to confirm it still exists. To confirm the *door* still exists."

Far off across the glass grass, where the horizon shimmered like heat-haze made permanent, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. A third time.

Not the guild's horns. Guild horns were bronze and mournful. This one was high, bright, and repeated in threes — the signal Isandro had read about in Dain Corvale's smuggled notes and prayed never to hear.

Army column. Two days out. Maybe less.

Isandro rose, tucked the master ledger under one arm, and looked east toward the gate that kept something in — and west toward the road where forty-one refugees waited in a cellar, and understood, finally, what kind of keeper they had become.

"We move tonight," they said. "Everyone. Before they arrive." A breath. "And Maren — send word to the salt roads. Tell the smugglers we're coming, and tell them the price just changed."

"How so?"

Isandro looked down at the ledger in their arms, at the blank space waiting after the last entry, and felt Oren's whisper settle into their bones like a debt called due.

"Tell them," Isandro said, "the keeper is selling the door."
