## 10. Flight Across the Steppe

Dain had learned to read smoke before he'd learned to read letters, and what the grassland told him at dawn was simple: something ahead of them was burning, and it was burning in a hurry.

"Waystation?" Sefa asked from beside him, her mare picking its way through the salt-crusted tussocks.

"No. Waystations smolder." He shaded his eyes against the rising light. "That's a signal fire. Wet fuel, green wood — someone wants to be seen."

Sefa spat thoughtfully over her horse's shoulder. "Or someone wants to be found by friends and not by soldiers, which is a different kind of fire entirely."

Behind them, the caravan strung out across the grass like a thread pulled loose from a hem: six wagons, forty-odd souls, and the smell of fear that no amount of salt wind could scour away. They'd been three days out from the burned villages, and Dain's shoulder wound had gone from hot to throbbing to a dull constant presence he'd begun to think of as another passenger. He rode with his bow strung and his quiver within reach, because the Hunt taught you that peace on open grassland was just a word for the space between disasters.

The fire resolved as they crested the low rise: not a waystation after all but a ring of wagons drawn up in a defensive circle around a stone marker — one of the Steppe's boundary stones, old as the guild itself, carved with weights and measures in a dead script. People moved inside the circle. A lot of people. Too many for the wagons they had.

"Refugees," Sefa said.

"I can see that."

"I mean I can see *armed* refugees. Look at the perimeter. That's not panic formation, that's organized. Somebody over there has read a manual."

Dain looked again and felt a cold thread slide down his spine. The circle was well-made — wagons angled, gaps covered, a picket line of horses inside rather than out. And standing at the gap facing them, small against the wide morning, was a figure in grey guild robes with a sword held in a way that said *I have never wanted to hold this and I will use it anyway.*

"They'll see us coming," Sefa said. "Question is whether they see huntsmen or merchants."

"We're neither."

"We're whatever they decide we are when we're still a hundred yards out. So let's decide first." She turned in her saddle and raised her voice across the caravan. "Weapons down! Bows slung, blades sheathed, hands where the nice people with the swords can count fingers! We are sad, tired traders, do you understand me? Sad! Tired!"

Someone near the rear laughed, high and thin, the laugh of a person who needed it more than air.

They walked the horses forward at a funeral pace. Dain counted heads in the refugee circle as they closed: perhaps two hundred. Children on wagon beds. Elders wrapped in every scrap of cloth they owned. Gifted families — you could tell now, after months of the Hunt, by the way the children flinched at uniformed silhouettes even at this distance. His own silhouette. His old company's cut of coat, worn into his shoulders like a second skin he couldn't shed.

The grey-robed figure stepped out to meet them alone. Close up, Dain saw the ledger-chain at their belt, the guild sigil, and a face he knew.

He stopped his horse so abruptly that Sefa nearly ran him down.

*Isandro Zhou.* Keeper of ledgers. Keeper of the gate. The name had been spoken in his hearing a hundred times in briefing rooms and mess tents — usually by officers tracing supply lines, sometimes in curses, once in a tone close to reverence by an old sergeant who claimed to have traded there before the war made trade a dirty word. Isandro Zhou, who logged impossible things. Isandro Zhou, whose guild had stayed neutral while the crown hollowed itself out.

Isandro Zhou, who was looking at Dain Corvale with an expression that had already completed several calculations and arrived somewhere between recognition and murder.

"You," Isandro said.

"Me," Dain said, because there was nothing else.

"The Ashford waystation." Isandro's voice was flat as a ledger line. "Three years ago. Crown requisition. You were there."

Dain had spent the ride rehearsing arguments, and every one of them died in his throat. There was no argument for Ashford. There had been a warrant, a company of twenty, and a Steppe waystation that sheltered gifted travelers off the books — and there had been a woman named Pell who ran it, who had stood in the doorway and told them the guild would remember, and who had been dragged out with her ledgers burning. Dain had carried the torch because he was senior and seniority meant dirty hands. Two of the people sheltering there had been children.

"Yes," he said.

Sefa's head swiveled between them like a spectator at a duel. "I'm sorry. *You're* the huntsman? The one who—"

"Burned a warrant and defected four days ago," Dain said. "After watching what 'rendering' actually means. Yes."

"That's a lovely story."

"It's true, which is lovelier."

"It is *not*, actually—" Sefa began, and then the grassland behind them all went quiet in the specific way grassland goes quiet when birds stop singing, and everyone — two hundred refugees, forty smugglers, one disgraced huntsman, one furious guildkeeper — turned to look south.

Smoke. Not one column. Three.

"Army vanguard," Dain said quietly. All his old training came back at once, unwelcome as a debt collector. "Light cavalry outriders, burning the grass to clear sightlines. The columns follow the burns. We have maybe half a day before those riders reach us, and if they find two hundred gifted souls sitting in a wagon circle, they won't wait for orders."

Isandro hadn't taken their eyes off him. "Why should we believe anything you say?"

"Because I'm still here," Dain said, "and I could be anywhere else. Because I know their doctrine better than anyone alive, and their doctrine says a group this size doesn't get escorted to a registration camp. It gets rendered in the field. Efficiently." He held Isandro's gaze. "Ask your refugees what happened to the last village that tried to run east. Ask them about Marrow Ford."

A ripple went through the circle behind Isandro. An old man called out, voice cracked: "We passed through Marrow. There's crows on the ford road thick enough to blacken the sky."

"There," Dain said. "That's my testimony. The rest you can weigh however you like."

Isandro's jaw worked. Dain watched them do it — watch the weighing happen, visibly, like someone setting objects on a scale. It occurred to him, absurdly, that this was probably how Isandro did everything: measured, entered, balanced. And that right now the scale must feel like it was full of knives.

"We don't have time for justice," Isandro said finally. "Which may be the only reason you're still breathing. Ride with us. If you slow us down, or betray us, I will log your death myself and sleep well."

"Fair terms."

"And you'll tell me everything about the Hunt's routes. Every patrol pattern. Everything."

"Every night we ride," Dain said, "you'll wish I'd talk less."

Something that was almost — almost — humor flickered across Isandro's face and was gone.

Sefa threw up her hands. "Wonderful. The alliance is sealed with threats and insomnia. Someone wake me when we're all dead."

* * *

They broke camp in eleven minutes, which Dain noted with professional respect; the Steppe trained its people well even when it wouldn't fight for them. The problem was arithmetic. Two hundred refugees moved at the speed of their slowest ox cart, and the usurper's outriders moved at the speed of hunger. Half a day was optimistic. A quarter day was honest.

Dain rode the flank with Isandro, who had apparently decided that keeping your enemy where you could see him beat hoping he'd wander off. For an hour neither spoke. Then Isandro said, without looking over:

"Ashford. Why?"

Dain took a breath that hurt. "Because the warrant said *containment* and I believed it. Because I was good at believing things that kept me promoted. Because the woman who ran it — Pell — looked at me like I was a person, and it was easier to burn the door than answer her." He watched the horizon instead of Isandro. "There's no version of the story where I come out clean. I've stopped looking for one. Now I'm just trying to be useful, which is different, and smaller."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. The grass whispered past below them.

"Pell kept a ledger too," Isandro said at last. "Off-book. Names of everyone she sheltered. When your company burned the station, someone saved one page." They didn't elaborate, and Dain understood suddenly that the page might be in a pack ten feet from him, and that some part of Isandro had carried it for three years the way he carried the smell of Ashford's smoke.

"I'm sorry," he said. It wasn't enough. Nothing would be. But Isandro nodded once, sharply, as though an entry had been made and closed.

Then the outriders appeared.

Not many — Dain counted eight, then twelve, fanned out along the southern rise like crows testing a field. Too far to identify faces, close enough to identify livery: the new crown colors, white and iron-grey, the colors of the Gift Hunt rebranded for conquest. One of them raised a horn, and the sound rolled across the grassland, flat and cold.

"They've seen us," Isandro said.

"They've seen *wagons*. They'll want to count before they commit." Dain was already calculating, hating himself for how easily the numbers came. "Twelve scouts won't charge two hundred. They'll shadow us, mark our heading, and report. The question is what's between us and Vhalen."

"The river," Isandro said. "Two days' hard ride. There's a crossing at Oldbridge, or there was."

"There isn't anymore. The Hunt burned the bridges in spring to choke free-city traffic. Your crossing is a memory." Dain turned in the saddle. "But there's a ford. Salt Ford, thirty miles north of Oldbridge. Shallow, wide, ugly, unmarked on most maps because it floods. In autumn it runs knee-deep."

"How do you know it?"

"Because I helped map the region for the Hunt's route surveys." He almost smiled. "My whole treasonous career turns out to be a gift that keeps giving."

Sefa materialized at his other side, having heard every word, because Sefa heard every word always. "Knee-deep. With two hundred people and heavy wagons."

"Heavy wagons sink in salt mud," Dain said. "We dump cargo or we cross light. Cargo or lives."

"Cargo," Sefa said, "is how we eat."

"Lives," Isandro said, "are why we run."

For a moment Dain thought Sefa would argue — everything about her posture argued, professionally, on principle — but then she looked back at the wagons full of children and swore with real feeling and started shouting redistribution orders down the caravan line. Within minutes her crew was hurling crates of trade goods onto the grass behind them like offerings, and Dain understood he was watching a woman spend her livelihood in real time and pretend it was nothing.

"Your captain's a better person than she advertises," he said.

"She'd gut you for saying so," Isandro said. "She'd also give you her last ration first. Don't tell her I noticed."

* * *

The outriders shadowed them through the afternoon, patient as weather. Twice, Dain peeled off with two of Sefa's best riders to make threatening feints toward the scouts' flanks — enough to keep them cautious, keep them counting, keep them from closing. It worked, mostly because the scouts were cavalry men used to routing farmers, and a former royal huntsman riding straight at you with a drawn bow reads differently than a farmer, even in stolen clothes.

The third time, one scout didn't fall back.

He came on alone — young, overconfident, the kind of trooper who'd been promised easy glory — and Dain took him at the gallop, arrow loosed at sixty yards, dropping the man's horse rather than the man. The scout hit the turf rolling and came up with a blade, and for one long second he and Dain faced each other across the trampled grass, and Dain saw the boy realize exactly who was looking down at him. The whole army knew Dain Corvale's face now. It hung in every mess tent with a price under it.

The boy ran. Dain let him. He heard Isandro draw breath behind him — to ask, or to object — and answered before the question came.

"A deserter's worth more alive than a corpse," Dain said. "He'll report I'm here. Good. Commanders get careful around me. Careful commanders move slow."

"You spared him."

"I killed his horse. That's the tax." Dain wheeled back toward the caravan, and only when his back was turned did he let the shaking start in his hands, the shaking that came after violence now instead of before, and hated how much gentler he'd become and how little good it did anyone.

Isandro rode beside him the rest of the evening and said nothing more about it. But at the midnight halt, when the fires were banked small and the sentries doubled, Isandro appeared at Dain's bedroll and sat down without asking, and put a strip of dried meat and a flask of something harsh in front of him.

"For the horse," Isandro said. "And the boy. Both entries noted."

Dain ate. The liquor burned pleasantly. Around them the Steppe breathed — wind, grass, a child crying softly somewhere and being hushed — and far to the south, faint as a lie, the glow of the army's cookfires stained the underside of the dark.

"They'll push hard tomorrow," Dain said. "They know our heading now. We'll reach Salt Ford hours ahead of them, maybe less."

"Then we cross fast."

"We cross *ugly*. Wagons unhitched and floated, people linked arm to arm, the strong carrying the small. Some of those elders won't survive the water." He looked at Isandro directly. "I need you to hear that now, not at the bank. I've commanded retreats before. Retreats cost. If you want someone to promise otherwise, I'm the wrong man."

Isandro was silent. Firelight moved on their face, and Dain watched them do the weighing again, the terrible scale-work, except this time Dain understood it wasn't Isandro weighing him. It was Isandro weighing themselves — the keeper of records confronting the fact that some nights, no ledger balances, and you choose anyway, and live with the entry.

"Then we cross ugly," Isandro said. "And I'll write down every name we lose, and the guild will owe them, forever. That's the only promise I know how to make."

"It's the only kind worth making," Dain said.

Isandro stood, paused, and looked down at him with an expression Dain couldn't file under any of the categories he expected — hatred, suspicion, grudging tolerance. Something else. Something that looked almost like the beginning of trust, extended the way you extend a hand across a gap you both intend to jump.

"The Ashford page," Isandro said. "Pell's ledger. It lists names. One of them is a girl who was nine years old when your company came. She's in the third wagon. She survived because Pell hid her in a grain bin." A pause, long enough to hurt. "She doesn't know your face yet. When she does, that will be hers to weigh, not mine. I'm telling you because you deserve to carry it before it arrives."

Then Isandro walked away into the dark, leaving Dain alone with the flask and the stars and the weight of a name he'd never known he owed.

He didn't sleep. Toward dawn he rose, checked the southern horizon — the glow closer now, the horns faint — and woke Sefa with a boot to the wagon wheel.

"Get them moving," he said. "Today we race an empire to a river."

Sefa sat up, hair wild, eyes instantly sharp. "Who's winning?"

"Nobody. Yet."
