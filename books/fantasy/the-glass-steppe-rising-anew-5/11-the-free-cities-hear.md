## 11. The Free Cities Hear

The Council Hall of Vhalen smelled of salt money and old cedar, and Maren Voss hated it on sight.

She had spent eleven days on the road since the Steppe fell — eleven days of grass horizon, refugee carts, Isandro's quiet arithmetic of who could walk and who had to be carried — and she had imagined the free cities as something bright. Something that would hear the truth and act on it. She had been a royal archivist for nine years; she knew how institutions worked. She had simply believed, against all her training, that freedom made people braver.

It did not.

The Council of Nine sat behind a crescent of polished blackwood, nine chairs, eight filled. The ninth stood empty with a wreath of dried lavender laid across it, which Maren learned was for Councillor Ashk, who had "taken ill" two nights after word reached the city that the crown's vanguard had crossed the Salt Marches. Taken ill. Everyone in the hall understood what the phrase meant. No one said it aloud.

Maren stood in the well of the chamber with Isandro at her left shoulder and Dain at her right, and before them, arranged on a folding table like evidence at a trial, lay everything she had stolen from the sealed archives: three folios bound in grey calfskin, the ritual fragments copied in her own hand, and the genealogies tracing the sorcerer-king's binding through six generations of crowned heads.

Isandro had argued for this. *If we can't fight them yet, we can at least make them look.*

"Speaker," the middle councillor said. He was a grain merchant named Ovist Halle, soft-handed, ringed, with the particular pallor of a man who had slept badly for a week. "You've petitioned this council for emergency audience. You have a quarter hour."

"Quarter hour," Sefa Ilun muttered from the gallery above, loud enough to carry. "For the end of the world. Generous."

A ripple of uncomfortable laughter ran through the public benches. Maren took a breath.

"Councillors. I was Royal Archivist to the House of Aldmere. I held keys to the Sealed Stacks under the Winter Palace. Nine days ago I broke my oath, burned my credentials, and fled the capital carrying these." She touched the folios. "I did not do this lightly. I did it because I read what is written here, and because no one else would believe me until they'd seen it."

"We are familiar," Halle said, dry as flour, "with rumors regarding His Majesty's health."

"Not his health." Maren opened the first folio. Her hands were steady. She had rehearsed this on the road, walking beside the wagons, reading aloud to Isandro until the words lost their power to frighten her — though they never quite did. "His *self*. What sits on the throne in Aldmere wears the king's body the way your clerks wear robes. Underneath, there is almost nothing left. And it is not the first time."

She began to read.

She gave them the binding-rite in plain speech, stripped of its ceremonial grammar: a court sorcerer named Vaelen Coru — the city's own founding name, and she watched several councillors flinch at that — who had bound his soul into the royal line four hundred years ago as insurance against death, anchoring himself to each heir in turn, drinking them slow. She gave them the succession records, the pattern she'd found: every third or fourth king dying young and strange, gentle rulers turning hard mid-reign, edicts issued in language older than any living chancery. She gave them the final page, the one that had made her run — an accounting, in the sorcerer's own hand, of what remained of King Emrys Aldmere. *Four parts taken. A fifth ripening. When the fifth is drunk, the vessel will hold me entire, and I will not need the line again.*

"And the Gift Hunt?" Halle asked, when she paused for breath. His voice had gone thinner. "How does that concern us?"

"Because the ritual needs fuel." Maren turned the last folio around so the council could see the column of figures — the sorcerer's projections, centuries old, updated in a newer hand. Dain had gone white when she'd first shown him; he'd recognized the accounting style as the Hunt's own ledgers. "Ten thousand gifted souls, rendered and poured into the binding at once. That is not containment, councillors. That is harvest. Your city is not being threatened by the usurper's armies. It is being inventoried."

Silence. Somewhere above, a gull screamed against a window.

Then Councillor Halle folded his hands, and Maren watched him become, in real time, exactly what fear wanted him to be.

"These are grave claims," he said. "Extraordinary claims. Made by a fugitive, a guild outcast, and—" his eyes flicked to Dain's burned warrant-stub, still carried openly on his belt like a wound, "—a deserter under sentence of death. You ask this council to declare war on the Aldmere throne on the strength of stolen papers."

"I ask you to read them," Maren said. "Send them to your own scholars. Send them to the temple archivists. I'll answer any challenge—"

"The council has heard enough." Halle's gavel came down once, softly, which was somehow worse than a bang. "Vhalen maintains its neutrality. We trade with the crown; we trade with the Steppe; we trade with whoever keeps the river open. This council does not make war on rumor. Petition denied. You will remove those documents from the chamber."

"They're copies," Maren said. "I have more."

"Then you will remove yourself."

* * *

They were escorted out through a side door, past guards who wouldn't meet their eyes, into the colonnade where the afternoon light came down amber and dusty. For a long moment nobody spoke.

"Well," Sefa said finally, dropping down the stairs from the gallery two at a time. "That went about as well as a funeral in a leaky boat."

"You knew," Isandro said quietly. Not accusatory. Just checking.

"I hoped." Maren pressed her back against a pillar. The folios felt heavier now than they had in the archive, heavier than they had on the road. "Nine years I served that palace. I told myself the free cities would be different. That freedom teaches people to value it."

"Freedom teaches people to count," Isandro said. "I kept ledgers for twenty years. I know the arithmetic." They looked up at her, and there was something in their face Maren hadn't seen before — not despair, but a kind of grim patience, like a person watching weather. "Halle wasn't lying when he said neutrality keeps the river open. He just didn't say open for whom. Give it a month. Give it a season. The crown's men will come to inventory, and the council will hand over lists with both hands and call it trade."

Dain said nothing. He stood at the colonnade's edge looking out over the harbor, where the fishing boats were coming in with the evening catch, ordinary and golden, and Maren understood he was memorizing it. Men who had done what Dain had done learned to hold images like that against the dark.

It was Sefa who noticed the boy.

"Maren." Her voice had changed entirely — the mockery gone out of it, low and sharp. "Don't turn around fast. Gallery window, above the west door."

Maren looked. A child crouched in the shadow of the buttress — thin, maybe ten, with the particular watchful stillness of someone who had learned young that stillness kept you alive. One of the refugee children from the Steppe? No. Wrong clothes. City clothes, patched, salt-stained. And the boy was staring at Maren's folios with an intensity that had nothing to do with curiosity.

He raised one hand, hesitated — then the loose cobblestones near Maren's feet shivered, lifted an inch off the ground in a perfect circle, and settled again.

Gifted. Unregistered. In Vhalen itself.

"He heard us," Maren breathed. "In the hall?"

"Saw us thrown out of it," Sefa said. "Word travels faster than armies, love. Half the dockside knows by now there's a woman telling stories about the king."

The boy met Maren's eyes. Then he was gone, quick as water between pilings, and Maren stood holding her folios against her chest and felt something shift — some small, stubborn mechanism turning over inside the despair.

*They're listening,* she thought. *The council isn't. But they are.*

* * *

By nightfall, she stopped needing to find her audience. It found her.

It began at the lodging house Sefa had commandeered — a harborside warehouse that smelled of tar and pepper, where the refugees from the Steppe were crowded among cargo. A fishwife came first, a broad woman named Odile who had a nephew the crown's tithing-men had looked at too long during the last census. Then a chandler whose daughter lit candles without fire when she dreamed. Then two deserters from the coastal garrison, still in their boots, who said they'd walked away from the Salt Marches after seeing what the Hunt's wagons carried south, and neither would say more than that, and both shook while they ate.

Maren read to them in the warehouse's lamp-lit heart. She read the same pages she'd read the council, but differently now — slower, plainer, letting the fishwife interrupt with questions, letting the deserters curse. Isandro sat in the corner with the ledger open on their knees, writing steadily, and when Maren glanced over she saw they weren't recording trades. They were writing down names. Every person who came, every gifted child named in a frightened parent's whisper, entered in the guild's impossible book as if courage were a commodity and this its market price.

On the third night, the smiths came.

There were seven of them, led by a woman with forearms like anchor chain called Brava Tetch, who owned the largest forge on Anchor Street. She listened to Maren's whole reading without moving, arms crossed, and at the end she said only: "The ritual needs ten thousand souls. How many does unbinding need?"

"One," Maren said. "One willing anchor-holder, standing at the gate where the rite is rooted. That part we may already have." She didn't say Isandro's name. She didn't look at them. Some bargains should not be spoken aloud in front of strangers.

Brava nodded slowly. "Anchor Street forge employs sixty. There's the ropewalk, the armorers' row, the shipwrights off the basin. Council won't move. Councils never do. But a city isn't its council, archivist. A city is its hands." She held hers up, scarred and certain. "Tell us what to build."

* * *

The riots started on the fifth day, and Maren would spend years afterward trying to decide whether she had caused them or merely failed to prevent them.

It was the proclamation that did it. The crown's criers had come up the coast road with fresh copies — the new decree expanding the Gift Hunt into the free territories, registration mandatory within thirty days, and the word *rendered* sitting in the text like a knife left on a dinner table. Someone nailed a copy to the customs house. Someone else nailed Maren's transcriptions beside it, side by side, so the whole quay could read the decree and then read what the decree was *for*.

By noon the crowd outside the Council Hall was five hundred strong. By dusk, twice that, and the mood had curdled. Maren stood on the warehouse steps watching smoke rise from somewhere near the tax-collector's row, hearing glass break, hearing the crowd chant — not her name, not the truth's name, just *no, no, no*, which was perhaps the most honest political slogan ever shouted.

Councillor Halle appeared on the hall balcony with lanterns behind him and disavowed everything in a voice pitched to carry across the square. The fugitives were liars, he said. The documents were forgeries, he said. Vhalen's neutrality was sacred, he said. The crown's goodwill must not be provoked. Any citizen aiding the fugitives would face charges of sedition — against *Vhalen*, mind, not the throne, which told Maren precisely whom Halle feared more.

A cobblestone arced out of the crowd and burst against the balcony rail a hand's width from his knee. He retreated. The crowd roared.

"They'll send soldiers to quiet it," Dain said beside her. He'd been quiet all evening, watching the square with a huntsman's eye, cataloguing exits and sightlines. "City guard first. If that fails, the crown will offer to help. Halle will accept help before he accepts truth."

"Then we shouldn't be here when it comes," Maren said. "We take the refugees east, toward the river—"

"No." Isandro's voice, from the doorway. They stepped out into the lamplight, and Maren saw they'd been crying, or close to it, though their voice was level. "Look again. Look at what's actually happening."

Maren looked. The riot was burning itself out along the edges — crowds do — but in its wake, in the side streets and doorways, people were *gathering*. Small knots. The smiths from Anchor Street, moving together with purpose. Deserters collecting other deserters. And children — gifted children, dozens of them now, drawn out of hiding by five days of whispered news that there were adults who wouldn't report them, who had *written their names down* as if the names mattered. Odile the fishwife was organizing buckets against the fires she hadn't started. A temple novice was leading a chain of volunteers carrying water and bandages toward the injured, ignoring the council's edict as thoroughly as if it had been printed in another language.

"It's not a riot anymore," Isandro said. "It's a muster. Nobody planned it. Nobody voted on it. It's just — what a city looks like when it decides." They closed the ledger gently. "The council disavowed us today. Tomorrow, half of them will deny it and the other half will follow the crowd. Fear doesn't survive contact with numbers. Halle knows that. That's why he's terrified."

Sefa leaned on the rail, grinning like a knife. "So what's the play, keeper? We've got smiths asking for orders, deserters asking for officers, and a mob that loves us tonight and might hang us Thursday. I've crewed ships with worse odds, but only just."

Maren thought about the sealed stacks. About nine years of filing truth away where it couldn't hurt anyone, and how the hurt had grown in the dark until it wore a crown. She thought about the boy with the floating cobblestones, and Brava Tetch's open hands, and the sound five hundred people made saying *no*.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we stop begging councils. We start building a company. Brava gets her forge list — pikes, shield-bosses, anything that holds a wall. Dain trains anyone who's ever held a weapon, and finds the ones who can learn. Sefa keeps the river open and starts moving supplies east." She paused. "And I write. Everything. Plain language, a thousand copies, sent to every town up the coast — because the council silenced us in one room, but paper doesn't need permission."

Dain nodded slowly. Then he straightened, and Maren saw the old huntsman's discipline come up in him like a wall, and knew before he spoke that the message had come.

"There's a rider at the gate," he said quietly. "Steppe colors. Alone, horse blown nearly dead." He met Isandro's eyes. "He's asking for you by name. Says the army's crossed the river at Marrow Ford — three days out, maybe less." A beat. "And says the usurper's herald rode ahead under a parley flag. He's given Vhalen until the walls are breached to surrender its registers." Dain's jaw tightened. "*All* its registers. Including yours."

Below them, the last fires were guttering. Above the smoke, the first stars were coming out over a city that had, tonight, chosen — badly, loudly, incompletely — to choose.

Isandro picked up the ledger and held it against their chest, the way Maren held her folios.

"Three days," they said. "Then we'd better give this city something worth defending."
