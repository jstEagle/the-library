## 12. What Isandro Carries

The caravan camp burned low on its fires when Isandro finally opened the ledger they had carried out of the Steppe.

Not the guild's great ledger — that one was ash now, or scattered in the hands of masters who had fled in three directions. This was their own: a slim book bound in grey hide, the kind every keeper kept for private entries, the kind no council ever audited. They had carried it through fire and across open grassland without once opening it. Some books you carry the way you carry a wound.

They sat apart from the others, on a rise above the tents where Sefa's smugglers slept in tangled rows and the gifted children dreamed whatever gifted children dream. Dain had taken first watch. Maren was still awake somewhere below, reading by candle stub, because Maren was always reading. The grass whispered against itself the way grass does when there is nothing else to say.

Isandro turned to the first page.

The ink was faded to the color of old tea. Their own handwriting — small, precise, the hand of a child who had been taught that a ledger entry was a promise made to the future. *Foundling, sex unrecorded, age estimated two winters. Left at the south gate of the Glass Steppe on the night of the Long Frost. No name given. Registered under ledger-name Zhou, per custom of the finder.*

Zhou. The name of the gate-keeper who had found them, dead eleven years by then, his line ended, his name kept alive only because the guild did not waste things — not names, not doors, not children. A ledger-name. Not a gift. An entry.

Isandro had spent forty years being an entry, and it had never troubled them until now.

*You will understand why it troubles them soon,* they thought, and then corrected themselves — *why it troubles me* — and the correction felt like a stone dropped down a well that took too long to hit water.

Below, a horse nickered. One of the children cried out in sleep and was quieted. The world went on being ordinary, which was its own kind of mercy, because nothing about what Isandro carried was ordinary anymore.

They turned the page.

* * *

The winter they were seven years old, the Steppe froze so hard the glass-grass rang when the wind moved through it, a sound like a thousand small bells mourning something. Isandro remembered the bells. They remembered everything from that year, which was the problem, and always had been.

The guild raised its foundlings communally but not carelessly. Every child was assigned a craft by the age of six, chosen by the masters after watching how the child played. Most children were given to trade-crafts: weighing, appraising, haggling, the gentle violence of commerce. Isandro had been watched for two full seasons while other children were already apprenticed, and at the end of it, Master Corvin — old even then, older than the ledgers he claimed to have helped bind — had taken them aside into the archive hall where the air smelled of dust and cold iron.

"You don't haggle," Corvin had said. It wasn't an accusation. He said most things as though reading them aloud from an inventory.

"I win," Isandro had answered, seven years old and already certain of arithmetic.

"You don't haggle," Corvin repeated, "because you can tell exactly what a thing weighs before anyone picks it up. That's not trade-craft, little one. That's keeping." He had crouched to their height, and his eyes had been the color of the frost on the windows. "There are two kinds of people in this world. Those who move goods across the Steppe, and those who keep account of the moving. The second kind are rarer. And lonelier. Think hard before you thank me."

Isandro had not thought hard. Seven-year-olds rarely do. They had thanked him, because being chosen felt like being real.

For ten years they learned the keeping. The weight of bottled storms — heavier empty than full, because a storm remembers being free and presses outward against its prison. The weight of borrowed years — lighter than the years they replaced, which told you something terrible about lending if you thought about it long enough. The door sold twice, which had required three separate reconciliation pages and one formal apology to a man in Vhalen who had bought the same oak door from two different brokers and installed it in a house that, by rights, should have collapsed from carrying double its history. Isandro had resolved that entry at fourteen, balancing the door's two provenances against each other until they canceled out, and Master Corvin had looked at the page for a long time and said, quietly, "You balanced something that shouldn't balance."

"Isn't that the work?"

"No," Corvin said. "That's the miracle. The work is knowing the difference."

It was good training for a life. It was, Isandro understood now, training for something else entirely. The guild had seen what they were decades before they saw it themselves. Had they been groomed? Prepared? Placed, like a piece on a board, next to the one door in the world that mattered?

Or had it all been accident, and accident was worse, because accident meant the prophecy hadn't needed any help?

* * *

The antechamber.

Every keeper of the Steppe learned the gate's outer rites: the oiling of hinges that had not swung in living memory, the tracing of the seal-script along the frame, the annual certification that the gate remained shut and would remain shut, entered into the master ledger in ink mixed with silver so that no forgery could stand. Isandro performed the rite for the first time alone at seventeen. Alone, because the rite was traditionally done alone; alone, because Corvin had insisted with a strangeness in his voice that Isandro only recognized, years later, as fear.

The gate stood at the heart of the Steppe's oldest hall, behind three locked doors and a corridor that the architects of some earlier century had built deliberately crooked, so that no straight line ran from outside world to gate-face. It was black glass shot through with veins of white, and it was warm. Everyone knew it was warm. The guild explained this with comfortable fictions — geothermal vents, residual enchantment, the friction of old seals — comfortable fictions that let everyone stop asking questions.

Isandro had never stopped asking questions. It was their worst trait and their only talent.

That night, having completed the certification and recorded the seal intact, they had done the thing apprentices were forbidden to do and knew some of them did anyway. They had put their palm flat against the glass.

And the gate had opened.

Not swung. Not shattered. It had simply become passable, the way a wall becomes a doorway in a dream, and Isandro had stepped forward before fear could arrive — stepped through into a room that should not have existed inside the gate's thickness, a low round chamber lit by nothing and yet visible, its walls the same black glass veined white, and in the center of the chamber—

Breathing.

Something breathing. Slow. Enormous. The breath of something that had been breathing a very long time and would go on breathing whether or not anything listened. The sound filled the chamber the way water fills a cup, and Isandro stood in it, seventeen years old, and felt the breath move through their own chest as though their lungs were being borrowed.

They could see almost nothing. A shape at the center, vast and folded, like a mountain that had learned to curl up small. Chains — no. Not chains. Lines of script, written in the air itself, thousands of lines, binding the shape the way a ledger binds a debt: entry after entry after entry, each line a clause, each clause a year.

And the thing behind the bindings had known they were there. Isandro had felt it notice — a pressure turning toward them like the attention of a storm. It had not spoken. It had done something worse. It had *waited*, with the patience of something that had learned that waiting is the only language everyone eventually speaks.

Isandro had run. They would admit that, always, to themselves: they had run, and the gate had sealed behind them, and when they pressed their palms to the glass again it was only glass, cold-veined and dumb, and the warmth was gone as if it had never been.

They had told no one. Here was the reason, the reason they had never spoken aloud even to themselves until the false entry arrived and cracked their life open like a struck bell:

Because they had checked the master ledger afterward. Of course they had. Keeping was their nature; they had gone straight to the records with shaking hands. And the certification entries for the gate stretched back four hundred years, one per year, each in a different hand, each attesting the seal intact — and every single entry was written in handwriting identical to their own.

Their own hand. Four hundred years of it. Entries dated centuries before a foundling was left at the south gate on the night of the Long Frost.

Isandro had sat on the archive floor until dawn with that impossibility pressing on them, and in the end they had done what keepers do with entries that cannot be reconciled. They had closed the book. Filed it under *unresolved*. Carried it.

Some debts you do not collect. You just make sure they're recorded.

* * *

Now, on the rise above the smugglers' fires, Isandro read the torn page again by moonlight.

*On the third night of the waning moon, in the season of the red grass, the gate shall be opened inward,* their own handwriting declared, *and the opener shall be Zhou, keeper, foundling, who has heard the breathing and kept the silence. What is bound shall ask to be unbound, and the price shall be weighed against the kingdom, and the kingdom shall be found lighter.*

Maren had translated the cipher around it. Maren had found, in stolen folios, the ritual that made sense of it all: a sorcerer bound to a royal line, consuming heir after heir, until the current king was a husk and the crown held by something else entirely. And the binding's anchor — the anchor of the whole monstrous arrangement — lay behind the gate. Behind the breathing. The first king, or the first sorcerer, or both, curled in the dark for four hundred years, writing certification entries in a foundling's hand because time, in that place, was a suggestion rather than a law.

The prophecy named them. Not *a* keeper. *Zhou, keeper, foundling.* There was only one.

And the last line, the one Isandro had read forty times and understood fully maybe twice: *the price shall be weighed against the kingdom, and the kingdom shall be found lighter.*

Weighing was what they did. It had been chosen for them at seven, trained into them for ten years, practiced for thirty more. Whatever the price was — and the lich-broker's terms already whispered in their memory, *ledger-name, identity, the self that anchors* — Isandro would have to weigh it themselves, honestly, the way they'd balanced the door sold twice. And they suspected, with the cold certainty of long experience with impossible entries, that they already knew how the scale would tip.

A boot scuffed the grass. Dain lowered himself onto the rise beside them, sword across his knees out of habit, and sat in silence for a while, watching the dark steppe.

"You've been up here an hour," he said.

"Reconciling an account."

Dain snorted. "You people even grieve in columns?"

"Especially in columns." Isandro closed their private ledger. Below, Maren's candle had gone out; the children slept; Sefa's sentries walked their rounds with the loose swagger of people pretending not to be afraid. "Dain. When you burned your warrant. Did you know what you'd do next?"

"No. Still don't, mostly."

"And it didn't stop you."

"It was the not-knowing that stopped me for years," Dain said. "Burning the paper was just admitting I'd already decided."

Isandro looked east, toward the Steppe, toward the gate, toward the breathing they had heard once and never forgotten. Three days to the lich-broker's salt-flats, if Sefa's maps held. Three days, and then a bargain in bone and ink whose price was the only self Isandro had ever had.

They had carried the silence for twenty-three years. They found it weighed less than they feared, and far more than they wished.

"Dain," they said. "At first light, I need your sword, and I need you not to ask where we're going until we're past the river."

The huntsman studied them — the ledger in their hands, the old grief in their face — and nodded slowly, the way men nod when they recognize a decision that has already been made.

"Past the river," he said. "Then you'll tell me what it costs."

"Yes," Isandro said, and did not add: *if I'm still the person who can weigh it.*

Far to the east, beneath four hundred years of seals, something breathed — slow, patient, waiting — and wrote, in a hand that was theirs, the next entry in a ledger no one had authorized.
