## 13. The Usurper's Court

The scouts came back tied to their own horses.

That was the first thing Dain noticed from the ridge above the river camp — two riders swaying in their saddles with hands bound to the pommels, faces gray as old tallow, and a third horse trailing behind them carrying nothing but a banner folded wrong. The free-company outriders who'd brought them in kept well clear of the prisoners' mouths, and when Dain saw why, he understood. Both men had sewn their lips shut. Not recently. The scars were years old, neat as ledger lines.

"Gifted?" Sefa asked beside him, her spyglass glinting. "Or just careful?"

"Careful," Dain said. "Huntsmen's scouts learn not to talk under anything. Sewing is the permanent version." He had taught the technique himself, once, to a training cohort at Caelmark. He wondered if one of these men had been his student. He hoped not. He was running out of hope that could survive being spent.

Below them, the usurper's camp spread along the north bank of the Vessa like a city that hated itself — ten thousand strong now, maybe more, the columns that had harried them across half the Steppe joined by fresh banners out of the heartland. Dain counted pavilions by habit and stopped counting when he found the command cluster: the black-and-gold of the crown, ringed by the white shields of the Hunt. His old company. His old life, pitched in orderly rows on the far side of a river he'd sworn to hold.

"We take them tonight," Sefa said. "Before the council meeting. Maren needs what's in their heads more than she needs sleep."

"You say that like heads are easy to open."

"Hers are." Sefa passed him the glass. "Look at the command tents. Third row. Tell me what you see."

He looked. He saw supply trains, forage pens, a smithy line, and then — set apart, guarded by no one, which was its own kind of guard — three long low structures roofed in pale canvas, too long for barracks, too wide for stores. Wagons stood outside them in ranks, iron-caged, and even at this distance through good glass he could see the cages were padded. Straw, sacking, quilted walls. Wagons built to carry something fragile and living.

His mouth went dry. "Those are transport wagons."

"I know what they are," Sefa said quietly. "I've moved cargo my whole life, huntsman. You don't pad a cage unless what's inside bruises."

* * *

They took the scouts an hour after full dark, in a hollow of dead grass two miles from the picket line, where the outriders said the pair had been drinking from a seep before the company swept them up. It wasn't much of a fight. It wasn't meant to be. Dain walked into the firelight alone with his hood back and his face bare, and both scouts went rigid in a way no rope could achieve.

"Corvale," the older one breathed through his scarred lips. The stitching had torn long ago on one corner; words came out soft and slurred, like speech remembered rather than performed. "They told us you were dead."

"They told you I was a traitor. Dead was the polite version." Dain crouched across the fire. Up close the man was younger than his voice — thirty, maybe, with a Huntsman's brand faded on his jaw and eyes that had done arithmetic they regretted. "Name?"

"Tollen. Tollen Marsh." A swallow. "This is Pem."

Pem, the younger, said nothing. His eyes flicked to the treeline, to the free-company shadows holding bows, and then to Dain, and stayed there with something worse than fear in them. Recognition. Hope. That was the worst thing a prisoner could offer a man who'd burned his own warrant — hope that the defection meant something, that it spread, that it was contagious.

Dain had learned to hate being someone's proof.

"You were scouting the crossing at Redford Ford," he said. "Water levels, garrison strength. Standard. But you came back by the long road, past the padded wagons. Why?"

Tollen's face did something complicated. "You've seen the wagons."

"Everyone's seen the wagons."

"No one's seen the wagons." The scout laughed, or something near it. "That's the whole point of them, Captain. You look at a caged wagon and you think prisoners, because that's what we always hauled. Gifted, chained and documented. But these aren't going to the rendering camps." He leaned forward, and the fire showed Dain a man standing at the edge of something he'd only ever circled. "These are coming *from* somewhere. They're empty, and they're riding ahead of the army, and every officer above captain has orders about them that come sealed from the crown itself. Not from the Marshal. From the crown. Sealed in wax nobody's supposed to be able to forge."

Dain felt cold start in his chest and work outward. "What kind of orders?"

"Capacity manifests." Tollen held up his bound hands, miming a page. "Numbers. How many souls each wagon holds if they're packed loose versus packed tight. Feeding schedules for transit. And a delivery date." He swallowed again. "Midsummer. After the cities fall. Ten thousand."

The word landed in the hollow like a dropped stone. Behind Dain, one of Sefa's people shifted, leather creaking.

"Ten thousand what?" Dain asked, though he knew. He had known since Caelmark, since the night he watched a girl named Wren go pale and obedient and *empty*, and told himself it was containment. He asked anyway, because asking was the last step before admitting, and some part of him still wanted a wall between himself and the truth.

"Gifted," Tollen said simply. "Every registered gifted soul in the conquered territories. The decree says *harvest*, sir. That's the word in the manifest. Harvest, midsummer, ten thousand." His voice cracked on the number. "We thought — when the Hunt started, we thought it was registration, containment, the usual. Keep dangerous folk where they can't hurt anyone. Then the renders started, and we thought the crown wanted soldiers, obedient ones, made out of gifted stock. But this—" He shook his head slowly. "This isn't making anything. This is *feeding* something. And whatever eats, sir, it's been hungry a long time."

* * *

They brought the scouts back to the free-city camp under cover of the grain carts, and Maren Voss met them in the archive tent she'd rebuilt from nothing twice already — once in Vhalen, once here, moving folios ahead of the army like a woman evacuating children. She looked thinner than she had at the Steppe. Her hands didn't shake anymore, which worried Dain more than shaking would have; steady hands meant she'd stopped being surprised by how bad things were.

She cut the scouts' bindings herself, poured them wine, and sat them down across a table laid with her stolen folios like a surgeon laying out knives.

"Tell me about the seals," she said. "The wax nobody can forge."

Tollen described it haltingly — black wax shot through with gold thread, pressed with a signet that wasn't the royal arms. Maren listened with her eyes closed, and Dain watched her lips move faintly, translating, until she opened her eyes and reached for a folio without looking, the way a person reaches for a wound.

"The binding rite," she said. "The original one, from the sorcerer's private papers. The anchor seal uses exactly that wax — blood-black, gold-threaded. It's not a forgery of royal authority, it's *older* than royal authority. Whoever writes these orders is sealing them with the same craft that binds the king." She flattened the folio open. Diagrams crawled across the page: circles within circles, a human figure at the center drawn with the casual precision of an anatomist. "And the harvest numbers aren't arbitrary. Look." Her finger traced columns. "Ten thousand. The rite requires a tithe scaled to the anchor's hunger — the older the binding, the larger the feeding. Ten thousand is what keeps a binding of this age alive forever. It's not conquest followed by atrocity. The conquest exists to gather the tithe. The war is a *harvest schedule*."

Silence held the tent. Outside, the river muttered over its stones, indifferent.

"And the officers," Dain said. His voice came out flat, which was how he knew how much it was costing him. "Tollen said orders above captain come sealed from the crown. Which officers carry them?"

Tollen and Pem exchanged a glance that lasted too long.

"Captain," Tollen said carefully, "you should sit down."

"I've been standing all day. Say it."

"Command of the harvest logistics sits with Lord-Commander Halvard Renn." The name came out of the young scout's mouth like something pulled with hooks. "Your—"

"I know who he is." Dain did sit down, after all. The stool was there and his legs made the decision without consulting him. Halvard Renn. The man who'd trained him, broken him, rebuilt him into the finest huntsman the crown had produced in a generation. The man whose approval Dain had chased for fifteen years like a dog chasing a hand that sometimes fed it. The man whose face had stared up at him from a wanted poster with Dain's own name beside it, both of them marked traitor in the same ink.

"He designed the Hunt," Maren said softly. She was watching Dain with that archivist's gaze, cataloguing him alongside the folios, and he found he didn't mind. "Think about it. The registration system, the rendering protocols, the transport standards — all of it predates the king's final decline. Someone built the machinery of the harvest *before* the harvest was announced. Someone who knew what the binding would eventually need, and prepared the kingdom to provide it." She turned a page. "Renn's family served the crown for six generations. Archivally speaking, that's exactly the lineage a court sorcerer would bind to itself. Not the kings — kings die, get replaced, get eaten. The *servants*. The constant. If any house carried the ritual knowledge forward, generation to generation, keeping it ready—" She stopped. She didn't have to finish. The shape of it stood in the tent with them, tall and patient as the man himself.

"Renn knew," Dain said. "Renn's always known. Every order I ever obeyed, every child I dragged to a rendering camp — I thought I was serving the crown. I was serving *him*. The Hunt was never the king's justice. It was a larder being stocked." He heard his own voice from far away, level and terrible. Fifteen years of warrants. Fifteen years of names in capture ledgers, and every name a ration.

Tollen shifted. "Captain. There's something else. About Lord Renn, and the wagons." He waited until Dain looked at him. "I escorted the first convoy. Before I was reassigned to scouting. We drove them to a rendezvous in the hills east of Caelmark, no destination given, and Lord Renn met us there personally. He inspected the cargo — the *people*, sir, he inspected them like horseflesh — and then he walked the line talking to them. Quietly. Individually. I was close enough once to hear a piece of it." The scout's scarred lips worked. "He was apologizing."

The word fell into the tent and lay there.

"He told a woman — a storm-touched woman, chains to the eyebrows — he told her, *it will be quick, and it will matter, and I am sorry your generation was born into mine.* And then he signed her transfer and sent her on, and got back on his horse, and his face was—" Tollen searched for it. "Clean. Like a man who'd paid a debt he'd agreed to long ago. Whatever Renn is, sir, he doesn't think he's a monster. He thinks he's a steward. Keeping a terrible thing fed so it stays terrible in only one direction." He looked down at his bound hands, freed now, still held together out of habit. "Like the rest of us were doing. Just... further along."

* * *

Later, past midnight, Dain stood at the river's edge where the sentries wouldn't bother a man washing, though he wasn't washing. The water ran black and quick under a thin moon, and beyond it the enemy fires made a second, inverted sky.

Sefa found him there, because Sefa found everyone eventually; it was her professional gift. She leaned on a fence post that had once belonged to a drowned farm and offered him her flask without comment. He drank. It tasted like a mistake someone had distilled.

"Ten thousand," she said.

"Ten thousand."

"My crew's forty-one. I've buried nine of them since the bridges." She turned the flask in her fingers. "I keep doing that sum. Nine against ten thousand. It comes out feeling very small."

"It's not the right sum."

"No?"

Dain looked north, toward the pale canvas roofs he couldn't see but could feel, the way you feel weather coming. Ten thousand gifted souls in padded cages, riding toward a midsummer appointment with a thing that wore a king. And behind it all, Halvard Renn, apologizing his way down a wagon line, a man who had known the ending before the story started and had chosen to keep the story moving because stopping it required a courage he'd apparently priced beyond reach.

Fifteen years ago, that man had put a sword in Dain's hands and said, *you'll do hard things, boy, and the hard thing is knowing they're right.* Dain had built a life on that sentence. Tonight he finally read it properly: not permission. Inheritance. Renn hadn't taught him duty. Renn had taught him the exact shape of the cowardice that calls itself duty, and Dain had worn it proudly, badge by badge, warrant by warrant, all the way to the border of this river.

"The right sum," Dain said, "is whether we get across that water and into those tents before midsummer. Whether we find where the binding anchors, and tear it out at the root. Whether Renn gets a chance to choose what I chose — or whether he dies comfortable." He handed back the flask. "Because I've met the man, Sefa. He'll stand in front of the gate of that thing's court and apologize to whoever we send against him. And he'll mean it. And he'll kill them anyway. Unless someone he trained is the one standing there."

Sefa studied him for a long moment. "You're volunteering."

"I'm the only one fluent."

From the archive tent behind them, a lantern flared — Maren, still working, still awake, bent over pages older than dynasties. And beyond her, upstream, the first scouts of Isandro Zhou's party were due by dawn, carrying word from the Steppe and something else besides, something about a gate and an antechamber and a price written in a hand that prophesied itself. The pieces were converging the way pieces do before a battle: slowly, then all at once.

Sefa pushed off the post. "Council at sunrise. Sleep if you can."

He couldn't. He stood at the black water instead, and across it, deep in the enemy camp, a single bell began to toll — slow, measured, wrong. Not an alarm. Not a call to prayer. A count. He listened, counting against his heartbeat, and stopped at thirteen, and the bell stopped with him, as if it had only ever been waiting for someone on this bank to hear it and understand.

*Harvest,* it said, in the language of bronze. *Soon.*

Dain turned from the river and went to wake the others.
