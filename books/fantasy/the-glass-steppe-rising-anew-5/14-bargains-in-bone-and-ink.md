## 14. Bargains in Bone and Ink

The lich-broker kept her shop where the Steppe's oldest maps said there was nothing.

Isandro walked out past the salt flats at dusk, ledger satchel tight against their ribs, following the only direction the guild's records gave: *north of the drowned well, until the grass forgets to grow*. The grass did forget. It thinned to wire, then to ash-colored stubble, then to a circle of bare earth maybe forty paces across, as if the land itself had flinched away from what stood in the middle of it.

The shop was a wagon. That was all. A black-wheeled caravan with lanterns burning green in its windows, parked on nothing, going nowhere. Smoke rose from its chimney and fell instead of rising, pooling around the wheels like spilled ink.

Isandro had audited every client account the guild kept. They knew the name that appeared once a decade, always paid in full, always in coin older than the dynasty: *Vess of the Quiet Margin*. They had assumed it was a title, a mask. Standing here, they understood it was closer to a warning label.

"You're late," said a voice from inside, dry as pages turning. "I've been dead longer than you've been alive, and even I find waiting tedious."

The door opened before Isandro knocked.

* * *

Inside, the wagon was larger than outside, which Isandro had expected, and colder than winter, which they hadn't. Shelves climbed into darkness overhead, and on the shelves were jars — hundreds of them, thousands — each holding something that might have been smoke, or breath, or light with nowhere to go. Small brass tags hung from every lid, inscribed in a hand so fine it looked printed.

Memories. Isandro realized it slowly, the way you realize water is cold. Each jar was a memory someone had sold, or lost, or been made to surrender. Near the door, a jar tagged in fresh ink held what looked like a child's birthday, glowing faintly gold.

"You keep them alive," Isandro said. "The memories."

"Of course I do." Vess turned from a workbench crowded with bone styluses and pots of ink that moved on their own. She was small, gray-robed, unremarkable except for her eyes, which were the flat silver of coins left too long in the earth. "A memory that dies is just a lie with good posture. I deal in true things. Sit."

There was one chair. Isandro took it and set the satchel on their knees like a shield.

"I know why you've come," Vess said. "Word walks ahead of people on this Steppe. The gate. The prophecy page. The king who isn't a king." She smiled without warmth. "You want the unbinding rite. Not the fragments your archivist salvaged — the whole of it. The words that actually tear a binding loose instead of just fraying it. And there is exactly one source in this world who holds the complete text."

"You," Isandro said.

"A man sold it to me four hundred years ago," Vess said. "A court sorcerer's apprentice, fleeing after his master bound himself to the royal line. He thought he could buy back what he'd helped create someday. He never came back. They rarely do." She folded her hands. "I'll give you the rite. Complete, accurate, verified against three independent copies I've collected since. You have my word as a broker, which is worth more than any crown's."

"And the price?"

Vess's smile deepened by a degree. "Your ledger-name."

The words landed in Isandro's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They waited a long time before speaking. "That isn't mine to sell. It's recorded in the guild rolls. It anchors—"

"It anchors everything," Vess agreed pleasantly. "Every entry you've ever written carries the weight of your name behind it. Your gift lives in that name — the way your entries become true, the way locked things open for you, the way you can hold a hundred impossible accounts in your head without dropping one. All of it braided into six syllables your foundling self was given by a guild clerk who couldn't be bothered to ask what your mother called you." She tilted her head. "Yes. I know your history. I know everyone's history. It's rather my business."

"If I give it up—"

"You won't remember losing anything," Vess said. "That's the mercy of it. You'll feel quieter. Like a room with the furniture taken out. Your gift will gutter — perhaps die, perhaps not; names are stubborn things. But the entries you wrote under that name stay written. The false expulsion record stays forged. The refugees' hidden names stay hidden." Her eyes gleamed. "Interesting, by the way, that a keeper of ledgers chose forgery as her first crime. I'd have paid handsomely just to watch that happen."

Isandro stood. Walked to the shelf nearest them. Looked at the jars. One near eye level held a tag that read, in that fine printed hand: *the smell of rain on the first street you ever loved*. Someone had sold that. Someone had decided they could live without it.

"Why does a memory-broker want a name?" Isandro asked. "It's not a memory."

"No," Vess said. "It's better. Names are memories other people agree to share. Yours has been spoken aloud in trade halls for twenty years, witnessed by thousands of transactions, anchored into the guild's own charter books. It's the richest thing to walk through my door in a century." She said it plainly, without greed, which somehow made it worse. "I don't consume what I collect, keeper. I curate. Somewhere in this world, when everything else has burned, your name will still exist. There are worse fates for a person than to be preserved."

"That's not preservation. That's taxidermy."

"All preservation is," Vess agreed. "Now. The rite, or the road home? Because the usurper's columns crossed the River Sere this morning, and your archivist's city has perhaps nine days. I don't sell urgency cheaply either, but tonight, I'm including it free."

Isandro sat back down.

They thought about Maren's face when she'd read the folios aloud — the court sorcerer's binding, generation after generation of kings hollowed like gourds. They thought about Dain's silence when the scouts described the feeding-camps. They thought about the children in the archive cellar, playing dice with knucklebones from a dead guild master's desk because grief needs somewhere to put its hands.

And they thought about the antechamber. The breathing thing behind the inner door, the thing they had heard when they were twelve years old and told no one. The prophecy page in their own handwriting, dated three years from now: *the gate opens inward, and the keeper opens it.* Whatever waited at the end of the rite, they would need themselves whole enough to walk through that door.

Wouldn't they?

"What exactly happens," Isandro asked slowly, "when you take it?"

Vess rose, selected a jar of clear glass, empty except for a single line of ink suspended in its center like a vein of night, and set it on the table between them. From a drawer she drew a bone stylus and a knife of black metal that drank the lantern-light.

"You write your name in the jar," she said. "In your own hand. The same hand that writes entries that come true. Then I speak the terms, you speak consent, and the name goes where recorded names go when they're sold." She laid the knife beside the jar. "You will remain Isandro in every way that matters to anyone but you. Strangers will still call you by it. Friends will still use it. But it will sit on you like a coat belonging to someone else. And the deep current underneath — the one that makes your pen an instrument of law over impossible things — that current will drain toward me, and quiet."

"Will my entries still hold?" Isandro asked. "The refugees. The forged record. If the guild council audits—"

"The entries stand. They were sealed under your name, and a sold name keeps its obligations. Even I can't unpromise a ledger." Something flickered across the silver eyes — amusement, or its fossil. "Though whoever audits them next will find the signature reads strangely. Distant. Like a letter from someone who's moved away."

Isandro picked up the stylus. Bone, worn smooth at the grip by centuries of other hands making other bargains.

"One condition," they said.

Vess raised an eyebrow — the most expressive gesture she'd made.

"When this is over. When the binding tears loose or it doesn't. If I come back and ask you what I paid—" Isandro met the flat silver stare. "You tell me. All of it. What it felt like, what went quiet, what I was. You owe the truth to whoever stands in front of you wearing my face."

For a long moment the lich-broker said nothing. Then she laughed — a real sound, rusty, startled out of her like a bird from a burned tree.

"Oh, you're a dangerous customer," she said. "Yes. Agreed and recorded. I keep my contracts better than any god ever kept anything." She slid the empty jar forward. "Write."

Isandro uncapped the ink pot — the moving kind, the kind that writhed faintly, hungry for surfaces — and dipped the stylus.

Their own name. Twenty years of writing it in margins and headers and certification lines, tens of thousands of times, until the letters flowed without thought. Now every stroke felt like walking on ice over deep water. *I-s.* The first curve. *An-dro.* The descender of the *d* like a door swinging shut. *Zhou.* The final stroke trailing off the way their hand always trailed, impatient to reach the next line.

The letters lifted off the glass and hung in the jar, coiling, alive, gold-edged.

"Consent?" Vess asked formally. "Given freely, under no compulsion, in full knowledge of the price?"

"Given freely," Isandro said, and their voice only shook a little.

Vess spoke a word that was less a sound than a removal of sound, and the jar filled.

Not with light. With weight. Isandro felt it leave the way you feel a tooth pulled — a wrongness, a new shape to the air of the mouth, an absence with edges. Something deep in their chest went still. Not silent — *still*, like a river freezing from the bottom up. The constant hum beneath their thoughts, the current that had run through every day since the guild clerk handed a nameless foundling six syllables and a life — it drained away through some channel Isandro couldn't see, and did not stop, and did not hurt, which was somehow the worst part.

Then it was done.

The jar sat on the table, holding a slow-turning coil of gold-threaded dark. Their name, turning. Looking almost peaceful.

Isandro reached for the sensation of their own gift, reflexively, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. Nothing answered. The ledgers in the satchel felt like paper. Just paper. The world felt slightly larger and much emptier, a room with the furniture gone.

"Well?" Vess was already lifting a second jar down from the shelf — one tagged with a symbol Isandro didn't recognize. "Do you want to sit a moment, or shall we do the part where I give you four hundred years of a dead apprentice's stolen knowledge and you try not to weep on my floor?"

"We proceed," Isandro said. Their voice sounded fine. That seemed obscene.

* * *

The rite came out of Vess in pieces, and each piece cost Isandro something they hadn't budgeted for.

Not blood. Not more name. Just comprehension — because Vess refused simply to recite it. *"A rite copied without understanding is a rite half-performed, and half-performed bindings tear sideways,"* she said, and so she taught, and Isandro learned, and learning meant seeing clearly for the first time what they were walking toward.

The unbinding required three anchors. A reader — someone to hold the text of the original binding in mind while it was torn, or the tear would heal around them like flesh around a blade. A warden — someone to stand on the threshold and keep the passage open against whatever the binding threw outward in its death throes. And a keeper — the one whose hand broke the seal, whose name was written in the gate's own registry.

"The prophecy page," Vess said, watching Isandro's face. "Dated three years out. In your hand. You understand now why it needed to be your hand."

"I understand that a version of me wrote it," Isandro said carefully, "and I'm increasingly unsure that version and I want the same things."

"Time is a ledger too," Vess said. "Entries get amended." She rolled the last scroll closed and pushed it across the table along with a strip of vellum covered in her precise print — the transcribed rite, complete, annotated in three colors of ink. "This copy is yours. The original stays in my collection, where it will outlast your civilization, your language, and possibly your sun. Consider it insurance that you succeed. I dislike holding worthless artifacts."

Isandro gathered the scroll, the vellum, the satchel. Stood. Their body moved correctly. Everything worked. But somewhere underneath, where the current used to run, there was a flatness, a plateau where a river had been, and they kept catching their mind wandering to it like a tongue probing the gap.

"Keeper." Vess's voice stopped them at the door. "One professional courtesy."

Isandro turned.

"The thing in the antechamber — the first king, the breathing thing your twelve-year-old self heard and wisely told no one about." The silver eyes held them. "When you open the gate inward, it will recognize what you've paid. It will smell the absence. Bindings and brokers read the same script, in the end. It may offer to fill the gap itself." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "Whatever it offers — and it will offer beautifully — remember that you came to me because you wanted to *unbind* a king. Not make a trade for a different kind of chain."

The cold of the wagon pressed close. In its shelves, ten thousand memories glowed in their jars, patient as stars.

"Noted," Isandro said.

Outside, the night had gone fully dark, and the grass beyond the bare circle whispered in a wind that smelled, distantly, of smoke from the west. Nine days. Maybe fewer. Isandro turned south, toward the lights of the Steppe, toward Maren and Dain and the children and the dead guild master's unfinished accounts, and began to walk.

At the edge of the bare earth they stopped, because something was wrong with their shadow.

It lay long behind them in the moonlight, correct in every proportion — except that where their head should have cast it, the shadow showed a blank oval, smooth as an unwritten page. Isandro stared at it for a long time. Then they made themselves look away, and made themselves keep walking, and did not let themselves think about how the blank space felt less like a loss than like a room being cleared.

Furnished for something. Waiting for someone.

Behind them, faint through the dark, they heard Vess shelving the jar — their name settling onto its shelf with a click of glass on wood, joining the collection of everything people had given up to buy their way forward.

Nine days. Isandro lengthened their stride, and the grass bent away from their footsteps as though it, too, was no longer entirely sure who they were.
