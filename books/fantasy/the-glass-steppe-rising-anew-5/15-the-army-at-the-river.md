## 15. The Army at the River

Sefa Ilun had run contraband past three navies, two storm-gods, and one very persistent customs witch, and she had never once been early for anything. So it was a particular insult to her professional pride that she arrived at the Vhalen river crossing four hours before the usurper's vanguard did — and that the free cities' defense committee had spent those four hours arguing about whether to defend the bridge or burn it.

"Both," she said, for the ninth time. "You burn it *after* you've moved your people across. You don't torch the only road out of the floodplain while there are still carts on it."

The committee was eleven merchants in good wool, and they looked at her the way merchants look at weather: as something inconvenient that would eventually pass.

"Captain Ilun," said Master Corvane, who owned half the granaries on the east bank and clearly intended to keep them, "the council has not authorized—"

"The council," Sefa said, "is currently voting on whether to send a strongly worded letter to an army that eats souls. I'm not waiting for the minutes." She turned to her crew, spread along the rail of the *Salt-Kissed* where it lay moored against the pilings. "Ondo! Pitch and oil on the midspan. Kess, get every barge we've leased upriver, now. If it floats and it isn't mine, it's leaving tonight."

"And if the council objects?" Ondo asked, grinning through his beard.

"Then the council can object from the far side."

She felt it before anyone said anything — a wrongness in the air, like the moment before a squall, except the sky was clean and blue and the wind had died entirely. Downriver, the water went still. Not calm. Still. The kind of still that meant something enormous was drinking the current.

Kess came up the gangplank at a dead run. "Scouts say the vanguard's crossed the ford at Marrow Bend. Three days out, maybe less."

"Maybe less," Sefa repeated. It was always maybe less. She looked downriver, toward the bend, and saw what the scouts had seen: a smudge on the horizon that might have been dust, except dust didn't move in ranks.

Three days. The whole plan — Isandro's plan, Maren's plan, the mad ledger-keeper's arithmetic of hope — rested on holding this river for three days while the gifted families and the archive wagons got clear, while word went out to the other free cities, while whatever Isandro Zhou was doing at that shut gate got done.

Sefa had signed on because the pay was impossible and the cause was better than most. She'd stayed because she'd watched a huntsman cry over a burned warrant and an archivist read a death decree aloud like it was a love poem, and somewhere in all that madness she'd stopped being able to imagine going back to smuggling pepper.

"All right," she said. "Let's ruin someone's conquest."

* * *

They burned the first bridge at dusk, and it was beautiful, and Sefa hated every second of it.

The *Salt-Kissed* sat midstream with her holds full of pitch-barrels, and when Ondo touched flame to the fuse-line the fire ran along the old timbers like it had been waiting years for permission. The bridge went up in a roar that rolled across the water and came back off the bluffs twice. On the far bank, the vanguard's outriders reined up at the edge of the light, small and black against the burning, and did not advance.

"They'll have engineers," Dain Corvale said beside her. He'd come down from the ridge with six of his defected huntsmen, armor scarred, face gray with a fatigue he wore like a uniform. "Bridge-builders, sappers. The crown keeps corps of them. Give them two days and they'll have pontoons across."

"Then we'll give them one day and a bad night." Sefa spat over the rail. "How many men do you actually have?"

"Forty-one who can fight. Twelve who can shoot. Four who can think."

"Luxury. I've got sixty smugglers, nine barges, and a ship I love more than my mother." She counted the fires on the far bank, marking their spread the way she'd once marked patrol boats. "Here's how it goes. They build pontoons, we burn pontoons. They bring fire-arrows, we scatter. We don't hold the river — nobody holds a river. We make the river expensive."

Dain watched the flames eat the last of the span. "And after three days?"

"After three days we're legends, and somebody else's problem." She clapped his shoulder, felt him flinch under the mail, and gentled her hand without commenting on it. She'd learned that much about soldiers. "Get some sleep, huntsman. Tomorrow I need you ugly and mean."

He almost smiled. "I'm told that's my natural state."

* * *

The first assault came at dawn on the second day, and it was not soldiers.

Maren Voss had warned them — she'd stood in front of the defense committee with her stolen folios and her shaking hands and told them exactly what the crown-thing fielded — but warning is a thin wall. When the pale things came down to the waterline, walking without hurry across ground no living man could cross so quietly, the east-bank militia fired one ragged volley and then simply stopped being a militia. Half of them ran. The other half stood very still, the way rabbits stand, and Sefa understood with a lurch that they couldn't do otherwise. Something in the air had taken their legs away.

The rendered ones didn't swim. They walked into the river and kept walking, under it, and came up the pilings of the ferry dock like drowned men climbing a ladder, water pouring off them, faces smooth as eggs.

"Fire!" Sefa screamed, and her crew gave them fire. Ondo's swivel-gun took the first apart in a spray of gray ash. Kess and the Teller twins poured burning oil down the dock pilings until the whole structure was a pyre standing in the river, and the pale things burned without screaming, which was worse than screaming, and kept climbing until the wood collapsed under them and took them down hissing into the dark water.

On the far bank, among the ranked banners, something raised a hand, and the assault simply ended. The rendered ones waded back across. The pressure lifted off the defenders' minds like a thumb removed from an eye.

Sefa leaned on the rail and shook, and made sure no one saw her shake.

"That was a probe," Dain said. His voice was steady but his knuckles were white on his sword-hilt. "It was counting our guns."

"It found them." She grinned at him with a face that felt borrowed. "Did you see its face? Neither did I. You can't kill what doesn't have a face, huntsman, but you can damn well drown it."

Below them, in the shallows, the river carried ash downstream in long gray ribbons, like the water itself was mourning.

* * *

By the second night the evacuation was nearly done, and Maren Voss worked the docks like a woman possessed by arithmetic.

Sefa found her at the east staging ground, hoarse from shouting, ink-stained fingers checking lists by lantern-light: families counted, families boarded, families unaccounted for. Behind her the archive wagons stood under canvas, guarded by two of Dain's huntsmen and a smith's apprentice with a hammer too big for him.

"Tell me it's done," Sefa said.

"Eleven hundred crossed today. There are perhaps two hundred left in the river villages — old ones, stubborn ones, a family of glass-singers who won't leave their kilns." Maren rubbed her eyes. "I sent word. Some of them are coming. Some of them—" She stopped. "Some of them remember when the crown protected them, and they're waiting for it to be that crown again."

"It's not that crown."

"I know. They know, somewhere. Knowing and believing are different rivers." Maren looked up, and in the lantern light her exhaustion cracked open into something fiercer. "Sefa. The folios say the binding needs ten thousand. Ten thousand gifted souls. There aren't ten thousand in the free cities — not yet. But if the usurper takes Vhalen intact, takes the temple registers, the birth-rolls, the census of the Gift Hunt's first year—" She pressed her palm flat on the list. "Every name in these books becomes a target. That's why I stay until the last wagon."

Sefa looked at this woman — a career archivist who had traded a warm office for a war — and thought about her own crew, asleep in shifts below decks, and about the *Salt-Kissed* riding low with evacuees' trunks instead of cargo.

"Last wagon leaves when I say it leaves," Sefa said. "Not when the army says. Not when the river says. Me. Get your names packed, archivist."

* * *

The third day, the usurper's engineers built their pontoon bridge, and Sefa learned what costly courage actually weighed.

They tried everything. Fire-barges on the current — two made it, one burned true, and the second was caught by grappling lines and dragged ashore, and the thing the crown-thing's officers did to her captured crew was something Sefa made herself watch from two hundred yards so she would never be able to pretend she hadn't known. Swivel-gun enfilades from the bluffs. Ondo led a boarding party onto the pontoons themselves at midnight, axes and oil, and came back with seven of twelve and a look Sefa had never seen on his face before.

At noon the pale things crossed again, forty of them abreast, walking on the water now as if the river had been told to permit them, and the defense broke in earnest. The east-bank guns held for an hour. The militia held for twenty minutes. The line bent, buckled, and began to die.

Sefa fought on the dock with a boarding axe in each hand, and it was nothing like the clean knife-work of smuggling disputes; it was butcher's labor, exhausting, graceless, and she killed four of the rendered things and they did not scream and they did not bleed and one of them had her brother's face, worn like a coat, and she put the axe through it anyway because the alternative was worse.

Kess died at the ferry stairs, holding the gap so the last glass-singer family could reach the barges. Ondo died an hour later on the *Salt-Kissed*'s deck, firing the swivel-gun into the boarding things until the gun burst and took him with it. The Teller twins lived. Half her crew did not.

When the order finally came — Dain himself, blood-soaked, hauling her bodily off the dock stairs — the *Salt-Kissed* was already lost, grounded on a sandbar the rendered ones had shifted somehow, the river itself fighting for the enemy now. Sefa stood on the bluff with forty survivors and watched the crown's soldiers swarm her ship, her beautiful ship, and put the usurper's banner on her mast, and she did not cry, because crying was a luxury and the wagons were still moving west.

"Three days," Dain said quietly beside her. "You promised three days."

"We gave them three days." Her voice came out scraped raw. "Count them, huntsman. Count every hour. Somebody paid for each one."

Behind them, the road to Vhalen filled with refugees and smoke. Ahead of them, the city walls waited, and everyone knew what the folios said about what was coming for the cities next.

Sefa turned her back on the river. "Get them moving. All of them. If I lost my ship and my crew for anything less than every soul on this road reaching safety, I will come back as a ghost specifically to haunt you."

"You'd haunt me anyway," Dain said. "I raided your waystations for a decade."

"Damn right I would." She shouldered past him toward the wagons, toward Maren's lantern and the long dark road west, and only let herself look back once.

Far upriver, past the burning pontoons and the occupied wreck of her ship, the water ran red with sunset — and beneath the red, faint as a vein under skin, she could have sworn the river glowed the pale gold of gate-light, as if something far away in the grasslands had just begun, at last, to open.
