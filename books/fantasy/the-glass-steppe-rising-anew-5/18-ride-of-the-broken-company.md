## 18. Ride of the Broken Company

They came to him at dusk, in ones and twos, the way penitents come to a shrine they're not sure still answers.

Dain was cleaning his sword by the fire when he heard the horses. Four riders, no banners, riding the way huntsmen ride — spread out, weight low, watching the treeline out of habit that dies hard. He knew three of them before they dismounted. The fourth he knew only as a shape against the last of the light, and then as a face he'd helped train.

"Corvale." Halric Vane said it like an accusation and a greeting at once. He'd been Dain's second for six years, back when Dain had still believed the warrant in his hand meant something clean. "We heard you were dead."

"You heard I was a traitor," Dain said. "Same thing, in the capital's mouth."

"That too." Halric looked at the fire, at Sefa Ilun's smugglers pretending not to listen along the far side of the camp, at the gifted children sleeping in a heap of blankets near the wagons. "There are eleven of us left from Third Company who wouldn't do it anymore. We couldn't do it anymore." He swallowed. "After Vhalen fell. After we saw what they did with the ones they took. We deserted in a body and we've been looking for you since."

Dain set his whetstone down. "Why?"

Halric didn't flinch, which Dain respected. "Because you burned your warrant before any of us had the spine. Because you saw it first. And because—" He stopped, jaw working. "Because there's a woman in the occupying camp at Vhalen. An archivist. They took her alive, which means she matters, which means whatever she knows, the crown-thing wants it out of her head or into its own. And you're the only man we know who's ever gotten inside one of their camps and walked out again."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind Dain, Isandro Zhou stood up quietly from where they'd been sitting, listening.

"They've tripled the watch since the walls broke," Dain said. "The commander will be Ser Aldous Ren. He doesn't make mistakes."

"He made one," said the fourth rider, speaking for the first time. A young woman, maybe nineteen, with the hollow-eyed look of someone who'd seen rendering done from the wrong end of it. "He kept her alive. Everything else he does perfectly. But he kept her."

Dain looked at her a long moment. "You were in the camp."

"I carried her water. She talked to me. Told me stories about archives." The girl's mouth twisted. "Nobody talks to the ones who carry water. She made me feel like a person. Then they moved her to the deep tents and I ran."

Dain turned to Isandro. The keeper of ledgers had that look they always had — weighing something, setting invisible numbers against each other.

"Maren held the ritual texts," Isandro said. "She destroyed most of them, but if she read them all before burning them—" They didn't finish. They didn't need to. Everyone at the fire understood: the unbinding rite lived now in one place, and that place was behind enemy lines in a cage.

"We go tomorrow," Dain said. "Before dawn. Before they move her east."

* * *

Eleven deserters, nine smugglers, two keepers of impossible books, and himself. Twenty-three souls to strike a camp of four hundred.

Sefa Ilun called it *a funeral with extra steps*, and then started planning it anyway, because Sefa had never met odds she didn't want to cheat. She spread a charcoal map of the occupying camp on the floor of a gutted farmhouse and weighted the corners with knife hilts.

"The prisoners' pens are here, southwest corner, against the old granary. The deep tents — command tents — are the center, on the foundations of the merchant hall. Ren's banner flies there." Her finger traced lines. "Patrols ring the perimeter every quarter hour, but here—" she tapped a seam between the granary and the collapsed tannery wall "—the fires from the burning quarter never went out. Smoke so thick the sentries can't see ten feet. It hasn't cleared in four days. Nobody patrols it because nobody can."

"Then that's our door," Dain said.

"That's your door. I'm not taking my people through smoke blind."

"You don't have to. You take the north road and make noise. Big noise. Burned wagons, screaming, the works — make them think the free cities are counterattacking from the river side. Pull the reserve companies north."

"And leave you eleven huntsmen to walk into the center of four hundred soldiers."

"Twelve," said the girl with the hollow eyes. "My name is Petra. I know where the deep tents open."

Dain looked at her. Nineteen years old and volunteering to walk back into the camp that had caged her. He thought about saying no. He thought about how many times someone older and wiser had said no to him, and how little good it had done.

"Twelve," he agreed.

Isandro spoke from the doorway, arms folded. "The rite requires Maren's testimony. What she memorized. But it also requires a threshold-holder — someone who can stand at a boundary and hold it shut while power moves through it. That's what you were trained for, huntsman. Your whole discipline is thresholds: tracking across borders, holding quarry at a line. You may be the only person alive whose craft fits the work."

"I'm a traitor with a burned warrant."

"You're a threshold-keeper who stopped keeping the wrong threshold." Isandro's voice was flat as ledger ink. "I've been reading about your order. The old oaths, before the crown bought them. Do you know what the first line was?"

Dain didn't answer.

"*I hold the door so that what is behind it cannot pass.*" Isandro let that sit in the smoky air. "Tomorrow night, when we pull Maren out, remember the first line. Not the one they gave you. The first one."

* * *

They went in through the smoke at the hour before dawn, when even the diligent get stupid.

Dain had crossed borders in the dark for fifteen years — mountain passes, salt marshes, the teeth of the Glass Steppe itself — but he had never moved through anything like the burning quarter of Vhalen. The smoke was a living thing, thick as wool, tasting of ash and rendered fat and something sweeter underneath that he refused to name. The fires beneath it glowed without light, a red murk like being inside a wound.

Petra led. She had said she knew the ground, and she did — turning left at rubble piles, counting paces between landmarks only she could see, once stopping dead and holding up a fist until Dain heard it too: the wet crunching of some scavenger thing feeding in the dark ahead. They went around.

Twelve of them, roped together wrist-to-wrist so the smoke couldn't steal anyone. Behind Dain came Halric and the deserters, moving in the old formation, the one they'd drilled ten thousand times, except every man in it had cut his own oath loose. Dain felt the strangeness of that — leading the same company through the same kind of dark, toward the same kind of violence, with everything underneath changed.

The smoke thinned at the granary wall. Above them, the deep tents glowed with a light that wasn't torchlight. Dain had seen it before, at renderings — a pale gold that seemed to come from nowhere, the color of the crown-thing's attention. It made his teeth ache.

Two sentries at the tent line. Dain took the first with a hand over the mouth and a blade through the kidney, lowering the body silent as snowfall. Halric took the second. Neither man said anything after. There wasn't anything to say anymore; they were past words about it.

Petra slipped forward to the third tent from the banner pole. "This one," she breathed. "She's in this one. There's a warder on the door — a real one, not a soldier. He sits cross-legged and doesn't sleep."

A warder. Dain's stomach dropped. Warders weren't soldiers; they were half-rendered themselves, hollow men filled with a single purpose, incapable of distraction, incapable of mercy, incapable of dying easily.

"How many times have you killed a warder?" Halric whispered.

"Never," Dain said. "Nobody has. You put them down and they get back up. You burn them and the ash stands." He looked at the tent, at the pale glow seeping under its flap. Inside, somewhere, Maren Voss was keeping a kingdom's worth of forbidden knowledge behind her teeth, and every hour she kept it, they tortured her for it, and every hour she kept it anyway.

"Then we don't kill him," Dain said slowly. An idea was forming, ugly and desperate. "We use him. Warders anchor to their charge. If we take the charge away—"

"—he follows the charge," Petra finished. Her eyes widened. "He *has* to. It's all he is."

Dain unslung his pack and pulled out the thing Isandro had given him at midnight: a scrap of parchment bearing Maren's own handwriting, copied from a letter she'd sent the Steppe months ago, steeped in something the lich-broker had sold them that smelled like cold copper. *A tether*, Isandro had called it. *It will believe she's leaving. It will follow her.*

It would follow her straight out of the camp. And then, at the edge of the smoke, they would put it down however they could and run.

"If this fails," Dain said to his twelve, "you scatter and you live. None of you die for me tonight. That's an order, and I've got no warrant behind it, so it's just me asking."

Halric snorted. "Shut up and open the tent, Corvale."

* * *

Maren Voss was chained to a post in the tent's center, and Dain's heart broke a little at the sight of her — not because she was broken, but because she wasn't. They had worked her over for days. Her left eye was swollen shut, her fingers were wrong, her voice when she whispered *who's there* was a ruin. But her back was straight against the post. She had kept herself like a sealed archive: damaged, intact.

"Dain Corvale." Even ruined, her voice managed dry amusement. "The deserter. Isandro said you'd come. I told them you'd come. They didn't believe me either."

"Hold still." He cut the chains while Petra watched the flap. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk. I can't run. I can talk, though, and I have a great deal to say, none of which I'll be doing until we're beyond that smoke." Her good eye found the warder's empty chair by the entrance. "Where is my keeper? He never leaves."

"He's about to wish he hadn't." Dain pressed the tether-scrap into her bound hands. "Hold this. Think about leaving. Hard."

The effect was instant and horrible. From somewhere outside the tent came a sound like a bell struck underwater — a single low tone — and then footsteps, fast and wrong, joints bending in directions joints shouldn't. The tent flap tore open and the warder stood there, gray-faced, eyeless, its whole hollow attention fixed on Maren like a compass needle finding north.

"Walk," Dain said. "Slowly. Toward the smoke. Don't run — it matches pace with its charge."

They walked. Twelve rescuers around one archivist and one monster, threading between tents, past sleeping soldiers, past the banner pole where Ser Aldous Ren's colors hung dead in the windless dark. Fifty yards. A hundred. The smoke wall rose ahead of them like a cliff.

And then a lantern flared at the tent line, and a voice Dain knew better than his father's cut across the camp.

*"Corvale."*

Ser Aldous Ren stood alone in the lane between tents, armored, unhelmed, sword drawn. No shout for the guard. No alarm. Just him, awake at the worst hour, standing between them and the smoke the way he had stood between Dain and every disaster for fifteen years.

"Go," Dain said to the others. "Take her. Go."

"You'll die," Maren said.

"Then I'll die knowing the archive lives." He pushed her gently toward Petra's hands. "First line, archivist. Somebody has to hold the door."

They went — Maren, Petra, Halric, all of them, melting into the red murk while Dain turned to face the only man who had ever taught him what honor was before showing him what honor-cost.

Ren walked forward, unhurried. "Do you know why I'm awake? Because I felt the warder move. Because I've felt everything wrong since the night you left us." His sword point rose. "Come back, Dain. Kneel. Tell them it was coercion, tell them the archivist ensorcelled you. The crown-thing is merciful to things that kneel. I've seen it pardon worse than you."

"Worse than me," Dain repeated. "You mean the children. You've watched it render children, Aldous. I stood next to you while you watched. Don't tell me what mercy looks like in that camp."

Ren's jaw tightened. For a heartbeat, something flickered behind his eyes — doubt, or its ghost. "I serve the crown."

"You serve the thing wearing the crown. There's a difference, and it's the width of a kingdom." Dain drew his own blade. "I loved you, old man. You were the best part of what I believed. That's why I'm giving you one chance to stand aside."

Ren attacked.

It was the fight Dain had rehearsed in nightmares for a year, and it was worse than the nightmares, because Ren fought exactly as beautifully as he remembered. Every parry he'd learned from this man came back at him; every feint he'd stolen from Ren was turned against him. They broke a tent pole, crashed through a cookfire's ashes, traded cuts that opened skin on both sides — and all the while the camp slept on, and the smoke waited, and somewhere beyond it Maren was walking free.

Ren backed him against the granary wall and put his point at Dain's throat, and paused. That pause. Fifteen years of knowing this man, and Dain knew what the pause meant: *last chance, lad. Be sensible.*

"Aldous," Dain said quietly. "Behind you. Look at the tents. Look at what that light is."

Ren didn't look. But his arm trembled — barely, a hair's breadth — and in that tremble Dain saw the truth he'd ridden a thousand miles to find: the old man already knew. Had known, maybe, for years. Watched the renderings. Kept the ledgers of the taken. Carried it the way soldiers carry everything, in silence, until the silence became the man.

"I can't stop it," Ren whispered. It wasn't a battle line. It was a confession, wrung out of him by the sight of Dain refusing to die. "If I lay this sword down, they'll feed me to the binding with the rest. And if I don't—"

"Then don't lay it down." Dain stepped inside the blade, close enough that the point scraped his collarbone, close enough to speak into the face of the man who'd raised him. "But you don't have to swing it. Not at me. Not tonight. Put it in the dirt, Aldous. Just for ten minutes. Ten minutes, and the woman walks out of your camp with the knowledge that ends this, and you can pick your sword back up and tell yourself whatever you need to survive. I won't even ask you to come with us."

The trembling grew. Ren stared at him, and Dain watched fifteen years of discipline war with fifteen years of buried horror on the old man's face.

"I should have burned my warrant first," Ren said hoarsely. "That's the worst of it, boy. You stole my defection right out from under me."

His sword-point dipped. Not lowered — dipped, angled into the ash at Dain's feet, a soldier's compromise, a door left unlocked rather than opened.

Dain could have killed him then. The opening was total; Ren knew it and offered it anyway, eyes closed, waiting for the stroke that would settle everything — the mentor's betrayal, the order's corruption, the whole rotten cycle — with one clean cut.

Dain stepped back.

"No," he said. "You don't get to die for this. Living is harder. Live, and remember that the man you trained wouldn't kill you, and let that rot at you until you do something about it." He sheathed his sword. "Ten minutes started now."

He ran for the smoke, and Ren did not call the alarm, and the camp slept on, and Dain Corvale came out of the burning quarter of Vhalen bleeding but unbowed, carrying the only victory that had ever mattered to him: the one where he chose not to become the thing he hunted.

* * *

They reached the farmhouse as the sky went gray. Maren collapsed onto a pallet, and Isandro knelt beside her, and the two of them bent their heads together over scraps of parchment and memory, trading fragments of the rite in voices gone rough with relief.

Halric found Dain at the door, watching the smoke over Vhalen thin in the first light.

"Ren let us go," Halric said. It wasn't a question; the absence of pursuit said it.

"He let *me* go. Whether that spreads—" Dain shook his head. "It doesn't matter yet."

"It might." Halric nodded southward, toward the occupied city. "There are others like him, Dain. Whole companies of others. Men who kneel because kneeling is survival and no one ever showed them another door."

"Then someone will have to show them." Dain looked back through the doorway, to where Isandro and Maren were piecing together the unbinding like survivors rebuilding a house from its ashes. "But first, someone has to end it. Come on. We've got a king to wake."

Behind them, Maren lifted her head from the parchments, her one good eye wide.

"There's a problem," she said. "The rite needs a gate. A real one. And I know exactly where the nearest one is — and what it will cost to open."
