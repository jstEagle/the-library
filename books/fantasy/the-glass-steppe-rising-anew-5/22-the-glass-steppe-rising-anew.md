## 22. The Glass Steppe Rising Anew

The grass came back first.

That was what Isandro noticed on the morning of the reopening, standing at the top of the long stair with a broom in their hands and nothing to sweep. A year ago the Steppe around the guildhall had been trampled to bare dirt by soldiers and refugees and the wheels of Sefa's gun-carriages. Now the green had crept back over it like ink soaking into paper, patient and total, as if the land kept its own ledger and had simply struck out the war.

Isandro leaned on the broom and let the wind move through them.

They still woke some mornings reaching for a name that wasn't there anymore. The lich-broker had taken it cleanly, the way a surgeon takes a limb — one moment part of you, the next a smooth absence that ached only when the weather changed. *Ledger-name: Isandro Zhou, keeper of the Glass Steppe.* Gone. Sold for a rite of unbinding, spent to wake a king, and Isandro had walked out of that bone-and-ink shop lighter than they'd expected and emptier than they'd feared.

But names could be written twice. That was the whole lesson of the year.

Below, in the courtyard, the first students were arriving.

* * *

They came up the salt road in ones and twos at first, then in carts, then in a river. Gifted children whose parents had spent the Hunt years hiding them in root cellars and chimney flues. Gifted adults who had never told anyone what they could do, not even their spouses. A girl of nine who made glass sing. An old ferryman who'd been holding back floods with his mind since before the dynasty and was tired, so tired, of pretending he hadn't.

Maren met them at the gate with the new registry — an honest one, open pages, nothing hidden inside trade entries, because there were no more trade entries. The ledgers of impossible things had been closed for good. What the Steppe traded in now was harder to weigh and worth more.

"Name?" Maren would ask each arrival, pen ready.

And they would give it — their true name, sometimes for the first time aloud — and Maren would write it down without flinching, and that act alone, Isandro thought, was a kind of magic older than anything bound behind the dead gate.

Maren herself had changed less than the archive had. She wore her archivist's grey still, but the seal of the crown was gone from her collar, replaced by a plain brass pin shaped like an open book. She had spent the winter cataloguing the salvaged folios — hers, the guild's, the fragments pulled from Vhalen's burning archive — and building something she called the Honest Record. Every entry dated. Every source named. Every lie the old archives had told, annotated in red ink in her small furious hand.

"The court sorcerer's binding took four generations to hollow one family," she'd said once, late over the candles, showing Isandro the timeline she'd drawn across three tables pushed together. "It took us one year to write down how we stopped it. Do you understand what that means? We are the first generation that gets to know itself."

Isandro understood. It was why they'd asked her to stay.

* * *

Dain Corvale arrived in spring with eleven wardens and a scarred face that finally matched his reputation.

He'd been offered a commission by the restored king — Lord Warden of the Realm, a title with lands attached — and had turned it down in front of the whole court, politely, which those who knew him agreed was the most frightening thing he'd ever done. "I've answered to a crown," he told Isandro later, sitting on the wall above the old caravan yard while his trainees ran drills below. "I know what it costs to obey. I'd rather teach people what it costs to be trusted."

His wardens answered to no crown. They answered to the Steppe charter, one page, written by committee — which meant argued over for six weeks — and signed by every hand that served under it. They escorted refugee caravans. They stood between gifted children and the fear that still lived in some villages like damp in a wall. They carried warrants issued by no king, which meant they could be revoked by no king either.

Dain trained them the way he'd been trained, except for the parts he refused to repeat. No oaths of blood. No quarry lists. And one rule he'd carved himself into the lintel of the warden hall, where every recruit passed under it on their first day:

*A hunt ends when you lay it down.*

Isandro had watched him say goodbye to his old commander, too — from a distance, at the amnesty trials in the capital, where the men who had built the Hunt were called to account. Dain had not asked for the axe. He had asked for the truth to be entered into the record, all of it, his own part included. Maren had transcribed every word. The Honest Record did not flatter its friends.

"He could have hated me forever," Dain said that day on the wall, watching a warden recruit — a stuttering boy who could quiet storms — fumble a shield drill. "I gave him reasons."

"You chose otherwise," Isandro said.

"Took me thirty years and one very bad warrant." He shrugged. "Everything else was just walking."

* * *

Sefa Ilun sailed again in summer, which surprised no one who had ever met her.

She'd lost her ship at the river — the *Salt-Luck*, burned to the waterline holding the bridge against the vanguard — and half her crew besides, and Isandro had braced themselves for grief worn as rage, the way Sefa wore everything. Instead she'd stood on the scorched quay at Vhalen, looked at the wreck, and said, "Well. She always said she wanted to go out loud."

The free cities bought her a new ship within the month. Three of them competed over it. She named it *Ledger-Entry*, "because I'm sentimental and nobody can prove otherwise," and ran cargo up and down the coast — grain, timber, books, letters between families the Hunt had scattered. No contraband anymore, she claimed. When Isandro raised an eyebrow at this, she amended: "No *registered* contraband."

She came up the salt road twice a season with goods and gossip and crew members who needed mending, physical or otherwise. Half her sailors had been rendered-adjacent — touched by the harvest camps, kin to the taken — and the Steppe's healers and teachers put them back together piece by piece. Sefa paid for it in freight, loudly, and pretended it was charity in reverse.

"Owe the place too much to let it starve," she told Isandro, dumping a crate of capital oranges on the kitchen table like tribute. "Besides, someone has to keep you supplied. You're terrible at asking for things."

"I'm a foundling raised by accountants," Isandro said. "Asking is the one trade I never learned."

"Well." She stole an orange and headed for the door. "Learn. You've got students now. They'll ask you for everything."

* * *

The school opened on the first day of autumn, because Maren insisted that institutions born in spring got sentimental about themselves, and the Steppe had been sentimental enough for one lifetime.

There was no ceremony. There was a bell — the old market bell, recast — and Maren reading the charter aloud, and then the doors were simply open, and children poured into halls that had smelled of ink and gold-dust for two hundred years and now smelled of chalk and bread.

Isandro taught one class. Just one. *Weights and Measures,* they called it, though it was really a class about judgment: how to look at a thing — a trade, a plea, a stranger at your gate — and decide what it truly weighed, and whether any scale could hold it. They used the guild's old casebooks as texts, the real ones, including Isandro's own crimes. The forged expulsion record. The hidden names inside trade entries. The bargain with the lich-broker.

"This entry is falsified," Isandro told the class, laying the page flat where everyone could see. "I wrote it to save lives. Discuss."

The discussions lasted until the candles drowned. Some students said a rule broken for mercy was still broken, and the next breaker might not be merciful. Some said rules that required cruelty were already broken and someone merely had to admit it. One girl — the one who made glass sing, who had spent the Hunt years in a cellar and come out fierce as a struck flint — said, "You didn't break the ledger. You wrote the truest thing in it. Everything else was prices."

Isandro had given her extra reading, mostly to hide their face.

* * *

On the last evening of the reopening week, when the students were abed and the wardens' lamps were low and Maren was asleep over a folio in the archive as usual, Isandro went down to the gate.

The stair was unchanged. Two hundred steps, spiraling into the earth beneath the guildhall, past the antechamber door that had not opened since the night the first king took Isandro's place at the threshold and the rite tore the binding loose across the whole breadth of the kingdom.

The gate was dead now. Truly dead — not shut, not sealed, but finished, the way a story is finished. The stone held no hum. The air beyond the door was just air. Whatever breathed behind it for two hundred years had gone home at last, or gone wherever kings go when their long captivity ends, taking the binding and the hunger and the crown's dark arithmetic with him.

Isandro had checked every week for a year. Not from fear. From something closer to tending — the way you visit a grave that holds someone you loved in a complicated way.

Tonight they brought a key.

Not the old key; that had melted in the final rite, along with much else. This one Maren had cast from the fragments, ceremonial, useless, engraved with the date of the unbinding and one line in the first king's own hand, copied from the letter he'd sent the Steppe before his death last winter — old, frail, weeping with gratitude, and sharper than any man his age had a right to be:

*Some doors are not for passing through. They are for remembering what we locked away, and why.*

Isandro set the key in the lock. Turned it. Heard the mechanism engage one final time, a sound like a full stop.

Then they knelt, and pressed their palm flat against the cold stone, and said the words they'd never managed to say during the fighting or the grief or the rebuilding, the words that had waited a year for a quiet room:

"Thank you. Rest now. We'll remember."

The stone stayed cold. The silence stayed silence. But Isandro rose feeling heard anyway, which was perhaps the whole secret of prayer, and perhaps of doors.

* * *

They climbed back up into the last of the light.

The Steppe stretched gold to every horizon, the grass tall enough to comb, moving in long slow waves like something breathing — but kindly now, the way a sleeping animal breathes, nothing waiting behind it. Out on the road, the last supply cart of the season was coming up from Vhalen, and Isandro could see Sefa on the box, arguing cheerfully with the driver about the definition of contraband. In the courtyard, Dain was running evening drills by lamplight, and the storm-quieting boy had finally gotten the shield form right, and Dain's shout of approval carried all the way to the stairhead. In the archive window, Maren's candle still burned, steady as an honest entry.

A year ago, Isandro Zhou had been a name in a ledger, and the ledger had been the world, and every weight had its price.

Now the ledger-name was spent. The ledgers were closed. And the person standing at the top of the stairs with the wind pulling at their coat was no one the old books had ever recorded — which meant, Isandro thought, that they were finally, fully, the only author left.

They walked to the new registry office, where the fresh ledger sat open on the desk, its first page blank, waiting the way all beginnings wait.

Isandro dipped the pen.

Wrote, in a clear and careful hand, the only entry that had ever truly mattered:

*Isandro Zhou. Keeper of the Glass Steppe. Chosen.*

Then they blew out the candle, locked the dead gate's memory behind them, and walked out into the grass.
