## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The salt had begun to whisper again.

Wren Dunmore noticed it the way they noticed most things on the Thornfell passes — not with eyes or ears but with the fine hairs along their forearms, the ones that had learned to read weather before they could read letters. Salt carried in canvas sacks across three mountain ridges developed a voice: a dry, shifting susurrus when the wind found a loose seam, a heavier silence when damp crept close. Tonight the whisper was wrong. Too rhythmic. Like something breathing under the cloth.

Or like Wren's own nerves, translated into grain.

They adjusted the pack straps where they crossed their shoulders — left strap over the old burn scar, always over, never against — and kept climbing. The trail here was barely a trail at all, a goat's argument with the mountainside that smugglers had adopted out of necessity. Below and behind them, the border wards of Vaelmark hummed their nightly hymn, a sound so constant that most people in the lowlands forgot it entirely, the way one forgets one's own heartbeat. Wren never forgot it. The wards had been the last thing they'd heard on the day of their exile, singing them out of the capital like a door closing.

*Seven years*, they thought, counting the switchbacks without meaning to. *Seven years since anyone sang me anywhere.*

The pass called the Shepherd's Tooth rose ahead, black against a sky so crowded with stars it looked flayed open. That was the other thing about Thornfell: up here, above the smog of the valley forges and the haze of the river country, the stars came down close enough to offend. Wren had grown up in the capital, where the sky was a polite rumor, and the first night they'd slept on these slopes they'd lain awake half the night feeling watched by a thousand cold eyes.

Now those eyes were the only company they wanted. Stars didn't ask questions. Stars didn't recognize your face from wanted notices seven years stale. Stars simply burned, indifferent and reliable, and Wren had built a whole life in the space of that indifference.

The salt whispered. Wren climbed.

* * *

They smelled the mule before they saw it — blood and fear-sweat cutting through the pine resin — and had their knife free before their mind finished the thought. Old instincts. The kind you paid for once and carried forever, interest accruing.

The animal stood thirty paces below the switchback, tangled in its lead rope, sides heaving. Its rider lay crumpled against a boulder with the particular boneless sprawl of someone who had stopped deciding things. Wren approached the way they approached all gifts on this road: sideways, slow, expecting the trap.

But there was no ambush. There was only a dead man with a merchant's soft hands and a ledger strapped to his chest, and a mule loaded with more salt than any honest trader would haul through the high passes in autumn.

Wren crouched beside the body. No wound they could find. The man's eyes were open and fixed on the sky, and his face wore an expression Wren had seen exactly once before, years ago, on the face of a soldier who had watched something impossible happen and understood, in the last instant, that the world was not what he'd been told.

"Bad luck," Wren said quietly, because saying nothing felt worse. They took the ledger. Taking from the dead was another thing exile taught you — not cruelty, just arithmetic. The man no longer needed accounts of who owed him what; the living did. And if Wren was going to survive whatever had killed him, coin would help.

The ledger's last entry, ink still faintly wet, read: *Delivered to Grayfen tollhouse, payment promised, wards flickering at the—*

The sentence stopped. Not abandoned. Stopped, as though the hand writing it had been interrupted mid-word, or as though the writer had looked up at something and never come back to the page.

Wren looked up too.

* * *

The wards shattered at the third hour past moonset.

There was no other word for it. Wren had felt magic break before — small things, a hexed lock failing, a charm souring in a rival smuggler's pocket. Breaking magic sounded like tearing linen, like a snapped bowstring. This was none of that. This came up through the soles of their boots and the bones of their shins and the roots of their teeth, a soundless concussion, a single vast note struck on an instrument the size of the horizon. Every hair on Wren's body lifted. The salt in their pack went silent all at once, as though even the grain were listening.

Then the afterimage hit, and Wren understood what they were hearing, because for one terrible heartbeat they *saw* it: the border wards rendered visible in their mind's eye, the great woven lattice of light that spanned the Thornfell passes north to south, a tapestry two hundred years in the weaving — and it tore. Top to bottom. All of it. Not fraying, not failing.

*Shattering.* Like a window struck by a bird. Like glass remembering it was sand.

Wren dropped to their knees on the frozen trail and vomited bile into the dark, because the breaking of the wards had passed through them like a blade through water, and some part of them — the part they kept locked and starved and pretended wasn't there — had *recognized* the signature of the working that broke it. Had known it the way you know a voice through a wall. Storm-work. Battle-craft. The ugly cousin of everything they had once been trained to do.

"No," Wren said aloud, to themselves, to the mountain. "No."

And then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. There were no clouds; Wren would have welcomed clouds, would have knelt in gratitude for a simple storm. The stars simply ceased, all of them, in the same instant, from horizon to horizon — snuffed like a thousand candles pinched between a thousand invisible fingers. The Milky Way, that pale river Wren had steered by for seven years, drained away to nothing. The Shepherd's Tooth, which had been black stone against silvered snow, became black stone against blacker nothing.

The dark that followed was total in a way Wren had never experienced. Not night-dark. *Absent*-dark. A darkness with weight, pressing on the eyeballs, filling the mouth.

They heard themselves making a sound. It took several seconds to identify it as their own voice, repeating a name they hadn't spoken in seven years — the name of the observatory hill outside the capital, where the star-priests had kept their instruments and their vows. Where the sky had been tended like a garden.

Where, according to the official histories, nothing bad had ever happened at all.

* * *

Fear is a fire, and Wren Dunmore had long ago learned to bank it rather than fight it. Fighting fear fed it. You let it burn down to coals, and then you cooked with it.

So they knelt in the absolute dark on a trail they could no longer see, and they breathed, four counts in, six counts out, and they made their mind do the thing it did best: inventory.

*What do I know?*

One: the wards are broken. All of them, or enough of them that the difference doesn't matter. Two: whatever broke them used storm-craft, which means soldiers or apostates or worse, and either way the passes will fill with armed men within days, because the crown cannot leave a hole in its border unfilled any more than a body can leave a wound unstitched. Three: the stars are gone, and the stars do not go out. Candles go out. Stars do not. Which means someone put them out, which means someone *could* put them out, which means every story Wren had ever dismissed as drunk-talk and priest-lies about the purge of the star-priests twenty years ago was suddenly, horribly, back on the table.

Four: I am alone on a mountain with no light, a dead man's ledger, a stolen mule's worth of salt, and a face that certain people would pay good coin to find.

Five: I am still alive, and alive is a direction.

That was the arithmetic. Dread sat in Wren's chest like a swallowed stone, but dread was a luxury for people with shelter. Survival instinct took the stone and ground it into grit and packed it into the soles of their boots, and Wren stood up in the blind dark and began, carefully, to move.

Because here was the secret of the Thornfell passes, the thing no map recorded: the mountain lived in the body. Seven years of running salt had written the Shepherd's Trail into Wren's muscles the way scripture was written into a monk. Left foot on the flat stone, right hand to the ironwood root — the root had been there seven years ago and would be there tonight, because ironwood outlived empires. Three steps, then the knee-high shelf. Four steps, then the drop-off on the left, so hug the wall. The body knew. The body had always known, even back in the capital, even in the uniform, even standing in the rain refusing an order. Some knowledge you couldn't exile.

Wren descended by memory, one blind step at a time, and the darkness pressed close, and somewhere far below — impossibly far, down in the valley where the border towns huddled against the cold — fires began to bloom.

At first they were scattered. A farmstead lighting lamps against nothing. Then more, clustering, spreading, a rash of orange points in the valley floor like embers kicked loose from a hearth. Wren counted as they climbed down toward them, because counting was a thing hands and minds could do together while the rest of the soul screamed.

Thirty fires. Fifty. A hundred. Whole villages lit at once, at the third hour of night, in a season when firewood was rationed like medicine. People were frightened. People were coming out of their houses to look at a sky with no stars in it, and finding the dark so complete that they were lighting everything they owned just to prove the world still existed.

And beyond the fires, far to the east, down where the valley narrowed toward the lowlands and the capital beyond — a different kind of light. White and moving. Lanterns in formation. A column of riders, maybe, or soldiers with torches, already moving toward the broken border like antibodies toward a wound.

Toward Wren.

*They'll seal the passes,* Wren thought, and the thought arrived calm and cold, stripped of everything personal, which was how they knew it was true. *First thing any competent commander does. Close the roads, question everyone on them. And every smuggler, every courier, every soul on these mountains becomes either a suspect or an informant.*

They reached the bottom of the switchbacks where the trail met the old drover's road, and there they stopped, and there they finally let themselves feel it — all of it, the ward-shatter still ringing in their teeth, the murdered stars, the dead man's interrupted sentence, the fires multiplying in the valley like a fever rash. Fear and grief and a fury so old they'd forgotten where it had been buried, all rising at once.

Wren let it rise. Then they let it settle. Then they turned it, as they had learned to turn everything in seven years of exile, into the only currency that mattered on this mountain: forward motion.

The drover's road ran down-valley toward Grayfen, toward the tollhouse, toward Toma the toll-keeper who owed Wren two favors and a lie, in roughly that order of reliability. Toma would have news. Toma always had news, and wine of criminal quality, and a cellar deep enough to hide a mule. If the roads were closing, Wren needed to know how fast, and whether there was still time to get over the southern ridge before the net drew tight.

They took the ledger and the salt and left the dead man his dignity and his boots — the boots were too big anyway — and set off along the road with the valley fires painting the underside of the clouds a bruised orange, and the sky above the clouds a darkness so profound it seemed less like night than like a held breath.

Half a mile on, the wind changed.

It came down off the northern peaks carrying the smell of snow and pine, and underneath those smells, faint as a thread pulled through cloth: ozone. The sharp, metallic tang of spent lightning.

Wren stopped dead in the middle of the road.

Ozone meant storm-work, recent, close. Within hours. Within miles. Someone else on this mountain had done what Wren had sworn never to do again — had opened that door in themselves and let the sky answer — and the wind had carried their signature straight down the drover's road like a scent to a hound.

Every instinct said *walk away*. Find a hollow, bury yourself, wait out whatever war was beginning. That was the rule that had kept Wren Dunmore alive for seven years: never be where the lightning has been.

But the wind shifted again, and beneath the ozone there was something else now. Something that stopped Wren's heart and restarted it wrong.

Blood. Fresh. And under the blood, threaded through it like a note struck inside a bell, a warmth that had no business existing on a freezing mountain under a dead sky — a warmth that felt, absurdly, impossibly, like sunlight.

Like starlight.

Wren stood on the dark road with the valley burning below and the empty heavens pressing down, and understood that the night had just asked them a question, and that whatever answer they gave next would follow them the rest of their life.

Their feet, which had always known the mountain better than their head, were already moving toward the light.
