## 2. Salt Roads and Old Wounds

The dark was wrong.

Wren had lived through storms that swallowed the sun whole, through blizzards that turned noon to twilight, through the ash-fall years when the Thornfell peaks burned and the sky rained cinders for a season. They knew every shade of darkness the mountains could produce. This was none of them. This was a darkness with weight, a darkness that pressed against the eyes like water, as though the night itself had been poured over the world in one unbroken sheet.

And it was empty. That was the part that made their hands shake. No stars. Not one. Wren had navigated by those stars since they were six years old, when their grandmother had put a calloused finger under their chin and said, *The sky is a map, little storm, and maps can't lie even when kings do.* The Shepherd's Crook hung low over the eastern passes in autumn; the Salt Queen's Crown wheeled above Grayfen at midnight; the pale smear of the River of Heaven marked true north better than any compass forged by human hands. Every smuggler on the high roads swore by them. Every exile like Wren had learned them before learning to read.

Now there was nothing. The sky was a page torn out of the book.

Wren stood at the mouth of the pass they'd descended from — the Kestrel's Throat, though no one had named it that yet in any map worth trusting — and let the darkness press in until their breathing slowed. Panic was a luxury. Panic got people killed on the salt roads, where a single misstep meant a hundred-foot fall onto rocks that had never been kind to anyone. They flexed their scarred fingers inside their gloves, felt the old burn-tissue pull and settle, and did what they always did when the world broke open: they started counting.

Three hours since the wards shattered. They'd felt it go through their teeth first, then their spine — a soundless detonation, like glass breaking somewhere behind the sky. The border wards had stood for two hundred years, woven by the star-priests back when the crown still pretended to honor them, humming so constantly along the mountain passes that the smugglers called it *the king's snore*. Wren had crossed beneath that hum a thousand times without thinking about it, the way you don't think about your own heartbeat.

Then tonight the heartbeat stopped, and every star went out with it, and somewhere below in the valley the watchfires of Thornfell village were burning orange and frantic, and people were screaming in ways Wren could hear even from this height, thin and carried on the wind like the cries of gulls.

*Don't go down,* said the voice of survival, the cold sensible voice that had kept them alive through seven years of exile. *Whatever happened, it happened everywhere at once. That means soldiers will move. Inquisitors will move. The smart play is to hole up in the salt cave until the roads sort themselves out.*

But another voice, older and less sensible, said: *People are screaming.*

Wren cursed softly and started down.

* * *

Navigating without stars was like walking through a house you'd grown up in after someone had rearranged all the furniture in the dark. Wren's body remembered the road even when their mind couldn't confirm it — the way the path bent left around the third sentinel pine, the dip where meltwater carved a gully each spring, the place where the cliff face leaned close enough to brush a shoulder. Their boots found these things by faith. Twice they stopped, closed their eyes, and listened to the mountain itself: the trickle of the hidden spring to the east, the groan of wind through the stone arch the locals called the Old Man's Mouth. Small certainties. Enough to keep moving.

They thought about lighting the lantern hanging at their belt and decided against it. Light drew eyes, and eyes on the high roads tonight would belong to things worse than wolves. Instead they moved by touch and memory and the faint orange stain the valley fires bled into the low clouds, a bruise-colored glow that gave them just enough horizon to keep their bearings.

It was on the long switchback above the Grayfen tollhouse — a stretch of road Wren knew so well they could have walked it blindfolded, which was fortunate, because they effectively were — that they smelled blood.

Not animal blood. Animal blood had its own signature, iron and musk and fear. This was human, sharp and coppery, threaded with something else underneath: a smell Wren hadn't encountered in seven years and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a song from childhood. Ozone. Clean rain. The smell of magic spent or bleeding out.

Wren froze, hand going to the knife at their hip.

"Who's there?" Their own voice sounded strange in the dead air, flattened by the starless dark.

Nothing. Wind. Then — a sound so small they almost missed it. A breath, wet and ragged, catching on something torn.

They found her ten paces off the road, crumpled against the base of the sentinel pine where the ground shelved away toward the gorge. She'd crawled, by the look of it — a furrow dragged through the frost-rimed scree, one boot half-shed, both hands locked around an iron lantern pulled tight against her chest the way a child holds a doll against nightmares. She was young, maybe mid-twenties, dark-haired, dressed in travel-stained clothes that had once been fine enough to mark her as somebody. Somebody from the capital, Wren's instincts supplied. Somebody who'd run a very long way.

The lantern was the strange thing. It was old iron, dented, unlit — and yet warmth rolled off it in waves Wren could feel from three feet away, like standing near a hearth. And beneath the warmth, so faint most people would have missed it entirely, it *hummed*. Not the king's-snore hum of the border wards. Something deeper. Something that resonated in Wren's chest cavity and set their teeth buzzing and made the fine hairs on their arms stand up like hackles.

Wren knew exactly one other thing that had ever made them feel that way.

Their own hands, the night the storm answered them for the first time.

"No," Wren said aloud, to themselves, to the mountain, to whatever gods had stopped maintaining the sky. "No, absolutely not."

The woman's eyes opened. They were gray, fever-bright, unfocused — but they fixed on Wren with startling ferocity, and her grip on the lantern tightened until her knuckles went white.

"Don't," she rasped. "Don't take it."

"I don't want your bloody lantern." Wren crouched, keeping their distance, cataloging wounds with the detached efficiency of someone who'd patched up enough smugglers to do it in their sleep. Knife wound, left side, deep but angled away from anything vital — someone had known what they were doing, both the cutter and whoever had stitched it shut with rough silk thread that had since soaked black. A second wound at the shoulder, shallower. Frost-nip on the fingers. She'd been running for days. "Who cut you?"

Her laugh came out cracked. "Would you believe me if I told you?"

"On this road? Tonight?" Wren glanced upward, at the nothing where the stars should be. "I'd believe you if you told me the moon itself came down with a sword."

Something flickered across her face — not quite amusement, more like recognition of a joke she didn't have the strength to share. Then her eyes rolled back and she slid sideways, and Wren caught her before her skull met stone, and that was that. Decision made. The universe had a cruel sense of humor about presenting choices only after they'd already been made.

*Leave her,* whispered the sensible voice, weaker now. *She's marked. Whatever bled that magic smell into the air is looking for her, and when it finds her, it'll find you.*

Wren looked down at the woman in their arms, at the iron lantern radiating its impossible warmth between them, at the blood soaking through to their own sleeves. Seven years ago they had stood in a marble courtyard and refused an order, and everything they loved had been taken from them piece by piece — rank, name, home, country. They had learned the lesson thoroughly. Self-preservation was a discipline, like any other. They were very good at it.

And yet.

"You'd better not be important," Wren told the unconscious woman grimly, hauling her upright across their shoulders. She weighed less than she should have. Days without food, then. "Because if you're important, I'm going to regret this in ways I can't currently imagine."

The lantern, slung against her back now, pulsed its warmth against Wren's spine like a second heartbeat. It felt, absurdly, like approval.

* * *

The salt cave was two miles north, off the proper road, behind a curtain of hanging roots that Wren parted by feel. It wasn't much — a hollow in the mountainside where an ancient underground brine-seep had left the walls glittering white and faintly sweet-smelling, wide enough for four people to sit comfortably, invisible unless you already knew it was there. Smugglers had used it for generations. Wren had slept in it perhaps fifty times. It was the closest thing to home the high roads offered, which said more about Wren's life than they liked to dwell on.

They built a fire small enough to hide — dry scrub and salt-bleached driftwood from the old lakebed, burning hot and nearly smokeless — and laid the woman down beside it. Then they worked.

The side wound had torn its stitching. Wren cleaned it with melted snow and the harsh spiritsmell they distilled themselves, and the woman woke halfway through, gasping, one hand flying to the lantern before her eyes had even opened.

"It's here," Wren said. "Your lantern. Nobody's touched it. Hold still or you'll tear what I'm fixing."

She held still. Mostly. Her breath came in short controlled pulls, the breathing of someone who'd been taught to endure pain quietly, and she watched Wren work with an attention that had nothing feverish in it at all. Assessing. Weighing. Wren had seen that look before, on wanted men and double agents and one memorable cardinal's courier, and it always meant the same thing: *this person is deciding how much truth you can survive.*

"You're good at that," she said, as Wren tied off the last knot.

"I've had practice. Exile teaches you field medicine whether you want it or not." They sat back, wiped their hands, and let the silence stretch just long enough to be a question. "Name?"

A pause. Long enough to matter.

"Kestrel," she said finally.

"That's a bird, not a name."

"It's the only one you're getting tonight." But something in her mouth twitched, almost a smile, and the ferocity in her gray eyes banked itself down to embers. "You haven't given me yours either."

"No," Wren agreed. "I haven't."

They fed the fire a stick and considered her. She was watching them still, and now her gaze had dropped — briefly, quickly, but not quickly enough — to their hands. To the gloves. Wren kept gloves on even indoors; everyone on the salt roads knew Dunmore the salt-runner wore gloves winter and summer and had never once explained why. Most assumed vanity or disfigurement and lost interest. But this woman's eyes had lingered with something else in them. Recognition, maybe. Or the hunger of someone hunting for a particular kind of answer.

"The stars," she said suddenly. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Did you see when they—"

"I saw."

"What does it mean?" And there it was, under the control, under the calculation: raw terror, the kind Wren had heard in the valley screams. "What *happened*?"

Wren fed the fire again rather than answer immediately. What did it mean? It meant the wards were dead, and the wards and the stars had been braided together since before living memory — every child in the kingdom knew the story, the priests' great working, heaven anchored to earth so the crown could never quite touch either. It meant either the greatest miracle in two centuries or the greatest crime. It meant the king's snore had stopped, and Wren did not believe for one moment that the king had simply stopped breathing on his own.

"It means the roads are about to get very dangerous," Wren said instead. "Soldiers moving in the dark, inquisitors with nothing to hold them back. You shouldn't be on the high passes. Whoever cut you will come looking."

"They always come looking." Kestrel's hand found the lantern again, resting on its cold-warm iron the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal. "They don't stop."

"Who is *they*, Kestrel-the-only-name-you're-getting?"

She looked at the fire for a long moment. The light moved across her face and Wren saw how exhausted she truly was, how far past the edge of collapse, running on some fuel deeper than muscle. When she spoke, her voice was barely above the crackle of the flames.

"If I tell you," she said, "you'll have a choice to make. And I've watched too many people make that choice badly. So I'm asking you — and I know we met an hour ago, and I know you owe me nothing — but I'm asking you anyway. Don't make me tell you yet. Let me sleep. Let me decide, tomorrow, whether you're someone who can carry it."

Any sensible person would have insisted. Any sensible person would have demanded answers at knifepoint, or better, taken the strange humming lantern and left the strange wounded woman to her secrets and her fate. Wren had survived seven years by being precisely that sensible.

Instead they heard themselves say: "Sleep. I'll keep watch."

Kestrel stared at them. Something in her expression shifted — the assessment dissolving into something rawer, more surprised, almost grief-stricken in its relief. "Why?" she asked. "Why would you help me? You don't know me. You don't know what I'm carrying."

Wren looked at the fire. At their gloved hands, scarred beneath the leather, hands that had once been famous in a marble courtyard and were now famous nowhere at all. At the dark mouth of the cave beyond, where the starless night pressed in like a held breath.

"Because someone once helped me when they shouldn't have," they said. "And I've spent seven years trying to pay it forward badly. Consider this an installment."

Kestrel made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter weren't too heavy to lift just then. She curled around the lantern like a wren — no, like a kestrel — around its kill, and within minutes her breathing deepened into true sleep, the first real sleep she'd clearly had in days.

Wren sat watch by the small fire, knife loose in their grip, and listened to the impossible lantern hum its low warm note in the darkness. It sounded, they thought uneasily, like a held note. Like something sustaining itself. Like a heart that refused, against all evidence, to stop beating.

Outside, the sky stayed empty. And far to the south, though Wren could not have known it, something was climbing into the mountains with a patience like falling snow, following a warmth only it could feel.

The sensible voice in Wren's head had gone quiet. In its absence, all they could think was:

*I am going to regret this. And I think I already know why.*
