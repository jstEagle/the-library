## 3. The Lantern That Remembers Light

Pain was a country Kestrel had learned to map.

She had been living in its borders for six days now — or seven, or nine; the hours had gone soft at their edges like wet paper — and she knew its geography intimately. There was the province of her left side, where the crossbow bolt had torn through muscle and left a hot red territory that pulsed with every heartbeat. There was the colder dominion of the fever, which came and went like weather, fogging her thoughts and painting colors across the inside of her eyelids. And everywhere, beneath it all, the deep bedrock ache of exhaustion, the body's slow insistence that she stop, that she lie down in this snow, that she let the mountain take what the capital's knives had failed to take.

She did not lie down. She held the lantern instead.

The iron lantern was small enough to cup in both hands, old enough that its handle had worn smooth where generations of fingers had gripped it. To any soldier who might find her, it would look like nothing — a smuggler's trinket, a tinker's curiosity, dented and dark and cold as any other piece of worked metal in the world.

It was not cold. It had never once been cold since the night Master Ellery had pressed it into her hands with his burned and trembling ones, since he had said *run, child, run north and do not stop until the stars themselves cannot see you* — and then the stars could not see anyone anymore.

The lantern was warm. It hummed against her palms like a sleeping bird, like a hearth heard through a wall, like something alive remembering that it had once been alive. And when the fever took her hardest, when she drifted near the white shore of delirium, she could swear she felt it *listening* — leaning toward her heartbeat the way a cat leans toward a warm lap, hungry for something she could not give it.

Not yet. Not here. Not for anyone.

She had made herself that promise in the burning capital, and she repeated it now like a prayer, mouthing it into the frozen dark: *not yet, not here, not for anyone.*

The snow was falling again. Or perhaps it was not snow but ash; in her fever-dreams the distinction had blurred, and she kept seeing Thornmarket burning behind her eyes, the spires of the Starfall Temple collapsing inward like hands folding in surrender, the white-robed bodies in the courtyard arranged by the soldiers in rows so neat they might have been harvested grain. She blinked and saw them. She blinked and saw snow. There was no reliable difference between the two anymore, except that one melted on her lashes and the other only fell forever.

Footsteps.

Kestrel's whole body went rigid before her mind caught up — some animal intelligence beneath the fever seizing control, flattening her deeper into the shadow of the rock overhang, dragging the lantern against her chest where its warmth beat against her sternum like a second, smaller heart. The footsteps crunched closer through the crust of the drifts. Heavy, unhurried, deliberate. A hunter would have moved quieter. So would a wolf.

Then light — real light, lantern-light, the ordinary amber glow of ordinary flame — swung across the rocks above her, and a voice spoke into the dark.

"I can see your blood on the stones. It's the only color left in the world tonight, so you'll forgive me for noticing."

The voice was low, roughened by cold, neither man's nor woman's — or perhaps both, the way a river is both. It sounded tired. It sounded, absurdly, almost annoyed.

"Whoever you are," the voice went on, "you're bleeding on my road. I'd take it kindly if you were dead already, because I've no skill for burying and even less for healing. But if you're alive—" A pause. The lantern-light shifted, swept nearer. "If you're alive, I suppose we'll both have to be disappointed about something."

Kestrel weighed her options in the space of three heartbeats, which was all the time she had.

She could play dead. But her breath was visible, pluming silver in the cold, and the blood trail behind her was wide enough to read like a signature.

She could fight. Her knife was still in her belt; her arm was barely hers; the arithmetic there was short and ugly.

Or she could gamble. She could open her eyes, show this stranger just enough harmlessness, and study them while they studied her. Because that was the thing the fever had not burned out of her — Master Ellery's training, drilled into her through ten years of lessons in dark rooms: *observe first, speak last, trust nothing that offers itself freely.* She was the last keeper of an ember that could unmake a kingdom's oldest lie. Every step she took from here was a wager with everything riding on it.

She opened her eyes.

The figure crouching at the mouth of her shelter was lean and weathered, wrapped in a smuggler's grey cloak gone stiff with frost. Dark hair cut practical-short. Hands — she noticed hands, always hands, Master Ellery had taught her that too — scarred across both palms in long pale ridges, like someone who had once gripped lightning and been marked for it. Their face was sharp-boned and watchful, lit from below by their own lantern, and their expression held no kindness and no cruelty either. Just calculation, plain and honest as a coin.

"Alive, then," they said. "Disappointment all around." They set their lantern down between them, equidistant, deliberately — a border marker. "Can you walk?"

"No," Kestrel said. It cost her something to admit it, and she watched to see what they would do with the information.

They exhaled through their nose — irritation, or maybe relief at having the truth plainly given. "Of course not. Of course the one night in eleven years that every star in the sky goes out, I find half a corpse on the Grayfen road." They rubbed their face with one scarred hand. "Where are you hurt?"

"Left side. Bolt. Three days ago." Also a lie about the days — it had been longer — but a useful one. Let them think her weaker than she was, not stronger.

"Three days walking on a bolt wound in the Thornfells." They said it flatly, without admiration, but something flickered behind their eyes that Kestrel chose to file away. Recalculation. "You're either desperate or stubborn."

"Both."

That earned her a short exhale that might, in better conditions, have become a laugh. Then the stranger leaned closer, and Kestrel felt the moment tip — the scales hanging by a hair — as those eyes moved over her face, her clothing, the mud-stained hem of her travel cloak. Looking for insignia. Looking for livery. Looking, she realized, for a reason to leave her here to die.

Give them none, she thought. Be nobody. Be a refugee, a salt-runner's daughter, a woman with bad luck and a bad leg. Be anything but what you are.

But the lantern against her chest chose that exact moment to pulse — one soft, deep throb of warmth, like a heartbeat answering a nearby drum — and the stranger's gaze dropped to it instantly.

"What's that?" Sharp now. All the tiredness gone out of their voice, replaced by something quicker. "Under your coat."

"A hand-warmer." Kestrel heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled distantly at the trick of it. "Iron. Filled with coals. Smuggler's make — you'd know the craft better than me, surely."

"It doesn't smell of coals."

"Fever," she said. "I don't trust my nose."

The stranger stared at her for a long moment. The wind moved the frost-fur on their hood. Somewhere far below, in the valley, the fires of the towns still burned — the whole world lit now by flame alone, because the sky had gone blind — and their light climbed up the mountainside faint as remembered things.

"You know," the stranger said slowly, "on most nights, I'd search you. Take whatever's worth taking, point you toward the lowlands, and count myself generous for not taking your boots. But tonight the wards are dead, the stars are dead, and I'm standing on a road I've walked two hundred times that suddenly looks like a stranger to me. So I'm going to do something foolish instead." They shrugged off their pack and pulled a waterskin free. "I'm going to give you water and ask your name. And you're going to decide whether to tell me the truth, because I'll know a lie when I hear one. Everyone lies differently. Most people lie badly."

Kestrel's mind ran the calculation at fever-speed, and the calculation came back wrong in every direction. Give a false name, and risk nothing — but also learn nothing, and she needed to learn something, needed desperately to know whether the person crouched in front of her was a danger or the nearest thing to shelter in a world that had lost its roof. Tell the truth, and—

Tell the truth. But only a piece of it. A name was a small coin to spend, if she spent it wisely.

"Kestrel," she said. "Just Kestrel."

"Just Kestrel." They passed her the waterskin, and she drank with the careful greed of someone who knew better than to gulp. "Wanted notices usually say 'just,' you know. 'Just Kestrel, wanted for—' whatever it is. You never see a full name on those papers. As if cutting the family off makes the crime cleaner."

The water turned to ice in her throat.

Because she knew wanted notices. She had spent the last weeks memorizing them — nailed to tollhouse doors, pasted to tavern walls in the gray towns, inked in the crown's precise bureaucratic hand. *By order of the Inquisition, seek and detain.* And among the faces and names she had committed to memory the way Ellery had taught her to commit star-charts, there had been one that surfaced now, rising through the fever-haze with terrible clarity:

*DUNMORE, WREN. Former Storm-Caller, Third Circle, Royal Legion. Wanted for treason and desertion. Last seen, Thornfell passes. Note: subject bears ritual scarring upon both hands. Approach with extreme caution.*

Scarred hands.

She looked down, casually, at the hands holding the lantern-light steady for her — the long pale ridges crossing both palms, the marks she had noticed and filed and not understood five minutes ago.

Oh, she thought. Oh, no.

"Wren Dunmore," she said, before she could stop herself, before the fever or the fear or some reckless instinct decided for her. She watched their face go absolutely still — the stillness of an animal that has heard its name spoken by a hunter. "Third Circle. Storm-Caller. The one who wouldn't fire on the refugee columns at Vell's Crossing."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for snow to accumulate in the space between them.

"That was eleven years ago," Wren Dunmore said at last, very quietly. "You were a child."

"I read a poster."

"You read a *notice*, and you're lying in the snow with a hole in your side, reciting it back at me." Their eyes narrowed, and Kestrel saw the exact moment the balance tipped the other way — saw suspicion flood in behind those eyes like water through a cracked dam. "Nobody crosses the Thornfells alone with a bolt wound and a head full of old treason notices unless they're looking for someone. Or running from someone. Which are you?"

"Neither." Both. *Both,* screamed everything in her that had survived the burning temple, *both, you fool, you're running from the Inquisition itself and the thing in your arms is worth more than every head on every notice in the kingdom.* She pressed the lantern harder against her ribs and prayed to gods whose priests were dead that it would keep its warmth quiet.

"The crown burned the Starfall Temple," she said instead, because it was true, and truth was the only coin she had left that wasn't counterfeit. "Three weeks ago. They killed the priests. All of them. And that same night—" she lifted her chin toward the black vault of sky above the peaks, empty as a scraped bowl, "—that happened. You noticed, I assume."

"Everyone noticed."

"Everyone noticed," Kestrel agreed, "and no one has asked why. Because asking why gets you a visit from men like Inquisitor Halvard, and Halvard's visits end in fires." She was talking too much; the fever was loosening her tongue; she clamped down on it. But not before adding, softly: "I was there, Dunmore. When it burned."

Something moved through Wren's face then — fast, quickly buried, but she caught it. Not fear. Something older and more wounded than fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a person hearing an echo of a sound they had spent eleven years trying to forget.

"If you were there," Wren said carefully, "then you're either a survivor of the massacre, or a deserter from the men who made it. And I've got no love for either kind, these days. Survivors want revenge, and deserters want forgiveness, and both of them want to use whoever they find on a mountain to get it."

"And what do you want?" Kestrel asked.

The question landed harder than she intended. She watched it land — watched Wren Dunmore flinch somewhere behind the eyes, in the place where wanted notices and old choices lived.

"What I want," Wren said finally, "is to finish my run, get paid, and wake up tomorrow to a sky that makes sense. Since none of that is happening—" they gestured at the starless dark with something close to bitterness, "—what I want is irrelevant. What matters is that you can't walk, I can't carry you twenty miles to Grayfen without killing us both, and leaving you here is murder. I've done enough things I regret. I'm not adding murder in the snow to the list."

"So you'll help me."

"I'll get you to shelter. The old shepherd's cave, half a mile on. Fire, walls, time for that wound to be looked at properly." They were already moving as they spoke, shrugging their pack higher, pulling a coil of rope from it with hands that moved with the economy of long habit. "And then, Kestrel-who-was-there, you and I are going to have a conversation about why the most dangerous person I've met in eleven years collapsed on my road on the worst night in history. And you're going to tell me the truth, because—" the ghost of something crossed their mouth, not quite a smile, "—as I said. Everyone lies differently. You lie well. That worries me more than lying badly would."

Kestrel closed her eyes and let them haul her up, one scarred hand braced under her arm — and through the contact, through the fever and the pain and the fog, she felt the lantern against her chest give one slow, deep pulse of warmth, like a word being considered.

*Not yet,* she told it silently. *Not here. Not for anyone.*

But the ember had waited a hundred years in the dark of the temple vaults, and it was patient, and it hummed against her heart like something remembering how to hope — and Kestrel, carried stumbling through the starless dark by a traitor with lightning written on their hands, found that her careful arithmetic had finally come undone, and that what remained underneath the calculation was something terrifyingly simple:

She hoped this stranger could be trusted. She hoped it so hard it frightened her more than Halvard did.

Half a mile on, in the mouth of the shepherd's cave, Wren fed a fire into being — and when they knelt to lift the lantern-light close and examine her wound, their sleeve drew back, and Kestrel saw the scars climb past the wrists, up beneath the cloth, branching like frozen lightning across skin that had once touched the sky.

"Start talking," Wren Dunmore said, and struck the flint, and the fire leapt up between them, throwing both their shadows huge and wavering against the stone.

Kestrel opened her mouth — and found she no longer knew which truth she was about to tell.
