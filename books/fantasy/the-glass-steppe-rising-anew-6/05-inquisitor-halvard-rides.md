## 5. Inquisitor Halvard Rides

The wards died at moonrise, and Inquisitor Halvard felt it the way a blind man feels a door open — a change in the pressure of the world, a draft where there should have been none.

He was kneeling in his tent outside the garrison town of Marrow's Ford, saying the evening offices of the Unblinking Eye, when the sensation came. He did not stop praying. He finished the litany — *let me be the instrument, let me be the blade, let me be the hand that steadies the kingdom's trembling pulse* — and only then did he rise, blow out the votive candle, and step out into the cold to confirm what his bones already knew.

The sky was wrong.

He had served the Crown for thirty-one years. He had walked the border wards at Grayfen as a young acolyte, had pressed his palm to the standing stones and felt the hum of them, the great patient chord of warding-magic that the star-priests had woven before the Crown was old. That chord had been the background music of his entire life, so constant that he had long ago stopped hearing it — the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat. Now it was gone. The silence where it had been was not empty. It was *loud*, the way a severed artery is loud.

And the stars — Halvard counted them out of habit, a devotional exercise taught to every child of the Eye. *Seven above the ridge, three above the river, the Shepherd's Eye at the crown of heaven.* He counted seven above the ridge. He counted three above the river. He looked for the Shepherd's Eye and found a hole in the firmament, a black socket where a star had been.

Then a second star went out. Then a third, like lamps being pinched by an unseen hand moving westward, unhurried, thorough.

The soldiers in the camp had noticed too. He could hear them murmuring, could see their faces turned up, pale in the firelight, wearing the expression of men who had just learned that the floor was a rumor. Somewhere a horse screamed. Somewhere a sergeant was already shouting men into ranks, because fear unmanaged becomes riot, and riot becomes heresy, and heresy becomes the kind of night that ends dynasties.

Halvard did not shout. He did not pray again, though he was a devout man and would be again before sleep. He stood in the cold and did the arithmetic that no one else in the camp was equipped to do.

The wards had not failed. Wards did not fail; they were maintained by the Office of Continuance, audited twice yearly, fed on tithe-silver and the disciplined attention of forty bound wardens. The wards had been *broken* — shattered from within or without, and shattered in a pattern. And something had come through the breach. Something that drank starlight.

He knew what it was before he let himself know. That was the discipline of the Eye: to hold a conclusion at arm's length until the evidence was arranged before it like a confession. But the evidence was already arranged. He had spent eleven years hunting the survivors of the Purge of the Celestial Court, eleven years following rumors of a surviving ember of sky-magic through the borderlands like a man following a scent of smoke through a dark house, waiting to find the fire. The wards were dead. The stars were dying. Therefore the ember had moved.

Therefore the ember had *fled* — and things only fled when they knew they were hunted, which meant somewhere in the last day, his quarry had learned his name.

Halvard found that thought did not frighten him. It steadied him, the way a blade steadies a hand. He had been named in the dark places of the world for a long time. Mothers in the Thornfell valleys used his name to quiet unruly children, which he considered a vulgar but not inaccurate use of it. He had earned his reputation honestly: not with cruelty, which was the vice of small men, but with thoroughness, which was the virtue of great ones.

He went to find his captain.

* * *

Captain Vess was a thick-necked veteran of the lowland wars who had learned, over four years of service under the Inquisitor, to stop asking questions and start answering them before they were asked. She was waiting at the command tent with the maps already unrolled when he arrived, which was one of the reasons she was still alive.

"Report," Halvard said.

"The Grayfen ward-stones went dead at moonrise. All of them, all at once — not a decay, a shattering. The garrison's hedge-witch took one look at the residue and vomited." Vess's mouth twisted. "Whatever came through, it came through *hard*. And there's this." She held out a scrap of parchment, its edges charred. "Found pinned to the tollhouse door at the ford. The night-watch swears it wasn't there an hour before the wards broke."

Halvard took it. The writing was hurried, the hand of a frightened educated man:

*The ember is out. She carries it west through Thornfell. If you are who I think you are, you are already too late, and I am already dead.*

No signature. But Halvard read the sentence three times, and by the third reading he had reconstructed the author entirely: a mid-level functionary of the Office of Continuance, someone with access to the ember's custody records, someone who had watched the Purge's last loose thread slip its knot and had spent years dreading this night. A man who had written his warning and then, in all likelihood, gone home to wait for the Inquisition to come for him, because men like that always did. They lacked the imagination to run.

"She," Halvard said. "The author says *she*."

"Doesn't narrow it much," Vess said. "The star-priests trained women. The Purge records list—"

"The Purge records list one hundred and six apprentices of the Celestial Court unaccounted for, of whom sixty-one were women, of whom perhaps nine had the rank and proximity to be entrusted with an ember of the First Flame." Halvard folded the parchment with precise creases and slid it into the breast of his coat, over his heart, where he kept his evidence. "It narrows it exactly enough. The ember cannot be carried by the untrained — it burns them. Whoever bears it was within the inner circle. That gives us a face, a name, and a temperament. And temperaments, Captain, are more useful than faces. A face can be disguised. A temperament must be lived with."

Vess looked at him the way she always looked at him when he did this — the cataloguing, the cold unfolding of a person from a scrap of ash — with respect and a faint unease she thought she hid well. She did not hide it at all. Halvard had long ago stopped being offended by it. Ordinary people found his method uncanny because they believed understanding required sympathy, and Halvard had never once in his life confused the two. He could understand a man completely and feel nothing for him at all. That was not a defect. That was the entire point of the Eye. Sympathy was how inquisitors became corrupt; sympathy was how the guilty went free; sympathy was a crack in the vessel through which the kingdom's enemies poured.

"Mount the company," he said. "Full kit, no banners. We ride for Grayfen within the hour."

"In this dark? Without the stars the passes are—"

"The passes are *open*," Halvard said. "For the first time in three hundred years, the border is a wound instead of a wall, and the thing that made the wound is bleeding across the countryside as we speak. Darkness is not our obstacle, Captain. It is our advantage. Our quarry is carrying the last light of the old order through a land with no stars in it. She will want to travel by night and hide by day, because that is what the trained do. But she is wounded — the tollhouse note says *carries*, and one does not *carry* an ember casually; it burns its bearer even in the best of cases — and wounded people make for roads, and roads lead to people, and people talk." He was already rolling the maps. "We do not need to find her, Captain. We need to find the first person who saw her, and the first person who saw her will be a smuggler, and smugglers can be bought. Everything in Thornfell can be bought. It is the one honest thing about the province."

Vess saluted and went out to shout the camp awake. Halvard remained a moment alone in the tent, and permitted himself — as he permitted himself each night, at the appointed hour, with the discipline of a man taking medicine — a single memory.

His daughter's face. She would be nineteen now. He did not know, because he had not been permitted to see her since he took the Eye's final vows, and this was correct and he did not resent it, because the Eye required a man with no unguarded flank, and a daughter was a flank wider than a city gate. But he remembered her at four years old, standing on a chair to look out the window at the stars, asking him which one was watching over her, and he had told her the truth: *all of them, little one, and that is why bad men fear them, because you cannot bribe a star.*

The star-priests had understood that. It was why they had to die. Not because they were wicked — Halvard had never believed the official line that the Celestial Court had been a nest of traitors; he had read the evidence and found it thin, assembled by lesser men with an outcome already chosen. They had to die because a power that answered to no crown, that could not be bought or taxed or commanded, was a power the kingdom could not survive *sharing a border with*. The Purge had not been justice. It had been surgery. And surgery was not murder, whatever the patient screamed on the table.

He was the man who held the knife steady while better, softer men looked away. Someone had to. He had volunteered. That was the whole of his biography, and he had never once wanted it to be otherwise.

He put on his coat, took up his staff of office — black iron, banded in silver, unadorned, because the Eye did not decorate fear — and went out into the starless dark to ride.

* * *

The village of Hallowmere lay in the first fold of the Thornfell foothills, forty houses, a shrine to the Shepherd's Eye with its star-carving defaced by weather, and a population that had survived three centuries of border life by perfecting the art of knowing nothing. Halvard chose it not at random but because it sat astride the one road that led from the Grayfen breach into the high passes, and because its headman, a widowed stonemason named Aldous Crane, had once, eleven years ago, been named in a seized ledger as a supplier of the salt-smugglers.

The company reached Hallowmere an hour before dawn, and Halvard had the village woken by putting a trooper with a horn at each end of the single street and having them blow until every door opened. It lacked subtlety. He had found that subtlety was wasted on the rural poor; they understood a horn and a line of soldiers far better than they understood a quiet question.

They assembled in the square before the defaced shrine, ninety souls in nightclothes, breath steaming, eyes darting from the soldiers to the black sky and back, because the sky was the thing that frightened them most tonight and Halvard intended to make use of that. Fear, properly channeled, was not cruelty. Fear was a door, and he was the man who knew where every door in a village stood.

He did not shout at them. He never shouted. He stood before them in the grey pre-light, an unremarkable man of fifty-odd years with a grey beard trimmed close and a voice like a ledger being opened, and he said:

"Last night the wards of Grayfen were destroyed. Last night the stars began to go out. Some of you saw it. All of you felt it." He let that sit. He watched them flinch, one by one, as the memory of the ward-silence moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. "The Crown does not blame you. The Crown knows that the people of Thornfell have suffered under the old magics longer than anyone, and that some of you may even rejoice at what has happened. I am not here to punish rejoicing. I am here to find a woman."

He let them look at each other. He let them do the arithmetic that villages always did: *do we know a woman, did a woman pass, was there a woman, and if we say nothing and she is found anyway, what happens to us?*

"A wounded woman," Halvard continued, "traveled or will travel this road, alone or nearly alone, bearing an iron lantern that does not behave as iron should. She may have offered you silver. She may have offered you a story. She is dangerous — not to you, perhaps, but to the kingdom entire, for she carries the last ember of the magic that broke your wards and murdered your stars, and while she runs free, the sky above your children's heads will remain black." He paused, and softened his voice by a degree, which was the most effective thing he did all night. "I know that some of you owe debts to the smugglers who use this road. I am not asking about smugglers. I am not asking about salt. Bring me the woman, or bring me word of her, and every debt this village owes the Crown — every fine, every tithe in arrears, every name on every list — is struck from the record. Refuse, and I will assume the village has chosen its loyalties, and I will act accordingly."

Silence. The good kind. He could hear the silence *deciding*.

Then an old man at the edge of the crowd — Aldous Crane, white-bearded, straight-backed, with the forearms of a man who had cut stone for fifty years — stepped forward, and Halvard felt the small cold disappointment of a plan encountering its first honest obstacle.

"There's no woman come through here," Crane said. His voice was steady. Too steady. "And if there was, we'd not sell her to the likes of you. The priests kept the lights on. You put them out. That's the whole of the arithmetic in Thornfell, Inquisitor, and no tithe-forgiveness buys it back."

Halvard regarded him for a long moment. He felt no anger. Anger was for men who had been surprised, and Halvard was never surprised by courage; he simply catalogued it, the way a surgeon catalogues the nerve he must cut.

"Headman Crane," he said. "Eleven years ago, a ledger seized at the tollhouse of Grayfen recorded three shipments of untaxed salt moving through Hallowmere under your mark. I did not pursue it then, because the Crown had larger wounds. I have not forgotten it now." He turned to Vess. "Captain. The headman is withholding material aid to an enemy of the realm. The penalty is forfeiture of the village's charter and the impressment of its men into road labor. However—" and here he turned back to Crane, and let his voice go gentle, which was worse than any threat, "—the law also provides that information freely given, before compulsion, may be weighed as mitigation. I will ask you once more, plainly, as one old servant of this border to another. Where did she go?"

"I told you," Crane said. "No woman."

Halvard nodded slowly, as if the answer had been what he expected, and perhaps it had been. He drew a thin knife from his belt — not the great ceremonial blade of the Eye, but a working tool, the kind used for trimming wicks and opening seals — and he did not lunge or threaten. He simply said, to the crowd, in the tone of a man announcing a change in the weather:

"The law is the law. The headman has chosen. Captain, carry out the sentence."

What followed was brief, and Halvard made himself watch every second of it, because a man who orders a thing must be able to bear the sight of it or he has no business ordering. He had borne it for thirty-one years. He bore it now, in the grey light, before ninety silent villagers, and when it was done he knelt beside the old stonemason's body for a moment — not in remorse, but in the manner of a man closing a ledger — and then stood and addressed the crowd, whose silence had changed texture entirely. It had gone from the silence of defiance to the silence of people doing arithmetic with their own lives.

"The debt is struck," Halvard said quietly. "The law was satisfied. Now — the woman. The lantern. The road. Someone in this village saw something, and I am no longer asking for courage from any of you. I am asking for sense. Who saw her?"

It took four minutes. It always took four minutes. There was a boy of perhaps twelve, gray-faced, who had been out checking a snare-line before dawn two nights past and had seen, up the shepherd's trail toward the high passes, a limping figure with a lantern that glowed *warm*, the boy said, warm like a living thing, warm like a coal that breathed. The figure had gone up toward the old salt road. Toward the tollhouse at Grayfen.

Halvard wrote it all down in his neat, terrible hand. He gave the boy a silver mark, because the Eye paid its informants promptly and visibly; it was better doctrine than any sermon. He ordered the company mounted. And as the sun came up over a sky with no stars in it — a sun that rose, he noticed, with the faintest wrongness to its color, as if it too had lost something — he stood beside his horse and consulted the map one final time.

The high passes. The salt roads. The tollhouse at Grayfen, where frightened men wrote warnings and nailed them to doors. And somewhere ahead, wounded and burning and carrying the last light of a murdered order, a woman who did not yet know that the most patient hunter in the kingdom had already read her temperament like a confession.

"Captain," Halvard said, swinging into the saddle. "Send a rider back to Marrow's Ford. Have the garrison seal every valley road out of Thornfell, and have them send word to our friend at the tollhouse that his debt has come due. Our quarry will need shelter, supplies, and a guide — and I know exactly which smuggler sells all three."

Vess frowned. "You have a name?"

Halvard put his heels to his horse and did not look back at the village, or the body, or the boy holding his silver mark in the road.

"I have a list," he said. "We start at the top."
