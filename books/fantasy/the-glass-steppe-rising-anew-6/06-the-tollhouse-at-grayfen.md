## 6. The Tollhouse at Grayfen

The rain found them an hour before the tollhouse did.

It came down the mountainside in gray sheets, turning the smugglers' road to a river of loose shale, and Wren walked into it without complaint because complaint changed nothing and rain, at least, was honest. It fell on the guilty and the innocent alike. It did not ask for names.

Kestrel walked behind them, one hand resting on the strap of Wren's pack, and she had not let go of that strap in two days. Not because she needed the support — her wound had closed to an angry red seam, and her stride had steadied — but because in the starless dark, touch was the only map either of them trusted. Wren had stopped mentioning it. There were worse things than being tethered to someone.

"There." They tilted their chin toward the valley floor, where a squat stone building crouched at the meeting of two roads, its single window glowing a greasy amber. "Grayfen Tollhouse. We'll get dry wood, hot food, and a bed that doesn't smell like goat."

"And your friend," Kestrel said. "The one who runs it."

"Odo Vane." The name came out of Wren's mouth tasting like old bread. "He owed me a favor from before. A big one."

"Before," Kestrel repeated carefully. "Before the exile."

Wren didn't answer. The rain filled the silence for them.

They came down off the ridge as the light died, which was wrong — Wren knew it was wrong even as they did it. Every instinct they'd sharpened over six years of moving through Thornfell like a ghost said: *never approach shelter after dark; never arrive when you can be seen arriving.* But Kestrel's fever had crept back that afternoon, climbing behind her eyes like water behind a dam, and the lantern's warmth had faded to almost nothing, and pride was a luxury they could no longer afford.

Besides. Odo Vane was a coward, but he was a coward who feared Wren Dunmore slightly more than he loved coin. That arithmetic had held for years.

Wren hammered twice on the tollhouse door — their old signal, three-and-two, learned so long ago it lived in their knuckles rather than their memory — and heard the bar scrape back.

Odo stood in the doorway looking exactly as Wren remembered him: soft-bellied, quick-eyed, with a smile that arrived a half-second too late to be sincere. His gaze flicked over Wren, then past them to Kestrel huddled in the doorway's shadow, then down the dark road behind them, and only then back to Wren's face.

"Dunmore." He said it like a man testing whether a floorboard would hold his weight. "Six years. You look terrible."

"You look rich," Wren said. "Toll roads been good to you?"

"Toll roads have been *quiet*," Odo said, stepping back to admit them. "Quiet's good. Quiet's what I pay for." He glanced again at Kestrel, at the iron lantern swinging at her hip, and something moved behind his eyes that Wren chose, in that moment, not to see. "Get inside. Both of you. There's stew on."

* * *

The stew was mutton and turnip and too much salt, and it was the best thing Wren had eaten in a month. They ate at the scarred plank table by the fire while Odo hovered, refilling bowls nobody asked him to refill, chattering about road conditions and garrison movements and the price of dried fish, never once asking where they'd come from or where they were going. Kestrel ate slowly, watching Odo the way a cat watches a dog that keeps smiling.

When she rose to see to her bandage, Odo leaned across the table. The firelight made his face look wet.

"They're offering full amnesty now," he said quietly. "Pardon, restoration of property, a stipend. For information leading to recovery of certain... contraband out of the capital." His eyes darted toward the stair where Kestrel had gone. "That's not rumor, Dunmore. That came through official channels. Through *me*."

"I know what channels you swim in, Odo."

"Do you?" His voice dropped further. "Because I also know there are riders on every valley road, and Inquisitors burning villages for less than what's sleeping upstairs, and I know that my quiet — my *paid-for* quiet — ended the night the stars went out. Whatever you're carrying through my tollhouse, it has brought the whole weight of the crown down onto Grayfen, and Grayfen is me." He wiped his mouth with a shaking hand. "I'm telling you this as a friend."

"No," Wren said. "You're telling me this as a man deciding how much a friend costs."

Odo's smile finally died.

Wren sat very still, listening — to the fire popping, to the rain hammering the shutters, to the small sounds of the house around them. The stairs. The kitchen. The back room where the toll-chest lived.

There should have been footsteps overhead. Odo kept a hired man, a half-wit giant called Brann, who slept above the kitchen and snored loud enough to rattle the chimney. Wren had heard no snoring since they entered.

"Where's Brann?" they asked.

Odo looked at the fire.

"Odo." Wren kept their voice level, almost gentle. "Where is Brann?"

"Garrison took him three days ago," Odo whispered. "Questioning. They'll give him back if—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Dunmore, they showed me his teeth. In a little box. They said the rest comes home when—"

"When you deliver us."

"*Deliver the contraband,*" Odo said, and now his voice cracked, and Wren saw with something like pity that the man was crying without knowing it. "Just the lantern. Just the woman's box. You walk away, I say nothing, everyone lives. That's all. That's *all*, Dunmore, I swear it on—"

"On what?" Wren asked. "On the stars? Look outside, Odo."

The knife came from under the table.

Later, Wren would understand that they had known — had seen Odo's hand move, had read the shift of his shoulders the way they once read weather fronts rolling off the peaks — and that some buried, exhausted part of them had simply decided not to dodge. But in the moment there was only white pain, low across the ribs, and the table going over, and Odo screaming "*I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry*" as though apology were a weapon he could still wield.

Wren caught his wrist on the second thrust, twisted, felt the blade skitter free and ring against the flagstones. Odo was strong in the soft way of well-fed men but weak everywhere it counted; Wren put him down with a shoulder to the chest and a knee on his sternum, and then the room tilted sideways, because their side was slick and hot and opening like a second mouth.

*Down,* they thought distantly. *You're bleeding down. Get up.*

They got up. The world went gray at the edges. From the foot of the stairs came running feet — Kestrel, pale, one hand pressed to her own healing wound — and beyond her, through the doorway to the yard, torchlight. Two, three, four torches bobbing through the rain. Soldiers. Already here, already close, drawn perhaps by the sound or perhaps having waited all along in the wet dark for exactly this moment.

"The back," Kestrel gasped. "Wren, the back—"

A crossbow bolt buried itself in the doorframe an inch from her head.

Wren shoved her toward the kitchen, and the world performed its slow trick again — the gray closing in, the floor leaning, their legs forgetting their trade. They went down hard against the hearthstone. Through the doorway they could see the soldiers fanning across the yard, and one figure standing apart from the rest, tall and dry beneath an oiled canvas, watching the tollhouse with the patience of a man watching a trap do its work.

Not Halvard. A lieutenant, maybe. It didn't matter. What mattered was the blood spreading under Wren's hands, and the distance to the back door, and the arithmetic of those two facts refusing to balance.

Kestrel knelt beside them. Her face had gone strange — not afraid, but *focused*, the way Wren imagined she must have looked over her studies in the capital, before everything burned.

"Wren. Listen to me. I can stop the bleeding, but you'll feel it, and afterward you may wish I hadn't."

"What—"

She pulled the iron lantern close. Its seams, dead and cold these two days, began to glow. Not gold — something older than gold, a color Wren had no name for, like the memory of light rather than light itself. Warmth poured off it in waves. And Kestrel pressed both bare hands flat against Wren's open wound.

The pain was extraordinary.

Not the knife-pain — that had been sharp and simple, a thing with edges. This was deeper, a heat that reached *into* the wound, into the torn flesh and the torn something-under-flesh, and gripped it, and *pulled*. Wren arched off the hearthstone. Somewhere far away they heard themselves make a sound they were ashamed of later. And through the agony, impossibly, they felt the wound knitting — felt flesh remember what it was supposed to be, fiber finding fiber, the hot flood of their own blood slowing, thickening, stopping.

Kestrel was whispering. Old words, or words that wanted to be old, worn smooth by use. The light from the lantern climbed the walls. Through the kitchen window, Wren saw soldiers shielding their eyes, stumbling back from the doorway, shouting about fires and sorcery — and the tall dry figure under the canvas raising one hand, unhurried, pointing at the back of the house.

Then it stopped. Kestrel slumped, catching herself on the hearth, and the lantern dimmed to a dull ember-glow, and Wren lay gasping on the stones with a closed seam of new pink skin across their ribs and no strength whatsoever in their limbs.

"You—" they managed. "That's not smuggling gear."

"No," Kestrel said.

"That's sky-magic. That's the thing they killed the priests for."

Her jaw tightened. She looked, for a long moment, like a woman deciding whether to lie to a dying man — and then she hooked her arms under Wren's shoulders and hauled them upright with a strength that had nothing to do with magic.

"Yes," she said. "And now they've seen it. So we run, or we die here explaining ourselves to men who burn villages. Choose fast."

Wren chose. It was the only kind of choosing left.

The back door opened onto the goat pen and beyond it the black shoulder of the mountain. Kestrel half-carried, half-dragged them through the mud while behind the tollhouse doors splintered and boots thundered and Odo Vane's voice rose, high and cracking, shouting words Wren couldn't make out and didn't need to — surrender, mercy, *it wasn't me, they forced me*.

At the tree line Wren looked back once. Torchlight spilled from the shattered doorway. A soldier dragged Odo out by his collar, and the tall dry figure bent to speak to him, courteous as a priest, and Odo shook his head and shook it and shook it.

"He sold us and it bought him nothing," Kestrel breathed.

"It bought him a day," Wren said. "Maybe. With Halvard, that's a fortune." Their ribs screamed when they spoke. New-healed skin, Kestrel had said. She'd pulled the wound shut but left the bruise, the ache, the memory of the blade — deliberately, Wren suspected. Magic that erased pain entirely would erase the lesson too.

They climbed.

The rain eased as they gained the ridge, and the wind took its place, and somewhere below them Grayfen burned — whether by accident or policy, Wren never learned. Its glow lit the clouds from underneath, a false dawn in the wrong quarter of the sky, and against that glow Wren watched Kestrel's face in profile: exhausted, furious, frightened, and more alive than anyone they had ever traveled with.

"You could have let them take me," she said suddenly, not looking at them. "In there. You knew Odo had turned. You knew the moment you asked about Brann. One word to the soldiers, 'she's in the back,' and you'd be drinking your pardon tonight."

"I don't drink with men who keep teeth in boxes."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Wren leaned against a pine trunk, breathing carefully around the ache. "You could have let me bleed out, too. Saved your secret. Instead you spent—" they gestured vaguely at her, at her gray lips and trembling hands "—whatever that cost you. On a stranger with a price on their head."

Kestrel was silent a long moment. Below them, the false dawn of the burning tollhouse flickered and shrank.

"We're even," she said finally.

"We're not even. You saved my life. I got stabbed by a man I should have gutted years ago." Wren winced, and something that might have been laughter escaped them both — thin, shocked, inappropriate, real. "But we can pretend, if it helps you sleep."

"It won't help me sleep. Nothing will help me sleep." She settled beside them against the pine, shoulder just touching shoulder, and pulled the iron lantern into her lap. Its faint glow lit her hands from below, making them look carved from candle wax. "Tomorrow," she said quietly, "you'll ask me what I am. And tomorrow I'll tell you. All of it. Because after tonight, keeping secrets from you feels like walking with a knife in my own ribs, and I've had enough knives this week."

"Tomorrow," Wren agreed.

Above them the sky stayed black and starless, vast and blind as a closed eye. But high on the mountain ahead, barely visible through the ragged cloud, Wren could just make out the silhouette of the old pass — the forbidden one, the one that climbed past the ruined watchtowers toward the broken crown of Thornfell Observatory, where their grandmother had once pointed at the stars and told them, *that is where our family's story ends, little storm, and yours begins.*

They hadn't thought of her voice in years. Tonight it came back whole.

"Rest," they told Kestrel. "At first light, we go up. And Kestrel—" they watched the tollhouse glow gutter and die below, and felt the cold settle into the empty places where trust used to live, and made the choice anyway, "—when you tell me tomorrow? Start with why the Observatory. Because I think you already know that's where we're going. And I think someone told you my name long before you collapsed on that road."

Kestrel said nothing. But her hand found theirs in the dark, and did not let go.
