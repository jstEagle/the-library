## 7. Ember and Ash

The cave was older than the kingdom, or so the smugglers claimed. Kestrel felt that age in the walls around her — stone worn smooth by centuries of wind, hollowed into a throat that swallowed the sound of the storm outside and gave back only silence. The fire Wren had built at the cave's mouth threw their shadows long across the rock, two figures stretched thin and wavering, like things that might dissolve if the light shifted.

Kestrel sat with her back against the coldest wall she could find.

It was not comfort she sought. It was clarity. The fever from her wound had broken sometime in the gray hours before dawn, but it had left behind a strange floating sensation, as though her body were a garment someone else was wearing. And in that loosened state, with the fire painting amber over everything and the rain hissing beyond the cave mouth like a whispered conversation, she found herself watching Wren Dunmore and thinking thoughts she had sworn not to think.

*Tell them.*

The lantern sat between them on the flat stone where they'd shared a meal of dried fish and hard bread. It looked, in the firelight, like nothing at all — a squat iron cage of tarnished bands, the kind of thing a farmer might use to carry coals to a far field. But Kestrel could feel it the way she had felt it every hour for eleven days: a warmth against her hip that had nothing to do with fire, a pulse beneath the metal slow and patient as a sleeping heart.

She had carried it out of the capital through gates that ran red. She had carried it across three provinces while the wanted notices bloomed on every post like white flowers. She had carried it through the Thornfell passes until her legs gave out and the dark sky pressed down on her like a lid, and she had sworn — *sworn* — that she would die before letting it fall into the wrong hands.

And now here was this stranger, this exile with scarred hands and a smuggler's drawl, who had found her dying on the road and asked no questions. Who had carried her when she could not walk. Who had fed her from their own pack and built this fire and turned their back to sleep within arm's reach of a woman they did not know, trusting her with a carelessness that was either courage or foolishness.

Or perhaps neither. Perhaps they simply had nothing left to lose.

Kestrel knew the name. Of course she knew it. *Wren Dunmore* had been carved into her memory years ago, in a lecture hall smelling of chalk and candle smoke, when Master Ellery had spoken of the court's storm-callers — the weapons the crown kept leashed — and of the one who broke the leash. She had been sixteen. She had thought, then, that Dunmore must be a giant, a figure of legend, someone who had stood before the assembled court and said *no* in a voice that shook the pillars.

The person sleeping ten feet away was lean and wiry, with ink-stained fingers and a face that seemed perpetually braced for a blow. When they slept, they slept badly — she'd watched them startle awake twice in the night, hands flying to a knife that wasn't there anymore because she'd needed it to cut her bandages. There was a faded brand on the inside of one wrist, half-hidden by the sleeve: the mark the courts gave to oath-breakers. She'd seen it when they'd lifted her over a stream two days past, and she had said nothing, and they had seen her see it, and they had both said nothing still.

Two people full of unsaid things, sharing a fire.

Outside, thunder rolled down the mountains — distant, directionless, the kind of storm that meant nothing and threatened nothing. But Kestrel saw Wren's shoulders tense even in sleep, saw their breathing change, saw their fingers curl against the stone as though feeling for something that used to be there.

*They can still feel it,* she realized. *The calling. They never stopped feeling it.*

Something in her chest cracked along an old seam.

She thought about the notices nailed to the tollhouse door in Grayfen, the ones Wren had torn down without comment: *WREN DUNMORE, STORM-CALLER, CONDEMNED FOR TREASON AGAINST THE CROWN.* She thought about what treason actually meant, in a kingdom whose king had murdered every priest of the sky in a single night and called it purification. If refusing to drown refugees was treason, then Kestrel had been committing it daily since the purge, and she had never once felt proud of it until this moment, watching an exile sleep badly beside a stolen fire.

The rain thickened. The fire popped. And Kestrel made her decision the way she made all her decisions now — quickly, before fear could dress itself up as prudence.

"Wren." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Wake up. I need to tell you something, and I need you awake for it."

*

Wren woke the way they always woke — all at once, hand closing on empty air where a knife should be, eyes finding the exits before they found her. Cave mouth, blocked by rain. One passage behind, choked with rubble. Two people, one fire, no weapons worth the name. Then the eyes found her, and some of the wariness drained out, replaced by something almost sheepish.

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I've been resting for two days. I'm tired of being a parcel you're carrying." Kestrel pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, and nodded at the space across the fire. "Sit. Please."

Wren sat. Up close, in the honest light, Kestrel could see how tired they were — the bruised skin under their eyes, the way they held their scarred hands folded in their lap like something hidden. The brand peeked out from the cuff, and this time Wren didn't pull the sleeve down. Maybe they were too tired. Maybe they understood what was coming.

"I know who you are," Kestrel said.

Wren went very still. It was remarkable, really, how completely they could stop moving — like a animal that had survived by freezing. "Do you."

"The whole capital knew your name, after the trial. The priests talked about you. Quietly. In closed rooms." She swallowed. "Master Ellery kept a portrait sketch of you pinned above his desk. He said you were proof the crown hadn't won yet."

"That's—" Wren exhaled slowly through their nose. "That's a very generous thing to say about a traitor."

"It's the only true thing to say." Kestrel leaned forward. The warmth against her hip pulsed, as if it knew. As if it had been waiting. "You refused to call storms down on civilians. On *refugees*. Families walking out of the border provinces with nothing but what they carried. And for that they took your rank, your name, your home, and they burned a hole in your wrist so everyone would know what you were." She shook her head. "I've spent eleven days running from soldiers, and I would trade every one of those days to have seen your face when you said no."

Wren's laugh was short and had no humor in it at all. "You wouldn't have. It wasn't brave. It was just — the only thing I couldn't do. Everyone else in that hall managed to say yes. I keep wondering what was wrong with me that I couldn't."

"Nothing was wrong with you."

"Then something was wrong with all of them." Wren stared into the fire. "Which is worse, honestly. Because if it was just me, I could be a coward with a conscience. But if it was all of us..." They trailed off, jaw tight.

Kestrel let the silence sit for a moment. Then she reached down, unhooked the lantern from her belt, and set it on the stone between them.

"Before I say anything else," she said, "I want you to understand that I've killed two men to keep this from falling into the wrong hands. I'm not proud of that either. But I need you to know what you've been carrying on your back for two days. What you bled for in Grayfen without asking why."

Wren's eyes dropped to the lantern. "It hums," they said quietly. "I noticed the first night. Iron doesn't hum. I told myself it was the mountain, or my imagination, or — I don't know. Something explainable." Their voice dropped lower. "It's warm. Through the metal. Like a living thing."

"It is a living thing." Kestrel's throat tightened. "Or the last piece of one."

She put her hands on the lantern's bands. The iron was cool to anyone else — she'd watched Wren touch it once, cautiously, and frown at the chill — but under her palms it warmed immediately, eagerly, the way a dog warms under a familiar hand. Inside the cage, where there should have been darkness, there was a faint glow. Not firelight. Not lamplight. Something older and whiter, like the memory of light rather than light itself.

"This is an ember," Kestrel said. "Not coal. Not anything the crown has a word for. When the star-priests worked the sky-magic — when we read the heavens, when we kept the wards, when we tended the lights that guided ships and healed fevers and told farmers when to plant — every working drew from a source. A flame that fell from the first stars when the world was young. There were nine embers once. Nine temples. Nine flames that had burned since before the kingdom had a name."

"And now?"

"Now there is one." Kestrel heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when grief got too large to hold any other way. "The night the wards collapsed, the king's men hit all nine temples in the same hour. They didn't send armies. They sent the Inquisition. Halvard's people. They came with writs and chains and they took the masters first, and then the flames." Her hands tightened on the iron. "I was the youngest apprentice at the Temple of the Veil. Third-rank. Nobody important. My master — Master Sefa — she grabbed me during the first alarm and shoved this into my arms and told me to run. She said, *'The ember chooses its keeper, and it has chosen you, so you will not argue with me.'*" Kestrel laughed, wet and broken. "I argued anyway. For about four seconds. Then she sealed the temple doors behind me and I heard—"

She stopped. Wren didn't prompt her. They just waited, patient as stone, and somehow that was worse than questions.

"I heard what happened to her," Kestrel finished. "Through the door. All of us outside heard what happened to all of them. Eleven days ago, there were two hundred and six star-priests in this kingdom. Now there is one, and she's sitting in a cave talking to a smuggler."

The fire crackled. Rain sheeted past the cave mouth. Wren hadn't moved, but their face had changed — Kestrel watched it happen, watched the smuggler's careful blankness crack and fall away, watched something raw surface underneath.

"The stars," Wren said. "The night the wards fell. That was the same night. The sky went black."

"Because the wards *were* the stars, in part. Or the stars were the wards — the priests never agreed on which way round it went." Kestrel touched the lantern again, and the glow inside brightened faintly, responding to her, recognizing her. "The embers anchored the great workings. With eight of them dead and the ninth in hiding, the sky-magic has nothing to stand on. The stars didn't go out. They were *put out*. Deliberately. So that no one would ever look up and remember what the crown destroyed."

Wren was silent for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was strange — stripped of its usual wry deflection, laid bare.

"In the army, they taught us the star-priests were relics. Decorative. Old men reading omens for superstitious farmers." A muscle worked in their jaw. "They taught us the purge was a reform. Modernization. I believed it — not entirely, but enough. Enough to keep my head down and my mouth shut for five years while they hunted whatever was left of you." They looked up, and their eyes were bright and miserable. "I'm sorry. I didn't know what I was apologizing for until right now, but I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize to me." Kestrel shook her head. "Apologize to the ember. It's the one that's been alone."

As if it heard, the glow inside the cage swelled — and this time Wren flinched, because the warmth coming off the iron was suddenly unmistakable, a breath of summer in the cold cave, and the light spilled between the bands and lay across the stone floor in pale threads like frost made luminous.

"It does that when it's spoken of kindly," Kestrel said softly. "Sefa told me it remembers everything it ever lit. Every ward, every healing, every harvest blessing since the beginning. It's not magic the way the court means magic — not a weapon, not a tool. It's more like... a witness. The oldest witness there is." She met Wren's eyes across the small white-gold light. "And it's the proof. You asked me once, in the pass, why I didn't just hide forever. This is why. Because this ember carries the signature of every priest the crown killed. Their workings are woven into it. Anyone who knows how to read it — anyone the old order trained — can hold it up and see the truth written in it. Who died. When. By whose order. The crown didn't just murder the priesthood, Wren. They murdered it *secretly*, and they've spent eleven days making sure no one could prove otherwise. This is the otherwise."

Wren looked at the lantern for a long time. Kestrel watched them do the arithmetic she'd known they would do — the same arithmetic she'd done herself, alone, eleven nights ago in a burning city. The weight of it settling onto their shoulders, changing how they sat, how they breathed.

"If the crown learns you're alive," Wren said slowly, "and that you have this—"

"They'll hunt me to the edge of the world. Which they're already doing." Kestrel gestured vaguely southward, toward the lowlands, toward the smoke she imagined rising from Grayfen. "There's an Inquisitor. Halvard. He was at the purge — I saw him across the temple square, standing in the snow like he was waiting for a coach. He's methodical. Patient. He doesn't rage, he *reasons*, which is infinitely worse. And he has hounds that track the ember's warmth."

"Hounds." Wren's mouth twisted. "Of course. Why use dogs when you can use abominations."

"You know about them?"

"I know about things the crown pretends it doesn't have." Wren rubbed their scarred hands together, and Kestrel noticed — really noticed, for the first time — the pattern of the scars. Not burns. Not rope. They were deliberate, precise, laid in lines across the palms and fingers like a script. Like something written. "Those aren't punishment marks," she said before she could stop herself.

Wren's hands went still. For a moment Kestrel thought they'd retreat, fold away, change the subject with one of their deflecting jokes. Instead, slowly, they turned their palms upward toward the firelight.

"When they sentenced me, they didn't just take my rank. They took the calling — or they tried to. There's a rite. Court sorcerers, iron needles, words in the old tongue." Their voice was steady, but Kestrel could hear the effort holding it steady. "It's supposed to sever the connection permanently. Most storm-callers who survive it never feel the sky again. But mine didn't take. Or it took incompletely. These scars are where they wrote the severance, and sometimes — when storms come close, like tonight — I can feel it anyway. Like a voice calling through a shut door." They curled their fingers closed. "I've spent five years telling myself I'm glad it failed. That I never want it back. Some days I even believe that."

"What do you believe the other days?"

Wren smiled, and it was the saddest expression Kestrel had ever seen. "The other days I believe that a part of me is still standing in that hall, saying no, and that the sky says yes every time it thunders, and that someday the two of us are going to have to settle our differences."

The ember pulsed again — brighter, warmer — and both of them looked down at it, startled. The glow had shifted color, deepening toward gold, and the threads of light on the floor had rearranged themselves, subtly, into patterns that Kestrel almost recognized. Constellations. It was throwing constellations across the cave floor, faint and trembling, the first stars either of them had seen in eleven days.

"Oh," Kestrel breathed. "Oh, you *foolish, beautiful thing.*"

"It's showing us the sky," Wren said wonderingly. "It's — it misses it?"

"It's been locked in iron in a cellar for eleven days. What would you do?" But Kestrel's eyes were stinging, and she had to blink hard, and when she spoke again her voice was not entirely steady. "This is what I couldn't explain in the pass. Why I can't just run forever, why I can't just bury it somewhere and disappear. Look at it. It's *dying*, Wren. Slowly. An ember needs tending — a keeper, a hearth, a working to feed it. Left in this cage, it will gutter out within the year, and then everything it witnessed dies with it, and the crown wins forever, and the sky stays black until the end of the world. There's a place it can be rekindled. My master told me about it before the end, in case — in case it ever came to this. An old observatory, north of here, in the Thornfells. Abandoned for generations, but the foundations are still true. If the ember could be brought to flame there—"

She stopped. Because Wren had gone rigid.

Not afraid. Something else. Kestrel watched their face and saw recognition move through it like weather — disbelief first, then a kind of grim confirmation, then something older and more complicated that they clearly didn't want to name.

"The Thornfell Observatory," Wren said.

"You know it."

"My grandmother used to sing about it." Wren's voice had gone quiet and distant. "A lullaby. About the watchers on the mountain who kept the lights burning so the lost could find their way home. I always thought it was just a song. She was from these mountains — before the crown paved the passes, her family held land up here. She used to say we were descended from the observatory's servants. Hearth-keepers. I thought she was romanticizing poverty." They laughed, short and unsteady. "Every smuggler route I've run for five years, every pass I've memorized, every blind trail through these mountains — I told myself I was doing it for the salt money. But I think some part of me has been navigating toward that ruin my entire adult life."

The fire had burned low. Neither of them moved to feed it. Between them, the ember glowed on, patiently laying its constellations across the stone — the Hunter, the Weaver, the Lantern-Bearer whose name Kestrel had learned at seven years old and never expected to see again. Above the cave mouth, through the ragged gap in the clouds, one real star had emerged, tentative and thin, the first either of them had seen since the night everything ended.

Kestrel looked at it, and then at Wren — this exile with severed scars and a grandmother's lullaby, who had carried her bleeding body through the dark without knowing what she carried, and who was looking at the single returned star with an expression she recognized because she wore it too. Grief and hope in the same face, indistinguishable.

"There's something else," she said. "About what rekindling requires. Sefa started to tell me, the night she gave me the ember, and she stopped herself. She said, *'Ask me again when you're somewhere safe,'* and then the doors sealed, and there was no safe anywhere ever again." She wrapped her arms tighter around her knees. "I've had eleven days to imagine what it might be. Every answer I imagine is worse than the last."

Wren reached across the space between them — hesitated, an inch short of touching her shoulder — and then completed the gesture, their scarred hand warm and light against her arm.

"Then we'll find out together," they said. "Whatever it is. Whatever it costs. You didn't let me bleed to death in Grayfen, and I didn't leave you on the road, and I'm not going to start keeping score now."

Kestrel nodded, because speech had temporarily failed her. And in the quiet that followed, with the ember dreaming its constellations and the lone star watching through the clouds, neither of them noticed the shape that had landed on the ledge above the cave mouth — small, feathered, wrong-eyed — nor the way it cocked its head at the glow below, memorizing it, before spreading wings that cast no shadow and lifting silently into the dark.

Somewhere south, a man in gray opened a brass instrument that should not have worked in a starless sky, read the needle's tremble, and smiled.

He knew exactly which mountain they were on.
