## 8. The Map of Dead Kings

Wren woke to the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of Kestrel arguing with a kettle.

Not a real argument — she was talking to it the way people talked to stubborn animals, low and coaxing, as though the dented tin thing might be shamed into boiling faster. The cave mouth showed a sky gone the color of wet slate, dawn arriving not as light but as a slow lessening of dark. There were no colors to speak of anymore. Without stars to anchor them, even sunrise seemed uncertain of itself.

Wren lay still for a moment and watched her. Kestrel sat cross-legged near the fire with the iron lantern between her feet, her wounded arm cradled against her ribs, her hair escaping its braid in pale wisps. The bandage Wren had tied last night had held. Her color was better. When she caught Wren looking, she did not flinch the way she had two nights ago on the smugglers' road — she only raised an eyebrow, as if to say *yes, I exist, get used to it.*

"You talk to kettles," Wren said, voice rough with sleep.

"It listens better than most people."

"Most people would boil faster just to make you stop."

Kestrel smiled — small, crooked, quickly hidden behind a cough that fooled neither of them. It was the first smile Wren had seen from her that wasn't a weapon or a wound. They looked away before it could mean anything, because meaning things got people killed in Thornfell, and poured themselves tea from the kettle after all.

They ate hardtack softened in the tea and a strip of dried goat that tasted like leather's grief. And then, because the fire was low and the sky outside was still wrong and there was nothing left to do but the thing Wren had been circling for two days like a hawk over a carcass, they said:

"I know where you need to go."

Kestrel went still. Not dramatically — she simply stopped moving all at once, the way prey animals did, the way people did who had spent their whole lives being hunted by institutions rather than wolves. "You said you didn't—"

"I said I didn't want to know what you carried." Wren turned their hands palm-up over the fire, feeling the old burn-scar tissue tighten in the heat. "I never said I didn't know where it came from. Or where it goes."

The lantern hummed. It did that sometimes — a low, resonant note, like a finger run around the rim of a glass — and Wren had learned in the last two days that it did it when Kestrel's heart rate climbed, or when she lied, or, once memorably, when Wren had made a joke so bad that Kestrel had laughed and immediately regretted it. The ember inside remembered things. Light remembered everything it had ever touched; that was the whole point of it. That was why the crown wanted it dead.

"Thornfell Observatory," Wren said.

Kestrel's breath caught audibly. "That's a ruin. That's been a ruin for sixty years. The purge burned it and the mountain swallowed what was left and every map in the kingdom marks it as collapsed."

"The maps are wrong." Wren reached into the inner pocket of their coat, past the salt ledgers and the forged passes and the knife they kept for people who read other people's papers too closely, and drew out something folded eight times, soft as cloth from handling, cracked along its creases like old skin.

"My grandmother's maps."

* * *

Wren spread it on the flat stone beside the fire and weighted the corners with pebbles, and for a long moment neither of them touched it.

It wasn't a surveyor's map. Surveyors drew roads and rivers and the tidy geometry of ownership. This was drawn in ink mixed, if family legend was true, with starfall ash, and it showed Thornfell the way a lover knew a body: the passes named for who had died in them, the caves marked by which ones flooded in spring thaw, the ridgelines annotated in a spidery hand with notes like *wind sings here at dusk — good omen* and *do NOT trust the ice bridge below the twin crags no matter how solid it looks, Marta didn't.*

And there, in the high northeastern wall of the range, where every official chart dissolved into a blank space labeled TERRA INCOGNITA in the confident capitals of men who had never been there, this map showed a winding thread of a path climbing through a notch called the King's Teeth, and a symbol Wren had stared at since childhood: a circle with eight rays, half-erased, as though someone had started to scratch it out and lost their nerve.

Beneath it, in Old High Script that Wren had needed three winters and a bribed monk to translate:

*THE HOUSE THAT WATCHES THE HOUSE OF HEAVEN.*

"The Observatory," Kestrel whispered. She hadn't touched the map yet either. Her fingers hovered above it as though it might burn, or bless. "Your grandmother mapped this?"

"My grandmother *was* this." Wren traced the ridge west of the observatory symbol, up toward a smaller mark, a hut with a tiny storm-cloud beside it. "Before the purge, the star-priests didn't work alone. Every observatory had lay families attached to it — guides, weather-readers, keepers of the ground routes. Storm-callers, back when calling storms was a service instead of a siege weapon. My family kept the Thornfell station for six generations. We guided pilgrims up to watch the readings. We kept the lower beacon lit when cloud closed in."

"And after?"

"After, we kept our heads down and pretended we'd always been simple mountain folk." Wren's mouth twisted. "It mostly worked. Until it didn't."

Kestrel looked at them across the fire, and Wren watched her assemble it — the name from the wanted notices, the scarred hands, the exile, and now this — and decided, visibly, not to ask. Not yet. There was a courtesy in that which Wren found more disarming than any question.

"The route is real?" she asked instead. "The pass isn't collapsed?"

"The official road is. Sluiced on purpose, twenty years ago, when the Crown decided ruins attracted pilgrims and pilgrims asked questions. But my grandmother's line goes over the top. Three days' climb from here, if the weather holds." Wren tapped the King's Teeth. "Which it won't. But it doesn't have to hold. It has to hold *long enough.*"

"And the observatory itself? You've been there?"

"No one living has been there." Wren paused. "That I know of. But my mother took me to the lower beacon hut when I was nine, before—" They stopped. Restarted. "Before everything. She showed me the trailhead and made me memorize it with my eyes shut. She said, *one day the sky will need mending, and the menders will need a road.* I thought it was just something mothers said. Like *eat your greens* and *don't trust smugglers.*"

Something moved through Kestrel's face then — hope, but hope with weight in it, hope like ore before smelting. She put her fingers down on the map at last, lightly, on the half-erased symbol, and the lantern sang one clear, rising note.

"Oh," Kestrel said softly, looking down at it. "Oh, it *remembers* this place."

* * *

They spent the morning planning, which meant they spent the morning arguing, which — Wren realized somewhere around midday, with a start of genuine alarm — had become almost enjoyable.

"You can't come," Kestrel said, for the fourth time. She was repacking the bandages with the brisk efficiency of someone who had done triage in worse places than a cave, and losing the argument with the same serene confidence she brought to kettles. "The pass in winter is suicide for one healthy climber. You're taking a wounded stranger up it for reasons you haven't even let yourself think about yet."

"You can't go *alone,*" Wren said, for the fifth time. "You can barely hold a kettle. If you fall on the Teeth, no one will find you until the thaw, and the thaw up there is June. Also, you don't know the route. Also, the route doesn't exist on any map anyone else owns. Also—"

"Also you're enjoying this."

Wren opened their mouth to deny it and found nothing there. The silence stretched. Kestrel's eyebrow climbed with the slow inevitability of a tide.

"I'm enjoying *winning,*" Wren said with dignity.

"You're enjoying having someone to argue with who argues *back.*" Kestrel tied off the bandage roll and tucked it into her pack. "How long has it been? Since someone argued back?"

The question landed somewhere under Wren's ribs and stayed there. Because the honest answer was *years* — since the court martial, since the exile, since they had learned that in Thornfell, disagreement with armed men was paid for in blood, and with friends was paid for in loneliness, and Wren had quietly stopped paying either. Smugglers agreed with each other. It was safer. Everyone nodded along the road and everyone died a little quieter.

"There was a mule," Wren said finally. "Named Duchess. Terrible animal. Bit me every time I loaded her wrong. Best conversation I'd had in a decade."

And Kestrel laughed.

It burst out of her before she could stop it — a real laugh, unguarded, startled out of her like a bird from cover, and then she clapped her scarred hand over her mouth as if she could stuff it back in, eyes wide over her fingers with something close to panic. The lantern rang bright and sweet, three notes, like bells in a tower that hadn't swung in years.

Wren stared at her. Then, helplessly, they started laughing too — quiet at first, then not, both of them hunched over the dying fire laughing at absolutely nothing, at a mule, at the absurdity of sitting in a cave under a murdered sky planning treason against a kingdom with a teakettle and a dead woman's map. The laughter echoed off the stone and came back changed, warmer, and for perhaps a full minute the world was not broken.

Then it stopped, the way fragile things stop, all at once, and they looked at each other across the ashes, and neither of them spoke, because there was suddenly far too much to say and none of it safe.

"Fragile as frost," Wren murmured, without quite meaning to say it aloud.

"What?"

"The—" Wren gestured vaguely at the air between them, at the laughter's ghost. "This. Whatever this is. It'll melt the moment the sun gets strong."

"Then we climb before noon," Kestrel said.

* * *

They struck camp in the grey afternoon light and stood at the cave mouth while Wren oriented them — not by the sun, which sulked behind cloud, and not by the stars, which were ash, but by memory. By the way the wind came off the western crags at this hour smelling of pine and old snow. By the shape of the valley below, where Grayfen's chimneys smoked thin and guilty. By the dead reckoning of a childhood spent learning these mountains from a woman who had loved them enough to draw them in secret.

"That spur," Wren said, pointing northeast, where a granite shoulder rose black against the slate sky. "We follow the stream to its source, then cut up through the scree before the second waterfall. Camp tonight at the old shrine — there's a roof, such as it is. Tomorrow, the Teeth."

Kestrel shouldered her pack, wincing only slightly, and squinted at the distant spur. "It looks very tall."

"It is very tall."

"I'm going to complain the entire way."

"Noted. Duchess complained too. It's how I'll know you're still alive."

Kestrel snorted — actually snorted, another crack in the ice — and turned to take her first step onto the trail.

She froze.

"Wren." Her voice had dropped all its warmth in the space of a heartbeat. "Look at the tree line. North side. Fifty yards. Do you see movement?"

Wren saw it. A flicker, no more — a shadow where shadows shouldn't be, at the edge of the pines where the light came flat and sourceless. Something small. Winged. It lifted from a branch, wheeled once against the grey, and flew south, fast and purposeful, wings beating in a rhythm that was almost right and entirely wrong.

Wren had grown up among ravens. Ravens cast shadows. Ravens did not fly straight toward the capital as though reporting for duty.

"That's twice now," Wren said quietly, remembering the shape that had perched above the cave mouth in the night, the one they'd both dismissed as a dream. "Someone's watching us, and they're not using eyes we'd recognize."

Kestrel's hand found the lantern strap at her shoulder and gripped it white-knuckled. "Halvard."

"Maybe. Maybe his birds, at least." Wren pulled their hood up and glanced once more at the spur, at the three days of climbing between here and the King's Teeth, at the map folded safe against their heart. The wind shifted, carrying the smell of snow coming down from the heights — early, heavy, wrong for the season.

Or maybe not wrong. Maybe the mountains themselves knew what was coming, and were drawing the clouds close like a door.

"We move now, fast, before whatever saw us finishes telling," Wren said. "Stay on my heels, step where I step, and if I say *down*, you go down and you don't ask why until we're alive."

"And if we're not?"

"Then complaining about the climb will be the least of your problems." Wren set off toward the spur at a pace just short of reckless, and heard Kestrel fall in behind, boots crunching frost, breath steaming, the lantern humming low and steady against her chest like a heart that refused to learn fear.

Behind them, far to the south and closing, something answered the wrong-winged bird's report — a horn, single and clear, threading through the passes like a needle through cloth.

The hunt had found their scent.
