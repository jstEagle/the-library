## 9. Wolves Without Stars

The bridge hung over the gorge like a dead man's spine.

Wren had crossed the Vell's Teeth a hundred times in the years since exile — salt runs, silk runs, once a coffin full of forged papers and a very angry widow — and every crossing had felt the same: a held breath, a prayer to no one, boots on wet planking while the river gnawed at the rocks far below. But tonight, under a sky with nothing in it, the bridge felt different. It felt like a sentence waiting to be signed.

They'd made good time out of Grayfen, three days of hard climbing through pine shadow and scree, Kestrel's wound knitting slowly, her strength returning in increments Wren measured by how far she could walk before her jaw went tight with pain. She never complained. That was its own kind of warning. People who didn't complain were usually saving their breath for something else.

Now she stood at the near end of the bridge, the iron lantern wrapped in sacking against her chest, and stared across the black gorge as if she could see all the way to the other side of everything.

"How deep?" she asked.

"Deep enough that you don't want to find out from the bottom." Wren tested the first rope. The fibers were slick with frost, but sound. "We cross single file. I go first, weight the planks, call out anything that gives. You keep your eyes on my shoulders, not your feet."

"And if something happens to you?"

"Then you'll have learned what my shoulders look like falling."

It was meant as a joke. It came out flat, because Wren's mouth had gone dry an hour ago, back when they'd found the first sign.

A snare line, cut clean. Not animal sign — the wire was smuggler's wire, Thornfell pattern, the kind Mother Ashe's people used. Someone had come through here recently, someone who knew the old paths, and they hadn't bothered to hide it because they hadn't expected anyone alive to be following behind them.

Or because they *wanted* someone to follow.

Kestrel had seen it too. She'd said nothing, just touched the cut wire with two fingers and looked up at Wren with those gray eyes that missed less than any eyes Wren had ever known. *They're close,* the look said. Neither of them had needed words for it.

Now Wren stepped onto the bridge.

The planks groaned. Frost crackled underfoot. Below, the Vell roared through its stone throat, loud enough to swallow footsteps, loud enough to swallow screams — which was either mercy or menace, depending on what came next. Wren moved with the old rhythm, weight low, hands loose on the guide ropes, counting planks without meaning to. Twelve. Twenty. Thirty. Halfway.

Behind them, Kestrel followed, and Wren hated how much of their attention kept turning backward — not toward the far bank, not toward survival, but toward the soft slap of her boots on wood, the small hitch of her breath when a plank shifted. Somewhere in the last week, without permission, without ceremony, this stranger had become a weight Wren carried willingly. And weights, Wren knew, were how you got killed on mountains. You leaned wrong. You reached when you shouldn't. You turned around.

At plank forty-one, Wren turned around.

Not by choice. By sound — the thin, high *twang* of a bowstring released somewhere above them, in the dark, where there should have been nothing but cliff face and wind.

"DOWN!"

The arrow took the guide rope six inches from Kestrel's hand. The whole bridge lurched sideways, swinging out over the gorge like a pendulum, and Kestrel dropped to her knees, arms locked around the lantern, and for one endless moment the world was only rope-creak and river-roar and the sick lurch of gravity reconsidering its loyalties.

Then the second arrow came, and Wren stopped thinking entirely.

*There.* Above the far anchorage, a shape against the marginally-less-black sky — a crouched silhouette re-nocking, unhurried, professional. And below, on the near bank they'd just left, torchlight blooming between the pines. Three torches. Four. Figures moving with the easy economy of people who had done this many times.

Halvard's hunters. They'd been herded onto the bridge like livestock into a chute.

"Kestrel." Wren's voice came out steadier than they felt. "Listen to me. The far anchor post is rotten — I've known it for years, everyone uses the near side because of it. If we can get off before they cut the near ropes—"

Another arrow. This one found the plank beside Wren's knee and stuck there, quivering, close enough to touch.

"—we need to *move*."

They moved. Wren hauled Kestrel up by the arm — too hard, they'd apologize later, if there was a later — and they ran the bridge together, planks bucking, the gorge yawning beneath them, torches flaring on the bank behind and the archer above drawing again, again, patient as winter. An arrow hissed past Wren's ear so close they felt the wind of it. Another punched through the sacking around the lantern and glanced off the iron inside with a sound like a struck bell — a bell that answered.

The lantern hummed. Loudly. Loud enough that Wren heard it over the river, felt it in their teeth, a rising note like a wineglass singing on the edge of shattering.

And on the far bank, half-hidden in the rocks, a fourth figure raised something to their eye — not a weapon. A glass. A spyglass, catching the faint warmth-glow leaking through torn sacking, and even at this distance Wren saw the figure go rigid, saw them turn and shout down toward the torches, and understood with cold, total clarity:

*That's why we're being driven. Not to kill us. To make the ember show itself.*

"They're not trying to stop us," Wren gasped. "They're trying to make you *glow*—"

The near ropes parted.

Not cut — burned. A line of orange fire raced along both guide ropes at once, alchemist's work, quicklime and worse, and the bridge swung violently starboard, planks tilting, and Kestrel slid.

She didn't scream. That was the worst part, the part Wren would carry for years after. She simply slipped, and her fingers found the lower rope, and she hung there over four hundred feet of roaring dark with one hand and a lantern chained to her wrist, and her face — even in that moment, even then — was calm. Resigned. As if she'd always known the story ended here.

Something in Wren's chest tore open.

Later, they would not remember deciding. They would remember the feeling: the storm that lived coiled behind their sternum since the court martial, since the day they'd sworn it shut behind bars of oath and shame, pressing outward like water against a dam. They would remember opening their hands — the scarred hands, the hands they'd hidden from her for days — and letting it out. Just a sliver. A thread pulled from the whole cloth of themselves.

The sky answered.

There were no stars to call anymore; the sky-magic's old anchors were dead or stolen. But storms had never needed stars. Storms needed only heat and cold and the wild difference between them, and the gorge provided all of it — the river's breath rising warm, the frost-air pouring down cold, and Wren standing on a broken bridge between the two like a key in a lock.

Wind hit the gorge sideways.

It came down the canyon walls in a single focused gust, screaming, and it caught Kestrel's falling body the way a father catches a child — not gently, but completely. It pressed her back against the swaying planks, pinned her there, gave her hand purchase where there had been none. Sleet ripped out of clear air, stinging, blinding, and somewhere above, the archer's next arrow went wide, torn from its line. On the near bank, torches died all at once, drowned in sudden horizontal rain.

For three heartbeats, Wren held the wind. Three heartbeats of pouring themselves through their open palms into the dark, feeling the old terrible joy of it, the *rightness*, the sky bending to their spine like a bow to its string —

— and knowing, even as they did it, exactly what it cost.

Because Kestrel was looking at them.

She'd climbed back onto the planks, soaked and shaking, and she was looking at Wren with the wind streaming around them both, and her expression was not gratitude. It was recognition. The specific, dawning horror of a woman watching a wanted notice come to life.

"*Storm-caller,*" she said. Not a question. The word fell between them like a dropped blade.

"Move," Wren said hoarsely. "Please. Talk when we're not dying."

They ran the last twenty planks. The far anchor held — rotten, yes, but holding, groaning like a damned soul as they threw themselves off onto solid rock and rolled apart among the boulders. Behind them the bridge swung empty over the gorge, its severed ropes trailing smoke, and the rain Wren had called began, slowly, grudgingly, to die.

Silence came back in pieces. River first. Then wind. Then breathing — theirs, ragged, and beneath it, faint and steady, the hum of the lantern.

Kestrel sat up. Water ran off her chin. She didn't wipe it away. She was staring at Wren's hands — bare now, gloves lost somewhere on the bridge or burned away by the calling, and the scars were plain in the lantern's leaked glow: pale lines running palm to forearm, branching like lightning frozen mid-strike. Lichtenberg marks. The signature every storm-caller wore, the mark the Inquisition catalogued, the mark Wren had spent seven years hiding under leather and lies.

"You," Kestrel said quietly, "are Wren Dunmore."

"I'm a smuggler."

"You're the Storm-Caller of the Eastern Reach. You're the one who refused the order at Marrow Ford." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Your face is on notices in every garrison from here to the capital. I've *seen* them. I memorized them, because—" She stopped. Started again, softer. "Because I thought if I ever found someone like you, it would mean there was still something left worth finding."

Wren said nothing. The rain dripped off the rocks. Their hands lay open in their lap, scars and all, and they could not seem to make fists of them.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Would it have changed anything?"

"Yes." Kestrel laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it. "No. I don't know. I've been lying to you since the night we met, Wren Dunmore. I suppose I assumed everyone was."

Above them, on the cliff, the archer had gone. On the far bank, the torches had withdrawn — not fled, merely paused, the way wolves pause. Wren heard them moving along the gorge rim, parallel, patient, keeping pace. There was a ford two miles north. There was always a ford. The hunt wasn't over; it had simply changed shape.

But none of that mattered as much as the thing sitting between them on the wet rock, wearing Wren's name and Wren's scars.

"The man with the spyglass," Wren said at last. "He saw the ember. He saw me call. By morning, Halvard will know exactly who he's hunting and exactly where we're going." They looked up at the starless sky, at the place where the Observatory's peak cut the horizon like a broken tooth. "I just handed him our map."

"Wren." Kestrel's voice was very quiet. "Why does a smuggler know how to do what you just did? Why does a smuggler have those scars?"

"Because I was a soldier once. Because they asked me to drown a column of refugees with a storm, and I said no, and this is what 'no' costs." Wren finally looked at her, and found they couldn't hold the look, and looked away at the black water instead. "You carry an ember. I carry this. Between us we're a complete set of everything the crown wants dead."

Kestrel reached out — hesitated — and closed her hand over Wren's scarred one. Her palm was warm. Impossibly, gently warm, the way the lantern was warm, and Wren realized with a jolt that some of that warmth was hers to give, that she was giving it deliberately, a small secret kindness in a night that had run out of them.

"We should keep moving," she said. "Before they find the ford."

"We should."

Neither of them moved for another long moment, hands joined on the rock, while the river thundered and the wolves paced the rim above them, and Wren understood, with a terror that had nothing to do with arrows, that they had just become legible — that everything they were, everything they'd buried, was now written in lightning across the sky for anyone who knew how to read it.

And that the person reading it best was holding their hand.

* * *

Inquisitor Halvard read reports the way other men prayed: on his knees, figuratively speaking, though he had actually knelt once, years ago, before the empty altar in the Hall of Records, and found the experience clarifying rather than comforting. Gods asked for faith. Reports asked only for accuracy. He knew which he trusted.

The message arrived an hour before dawn, carried by relay hawk to the forward camp at Grayfen, and Halvard read it twice by lamplight while his adjutant waited with the particular stillness of a man who has learned not to breathe near his superior before coffee.

*Contact at the Vell's Teeth crossing. Subject female confirmed carrying anomalous luminous artifact. Subject male confirmed as Dunmore, Wren — ex-storm-caller, court-martialed 7 years prior. Dunmore employed active weather-working during engagement. Recommend escalation.*

Halvard set the paper down.

*Dunmore.* He remembered the name from the tribunal transcripts, one file among hundreds he'd reviewed during the great purging, back when the crown had still bothered with tribunals. The storm-caller who wouldn't. There had been a debate, he recalled — brief, academic — about whether refusal was treason or mere weakness. The tribunal had settled on cowardice dressed as conscience, stripped the man's rank, scarred his record, and let him vanish into the mountains. Halvard had thought no more about it for seven years.

Now the coward and the ember were traveling together, and the coincidence sat in Halvard's chest like a swallowed stone.

Coincidences were how the old orders had hidden their work. He'd documented it exhaustively. The star-priests had not simply been murdered, whatever the sentimental revisionists whispered; they had been *disbanded*, their magic confiscated for the crown's protection, their observatories decommissioned as hazards. That a surviving apprentice should stumble, of all the roads in all the kingdom, into the company of the one storm-caller who had defied the confiscation order — that was not chance. That was a network. A remnant. The old faith, gathering its bones.

And bones, Halvard believed firmly, were meant to stay buried.

"Master Quill," he said.

His adjutant straightened. "Inquisitor."

"Dunmore is taking them to Thornfell Observatory. There's nowhere else. The ember needs an anchor-point, and there are only three in the range, and two of them are watched." He rose, fastening his coat with the deliberate precision of a man dressing for a wedding. "Double the pickets on the north ford. Send riders to the Ashen Company — I want mercenaries, not soldiers, for what comes next. Soldiers ask questions."

"And the woman, Inquisitor? If we take her alive—"

"The woman is a vessel, Quill, not a person. What's inside her is the property of the crown, stolen property, and we are going to recover it." Halvard gathered his maps, his seals, his instruments of gentle persuasion. "But Dunmore interests me. A man who refused one massacre may be persuaded to prevent another. Mercy, properly applied, is the sharpest tool the Office possesses."

He blew out the lamp. Through the tent flap, the sky showed black and starless, and Halvard regarded it with the satisfaction of a clerk regarding a corrected ledger.

Soon, he thought. Soon the ledger would balance.

Far to the north, two figures climbed through the freezing dark toward a ruin neither of them fully believed in, pursued by certainty itself — and the first pale hint of false dawn found no stars to greet, only the smell of coming snow, and the sound, very far behind them, of hoofbeats on the high road.
