## 10. The Price of Survival

Kestrel woke to the smell of pine smoke and the sound of Wren humming.

It was a low, tuneless thing — not a song so much as a habit of breath, the way a person might hum while their hands were busy and their mind was far away. She lay still in the cave's mouth with her blanket pulled to her chin and watched them work at the fire: sleeves rolled, forearms bare in the gray pre-dawn light, turning a flat stone that served as a griddle for the last of their oats.

The firelight moved across Wren's hands, and Kestrel looked away too late. She had already seen them again — the scars. Pale ridges crossing the backs of both hands like river deltas drawn on old maps, running up beneath the rolled cuffs to who knew where. She had noticed them the first night, when Wren had carried her from the road and she had been too fevered to pretend not to notice. She had noticed them again by the tollhouse fire, when Wren's blood was on the stones and Kestrel's own hands had glowed like captive suns.

She had never asked. Wren had never offered. That unspoken treaty had held for six days now, through Grayfen and the high switchbacks and the cold cave where they slept back to back for warmth, each pretending sleep came easily.

But this morning the treaty felt thin as ice over deep water.

Because last night, half-dreaming, Kestrel had heard voices on the wind.

Not spirits. Not the ember. Actual voices, human ones, carried up the pass on a thermal that rose from the valley below like a slow exhalation. She had been dozing against the cave wall, and the words had come to her in fragments, clear as if spoken into her ear:

*—the storm-caller Dunmore, they say. The one who would not burn the refugee columns at Vell's Crossing.*

*—Halvard wants them alive. Wants them found. There's a purse in it.*

*—and the woman he travels with. The ember-bearer.*

Kestrel had sat rigid in the dark until the wind shifted and the voices dissolved, her heart hammering against her ribs. Not because of Halvard. She had known about Halvard since the capital burned; fear of him was as constant as her own pulse, a low note always sounding beneath everything else.

No — what had kept her awake was *Vell's Crossing*. Because she remembered it.

Everyone in the capital remembered it, or claimed to. It had been three years ago, near the end of the border war, when the eastern provinces rose and the crown's answer was fire. The refugee columns fleeing Vell's Crossing had been called rebels, traitors, a fifth column marching under cover of civilians — and the order had gone out to a young storm-caller whose name had been famous even then, the prodigy of the Thornfell weather-courts, the one they said could read a sky the way scholars read scripture.

Wren Dunmore had refused.

Kestrel had been sixteen, an apprentice in the star-priests' cloister, and she remembered the whispers moving through the halls like a draft under a door: *Dunmore stood before the War Council and said no. Said the columns were families. Said he would call rain on their crops and calm for their roads, but no lightning, no hail, no storm turned to slaughter.* She remembered the older priests shaking their heads — pity mixed with something like awe. She remembered the proclamation a week later: treason, exile, the stripping of rank and name. And she remembered, most of all, how quickly the story had been buried. Within a season, the court's version had hardened: Dunmore had been a coward, or mad, or secretly a rebel agent. The truth — that a soldier had simply refused to murder children — had been too dangerous to leave standing. So it had been dug under, like everything else the crown could not abide.

Like the star-priests themselves.

Kestrel opened her eyes fully and watched Wren across the small fire, and the knowledge rearranged itself inside her with the slow, grinding weight of a millstone settling into its housing.

*That is who carries me,* she thought. *That is who bleeds for me.*

* *

They ate breakfast in companionable silence, and Kestrel used the silence the way she used all silences now: to calculate.

It was a habit she hated in herself. Her teachers at the cloister had called it *discernment*, had praised her for it — *Sister Meren said you have a jeweler's eye, Kestrel, you weigh everything* — but out here, in the world after the purge, discernment felt more like rot. Every kindness she received, she weighed. Every gift, she measured against what it might cost her, or cost the ember. Six days ago a stranger had found her dying on a smuggler's road and had carried her, fed her, fought for her, bled for her — and Kestrel had lain awake cataloguing all of it like a merchant taking inventory, because that was what survival had made of her.

And this morning the inventory turned up something unbearable.

Because she knew — had known since the second day, when she'd first seen the scars and started piecing the wanted-notices together in her memory — that Wren Dunmore's exile was not merely a sentence. It was a door that could be reopened. The proclamations had been specific; she'd read them posted on the cloister gates. *Pardon may be granted upon proof of loyalty.* Exiles who performed some great service to the crown could be restored. Rank, name, home. All of it returned.

And what service could be greater than delivering the last ember of the sky-magic into Inquisitor Halvard's hands?

Kestrel stared into the fire and felt the thought move through her like something swallowed wrong — a cold weight behind her breastbone that would not go down. She did not want it. She wanted to be clear about that, wanted to stand inside the wanting and inspect it and find it absent. But honesty was the discipline her order had drilled deepest into her, deeper even than prayer, and honest inspection found the thought there, coiled and patient:

*If I surrendered the ember, Wren goes home.*

The ember warmed against her sternum, where it lived beneath her skin these days — not literally, not anymore, but close enough that she had stopped being able to tell where her heartbeat ended and its pulse began. It had saved Wren's life at the tollhouse. It had lit their way through passes so dark the darkness seemed to have texture. It was, as far as she knew, the last living fragment of the old sky, the seed from which every star might grow again — and it could also, quite easily, buy one pardoned soldier his life back.

She could walk into the nearest garrison post tomorrow. She could say the words. *I am Kestrel of the Order of the Veiled Sky, and I carry the ember, and I will give it to you if you restore the exile who walks beside me.* Halvard, she suspected, would laugh at the bargain — he wanted the ember regardless — but the offer would spare Wren the hunt, perhaps spare Wren's life entirely. A trade. One relic for one soul. Clean arithmetic, the kind the Inquisition itself would appreciate.

And it would end everything the ember was meant to do.

It would end the stars ever returning. It would seal the crown's lie forever — the lie Kestrel had run three hundred miles carrying in her chest, the truth that the king's men had slaughtered the star-priests in their observatory while they slept, that the "plague" announced from the palace balconies had been swords, that the vanished stars were not a natural grief but a wound deliberately made. If she gave up the ember, the truth died with it, and Wren Dunmore got a pardon bought with that death.

*He would refuse,* she told herself. *He refused once already. He'd refuse again.*

But that wasn't the point, and she knew it. The point was that she had *thought* it. That some shrunken, starving part of her — the part that was tired, that missed warm bread and walls and the smell of incense, that wanted desperately to stop running — had looked at the man who had carried her and seen, for one flickering moment, not a person but a price. A coin large enough to purchase safety. Hers, and his, purchased with the future of the sky.

Her tea had gone cold. She drank it anyway.

"You're making that face," Wren said.

Kestrel blinked. "What face?"

"The one where you're arguing with yourself and losing." Wren scraped the griddle stone clean with a stick, not looking up. Their voice was easy, almost lazy, but Kestrel had learned to hear the attention underneath it — Wren noticed everything, filed everything, the way soldiers did. "Whatever it is, eat first. Arguments go better fed."

"I'm not—" Kestrel started, and stopped, because lying to Wren had begun to feel like throwing stones into a well and never hearing them land. "It's nothing. Bad dream."

Wren glanced up then, and for a moment their eyes met across the fire, and Kestrel saw something in them that undid her a little: trust. Unguarded, undeserved, offered without arithmetic. This person who had been betrayed by their own government, stripped of their name, hunted across their own mountains — this person looked at her and simply believed she was worth the trouble.

*You don't know what I almost traded you for,* she thought. *You don't know that last night I lay ten feet from you doing sums with your freedom.*

"Bad dreams are usually true," Wren said mildly, rising to kick dirt over the fire. "Just wearing costumes. Come on — we make the ridge by midday or we lose the light."

* *

They climbed.

The pass narrowed above the treeline into a corridor of gray stone, and Kestrel climbed it with the ember's warmth pulsing in time with her effort, and all the while the guilt climbed with her, matching her stride for stride.

She kept seeing the massacre. Not the real one — she had fled the capital before the swords came, had only heard it afterward from the one surviving acolyte who reached the mountain roads ahead of her — but a version of it her mind insisted on building, night after night: the observatory dome split open like an egg, the brass instruments toppled, the old priests' robes dark on the marble floors. And superimposed over it now, horribly, a new image: Wren Dunmore standing in a garrison courtyard, straightening out of exile's stoop, receiving back a uniform and a name while Kestrel knelt in chains nearby, emptied, the last light of the old sky guttering in a lantern on an Inquisitor's table.

In that image, Wren never looked at her. That was the detail that hurt most, though it was her own invention. In the image, Wren took the pardon and walked away without looking back, and why would they look back? What had Kestrel given them except danger? She had brought Halvard down on them like a hawk on a hare. She had gotten them cut at the tollhouse — she had seen the blade open Wren's side and had burned a hole in the world to close it, revealing herself, spending secrecy she could not afford, and Wren had thanked her. Had *thanked* her, gripping her glowing hands like they were something precious rather than something catastrophic.

"They'd say yes," Kestrel murmured to herself, half a syllable lost in wind. "Anyone would. Anyone sane."

"What?" Wren called back, twenty feet up the switchback.

"Nothing. Talking to the mountain."

"The mountains here gossip," Wren said gravely. "Careful what you confess."

Kestrel laughed despite herself — a short, startled sound — and hated how easily this person pulled laughter out of her, how the sound seemed to loosen the millstone for whole seconds at a time before it settled back into place, heavier than before.

At midday they gained the ridge, and the world opened.

Below them the Thornfell foothills tumbled away southward in waves of forest and shadowed valley, and beyond, impossibly far, the faint smudge of the lowlands where the capital sat like a spider at the center of its web. Above them — nothing. No stars, even in daylight's memory; the sky was a flat, sourceless gray, like the inside of a closed lid. Six days since the wards broke and the stars went out, and Kestrel still caught herself reaching for constellations that weren't there, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

"There." Wren pointed north along the ridgeline, where the peaks stepped upward toward a jagged white saddle. "Tomorrow we cross the saddle. Day after, we should see the Observatory's tower — if it still stands."

"And if it doesn't?"

Wren was quiet a moment. Wind pulled at their hair. "Then we'll decide what comes next," they said. "Together. Whatever that turns out to be."

*Together.* Kestrel turned the word over and felt its edges, sharp and soft at once. Together meant she had more to lose than an ember. Together meant the arithmetic she'd done in the dark was not just betrayal of a cause — it was betrayal of a *person*, one who said *together* as naturally as breathing, one who had refused to burn refugee columns because he could not stomach the death of strangers, and whom she had contemplated selling for the comfort of walls.

"I need to tell you something," she said.

Wren turned. The gray light flattened their features, but not their attention; that came through undimmed, total, patient. Waiting. They were very good at waiting. It occurred to Kestrel that this was what refusal must have looked like at the War Council — not a dramatic defiance, not thunder, just a person standing still while everyone around them demanded motion.

"I know who you are," Kestrel said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Wren. I've known since the second day. The notices — I saw them in the capital, years ago. And last night, on the wind, I heard bounty-talk. Vell's Crossing. The refugee columns." She forced herself not to look away. "I know what you refused to do. I've known for days, and I didn't say anything, and that wasn't fair to you."

The wind moved between them. Somewhere below, a bird cried once and went silent, as if embarrassed.

"All right," Wren said finally. Not angry. Not wounded, even — or if wounded, folding it away somewhere she couldn't see. "Thank you for telling me. Are you afraid of me now?"

"No." The answer came instantly and surprised her with its truth. "No, I'm—" *Ashamed,* she almost said. *I'm ashamed, and I can't tell you why, and the reason is monstrous.* "I'm sorry I didn't say sooner."

"You had your reasons." Wren shrugged, shouldering the pack. "We all keep our wounds folded. Mine are on my hands, yours are wherever you keep yours." A pause, and then, gently, with terrible perceptiveness: "Whenever you're ready to unfold them, Kestrel, I'll be here. Not before. I won't pry at you."

They started walking, and Kestrel followed, and the confession she owed — *I thought about trading you; I did the sums; the ember could buy your pardon and I let myself imagine it* — sat in her throat like a swallowed stone, going nowhere.

Because that was the price of survival, she understood now. Not Halvard, not hunger, not the miles. The price was this: that staying alive long enough required becoming someone who calculated, and calculation was a knife with no safe edge, and tonight, when they camped, when Wren slept trusting and scarred-handed beside her, Kestrel would have to lie there holding the secret of her own temptation, guarding it the way she guarded the ember —

— and it was then, cresting the ridge behind Wren, that she saw the smoke.

Far to the south, back along the trail they'd left yesterday, thin black columns rose from the tree line. Too many. Too deliberate. Not wildfire — wildfire didn't burn in neat, spaced plumes.

Someone was burning the villages behind them, asking questions.

And the wind, shifting, brought up the smell of it, and beneath the smoke something else — a sound like iron being dragged slowly across stone, rhythmic and patient, coming from the pass ahead.

Wren had heard it too. They had stopped walking, head tilted, hand drifting to the knife at their belt.

"That," Wren said quietly, "is not a wolf."
