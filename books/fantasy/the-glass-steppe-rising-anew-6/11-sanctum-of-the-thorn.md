## 11. Sanctum of the Thorn

The house appeared the way Mother Ashe always had: when you stopped looking for it.

Wren had walked this stretch of the Grayfen lowlands twice in their life, both times at night, both times half-lost, and both times the path had refused to exist until it decided to. Now, with Kestrel limping beside them and Halvard's hunters surely a day behind, the thorn hedge simply opened — a black seam in a wall of brambles that had no business having a door — and exhaled the smell of woodsmoke, tallow, and something medicinal and bitter, like yarrow boiled too long.

"Stay close," Wren murmured. "And whatever she says to you, don't argue. She wins arguments the way rivers win against stone."

Kestrel's answer was a look. She'd been giving Wren that look for two days now — the one that said *I have survived worse than your mentors* — but her hand stayed near the iron lantern under her cloak, and her weight kept drifting toward Wren's side as her wound pulled at her. The ember's warmth had kept infection out of the gash on her thigh; it could not keep exhaustion out of her bones.

They passed through the seam and the hedge closed behind them like water closing over a dropped coin.

Inside was a courtyard no larger than a barn floor, ringed by the hedge on three sides and by the leaning bulk of the house itself on the fourth — a long, low building of fieldstone and turf roof, its windows glowing amber, its chimney breathing slow gray smoke into a starless sky. Chickens muttered somewhere in the dark. A goat regarded them with ancient disapproval from atop a fallen cart.

And in the doorway, framed by lamplight, stood a woman built like a door herself: broad, immovable, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, her silver hair braided tight against her skull. Her face was a map of every hard year the Thornfells had ever inflicted on anyone, and her eyes — pale, sharp, unhurried — went first to Kestrel, then to Wren, then back to Kestrel, and narrowed.

"Well," said Mother Ashe. "You've brought me a corpse."

"She's not—"

"Not yet." Ashe stepped aside. "In. Before something sees you. And boy—" her voice caught Wren at the threshold like a hand on the collar, "—you look like a man who's been walking through his own funeral. We'll deal with that after."

* * *

The interior of the house was warm in the way that only very old houses are warm, heat soaked into the stones themselves over decades of fires. Ashe put Kestrel in a chair by the hearth, propped the wounded leg on a stool, and set about her with the brisk, unsentimental competence of a woman who had stitched smugglers, soldiers, and at least one Inquisitor's deserter back together in her time. She cleaned the wound without apology for the pain it caused, packed it with a poultice that smelled of comfrey and something sharper, and bound it while Kestrel sat white-knuckled and silent, refusing even to hiss.

"Tough," Ashe observed. "Or stupid. Often the same thing."

"Often," Kestrel agreed, through her teeth.

Ashe snorted. Then she turned, took Wren's chin in one calloused hand, and turned their face to the light with the proprietary roughness of someone who had done it since Wren was fourteen and bleeding from their first run gone wrong.

"You've not eaten properly in a week. You flinch when the wind changes. And you came through my hedge at a dead run, which means whatever's hunting you is close enough to smell." She released them. "So. Start talking."

Wren talked. Not all of it — not the ember, not yet, though they felt Kestrel's eyes on them the whole time, weighing what would be told and what withheld — but enough. The wards falling. The stars going out. The woman on the road. The tollhouse at Grayfen, the betrayal, the blade, the light that had poured out of Kestrel's hands and thrown three armed men across a room. The riders they'd seen on the ridgeline at dusk, torches moving in a pattern Wren recognized from the war: a sweep line, methodical as a comb drawn through hair.

Ashe listened without interrupting, which was worse than interruption. When Wren finished, she was quiet for a long moment, feeding a split log to the fire.

"The tollhouse keeper sold you," she said at last. It wasn't a question.

"He did."

"And he'll sell me next, once they lean on him hard enough. He knows I exist. Everyone in the lowlands knows Mother Ashe exists; that's the point of me." She looked at Kestrel then, really looked, and Wren saw something pass over her face that they had almost never seen there: uncertainty. "And you. The thing you did at the tollhouse. There's folk who'd burn a village to get their hands on that kind of light."

"There are folk who already have," Kestrel said quietly.

The silence that followed had weight. Ashe glanced at Wren — a quick, measuring glance, the glance of a woman doing arithmetic with lives — and Wren understood, with a cold drop in their stomach, that she had guessed more than they'd said.

"Right," Ashe said, standing. "Nobody decides anything tonight. Kestrel sleeps in the east room. Wren, you're on the settle by the fire. And if either of you sets foot outside my hedge before I say so, I'll turn you over to the Inquisition myself, out of sheer irritation."

It was the closest thing to tenderness she knew how to offer. Wren took it gratefully.

* * *

Sleep did not come easily, and when it came, it came with the court martial again.

The white room. The long table. The officers in their dress grays, faces smoothed into the particular blankness of men about to do something unforgivable and calling it duty. General Corvane's voice, mild as milk: *Refugee columns moving through the Vell Pass, Storm-Caller Dunmore. Third Battalion cannot engage without exposing our flank. You will call the storm.*

*I will not.*

*You will be given one more order, Dunmore, and you will follow it, or you will learn what we do with weapons that misfire.*

And Wren, twenty-three years old, scarred hands flat on the table, hearing themselves say the words that unmade their life: *Then I am a weapon that misfires.*

They woke in the dark with their heart slamming and their scarred hands curled into fists against their chest, and for a moment they didn't know where they were — only that the stars were gone, the stars were gone, the sky was a lid nailed down over the world—

A sound. Soft, deliberate. Kestrel, sitting up in the corner of the settle's shadow, watching them.

"I make noise," Wren said. Their voice was gravel. "When I sleep. Sorry."

"You say names," Kestrel said. "Mostly one name. Corvane." She paused. "I'm not asking. I'm telling you I heard, so you don't wonder later whether I did."

Wren lay back and stared at the ceiling beams. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse went about its small, urgent business.

"They wanted me to drown a column of refugees in rain and lightning," they said, because it seemed suddenly unbearable to let the silence stand. "Two hundred people, maybe more. Families. They called it a tactical necessity. I said no, and everything I was — rank, name, home, country — they took it all and burned it and told everyone I died in disgrace." A breath. "So when your lantern hums and the wards fall and an Inquisitor rides, I find I don't have much patience left for kingdoms."

Kestrel was quiet a while. Then: "I know why I ran. Do you want to know?"

"Only if it costs you nothing to tell."

"It costs plenty." But she told it anyway, low, in the firelight: the observatory towers in the capital, the night the soldiers came, her teacher pressing the ember into her hands and shoving her down the ash-chute while the doors were broken in. The weeks of running. The way the ember sang to her in dreams, and how lately the singing had changed key, become urgent, become almost frightened.

"It wants to go home," she said. "There's a place where it can be made safe again. My teacher told me the name of it before the end, and I've been walking toward it ever since." Her eyes found Wren's. "Thornfell Observatory."

Wren laughed — one short, cracked note with no humor in it at all. "Of course it is."

"You know it?"

"I grew up in its shadow. My grandmother kept bees on its lower terraces, back when the priests still let folk climb that far. It's a ruin now. Has been since the purge." They rubbed their scarred hands together, an old habit, a self-soothing older than shame. "Four days' walk, if the weather holds. Five if it doesn't. Six if Halvard has the passes watched, which he will."

"So we're agreed," Kestrel said. "We go together."

Wren didn't answer. Because at that moment, in the doorway behind Kestrel, silent as smoke, stood Mother Ashe — and the look on her face said she had heard every word, and had come to a decision, and that the decision had been made some time ago.

"Morning," Ashe said. "Both of you. My parlor. We'll talk business." And she was gone again, leaving the fire crackling and the word *business* hanging in the warm air like a blade left unsheathed.

* * *

The parlor was a small room off the main hall, dominated by a table scarred with candle burns and old knife marks, and by the maps pinned to its walls — maps of the Thornfells, of the borderlands, of routes that officially did not exist. Ashe sat behind the table with a ledger open before her and two cups of chicory steaming on the wood, and she waited until Wren and Kestrel had taken the chairs opposite before she spoke.

"I've been running people out of this kingdom for thirty years," she said. "Deserters, debtors, heretics, one prince — don't ask. I know every hole in every wall between here and the coast, and I own, outright or otherwise, the loyalty of every soul who guards them." She folded her hands. "So understand that when I tell you what I'm about to tell you, I am offering you the best work of my career."

She slid a folder across the table. Inside: papers. Beautiful papers — letters of passage under the seal of a minor coastal lordship, a bill of health, a marriage certificate made out in two names that were not Wren Dunmore's and not anyone's. A whole new life, laminated in ink and wax.

"A shipmaster in Saltmere owes me more than money can cover. In nine days a grain vessel leaves for the southern isles, and it does not ask questions, because I pay it not to. New name. New history. Sun that actually shines, sea air, fish so fresh it insults you." She leaned back. "You'd be free, Wren. Truly free. No Inquisitor, no bounty, no looking over your shoulder until your neck gives out. That's the offer. Take it or leave it, but decide fast, because the man hunting you doesn't sleep."

Wren looked at the papers. The name on the marriage certificate was soft and foreign and utterly without history. *Tamsin Hale.* Someone who had never called a storm. Someone whose hands were unscarred.

"And Kestrel?" they asked.

"What about her?"

"Do the papers cover her? Does the ship take two?"

Ashe's silence was the answer. Wren felt it land in their chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"The ship takes one," Ashe said, gently, which was worse than if she'd been cruel. "One fugitive with forged papers is cargo. Two is a conspiracy. And her—" she nodded at Kestrel, and lowered her voice as though the walls themselves might inform, "—whatever she's carrying, it's not a person you can smuggle, Wren. It's a beacon. Every ward, every scrying-glass, every Inquisitor's compass from here to the capital is straining toward her like a dog toward a whistle. You go where she goes, you die where she dies. And she *is* going to die, boy, unless she reaches wherever she's going, and she'll reach it faster and likelier without you slowing her down and doubling the trail."

"That's not your decision."

"No. It's yours. That's why I'm making it expensive." Ashe's pale eyes held theirs, steady and pitiless with love. "I buried friends who hesitated, Wren. I'm not burying you. Nine days. The offer stands until the tide turns, and not one hour past."

She closed the ledger. The audience, such as it was, was over.

* * *

Wren walked the courtyard at dusk, because walls had begun to feel like the inside of their own skull.

The thorn hedge stood black against a sky the color of a bruise fading to yellow. No stars. There would never be stars again, unless — unless a wounded woman carrying the last ember of a murdered faith reached a ruin in the mountains where Wren's grandmother had kept bees, and did whatever impossible thing the ember demanded, and survived it.

*Tamsin Hale,* said the papers in their pocket. *Saltmere. The southern isles. Sun.*

They tried to imagine it. A small white house above a harbor. Hands learning nets instead of blades. Sleep without names shouted into the dark. It was not a bad life. It was, in fact, the exact life they had dreamed of during the worst years of exile, huddled in smugglers' caves with frost in their boots: a life so far removed from kingdoms and their appetites that the kingdom forgot you existed.

And Kestrel would walk north alone, four days ahead of a sweep line, with a beacon in her cloak and a wound in her leg, and Wren would hear about it eventually, the way you hear about weather. *Ember-bearer captured in the high passes. Inquisition commends the swift resolution.* And the stars would stay dead, and the lie would stand forever, and Tamsin Hale would eat fresh fish under a clean sun and slowly, slowly rot from the inside out.

"You're doing the thing," said Kestrel's voice behind them, "where you stare at a hedge like it owes you money."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. "Ashe talks to you?"

"She fed me. Yelled at my bandaging technique. Asked me eleven questions about the observatory road and answered none of mine." Kestrel leaned on the fence rail beside them, favoring her good leg, her breath fogging in the cooling air. Below the hedge, the valley was filling with blue evening shadow. "She offered you a way out."

It wasn't a question. Wren looked at her. "How—"

"Because she looked at you exactly the way my teacher looked at me, the last night. The way people look at someone when they're handing them a life and apologizing for the price." Kestrel's jaw tightened. "What was the price?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. The goat had come down from its cart and stood watching them both with the weary patience of an animal that had seen many humans make fools of themselves.

"Everything except you," Wren said finally. "That was the price. The papers are for one."

Kestrel absorbed that. Wren watched her absorb it — watched her face do the terrible arithmetic that Wren had been doing all day, the sum that always came out the same. If Wren took the papers, Kestrel lived longer and freer and alone. If Wren burned the papers, they both probably died on a mountain, together.

"Don't," Kestrel said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Don't decide based on me. I've watched people decide things based on me my whole life — my teachers, the priests, the poor fool who carried me out of the city. It gets them killed, and then I carry that too, on top of everything else I carry." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she hated it, Wren could see her hating it. "I am so tired of being the reason."

"You're not the reason," Wren said. "You're the—"

They stopped. The sentence had been heading somewhere dangerous — toward a word neither of them had earned yet, a word that needed more miles and more blood and more laughter before it could be spoken honestly. Kestrel looked at them, waiting, and the dying light caught the ember-glow deep in her eyes, faint as a coal under ash.

Somewhere beyond the hedge, far down the valley, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then a whole chorus of them, ragged and frantic, sweeping along the valley road from the south — the direction of Grayfen, the direction of the tollhouse, the direction of everything they had fled.

Kestrel went rigid. "Hunters?"

"No." Wren's stomach dropped anyway, because they knew these dogs, knew this valley, knew what barking meant at this hour in this place. "Those are Ashe's dogs. All of them. All at once." They turned toward the house, where a lamp had just flared in the parlor window — one flash, two, three. An old signal. A signal Wren had learned at fifteen and prayed they'd never see used.

*K riders on the road. Come now.*

Mother Ashe's voice cut across the courtyard from the doorway, calm as a millstream and cold as the bottom of one:

"Wren. Get inside. They're not here for the girl."

A pause, one heartbeat long, and then the rest of it, delivered flat, the way you deliver news you've already finished grieving:

"They asked for you by name."
