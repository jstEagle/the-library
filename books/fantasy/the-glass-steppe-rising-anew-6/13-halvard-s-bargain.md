## 13. Halvard's Bargain

The cell smelled of tallow and old stone, and Mother Ashe sat in it as though she had chosen the room herself.

Inquisitor Halvard had seen prisoners weep, beg, curse, go silent as graves. He had catalogued every variety of human fear across nineteen years of service to the Crown's Inquisition, and he prided himself — quietly, without vanity, for vanity was a form of noise — on knowing what each kind of fear meant. But he had rarely encountered a woman who looked at her chains the way a carpenter looks at someone else's shoddy joinery.

"You've bound my wrists with iron," she said. Her voice was smoke-cured, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had spent forty years talking men out of their coin and their caution in equal measure. "Iron deadens nothing but itself, boy. You'd want silver for a storm-caller, and even then—" She flexed her fingers within the manacles, testing, the way one might test a fence post. "Even then you'd be guessing."

"You are not a storm-caller," Halvard said. He stood at the edge of the torchlight, hands folded behind his back, his gray coat brushed clean of the mountain dust that still clung to his soldiers' cloaks outside. "You are a smuggler. A receiver of stolen goods. A facilitator of heresy." He let each word settle like sediment. "And as of three days ago, an accessory to the flight of the ember-bearer."

"Is that what they're calling her now?" Ashe smiled. It was not a defiant smile; defiance was a performance, and Ashe was too old to perform for free. It was simply a smile, offered the way one might offer bread. "In my day we called them star-priests. Nicer word. Warmer."

"The star-priests were traitors."

"Mm. And the men who burned their observatory were heroes. I remember how the story goes. I've heard better stories from drunkards."

Halvard did not answer. This was part of his method, and he knew it well: silence was a room, and most people could not bear to stand in it alone. They would fill it with words, and words were doors, and doors opened.

Ashe filled the silence by humming.

It was, he realized after a moment, a lullaby. An old one, from the lowland provinces — *the moon is a lantern hung out for the lost* — and she hummed it with her eyes half-closed, her chained hands resting in her lap, as if she were sitting by her own hearth instead of in the deepest cell of Grayfen garrison. The tune wandered off at the end, unfinished, the way lullabies do when the child has already slept.

"You have information I require," Halvard said at last.

"I expect so. You went to a great deal of trouble."

"Where is Wren Dunmore?"

"Ah." Ashe opened her eyes. In the torchlight they were pale as river ice. "There it is."

* * *

He had come up into the Thornfells chasing a signature only he seemed able to read.

When the border wards collapsed — when the great lattice of suppression-magic that had lain over the kingdom for thirty years, invisible as breath, suddenly shattered like frost off a windowpane — the Court had panicked. The wardens spoke of sabotage. The generals spoke of invasion. The Archon spoke, in his silk-and-ash voice, of enemies within, and set the Inquisition loose to find them.

But Halvard had spent those same thirty years studying what the wards were built to smother. He had read the confiscated star-priest texts until he dreamed in their geometry. And so when the lattice broke, he alone understood what the breaking meant: something old had woken beneath it. Something warm. The wards had not been destroyed. They had been *outlived* — cracked open from within by a heat they were never forged to contain.

An ember. The last ember of sky-magic, moving north through the high passes, leaving a trail of warmth through dead wards that any properly educated man could follow like footprints in snow.

He followed it. And everywhere the trail touched, he found people who had helped, or might have helped, or knew someone who had helped. That was the nature of smuggling routes: they were made of people, and people could be broken, bought, or — in the rarest cases — reasoned with.

Mother Ashe, he suspected, would need to be reasoned with. Breaking her would take time, and time was the one currency the ember's trail did not honor. It moved. It warmed. Somewhere ahead of it walked a wounded woman and — the reports from the tollhouse skirmish had been fragmentary, but consistent on this point — a tall figure who fought like a soldier and whose blade-work bore the unmistakable stamp of Crown military training.

Wren Dunmore. The exile. The storm-caller who had refused the order at Vell's Crossing and vanished into these mountains rather than face the tribunal's sentence. Seven years of silence, and now the trail said Dunmore was walking beside the ember, guarding it, bleeding for it.

Halvard found this fascinating. Not troubling — fascinating. Because Halvard had read Dunmore's file more times than he had read scripture, and he understood something about the case that the tribunal, in its haste, had missed.

Dunmore had not refused the order out of weakness. Dunmore had refused it out of *conviction*. And conviction, Halvard believed, was not a flaw to be punished. It was a strength to be *redirected*. A soldier who would defy an order for conscience's sake was a soldier who understood, at the marrow level, what duty actually meant. Such a person did not need to be destroyed. Such a person needed to be *shown* — shown that their conscience and their kingdom were not enemies, that the order at Vell's Crossing had been a tragedy of imperfect information, that the Crown grieved its errors and honored the ones who took a stand.

That was why Halvard had come himself, rather than sending hunters. Some negotiations required rank. And some required sincerity.

* * *

"You may as well ask your questions," Ashe said, when the humming stopped. "You'll get answers. Just maybe not the ones you're carrying in that neat little ledger of yours."

"Why does Dunmore protect the ember-bearer?"

"Because Wren Dunmore has a spine. Rare commodity. You should look into stocking some."

"What is the ember-bearer's destination?"

Ashe studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to amuse her, or perhaps sadden her; with old smugglers the two were often indistinguishable. "You genuinely don't know," she said softly. "You've read all their books and you still don't know where she's going."

"Enlighten me."

"No." Ashe leaned back against the stone. "I'll tell you what I'll do instead, Inquisitor. I'll tell you about Wren Dunmore, since you're so fond of the file. You've got the tribunal record, the refusal, the exile. What you don't have is the seven years after. What you don't have is every refugee family that came over the passes fed and hidden and pointed safe because a wanted traitor spent their own last coin doing it. What you don't have is the letters."

"Letters?"

"Wren writes to mothers whose sons died at Vell's Crossing. Has done, every year, on the anniversary. Never signs them. The mothers know anyway." Ashe's voice dropped, and for the first time there was something beneath the smoke — something that had been banked a long time and burned very hot. "You think you're hunting a fugitive. You're hunting the best person I have ever known, and I have known murderers, saints, and tax collectors. So no, boy. I won't enlighten you. I'll just sit here and be disappointed in you, which takes less effort and wounds deeper."

Halvard absorbed this without visible reaction. Inside, something turned over — not doubt, never doubt, but a sharpening. The picture she painted confirmed his theory. Dunmore was exactly what he had believed: a person of extraordinary loyalty, misdirected. Loyal to refugees. Loyal to dead soldiers' mothers. Loyal, now, to a stranger carrying stolen fire.

All that loyalty needed was a worthy object.

"You will be tried," he said. "Your network will be dismantled. Your people will be questioned. Unless."

"Unless," Ashe repeated dryly.

"Unless you deliver a message for me. To Dunmore."

"And why would I do that?"

"Because the message is genuine mercy, and you are too honest a woman to withhold it from someone who deserves to hear it." He drew from his coat a letter — written the night before in his own hand, sealed with the Inquisition's gray wax — and laid it on the small table between them, beyond her reach but within her sight. "Full pardon. Restoration of rank and standing. The charges at Vell's Crossing formally reviewed, with the Crown's acknowledgment of error. A home. Not a cell. A home, in the capital, with honors."

Ashe looked at the letter the way she might look at a snake wearing a hat. "And all Wren has to do is hand over the girl."

"Hand over the ember," Halvard corrected. "The girl is incidental. She is a vessel, and vessels can be emptied safely. No harm needs to come to her. I give you my word."

"Your word." Ashe laughed — a short, unlovely sound. "Boy, I've watched your word at work. There's a village called Harrowmere up the valley road. Old man Tammas kept it. He told your riders what he knew, which was nothing, and you hanged him anyway, slow, in front of his neighbors, to make a point about thoroughness. Was that your word too?"

"Harrowmere was regrettable." And Halvard meant it; he felt the weight of it, carried it properly, the way a good officer carries his dead. "Elder Tammas withheld information he possessed. The law required—"

"The law required a corpse to frighten the rest of us. Don't dress it in regret, it doesn't fit you." Ashe's eyes had gone hard as river stones. "Here's my counteroffer, Inquisitor. I'll carry your letter. I'll put it in Wren's hands myself, if your people escort me close enough and then lose me, which they will, because my people are already watching your camp. And in exchange you don't touch my network. Not one runner, not one safehouse. Or I swallow that letter and die of natural causes in your custody, and you learn nothing ever again."

Halvard considered her. He had expected resistance; he had not expected terms. It occurred to him, distantly, that he was being negotiated with by a woman in manacles, and that he was losing.

"You will carry the letter," he said. "My escort will accompany you to the pass at Cindermark. Whether they lose you there is a matter of your own conduct." He paused. "The network question remains open."

"Everything remains open with you people. That's the whole business model." She held out her chained wrists. "Well? Are you going to unlock these, or shall I hop to Cindermark?"

* * *

That night, Halvard stood on the wall of Grayfen garrison and looked north into the starless dark.

It was the thing visitors could never understand, he thought — the sky without stars. Thirty years of the suppression wards had dimmed them gradually, gently, so that most citizens below remembered stars the way they remembered grandparents: fondly, vaguely, as figures from stories. But up here in the high country, under the dead lattice's thinnest veil, the absence was total. A black lid pressed down on the world. Travelers swore the mountains felt closer for it, as if the whole sky had lowered itself to listen.

His soldiers hated it. They lit extra fires and spoke of it as a blight, a curse, evidence of the enemy's malice. Halvard did not hate it. He had never hated anything; hatred was imprecise, and imprecision killed the wrong people.

But he understood, standing there, why the ember mattered. Why the Court feared it. When the stars returned — if they returned — everything the Crown had built its peace upon would stand in that light to be seen. Every purge, every silenced priest, every careful lie about the night the observatory burned. Light was not neutral. Light was judgment.

Which was precisely why it must not return. Or rather — and here Halvard corrected himself, as he always did, with the patience of a man aligning a precision instrument — why it must return *only under proper authority*. That was the distinction his superiors lacked the subtlety to grasp. The Court wanted the ember destroyed. Destruction was waste. Halvard wanted it *channeled*: taken, studied, bound into the Crown's service, its light made lawful. A kingdom with sanctioned stars was a kingdom twice blessed. A kingdom with wild stars was a kingdom on trial.

And Wren Dunmore was the key to it. Not the vessel-girl — the vessel was only cargo. Dunmore was the one who could legitimize the whole enterprise. Imagine it: the famous refuser, restored, standing before the Court to declare that the ember had been recovered *with his help*, that his conscience and the Crown's purpose had reconciled. The exile returning home in honor. The wound at Vell's Crossing closed at last, publicly, officially, with both sides absolved.

It would be the greatest act of mercy in a generation. Halvard believed this with the calm, load-bearing certainty that other men reserved for gravity. He was not a cruel man. Cruelty was inefficiency. He was a man who had devoted his life to the belief that order and compassion were the same project, properly administered — and that anyone who opposed him had simply not yet understood this, and deserved, patiently, to be brought to understanding.

Somewhere north of him, in that listening dark, Dunmore was reading his letter. Or soon would be. And Halvard imagined — not hoped, he did not hope, he *projected outcomes* — the moment the exile read the words *full pardon* and *home*, and felt the terrible sweet pull of them. Seven years of cold passes and salt-smuggling and unsigned letters to grieving mothers. Seven years of exile. And here, in gray wax and neat script, was the door back.

Everyone wanted to belong. Halvard had learned that early, in his own long-ago life, before the Inquisition had given his longing a structure and a salary. The refugees Dunmore protected wanted a homeland. The mothers wanted their sons honored. Even the vessel-girl, carrying her stolen fire through the freezing dark — she wanted, surely, to stop running. Belonging was the lever that moved every soul ever born, and Halvard had simply built himself the longest lever in the kingdom.

He would use it gently. That was what made him merciful.

Below the wall, a lantern bobbed through the garrison gate: Mother Ashe, released as agreed, flanked by four riders who would accompany her to Cindermark and report, in due course, exactly how they lost her. Halvard watched the light dwindle up the valley road until it was one spark among the dark peaks, and then not even that.

He turned to his adjutant, waiting respectfully at the stair.

"Ready the company," he said. "We ride at first light. If the pardon is refused—" and his voice remained perfectly level, perfectly reasonable, the voice of a man stating arithmetic "—then we proceed to the alternative. The ember will be secured either way. See that the archers are issued the heavy bowstrings. Mountain air is thin; arrows fly true, but I want margin."

"Yes, Lord Inquisitor."

He looked north once more. The black sky pressed close, patient, full of absent stars that did not yet know they belonged to the Crown.

*Come home, storm-caller,* he thought, with something almost like tenderness. *Come home, and be forgiven. It is such a small thing I am asking. It is only everything you are.*

Far up the valley, unseen, a second light answered the first — small, swift, traveling north along a smuggler's road toward a cave where two fugitives waited by a dying fire, one of them carrying a letter that smelled of gray wax and mercy, and the other carrying an ember that smelled, faintly and terribly, of morning.
