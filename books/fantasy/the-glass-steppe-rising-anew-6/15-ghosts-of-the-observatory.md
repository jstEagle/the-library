## 15. Ghosts of the Observatory

Kestrel had imagined the Observatory a hundred times on the long climb — always as something luminous, something waiting. In her mother's stories it had been a crown of white stone at the roof of the world, its dome open to the heavens like a flower turned toward the sun, its halls full of voices reciting the names of stars the way other people recited prayers. In her worst dreams it was ash. In her best ones it was salvation.

The reality was neither.

It rose out of the saddle of the mountain like a broken tooth: a black spire sheared off at half its height, the great dome beneath it caved inward so that it looked, from this distance, like a skull with its crown stove in. Snow filled the gutters where starlight had once been channeled down through silvered channels into the ember-vaults. The terraced gardens that should have been blazing with nightbloom and frostfire were tangles of dead thorn, gray as old bone.

But it stood. After twenty years, after the purge, after everything — *it stood*.

Kestrel stopped at the edge of the last switchback and let herself feel it, just for a moment. The iron lantern hung against her chest under her coat, and inside it the ember stirred, warm as a sleeping bird shifting in its nest. It felt the place too. She could tell by the way the warmth spread, by the faint pressure behind her sternum, like a hand pressing gently outward from within her ribs.

*We're home,* the warmth said. Or she imagined it said. Twenty years alone with a secret will make you hear voices in anything.

Behind her, Wren's boots crunched to a halt. They were leaning hard on the walking staff they'd cut two days ago, their left side stiff where Halvard's hunters had caught them with a crossbow bolt on the rope bridge — a graze, deep but clean, bound now with Ashe's linen and Kestrel's increasingly competent field surgery. Their face was gray with exhaustion, lips cracked from the cold, and when they looked up at the ruined spire, something moved across their features that Kestrel couldn't name. Grief, maybe. Recognition.

"It's smaller than I remembered," Wren said.

"You've been here before?"

"When I was a child. My mother brought me." Wren's voice went carefully flat, the way it always did when their family surfaced between them like a body rising in still water. "She said the Dunmores had called storms for the Observatory since before there was a kingdom. That we were the lightning's shepherds." A short, bitter breath. "I believed her."

Kestrel touched the lantern through her coat. "The keeper," she said. "If anyone survived the purge—"

"Twenty years, Kestrel. In a place like this." Wren didn't finish the sentence. They didn't have to. Hope was a currency Kestrel had learned to spend carefully; Wren had learned not to carry it at all.

But the ember pressed warmer against her breastbone, insistent, and hope was not something she could simply decline to feel. Not here. Not with the broken dome above them and the dead gardens below and the whole dark sky — still starless, still blind — hanging over everything like a held breath.

They climbed the last stretch in silence.

* * *

The outer gate was bronze, taller than three men, worked over every inch with constellations worn nearly smooth by centuries of pilgrims' hands. It hung open on one hinge, frozen mid-swing, and the gap it made was furred with ice. Beyond it lay the entrance hall, and Kestrel's breath caught as they stepped through.

Mosaics. Thousands of them, covering every wall and arch, tiles of lapis and gold and mother-of-pearl depicting the sky as it had been: the Shepherd's Arc, the Weeping Sisters, the great burning wheel of the Ember Throne itself at the center of the ceiling, directly beneath the shattered dome. The purge had left the mosaics untouched — why deface pictures when you could murder the people who understood them? — and so the hall remained a tomb of beauty, dust-silvered and silent, lit by the gray snow-light falling through the broken roof.

Kestrel walked forward slowly, her boots raising small ghosts in the dust. Here, she had been told, the apprentices slept in alcoves carved with their first-learned constellation. Here, the novices' choir had sung the dawn-count. Here—

A sound stopped her mid-step.

From somewhere deeper in the ruin came a voice. Thin, cracked, wavering up and down like a bird with a broken wing — but unmistakably a voice, and unmistakably human, and unmistakably *singing*.

Wren's hand closed around her arm. "You hear that too."

It wasn't a question. Kestrel nodded, her heart hammering so hard she could feel it in her throat. The song resolved as they listened — an old counting-song, the kind used to teach children the order of the stars. *First the Ember, then the Arc, then the Sisters three by three...* Her mother had sung it to her. Every apprentice learned it before they learned to read.

"Someone remembers it," she whispered. "Wren. Someone is *still singing it*."

They followed the sound.

* * *

The passage led down and inward, past collapsed galleries and doors swollen shut with rot, toward the heart of the Observatory — toward, Kestrel realized with a jolt, the ember-vaults. The song grew louder, and stranger. The singer changed keys without reason, repeated phrases, sometimes dropped words entirely and hummed instead, filling the gaps with a tune that wandered like smoke. And threaded through it all, underneath it, was another sound: a low, rhythmic tapping, metal on stone, patient as a heartbeat.

The corridor opened into the vault antechamber, and Kestrel saw him.

He sat in the center of the floor on a chair that had been built — no, *grown*, assembled piece by piece over years from scrap — facing a wall of stone he had polished to a mirror shine with decades of rubbing. He was ancient beyond guessing, wrapped in layer upon layer of robes that might once have been star-priest white and were now the color of old teeth. His beard reached his waist, knotted with small objects: buttons, beads, bits of tile, a tiny brass astrolabe no bigger than a coin. His hands, resting on the arms of his chair, held a small hammer, and even as they watched he lifted it and tapped the wall — *tink* — then returned to his song.

Around him, the chamber was covered in tallies. Thousands upon thousands of marks scratched into the stone, in rows, in columns, in spirals, climbing the walls to the ceiling. Days. Kestrel's stomach turned. Counted days, counted years, counted in a hand that had begun precise and scholarly and had deteriorated into scratches, into gouges, into madness.

"He's alive," she breathed. "Wren, he's — he's one of them. He's a keeper."

The old man's song stopped.

His head turned. It was a slow, grinding motion, like a gate unused for years, and when his face came around into the light Kestrel saw his eyes — milky, clouded, but fixed on her with terrible precision. On her. Not on Wren. On her, and more specifically, on her chest, on the shape beneath her coat, and the hammer began to tremble in his grip.

"No," he said. His voice, freed from song, was rust and gravel. "No, no, no. You're early. You're *early*. It isn't time. It isn't time for twenty years and it isn't time now."

"Keeper—" Kestrel stepped forward, hands open, the way you approached a frightened animal or a wounded soldier. "My name is Kestrel. I was apprenticed to the order — my mother was Sister Veyra, of the Ember Throne choir, she—"

"*Veyra.*" The name hit him like a physical blow. The hammer clattered to the floor. For a moment his clouded eyes cleared, and what looked out of them was so raw with grief that Kestrel took an involuntary step back. "Veyra sang the dawn-count. Veyra had a daughter with her laugh." His face crumpled and reformed, crumpled and reformed, grief and suspicion fighting for the same ground. "Veyra is dead. They burned the white tower with everyone in it. So either you are a ghost, girl, or you are *her*, or—" and here his voice dropped, dropped, dropped until it was barely a whisper, and the whisper was worse than any shout, "—or you are the thing that wears her daughter's face because the ember has come home and it is *hungry*."

"I don't understand," Kestrel said, and hated how small her voice sounded.

"The ember demands sacrifice." He said it simply, the way you'd state that water is wet, that winter follows autumn. "It always did. Why do you think the old kings fed it? Why do you think the spire stands?" He pushed himself up out of his chair with a strength that seemed impossible for his frame, joints cracking like green wood, and pointed one shaking finger at her chest. "It sleeps in its iron cage and it dreams of a throat to burn. And someone has carried it all this way, up my mountain, through my wards, to give it exactly what it wants."

"That's not—" Kestrel's hand flew to the lantern. Through the iron she could feel the ember's warmth, steady, gentle, *innocent*. Wasn't it? Hadn't it kept her alive through the purge, through the passes, through the dark? Hadn't it warmed her hands on the coldest nights like a living thing seeking contact? "The ember doesn't demand anything. It's the last light of the sky. It's *hope*."

"Hope." The old keeper laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound yet — high, cracked, echoing off the tally-marked walls. "Child. What do you think anchored the stars before? What do you think the Ember Throne *was*?" He spread his arms, encompassing the chamber, the ruin, the mountain. "Every age of light was paid for. The first keepers gave their years. The middle keepers gave their minds — look at the walls, girl, look at the walls, I have given mine gladly, I count for the sky, I count, I count—"

His voice fractured. He steadied himself against his chair, breathing hard, and when he looked up again there were tears cutting tracks through the grime on his cheeks.

"And the last keeper," he said quietly, "was meant to give everything. That was the rite. That was always the rite. A willing storm-caller, bound to the spire, burning so the sky could burn. Your mother knew. Veyra knew. Why do you think she sent you away with it instead of staying?" He shook his head slowly. "She wasn't saving the ember, child. She was saving *you* from what the ember would ask."

The world tilted. Kestrel heard it distantly, as if from underwater — Wren saying her name, Wren stepping between her and the old man, Wren's voice sharp with alarm — but she couldn't look away from the keeper, from his clouded eyes, from the truth settling over her colder than the mountain snow.

Her mother hadn't sent her away to preserve the ember.

She had sent her away to preserve her *life*.

And now Kestrel had climbed back up the mountain carrying it anyway, carrying it straight to the place where it could ask its price, and standing beside her — bleeding, exhausted, storm-caller, exile — was the only person in the world who could pay it.

"No," she said aloud. Her own voice sounded foreign. "No. There's another way. There has to be another way. The texts—"

"The texts are ash." The keeper bent painfully, retrieved his hammer, and settled back into his chair as though the interruption were over, as though he could return to his counting. But his eyes stayed on her, and in them, beneath the madness, was pity — vast, ancient, unbearable pity. "You brought a storm-caller to my mountain, girl. You brought the anchor to the flame. It knows. Oh, it knows." He raised the hammer and tapped the wall — *tink* — and resumed his song.

*First the Ember, then the Arc...*

Kestrel's hand was shaking against the lantern. Inside, the ember pulsed — warm, warm, warm — and for the first time in twenty years, she wondered whether the warmth had ever really been comfort.

Or appetite.

"Wren," she whispered. "What have I done?"

Beside her, Wren had gone very still, staring up through the broken dome at the empty, starless dark — and Kestrel saw, with a horror that curdled everything hopeful in her, that they had already understood.
