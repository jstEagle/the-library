## 16. What the Ember Demands

The last keeper's name was Ovid Sarn, though he had not answered to it in a long time.

He moved through the Observatory's great hall like a man walking through his own memory — past shattered orreries and star-charts gone brown as autumn leaves, past the cracked dome where snow sifted down in thin silver threads to pool on the mosaic floor. The mosaic showed the old sky: constellations rendered in lapis and gold, each star a small sun of glass that caught what little light remained and threw it back doubled. Wren walked on the Hunter. Kestrel walked on the Weaver. Between them, the empty socket where the Ember Star had been was a circle of bare black stone, and Ovid Sarn stepped around it with the care of a man avoiding an open grave.

"You brought it," he said. Not a question. His voice was dry as the charts, and it seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his throat. "I felt it cross the pass. Like a hand closing around my heart after forty years of nothing."

Kestrel held the iron lantern a little closer to her chest. In its lattice cage, the ember pulsed — not steadily, but in something like breath. In like a tide going out, out like a tide coming home. Wren had watched it for three days now, through the long dark climb, and had stopped pretending they didn't see the pattern. It breathed. It waited. It wanted something.

"Master Sarn," Kestrel said carefully, "the texts say the ember can be rekindled at the original anchor point. That's why I carried it here. If you can show us the rite—"

"The rite." The old keeper laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the hollow ring of a bell with its clapper torn out. He turned, and up close Wren saw what the gloom had hidden: his eyes were milk-pale, scarred over, blind as river stones. He had burned them out himself, perhaps, or the purge had done it for him. He looked at neither of them and yet somehow at both. "Child, I wrote those texts. I taught the apprentices who carried you. Do you think we hid the truth out of cruelty? We hid it because no one would walk this road if they knew what waited at the end of it."

Snow whispered through the broken dome. Somewhere below them, faint as a heartbeat heard through stone, Wren felt the storm gathering over the Thornfell massif — the one Halvard would be riding beneath, if Halvard still lived, and Wren had never once believed otherwise.

"Then tell us," Wren said. "Whatever it is. Tell us."

Ovid Sarn found a bench beneath the mosaic of the Weaver and sat down heavily, folding his ruined hands in his lap. For a moment he only breathed, and the ember in its lantern breathed with him, and Wren understood suddenly that it had been listening the whole time.

"A star is not fire," the old man said. "That is the first lie the crown told, when they called our work sorcery and our priests idolaters. A star is a promise. It is a soul that has agreed to burn in one place, forever, so that everything beneath it can find its way. The sky-magic was never ours to command. We were only ever its couriers. Its witnesses." He lifted his blind face toward the broken dome. "When the kings murdered the Choir, the stars did not go out because their magic died. They went out because their anchors died. Every star in your grandmother's sky was bound to a living heart. Two hundred hearts, seated in this hall, holding two hundred promises."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. They thought of the mosaic floor, of the hundreds of worn depressions in the stone where benches had once stood in perfect rings. Seats for the anchored. A congregation of the conscripted.

"And the ember," Kestrel said. Her voice was very quiet. "It's the last anchor. The last promise that hasn't been kept or broken."

"It is the last *heart*," Sarn corrected. "And a heart cannot hold itself. Fire needs a hearth. A promise needs a keeper." He turned his head, unerring, until those scarred eyes rested on Kestrel — or on the lantern she carried, which amounted to the same thing. "You have kept it alive for a year, child, and it has cost you. Don't lie to an old blind man; I can hear it eating at you every time you sleep. But you are not what it needs. You are only its cradle. To rekindle the stars — all of them, the whole wheel of heaven — the ember must be given to a storm-caller. One who calls lightning willingly. Lightning is the sky's own blood; it is the only thing that can carry a promise back up to heaven. And the caller must stand at the spire's crown and let the flame take root in them. Permanently."

The word landed in the hall like a dropped stone.

"Permanently," Wren repeated.

"The binding does not release," Sarn said simply. "Not in death, even. The anchor becomes part of the spire, and the spire becomes part of the sky. You would feel every wind that touches this mountain. You would hold two hundred burning promises in your chest until the world ends or the stars are murdered again. You could descend, briefly — the bond allows a leash of some few miles before it begins to drag you home like a hooked fish. But home you would always be. This hall. This cold. This ruin." He smiled, and it was terrible and gentle at once. "We did not call ourselves keepers because we kept books."

* * *

Afterward, Wren stood alone at the base of the spire stair and looked up into the dark where it spiraled away beyond sight.

They had known. That was the thing they could not stop circling, the thought that returned like a tongue to a broken tooth. Standing in the hall, listening to Sarn, they had known before he finished saying it — had known somewhere below thought, in the place where muscle memory lived, the same place that had known how to shape a storm before Wren was old enough to name it. Some part of them had been climbing toward this answer since the night the stars went out. Perhaps since the court martial. Perhaps since the day they were seven years old and their grandmother pointed at the winter sky and said, *every light up there is someone who stayed.*

Their hands ached inside their gloves. They took the gloves off — a habit now, a penance, done when no one was watching — and looked at the scars that ladder-crossed both palms. Lightning-written. The mark of a caller who had refused to call. The tribunal had called it cowardice. Halvard had called it treason. Wren had spent six years calling it survival, and none of the three names had ever fit.

What it had actually been, they understood now, standing in the dark under the broken dome, was a refusal to spend themselves cheaply. A storm poured onto refugee columns was a storm wasted — a promise burned for nothing, a heart spent on fear. And this —

This was the opposite. This was the same choice, inverted, made whole.

"Wren."

Kestrel's voice behind them. Wren pulled the gloves back on too fast, and knew she'd seen, and hated that she'd seen, and hated more that it mattered.

She came to stand beside them at the foot of the stair. Up close, in the ember-light leaking from the lantern, her face was drawn and gray with exhaustion, the wound in her side pulling at her every time she breathed wrong. She had climbed a forbidden pass on that wound. She had bled for this lantern across half a kingdom. She was, Wren thought with a clarity that felt almost like grief, the bravest person they had ever met, and she was about to be robbed.

"Sarn is sleeping," she said. "Or pretending to. He says the rite must happen at moonrise tomorrow, while the storm front is overhead. He says—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He says it has to be a storm-caller. And Wren, there are only two of us on this mountain, and only one of us can call lightning."

"I know."

"So we have to talk about—"

"There's nothing to talk about." Wren heard their own voice go flat and reasonable, the voice they'd used at the tribunal, the smuggler's voice, the voice that closed doors gently so no one heard them close. "You're the courier. Your whole year, your whole order's last year, was about getting the ember here intact. It arrived intact. The rest is mine to do."

"That is not—" Kestrel's composure cracked, and beneath it was something raw and young. "You don't get to decide that alone. You don't get to make it tidy. This isn't a smuggling run, Wren, this is your *life*. Forever. Did you hear him? You'd be chained to this spire until you die, and maybe after. You'd never see the lowlands again. You'd never—"

"I was exiled," Wren said. "Did you forget? Everything you just listed, I already lost. Six years ago. The crown took the lowlands, the rank, the name, the family — took it all and left me the passes and my own two scarred hands." They flexed those hands inside the gloves, feeling the old ache answer. "Halvard is coming. You know it and I know it. When he comes, he comes for the ember, and the ember is in your arms, and you are wounded and exhausted and the bravest person I know, but you cannot fight an Inquisitor's column. So the question isn't whether someone binds themselves to this spire tomorrow. The question is whether it's the person the ember actually needs, or the person carrying it who'll die trying to run."

Kestrel stared at them. The lantern-light moved in her eyes like weather.

"You're doing it again," she said finally. "The flat voice. The door closing."

"Maybe."

"I hate the flat voice." Her jaw tightened. "I've watched you use it for a hundred miles. When you were hurt at Grayfen and wouldn't say how badly. When Mother Ashe offered you the papers and you smiled with it. When you told me about the massacre and made it sound like a supply ledger." She stepped closer, and the ember flared between them, warm as a held breath. "You keep deciding things alone and then narrating them to me afterward like I'm cargo. Well, I'm not cargo, Wren Dunmore. I'm the last apprentice of the star-priests, and I am telling you that whatever happens at moonrise, we decide it together or not at all."

*Together.* The word went through Wren like a blade finding the gap in armor. They thought of the rope bridge, the moment of storm-calling, the look on her face afterward — terror and wonder braided together. They thought of the cave, the firelight, the fragile frost-bright laughter over the map of dead kings. They thought of the crossroads confession, her shame and theirs laid side by side like two wounds from the same blade.

They thought: *if I ask her, truly ask her, she will argue until the argument kills the hour, and then Halvard will arrive and there will be no moonrise and no rite and no stars, ever, and it will be because I wanted one more conversation.*

They thought: *love is not a negotiation.*

"Kestrel." Wren reached out and took the lantern from her hands. She let it go — surprise opening her fingers before understanding could close them. The iron was warm. It had always been warm, but now it felt like holding a living thing against their palms, like holding a bird that trusted you not to close your fist. Through the gloves, through the scars, Wren felt it settle against them with something horribly like recognition. "Thank you for carrying it this far. Further than anyone. Further than the whole dead Choir could have asked."

"Don't." Her voice broke on the word. "Don't do this. Don't you dare do this the way you do everything else—"

"Someone has to stay," Wren said softly. "Someone has always had to stay. That's the whole secret of the sky, Kestrel. Every light is someone who stayed. And I have spent six years learning exactly one skill worth having: how to refuse to burn for the wrong thing." They lifted the lantern between them, and the ember rose inside its cage like a moth waking. "Let me burn for the right one."

"You don't even know if I—" She stopped herself, white-knuckled, and what she didn't say filled the space between them louder than any words: *you don't know if I love you, you don't know what I'd have chosen, you're stealing the choice from both of us.*

"I know enough," Wren said. "I know that when the stars come back, the first thing everyone in the valley will see is the sky. And I want the second thing they see to be you, standing free at the bottom of this mountain, alive, with the whole rest of your life in front of you. That's the rite. That's the whole rite. Everything else is lightning."

Below them, far off across the black valleys, thunder muttered — the storm front crawling in ahead of schedule, or perhaps exactly on time. The ember flared against Wren's palms, brightening, straining upward like a plant toward a window, and in its light Kestrel's face was wet and furious and undone.

"Wren—"

"Keep him talking when Halvard comes," Wren said. "You're good at that. You talked me out of a dozen bad decisions on the pass; you can certainly stall one Inquisitor." They were already turning toward the stair, already climbing, the lantern heavy and eager in their grip, and the spire rose above them into the dark like a held-out hand. "Moonrise is in four hours. I intend to be at the top when it comes."

Behind them, Kestrel shouted their name — once, twice, a third time that cracked in the middle — and then came running after, boots ringing on ancient stone, and Wren climbed faster, because if she caught them, if she touched them, they were not at all certain they would have the strength to let go.

Above, through the broken crown of the tower, the first flakes of the coming storm began to fall — and for the first time in six years, Wren Dunmore reached out with the other sense, the buried one, and felt the storm reach back.
