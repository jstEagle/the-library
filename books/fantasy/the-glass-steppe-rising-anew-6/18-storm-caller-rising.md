## 18. Storm-Caller Rising

The wind at the top of the world did not howl. It waited.

Wren felt it the moment their boots left the last solid stair and found the iron rungs of the spire's outer ladder — a stillness so complete it pressed against their eardrums like deep water. Below, Halvard's soldiers were pouring through the observatory gates, torches blooming in the dark like a crop of poisonous flowers. Below, Kestrel was standing in the courtyard with her back straight and her chin lifted, buying them time with nothing but her voice and her nerve. Below, Mother Ashe knelt in chains between two spearmen, and she had looked up — of course she had looked up — and mouthed something Wren could not hear but understood anyway.

*Up, fool. Climb.*

So Wren climbed.

The rungs were cold enough to burn through their gloves. The stone of the spire was slick with old frost, and somewhere far beneath them the mountain groaned, settling its ancient weight, as if the whole of Thornfell were shifting in its sleep to watch what happened next. Wren counted rungs because counting kept the fear in a box: *one, two, three*, breath on four, *five, six, seven*, breath on eight. Their wounded side pulled with every reach, the blade-scar from Grayfen tearing open again, warm blood soaking into the bandages Kestrel had tied that morning with hands that shook only a little.

*Don't think about her down there. Don't think about the arrows.*

They thought about the arrows anyway.

The last time Wren had stood before soldiers in formation, they had been twenty-two years old and wearing the silver sash of the Crown Storm-Company, and General Vess had pointed at a column of refugees on the Salt Road and said, *Call it down.* Nine hundred people. Farmers and grandmothers and children carrying their whole lives in bundles on their backs. And Wren had stood there with the storm coiling in their chest like a hound straining at its leash, feeling every lightning-bolt already named and numbered, and they had said no.

That no had cost them everything. Their rank, their name, their country. Seven years of salt-smuggling and cold camps and learning to flinch whenever thunder rolled, because thunder remembered them, thunder always came looking for them, and they could not bear to be found.

Now they climbed toward the storm on purpose.

The rungs ended. Wren's hand found the lip of the spire's crown, and they hauled themselves up over the edge and onto the platform where, three hundred years ago, star-priests had stood to read the turning of the heavens. The dome above was shattered — half of it had fallen inward decades ago, leaving a jagged crown of glass and iron open to the sky. The sky that was empty. The sky that had been empty for seven years, black as a closed eye, and tonight it seemed to lean down close, curious, waiting to see if anything would ever fill it again.

In the center of the platform, set into a cradle of tarnished bronze, sat the ember.

Kestrel had carried it up here an hour ago, before Halvard arrived, before the gates broke. She had placed it with the tenderness of someone laying a child in a cradle, and then she had kissed Wren — hard, fierce, tasting of blood and iron lantern-oil — and said, "If this doesn't work, I want the last thing I remember to be your face, not his."

Then she'd gone down to stall him. Alone.

Wren crossed the platform on their knees, too dizzy to trust their legs, and knelt beside the ember. Up close it was smaller than memory: a knot of light no bigger than a fist, gold-white at its heart, pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse. But the air around it bent. The frost on the bronze cradle had melted into a ring of clear water, and tiny motes of dust drifted upward around it, falling *up*, as though the ember were a small stubborn piece of sky refusing to accept which way was down.

"Hello," Wren whispered. Their voice cracked on the second syllable. They hadn't spoken aloud since the gates. "I don't know if you can hear me. Kestrel says you're alive. She says you remember everything the priests ever fed you — every prayer, every song." They swallowed. Their scarred hands hovered over the light without touching it. "I'm not a priest. I'm not anything anymore. I'm just a smuggler who used to call storms, and I'm asking — I'm *begging* — take what I have. Take all of it."

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. Like something listening.

Below, a horn sounded — Halvard's signal horn, three long notes, the same three notes that had ordered the massacre on the Salt Road. Wren's whole body went rigid with old terror, and then, deliberately, breath by breath, they let the rigidity melt. Not into calm. Into something sharper.

Into purpose.

They pulled off their gloves.

Their hands were ruined — everyone said so, though never to their face. The storm-calling had scarred them from palm to wrist in branching white lines, Lichtenberg figures, the trees lightning leaves behind when it passes through flesh. Inquisitors had once measured those scars to prove what Wren was. Now Wren spread those hands over the ember, palms down, fingers spread wide, and for the first time in seven years did not hide them.

"I was told once," they said to the light, to the dark sky, to Kestrel below and Halvard below and every soldier with a drawn bow below, "that my gift was a weapon. That storms exist to break things. That the only question worth asking is *what shall we point it at*." A laugh escaped them, thin and wild. "I refused to point it. So they took my name. Fine. Take this instead."

They reached inward, to the place they had kept locked for seven years.

It opened easier than they feared and harder than they hoped. The lock was rusted; the door came off its hinges screaming. And the storm came out.

It did not arrive from outside. That was the thing no one understood about storm-calling, the thing the Company had never grasped even as they chained Wren to their war-machines: the storm was not summoned, it was *released*. It lived in the caller always, a second heartbeat made of pressure and ozone, and when Wren finally let theirs loose, the world answered from every direction at once.

Clouds boiled over the peaks. Wind slammed the platform hard enough to stagger them upright. Somewhere below, torches guttered and men shouted, and the great broken dome of the observatory rang like a struck bell as the first drops of rain hit it — fat, electric, humming drops that steamed where they touched the bronze.

Wren stood at the crown of the spire with their arms out and their head thrown back, and the storm recognized them.

*There you are,* it said, in the language of pressure and charge, in the language they had been born speaking and had sworn off speaking forever. *Seven years. Did you think we'd forgotten?*

"No," Wren said aloud, laughing now, rain streaming down their face, blood streaming down their side, both of it irrelevant. "I hoped you would. I'm not here for you. I'm here as your road."

Because that was the difference. That was the whole of it, the choice within the choice. On the Salt Road, General Vess had wanted Wren to be a fist. Tonight Wren offered to be a bridge. Lightning wanted, above all things, to *travel* — from cloud to ground, from high to low, from potential to spent. All Wren had to do was stand between the sky and the ember and refuse to flinch.

Above them, the clouds split with a sound like the world tearing along a seam.

The first bolt came down and Wren caught it.

Not with their hands — with everything. With the branching scars that lit up white-hot, with the marrow of their spine, with the seven years of exile and the nine hundred dead they had refused to add to and the one woman below them whose name they had not yet dared to say in the plain words it deserved. The lightning poured through them and they shaped it as it passed, not blunting it but *aiming* it, funneling the sky's fury down through their own body into the cradle of bronze, into the knot of living light.

The ember drank.

It flared so bright that the whole mountaintop threw shadows — Wren's shadow huge and wing-shaped across the clouds below, the spire's shadow like a spear thrust at the heart of the darkness overhead. The pulsing quickened. The motes of dust fell faster, spiraling upward in a golden helix, and the frost on the shattered dome began to sing, a high crystalline tone that Wren felt in their teeth.

*More,* the ember said. Or the sky said. Or Wren's own buried self said, from some place deeper than either. *More.*

"Take it," Wren gasped. "All of it. Everything I—"

An arrow took them in the shoulder.

The impact spun them half around, tore their grip on the storm, and for one horrible instant the lightning *scattered* — wild arcs leaping from their fingertips, gouging smoking lines across the platform, and Wren felt the ember's hunger turn feral, unguided, dangerous. They dropped to one knee, clamped their good hand over the arrow's shaft, and *pulled*. The barbed head came free with a sound they would hear in dreams for the rest of their life, however long that turned out to be.

They looked down.

Halvard stood at the edge of the courtyard, rain plastering his grey hair flat, and he was not shouting orders. He was simply watching, face tilted up, and beside him a line of archers drew and loosed in perfect unison, and Wren watched the arrows rise —

— and the storm caught them.

Every shaft. Mid-flight, forty feet below the platform, the wind closed around the arrows like a patient hand and held them there, quivering, suspended in the rain, pointing uselessly at nothing. Wren hadn't commanded it. Hadn't even thought it. The storm was inside them now, and they were inside the storm, and the distinction between *my will* and *the weather* had dissolved the way salt dissolves in seawater.

*You are not a weapon,* the storm said, in the voice of every thunderhead Wren had ever called and every one they'd refused to call. *You never were. You are the weather itself. Weather does not aim. Weather simply IS, and small men with bows may do nothing about it.*

The second volley rose and hung beside the first. The third joined it. Soon a slow orbiting halo of arrows circled the spire's crown, dozens of them, turning gently in the wind like the ribs of some enormous dying bird, and below, Wren saw the archers lower their bows and stare, and saw Halvard's face change — not to fear, not yet, but to the terrible expression of a man watching his worldview develop a crack straight down the middle.

Good, Wren thought. Let it crack. Let it shatter. Let every lie he built his certainty on come down around his ears like the dome above me.

Lightning struck again. And again. Wren stopped counting. Each bolt came down the road their body made for it, and each one went into the ember, and the ember grew — fist-sized, then head-sized, then the size of a bonfire, though it burned without heat, without smoke, without any of fire's cruelty. It burned the way stars burn: patiently, enormously, with the light of things decided long ago.

And the sky above the observatory began, very faintly, to change.

It started as a single point of white directly overhead, where the hole in the dome framed the zenith. A pinprick. A mistake of the eye. Then another, off to the east. Then a scatter of them, tentative, like sparks from a flint — and Wren, kneeling in the rain with an arrow-wound in their shoulder and lightning walking down their spine, felt tears cut hot channels through the water on their face.

The stars were coming back.

Not all at once. The ember was strong but the dark was seven years deep, and every star had to be rekindled from the single flame Kestrel had carried out of a burning temple in her arms like the last child of a slaughtered house. Wren poured the storm into that flame and the flame climbed, and climbed, and somewhere below Kestrel must have seen it beginning, because a sound rose from the courtyard — her voice, raw and triumphant, crying out words in the old priest-tongue that Wren didn't know but understood perfectly anyway:

*Light the lamps. Light the lamps. Light every lamp in heaven.*

The storm was spending itself now, Wren could feel it, the vast pressure in their chest draining with each bolt like a lake through a broken dam. Their arms shook. Their vision swam. The wound in their shoulder had soaked half their shirt red, and the older wound, the one from Grayfen, had soaked the rest. It didn't matter. Nothing mattered except staying open, staying a road, staying a bridge for as long as the sky needed one.

*One more,* the storm whispered. *One more and the work is done.*

Wren gathered it — the last of it, the deepest vein, the part of the storm that had been theirs since childhood, since the night ten-year-old Wren Dunmore had cried at their first thunderstorm and their grandmother had laughed and said, *child, that isn't the sky frightening you, that's the sky saying hello.* They gathered all of it, every memory-laden volt, and lifted their face to the torn-open heavens, and called it down.

The bolt that fell was not white. It was every color at once, or none — a column of pure dawn splitting the night, and where it met the ember, the ember *bloomed*.

Light flooded outward in a silent ring, rolling down the mountainside, across the valley, over the passes and the salt roads and the refugee columns and the villages and the capital itself, and everywhere it passed, stars ignited. Hundreds. Thousands. Whole constellations waking as if from a seven-year sleep, the Old Hunter drawing his bow again above Thornfell, the Salt Road's guiding chain of lights re-strung across the dark, and at the center of the sky the great constellation of the Priests Eternal blazing brighter than all the rest, so that no one in the kingdom could look up tonight and fail to see the faces of the murdered staring down.

Wren slumped forward against the bronze cradle, spent, hollow, laughing.

The ember settled into its new shape — no longer a coal but a star in miniature, steady and eternal, anchored now to the spire, and Wren felt the anchor catch in their own chest like a fishhook of light. A binding. Permanent. They had known it would be. Kestrel had warned them, and they had volunteered anyway, and the price seemed, in this moment of drowning light and returning stars, almost laughably small.

Below, the courtyard had gone silent. Even Halvard had stopped moving. Every soldier, every hunter, every chained prisoner stood looking up at a sky that testified against everything they'd been told — and Wren, bleeding and bound and more exiled than any decree could make them, heard boots on the spire stairs, fast and desperate and unmistakable.

Kestrel. Climbing toward them through the rain, under the new-made stars, screaming their name.

* * *

The door at the top of the stairs burst open, and Kestrel stood silhouetted against the light of a sky full of stars — and behind her, far below, Wren saw Halvard raise his hand for one final volley.
