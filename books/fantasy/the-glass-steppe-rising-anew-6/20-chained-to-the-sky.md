## 20. Chained to the Sky

The first time Wren tried to leave, Kestrel watched from the observatory steps and counted.

They had made it to the third milestone marker on the Grayfen road — she could still see them, a small figure in a patched smuggler's coat, walking with that deliberate, unhurried stride they used when they wanted the world to believe they were unbothered. Then Wren stopped. Stood very still, the way people do when something inside them has gone wrong but they haven't yet admitted it aloud. And turned around.

Kestrel counted four hundred and eleven steps before Wren reached her again, and by the last hundred their gait had broken — not running, nothing so dignified as running, but a stumbling half-jog like a man wading against a river no one else could see.

"It's fine," Wren said, when they reached her. Their face was gray. "I just forgot something."

"You own six things, and I can name all of them."

Wren's jaw tightened. Above them the stars burned — *burned*, after twenty years of darkness, and Kestrel still caught herself looking up at night with the breathless disbelief of a child — but Wren did not look up. They never looked up anymore if they could help it.

"The second try," Kestrel said quietly. "You made it past the river."

"I know how far I made it." Wren sat down heavily on the lowest step, elbows on knees, and pressed the heels of their scarred hands against their eyes. The bond ran through them like a rope through a ring; Kestrel couldn't see it, but she had learned to feel it anyway, the way you learn to feel weather coming in your teeth. When Wren walked away from the spire, the air around them thickened. Light bent toward the mountain. And Wren — stubborn, proud, infuriating Wren Dunmore — began, slowly, to come apart.

"How far?" Kestrel asked.

"A day's walk. Maybe two if I fight it." A bitter almost-laugh. "Fighting it feels like drowning. I don't recommend it."

Kestrel sat down beside them on the cold stone. Below, the Thornfell valley was waking into its first true spring in a generation — the crown's grip loosening, the border wards rebuilt not by royal masons but by villagers and smugglers and ex-storm-callers working from Wren's instructions carried on the wind. There should have been triumph in all of it. There was, some days. But there was also this: the man who had bought back the sky could not walk three miles from the place where he'd paid for it.

"Say it," Kestrel said.

"Don't want to."

"Say it anyway."

Wren lowered their hands. Looked at her with an expression she had come to know better than her own heartbeat — defiance fraying at the edges into something rawer. "If I leave," they said, "the binding unravels. Slowly, maybe. Over months. And the stars go out again." A pause. "And I die. Not slowly."

The words fell between them like stones into still water.

"So that's the arithmetic," Kestrel said. Her voice came out flat, which was the only way she could get it out at all. "One storm-caller for ten thousand stars. Forever."

"That was always the price. I read the same inscription you did." Wren's mouth twisted. "I just volunteered before anyone could tell me the fine print."

*Before I could object.* She heard it anyway, unsaid. She had been asleep in the keeper's ruined library when Wren climbed the spire. She had slept through the single most important moment of both their lives because she'd trusted them to wait, and Wren had known exactly what she would say, and so had chosen not to ask.

She had forgiven them. Mostly. On good days.

On bad days she wanted to shake them until their teeth rattled, and then hold them until the shaking stopped, and she had never once managed to do either in the right order.

* * *

The Observatory rebuilt itself the way wounds close — unevenly, painfully, and not quite the way it had been before.

In the first weeks, while the kingdom below reeled under what its people had seen written across the heavens — the ember's light had shown them everything, every lie, every murdered priest, and King Aldric's court had begun to crack like river ice in thaw — workers came up the mountain. Masons first, then carpenters, then a whole ragged procession of volunteers who arrived with tools and timber and the particular ferocity of people who needed to build something after watching everything they believed burn.

Wren directed them from the spire's base, voice carrying on wind they no longer bothered to disguise as ordinary. Kestrel organized the supply lines down through Mother Ashe's network — Ashe herself had survived Halvard's custody, freed in the chaos after the rekindling, and had appeared at the Observatory gates two weeks later with a wagonload of glass and a grudge against stairs — and somewhere in those weeks, without deciding to, Kestrel stopped thinking of the Observatory as a destination.

She started thinking of it as home.

It happened so gradually she didn't notice until Mother Ashe pointed it out, over tea brewed on a brazier in the half-finished east hall, surrounded by crates of star-glass waiting to be set into the great dome.

"You've got a room," Ashe said. It wasn't a question. The old woman's eyes were sharp as awls and twice as likely to draw blood. "Third door past the library. Got your books stacked in it. Your coat on a peg. Been there two months."

"I'm overseeing the restoration."

"Mm." Ashe sipped her tea. "And the restoration needs overseeing from a room with your name scratched into the doorframe?"

Kestrel looked down and saw it — her own handwriting, *K.*, carved small and careless into the wood, done weeks ago without thought, the way a child marks territory. Heat crawled up her neck.

"Ashe—"

"I'm not saying anything." The old woman raised both hands, the picture of innocence. "Just observing that the last apprentice of the star-priests, heir to the entire tradition of sky-magic, has taken up residence in a ruin on top of a mountain to watch over a man who can't walk away from her." She set down her cup. "Your teachers would be proud, girl. Or furious. Hard to say which."

"They'd say the work comes first."

"And is that what you tell yourself? Because from where I sit, the work is rebuilding itself just fine, and you're spending most of your hours sitting with a stubborn exile on the spire steps, teaching him to look up again."

Kestrel said nothing, because there was nothing honest to say that wasn't what Ashe had already said.

That night she stood in her room — *her* room — and unpacked the last crate she had been keeping sealed since the capital. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the things she'd carried out of the purge: her master's astrolabe, dented and dark. A bundle of prayer-ribbons, silver thread gone gray. And the iron lantern, empty now, humming faintly even in its emptiness, remembering the light it had once held.

She hung it by the window, where it caught the starlight, and understood that she had been unpacking this room in her heart long before she touched the crates.

* * *

Winter came early to Thornfell, as it always had, but now the snow fell beneath a sky full of witnesses.

Kestrel learned the Observatory's rhythms the way she'd once learned scripture: by repetition, by devotion, by the slow accumulation of habit into meaning. Mornings she worked in the library, cataloguing what the mad old keeper — dead now, buried with more gentleness than he'd shown anyone living — had left behind in his decades of solitude. Afternoons she trained the first students.

That was the part that undid her, some days. They came up the mountain in ones and twos at first — a shepherd's daughter who could make frost patterns bloom on glass, a ferryman's son whose moods changed the weather over the lake, a pair of twins from the lowlands who finished each other's sentences and each other's charms. Children of scattered bloodlines, descendants of priests who had hidden their gifts for generations, drawn by rumor and by the simple undeniable fact that the stars were back and someone had to teach the world what that meant.

Kestrel taught them what she remembered. It was less than she wished. She had been an apprentice for only three years before the purge; much of the tradition had died with her masters, and what remained lived in her memory like light through fog. But she taught them the grounding meditations, the ethics of power that the crown had spent decades perverting — *the sky belongs to no one; we are its servants, never its masters* — and she taught them the histories, the true ones, including the massacre at Redford Ford and the storm-caller who refused it.

She did not mention, in those lessons, that she shared a roof with him.

Wren, meanwhile, had become something between a landmark and a legend.

They could leave the spire now — briefly. The binding had settled into a rhythm they'd mapped together through grim trial and error: Wren could walk down as far as the shrine at the mountain's shoulder, spend perhaps an hour there, and return before the pull became agony. On clear days they made the trip deliberately, standing at the shrine among the offerings villagers left — bread, salt, ribbons, once an entire goat, which had been awkward — letting people see that the Storm-Caller was flesh and blood and bad-tempered before noon.

But they always came back. And every time, Kestrel watched them make the choice anew — watched them turn at the boundary of their freedom and climb back up without being summoned — and every time something in her chest pulled tight as wire.

Tonight she found Wren in the dome chamber, standing beneath the incomplete skeleton of the new lens-frame, looking up through the gap where glass had yet to be set. Snow ticked softly against the copper ribs. The stars wheeled beyond, indifferent and magnificent.

"You're staring at Orion's belt," she said. "Leftmost star. That's the one you flinched at during the rekindling. You've never told me why."

Wren didn't startle; they'd known she was there. They always knew. "When the lightning went through me," they said, "there was a moment. Right at the center of it. Everything went quiet, and I saw—" They paused, hunting for the shape of it. "I saw the stars the way they must see us. From outside. And I felt like a knot in a rope. Like the whole sky was holding me the way I'm holding it now." A breath. "It wasn't terrible, Kess. That's the strange thing. Being bound. In that moment it felt like belonging."

"And now?"

"Now it feels like a cage with excellent views."

Kestrel laughed — short and surprised — and crossed the cold floor to stand beside them. Shoulder to shoulder, they looked up together through the gap in the dome. Her breath fogged. His didn't seem to anymore; the bond kept Wren warm the way a hearth keeps a room warm, evenly, ceaselessly, whether or not anyone wanted it.

"I've been thinking," she said.

"Dangerous."

"Hush." She gathered the words she'd been hoarding for weeks. "The students need a senior teacher. Someone who knew the tradition whole, not fragments. I'm not that person. But the binding—" She made herself say it plainly, because he deserved plainness. "The binding anchors through you, but it draws on both of us now. You know that. The ember passed through my hands; the spire knows my signature as well as yours. If we anchored the ritual formally — renewed it, together, with intent instead of accident — I might be able to extend your range. A day's walk becomes three. Maybe five. Enough to visit the valley. Enough to—" *Enough to see the sea, someday. Enough to attend my students' weddings in the lowlands. Enough for a life instead of a sentence.*

Wren was silent for a long moment. Snow ticked. Somewhere below, a student laughed.

"You'd bind yourself tighter," they said finally. "To this mountain. To me."

"I've already bound myself to both. I just haven't signed the paperwork." She turned to face them. "Wren. I could leave. Ashe offered me a position in Grayfen, a real one, teaching. I could go down and live a whole life and come up to visit." She let that stand in the cold air between them, honestly offered, honestly meant. "I'm telling you I choose not to. Not because of duty. Not because the stars need me. Because I sat on these steps with you in autumn and watched you count four hundred steps back to me, and I realized I would rather live in a beautiful cage with you than a free house without you. That's the whole speech. I've been rehearsing it for a month and that's the best version."

Wren looked at her. In the starlight their eyes held the faint residual glow of the lightning that had remade them — barely visible unless you knew to look, and Kestrel always knew to look.

"You rehearsed a speech," they said, voice rough, "for a month."

"Every word. Ashe critiqued drafts."

A laugh broke out of them, startled and real, echoing off the copper ribs — and then Wren took her hand, scarred palm to scarred palm, and pressed their forehead to hers, and the speech she'd rehearsed dissolved entirely because the answer had never needed words.

Above them, through the gap in the unfinished dome, Orion wheeled slowly westward, and the leftmost star of his belt burned steady and bright, and neither of them flinched from it at all.

* * *

It was nearly dawn when the lantern woke.

Kestrel came out of sleep knowing it before she understood it — the iron lantern hanging by her window had begun, for the first time since the rekindling, to hum. Not its old faint remembering-hum but something structured. Deliberate. A note held and repeated, patient as a knock at a door.

She lit a candle and unwrapped the lantern from its winter cloth. Inside the empty iron cage, where no flame had burned in twenty years, a point of light was gathering. Small. Silver-white. Pulsing in rhythm with the hum — and the rhythm, when she stilled herself enough to listen, resolved into something horribly familiar.

Not random. Not resonance. A pattern.

Three long pulses. Three short. Three long.

Her blood went cold. It was the old distress call of the star-priests, the signal every apprentice learned first, the call that meant *the sanctuaries are breached, run, carry the light, do not stop.*

But the sanctuaries were ash. The priests were dead. She was the last, and she was here, and the lantern was sounding an alarm twenty years too late — or sounding it for something else entirely.

Down the hall, she heard Wren's door open. Footsteps. Then his voice, tight with a fear she had never heard from him even on the night the hunters came:

"Kestrel. Get dressed. Every star in the western sky just flickered — all at once, like something breathed on them." A pause, and then, quieter, worse: "And there's someone climbing the mountain road. Alone. On foot. In this cold." Another pause.

"She's carrying a staff with seven rings on it. Kestrel — I think it's a priest's staff."
