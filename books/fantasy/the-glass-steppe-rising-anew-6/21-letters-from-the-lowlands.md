## 21. Letters from the Lowlands

The wind woke him before the sun did.

It came up the mountain in the old way, the familiar way, threading through the observatory's broken ribs and finding him where he slept — or half-slept; the bond did not permit true sleep anymore, only long shallow drifts that ended whenever the sky needed tending. Wren opened their eyes to the pale gray of pre-dawn and felt the pull at the base of their spine, gentle as a hand on a shoulder. *Come back. Come home.* Home was forty feet above, at the crown of the spire, where the ember burned in its cradle of star-glass and Wren's name was written into it in lightning.

They lay still for a moment anyway, listening to the wind sort itself through the shattered dome. In the first weeks after the binding, they had fought it. They had walked down as far as the treeline with Kestrel walking beside them, silent, both of them pretending this was an ordinary stroll, and then the pull had come like a hook behind the sternum and turned their legs around without asking permission. They had made it three miles once. Three miles and back, and Kestrel had held them while they shook, and neither of them had spoken about it afterward.

Now, eight months on, they had learned the shape of the leash. It was not cruelty. That was the strange mercy of it — the ember did not drag, it *invited*, and the invitation grew louder the longer one ignored it, until ignoring it felt like holding one's breath underwater. But within the radius of the mountain — the passes, the valley, the high meadows where the goats of neighboring farms wandered stupidly into the snow — Wren could move freely. Could live, even.

Could teach.

Wren rose, dressed in layers against the thin cold, and climbed the interior stair of the spire, past the keeper's mad carvings (they had buried old Serevan in the spring, beneath the east wall, with a stone that read only *HE KEPT THE LIGHT*), up to the crown where the morning waited.

The stars were going down. Not out — never out again — but dimming into the blue wash of dawn, and as always Wren stopped at the top step to watch them go. Eight months, and they still could not look away. Every night the sky filled, wheeling and burning, and every morning it surrendered gracefully, and every time Wren thought: *I did that. We did that.* And every time the thought landed differently than pride. It landed like weight. Like a stone set carefully on a shelf that would have to be carried forever.

The ember pulsed at the spire's heart, slow and steady, a heartbeat the whole kingdom could feel if they knew how to listen. Wren pressed their scarred palm flat against the star-glass cradle and let the morning check-in happen — the wordless exchange, the ember taking the measure of its anchor, Wren taking the measure of themselves. Stable. Steady. No fractures in the binding, no storms building unseasonably in the upper air that needed coaxing.

Below, the valley was waking. Smoke from chimneys. The road out of Grayfen already showing cart traffic, small as beetles from this height. And along the road, moving east toward the lowlands, a line of travelers — Wren counted them out of habit — perhaps thirty, maybe forty, walking in the open. Unhidden. Under the restored stars, refugees no longer traveled by night in terrified clumps, paying smugglers' tolls to avoid the ward-stations. The wards were dead everywhere now; the truth of what the crown had done to the star-priests had spread faster than any army could march, and the border garrisons had melted away like frost in sun. People moved in daylight now. People looked *up* now, all across the Thornfell and beyond, and what they saw was proof.

Wren watched the traveler-line dwindle around a bend and went down to make tea, because even anchors got thirsty, and because Kestrel would be awake by now in the little room she'd claimed on the observatory's second level — the one with the intact roof and the window facing east.

She was indeed awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor amid a chaos of paper, her braid coming loose, copying something by lamplight with the fierce concentration she brought to everything. She'd turned the room into an archive over the winter: recovered fragments from the capital's purge, testimonies taken from survivors, copies of the ledgers Halvard himself had kept — the man's meticulous records had convicted his own masters more thoroughly than any tribunal could have. Kestrel was writing the history no one else would write. She said someone had to, before the crowns — there were rumors of two crowns now, the old king's daughter raising her banner in the south — rewrote it all as weather.

"Letter day," she said without looking up. "Ashe's runner came in before dawn. There's a stack."

"How big?"

"Big." She held up a sheaf nearly two fingers thick, tied with the blue cord Mother Ashe used for anything that wasn't smuggling contraband. "And there's one with a Thornfell seal. A real one. From the provisional council."

Wren took the tea she poured and didn't take the letter. "Later."

"You say that every time."

"And I mean it every time." They sat down beside her amid the papers, shoulder against hers, and blew on the tea. "Tell me the good ones first."

Kestrel smiled and pulled a smaller bundle from the stack. "Marta Vey writes again. From Fenharrow."

"The girl who heard the storm coming before anyone else did."

"The same. Her mother says she's been standing on the sea wall talking to clouds, and the clouds—" Kestrel squinted at the cramped handwriting, "—*answer*. Wren, she's nine."

"They're starting younger than I did, then." Something warm and frightened moved together in Wren's chest. "What does she want?"

"To talk to you. Her mother asks — very politely, twice apologizing for asking — whether the Storm-Caller of the Spire would speak with her daughter."

*The Storm-Caller of the Spire.* Wren still hadn't gotten used to the name. For years their name had been a thing whispered in smugglers' camps, a cautionary tale: *the caller who refused, who vanished into the Thornfell, don't ask questions.* Now children asked after them by title. Now there were songs — Kestrel had collected three versions, each more embellished than the last, one of which had Wren single-handedly defeating an army of shadow-soldiers that existed nowhere outside the songwriter's imagination.

"Tonight," Wren said. "When the air is clean. Tell Ashe's runner we'll send word down."

"I'll write it." Kestrel was already pulling fresh paper close. Then she paused, pen lifted, and gave Wren the look — the sideways, careful look she'd been giving them more often these last few weeks. "There's also one from the council. And one from the southern camp. And—"

"Kess."

"One from the king's daughter," she finished quietly.

The tea went cold-tasting in Wren's mouth. They set the cup down. "Read the others first."

* * *

Letters had become the rhythm of Wren's days, the way patrols had been the rhythm of their soldiering life and runs had been the rhythm of exile. Mother Ashe's network, which had once moved salt and stolen documents and fugitives through the mountains, had become something no one had planned: a postal service of the dispossessed. Ashe herself had survived Halvard's capture — freed in the chaos when the truth broke over the kingdom like a wave — and had returned to Grayfen with a limp, a grudge, and an expanded operation. Half her runners now carried nothing but paper. Letters came up the mountain in bundles: from refugees resettled in the river valleys, from villages rebuilding their shrines to the old sky-priests, from mothers asking after missing sons, from strangers who simply wanted to tell the person on the spire what the stars looked like over *their* rooftops.

Wren answered what they could. Kestrel drafted most of the replies — her hand was quick and her patience infinite — and Wren added lines in their own scrawl where the words mattered most. But some letters couldn't be answered in ink.

Those, Wren answered on the wind.

It had started by accident, in the second month, when Marta Vey's first letter arrived and Wren, reading it aloud to Kestrel on the spire's balcony, had felt the evening air lean in around their words like a listener. Old instinct. Storm-callers learned early that their voices carried strangely — that a whisper pitched right could ride a thermal for miles, arriving soft and unmistakable in a distant ear. It was a minor gift, the least of their training, and they had used it once in the army to pass orders along a front line in fog. They had never imagined using it for anything kind.

But that night, with Marta's letter trembling in their hand, Wren had stood at the balcony rail and spoken her name into the west wind, softly, the way you'd speak to a skittish animal — and somewhere far below, a nine-year-old girl on a sea wall had heard a stranger's voice come out of the clear dusk saying, *I hear you too.*

After that, it became a practice. Two evenings a week, weather permitting, Wren climbed the spire and taught.

The students were scattered and few — a girl in Fenharrow, twin boys in a mining camp who'd been caught in the edge of a storm-wake and come out changed, a shepherd in the western passes whose voice cracked thunder when he shouted at his dogs, a woman in the lowlands who'd hidden her calling for thirty years and wept the first time she let herself use it. Seven, the last count. Seven storm-caller children and adults in a kingdom that had spent decades pretending there were none, because the crown had culled the gift wherever it bloomed, and the ones who survived had learned to bury it deep.

Wren taught them breathing first. Then listening — how to feel a pressure change in your teeth, a charge in the hair on your arms. How to ask a cloud to shed its rain gently instead of all at once. Never how to strike. Wren had decided that in the first week, standing at the rail with the wind waiting, and had never revisited the decision, not even when the twins wrote asking, eagerly, whether the Storm-Caller of the Spire could throw lightning, because their uncle said so, because it was in the song.

*I don't throw lightning,* Wren had told them on the wind, patient as stone. *Lightning comes to me when I'm needed. There's a difference, and someday you'll understand why the difference is everything.*

Tonight, though, there was Marta.

The evening came down clear and cold, the new stars sharp as nailheads, and Wren stood at the balcony with Kestrel nearby mending a cloak by lamplight — she liked to be present for the lessons, listening to Wren's half of conversations that arrived only as pauses. Wren breathed the way they'd been teaching for eight months, found the clean channel of the west wind, and sent their voice down the darkening miles.

*Marta. Can you hear me?*

A pause — the long pause of distance, four breaths, five — and then the wind brought back a sound like a bell breaking: a child's laugh, high and astonished, carried up the whole spine of the Thornfell.

*You came! You really came! Mama, it's him — it's them — I told you—*

*Steady,* Wren said, smiling despite themselves. *Breathe first. Show me what we practiced.*

The lesson lasted an hour. Marta had been working on gathering — drawing stray charge out of the air and holding it harmless in cupped hands, a firefly-ball of potential that tickled and hummed. She reported, breathless, that she'd held it for a hundred heartbeats yesterday. Tonight she held it for a hundred and twenty, and Wren talked her through releasing it safely, feeding it back into the sky instead of letting it snap, and when it went — a soft sigh of light, a smell of rain that never fell — her joy came up the wind so fierce that Kestrel looked up from her mending with tears standing in her eyes.

*You're getting strong,* Wren told her. *Stronger than I was at nine. But strength isn't the lesson, Marta. Say it back to me.*

The girl's voice, solemn now, reciting the catechism Wren had given her months ago and would give until the day the bond finally stilled them: *The sky doesn't belong to us. We belong to the sky. What we carry, we carry for everyone.*

*Good girl. Same time next week.*

And then, because the wind was clean and the night was generous, Wren lingered, and spoke to each student in turn — the twins, the shepherd, the woman in the lowlands whose name was Odile and whose progress was slow and sacred. By the time they finished, the moon had crossed a third of the sky, and their throat ached pleasantly, and Kestrel had finished the cloak and started a second one and finished that too.

"She asked about the council letter," Kestrel said, when Wren came in stiff-necked from the cold. "Marta. Her mother wants to know if it's true the provisional council offered you a pardon."

"It's true." Wren sat heavily, working the ache out of their neck. "They offer one every six weeks. It's becoming a ritual. They enclose the same document with different dates."

"And the southern camp?"

"Same offer, different seal. The king's daughter signs hers personally now." Wren almost laughed. "She writes well, actually. Warm. Reasonable. Last month she said the kingdom owes me a debt it can never repay, which is true, and that the first installment should be my freedom, which is also true, and completely beside the point."

"Because you can't leave."

"Because I can't leave." Wren spread their scarred hands, palms up, the old burn-patterns silver in the lamplight — marks from the court-martial branding, from years of rope work, from the night they'd anchored lightning with bare hands on the spire. The bond hummed faintly under their skin, acknowledging the topic. "They can pardon the exile. They can't pardon the anchor. There's no document that unbinds me from this mountain. Believe me, I've asked the ember directly."

"What did it say?" Kestrel asked. She always asked, and Wren always gave the same honest answer, and somehow it helped them both.

"It said nothing. It just pulled. Gently. The way it always does." Wren looked toward the window, where the stars wheeled in silence. "I think they know, down there. I think that's why they keep sending the letters anyway. So the record shows they tried. So when the histories are written, someone stands between me and the accusation that I *chose* the cage."

"Did you?"

Wren considered the question honestly, the way they considered everything now — slowly, with the ember's patience soaked into their bones. "I chose the spire. The cage came with it." They reached over and touched Kestrel's knee. "And I chose knowing you'd stay. Don't make that face. It's not a burden I put on you. It's just the truth, and we agreed to stop flinching from those."

Kestrel covered their hand with hers. Outside, a nightjar called, and far below in the valley a dog barked at nothing, and the stars kept their steady watch, and for a while neither of them said anything at all, because eight months had taught them the value of silence shared.

Then Kestrel said, "There's one more letter. I saved it."

"Of course you did."

"It's from Halvard."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Wren withdrew their hand slowly. "Halvard is in the capital prisons. awaiting trial. Everyone knows—"

"He was released last month. Pending trial. House arrest in his brother's keep." Kestrel's voice was careful, flat, the voice she used for testimony. "Ashe's runner confirmed it. He's allowed correspondence." She held out the envelope — heavy paper, official watermark, the wax seal of the Inquisition broken cleanly, deliberately, so Wren could see it had been opened and read by someone before traveling. "He wrote to you, Wren. By name. Not by title."

Wren took it and did not open it. The paper seemed colder than the mountain night. Eight months ago, on this balcony's predecessor of broken stone, they had watched this man's conviction crack under the light of returning stars — watched him lower his bow, watched him fall to his knees in the snow with his soldiers staring, watched something break in him that might have been faith shattering or might have been faith *transmuting*, and they had never learned which. His testimony had convicted three chancellors. His testimony had also, people whispered, been suspiciously incomplete about certain orders, certain nights, certain graves.

"He's found religion, they say," Kestrel said quietly. "Some of the prisoners say he prays to the star-priests now. Others say a man like that can't change, only wait." She met Wren's eyes. "You don't have to read it tonight."

"No," Wren said. "But I will tonight. If I sleep on it, it'll grow teeth."

They broke the seal. Inside, in a precise clerk's hand, was a single page.

*Storm-Caller —*

*I will not waste your time with apology. You saw what my apologies were worth on the mountain, and I saw what they were worth to myself, which was less.*

*I write because I have remembered something, and remembering is the only penance I trust myself to perform. Before the purge, before the ember was taken to the capital, the star-priests kept a secondary repository. A reserve flame, smaller, hidden. My orders mentioned it once, in a context I did not understand until your binding showed me what anchoring costs. I believed the repository destroyed. I have reason now to believe it was not. I believe it lies beneath the old shrine at Cael Hollow, in the southern foothills, and I believe it has been burning untended for twenty years.*

*A reserve flame means a second anchor need not be made. It means a binding might be... transferred. Lightened. I cannot verify any of this from house arrest, and I do not ask you to trust me. Send someone you trust. Send no one. Burn this letter. I only could not carry the knowledge into the ground unspoken.*

*The stars are beautiful over the capital. I did not know what we had extinguished until I saw it relit.*

*— H.*

Wren read it twice. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; inside, something was not steady at all. A reserve flame. A transfer. The words rang against the walls they'd built around hope — walls mortared daily, carefully, over eight months of learning to live inside the leash — and the walls held, but they heard the ringing.

"Well?" Kestrel asked.

Wren set the letter down on the table between the lamp and the unfinished archive, where the ember's distant pulse seemed, faintly, to lean toward it.

"Get me a map," Wren said. "And wake the runner. Someone has to get to Cael Hollow before winter closes the passes."

Outside, above the spire, the stars burned on — and for the first time in eight months, Wren Dunmore looked up at them and wondered not what they had cost, but what they might yet give back.
