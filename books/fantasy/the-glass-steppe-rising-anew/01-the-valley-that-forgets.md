## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink was the first thing Ivo Nakamura ever loved, though he had no word for it at seven years old, kneeling on the cold slate floor of the Scriptorium Annex while Elder Tomasen held his small wrist in a grip like a bird trap and taught him how to grind lampblack into linseed oil. *Slowly*, the elder had said. *Ink remembers impatience.* Ivo had thought this a figure of speech then, a thing old men said to children to make them careful. Twenty years later, grinding that same ink every dawn of his debt-bound life, he still did not know that the elder had been speaking with more precision than anyone in the valley suspected.

He knew only that the valley had no magic, that magic was a word for stories told about the world beyond the rim of gray peaks, and that he owed the Temple of the Quiet Ledger eleven more years of service for a debt his father had signed before Ivo could walk.

On the morning everything began — though nothing about that morning announced itself, which was the nature of endings, they arrived dressed as ordinary days — Ivo sat at his desk by the eastern window copying the tithe ledgers of the village of Ashenbrook, and the light came through the glass in long amber bars, and dust drifted through those bars like something patient, and Ivo was, as he was most mornings, quietly miserable in the way of a man who has never once considered that misery might be optional.

"Entry forty-one," he murmured to himself, dipping his pen. "Ashenbrook. Barley, twelve measures. Wool, three fleeces, one blemished."

His handwriting was famous within the temple's walls, or at least remarked upon: narrow, upright letters with a scribe's disciplined evenness, each line ruled so precisely that visiting clerks from the lowland missions sometimes asked whether he used a frame. He did not. He used twenty years of habit, which is stronger than any frame.

The Scriptorium Annex occupied the temple's oldest wing, a low stone building whose roof sagged in the middle like an old man's shoulders. It smelled of vellum and tallow and the particular mineral coldness of stone that has never once been warm. Beyond its single window lay the courtyard, then the cloister wall, then the terraced fields climbing toward the tree line, and beyond all of that, faint in the morning haze, the gray teeth of the Rimward Peaks, which enclosed the valley like two cupped hands — or, if one were of a darker temperament, like a cage.

Ivo had never seen beyond them.

No one in the valley had, not in living memory. The mountain passes were closed — *warded*, the elders said, using the word the way other men might say *plague-stricken* — and the few traders who were permitted through came down the single western road under escort, blindfolded for the final league, their goods inspected, their questions deflected with the gentle immovability of men who have answered the same question ten thousand times. *The world outside is dangerous. The valley is safe. Be grateful.* Ivo had heard the litany so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and like a river stone, he had stopped seeing it.

He dipped his pen again. Entry forty-two.

It was somewhere around entry sixty — the afternoon light gone honey-colored, the annex empty except for himself and Brother Aldous snoring gently over his own stack of correspondence across the room — that Ivo found the loose leaf pressed inside the ledger's back binding.

It was not unusual for old ledgers to carry insertions: notes tucked between pages decades ago and forgotten, receipts, prayers, the occasional dried flower flattened to translucence. This was different. This was a single sheet of heavy paper, older than anything else in the book, and on it was written a glyph.

Or what Ivo took for a glyph. He had spent his life among letters, and letters were his country, but this was not writing in any script he knew — not the round temple hand, not the angular trader-marks of the western road, not even the half-remembered shapes of the Old Tongue that appeared carved above the chapel door where no one could read them anymore. This was a single figure, drawn large, occupying the whole page: interlocking lines like a knot that had been untied halfway and then abandoned, with a hollow space at its center shaped almost — Ivo tilted his head — almost like a star with the light scooped out of it.

Beneath it, in faded brown ink, someone had written four words in a hand so cramped it might have been written running:

*Do not copy. Ever.*

Ivo looked up. Across the room, Brother Aldous slept on, his breath a slow tide. Through the window, the valley went on being the valley: smoke rising straight from chimneys, a dog crossing the courtyard with purpose, the eternal gray mountains standing their watch.

He should have set the page aside for the elders. He knew this the way he knew the tithe schedules, the way he knew the shape of his cell at night. Anything anomalous went to the Elders' Council; anything unexplained went to the Elders' Council; anything that made the skin prickle went to the Elders' Council, and preferably quickly, and preferably without lingering over it.

But here is what must be understood about Ivo Nakamura, at the hour before his life changed: he was a man who had spent twenty-two years being obedient in a place that rewarded obedience with more obedience, and somewhere beneath all that discipline there lived a hunger so old and so carefully buried that he had mistaken it for a feature of his character rather than a wound. He wanted things. He could not have named most of them. He wanted to know what color the sky was at the top of the mountains. He wanted to know if the sea existed, or whether it too was a story. He wanted, on certain bad nights, simply to walk out the west gate and keep walking until someone told him to stop, just to hear what his own refusal would sound like.

And now, on a loose leaf hidden in a ledger's spine, someone had left him a door.

*Do not copy. Ever.*

He copied it.

Later — much later, when the world had ended and begun again several times — Ivo would try to reconstruct that moment honestly, and would find he could not. Had some part of him known? Had the glyph chosen him, or he it? All he could recover was the sensation: the strange gravity of the lines, the way his pen seemed to move before his mind gave permission, the scratch of the nib sounding louder than it should, as though the whole silent annex were holding its breath around him.

He drew it faithfully. Every curve. Every intersection. And when he reached the hollow center — that star-shaped absence — he hesitated, because the ink in his well had gone strange.

Not wrong. Strange. It had always been black; it remained black; but the blackness had acquired depth, the way a well acquires depth when you finally look down into it instead of past it. The surface of the ink in his dish trembled, very slightly, in a room with no draft.

"You're tired," Ivo told himself aloud, softly, because speaking aloud was a comfort against nonsense. "You've been hunched over Ashenbrook's barley since dawn."

He finished the last stroke of the glyph.

For three heartbeats, nothing happened. The annex was quiet. Aldous snored. Dust drifted through amber light. Somewhere out in the courtyard, the dog barked twice at nothing, which dogs do.

Then the ink moved.

It did not smear. Smearing he would have understood; smearing was a scribe's grief, familiar as an old scar. This was movement with intention. The wet lines of the copied glyph crawled — slowly, deliberately, the way a spider reweaves — drawing themselves tighter, tightening the knot, closing that hollow center by a fraction. And in the instant before Ivo's mind caught up with his eyes and began shrieking that this was impossible, that there was no magic in the valley, that there had never been magic in the valley, he felt something answer the glyph's motion from inside his own chest. A pull. A recognition. Like hearing your own name spoken in a crowd by a voice you almost remember.

Ivo stood up so fast his chair went over backward, and the crash of it echoed off stone, and Brother Aldous jerked awake mid-snore with a strangled sound.

"What— boy, what in the Quiet's name—" Aldous pushed himself up, blinking, and followed Ivo's rigid pointing finger to the desk.

To the page. Where the glyph sat finished, dry already, inert, black lines on white paper, doing nothing at all. A drawing. Only a drawing.

Aldous squinted at it, then at Ivo, then at the overturned chair. "What is that?"

"I don't know." His voice came out steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was ringing like a struck bell. "It was pressed in the binding of the Ashenbrook ledger. There's a warning on the original. *Do not copy.*"

Something moved behind Aldous's eyes — quick, and quickly suppressed, but Ivo had spent twenty years learning to read faces the way he read text, and he saw it plainly: fear. Not confusion. Fear.

"Give it to me," Aldous said. "Both pages. Now."

"Aldous—"

"*Now*, Ivo." And then, gentler, in the tone the elders used for handling the young, the tone that was itself a kind of wall: "There are things you don't need to understand. That's not a cruelty. It's a kindness. The valley is safe because we keep it simple. Give me the pages, sweep up your chair, and go get your supper. Tomorrow you'll be on the Kellin farm accounts, and this will be a strange story you tell yourself at night until you forget it."

Ivo handed over the pages. What else could he do? He watched Aldous fold them away into his sleeve with hands that were not quite steady, and he righted his chair, and he walked out through the cloister into the evening as instructed, and got his supper as instructed, and ate it as instructed, tasteless as ash.

*The valley is safe because we keep it simple.*

He stood in the little walled garden behind the novices' cells after dark, looking up at the sky, and the stars were out in their usual modest numbers — the valley's sky was never quite as full of stars as the traders claimed their skies were, something about the mountains, everyone said — and Ivo tried to feel simple. Tried to feel safe.

Instead he kept seeing the ink move. Kept feeling that pull behind his sternum, that almost-remembered voice. And beneath the fear — he examined it honestly, turning it over like a coin — beneath the fear was something else, something that shamed him with its brightness.

Wonder.

For twenty years he had ground ink and ruled lines and copied tithes and never once questioned the walls, because questioning them had seemed as pointless as questioning weather. But tonight the walls felt thin. Tonight the ink had remembered impatience. Tonight some sealed room inside Ivo Nakamura had heard a key turn in its lock, and though the door had not opened — though Aldous carried both pages away into the elders' wing, and the annex was locked, and the valley slept its dreamless, guarded sleep — Ivo lay awake in his narrow cell listening to his own heartbeat and understanding, with the absolute clarity available only to men on the far side of a threshold, that nothing was going to be simple again.

Three nights later, the stars went out.

* * *

They went out all at once, everywhere, while the whole valley stood in the streets and fields and doorways looking up, and the sound that came with it rolled down from the peaks like the sky itself shattering — a vast crystalline breaking, a thousand leagues of glass giving way — and the wards that had held the valley safe for longer than memory fell silent forever.

But that night, the first night, Ivo knew none of what it meant. He knew only that he was lying awake again — three nights of it now, wonder keeping vigil where sleep should be — and that the darkness outside his cell's high window was suddenly, horribly total. Not cloudy-dark. Absent-dark. As if someone had reached up and taken the stars the way a thief takes candles from a shrine.

He was out of bed and in the courtyard before he had decided to move, barefoot on frozen flagstones, surrounded by neighbors and brothers and elders, every face in the valley tilted upward toward a sky with nothing in it at all.

And in the terrible silence that followed the shattering — a silence in which two hundred people forgot to breathe together — Ivo felt the pull behind his sternum surge like a tide answering a moon that was no longer there.

Beside him, Elder Tomasen — old, bent, his breath steaming — whispered words Ivo had never heard him say, words that would follow Ivo beyond the mountains and all the way to the end of everything:

"The wards are down. The wards are *down*. After all these years — the binding has failed, and it will know, it will come looking—"

"Who will?" Ivo asked.

Tomasen turned. In the starless dark, the old man's face was unreadable, but his eyes, when they found Ivo's, widened — just slightly, just for a moment — with something that looked terribly like recognition.

"You," the elder said softly. "Nakamura. Why does it feel like *you*?"
