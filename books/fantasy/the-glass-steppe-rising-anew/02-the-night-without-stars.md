## 2. The Night Without Stars

The ink moved first.

Ivo Nakamura did not see it happen — he felt it, a prickle along the back of his neck like the moment before a sneeze or a confession. He was three lines from finishing the day's ledger, copying tithe records in his careful temple hand, when he glanced down and saw that the word *harvest* had shifted on the page. Not smeared. Shifted. The letters had drawn themselves tighter together, as if huddling against something coming.

He sat back. The scriptorium was empty at this hour; lamplight pooled yellow around his desk and left the rest of the long room in brown shadow. Rows of desks stretched away into the dark like sleeping animals. Somewhere above him, the temple bell marked the ninth hour with its usual patient bronze voice, and below, the kitchens were scrubbing pots, and beyond the walls the valley lay quiet under a sky so familiar that Ivo could have drawn its constellations from memory — the Plough Ox, the Weaver's Loom, the three bright sisters of the Shepherd's Crown.

He looked back at the ledger. The word had settled. *Harvest.* Ordinary. He almost laughed at himself.

Then every lamp in the scriptorium guttered at once, and the sound began.

It came from everywhere and nowhere — high, thin, crystalline, like a wineglass singing just before it breaks. It rose through the floor stones and down through the rafters, through the bones of the temple itself, which was older than the town and had been raised, everyone said, on foundations no one remembered laying. Ivo stood up so fast his stool went over backwards. The sound climbed. It climbed until it was less a sound than a pressure, a note held behind the teeth of the world, and then —

Glass. A thousand thousand panes of it, breaking all at once, somewhere impossibly far above.

He ran for the window.

The courtyard below stood empty in the last of the lamplight spilling from the doors. Beyond it, the terraced fields, the dark line of the orchards, the black shoulder of Mount Sereth — and above it all, where the sky should have been, nothing.

Not clouds. Clouds he would have understood. Not fog, not smoke, not the deep blue of a moonless night. *Nothing.* The stars were gone — all of them, every one, snuffed as neatly as candles pinched between wet fingers — and in their place was a smooth, lightless black, like ink poured across silk, like the inside of a closed eye. The valley had never known such darkness. Even on the cloudiest nights there had been the faint grey wash of starlight to shape the hills by. Now the hills themselves seemed to be dissolving into the sky, boundaries failing, the world losing its edges.

Ivo gripped the windowsill and made a small sound he would be ashamed of later.

The wards. Every child in the valley learned it before they learned their letters: the wards held the magic out, and the stars watched over the wards, and because the stars watched, nothing crossed, and nothing changed, and nothing *happened* here that had not happened for four hundred years. He had copied the liturgy a hundred times without ever hearing it. *Blessed are the fixed lights, which keep the border of our sleep.*

The stars had stopped watching. And whatever they had been holding back was now, presumably, free to come in.

He did not remember crossing the courtyard. He was simply running, ledger forgotten, ink still wet on his fingers, bursting into the elders' hall where the lamps were already lit and voices already rising. Elder Maren stood at the head of the table, and for the first time in Ivo's nineteen years of service he saw her look old — truly old, her spine bent, her hands flat on the wood as if the table were the only solid thing left.

"The bells," someone was saying. "Ring the bells."

"For what?" That was Elder Corvan, sharp-voiced, frightened. "What will you tell them? That the sky broke?"

"That the wards broke," Maren said quietly, and the hall went silent around her words. "Look outside, Corvan. Look at the sky and tell me the wards didn't break."

Ivo hovered at the door, unnoticed, ink-stained, breathing hard. Through the hall's tall windows the black sky pressed against the glass like a living thing. He thought of the word that had moved on his page. *Harvest.* As if something had come to collect.

"They'll say it's a punishment," murmured a young acolyte near the window, gray-faced. "The faithful will say we sinned."

"The faithful can say what they like." Maren straightened, and some of the iron came back into her voice. "Wake the wardens. Open the reliquary. If any fragment of the old binding survives down there, I want it on this table before dawn."

"There is nothing down there," Corvan said. "You know there's nothing. The vault's been empty since my grandmother's time—"

"Then find me something else. Anything." She pressed her knuckles to her mouth for one unsteady breath, and Ivo saw, with a strange lurch of pity, that she was afraid not of the dark but of being seen to be afraid. "Four hundred years, gentlemen. Four hundred years the wards have held, and tonight they fell, and we do not know why, and we do not know what comes through now that they're down. So we will do the only thing scribes and elders know how to do. We will write it down. We will record everything. Because someday, someone is going to need to know how it felt, the night the stars went out."

Someday, someone is going to need to know how it felt. Ivo carried those words back up to the scriptorium like a coal cupped in his bare hands. It was the first time in his life anyone had suggested his work might matter to someone who did not yet exist.

* * *

He could not sleep, of course. No one slept. From his narrow cell he heard the whole valley waking: dogs first, confused by a darkness too complete to navigate, then people, then the temple bell ringing over and over in the old emergency pattern that hadn't been used in living memory. By the second hour past midnight, the courtyard was full of torches and frightened faces, and Ivo stood at his window watching the flames, thinking about how strange it was that fire, which he'd seen ten thousand times, suddenly looked like the only proof the world still existed.

That was when the knocking started on his own door. Three slow strikes.

"Ivo Nakamura." The voice was dry as old paper. Master Odric. His debt-holder. His owner, though the ledgers called it *guardianship*. "Open the door. We must speak."

Odric filled the doorway when Ivo opened it, a broad man gone soft with years of sitting behind accounts, his lantern held high so that its light fell full on Ivo's face — an old habit, checking on collateral. Behind him the corridor was dark, and beyond the little window at its end, the sky was a wound.

"You've heard the bells," Odric said. It wasn't a question. "Come to my office. There are documents to review."

"Documents? Tonight? Master, the sky has *fallen* —"

"And contracts," Odric said, turning already, "do not fall."

The office smelled of dust and sealing wax and forty years of Odric's pipe smoke. He did not offer Ivo a chair. Instead he unlocked a cabinet Ivo had never seen opened, and drew out a scroll so old its ends had gone amber and brittle, tied with cord the color of dried blood.

"Your grandfather signed this," Odric said. "And your mother before the debt passed to you. The Nakamura indenture. Do you recall its terms?"

"I recall I copy ledgers until the debt is paid, master. Forty more years, at current rates."

"Forty more years *within the valley*." Odric laid the scroll flat and weighted its curling ends with an inkstone and a paperweight shaped like a bull. "Read the fourth clause. Aloud. You're a scribe; you'll read it faster than I."

Ivo leaned over the ancient characters. They were written in a formal register, dense and archaic, and he read slowly, translating as he went:

"'Should the protections of the valley fail — should the fixed lights go out or the borders stand open — then the debt shall no longer be discharged by labor within, but by service beyond.'" He stopped. Read it again. The words didn't change. "Service... beyond."

"Beyond the wards," Odric said. "Outside the valley. Into the world." He said the last two words the way other men named diseases.

"That clause is dead law. The wards have held for four centuries. No court would—"

"No court sits outside this valley that recognizes anything but its own seal," Odric said, tapping the bull-shaped weight, "and within this valley, that document is the law of your body. The stars are out, Ivo. The border stands open. The clause has woken."

Ivo stared at him. "You want me to leave."

"What I *want*," Odric said, with the first flash of genuine feeling Ivo had ever seen cross his face, "is irrelevant. What I am owed is a debt, and the debt has changed its shape. Your grandfather borrowed against the temple's future to keep your family fed through the famine year. The temple lent. The temple now requires service it cannot get from a scribe sitting safe inside walls that may not hold." He rolled the scroll closed. "There are those in the capital who understand what happened tonight. Who have understood for longer than we have. The temple received a summons weeks ago — I confess I dismissed it as the capital's usual meddling — demanding the loan of any scribe with... particular aptitudes."

"Aptitudes," Ivo repeated dumbly. "Master, I copy harvest records."

"So it would seem." Odric looked at him a moment too long, in a way that made the skin between Ivo's shoulders itch. "Regardless. The clause binds. At dawn you walk out the gate with the temple's escort, and you serve beyond the wards until such time as the debt is judged discharged. Or until—" he paused delicately "—circumstances resolve otherwise."

Until you die out there, he meant. In a world of magic, wearing a valley boy's soft hands.

Ivo's mouth had gone dry. Outside the window, the black sky hung over the rooftops, absolute, hungry-looking. He had never in his life been more than a day's walk from the temple bell. He knew the smell of every season of this valley, the exact weight of its rain. And beyond the border — the maps in the library went blank at the passes, annotated in faded red: *Here the world resumes. God keep the reader.*

Terror, yes. Terror like cold water filling his chest.

But underneath it, shamefully, unmistakably, something else. Something that had woken when the ink moved on his page and was awake now, beating its wings.

He was going to see where the world resumed.

* * *

They gave him until dawn, which is to say they gave him nothing, because what does one pack for the end of everything? He took his good pen, a spare nib, a stoppered bottle of ink, the copybook he'd kept since childhood. He stood a long time looking at his narrow bed, his one shelf, the hook where his coat hung, trying to memorize the cell the way he'd memorize a page he'd have to reproduce from memory later.

At the gate, the eastern horizon stayed stubbornly dark. No dawn blush. The elders had noticed it too; he heard them murmuring — perhaps the sun itself would fail, perhaps the blackness would eat even morning. But the sun rose anyway, a bruised and bloodshot thing, climbing into a sky utterly stripped of everything else, and somehow that was worse. The world going on without its stars. The ordinary machinery of light grinding forward while heaven stood empty overhead.

Maren met him at the gate. To his surprise she pressed a slim book into his hands — blank pages, good vellum.

"You heard what I told the others," she said. "Write it all down. Whatever happens out there, however bad it gets — write it down. Someone will need to know."

"Will they let me come home, Elder? When the debt's discharged?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and her eyes went briefly, involuntarily, to the empty sky, and he understood that she did not know, that maybe no answer existed anywhere in her books.

"The gate will be here," was all she said. "Walk well, Ivo Nakamura."

Then the escort formed up — four wardens in dull mail, nervous men glancing at the sky between every step — and the gates swung open onto the mountain road, the road no valley-born foot had traveled in living memory, and Ivo stepped through.

The air changed first. He couldn't have said how — thinner, sweeter, charged, like the moment before lightning — and then the road bent around the shoulder of the pass, and the wards fell away behind him like a garment slipped off, and the world's true magic flooded into the gap where his soul had been quietly starving all these years.

It hurt. That was the first truth of it, the truth that dropped him to his knees on the frozen road with the wardens shouting: it hurt like swallowing broken glass, like every nerve in his body being strung and plucked at once, because nineteen years of suppression were tearing open all together, and something vast and wild rushed into him, something that recognized him—

—and reached, hungrily, for the glyphs sleeping in his blood.

One of the wardens grabbed his shoulder. Ivo looked up, tears streaming, gasping, and saw the man's face go slack with horror — not at Ivo's pain, but at the road behind them, where the air had begun, faintly and terribly, to shimmer.

Where, far below in the valley, the border stones were glowing.

Where something was already following.
