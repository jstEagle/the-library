## 4. The Knight Who Broke Her Oath

Sera Voss had arrested forty-one people in her years of service to the Ward-Order, and she had never once sat across a fire from one of them, sharing her rations, wondering if she'd made the worst mistake of her career by letting him live.

Ivo Nakamura sat with his back against a lightning-split oak, wrists still raw from where her bindings had been, hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea she'd brewed because it seemed absurd, somehow, to arrest a man and then let him shiver. He drank like someone who had never been offered anything he didn't have to repay. She recognized that. She'd worn that same hunger once, before the Order gave her a different kind of debt.

"The wards fell at moonrise," she said, because someone had to say it aloud, and the silence between them had begun to feel like a third person at their fire. "You understand what that means? Not weakened. Not frayed. Fallen. Every ward-stone from the Glass Steppe to the eastern sea went dark in the same breath. That hasn't happened in four hundred years."

"Three hundred and twelve," Ivo said quietly. "If the ledgers I copied were accurate."

She stared at him. "You know the founding date."

"I copy temple ledgers." He said it the way other men said *I dig graves* — an occupation, a weight. "The Binding of the Valley, the Star-Wards, the border seals. I've transcribed the maintenance records for six years. I know every stone's consecration date, every renewal cycle, every hand that ever touched them. I just never thought—" His jaw tightened. "I never thought I'd hear one fall."

"You heard it?" Sera leaned forward. "From inside the valley?"

"All of us did. Like glass breaking under a blanket. Muffled, but everywhere at once." He looked up, and the firelight caught something in his face that wasn't fear, or wasn't only fear. "And then the stars went out. All of them. At once. Do you know what the elders said? They said it was a test of faith. They lit candles and sang hymns while the sky *bled*, and when I asked what could snuff a star, they told me my question was pride."

"What did you tell your debt-holder?"

Something flickered across his face — quick, bitter, quickly buried. "That's how you found me, isn't it. Master Corven invoked the old clause. Service beyond the valley, owed to the holder of my bond. He walked me to the border himself. He was smiling."

Sera had met men like Corven in the capital — the kind who kept ledgers of other people's lives and called it commerce. She filed the name away. It might matter later.

"You crossed the border," she said, "and magic flooded into a valley-born scribe who should have felt nothing worse than wind. And instead of collapsing—"

"I collapsed," Ivo said dryly. "Twice."

"But you *unbound* things on the way down." She reached into her satchel and drew out the evidence, wrapped in oilcloth: three fragments of enchanted chain, dead as river stones, their binding-sigils unraveled into clean, empty spirals. "These were active restraints. Ward-forged. When I found you, you were lying in the road with your hand around this one, and it was already coming apart. No cutting tools. No counter-charms. You touched it and it *forgot what it was*."

"I don't know how I did it."

"No. But you did it instinctively, without training, without killing yourself in the process — and that's the part that matters." She set the chain fragments down between them, deliberate as laying cards. "There are perhaps a dozen living unbinders registered with the Order. Each trained for twenty years, each capable of unraveling maybe one minor enchantment a day before the backlash puts them in bed for a week. You unbound three chains, half a ward-stone's residual field, and whatever Corven had wrapped around your bond-tattoo — all in your first hour outside the valley. Either you're the strongest unbinding talent born in a century, or—"

"Or I'm the saboteur who brought down the sky." His voice was flat. "That's what you thought when you arrested me."

"It's what I was ordered to think." She held his gaze. "The Order's standing directive since the Night Without Stars: detain any gifted individual near a fallen ward-site. You were kneeling in the wreckage of a border seal, glowing like a lantern. What was I supposed to conclude?"

"The truth would have been a start."

"The truth was not available to me." She heard the edge creep into her own voice and didn't bother to blunt it. "You want honesty, scribe? Here it is. When I cuffed you, I believed there was a decent chance I was holding the person who murdered the stars. And I arrested you anyway instead of putting a blade through your heart, because your hands were shaking and you were crying about the stars themselves, and saboteurs don't weep over the things they break. So don't sit there and tell me I owed you trust I didn't have. Trust is earned in coin neither of us is carrying."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark beyond their little ring of light, an owl called — a real owl, mundane and hungry, and Sera found the sound almost unbearably comforting after two nights of a silent, starless sky.

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled — small, tired, real. "Fair. I suppose I haven't earned much trust either. I did run from you twice."

"You ran *slowly*. For a man who supposedly killed heaven, you're a remarkably poor fugitive."

"I'd never been outside the valley walls before last night. I didn't know which way roads went. Everything past the border stones was—" He gestured vaguely at the darkness, and she understood him completely, because she remembered her first posting, her first night under open sky, the vertigo of a world without edges. "It was all story. Map-makers' inventions. And now the stories are eating people, apparently."

Sera went still. "What do you mean, eating people?"

"The village we passed. Millbrook, the sign said. There was a woman in the square asking everyone where her daughter was. Describing her. Brown hair, gap teeth, seven years old." Ivo's voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something fragile. "No one in the village had ever seen the girl. Not her neighbors, not — Sera, not the woman's own mother. They looked at her like she was mad. But the woman had a second bowl set at her table, and children's shoes by the door, and a drawing pinned to the wall in a child's hand. Something took the girl *and the memory of her*, and left everything else behind like a coat."

Sera had seen the same thing in Harrow's Ford, two days south, and told herself it was grief-madness, mass hysteria, anything but the truth pressing at the edge of her understanding. Now she couldn't pretend anymore.

"The Order's reports call it 'the Hollowing,'" she said slowly. "Villages going quiet. Not emptied — *hollowed*. People still there, still working their fields, but missing pieces. A blacksmith who can't remember his craft. A priestess who's forgotten every prayer but keeps mouthing the shapes of them." She pulled off one gauntlet and flexed her bare fingers toward the fire. "And here's the part the reports don't say, because the reports are written in the capital, and the capital doesn't want it said: the Hollowing started the same night the stars went out. Same hour. Whatever devoured the starlight is feeding on more than light."

"You think it's taking memories too."

"I think memories and starlight weigh the same, to the right kind of mouth."

Ivo shivered. She pretended not to notice, and poured him more tea, and hated herself a little for how naturally the gesture came.

They sat in silence again, but it was a different silence now — the working kind, the kind where two minds turn the same problem. Sera used it to look at him properly, without the adrenaline and the suspicion distorting everything. He was younger than she'd first taken him for; mid-twenties, maybe, with ink stains permanent in the creases of his fingers and the posture of a man who'd spent six years bent over a desk. Valley-born, debt-bound, walled-in his whole life. By rights he should be cowering. Instead he was watching the dead chain fragments with the expression of a scholar confronting a fascinating disease.

"Why did they exile you?" he asked suddenly.

The question landed like a dropped stone. "What?"

"Your gauntlets bear the Order's mark, but the crest has been scored through. Deliberately — see, the lines cross the griffin's throat, not its shield. That's not wear. That's a sentence." He said it mildly, factually, the way he must have catalogued ten thousand ledger entries. "Ward-knights aren't posted to border patrol unless they're being punished. The border was considered a dead assignment even before the wards fell. So. Why did they exile you, Sera Voss?"

She should deflect. Every instinct of training and self-preservation said deflect, smile the knightly smile, give him nothing. But he'd handed her his humiliation freely — the debt, the smiling master, the tears — and there was a mathematics to trust, and she felt the scales tipping whether she liked it or not.

"I reported the Lord Warden of the Southern Reach for selling ward-exemptions," she said.

Ivo blinked. "Exemptions."

"Passes letting merchant caravans cross warded ground without inspection. Smugglers paid him; he paid his superiors; everyone got rich except the people the wards existed to protect. Three villages burned because uninspected cargo carried ash-wyrm eggs." Her voice stayed level through long practice. "I filed a formal report. Sealed testimony. The proper channels, all of them. Six weeks later my report was missing from the archives, the Lord Warden had been promoted to the capital, and I was reassigned to the Glass Steppe border for 'temperament unsuited to command.'"

"That's obscene."

"That's the Order." She turned the scorched crest on her gauntlet so the firelight caught the cut. "I appealed twice. The second appeal cost me my rank insignia. After that I stopped writing reports and started writing nothing, because I understood at last what the Order actually wanted from me: my sword arm and my silence, in that order. Seven years on the border, watching the wards, keeping my mouth shut." She laughed, short and ugly. "And then the wards fell anyway, and the stars went out, and do you know what my first thought was? Standing on the wall, watching the sky die? Not *how*. Not *why*. My first thought was — *they'll blame me for this*. Because that's what the Order taught me. When something breaks, find the inconvenient person and make them carry it."

"So when you found me glowing in the ruins—"

"You were the most convenient person in the world." She met his eyes. "And then you weren't. Because I've spent seven years learning what corruption looks like up close, scribe, and you don't look like it. You look like what I was, before. Someone the system chewed up and pointed at a scapegoat."

"Is that why you let me go?"

"It's why I'm *talking* to you. Letting you go comes later, if it comes at all." But her voice had softened despite itself, and they both heard it.

Ivo set down his cup. "Tell me what you actually want. Not the Order's orders — yours. Why chase me past the border stones? Why camp with me and feed me and explain the Hollowing? You could have dragged me to the garrison at Harrow's Ford and collected whatever bounty they're offering for gifted detainees."

"Because the garrison at Harrow's Ford reports to the capital," Sera said, "and the capital is lying to us."

She let that settle. Then she reached into her satchel again and produced the thing she'd been turning over for two days, the thing that had finally driven her to desert her post: a summons, printed on heavy cream paper, bearing the royal seal.

"Three days ago, a courier came through the border post carrying these. Royal decree, distributed to every garrison, every temple, every town crier: all persons discovered with binding-gifts are commanded to present themselves to the palace, where they will be 'honored guests of the crown' and 'aid the throne in restoring the heavens.'" She tossed it onto the oilcloth between them. "Read the language closely. Honored guests. Not conscripts. Invitations. From a king who has never once, in fourteen years of reign, shown personal interest in ward-craft."

Ivo picked it up carefully. "You think it's bait."

"I think the stars went out and the king's response was to *collect magicians*." She stood, restless, and paced to the edge of the firelight where the dark pressed close. "I think whatever happened to our sky is sitting on the throne right now, wearing the crown, and it wants every person alive who might know how to undo it gathered up in one place. In its house. Behind its walls." She turned back to him. "Or I'm wrong, and the king is desperate and honest, and answering the summons is exactly what a loyal knight should advise. I genuinely don't know which it is. That's what terrifies me — I've served the crown my whole adult life, and I no longer know whether it's wounded or *hungry*."

"And so you need me." Ivo's voice was quiet. "Because if it comes to breaking into places — palaces, vaults, wherever the answer hides — you can't unbind a lock or a ward or a lie. But I can."

"I need you," Sera admitted, and the words cost her, and she let him watch them cost her, because that was the coin trust was minted in. "And you need me. Because you're a scribe who's never slept outdoors, whose gift draws attention like blood draws sharks, and who is currently the most valuable loose object in a kingdom gone mad. Alone, you'll be taken within a week — by the crown, by whatever ate the stars, or by some lordling who wants an unbinding tool of his very own. With me, you have armor, a blade, seven years of survival craft, and one person in the world who knows what you really are and hasn't sold you yet."

"One person," Ivo repeated softly. "That's a thin garrison."

"It's the only kind I can staff."

He laughed — startled out of him, brief and bright, and something in Sera's chest loosened at the sound in a way she chose not to examine. Then his face grew serious, and he looked down at his ink-stained hands.

"There's something you should know before we shake on anything," he said. "My name. Nakamura. I always assumed it was just a valley name, one family among dozens. But after the wards fell, Master Corven looked at me strangely and said the name was older than the valley's records. And when your chain broke under my hand, I saw the sigils unraveling, and I *read* them, Sera. Not recognized — read, the way I read temple script. The binding-language in those chains shares root-forms with glyphs in the oldest ledgers I copy. The ones written by the valley's founders." He lifted his eyes to hers, and the fire reflected in them like trapped stars. "I think my family built something. Or bound something. And I think that's why I am what I am, and why the wards fell the way they did — around me, like a door opening for the right key. If we're going to travel together, you deserve to know you're allying with a mystery, not a man."

Sera looked at him for a long moment — this ink-fingered, debt-marked, star-struck stranger who had crossed the border of his entire world yesterday and was already talking about keys and doors.

"Mysteries are the only honest companions left," she said. "Everyone else I've ever trusted turned out to be wearing a mask. At least you warn me yours exists." She extended her hand, gauntleted, the broken griffin catching the light. "Terms, then. We go together toward the capital, but not to the palace — not yet, not while that summons smells like a trap. We follow the trail of the Hollowing and the fallen wards. You teach me what you learn from the bindings; I keep you breathing. Neither of us surrenders the other to anyone — crown, Order, or god. Agreed?"

Ivo took her hand. His grip was ink-stained and steady. "Agreed."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, as the grey light began leaking back into a sky that stayed stubbornly, horribly blank. Sera saddled the horses while Ivo kicked earth over the fire, and she caught herself studying him — the way he moved in the new world like a man testing ice, wonder and wariness balanced in every step.

"We'll make the river crossing by midday," she said, swinging up. "After that, the map stops helping us. Past the Ashwood, the roads are more rumor than road."

"Good," Ivo said, and she glanced over, surprised.

"Good?"

"I spent six years copying maps of places I'd never see." He urged his horse up beside hers, and though his knuckles were white on the reins, his eyes were fixed eastward, toward the unknown, toward whatever waited in the hollowed villages and the starless dark. "I'd rather walk roads where maps end than copy another line of a world that isn't there."

Sera Voss, oathbreaker, exile, deserter, rode out beside him into the blind country — and if a part of her whispered that she had just tied her fate to the strangest thread in a kingdom unraveling, she silenced it. Outcasts had to hold to something.

For now, they held to each other.

Behind them, far to the west, the sun rose over a horizon where no star had ever quite finished setting — and ahead, at the edge of hearing, something in the empty sky seemed to shift its weight, patient as hunger, waiting to be followed home.
