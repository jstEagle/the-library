## 6. What the Hollow Regent Wants

The waystation at Coldmere Crossing had once been a place of warmth. Sera could see it in the bones of the building — the great hearth built to roast an ox whole, the long tables scarred by a century of tankards, the iron hooks above the fire where travelers' cloaks had steamed and dripped for generations. Now the hearth was cold, the tables empty, and the only steam rising was from the blood soaking through a courier's coat on the floor beside the bar.

He had come out of the north road at dusk, riding a horse that died three strides past the door, and he had not stopped bleeding since.

"Pressure," Sera said, kneeling beside him with her hands buried in the wound below his ribs. "Keep pressure on it."

Ivo knelt across from her, his scribe's fingers pressing where she told him, his face gone the color of tallow. He had seen wounds before — who in a border valley hadn't — but he had never seen one that would not close, and Sera watched him understand it, watch the knowledge arrive in him like winter arriving over a mountain pass: all at once, and from every direction.

"He's fading," Ivo said. "Sera, he's—"

"I know." She did not look up. "You. Courier. Look at me. What's your name?"

The man's eyes found hers with effort. He was young — younger than her, maybe twenty-five — with road dust ground so deep into the lines of his face it looked like age. His lips were gray. "Tomas," he said. "Tomas Reyle."

"Tomas Reyle. You're going to tell me what you're carrying, and then you're going to stay awake, because if you sleep you die. Do you understand?"

A ghost of a smile. "Ward-knight," he said, reading the faded badge of her order on her shoulder. "Thought you lot were all dead or disgraced."

"Both, mostly. The message."

His hand crawled toward his coat, toward the inner pocket over his heart, and Sera helped him, drawing out an oilskin packet stained dark along one edge. She cracked the seal — royal wax, the crowned stag of House Aldermere — and unfolded the decree inside.

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings refused to cohere into sense.

*By order of the Crown, let it be known throughout the realm: all persons gifted in the arts of binding, warding, or unbinding are hereby summoned to the capital, there to serve in the restoration of the sky-wards. Travel will be provided. Shelter will be provided. Refusal will be construed as treason against the realm in its hour of need.*

It bore the king's seal. It bore the king's signature. And every line of it was wrong.

"Binders," she said quietly. "Summoned to the palace."

"That's the hundredth copy," Tomas said. His voice was thinning, fraying like rope under load. "I've got forty more in my saddlebags. Postmasters are nailing them to every door from here to the capital. They want everyone with the gift. Everyone."

"The gift doesn't work like that." Sera heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she'd used on green recruits, on lying quartermasters, on the tribunal that had ended her career. "Binding talent runs in perhaps one person in ten thousand, and half of those can barely charm a latch. A decree like this isn't a summons. It's a net."

"Maybe they just need help," Ivo said. "The wards fell. The stars went out. Maybe the crown is actually trying to—"

"Look at me, Ivo." She looked up from the decree at last, and made herself hold his eyes, because this mattered more than kindness. "Six weeks ago the entire border network fell in a single night. Not frayed. Not neglected. *Fell*, all at once, clean as a cut rope. In six weeks, the crown has issued no evacuation orders for the villages nearest the broken wards, no rationing, no musters. But within a month, it produces a perfectly drafted decree conscripting every gifted soul in the realm to the palace?" She shook her head slowly. "Governments move like glaciers. This moved like a hunting cat. Something down there already knew what it wanted and how to ask for it."

"You think the king is behind it."

"I think whatever wears the king is behind it." She said it plainly because there was no gentler way to say it, and because she had spent four weeks watching Ivo flinch from the shape of the thing they both knew was coming. "Think about what happened at the ford last week. Think about the well at Harrow's Bend."

At Harrow's Bend, three days back, Ivo had unbound a hunger-ward from a village well — a knot of starving magic coiled around the water like a tapeworm, drinking something from the people who drank there. Not water. *Memory*. The villagers had forgotten their dead, their marriages, their children's faces, and when Ivo cut the ward loose, an old woman had wept for a son she'd mourned years ago and only just remembered grieving. Word of it had spread faster than they could walk. People had started leaving tokens on the road for them — bread, candles, a child's carved wooden bird.

And every token, every whispered story, every grateful village had made them easier to find.

"It's gathering them," Sera said. "Whatever ate the stars is hungry, and memory and magic are its food, and it has issued a royal decree asking every gifted person in the realm to walk into its mouth voluntarily." She folded the decree along its creases, precise as a drill instructor. "This isn't a summons. It's a menu."

* * *

Tomas Reyle lasted another hour.

They moved him near the hearth, though the fire was dead, and propped him up against the bar because he said lying flat made the breathing worse. Sera gave him poppy syrup from her kit and did not pretend it would do anything but soften the edge. Ivo sat beside him and held the cup to his lips, and somewhere in the second hour the courier stopped talking about the road and started talking about his sister in Vailholm, who made glass beads, blue ones, and how he'd promised to bring her a star.

"A real one," he said, laughing, which turned into coughing, which turned into red on his teeth. "Everyone's saying the stars'll come back. The criers say it. The decree says the wards'll be restored. So I told her — wait for me, Nessa, I'll bring you one home in a jar."

Ivo's hands went still on the cup.

"They're lying to you, Tomas," Sera said. There was no point in anything else. He was dying; lies were for the living.

"I know." He smiled at her with bloody teeth, and it was the worst thing she'd seen in weeks, and she had seen the Ashwood. "But it's a nice lie. Nessa likes nice lies. She puts them in her window with the beads." His eyes drifted toward the shuttered windows, toward the starless dark beyond. "Do you think they miss us? The stars? When something eats you, do you know it's happening?"

No one answered him. After a while his breathing changed, and after a while more than that, it stopped, and Sera closed his eyes with two fingers and sat back on her heels and felt the weight of the oilskin packet in her lap like a stone.

Ivo washed his hands at the rain barrel outside. She heard the water moving, unhurried, thorough — a scribe's habit, scrubbing ink from skin — and when he came back in his face was composed again, mostly, the way a lake composes itself over something drowned.

"We should bury him," Ivo said.

"There's a spade behind the station. I saw it." She rose. "Ivo. Before we do. I need you to look at this again."

She spread the decree on the bar top and weighted its corners with two mugs and a candlestick, and lit the candle, and made him read it aloud, line by line, the way he'd been taught to read ledgers — slowly, hunting for the error in the column.

"*All persons gifted in the arts of binding, warding, or unbinding.*" He paused. "Unbinding. Sera, binding law hasn't mentioned unbinding in two hundred years. It's not even taught anymore. The order classified it as theoretical."

"And yet here it is, named in a royal decree drafted within weeks of the night a valley scribe accidentally unbound a forbidden glyph." She tapped the parchment. "Someone knows what you are, Ivo. Not suspects. *Knows*. This line wasn't written for the realm. It was written for you. The rest of it — the summons, the promise of shelter, the treason clause — that's just the net's mesh. You're the fish it's shaped around."

He stared at the page. In the candlelight she watched him do the thing he always did when the world broke open — go very quiet, go inward, reach for the discipline of copying, of exactness, of one true stroke at a time. She had learned to recognize it. She had also learned, though she'd have cut out her tongue before admitting it, that she found it steadying. Whatever else the world did to Ivo Nakamura, he met it with a pen's patience.

"My parents," he said finally. "When the Regent offered—" He stopped. Started again. "In the library, you weren't there. It spoke to me. It knew my ancestor's name. It said my parents didn't abandon the valley. It said they were taken." His jaw tightened. "If it knows my family that well, then yes. This decree has my name woven through it whether it's written there or not."

"Then we don't answer it."

"No." He looked up, and there was something new in his face, something she hadn't seen in the valley, hadn't seen even at Harrow's Bend when the old woman wept. It took her a moment to name it, because she had never once been allowed to feel it herself: purpose, chosen freely. "We don't run from it either. Every gifted person in the realm is being funneled toward that palace, Sera. People like Tomas's sister, people who can charm a latch, walking into that thing's mouth because a piece of paper told them to and the alternative is being called traitors. If someone doesn't warn them—"

"With what army? With what authority? I'm a disgraced knight and you're a debt-bound scribe. The moment we speak against the decree, we make ourselves outlaws, and outlaws can't help anyone."

"Outlaws can't be summoned, either." A ghost of the dry humor that surfaced in him at odd hours. "There's a symmetry there."

She wanted to laugh. She wanted, absurdly, to put her hand over his on the bar top, and she did neither. Instead she rolled up the decree and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart, where Tomas had carried it, because some cargoes change the carrier.

* * *

They buried Tomas Reyle at dawn behind the waystation, under a rowan tree whose leaves had gone grey at the edges — star-sickness, the blight creeping outward from the wounded sky, killing things slowly instead of devouring them fast. Ivo dug; Sera laid him straight. Neither of them had words worth saying, so they said the old soldier's words, four lines, older than any order or kingdom:

*Earth to earth. Dark to dark. What the light lent, the light reclaims. Walk on, walker.*

Ivo set the courier's satchel upright at the head of the grave, emptied of everything but the forty unposted decrees, and Sera understood the gesture a beat before she could have explained it: let the paper serve the dead man's warning, since it had failed to serve the crown's.

They rode out as the sun came up — a pale, watery sun these days, as if it too had been fed upon — and the capital lay somewhere south beyond the hills, and the decree lay against Sera's heart like a second heartbeat, counting down.

Because here was the truth she had not said aloud, the truth that had been sharpening all night in the dark of the empty waystation while a good man bled out beside a dead fire: she had read the decree three times, and on the third reading she had seen what she'd pretended not to see. The summons required presentation at the palace within sixty days. Sixty days from its issue date.

Which was nine days ago.

Which meant that whatever trap was closing around the realm's gifted, it was closing fast, and the only path she and Ivo had ever intended to walk — the road to the capital, the road to the Regent, the road to answers about his blood and her oath and the binding that held the sky together — ran directly through the center of it.

The bait was the road itself. And they were going to take it anyway.

Sera turned her horse south, and beside her Ivo did the same, and ahead of them the grey hills rolled down toward the plains where maps grew vague and the horizon held nothing at all where stars used to be.

"If we're riding into a trap," Ivo said quietly, "we should at least know the shape of it."

"Then we ride slow," Sera said, "and we watch everything, and we trust no one between here and the gates." She paused. "And Ivo — if anyone asks, we're answering the summons. Two more binders, come dutifully to serve the crown."

His mouth curved, thin and humorless. "Bait, walking into the trap, pretending to be willing."

"Willing prey gets closer than wary ones." She spurred her horse forward, and the wind coming off the starless hills smelled faintly of ash. "Let's go see what wants us so badly."

Behind them, the rowan tree stood over a fresh grave, its grey leaves whispering in a wind that carried, from far away and getting nearer with every mile, the smell of hoarded lantern-light and a city holding its breath.
