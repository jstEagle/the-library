## 8. Hunted Through the Ashwood

**Ivo**

The Ashwood had been dying for a long time, Sera said, but Ivo had not understood what that meant until he stood beneath it.

The trees rose around them like the ribs of drowned ships — grey bark, grey leaves that had stopped bothering to fall, a canopy so dense and colorless that noon looked like dusk. There was no birdsong. There was no birdsong anywhere anymore, but here the silence felt deliberate, as if the forest were holding its breath. Frost clung to things that should not have been frosted in the height of summer: moss, mushrooms, the droppings of some animal long since fled. And overhead, through the gaps in the branches, the sky was a smooth, starless black even in daylight — a ceiling instead of a heaven.

"It's worse than the villages," Ivo said quietly.

Sera walked ahead of him, hand resting on her sword hilt out of habit rather than fear, or so he told himself. "The Regent's feeding deepens as you go north. The well-wards we've unbound were small hungers. This—" she gestured at the grey canopy, at the frost, at the lightless sky pressing down between the branches, "—this is where the meal started."

They had been on the road eleven days. Eleven days of forgotten children's names and empty chairs at supper tables, of Ivo kneeling by wells and doorposts and one shrine with his palms flat against the stone, feeling the hungry little spells beneath like ticks fastened into the world's skin, and pulling them loose. Each time it cost him something. He'd learned to hide how much. He suspected Sera had learned to see through the hiding.

Now she stopped so abruptly that he nearly walked into her back.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Smoke." Her nostrils flared. "Not woodsmoke. Pitch and cold iron." She turned, and in the grey half-light her eyes found his with an intensity that made his chest tighten. "Off the road. Now."

He didn't argue. He'd learned that too.

They plunged downslope into the bracken, Sera leading, choosing their footing without seeming to look, and Ivo followed with the clumsy urgency of a man whose body still remembered years of sitting at a copying desk. His satchel thumped against his spine — inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the archive pages they'd carried out of the mountain, his ancestors' handwriting crawling across vellum like frost forming on glass. *The Great Binding.* The words that had made the wards, the valley, the vault, the whole architecture of a hidden world. The words that had failed.

The words that, according to everything they'd read, could only be reforged from within the capital — and only by blood that had written them.

His blood.

A horn sounded behind them. Not a hunting horn's bright call — something lower, uglier, a note that seemed to bend the air sideways. The frost on the bracken around them shivered into powder.

"Crown hunters," Sera said, and there was no surprise in her voice, only a grim settling, like a door closing. "Three horns means they have your scent-sigil. They copied it off the waystation ledger."

"I thought the ledgers were destroyed."

"I burned them. They'd already been read." She drew her sword. The steel looked wrong in this place — no shine, no gleam, just a dull line of grey. "Listen to me. When they come — and they'll come from both flanks, they always do — you don't fight. You run when I tell you to run, you stop when I tell you to stop, and you touch nothing they throw at you."

"And if they throw something at *me*?"

"Then you do what you do." For a moment something moved behind her sternness — not softness exactly, but the place where softness would live if she allowed herself any. "Better than anyone alive."

The second horn answered the first, closer, from the left. The third came from the right almost immediately after, and Ivo understood with a lurching horror that they hadn't been following the road at all. They'd been herded along it, the way a shepherd moves sheep toward a gate.

"They knew," he breathed.

"The courier's death was reported. Someone connected us to the decree." Sera rolled her shoulders once, a small ritual, and Ivo realized he was watching her say goodbye to hesitation. "Stay behind me."

* * *

They came out of the grey trees like wolves out of fog.

There were six of them, maybe seven — hard to count, because they wore the king's colors dyed into cloaks the same ash-grey as the forest, and they moved between the trunks the way smoke moves, without disturbing anything. Their faces were masked in dull iron. Their weapons were not swords, mostly. Chains — long, whispering lengths of enchanted links that floated a half-inch above the ground and moved as if they were swimming.

"Voss," said the one who seemed to lead, and even muffled by iron, the voice was familiar with contempt. "Still playing knight errant with the founder's whelp."

"Captain Dren," Sera answered. "Still fetching for a crown that isn't a crown anymore."

"You don't know what you're saying."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "I know what I'm *seeing*. That's different."

The captain raised one gloved hand, and the chains rose with it.

What happened next happened very fast and also unbearably slowly, the way disasters do. Ivo saw Sera step forward into the space between himself and the hunters, saw her sword come up in a guard position he recognized now from a hundred evenings of practice in waystation yards — low left, weight back, inviting the strike she wanted — and then the chains came on like floodwater and the world became noise and motion.

She was magnificent. He hated himself for thinking it in the middle of a fight, but there was no other word. She fought the way water fights, giving ground and taking it back, turning the hunters' own momentum against them. One chain whipped past her cheek close enough to cut hair; she caught its return arc on her crossguard and *wrenched*, and somewhere in the grey trees a hunter screamed as his weapon was torn from his grip and his fingers with it. Another came at her from the right and she dropped under his swing and put her pommel into the joint of his mask with a sound like a bell cracking.

But six was six, and one was one.

Two chains slipped past her entirely — one coiling toward Ivo's legs, the other arcing high to drop over his shoulders like a snare. He heard Sera shout his name, heard the raw panic in it stripped of all discipline, and something in him went very quiet and very clear.

He caught the falling chain.

It should have burned him. It did burn him — cold iron enchantment searing up his palms like grasping a rope pulled from a fire — but beneath the pain he felt what he always felt when he touched the Regent's work: the *seams*. Magic was language, and the Hollow Regent's people wrote carelessly, greedily, spells crammed together like hoarded coins, and every spell had a seam where one word ended and another began.

Ivo Nakamura had spent his whole life reading other people's words.

"*Loose,*" he whispered — not the word itself but the meaning of it, the unbinding that lived in his blood like a second heartbeat — and the chain in his hands came apart.

Not broke. *Came apart* — link from link, spell from metal, the enchantment sighing out of it like breath leaving a body, until he held nothing but a length of dead ordinary iron that clattered to the frost. The second chain, mid-strike, unraveled the same way, its binding dissolving before it reached him, and the hunter guiding it staggered as if shoved.

For one heartbeat, the clearing went silent except for Sera's ragged breathing.

Then the captain said, softly, reverently, horribly: "*Take him alive.*"

Everything after that was worse.

They stopped trying to kill him, which meant they stopped holding back everywhere else. Three hunters converged on Sera at once, chains weaving a net, and Ivo watched her sword finally be dragged from her grip by sheer weight of enchanted links. He saw her go down to one knee, saw blood sheeting darkly from a gash above her eye, saw the captain raise his hand for the blow that would end her —

And Ivo ran *toward* her.

Later he could never reconstruct the choice. There wasn't one. There was simply the fact of Sera Voss kneeling in the frost with her blood on the grey leaves and the certainty, absolute as arithmetic, that the world without her in it was not a world he intended to inhabit. He crossed the clearing with his hands outstretched and screaming words he half-knew from the archive pages, the old covenant-language, and everything the hunters had bound — chains, wards, the masking-spells on their faces, even the enchantment stiffening their boots — came undone around him in a widening ring like ice breaking off a pond in spring.

Masks fell. Wards guttered. Six chains lay dead in the bracken, plain rusted iron.

The hunters themselves he left untouched — he didn't know how to unbind a person and didn't want to learn — but they stumbled backward anyway, staring at him with naked human faces suddenly stripped of anonymity, and what was in those faces was not courage.

"Go," Ivo gasped at Sera.

She was already moving. She hauled him upright by the collar with a strength that seemed impossible from a bleeding woman, and they ran — downhill, crosswise, crashing through bracken while behind them the captain's voice cracked across the clearing: *"After them! He's worth more than the whole border province — AFTER THEM!"*

* * *

**Sera**

Sera counted her wounds the way she'd been trained to: later, never during. During was for the running.

She took them off the obvious lines — down the streambed first, then out of it on bare stone, then through a stand of deadfall so dense they had to crawl. Behind them, horns sounded, doubled, trebled, but the sound grew confused, spreading, the hunters splitting to sweep. Good. Sweeping took time. Time was the only ally they had.

Ivo lasted longer than she expected him to. That was the thing about him that kept undoing her assumptions — the scribe's soft hands and the scholar's stoop, and underneath it a stubbornness like bedrock. He crawled when she crawled, froze when she froze, breathed through his nose the way she'd taught him on the third day out, slow and silent, even when his lungs must have been on fire.

But she was watching his hands. From the moment they'd gone to ground in the hollow beneath the deadfall, she'd been watching his hands.

They wouldn't close properly. The fingers lay curled half-open against the frozen earth, trembling with a fine continuous shiver, and the skin was grey — not frost-grey, a deeper wrongness, the colour of a wick burnt to its socket. Unbinding that many spells at once, that fast, that violently — he'd poured himself through the seams of the Regent's magic like water through a cracked dam.

"How bad?" she asked, because lying to each other was one luxury they'd never permitted.

"I can't feel my fingers." His voice was steady. His eyes weren't. "Or my arms. Or — Sera, I think I might be seeing two of you."

"That's flattering, at least."

"Don't make jokes." A ghost of a smile flickered across his mouth anyway, and died. "How far to the river?"

"Two days. If the ford's still standing, three to the shrine road beyond it." She tore a strip from her cloak hem — the cloak was ruined anyway; the chain had opened her shoulder and the cold was getting in — and began wrapping his hands, gently, the way she'd wrap her own sword-hand after a long drill. "You should have run."

"I know."

"When I tell you to run, you run."

"I know." He looked at her then, and the fever was starting to build behind his eyes, she could see it, a brightness that had nothing to do with health. "But you were going to die. And I found that I — that I couldn't—"

He stopped. So did she. The hollow beneath the deadfall was very small, their shoulders touching, her fingers still wrapped around his ruined hand, and the silence between the unfinished sentence and its unspoken ending was the loudest thing Sera Voss had heard since the stars went out.

*Couldn't,* she thought. *Couldn't watch. Couldn't bear it. Couldn't—*

She finished binding the bandage and said, gruffly, "Good instincts. Terrible discipline."

"Says the oathbreaker."

"Says the *disgraced* oathbreaker. There's a difference. Disgrace means I was right and got punished for it." She settled back against the deadfall trunk, positioning herself between him and the direction of pursuit out of pure reflex. "Sleep if you can. You'll need — "

"I'm not going to be able to do anything for days, am I."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't insult him with a lie. "Days," she confirmed. "Maybe four. Your talent doesn't draw on the wards like a knight's does — it draws on *you*. You emptied yourself out there." She hesitated, then said the rest, because he deserved the rest. "If they find us before you recover, I can't hold six again. Not chained to protecting you."

"Then don't protect me."

"Shut up, Ivo."

A weak laugh escaped him, half-delirious already. "Yes, Ward-Knight Voss."

"Not a ward-knight anymore."

"Yes you are," he murmured, and his eyes were closing, and his voice was going soft and slurred at the edges, sliding down into the fever like a man sinking into warm water. "That's the whole problem. That's — you're the most — you keep *duty* like other people keep — like a candle in — "

He slept. Mid-word, mid-thought, gone.

Sera sat in the grey half-dark with his bandaged hand still in hers and let herself, for exactly ten measured breaths, feel what she was not permitted to feel.

Then she checked her sword, checked the wind, and began planning how to carry a grown man three days through hostile country alone.

* * *

**Ivo**

The fever made the days into one long grey corridor, and he walked it in fragments.

He dreamed of the temple scriptorium, of ink moving under his pen in patterns no master had taught him, of the night the stars vanished — and the dreams kept folding into the present until he couldn't separate them. He dreamed Sera was fighting chains in a starless clearing forever. He dreamed his ancestors' handwriting peeling off the vellum and wrapping around his wrists like manacles, and a voice made of many voices asking him, patiently, *why do you resist what you were made for?*

Between the dreams, there were islands. Waking islands. He learned to recognize them by certain fixed points: the taste of willow bark tea, bitter enough to make his eyes water, pressed to his lips by fingers more careful than he'd ever imagined Sera Voss capable of being. The smell of woodsmoke — real woodsmoke, pitch-free, safe — from a fire built in a dugout so shallow its light would never show. The sensation of being moved, slung over her shoulders like a sack of grain, her heartbeat steady against his side even after whatever she was carrying became, unmistakably, heavier than any woman should carry alone for hours over broken ground.

Once, surfacing near dawn, he heard her talking to him. Not the brisk field-voice she used when he was conscious. Something lower. He caught only the last of it:

"—and I swore I'd never watch another person destroy themselves for something bigger than they were. So don't. Do you hear me? Whatever the archive says your blood is *for* — you get a vote now. You get to choose. That's the whole point of everything we're doing."

He tried to answer. His mouth wouldn't cooperate.

On the fourth day, he woke for good.

He knew it was for good because the world stayed still when he opened his eyes. He was lying in the lee of a fallen oak, bundled in both their cloaks, and Sera sat three feet away cleaning her sword with the mechanical thoroughness of someone who has done nothing else for a long time. The light through the canopy was grey but less wrong than he remembered. His hands, when he flexed them, obeyed. They ached like healing burns, but they *obeyed*.

"Four days," he rasped.

"Four days." She didn't look up from the blade. "River's half a day east. Ford's intact. We lost them, or they lost us, which amounts to the same thing for about a week."

"You carried me."

"You weigh less than my armor kit."

"You carried me for three days."

Her hands stilled on the whetstone. For a moment the only sound was the wind moving restlessly through the grey leaves, and Ivo understood that she was deciding something — deciding whether to let him have seen it, all of it, the tea and the talking and the terrible stubborn tenderness of her.

"Drink your tea," she said at last, and handed him the cup, and that was the closest either of them came to naming it. Some things, he was learning, were true precisely because no one said them aloud.

He drank. The bitterness grounded him in his body, in the ache and the cold and the astonishing, ordinary fact of being alive. Then he reached into the satchel and drew out the archive pages, and his breath caught.

The vellum had changed.

Where four days ago the ancestral script had lain flat and inert, now faint marks glimmered between the old lines — new writing, silver-pale, appearing stroke by stroke like frost forming on a window. Not his ancestors' hand. Something answering them. Or answering *him*.

"Sera."

She was beside him instantly, sword forgotten. They stared together as the pale letters gathered themselves into words, in a language he shouldn't have known and read effortlessly, the way he'd read the covenant-texts in the mountain archive — the language of the Binding itself.

*The Stone is broken,* the silver writing said. *The anchor is dead. But the covenant remembers its founders, and the founders' blood walks toward Us.*

"Us," Sera repeated softly. Her face had gone the colour of the frost. "Not 'the capital.' Not 'the palace.'"

Ivo traced the last line with an unbandaged finger, and the moment he touched it, the final phrase shimmered and rewrote itself, the silver script rearranging like a living thing — like something patient, and aware, and *glad*.

*Come home, little scribe,* the page said. *We have kept your parents' room exactly as you left it.*

The wind moved through the Ashwood, and every grey leaf shivered at once, and somewhere far to the north, so faint it might have been imagination, a horn blew — one note only, sweet and welcoming, like an invitation to a feast.

Sera's hand found his. Neither of them looked away from the page.

"We don't have to go," she said.

"We were always going to go," Ivo said. "It knows my parents are alive, Sera. Or it wants me to believe they are." He folded the page closed with hands that only slightly trembled. "Either way — now I have to know."
