## 9. Fever and Confession

The fever came at dusk on the third day after the Ashwood, and by midnight it had teeth.

Sera Voss had seen backlash before. Every ward-knight saw it eventually — a binder who reached too deep into a working and pulled something back that did not belong in flesh. It was a hazard of the trade, taught in the second year of training alongside diagrams of the body's channels and warnings delivered in a bored monotone: *the unbound power will seek a vessel; do not be its vessel.* She had watched a senior knight burn out his own gift once, screaming at shadows no one else could see.

She had never watched anything like this.

Ivo lay on the pallet she'd made for him in the abandoned shepherd's hut, and he was *glowing* — not metaphorically, not in the way poets meant when they spoke of fever-bright cheeks. Faint threads of light moved beneath his skin like fish under ice, silver-blue, tracing paths along his arms, his throat, the hollow of his collarbone. Where they passed, the old scars from the border crossing flared white and then faded, as if something inside him were rewriting him stitch by stitch and getting it wrong.

"Unbinding chains," Sera muttered to herself, wringing out the cloth in the basin for what felt like the hundredth time. "Of course. Of course you couldn't just *run*."

He had torn apart the hunters' bindings — six enchanted chains, each one anchored to a crown-writ sigil that should have taken a trained binder an hour to unpick safely. He had done all six in under a minute, one after another while arrows sang past his head, because she was fighting three hunters alone and he had decided, in his infuriating, debt-blind way, that her life was worth more than his own strength.

And now the power he'd borrowed from the breaking had nowhere to go. It sat in him like swallowed lightning, looking for doors.

"Ivo." She pressed the cool cloth to his forehead and he arched into it, gasping, eyes rolling beneath closed lids. "Ivo, listen to my voice. You're in a shepherd's hut. You're safe. The hunters are two days behind us and losing ground."

He wasn't listening. He was somewhere else, and whatever he saw there made his hands claw at the blanket.

Sera caught his wrists — carefully, so carefully, because touching a fevered binder's skin could pull her into the backlash's orbit, and she had already spent enough of her own wards dampening his thrashing that her arms ached down to the bone. "Hey. Hey. Nakamura. Look at me."

His eyes opened. For one terrible moment they were wrong — pupil-less, filled with that same silver light, reflecting a sky that had no stars in it — and then they were only his again, brown and frightened and *present*.

"Sera," he rasped. His voice sounded like paper tearing. "It's — there are words in me. All the ones I took apart. They're trying to put themselves back together and they're using *me* as the mortar—"

"Then don't be mortar." She kept her voice level the way she'd been trained to, the calm that was drilled into ward-knights until it outlived every other virtue. "Push them out. You unbind things. Unbind yourself."

"I can't—" A shudder went through him so violent the pallet frame knocked against the stone wall. "I can't find the edges. Everything is — everything is *edges*, Sera, why didn't anyone tell me magic was all *edges*—"

"You're rambling."

"You're beautiful when you're worried." He said it with the loose, guileless honesty of the very ill, and then his eyes widened in horror at himself, and then the fever dragged him back under before either of them could survive the moment.

Sera sat very still.

The fire crackled. Rain had started outside, drumming on the sod roof, and through the gap where the door hung crooked she could see the sky — that sky, the starless one, black as spilled ink from horizon to horizon. Three weeks since the night the stars vanished, and she still caught herself looking up expecting them, still felt each absence like a tooth missing from her jaw.

She looked down at Ivo instead. Sweat had plastered his dark hair to his forehead. The light under his skin pulsed slow and arrhythmic, like a heart learning to beat.

"You're going to live," she told him firmly, because saying it aloud made it a fact she had to defend. "You're going to live because I have carried you over half the Ashwood on my back, and I refuse to have done that for a corpse."

He mumbled something that might have been agreement.

* * *

She kept the vigil the way she'd been taught to keep watch on the wall: divided into watches, each with its task. First watch: cool cloths, water forced between his lips drop by drop. Second watch: checking the backlash channels, pressing two fingers lightly to the pulse-points where the light ran strongest and counting whether it slowed or quickened. Third watch: hers alone, the long dark hours before dawn, when there was nothing left to do but sit with her sword across her knees and let the silence ask her questions she didn't want to answer.

It was during the third watch, on the second night, that she began talking to him.

She hadn't planned to. But the hut was small and the silence was enormous, and the fever had burned away whatever pride usually stood between her thoughts and her mouth. And he was asleep. Sleeping men heard nothing. That was the whole point — that was the *mercy* of it.

"The first person I loved that the Order destroyed," she said, apropos of nothing, to a man who could not hear her, "was named Wren Calloway."

The name came out of her like a splinter coming free — painful, sudden, followed by a strange lightness.

"She was my instructor. Ward-theory. Third-year cadets." Sera fed another stick into the fire, watching the sparks climb toward the smoke hole and die. "Everyone thought she was cold. She had this way of looking at you like she was already grading your failure. But she stayed late every evening helping the ones who struggled, and she never once reported a cadet for minor infractions even though it cost her advancement, and when I froze during my first live ward-collapse drill — completely froze, wet myself in front of the whole class — she walked me out afterward and said, 'Voss, fear means your mind works. Panic means it doesn't. You panicked for four seconds and then you worked. That's better than most.' Nobody had ever said anything kind to me like that. Like it was *data*. Like kindness was just an accurate observation she happened to make."

Ivo shifted on the pallet. The light under his skin flickered. Sera paused, watching, but it steadied.

"She found the corruption first," she went on, quieter. "Before I did. Skimmed ward-stones being sold off the northern line, protection contracts going to houses that paid rather than qualified, entire villages' wards maintained on paper and nothing else. She wrote it up. Filed it properly, through proper channels, because she believed — she genuinely believed — the Order was worth trusting with the truth." Sera's hand had curled into a fist without her noticing. She opened it deliberately, finger by finger. "Three months later she was reassigned to a survey post in the Glass Reach. Six months after that, her survey team reported her dead in a rockslide."

The rain intensified, hammering the sod roof.

"There was no rockslide in the Glass Reach that season. I checked. Years later, I checked everything. Weather records, survey logs, supply requisitions. Her team's rations were drawn for five people and returned unused for four. Do you understand? Someone drew her share and *no one ate it*. They knew before the report ever came down the mountain." Sera laughed, and hated how thin the laugh was. "I was nineteen. What was I going to do? So I did nothing. I did nothing for eight years, and I got very good at doing nothing — promotions, commendations, a reputation for incorruptibility, which is the Order's favorite joke, promoting the blind — and then I found the same signature on new contracts. The same pattern, larger. And I thought about Wren Calloway drawing rations for a dead woman, and I thought, *not again, not this time, not while I'm breathing,* and I filed my report."

She looked at him. His face in the firelight was slack, young, the lines that worry carved into it smoothed away by fever until he looked like what he probably would have been without the debt and the temple and the valley walls: a scholar. An ordinary man who liked books.

"And it destroyed me instead," she said. "That's the part nobody tells you. Wren got a rockslide. I got exile dressed up as a posting. 'Border duty, Voss, we need our best people watching the valley.' Watching the valley. Watching *you people* copy your ledgers behind your anti-magic walls while the rest of us pretended the realm wasn't rotting from the crown down." She shook her head slowly. "I used to hate this assignment. I want you to know that. First week here I wrote a letter requesting transfer. Never sent it. Couldn't articulate why. And then the stars went out, and the wards fell, and I arrested a scribe in a mud-spattered temple robe, and within two days I understood that the Order had posted me at the exact place where the answer to everything was sleeping. Maybe they feared it. Maybe they served it. I don't know which is worse."

Ivo stirred. His lips moved. She leaned close, and heard him whisper, "...the edges..."

"You'll find them," she said softly. "You found them in the Ashwood. You'll find them again."

She settled back against the wall, sword across her knees, and discovered that the splinter was out and the wound underneath wanted air.

"Here's the thing I never told anyone, Ivo. Not the tribunal, not the inquiry board, not myself, really, if I'm honest." Her voice dropped almost to nothing, swallowed by rain and fire-crackle. "I didn't report the corruption because it was corrupt. Plenty of people knew and slept fine. I reported it because they killed *her*, and I needed the world to make one piece of sense — just one — where caring about someone wasn't a fatal error." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "And then you come along. Debt-bound, half-trained, self-sacrificing to the point of pathology, taking apart cursed chains with your bare talent while I'm thirty feet away and can't reach you. And I stand there in that grey forest thinking: *this is the error. This is exactly it. You're doing it again, Voss. You're letting someone matter.*"

The fire popped. Sparks rose and died.

"And I can't stop it," she said. "I've fought worse things than a feeling. I've fought the Regent's hunters with a broken shield strap and no sleep. This is harder. This is *so much harder*, because you keep looking at me like I'm someone worth trusting, and I don't know how to be trusted, I only know how to be *useful*, and those aren't the same thing, and you deserve —"

Her voice cracked. She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter, the confession pared down to its barest bone:

"You deserve someone whose love isn't built on a grave. And I don't know yet if I'm that person. But I know that when you fell in the Ashwood, everything in me went quiet except one thought, and the thought was *not him, please, anyone but him.* And I've said my oaths to the Order and I've said them to the crown and I've said them to Wren Calloway's memory, and none of those oaths ever scared me the way that one sentence scared me. Because it wasn't duty. It was just... true."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like finishing a chore.

"So get well," she told him. "Get well, and forget every word of this, and we'll go to the capital and save the king and the sky, and I will never mention any of it. That's the arrangement. That's what vigils are for — saying the unsayable to the unreachable."

On the pallet, Ivo Nakamura breathed slow and deep, and the silver light beneath his skin had begun, very gradually, to move in patterns instead of chaos — spirals, repetitions, the ghost of grammar returning to a language that had been screaming.

If his fingers curled slightly, there in his sleep, toward the sound of her voice — the firelight was unsteady, and she chose not to look closely enough to know.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the smoke hole. The fever broke an hour after sunrise, the way fevers do — all at once, like a tide going out, the light sinking beneath his skin and leaving him pale, soaked, and human.

He slept then, truly slept, dreamless, and Sera finally let herself doze in a chair with her sword within reach, waking at every small sound, unable to help it, unwilling to stop.

Near midday his eyes opened. He looked around the hut slowly, cataloguing: the basin, the pile of wrung cloths, the burnt-down candles, her.

"How long?" he croaked.

"Three days."

"Three—" He pushed himself up on trembling arms, and she moved to help him before she could decide not to, her hand at his back, steadying. He stared at her. "You stayed. The whole time?"

"It's what ward-knights do. We keep watch." She said it lightly, and it was mostly true, and the mostly was a country she hoped he'd never map.

Something moved behind his eyes — something he'd carried back from wherever the fever had taken him, some fragment that hadn't burned away. He opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again.

"Sera. When I was under. There was — I heard..." He frowned, chasing it, and she felt her heartbeat go slow and heavy, a drum before a gate. "There was a voice. Reading. Like a ledger entry. Something about — ration counts? Five drawn, four returned?" He rubbed his eyes. "That's nonsense. Fever-nonsense."

"Fever-nonsense," she agreed, and her voice did not shake, and it was the finest piece of ward-work she had ever performed, holding that single syllable steady.

But he was still watching her, and Ivo Nakamura had spent his life reading texts others thought sealed, and something in his gaze said he had just brushed his fingers across a page written in a hand that trembled.

Whatever he might have said next was cut short — hooves. On the ridge above the hut, many horses, moving fast. Sera was on her feet with her blade drawn before conscious thought caught up, pulling Ivo off the pallet and behind her, because the riders cresting the hill wore no livery of any house she knew.

They wore lanterns at their belts. Dozens of them, lit at noon, burning with that thick amber glow the capital had begun hoarding since the stars died — and on their banners, where the king's crowned hawk should have been, there was only a smooth black circle.

An open eye, reversed.

"They've stopped hunting us," Sera breathed, backing toward the rear wall, calculating distances she already knew were impossible. "They were never hunting us. We're not fugitives anymore, Ivo. We're *summonsed*."

At the head of the riders, a woman in crown-black raised a scroll sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and called down in a voice like polished stone:

"Sera Voss. Ivo Nakamura. In the Regent's mercy, you are invited home."
