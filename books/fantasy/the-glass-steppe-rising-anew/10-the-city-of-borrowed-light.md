## 10. The City of Borrowed Light

They saw the capital three days before they reached it.

Sera spotted it first from the crest of a hill where the road bent around a dead orchard — a smudge of gold on the horizon, like an ember refusing to die. She said nothing at first, only stopped walking and stood with her hand shading eyes that had gone very still. Ivo had learned to read her silences the way he once read temple script: carefully, line by line, alert for what was written between them.

"That's it?" he asked.

"Aurelith." The name came out of her flat and heavy, a stone dropped into still water. "The Lantern City."

From the hilltop it did look like a city made of lanterns. But as they descended through the next two days of travel — past fields gone strangely tidy, past villages whose inhabitants watched them pass without curiosity, past wayshrines stripped of their old symbols and hung instead with unlit candles — the light resolved into something else. Something that sat wrong in Ivo's chest the closer they came, the way a chord can sit wrong before you understand why.

It was hoarded light. That was the wrongness. Every glow in Aurelith came from glass and oil and flame, thousands upon thousands of lanterns, and none of it rose freely into the sky. It was caged light, banked light, light that had been gathered and guarded like grain against famine. From a distance the city glittered like a fallen constellation pinned to the earth, and Ivo understood, with a scribe's instinct for the meaning beneath the words, that this was precisely the point. Someone had arranged it to look that way.

"They're mimicking the stars," he said quietly, on the last morning, as the road joined a river of other travelers all moving toward the gates.

"Of course they are." Sera had pulled her knight's tabard inside-out days ago; now she wore the grey of a mercenary escort, and she kept her hood up even though the sky offered nothing to hide from. "When the lights went out, people didn't grieve. Grief takes time. Fear is faster." Her jaw tightened. "Someone gave them something to worship instead."

* * *

The gates of Aurelith were wide enough to admit six carts abreast, and they never closed. Ivo had expected queues, inspections, the nervous scrutiny of a city under siege. Instead the guards at the gatehouse were smiling. That was the first horror: the smiles. They were warm, practiced, identical — each guard greeting travelers with the same uplifted cadence, *Welcome home, welcome to the Cleansed City, may the Empty Sky keep you.*

Inside the walls, the city opened like a flower made of flame.

Lanterns everywhere. Lanterns on iron posts along every avenue, lanterns strung between rooftops on silver wire, lanterns floating on the canal barges, lanterns stacked in pyramids in the public squares so tall they needed scaffolding. Glassblowers' districts glowed day and night. Oil carts rolled through the streets in procession, and children ran alongside them not begging for bread but for *light*, holding up little wicks to be dipped, cradling the small flames back to their doorways like chicks returned to a nest.

And above it all, the sky. Starless. Not clouded — clouded would have been bearable, would have been merely weather. This was a clean, polished, absolute black, a ceiling of lacquered nothing, and the whole city turned its face toward it the way sunflowers turn toward the sun.

"It's beautiful," Ivo said, and hated himself for saying it.

"It's meant to be," Sera murmured. "That's how you know it's a lie worth fearing. Truth doesn't usually bother to be beautiful."

They found lodging in a caravanserai near the Tallow Quarter, paying for a room whose window faced inward toward the courtyard rather than outward toward the street. Sera counted out coins with the economy of someone who had learned exactly how long money must last and no longer than that. Ivo unpacked his scribe's kit — the one thing he had carried across half the world without question, the way another man might carry a heartbeat — and tried to make sense of what he'd seen since the gates.

Every wall bore the same proclamation, printed in bold black type on white paper, pasted over older proclamations, pasted over older ones still, layers deep like the sediment of some geological event:

*THE STARS ARE TAKEN. THE SKY IS CLEANSED. WHAT WATCHED US FROM ABOVE HAS BEEN REMOVED BY THE GRACE OF THE CROWN. REJOICE, CHILDREN OF THE EMPTY HEAVEN, FOR NOTHING LOOKS DOWN ON YOU NOW.*

Beneath the words, always, the same image: a crown rendered in simple lines, hovering in a field of black, radiant with nothing.

"The Regent did this," Ivo said. He was standing at the window — the inward-facing one — but he could hear the street anyway, the hymns. People sang in the evenings here. He'd heard three different melodies already, all praising the same thing. "It ate the stars and told them it was mercy."

"It ate the stars and *made them grateful*." Sera sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. She did that when she was thinking; he'd begun to catalog her habits the way he catalogued everything, helplessly, compulsively, because attention was the only prayer he knew. "I've seen famine do this. Plague. I stood in cities after the Second Grey Winter and watched people thank the crown for soup while the granaries sat full behind locked doors. Hunger makes believers faster than hope ever could. Take away the thing people trusted most and then hand them a candle—" she flicked the knife closed "—and they'll build you a cathedral around it."

"But they *saw* the stars. Their whole lives. How does anyone believe the sky was better empty?"

"You're asking the wrong question." She looked up at him then, and in the borrowed lamplight her face was all angles and exhaustion and something underneath both that she never let him look at directly. "You grew up in a valley that told you magic was impossible, and you believed it. You copied ledgers for a debt you never chose, and you called it duty. People don't believe what's true, Ivo. They believe what keeps the pain quiet. The Regent understood that before it ever opened its mouth."

He wanted to argue. He found, turning it over, that he couldn't.

* * *

They went out at dusk, because Sera wanted to see the squares, and Ivo went because he could not have stayed behind if she'd ordered him to.

The Grand Concourse was the largest square in the city, or had been — now it was less a square than a congregation. Ten thousand people, perhaps more, standing shoulder to shoulder in the gathering dark, faces tilted upward. At the center rose the Lantern Spire: a tower of tiered glass, forty feet high, burning with what the heralds claimed was the Last Light of the final star, captured and preserved by the Crown's grace on the night of the Cleansing. Whether it was true or not hardly mattered. The crowd believed it the way water believes in downhill.

A speaker stood on a platform near the spire's base, robed in white, voice amplified by some acoustic trickery of shaped stone. Ivo, who had spent his life among texts, heard the liturgy immediately — the call-and-response structure, the fixed phrases, the rhythm engineered to carry a crowd past thought into unison.

*"What did the sky hold?"*

**"EYES!"**

*"And what do eyes do?"*

**"WATCH! JUDGE! TAKE!"**

*"And what has the Crown given us?"*

**"AN EMPTY HEAVEN AND A FULL LAMP!"**

The response crashed over the square like surf. Beside him, Ivo felt Sera go rigid. He glanced at her and saw her staring not at the crowd but at a knot of young men near the platform — wearing grey armbands, moving through the throng with purpose, stopping here and there to speak to individuals. Most conversations ended with the individual nodding quickly. One did not. An old woman, small and stooped, shook her head at whatever the armbands asked, and one of them leaned close and said something Ivo could not hear, and the old woman's face changed — collapsed, really, like a house whose beams are pulled — and she raised her own arm and pointed, trembling, toward a doorway across the square.

Two armbands went. They came back leading a man between them, middle-aged, unresisting, weeping without sound. The crowd parted for them smoothly, without comment, without even much looking. The man was walked to the edge of the concourse and down a side street, and the hymn resumed as though a note had merely been held a moment longer.

"What just happened?" Ivo asked. His mouth had gone dry.

"Confession," Sera said. Her voice was very quiet and very controlled, which he had learned was worse than shouting. "Or reporting. In a healthy city those are different things." She turned, took his sleeve — not his hand, never quite his hand, though her grip lingered a half-second past necessity — and drew him back into the shadow of an arcade. "We're leaving the square. Now. And tomorrow we stop looking like strangers. Strangers get pointed at."

* * *

That night Ivo could not sleep. He lay on the narrow bed listening to the city sing itself to sleep — the hymns fading, the watch-criers calling the hours in that same uplifted cadence, *All is well under the Empty Sky, all is cleansed, all is kept* — and he did what he always did when the world refused to make sense. He wrote.

By lamplight, in the back pages of his ledger, he recorded the day the way he'd record any document: the wording of the proclamations, the structure of the liturgy, the number of lanterns he could count from the window (four hundred and eleven visible, though the true number was surely ten thousand times that), the description of the man led away weeping. Scribe's discipline. Hold the world still long enough to copy it, and eventually it will tell you what it means.

But halfway through, his pen slowed, and stopped, and he sat looking at what he had written without seeing it, because what he was actually seeing was the valley.

Marrowmere. The wards that had hummed invisibly over every field and fencepost his whole life. The elders who had told him the world beyond the border was chaos and hunger and sorcery, and who had been right about the sorcery and wrong about everything else, and who had known — hadn't they? Some of them must have known — that the wards were not protection but suppression, that they were built to cage a gift in a boy copying ledgers, that the whole valley was a vault with a child locked inside it and told the lock was love.

*People don't believe what's true,* Sera had said. *They believe what keeps the pain quiet.*

He looked at the proclamation text he'd transcribed. *REJOICE, CHILDREN OF THE EMPTY HEAVEN.* And he thought: I could have written this. That was the thought that finally frightened him, the cold one that slid under all the others. Not that these people were fools, not that they were deceived — but that the machinery of it was legible to him. The repetition, the ritual, the enemy named and removed, the comfort distributed nightly in small glass portions. He understood it the way a locksmith understands a lock. Given a desk, an inkwell, and twenty years, he could have built this faith himself, stroke by careful stroke, and the people singing in the streets would have sung his words with tears of gratitude running down their faces.

Fear becomes faith, he wrote at the bottom of the page, because faith gives fear somewhere to live. And whoever builds the house owns everyone sheltering in it.

Then, because the honesty demanded it, he added one more line, smaller:

*I left my valley believing I was free. I am not certain yet that I know the difference between escaping a lie and being recruited by a larger one.*

He blew out the lamp and lay in the dark, and the dark here was not dark at all — light bled under the door, seeped around the shutters, the city too afraid of its own sky to ever fully close its eyes. Even sleep, in Aurelith, was conducted by lantern-light. Even dreams were borrowed.

* * *

Near dawn, boots on the stair. Two sets, unhurried, stopping at doors along the corridor. A knock. Muffled voices. Another knock, further off, then another, closer.

Ivo was out of bed and through the connecting door before his mind finished forming the thought, and found Sera already awake, already dressed, knife open in her hand, finger to her lips. Through the wall, a voice — pleasant, official, the same uplifted cadence — asking questions of their neighbor. Words drifted through the plaster like smoke through cloth.

"...census of the gifted... by decree of the Crown... all binders, hedge-mages, charm-wrights, and unbinders are summoned to the Palace of Lanterns for registration and honorable service... failure to present oneself within three days will be construed as ingratitude toward the Cleansing..."

Registration. Honorable service. The summons Sera had predicted at the waystation, the bait laid out in the open now, delivered door to door with smiles. Three days.

The boots moved on. The knock came at the room beside theirs, then receded down the stair, out into the streets where the criers were already calling the new decree to the waking city, and Ivo stood in the grey pre-dawn light with Sera's shoulder almost touching his, both of them listening to the city learn its orders.

"Three days," he whispered.

"Three days," Sera agreed. Her eyes were on the door, but her voice, when it came again, was aimed at something further — at the palace rising above the rooftops in the distance, crowned in hoarded light, waiting. "Which means tonight we find out who else in this city refuses to be grateful."
