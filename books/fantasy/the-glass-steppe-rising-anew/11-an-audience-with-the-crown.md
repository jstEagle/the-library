## 11. An Audience With the Crown

The summons arrived on vellum the color of old teeth, and Ivo's hands shook so badly he nearly dropped it.

*By decree of His Radiant Majesty, all gifted binders within the capital's bounds are commanded to present themselves at the Lantern Gate by the third bell of morning. Refusal is heresy against the Cleansed Sky. Compliance is salvation.*

The seal was pressed in black wax, and where a royal crest should have been there was only a smooth depression, as if something had eaten the sigil out of the middle.

"They're not looking for binders," Sera said, reading over his shoulder. Her breath stirred the hair at his temple. "They're looking for *you*. The courier at the waystation said the conscription lists were drawn before we ever crossed the border. Before the wards fell, maybe." She took the letter from him with careful fingers, the way one handles a snake that might yet be sleeping. "Which means somewhere in that palace, something wrote your name down long ago."

"It didn't write my name." Ivo's voice came out thinner than he intended. "It wrote my ancestor's name. You heard what the archive said. The Nakamura line *made* the binding. If the thing wearing the crown wants to finish devouring the sky, it doesn't need just any binder."

He didn't say the rest of it. He didn't have to. They both understood: if the Regent wanted to unmake the Great Binding, it needed the blood that had made it — or someone close enough to bleed.

"So we walk through the front door," Sera said, "of the thing that has been hunting us across half a kingdom."

"Yes."

"And you're smiling."

Ivo realized she was right. It felt strange on his face, this small, terrified curve of the mouth. "It's been waiting for me my whole life, Sera. Every wall in that valley, every rule, every ledger I copied by candlelight — I think they were all built to keep me from exactly this door. I'm tired of being kept from doors."

She looked at him for a long moment, and in the borrowed lantern-light of the safehouse cellar her eyes were the grey of storm-water over stone. Then she reached up and straightened the collar of his borrowed coat — a binder's coat, stolen from a dead man whose guild badge they had pried off and re-pinned — with a tenderness that contradicted everything else about her.

"Then we go through it together," she said. "And if anything moves wrong in there, you run. You don't argue. You don't look back. You *run*."

"You know I won't."

"I know," she said quietly. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

The Lantern Gate was aptly named. Ten thousand lanterns hung from its arches and battlements, hoarded light burning in glass and paper and brass, and beneath them the queue of summoned binders shuffled forward like penitents. Ivo counted soldiers on the walls and stopped counting when he passed forty. Their armor was lacquered white, star-white, and their faces beneath the helms were carefully, perfectly blank.

"Names," said the clerk at the gate, a small man whose ink-stained fingers trembled in a way Ivo recognized. A scribe. Another debt-bound scribe, perhaps, ground down to a nub of function.

"Sera Voss," Sera said, steady as bedrock. "Ward-knight, second circle, detached duty. And Ivo Nakamura, journeyman binder, guild-certified."

The clerk's quill scratched. Then it stopped.

"Nakamura," he repeated, and looked up, and his eyes went briefly, horribly vacant — like windows with the lamps blown out behind them. For a heartbeat Ivo felt the attention of something vast pass over him, the way a hawk's shadow passes over a field mouse without slowing. Then the clerk blinked, smiled a smile that used none of his face, and waved them through.

"His Radiance will be pleased," the clerk said. "*Very* pleased."

Inside the gate, the city changed. The streets of the outer districts had been poor but alive — hawkers, children, the smell of frying onions and horse dung, the ordinary music of ten thousand lives. Here, past the inner walls, the streets were swept clean and utterly silent. Lanterns burned at every corner, more light than Ivo had ever seen gathered in one place, and still the darkness between them seemed thicker than darkness had any right to be. As though the light were not pushing the dark back but merely decorating it.

"Do you feel that?" Sera murmured. Her hand had drifted to the hilt of the sword disguised under her cloak.

"The shadows are wrong," Ivo said. "They lean."

Every shadow in the palace district leaned the same direction — toward the keep at the city's heart, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone, the way rivers lean toward the sea. Toward something that drank.

They climbed the great stair to the audience hall behind a procession of other summoned binders — eleven of them in all, Ivo noted, a number that felt chosen rather than accidental. An old woman with burn-scarred hands who kept whispering to herself. Twin brothers who walked pressed shoulder to shoulder like men wading a current. A boy no older than sixteen whose eyes were already hollowing, the light in them going out in patches, like stars.

Ivo looked away from the boy because looking at him felt like looking into a mirror held up to next week.

* * *

The audience hall of the king of Vael had been built to make human beings feel small before the crown. Vaulted ceiling lost in gloom above; pillars like a forest of stone; a floor of pale marble polished to mirror-brightness, so that everyone who walked it appeared to be walking on the sky. Once, Ivo supposed, the mirrors of the floor had reflected painted constellations on the vault above. Now the vault was bare, and the floor showed only an empty black heaven, and walking it was like walking on the wound in the world.

At the far end, on a throne of white stone, sat the king.

Kaelen Vael had been crowned at nineteen, the archives said. Coin portraits from five years ago showed a lean, quick-eyed young man with his mother's dark curls and his father's stubborn jaw. The thing on the throne wore those features the way a glove wears a hand.

He was smiling. He had been smiling, Ivo realized, since before they entered — the same smile, fixed, wide, showing too many teeth, a smile that had never once in its existence expressed joy. His eyes were open and lit by the lanterns and entirely empty, twin holes punched through the face into some deeper dark. And his shadow —

Sera's breath caught beside him. She had seen it too.

The king's shadow did not match the king. The lantern-light fell from the left; the seated figure cast its shadow to the right, correct and proper. But the shadow *moved*, slow and liquid, pooling and rising, and its shape was wrong — too tall, too many-jointed, crowned with spires of darkness that writhed like smoke given ambition. While the body on the throne sat perfectly still, the shadow leaned forward, attentive, hungry, a hound straining at a leash made of flesh.

"Hollow," Sera breathed, so softly only Ivo could hear. "Saints preserve us. It's worse than the reports. The reports said *influence*. This is occupation."

One by one, the eleven binders were called forward. The old woman first. The king — the Regent — spoke to each in a voice like warm honey poured over broken glass, gentle and resonant and wrong, asking questions about their gifts with the tender interest of a connoisseur selecting wine. Each answer seemed to please it. Each binder stepped back from the throne looking faintly dazed, smiling a little, as though a warm hand had passed through their mind and left prints.

The twins went together. The boy with the hollowing eyes went fourth, and when the Regent smiled at him — wider, Ivo thought, wider than before — the boy swayed like a candle in wind.

Then: "Ivo Nakamura."

The hall went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sound. Even the whispering old woman fell silent. Even the torches seemed to hold their breath, flames standing straight as nails.

Ivo walked the mirror-black floor alone. Sera, ranked among the binders' escorts, could only watch, and he felt her gaze on his back like a hand pressed between his shoulder blades — *run, if anything moves wrong, run* — and he kept walking, because he had promised himself he would not be kept from doors anymore.

Ten paces from the throne, the guards stopped him. Five paces. Close enough now to see that the king's skin was very slightly translucent at the temples, veins showing dark beneath like roots under snow. Close enough to smell him: cold air, old parchment, and underneath, faintly, the smell of the moment after lightning.

The Regent rose. The movement was fluid, graceful, entirely human — and that was the most horrifying thing yet, how well it wore humanity, like a scholar wearing a stolen coat.

"Ivo Nakamura," it said, and then, tilting its head with a curiosity that would have been charming on anything else alive: "No. Forgive me. That is the name you carry. But you are — let me see — *Aldric's* blood. Seventh of the line. The little scribe from the valley that forgets."

Ivo's mouth went dry. "Your Radiance knows my family?"

"Knows you?" The Regent laughed, and the laugh was beautiful, and every shadow in the hall flinched from it. "Child, I have *waited* for you. Do you know how long a sky takes to eat? How patient one must be, nibbling at the seams of a binding woven by your clever, clever ancestors?" It descended the three steps of the dais, unhurried, and its shadow flowed down ahead of it like spilled ink finding the cracks in marble. "Generations. I planted the valley's silence myself, in a manner of speaking — I whispered the fear into the ears of the founders, taught them to hide their greatest work inside a ward so dense that magic itself forgot how to exist there. A vault, yes. And a cradle. And a cage. I wanted the last Nakamura raised soft, and safe, and *suppressed*, so that when the hour came, his gift would burst out of him raw and enormous and — malleable."

It stopped before him. Up close, its eyes were not empty after all. They were full — stuffed with darkness the way a grave is full, packed down and patient.

"And here you stand," the Regent murmured, "right on schedule. Do you know, when the wards fell, I tasted the shape of your unbinding from a hundred leagues away? Like hearing a stranger hum a song your mother wrote." Its smile widened by a degree that should not have been possible. "You are going to help me finish it, Aldric's heir. Not because I will force you — oh no, coercion leaves such *scars* in a weaving — but because you will choose it. They always choose, in the end. I am very good at choices."

Every instinct Ivo possessed screamed at him to step back, to run, to put the whole burning palace between himself and this thing. Instead — because Sera was watching, because the boy with the hollowing eyes was watching, because eleven binders and a hall full of blank-faced guards were watching — he bowed. Deeply. Precisely. The bow of a debt-bound scribe before his holder.

"Your Radiance honors me," he said, and marveled at how steady his voice came out. "I am only a journeyman. Whatever service I can render the crown, I render gladly."

*Liar,* said his own heartbeat. *Liar, liar, and may it keep you alive.*

"Oh, I believe you will render it," the Regent said softly. It reached out — and Ivo could not flinch, could not move at all — and laid two cool fingers beneath his chin, tipping his face up into that packed-grave gaze. "But first, a gift. A token of welcome. Every guest in my house should carry a piece of the house with him."

Cold poured into Ivo's chest through the points of contact. Not pain — worse than pain, a *presence*, a splinter of attention lodged behind his sternum, curling comfortably into place like a cat settling in for a long sleep. Somewhere far away he heard Sera's sharp intake of breath, heard the scrape of her boot as she started forward and was caught by guards' gauntlets.

"There," the Regent said, releasing him, resuming its smile. "Now I can find you anywhere, little heir. Now we need never be strangers." It turned back toward the throne, waving one hand in gracious dismissal. "Escort the binders to the Guildhouse of the Summoned. Rest, refresh yourselves. The work begins soon — there is so much sky left, and I do hate to leave a meal unfinished."

As the guards closed around them, Ivo risked one glance back. The Regent had paused on the dais, half-turned, and for just a moment the fixed smile dropped from its face like a mask slipping — and what looked out from beneath was not malice, not hunger, but *recognition*, ancient and intimate and terribly pleased, the look of a collector finding the final piece of a set it had begun assembling centuries ago.

Its shadow, pooling on the marble behind it, formed — briefly, deliberately — the shape of a hand. Beckoning.

* * *

They did not speak until the Guildhouse door closed behind them and Sera had checked the room — walls, hearth, the seam of every shutter — with the thoroughness of a woman expecting assassins in the furniture. Only then did she turn to him, and her composure cracked down the middle.

"Show me," she said. "Now."

Ivo pulled his collar aside. Over his heart, faint as frost on glass, a mark had bloomed: a small dark sigil, intricate as a snowflake, pulsing gently in time with his pulse.

"It's a tether," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Or a leash. I can feel it when I pay attention — like a finger hooked behind my ribs. When it... when *it* thinks of me, I'll know."

Sera crossed the room in three strides and took his face in both hands, forcing him to meet her eyes, and her own were bright and furious and afraid. "Listen to me. Whatever it gave you, whatever it whispered — we cut it out. Tonight. I've seen tethers before, in the corruption cases, before my disgrace. There are counter-weaves—"

"It said it wanted me willing." Ivo covered her hands with his own. They were shaking now, now that no one was watching, now that the performance was over. "Sera, it *planned* the valley. It planned me. My whole life has been a garden it planted, and it just told me so to my face, smiling, because it's certain that knowing won't change anything. Because it's certain that in the end I'll choose what it wants." He laughed, and hated the sound. "And the worst part — the part I can't say in front of anyone — is that I understand it. All those years copying ledgers in a cage, wanting more. It offered me *more*. It knew exactly which door to hold open."

"But?"

He looked at her. At the woman who had broken her oath for the truth, crossed a starless kingdom at his side, confessed her griefs to his fevered sleep thinking he'd never hear them. He had heard them. He had never found the moment to tell her so.

"But it made one mistake," he said. "It counted everything I was kept from. It never counted what I found anyway."

Outside the shuttered window, the city of borrowed light glittered against a sky with no stars in it, and beneath Ivo's ribs, patient as a seed, the Regent's mark pulsed once — twice — like something knocking politely on the inside of a door.

And far away, through the tether, faint but unmistakable, Ivo felt it begin to speak.
