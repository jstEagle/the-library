## 12. The Thing Wearing Him

The royal library smelled of dust and something sweeter underneath — a cloying, honeyed rot, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. Ivo noticed it the moment the doors closed behind him, sealing out the lantern-glow of the corridor and the two guards who had escorted them with the smooth, unbothered courtesy of men who had never once doubted the world they served.

*Do not touch anything,* Sera had murmured as they walked. *Do not look at anyone too long. And whatever it says to you — remember that everything it offers is a hook.*

He had nodded. He was very good at nodding. Twenty-six years of debt-bound servitude in the Valley That Forgets had made him fluent in the language of lowered eyes and folded hands. But standing now beneath the vaulted ceiling of the greatest library he had ever seen — greater than the temple archive, greater than the hidden repository beneath the mountain where his ancestors' covenant slept in shattered stone — Ivo felt the old obedience curdle into something harder.

The Regent had asked for him alone.

"His Majesty wishes to examine the young binder's craft," the chamberlain had said, and Sera's hand had twitched toward her sword before she mastered it. She stood now on the far side of the library doors, beyond his sight but not, he hoped, beyond his need. The thought of her there — arms crossed, jaw set, watching the door — steadied him like a hand on his shoulder.

The shelves rose three stories high, ladders on brass rails, globes of forgotten constellations turning slowly in glass cases. Constellations. Someone had mapped the sky here, once, lovingly, in silver leaf — and every one of those silver stars was dark now, painted over or tarnished, as if the room itself had been quietly edited to match the sky outside.

"You find us changed."

The voice came from the reading alcove at the library's heart, where a single lamp burned behind a screen of amber glass. King Aldric Vael — no. Not Aldric. The man who wore Aldric's face stepped out from behind the screen, and Ivo's stomach dropped through the floor.

Up close, the wrongness was worse than it had been in the throne room. There, at distance, amid ceremony and shadow, one could almost believe the king was merely ill, merely strange. Here, in lamplight, Ivo could see what the smile was built over: a face used like a mask, its muscles moved by something that had studied human expression the way a scholar studies a dead language. The king's eyes were pale as river ice, and they did not blink when they should have. His shadow, cast long across the mosaic floor by the amber lamp, lay at an angle the lamp could not have made.

"Sire." Ivo bowed. The gesture cost him more than it once would have; his body remembered the Ashwood, remembered chains, remembered running.

"No." The Regent tilted its head, birdlike, delighted. "None of that. Not with you. You are not a courtier, Ivo Nakamura. You are *library*. You are *ledger*. You are ink under the fingernails and the smell of vellum." It drifted closer, robes whispering though the air did not stir. "I have read your file. All of it. Did you know the crown keeps records? Every bonded scribe in every valley, catalogued for three hundred years. Your grandfather's grandfather is in these shelves. Your mother's name is in these shelves."

Ivo's breath stopped.

*Your mother's name.*

He had been nine when the wardens came for his parents — no, that wasn't right either. They hadn't come for his parents. His parents had simply been gone one morning, their bed cold, their names already softening in the mouths of neighbors, and the elders had said *the wards took them, the wards provide, do not grieve what the valley cannot hold.* He had spent seventeen years telling himself it was mercy. A quiet erasure. No one suffered in the Valley That Forgets; that was the whole point of forgetting.

"Do not look so stricken," the Regent said gently. "I have not told you anything yet. I am only showing you the shape of the shelf." It gestured, and a section of shelving slid aside without being touched, revealing a narrow door of black iron banded in silver script — binding-script, Ivo realized, the same flowing characters from the archive beneath the mountain, the same hand. *His family's hand.* "Come. Kings do not beg. But kings do ask."

Every instinct Sera had planted in him screamed. He followed anyway. That was the trap of it, he understood even as his feet moved: the thing did not need to force him. It only needed to want what he wanted, and then stand beside the wanting like a courteous stranger offering directions.

Beyond the iron door was a smaller room, round, windowless, lit by a ring of candles that burned without consuming. In the center stood a desk, and on the desk lay a single ledger, bound in grey leather, its spine cracked with age.

Ivo knew that binding style. He had repaired a dozen ledgers just like it in the temple scriptorium, his fingers learning the stitching patterns before he ever understood what they were for.

"The Nakamura registry," the Regent said. "Valley births, valley bonds, valley... releases." It let the last word hang like a blade balanced on its point. "Open it. This is not a trick. Tricks are for people who need to be forced. You, I need *willing*. Willingness cannot be faked, scribe. Believe me. I have tried."

His hands were shaking. He opened the ledger.

The pages were written in the tight, beautiful hand of valley clerks across generations, and he found himself reading backward through time — entries growing older, ink browner, until the paper itself changed quality and the handwriting shifted into a style he recognized from the mountain archive: angular, severe, unmistakably his bloodline. And there, near the middle of the book, in an entry dated the year of his ninth winter:

*Nakamura, Ren and Ishi. Bond released. Anchor maintenance required. Recalled to source.*

*Recalled to source.*

Not taken. Not devoured. *Recalled.* As if they had been components all along — parts of the great machine his ancestors had built, living anchors in rotation, fed into the binding the way coal feeds a furnace. The valley had not lost them. The valley had *used* them.

"They were keepers," Ivo said. His voice sounded very far away. "They weren't exiled. They were — fuel."

"They were faithful," the Regent corrected softly. "There is a difference, though I grant it is a cruel one. Your parents understood the price of the walls you grew up inside. They went into the binding so that you could grow up safe behind it. Sentimental, really. Almost human." The Regent watched him with those ice-pale eyes, and its smile widened by a precise, measured degree. "You want to know if they still exist in there. Inside the Stone. Inside the weave. I can show you. I have stood at the heart of the Great Binding, little scribe. I have tasted it. Your mother's signature is still legible in the deep structure. Faded, but legible. Like a letter half-erased."

"And you erased the rest."

"I *ate* the rest," the Regent said, with the simple pride of a craftsman. "Stars first. Then the outer wards. Then memories — memory is only starlight folded small, did you know that? Every mind keeps its own little sky. I have been eating skies for a very long time." It circled him slowly, and its shadow circled the other way. "But the heart of the Binding has always resisted me. Founder's blood, founder's words. A lock built by your line, against things exactly like me. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"A key," Ivo said.

"A *co-author*." The Regent stopped before him, close enough that he could see there was nothing behind the king's eyes at all — no pupil-depth, no wet darkness, just pale glass all the way down. "The binding fails because it was written in blood and duty, and blood runs out and duty rots. But you — you have already rewritten a fragment of it. In the well village. In the Ashwood. Unbinding and rebinding in a single gesture, a grammar no ancestor of yours ever attempted. You don't just maintain the covenant, Ivo Nakamura. You could *improve* it. Reforge the sky-wards so they never fail again. Feed them something better than lives. Imagine it: a binding sustained by will instead of sacrifice. You of all people know how much of your life has been spent paying debts you never chose. End debt. End the feeding. Write a covenant that costs nothing and holds forever."

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it — how beautiful it was. Ivo felt the vision unfold in his chest like a page opening: a world with no valleys walled against magic, no children bound at birth, no parents recalled to source, no night sky held hostage by hunger. Everything he had chafed against since childhood, dissolved by his own hand.

And underneath the beauty, thin as wire, he heard the hook.

"And what does it cost?" he asked quietly. "Nothing holds forever for free. My ancestors learned that. What's the price of your version?"

The Regent's smile did not falter, but something behind it *leaned*, the way a wolf leans before a lunge. "Only access. Let me stand within the reforged weave — as a partner, a co-signatory. The Binding was built to exclude my kind. Build a new one that includes me, and I will never need to devour another star again. We would be colleagues, you and I. Custodians. The sky kept by the scribe and the shadow both."

"There it is," Ivo said.

"You wound me."

"You're asking me to open the lock from the inside." He closed the ledger carefully, aligning its edges, because his hands needed something orderly to do. "My family built this binding to keep things like you *out*. You're not offering partnership. You're asking the prisoner to thank the jailer and widen the bars."

For a long moment the candle-ring flickered, and the sweet rot-smell thickened, and Ivo understood that he was standing on a knife's edge of the thing's patience. Then the Regent laughed — a warm, human, terrible laugh, borrowed from some dead courtier, worn like a coat.

"Honesty. How refreshing. Yes. I want in. I have wanted in since before your valley had a name. But consider, scribe: I *will* get in eventually. I am patient and the sky is wounded and your true allies are a disgraced knight and a handful of gutter-forgotten ghosts. When I get in — and I will — would you rather I arrive through a door you shaped, on terms you wrote? Or through a wall I break?" It leaned close, and its breath smelled of extinguished candles. "Refuse, and I take the sky piece by piece and every mind in it. Accept, and perhaps we find a gentler arithmetic together. I am offering you the only version of the future in which your mother's signature survives."

*Compassion or temptation.* He could not tell where one ended and the other began, and he suspected neither could the Regent, and that was the most frightening thing of all.

"I need time," Ivo said. "To study the registry. To consult the archive texts. If I'm to rewrite a covenant, I do it properly. You of all beings should respect proper scholarship."

Something flickered across the king's borrowed face — surprise, maybe, or pleasure, or the simulation of both. "Time," it repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. Scholars need time. Take three days. The palace will host you and your knight as honored guests." Its tone cooled by a single degree. "Honored *closely* watched guests. And Ivo — one more gift, freely given, to aid your studies."

It extended its hand.

Every nerve in Ivo's body recoiled. But he thought of the ledger, of *recalled to source*, of his mother's name in faded ink, and he understood that this handshake was the real test — the thing wanted to see what he would reach for. He took the king's hand.

The world inverted.

He was falling through pages — through *language* — through corridors of light structured like grammar, vast and singing and wounded. And there, at the center of it all, wrapped in chains of his own body, he found him: a man with dark hair and a scholar's calluses, curled around a small, stubborn flame of self like a man cupping a match in a hurricane.

*Kaelen.* The true king. Not dead, not devoured — *imprisoned*, pressed into a cell no bigger than a heartbeat, and fighting. Even now, after however many months of hollowing, the flame bent toward Ivo like a plant toward sun, and a voice that was not spoken but *remembered* broke against him:

*—who— you're— the founder's— listen, LISTEN—*

And beneath the voice, images, flung desperately: a nursery. A sealed box of letters. A word — a *name* — that Kaelen pressed into Ivo's hands like contraband, screaming silently as the Regent's attention turned back inward—

Ivo tore his hand free.

He was on his knees on the mosaic floor, gasping, the candle-ring guttering, the Regent regarding him with its head tilted and its smile gone soft and speculative.

"Curious," it said. "What did you see?"

"Ledgers," Ivo lied, rising on shaking legs. "Dust. Old catalogues."

"Mm." The Regent studied him for a long, glacial moment. Then the smile returned, perfect and warm. "Three days, scribe. Read well. Choose wisely. The sky grows hungrier every night you deliberate."

Ivo bowed — the bow of a debtor, the bow of a survivor — and walked from the library with his heart hammering a single phrase against his ribs, over and over, a name wrapped in a location, a message smuggled out of a prison of flesh:

*The letters. Find the letters. I hid the truth where only a child of the founders would think to look.*

Behind him, the iron door slid shut with a sound like a page turning.

Outside, in the lantern-lit corridor, Sera Voss straightened from the wall the instant she saw his face. She didn't ask questions. She simply fell into step beside him, her shoulder brushing his, her hand resting near — not on — her sword.

"That bad?" she murmured.

"Worse," Ivo said. "Sera. I know where Kaelen hid the truth about the binding. And I think I just made the Regent curious enough to follow me."
