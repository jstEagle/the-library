## 13. Betrayal at the Lantern Gate

Sera had learned to read a city the way other soldiers read a battlefield, and Veylmark's capital — Cor Adara, the City of Borrowed Light — was screaming at her.

Not aloud. The city never said anything aloud anymore. It murmured. It glowed. Ten thousand lanterns hung from every eave and archway, hoarded against a sky that gave back nothing, and beneath all that borrowed gold the people moved with the careful hush of mourners who had decided their grief was a festival. Sera walked through it with her hood up and Ivo's sleeve gripped in her fist, and every instinct she owned told her they were already too late.

"You're hurting me," Ivo said.

"Good. It means you're paying attention." She loosened her grip anyway. "Eyes forward. Don't look at the shrines."

He looked at them anyway. Of course he did. He was a scribe; looking was his whole nature. Along the processional way, little alcoves held candles arranged in spirals, and before each one knelt citizens with their palms turned upward to the starless dark, whispering thanks. *The Cleansing*, the priests called it now. The devouring of the sky, reframed in six weeks as mercy. Sera had seen men do this before — not the kneeling, but the rewriting. Corruption in the crown worked the same way: first the lie, then the liturgy.

"They think it saved them," Ivo murmured.

"Fear is the cheapest building material there is. You can raise a cathedral out of it in a month." She steered him left, off the processional and into a lane where the lantern-light thinned and the shadows had honest shapes. "We go down tonight. The undercity has ways into the palace that the front gates don't. We rest first — you still look like something a cat dragged in twice."

"I feel fine."

"You unbound three hunters' chains in the Ashwood and didn't eat for two days afterward. You are many things, scribe, but fine is not one of them."

That earned her the ghost of his smile, tired and crooked, and she hated how much warmth it put behind her ribs. Six weeks on the road. Six weeks of shared fires and shared watches, of him asking questions no one had ever bothered to answer honestly, of her telling truths she'd sworn she'd take to her grave. Somewhere between the well-village and the Ashwood ambush, *the scribe* had become *Ivo*, and *Ivo* had become the person whose breathing she listened for across a campsite to confirm the world was still ordered correctly.

She knew what that meant. She refused, with professional discipline, to know what that meant.

They cut through the Lantern Market, where the Regent's bounty-bills papered every wall. *ALL GIFTED BINDERS SUMMONED BY ROYAL DECREE. LOYALTY IS LIGHT.* And beneath those, newer, angrier sheets: a sketch of a woman in ward-knight armor with her helm under her arm — her face, rendered by someone better with charcoal than with truth — and the word **OATHBREAKER** stamped across it in red wax.

Ivo stopped dead when he saw it.

"Keep walking," Sera said.

"That's—"

"Keep. Walking." She took his elbow and moved him along, past the bills, past a pair of city watchmen who glanced their way with the flat incuriosity of the well-fed. Her own face stared back at her from three more walls before they reached the market's edge. The likeness was good. That was the part that sat wrong in her stomach. Someone had described her carefully, recently, and in detail.

Only four people alive could have done that.

* * *

The safehouse was a lamp-oil merchant's shuttered storeroom above the Lantern Gate itself — the great southern arch where the old pilgrim road entered the city, named for the hundred lamps its masons had set into the stone three centuries ago. Now the gate's lamps burned day and night, an offering to nothing, and the storeroom above smelled of oil and dust and old rope.

Sera checked the sightlines from the window out of habit, then made Ivo sit and eat hardtack and dried plums while she laid out the map of the undercity approaches she'd bought from a smuggler with more scars than teeth.

"The cistern route comes up inside the palace's old well-house," she said, tracing it. "It hasn't been used since the last reign, which means it either works perfectly or doesn't exist. Either way, we move at the change of watch, third bell."

"And if it's a trap?"

She looked up. He'd gone quiet in that particular way he had — not afraid, exactly. Weighing.

"Then we find out together," she said, "and I get us out the way I always get us out."

"You got us out of the Ashwood by setting a fire that nearly caught *us*."

"Fire doesn't check faces." She rolled the map closed. "Sleep. Third bell."

But he didn't sleep, and neither did she. They sat in the lamplight instead, shoulder to shoulder against the wall, and after a long silence he said, "You never told me who she was."

Sera went very still. "Who."

"The one you loved. The one the order destroyed. You talked to me when I had the fever — I heard pieces. I wasn't going to ask. But if we're walking into the palace tomorrow—" he turned his head, and his eyes in the low light were steady and unbearably kind, "—I'd rather know what I'm carrying in with you."

For a moment she almost told him. Maerin's name rose in her throat like floodwater — Maerin, who had bound wards for villages the crown wanted quietly emptied, who had been tried in absentia and broken on the wheel of procedure until even her name was struck from the rolls, who had died in a border cell while Sera stood in the corridor holding a report she'd been ordered to sign and hadn't, and so had lost everything except the truth, which turned out to be worth nothing at all.

"Not tomorrow," Sera said finally. "If we live through tomorrow, I'll tell you everything. All of it. That's a promise I can actually keep."

He studied her, then nodded, and let it go, because he was Ivo, and letting people keep their secrets until they were ready was the deepest courtesy he knew.

Third bell came.

* * *

It came with boots on the stairs.

Sera was awake before the first footfall finished landing — years of border duty had burned that reflex into her bones — and she had Ivo by the wrist and the room assessed in the same breath. One stair. Two. Too heavy for the smuggler. Too many, for anyone friendly.

"Lantern," she breathed. "Kill the light."

He snuffed it. Darkness dropped over the storeroom, and in the darkness Sera drew her blade and put herself between the door and Ivo, because some geometries were older than thought.

The door didn't burst. It opened — politely, terribly — and lantern-light spilled in around a silhouette Sera knew better than her own reflection in a polished breastplate.

"Dame Voss." Captain Aldric Renne stood in the doorway in full order regalia, silver-and-white, the crest of the Wardens bright on his pauldron. His face wore the expression he'd worn at every tribunal Sera had ever attended: regretful, immaculate, and utterly without mercy underneath. Behind him filled the stairwell — six, eight, ten white-cloaked figures, crossbows leveled past their captain's shoulders. "Or should I say, *former* Dame Voss. The order prefers 'the traitor Voss.' I argued for keeping it simple."

"Aldric." Her voice came out level. She was proud of that. "Four people knew I'd come to Cor Adara. You were one of them."

"I was the one who reported the others." He stepped into the room, and his lantern found Ivo crouched against the far wall, and something hungry moved behind the captain's eyes. "And there he is. The binder of Nakamura blood. His Grace has been *very* interested in you, scribe. Very patient. Do you know how rare it is, these days, for the crown to be patient?"

"What did they give you?" Sera asked. "Reinstatement? Your border command back? Or just permission to stop pretending you ever doubted?"

Aldric sighed, genuinely sorrowful, which was the worst thing about him — he had always been able to grieve beautifully for things he himself had killed. "I gave my oath to the crown, Sera. Whatever wears it. That's what an oath *is*. You never understood that. You thought it was a contract you could renegotiate whenever your conscience got loud." He gestured, almost gently. "Come quietly. Both of you. His Grace wants the scribe unharmed. You, he says, are negotiable."

Crossbows shifted. Eight points of steel in the lantern-glow.

Sera did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the first stair creaked. Ten fighters, one narrow door, one window over a forty-foot drop onto the gate's lamp-ledge. No second exit. No time.

One variable she controlled.

"Aldric," she said, and let her voice drop into the register she'd used on green recruits and dying friends alike, warm and certain. "Look at me. Not the scribe. Me. You trained me. You know I don't surrender, and you know what happens in this room if you force it. Let him walk out that door and I'll come with you quietly. You get your prize — the traitor, delivered, branded, everything your report requires. He's nothing. A clerk. You said it yourself at the tribunal: the order doesn't waste chains on clerks."

"Sera." Ivo's voice cracked on her name. "No. *No.*"

"Quiet," she said, without turning, and hated herself with a completeness that felt almost holy.

Aldric considered. She watched him consider — the ambition and the caution wrestling behind his handsome face — and she knew she'd won the argument even before he spoke, because Aldric always took the version of victory that required the least risk.

"The scribe walks," Aldric said. "Down the stairs, into custody, gentle as lambs. Move, clerk."

"No." Ivo was on his feet now, and the air in the storeroom had begun to hum, faintly, the way it did when his gift woke — a sound like a struck glass remembering it could ring. "You don't get to trade her. You don't get to trade *anyone*. That's the whole— that's the entire rotten architecture of this kingdom, everyone trading everyone, and I am *done* being currency—"

"*Ivo.*" Sera kept her eyes on Aldric. "Listen to me. The window. The lamp-ledge, then the drainpipe, then south along the wall — the undercity entrance is the grate behind the tannery, you know the one, I showed you the map. Go now."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are, because I'm ordering you, and because staying gets us both taken, and because—" her voice betrayed her, just barely, just once, "—because someone has to finish this, and it was never going to be me. It was always going to be you. Go *home*, Ivo. Make it mean something."

The humming swelled. Two crossbowmen flinched as their bowstrings frayed visibly, fibers unraveling like rotting rope — his gift reaching out, unbinding, terrified and magnificent.

"Take him," Aldric snapped, losing patience, and the room exploded.

Sera met the first man through the door with her pommel and took his knee out on the follow-through. The second got a boot to the chest and the stairs. A crossbow loosed and the bolt grazed her shoulder, hot as a brand. She fought the way she'd taught recruits to fight — ugly, fast, economical — and it worked, it worked for eleven full seconds, which was nine seconds longer than she'd promised herself.

Then a bolt took her in the thigh, and the floor came up generous and hard, and through the roaring in her ears she saw Ivo at the window, half-through it, looking back at her with a face that was coming apart at the seams.

"*Go!*" she screamed.

He went. She saw him drop to the lamp-ledge, stumble, catch himself, vanish below the parapet — and the last thing she registered before a gauntleted fist closed on her collar was not relief but a fierce, furious gladness, sharp as the bolt in her leg: *he's out, he's out, he's out.*

Then the boots, and the dark, and the weight of many hands.

* * *

They marched her through the city at dawn, because Aldric understood theater.

Not through the back lanes — through the processional way, past the candle-shrines, past the crowds gathering for morning devotions, with her hands bound in iron and her armor stripped to the padded jack beneath and her limp made visible on purpose. A herald walked ahead reading her crimes from a scroll: desertion of post, false witness against the crown, consorting with saboteurs, and — added in fresh ink, Aldric's own flourish — *oathbreaking, the root and sum of all the rest.*

The word ran ahead of her through the streets like fire through dry grass. *Oathbreaker. Oathbreaker.* Mothers pulled children close. A shopkeeper spat. An old woman pressed a candle into a shrine niche as Sera passed, as though the sight of her were a pollution requiring immediate remedy.

At the Lantern Gate itself they stopped her, beneath the hundred lamps, and Aldric read the formal sentence in a voice pitched to carry to the far rooftops: stripped of rank, stripped of name from the rolls of the order, branded — the iron heated while the crowd watched, because fear was the cheapest building material and Aldric was a master mason.

Sera did not scream. She had decided that on the march, and she kept it, and the keeping cost her everything she had left, and it was hers.

When it was done, when the smell of her own burned flesh rose under the sweet lantern-oil stink of the gate, Aldric crouched beside her where they'd chained her to the gate's iron ring — public, pending transfer to the palace dungeons — and spoke low enough that only she could hear.

"He escaped, you know. Your scribe. Down the drainpipe, clean away." Something almost like respect crossed his face. "His Grace is displeased. Which means, Dame Voss — *former* Dame Voss — that you've just become useful again. Bait keeps longer than prisoners."

He straightened and left her there.

The crowd dispersed slowly, reluctantly, the way crowds do around suffering they've decided to enjoy. The lamps overhead burned on, indifferent, borrowed light for a borrowed faith. Sera sagged against the iron and let the pain have her, wave after wave, and in the space between waves she found herself thinking — absurdly, helplessly — of a storeroom, and hardtack, and a young man asking *who was she*, and her promise: *if we live through tomorrow.*

Well. Tomorrow had come. She had lied to him after all, in the end — the oldest lie, *go without me, I'll follow, I'll be fine* — and he would carry it the way she carried Maerin, and hate himself for surviving, and she knew it because she'd done the same arithmetic for seven years.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, at the sky that had no stars to hear her. *I'm sorry. Finish it anyway.*

Chains clinked. Boots approached — not Aldric's stride; lighter, quicker, wrong. Sera lifted her head, vision swimming, and saw a figure in a courier's leathers crouching at the edge of the lamplight, face shadowed, holding something out toward her with a hand that shook.

"You're the knight from the well village," the figure whispered. "The one who broke the hunger-ward. My sister's child drank from that well." The hand extended further — a slim iron pick, a file, a vial of something dark. "The undercity knows what happened here today. And the undercity, Dame Voss, does not forget its debts."

Beyond the gate, deep beneath the city, somewhere in the dark, Ivo Nakamura was running — alone, grieving, and heading straight for the only people in Cor Adara who might still help him.

Sera closed her bleeding hand around the pick, and began, very slowly, to work the lock.
