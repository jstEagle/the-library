## 14. The Undercity's Forgotten

The undercity smelled of wet stone and tallow and old rain that had never found a way out.

Ivo learned its geography by stumbling through it, one hand trailing the slick walls, the other pressed against the bruise on his ribs where Sera's name lived now, a constant ache he could not stop touching. Above him — impossibly far above, three levels of forgotten architecture and centuries of rubble — the capital went on with its borrowed light. He could hear it sometimes, muffled through the earth: bells that rang for a king who smiled wrong, hymns sung to an empty sky, the great machinery of a city convincing itself that darkness was holy.

Down here there were no lanterns worth hoarding. Down here the dark was honest.

He had been running for two days when the tunnel simply ended in a wall of woven scrap — cart canvas, broken shutters, strips of dyed cloth from a hundred different guilds, all of it stitched together into something that was less a door than a declaration: *someone lives here, and they have decided you cannot.* He stood before it, swaying on his feet, and understood with sudden clarity that he had no plan beyond *away*. Away from the Lantern Gate where Sera had turned to face her captors so that he would not. Away from the sound of her voice cracking on his name. Away from himself, if such a thing were possible.

A voice came through the fabric, low and unhurried. "You're bleeding on our threshold."

"I'm sorry." His own voice sounded foreign, scraped raw. "I'll go."

"Did I say go?" A pause, the rustle of someone shifting their weight. "I said you're bleeding. Those are different sentences, scribe. Come in before the patrol rounds the cistern bend. They don't come down here often, but when they do, they take anyone standing still."

The wall of scraps parted along a seam Ivo couldn't find, and a woman looked out at him. She was perhaps sixty, broad-shouldered, with a face like a riverbed — worn smooth by things that had once been violent. Her left arm ended at the elbow, the sleeve pinned and folded with neat, deliberate stitches. Her eyes moved over him the way a scribe's eyes moved over a damaged page: cataloguing damage, estimating what could be salvaged.

"You're the one they're hunting," she said. It wasn't a question. "The lantern-criers have your face memorized. *Scribe of the fallen wards, enemy of the cleansing.* They've made you into scripture already." Something flickered across her expression that might have been contempt, though not, he realized, for him. "My name is Odile. You'll eat first and answer questions after. That's the rule. Nobody answers questions hungry."

"I can't pay—"

"Pay." She said the word like she was tasting something spoiled. "Inside, scribe."

* * *

The place they took him was a drowned chapel.

That was Ivo's first thought, and his scribe's mind clung to it because it was the only familiar thing: vaulted ceilings lost in shadow, pillars furred with mineral bloom from some ancient leak, and rows of benches arranged — still arranged, after who knew how many years — facing a dais where no god sat. Someone had built fires in iron braziers between the pews. Someone had hung lines of drying laundry where votive banners must once have hung. Children's chalk drawings covered the lower stones: stick figures holding hands beneath scribbled stars.

The stars in the drawings had faces.

"There are more than forty of us," Odile said, walking him down the aisle while the community paused in its small labors to watch him pass — not hostile, merely watchful, the way people watch weather. "Some fled the conscription decrees. Some fled worse. The Regent's feeding doesn't only take memories from the sky, scribe. It takes them from people. There are those up top whose children walked past them in the street and felt nothing. And there are those down here who woke one morning with holes where twenty years used to be." She touched her truncated arm absently, an old gesture. "The city calls us the Forgotten. We decided to keep the name. It's honest."

An old man by the nearest brazier looked up as they passed. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they fixed on Ivo with terrible gentleness. "You've got a hole too," the man said. "Big one. Right through the middle."

"Tomas," Odile said quietly, a warning.

But Ivo stopped. "What do you mean?"

Tomas smiled, and the smile was kind and awful. "I forget things. That's my hole. Yours is different. Yours is a person-shaped hole, and you keep reaching into it like it still has edges." He turned back to his fire. "We all reach. That's how I know."

Ivo did not remember sitting down. But he was sitting, on a cold pew, and a girl of maybe twelve had appeared beside him with a bowl of barley soup that smelled of nothing so much as care — someone had spent their scarce salt on this, someone had skinned roots for it, and the flavor of that effort outweighed any feast he had ever copied descriptions of in temple ledgers.

"Eat," the girl said. "Odile's rules."

"What's your name?"

"Pell." She watched him take the first spoonful with open satisfaction, as though his hunger were a debt she'd personally settled. "You're the star-scribe. The one who unbinds things."

Ivo's spoon stopped. Here it was, then. The moment the shelter became a transaction, the moment his gift became his currency again, the moment he was worth keeping only for what he could do. He had known it would come. Debts always came due; that was the first law of his life, older than any ward.

"I can't unbind anything right now," he said quietly. "It nearly killed me last time. And if I tried, I'd bring every hunter in the city down on you."

Pell blinked at him. Then she laughed — a bright, startling sound in the drowned chapel. "Nobody asked. Odile said tell him nobody asked. She said he'd think we wanted something, because people like him always think that." She shrugged, matter-of-fact. "You looked like you needed somebody to not want something from you. So. Nobody wants anything."

She hopped off the pew and ran off toward the laundry lines, leaving Ivo holding a bowl of soup and a sentence that refused to fit inside any ledger he had ever kept.

* * *

They gave him a corner behind the old altar — a pallet, a hook for his coat, a candle stub of his very own. "Yours," Odile said, setting the candle down with ceremony. "Not borrowed. Not owed. Yours. You'll find we're strange about property down here. What's given is given."

For three days he waited for the other shoe. It was a habit of debt-bound men, waiting: every kindness weighed against the eventual invoice. The temple had taught him that love was arithmetic — his keep balanced against his copying, his meals against his obedience, the very roof over his head entered in a column marked *outstanding*. When Odile set aside the softest bread for him without being asked, he braced. When Tomas spent an afternoon teaching him the tunnels' true map, drawn in charcoal on slate because paper was precious, Ivo waited for the price.

"The price," Tomas said, chuckling, "is that you learn it well enough to teach Pell. She's got a better memory than me these days, but maps need two people who know them, in case one gets taken." He tapped his temple. "Holes, see? Best to pour knowledge into more vessels than one."

So Ivo taught Pell the tunnels. And Pell taught him which of the Forgotten could still laugh and which only remembered how, and which children drew stars with faces because they believed the stars had been people once — devoured ones, watching — and which drew them that way because it was prettier. He mended books for them. There were books down here, a dozen water-swollen survivors of some burned library, and the sight of them had gone through him like a hook: pages warped, ink bloomed into gray flowers, spines split. He worked on them by candlelight with paste made from flour and patience, and the community began, without discussion, to leave their few papers near his corner — letters from before, fragments of lives, things they could no longer quite read and could not bear to throw away.

"Can you fix this one?" A man named Corvin, huge and slow-spoken, held out a single sheet gone brown with damp. "It's my daughter's handwriting. I can't—" His jaw worked. "I remember her face. Mostly. The letter's the part that keeps slipping. If I can read it, I think I can keep her."

Ivo smoothed the page. The ink had run, but a scribe reads ruined text the way a mother reads a child's cry — by shape, by context, by love. He deciphered it line by line, transcribing it fresh onto clean paper in careful capitals, and when he read it aloud — *Papa, the plums came in early this year, come see before the birds do* — Corvin put his face in his enormous hands and wept without any sound at all, the way people weep who learned long ago that noise brings patrols.

Afterward, Corvin gripped Ivo's shoulder hard enough to ache and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the moment hadn't said already.

That night, Ivo lay on his pallet and stared at the dark vaulting above him and understood that something in him had quietly come apart and reassembled differently. All his life he had believed belonging was a debt structure — that you were bound to people by obligation, and obligation was the only form love reliably took. The temple had written it into his bones: *you owe, therefore you are kept; you are kept, therefore you belong.*

But these people owed him nothing. He owed them nothing. Pell had fed him because she chose to. Odile had given him a candle because giving was the law down here, the only law, and it was a law no one enforced because no one needed to. Belonging, it turned out, was not a chain. It was a hearth. You didn't inherit it and you couldn't be sold into it. You built it, stick by stick, out of soup and mended letters and chalk stars with faces, and it stood or fell by nothing but the choosing.

*I chose to run,* he thought, and the thought brought Sera's face with it, Sera turning to face the hunters with her chin up and her eyes finding his for half a heartbeat — *go* — and he had gone. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. Even here, even among the hollowed and the forgotten, that hole in him remained, person-shaped, exactly as Tomas had read it. He wondered if she was alive. He wondered if knowing would be worse than not knowing. He wondered whether love that you abandoned to save could ever be anything but a wound you carried.

"You're doing the reaching again," Tomas said from the next pew, without opening his eyes. "Stop it. Sleep. Holes heal slower when you poke them."

* * *

On the sixth day, the undercity gave him a gift he had not asked for and would never return.

It happened at the evening meal, when the whole community gathered in the nave among the braziers, and Odile stood, as she did each week, to speak the names. It was their liturgy, Ivo had learned — the only rite the Forgotten kept. One by one, they spoke aloud the names of the people they had lost: the eaten, the taken, the forgotten-by-others. Names spoken so that somewhere, in someone's living mouth, they continued to exist. Against the Regent's great devouring silence, it was a small defiance, repeated nightly: *we remember, therefore you were.*

When the names wound down and the quiet settled, Pell stood up.

"Ivo Nakamura," she announced, to the whole drowned chapel, with the gravity of a priestess. "Scribe. Mender of books. Teacher of tunnels. He eats like a bird but works like an ox." She sat down again, satisfied.

And the community — forty-odd broken, hunted, hole-riddled people — murmured his name back, the way they murmured all the names, and just for a moment Ivo felt it happen: he was entered into their book. Not as a debtor. Not as a gift-bearing asset. As a name they intended to keep.

He had to look down at his bowl until his eyes stopped burning.

Later, Odile found him at his corner, checking the stitching on his coat with unnecessary concentration. She sat beside him on the floor, unhurried, her folded sleeve resting in her lap.

"You're going to leave," she said.

Ivo's hands stilled. "How—"

"Because you're starting to sleep well. People who are staying start to sleep badly again around now — the fear catches up. People who are leaving sleep well, because they've decided, and the deciding is restful." She smiled faintly at the riverbed face. "Also you keep looking north, toward the palace district, like a man reading a letter he hasn't opened yet."

He was silent a long time. "There's a king in there," he finally said. "A real one. Trapped inside his own body — I saw him, I *touched* him, Odile, he's still fighting, after everything, he's still—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "And there's Sera. In their hands because of me. Every hour I spend safe down here is an hour they're paying for. I keep telling myself I'm healing. I keep telling myself I'm no use to anyone half-dead. Both things are true. Neither one helps."

"No," Odile agreed. "They don't." She was quiet, watching the nearest brazier breathe. "Let me tell you what I know about debts, since you wear yours like a coat. A debt says *pay what you took*. It doesn't say *throw yourself into the fire so the ledger balances*. That second thing isn't payment, scribe. It's just another way of disappearing." She reached over and straightened his collar with her single hand, briskly, the way mothers do. "Whatever you do up there — and you will do something, I've known you six days and I already know it — you go as a man with a home. Not a man fleeing one. Men with homes come back. Men fleeing never do."

"And if I don't come back?"

"Then we say your name anyway." She said it simply, terribly, without ornament. "Every night. For as long as any of us live. That's what we do. That's the whole religion." She rose, joints cracking. "Rest tomorrow. Leave the day after. And take the east sluice — the patrol schedule changed; Tomas will show you."

At the mouth of the drowned chapel, later that night, Ivo looked back once. The braziers glowed among the pillars like a constellation brought down to earth — a sky's worth of small stubborn lights that no Regent could devour, because they were not hoarded, only shared. Pell waved to him from the laundry lines, chalk dust on her fingers. Tomas raised his cup. Odile nodded, once, the way you nod to someone going on a long errand rather than a war.

*Come back,* the light said. *We'll keep your name either way.*

He slept well that night, as Odile had predicted, and dreamed — for the first time since the Lantern Gate — not of running, but of returning.

* * *

He left at dawn through the east sluice, Pell walking the first mile with him to the fork where the charcoal-map ended and the palace district's shadow began. She made him recite the tunnel branches back to her twice, then pronounced him adequate, then hugged him quickly around the middle, fierce and brief, the way children hug when they've decided hugs are a debt they're willing to owe.

"Say her name," Ivo said, kneeling to her height. "While I'm gone. Sera Voss. So it stays somewhere warm."

"Sera Voss," Pell repeated solemnly, committing it to the undercity's book. "And you say ours. All forty-one."

"All forty-one," Ivo promised. "I'm writing them down tonight. Every one. Where I'm going, there are archives, and archives are how you fight things that eat remembering."

The last stretch he climbed alone, up a rusted ladder and through a grate that opened behind a collapsed fountain in a merchant quarter grey with morning fog. Above him the palace district rose against the starless sky, its towers crowned with hoarded lantern-glow, beautiful and obscene, a crown of stolen light upon a city of the afraid.

Somewhere inside those towers, Sera was paying for his life. Somewhere below them, a true king fought alone in the prison of his own flesh. And somewhere in the royal library — he was sure of it now, sure the way he had been sure of the glyph that moved beneath his pen — waited the records of Kaelen's childhood, sealed letters, the founder's covenant, the plain and terrible price of reforging the binding.

Ivo pulled his hood up and stepped out of the undercity's dark into the borrowed light, a scribe with forty-one names in his pocket and a home to come back to.

He did not see the figure detach itself from the fountain's broken rim and follow him into the fog — moving with the smooth, wrong grace of something that had been waiting in the deep places far longer than the Forgotten had.
