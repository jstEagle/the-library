## 15. Kaelen's Last Letter

The letters were not where the archive said they would be.

Ivo had spent three days learning this the hard way — three days of the undercity's borrowed dark, three nights of sleeping in shifts in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop where the candles were made of tallow and desperation and smelled like both. Three days of cross-referencing the fragmentary index his ancestors had left behind in that buried vault beneath the mountain, the one written in a hand so like his own it had made him ill to read it.

*The royal correspondence of Kaelen, third of his name, age nine through twelve years: sealed under covenant, access by founder's blood only.*

Founder's blood. The phrase had seemed like poetry when he'd first copied it. Now, with a thin knife lying open on the chandler's worktable and his own palm already scarred from a dozen smaller tests, it seemed more like a lock designed by someone who had known — *known*, centuries in advance — exactly who would come knocking.

"You're going to cut yourself again," said Wren from the doorway, not looking up from the candle she was trimming. She was perhaps sixty, hollowed at the cheeks in the way all the undercity's people were hollowed, but her hands were steady as bedrock. "The last time, you bled on the shelf and the shelf drank it. I don't trust shelves that drink."

"The shelf is part of the seal," Ivo said. He turned his hand over, studying the half-moon cut already closing on his palm. "Everything down here is part of something else. That's rather the point."

He had learned that much about bindings in the weeks since the Ashwood, since the fever, since Sera's voice confessing things in the dark that he had never found the courage to tell her he'd heard. Bindings were not walls. They were *sentences* — grammar laid down against chaos, every clause connected to every other clause, and if you understood the grammar well enough you could find the seams. His gift for unbinding was nothing more than reading faster than the spell could hold its breath.

The problem was that some sentences were written so deeply into the world that reading them changed the reader.

"Kaelen," Wren said, testing the name. "The king's name. You're certain these letters are his? Real ones? Not some priest's forgery?"

"They're real." Ivo had touched them once, briefly, through the outer wrapping, before the seal had pushed him back with a sensation like cold water poured down his spine. Paper remembers. It was the first thing any archivist learned, and the first thing everyone forgot: paper holds the shape of every hand that has pressed it, every tear that has fallen near it, every fire it has narrowly escaped. These letters remembered a child. A lonely, furious, grieving child writing to someone named *Maren* about the weight of a crown he hadn't asked for and the sound his mother made when she laughed, which he was afraid he was forgetting.

Ivo knew what it was to be afraid of forgetting.

"Then why," Wren said, setting down her trimming knife at last and giving him the full weight of her attention — an attention that, in the undercity, was worth more than coin, "does the Hollow King's own childhood correspondence sit unguarded in my cellar?"

"Because no one alive can open it," Ivo said. "And because the thing wearing him doesn't want them opened. I think—" He stopped. Started again. Honesty was a discipline here; the hollowed people of the undercity could smell evasion the way dogs smelled rain. "I think it's afraid of them. Or it would have burned them. It burned everything else."

The Regent had burned a great deal. The great library above had been reduced to a shell of blackened shelves and ash-sifted air, officially due to a lightning strike in a city where lightning no longer fell. But the undercity remembered where things had been carried when the fires came, because the undercity remembered everything the upper city chose to forget. And among the things carried down, generations ago, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax stamped by a sigil Ivo now knew better than his own signature, had been a bundle of a dead boy's letters.

He picked up the knife.

* * *

The seal did not want blood in the abstract. He learned that quickly, wincing, as his first two drops slid off the wax like water off glass. It wanted blood *given* — pressed deliberately, offered, the way his ancestors' whole cursed covenant seemed to demand intention as a component of every working. So he set the blade aside and used his thumbnail instead, dragging it slowly across the scabbed cut until the skin reopened and he could press his palm flat against the cooling wax and think, very clearly: *I am Ivo Nakamura. I am asking. I am not stealing.*

The wax softened.

It did not melt — it *softened*, the way snow softens under a hand, and then it parted along lines that had been waiting in it all along, and the bundle opened in his lap like something exhaling after a long held breath.

There were eleven letters. No — eleven visible letters, tied in their original twine, but the wrapping was deeper than it looked, and beneath the twine, folded separately and sealed with different wax — black wax, formal wax, the wax of state — lay a single envelope marked in an adult hand:

*To whoever comes after. If you are reading this, the Binding has failed or will fail, and you are of our blood, and I am sorry.*

Ivo sat very still for a long moment.

Outside the cellar door, the undercity went about its evening: the scrape of the lamplighters who lit lamps that gave only a fraction of the light they should have, hoarded glow rationed like medicine; the distant hymn of the Starless Faith drifting down from the temple district, sweet and wrong, praising the devourer for eating heaven; a child laughing somewhere, which was the sound that undid him most, because children here laughed without knowing there had ever been stars, and someday — if he failed — they would laugh the same way about the sun.

He unfolded the adult letter first. Some instincts you didn't argue with.

* * *

The handwriting shifted twice within a single page, from the careful script of a trained scribe to something ragged and hurried, and Ivo realized with a lurch that he was watching a man write through tears and keep writing anyway.

*My name is Aldric Nakamura, and I was the last anchor of the Great Binding, and I am dying, which is why I am allowed to tell you the truth. The order forbade us to write it while we lived. Perhaps they feared copies. Perhaps they feared that a written price becomes a chosen price, and a chosen price is too easy for the desperate to pay. They were right to fear it. Read this, and then decide whether you needed to know.*

*You already know what the Binding does. The star-wards are not a shield laid over the world; they are a hunger given boundaries. Before theBinding, the sky was not empty and it was not safe. There are things between the stars, in the deep places where light thins, and one of them is older and cleverer than the rest, and it eats light the way a fire eats a forest — from the edges inward, patiently, forever. Our ancestors caught it. They did not kill it — killing was beyond them — they bound it into a cage of covenant, and the bars of that cage were woven from starlight itself, and the valley your family kept was not a prison for it but a vault for the key.*

*Here is what they did not tell the kings. Here is what they told only each other, whisper by whisper, generation by generation, until the whisper reached me.*

*A binding of that scale needs an anchor. Not a stone — stones crack, and ours cracked, and that is the beginning of your troubles and mine. It needs a will. Something that chooses, every day, to hold. The founder understood this and hid it, because the truth is unbearable: the first anchor was a person. The founder herself. She stepped into the heart of the covenant and let it root in her soul, and she held the sky up for forty years while it ate her life away grain by grain, and when she was nearly spent, the covenant passed to her daughter, who had watched her mother fade and understood what she was inheriting, and who took it up anyway. Freely. That word matters more than any word ever written in any language. The binding cannot compel. It can only receive what is given. That is its strength and its terrible weakness, because a gift freely given cannot be faked, cannot be forced, and cannot be taken back.*

*I was the last. I held it for twenty-three years. When I felt myself failing — and you feel it, always, like carrying water in cupped hands while the wind blows — I searched for another of our blood willing to take the burden, and I found none. My son refused me. I do not blame him. God help me, I begged him, and he refused me, and I love him still, and I hope you never learn what it costs to ask that of someone you love.*

*So I did the only thing left. I restructured the covenant. I spread the anchor across many points — the ward-stones, the temples, the old valley itself — and I diluted the need for a living will until it was almost nothing. Almost. The wards held for a hundred years on that dilution, and then they began to thin, and now, tonight, as I write this with the last strength in my arms, they are failing, and whatever comes next will come because I bought the world a century instead of saving it.*

*I am sorry. I was a coward at the end, and clever cowards make the worst debts, because they leave them to people who come after.*

*If the stars go out, it means the cage broke. If the cage broke, the eater is loose, and it will do what it has always done: it will wear someone. It prefers crowns. Crowns are doors that open themselves. Find the one it wears, and look underneath — there may be someone inside still fighting. There was, in my day. There usually is. The eaten rarely stop resisting entirely; resistance is the last thing they lose.*

*And if you are of our blood — and the seal would not have opened otherwise — then understand what I am about to say, and hate me for saying it, and then decide anyway:*

*The binding can be remade. The Stone can be rewoven. But it must have what it had at the beginning: a willing anchor of the founder's line. One person. A life given freely, wholly, and forever. Not a death — deaths are cheap and the covenant knows it. A *life*. You will not die on the steppe. You will live, anchored, holding, for as long as the world needs holding, and the world will always need holding, and you will not be released, and you cannot set it down, and no one — not love, not duty, not mercy — can take it from you except your own dissolution.*

*I have watched my mother do it. I have done it myself. I would not wish it on the enemy that makes it necessary.*

*But I will not lie to you either, because lies are how the last failure started. There is a kind of peace in it, at the end. The holding becomes a kind of prayer. The prayer becomes a kind of home. My mother smiled, in her last year. She said the stars spoke to her through the weave. She said she was never lonely.*

*I choose to believe her.*

*Decide freely. That is the whole of the law. Decide freely, or do not decide at all.*

*— Aldric Nakamura, anchor of the Great Binding, to whoever comes after*

* * *

Ivo did not remember standing up. He found himself at the little window high in the cellar wall, forehead against the cold stone, looking out at a slice of starless sky the color of a dead hearth.

A life given freely, forever.

He had known, in the way you know the endings of stories you refuse to finish reading, that it would come to something like this. Since the mountain archive. Since the moment he'd traced his own surname across the founding covenant and felt the ink warm under his finger like a pulse. Since Sera, feverish vigil, had murmured truths to his sleeping body that he'd carried around since like a coal in his pocket — *I loved someone once and the order burned it out of me and I swore I'd never again love anything the crown could take, and then there was you, you impossible scribe, you walking seam in the world* —

He could not give himself to the binding and give himself to her. Both gifts wanted all of him. That was what *freely* meant. That was the whole of the law.

And yet.

He thought of the villages where people had forgotten their children's faces. He thought of the child at the well, gasping clean air after the hunger-ward loosened, looking up at him with eyes that held no gratitude yet, only surprise that the world had suddenly gotten kinder. He thought of the undercity, the hollowed ones, Wren and her steady hands and her mistrustful shelves, who had sheltered him not for his gift but because he looked cold. He thought of Kaelen — the true Kaelen — imprisoned behind his own smiling face, still fighting, still refusing to stop resisting even after everything, because resistance was the last thing he would lose.

Someone had to hold the sky. Someone of his blood, freely. And the list of candidates, as far as Ivo knew, began and ended with a debt-bound scribe who had spent his whole life longing for a world bigger than a valley and had finally, finally seen it — and found it starving.

The cruel joke of it almost made him laugh. All those years pressing his palms against the valley's borders, feeling the wards hum like a struck bell, wanting *out*. He'd thought the wards were his cage. They had been his inheritance, waiting.

"Ivo?" Wren's voice, quiet, from the doorway. She had come back without his noticing; the undercity folk moved like that, soft-footed, respectful of grief. She looked at his face, and at the letter in his hand, and whatever she saw made her cross the room and put her steady hand on his shoulder. "Bad news?"

"No," he said, and was surprised to find it was almost true. "The worst kind. The kind where you get exactly what you asked for."

She waited. She was good at waiting. Everyone down here was good at waiting; waiting was the local craft.

"There's a way to save everything," he said. "There always is. There's just a price, and the price is—" His throat closed. He tried again. "Forever."

Wren nodded slowly, as though he'd said something ordinary, something she'd heard a hundred times. Maybe she had. Maybe everyone in the undercity had paid some version of forever already, and that was why they lived down here among the forgotten, keeping each other company in the wreckage.

"Then you'd best read the rest," she said, nodding at the bundle. "The boy's letters. Whatever he wrote when he was young and the sky was still full — that's the part that'll tell you whether forever is bearable."

So Ivo sat back down among the candles, and untied the twine, and opened the first of a nine-year-old king's letters, and read:

*Maren — Mother says I must practice being solemn but I don't see why. The crown is heavy enough without being sad too. Today the tutor showed us the old star-charts, all the constellations with their names, and I made him point out the one our family keeps. He got very strange about it. He said, "That one is not for looking at, Your Highness. That one is for remembering." I asked him what I'm supposed to remember and he wouldn't say. He looked frightened. Kings aren't supposed to notice when servants are frightened but I noticed. I notice everything. That's my problem, Maren. Everybody says so.*

*Write back soon. If you don't I shall assume the wolves got you and I shall be extremely dramatic about it at dinner.*

*K.*

Ivo read it twice. Then a third time, slower, and somewhere in the middle of the third reading his vision blurred, because he recognized the loneliness in it — the specific loneliness of a child surrounded by people and understood by none, pressing questions against walls that would not answer. He knew that country. He had grown up there.

And beneath the loneliness, plain as a signature, was the thing the tutor had been frightened of. *That one is not for looking at. That one is for remembering.* Even as a boy, even before the crown, before the thing in the deep places had begun its patient approach — Kaelen had been chosen for something. Marked. The bloodline of anchors ran through kings as well as scribes, and the crown had always been heavier than gold.

Which meant the letters might contain more than comfort. They might contain the *covenant itself* — the founder's original words, the grammar of the Great Binding, preserved by a child who noticed everything precisely because no one thought a child mattered.

Ivo turned to the second letter with hands that were not quite steady, and found, tucked inside its fold where no archivist had thought to look, a slip of vellum in a cramped, secretive, unmistakably childish hand — a copy, made by a boy who wasn't supposed to know what he was copying, of words far older than he was.

At the top, in the founder's own tongue, a single phrase that Ivo's blood recognized before his mind did, the way a tuning fork recognizes its note:

*The Anchor's Covenant — first words.*

And below it, faintly, in wax-black ink that seemed to drink the lamplight, the opening line of the ritual that could remake the sky — and remake the one who spoke it.

Somewhere above, distantly, the bells of the Starless Faith began to ring for evening devotions, and Ivo understood with sudden terrible clarity that he had very little time left to decide freely — because tomorrow, the palace would come looking for the last of the Nakamura blood, and it would not come gently.
