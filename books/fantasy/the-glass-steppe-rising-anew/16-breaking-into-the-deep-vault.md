## 16. Breaking Into the Deep Vault

The undercity did not sleep so much as hold its breath.

Ivo had learned its rhythms in nine days of hiding — the way the lantern-sellers doused their stalls at the third bell, the way the hollow-eyed ones drifted toward the warm grates beneath the palace's foundations, drawn like moths to a heat that was slowly eating them. The dispossessed called it the Underlight. They shared it the way starving people share bread: carefully, with both hands, watching who took too much.

It was there, crouched over a grate in the tunnel called Old Mercy, that Wren found him on the night of the attempt.

"They've moved the schedule," she said without preamble. She was a small woman with a scarred throat and a voice like gravel sifted through water; the Regent's feeding had hollowed her once and she had crawled back out, thinner but unbroken. "Vault rotation. Two bells earlier than your source promised. If you're going, it's now or never."

"Now," Ivo said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. His hands were not steady. He pressed them flat against his knees until they remembered how to be still.

Wren looked at him for a long moment. In the grate-light her face was all planes and shadow, and behind her stood the others — Tam, broad-shouldered and silent, carrying the coil of dead-rope that could pass through wards because it had been woven from wool of sheep that had never seen starlight; old Maretta, whose gift was small and strange, the ability to make a person briefly forget a door existed; and the twins, Ferren and Lo, who could walk without sound when they chose to.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Wren said. "We could wait. Plan better."

"No." He said it before he could think about what waiting would cost. Every day, more stars' worth of light drained out of the world. Every day, more people in the undercity went quiet-eyed and soft-spoken, their edges worn smooth by something that drank memory. Somewhere above him, in the palace, Sera Voss sat in a cell branded an oathbreaker, and every hour he spent planning instead of moving was an hour she paid for. "The longer we wait, the stronger it gets. And she—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I've waited long enough."

Maretta shuffled forward and pressed something into his hand: a stub of tallow candle, half-burned. "For the dark places," she said. "Not to see by. To remember you can make fire even when everything's taken away."

He closed his fingers around it. It was such a small thing, such a foolish, human thing, and it nearly undid him.

* * *

They went up through the bones of the mountain itself.

The palace had not been built so much as grown, ages ago, around the vault that predated it — Ivo had learned this in the archive beneath his family's mountain, reading words his ancestors had written in ink that still glimmered faintly with binding-script. The Deep Vault lay at the root of everything, below the throne room, below the crypts, below the flooded galleries where the first kings were laid. And the only way in that the Regent had never thought to guard was the way its builders had used: the old construction shafts, sealed with plain mortar and forgotten, because no one imagined anyone would want to go down into the place where the sky had been anchored.

Tam broke the final seal with a mason's hammer wrapped in cloth, each blow muffled, patient, rhythmic as a heartbeat. Dust breathed out of the crack, air that had not moved in four hundred years. It smelled of stone and cold and, faintly, impossibly, of starlight — the same clean silver scent Ivo had come to know from the pages of his ancestors' books.

"That smell," Ferren whispered. "What is it?"

"Hope," Ivo said, "that got put somewhere safe."

They descended by dead-rope into blackness absolute. Ivo lit Maretta's candle, and the flame burned strangely — tall and white-edged, leaning always toward the depths, as if pulled by the broken thing below.

The shaft ended in a gallery of pale stone, and the pale stone was not stone at all. It was glass. Fused, rippled, running in frozen waves across the walls and floor like a sea caught mid-motion, and Ivo understood with a lurch that he was walking on the Glass Steppe's buried twin — the place where the Great Binding had been forged, where the first anchor had poured herself into the covenant and the heat of that giving had melted the rock to mirror. His ancestors had written about this. *The floor remembers what was given,* one line read, *and shows it back to those who carry the blood.*

The floor showed him nothing yet. But it felt — aware. Attentive. Like a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep.

Then he saw the Binding Stone.

It lay at the center of the gallery in a shallow basin carved with the Nakamura seal — the eight-pointed star within the circle, worn nearly smooth by centuries. The Stone itself had been magnificent once, he knew: a heart of gathered ward-light, the crystallized first oath, big as a millstone and bright as dawn. Now it was shattered. Hundreds of fragments lay scattered across the glass floor, dull and grey, drinking the candlelight rather than returning it. Some pieces had been swept into corners. Some had been carried off entirely — he could see the empty spaces in the pattern, gaps shaped like missing teeth.

"It wasn't just broken," Lo murmured, circling the basin. "It was *dismantled.* Deliberately."

"The night the stars vanished," Ivo said quietly. "When the wards fell, this broke apart. Or—" He knelt at the basin's edge and touched one fragment. Cold. Dead as river stone. "Or it broke apart first, and that's why the wards fell. I need to know which. Because if I rebuild it wrong, if I rebuild it as a cage instead of a covenant—"

"Then you become your ancestors," Wren finished. "And the valley closes again, and the world goes blind inside its own protections." She set her jaw. "So don't rebuild it wrong, scribe."

*Thank you,* he almost said, *I hadn't thought of simply doing it right.* But her roughness was kindness wearing work clothes. He'd learned that about the undercity folk. They gave you the truth plainly because they'd all been lied to beautifully, once, by a crown that smiled wrong.

He stepped into the basin.

The glass floor woke under his feet. Light moved through it in slow veins, silver-white, converging toward him, and Ivo felt the blood in his body answer — felt it *recognize* the place, the way a child recognizes a grandmother's voice though the grandmother died before the child could speak. His parents had carried this. Their parents. Back and back to the woman who had first knelt here and said *let my life be the lock.*

He picked up the first fragment.

It warmed in his palm like a bird coming awake. Not magic — the valley had taught him to fear that word, and besides, this was older than magic's petty categories. This was covenant. He fitted the fragment against a second piece, and a third, and where the broken faces met, seams of light ran like solder, knitting shard to shard. Unbinding and binding were the same craft seen from opposite sides; he had learned that in the fever, in the Ashwood, in the long grey weeks since. To unmake a chain you had to understand every link. To remake a bond you had to forgive every break.

Piece by piece. An hour, then two. Sweat ran down his spine despite the cold. His fingers bled where sharp edges bit, and where his blood touched the Stone, the fragments drank it eagerly and glowed brighter, and he let them, because the archive had been clear: the founder's blood was the key, and his blood was the only founder's blood left in the world.

The Stone rose under his hands like a thing being born rather than built. When the last piece settled into place — a sliver no bigger than his thumbnail, which he found wedged in a crevice of the basin as if someone, long ago, had hidden it deliberately against this very night — the whole heart of the Great Binding flared white, and the gallery blazed like noon, and every fused-glass wall threw his own image back at him multiplied a hundred times: a thin, bleeding scribe kneeling inside a circle of returned light.

For one moment — one impossible, singing moment — Ivo felt the whole sky. Felt the torn places where stars had been, felt the Regent's hunger crouched over them like a wolf on a kill, and felt the Stone straining upward through the mountain toward its purpose, asking him only one question:

*Who anchors?*

His mouth opened to answer.

And the doors of the gallery opened above — the real doors, the ones built for kings and keepers, wide enough for a procession — and the answer died in his throat.

* * *

They came down the grand stair in ranks, and they did not rush, because rushing was unnecessary. Crown hunters in grey lacquer. Priests of the Cleansing in their starless-white vestments. And between them, walking with her wrists chained in enchanted iron and her head held high anyway, Sera Voss.

She was alive. That registered first, and shamefully, Ivo felt relief flood him before horror could arrive. Alive, but — her lip split, one eye swollen half-shut, her ward-knight's coat gone, replaced by a prisoner's rough grey smock. The brand on her cheekbone had been freshened; he could smell the cauterized flesh from ten paces. Oathbreaker, it said. Traitor. The order's mark, cut deeper than before.

Her good eye found him across the blazing gallery, and what was in it was not surprise.

That was the worst of it. Not surprise. Grief, and fury, and beneath both a terrible resigned tenderness, the look of someone who has already done this arithmetic and hates the total.

"No," Ivo said. His voice cracked against the glass walls and came back to him a hundred times over. "No, no — she was in your cells, I *saw* the records Wren stole, she was in the Lantern Gate cells—"

"She was," said the Regent.

It came down the stairs last, and the temperature of the room changed around it the way weather changes before a storm. It wore King Kaelen's body still — the tall frame, the golden crown, the handsome face — but the face had stopped pretending since Ivo had last seen it. The skin hung looser, the smile sat wider, and the eyes were windows into a room where nothing lived. Its shadow came down the steps a half-second after the body did, lagging like sound after lightning, and pooled at the bottom of the stair far larger than any man's shadow should be.

"Ivo Nakamura," it said warmly, in Kaelen's ruined voice. "Founder's heir. Look at you. Look at what you made." It gestured, gracious as a host, at the burning Stone. "Four hundred years my predecessors searched for this vault. Four hundred years the valley hid it, warded blind, buried under forgetting. And you walked in and rebuilt it in an evening, because it *wanted* you. Do you know how beautiful that is? How delicious?"

Ivo's hands were still on the Stone. He could feel its power thrumming up his arms, could feel the question still hanging unanswered — *who anchors?* — and he understood, with sickening clarity, why the Regent had not simply seized the vault decades ago. Why it had needed him lured, summoned, hunted, driven here step by step.

Because the Stone answered only to the founder's blood. And it required consent.

"You can't use it," he said. "You know you can't. It won't take you."

"Oh, I don't want it to take me." The Regent descended another step. Its shadow spread across the glass floor, and where the shadow touched the Stone's light, the light guttered like a candle in wind. "I want *you* to take it. Anchor yourself to it, Ivo. Bind yourself in, willingly, forever — exactly as your ancestor did, exactly as the ritual demands. And then—" the smile widened past the limits of Kaelen's face, "—then I will simply eat the anchor. Slowly. From the inside. As I have eaten every other keeper of every other ward, one village memory at a time, while the world thanked me for cleansing its sky. You'll be the greatest meal of my existence. The founder's own blood, freely given, holding the door open for me."

Silence. Even the crown hunters had gone still.

"And if I refuse?" Ivo asked, though he already knew. God help him, he already knew.

"If you refuse?" The Regent turned, unhurried, and gestured to the hunters beside Sera. One of them drew a short sword with the bored efficiency of a man performing a chore. "Then the oathbreaker dies here, in front of you, and I find another way. There are always other ways. Other bloodlines, diluted but present. Other anchors, weaker, less willing. It might take me another century." It shrugged Kaelen's shoulders elegantly. "But you would have watched her die for a century you bought no one."

Sera spoke then. Her voice was hoarse but level, pitched to carry across the glass. "Ivo. Listen to me. Don't—"

"Gag her," the Regent said mildly, and iron went into her mouth, and her eyes over it were blazing, blazing, saying everything the gag prevented: *Don't you dare. Don't trade the world for me. Don't you dare love me that small.*

The Regent spread its hands. Patient. Certain. It had planned this, Ivo realized — the stolen records, the false cell locations, the whole architecture of his hope — and it had brought him to the exact center of his own trap and handed him the choice it had designed him to make. Choices collapsing, narrowing, funneling down to a single door.

Except.

Except that the Stone was burning under his hands, and the glass floor was singing in his blood, and his ancestors had left one fragment hidden in a crevice against this very night — which meant they had foreseen betrayal too, had written contingency into covenant, had trusted some future Nakamura to be cleverer than the darkness hunting her.

*I am a scribe,* Ivo thought. *My whole life is words. Let it think it has cornered me in the one place words are weapons.*

He lifted his bleeding hands from the completed Stone, and let them shake visibly, and let the Regent see despair arrive on his face like a guest expected.

"Let her go," he said hoarsely. "And I'll anchor. Freely. Tonight."

The Hollow Regent smiled its terrible smile, and above the mountain, in the starless dark, something vast leaned closer to listen.
