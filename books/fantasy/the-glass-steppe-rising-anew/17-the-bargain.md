## 17. The Bargain

They brought Sera in through the vault's high doors the way one carries a thing of no consequence — two hollow-eyed guards, her arms bound behind her with chain that drank the light, her ward-knight's coat torn from shoulder to hem. They set her on her knees on the black glass floor of the deep vault, and Ivo's heart went with her, all the way down.

He had known it was a trap. He had walked into it knowing. Knowing had not made it hurt less; knowing had only made him complicit, and there was a particular kind of grief in that — grief with his own fingerprints on it.

The Binding Stone lay reassembled before him on its plinth of fused glass, whole for the first time in three hundred years, and still dark. Still waiting. Around the vault, the walls wept shadow: not darkness but *movement*, slow tides of it sliding along the seams where stone met stone, breathing without lungs. The Regent did not need to be present to be present. It was in the walls the way rot was in fruit.

Sera lifted her head. One eye was swelling shut; blood had dried in a line from her hairline to her jaw. She found Ivo across the twenty feet of glass between them, and she did not look afraid. That was the worst of it. She looked at him the way she had looked at him across a hundred campfires, a hundred bad roads, a hundred nights when the sky had nothing left to give them — steady, level, *hers* — and then she shook her head, once, small.

*Don't,* she said with everything except words. *Don't you dare.*

"Ivo Nakamura." The voice came from everywhere, from the shadow-tides, from the plinth, from inside his own skull where it made itself comfortable like something returning to an old house. "Founder's blood. You have made my vault beautiful again."

"It's not your vault," Ivo said. His voice cracked on the second word. He hated that. He planted his feet anyway. "It belongs to my family. To the people who built the wards. To everyone whose stars you ate."

"Belonging." The shadows rippled, and he could have sworn the sound they made was amusement. "Such a small word for such a large hunger. You have carried it all your life, haven't you? The debt-child. The scribe who copied other people's names because no one would remember his own. And now you stand before the last covenant of your ancestors, and you still do not know what you are owed."

Ivo's hands were shaking. He folded them behind his back so the thing wearing the crown could not see. "You brought her here to make me an offer. So make it."

* * *

Sera heard the offer being shaped before it was spoken — heard it in the way the shadows drew inward, gathering themselves like a speaker drawing breath. She had spent six years learning to read rooms for ambush, and this room was nothing but ambush, wall to wall, floor to ceiling, and she was kneeling at the center of it with her hands chained and her oath broken and the only person left in the world who mattered standing ten paces away about to do something unforgivably noble.

"I will trade you," the Regent said, and its voice slid into Sera's ears like cold water. "Her life, freely released — her chains unmade, her wounds closed, her road home opened. In exchange: your cooperation. Your hand upon the Stone. Your founder's blood, willingly given, to complete what your ancestors began. The sky is half-devoured, scribe. Help me finish the meal, and I will let you both walk out of here under a starless heaven that will never trouble either of you again."

"You're lying," Sera said.

The shadows turned toward her with the patience of weather. "Am I?"

"You don't release things. You eat them. You ate the stars, you ate the memories of every village between here and the Ashwood, you ate—" Her voice caught, and she hated herself for it, and pushed through anyway. "You ate the courier at the waystation while he was still trying to tell us how to save people. You don't bargain. You *consume*. Whatever you're offering, the price is written somewhere he can't read it."

"Clever knight." Something moved in the dark — a suggestion of a smile, vast and wrong. "And yet here you kneel. And yet here he stands. And yet the offer stands regardless. Refusal is also a choice, and refusal has a shape too. Shall I describe it? It looks very much like you, knight. It looks like the moment after."

"Sera." Ivo's voice, low, urgent. "Look at me."

She looked at him. Twenty feet of black glass, and his face above it pale and set, and she thought: *six years ago I reported a corrupt chancellor and lost everything, and I told myself I would never again watch good people be destroyed by things bigger than them while I stood by.* She had broken one oath already. She would break every oath ever sworn if it kept him from doing this.

"Ivo, listen to me." She made her voice flat and clear, the voice she'd used on green recruits, the voice that did not shake. "Whatever it offers, it's lying. Whatever it promises, the price is you — all of you, forever, the way it took the king. You are not trading my life for anything. You're trading it for *nothing*, do you understand? There is no version of this where you cooperate and I walk out. It needs your consent because consent is the lock, and once you turn the key—"

"I know," he said.

"—and I am *not worth*—"

"*I know.*" Louder than he meant. The shadows stirred. He lowered his voice, and it broke anyway, and he let it. "Sera. Look at me. I have been owned since the day I was born. Debt-bonded before I could write my own name. Every choice I ever made belonged to someone else — the temple, the ledger, the clause, the road. This is the first time in my entire life anyone has ever wanted me enough to put me at the center of a bargain." A breath. "Don't take that from me by deciding it isn't worth it."

"That's not—" Her composure finally cracked, and what came through underneath was raw and young and terrified. "That is the most *stupidly noble* thing I have ever heard, and I served with knights who died stupidly noble deaths for *less*, and I buried every single one of them, and I am telling you as the person who will have to live in the world you leave behind — Ivo, please. Please refuse. Please let me be the price. I chose this. I chose to come back for you, I chose to get captured, I choose it again right now, out loud, in front of this thing — *let me pay it.*"

The vault was silent except for the slow breathing of the shadows.

And Ivo Nakamura looked at the woman he loved, kneeling in chains on black glass, offering her life for his, and felt something in his chest go quiet and cold and perfectly clear — the same clarity that had come over him in the archive beneath the mountain, reading his ancestors' handwriting, understanding at last what the binding demanded.

*A willing anchor. A life given freely.*

The Regent thought it was buying his surrender.

He let it think that.

* * *

Ivo stepped forward. One step, then another, crossing the black glass toward the plinth, and he kept his eyes on Sera because if he looked anywhere else he would lose the thread of the lie he was spinning, and the lie had to hold. It had to hold for both of them.

"I accept," he said.

"No," Sera whispered.

"On three conditions." He raised his voice, addressed the shadows, the walls, the hunger wearing a kingdom. "One: her chains come off *now*, before I touch the Stone. Two: she walks out of this vault free, past your hunters, past your guards, untouched, and stays untouched. Three: when it's done, you show me my parents' fate. All of it. No edits."

A long pause. The shadow-tides slowed, considering.

"Agreed," said the Regent.

*I'm sorry,* Ivo thought at Sera, as hard as he had ever thought anything. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, watch my hands, watch my hands.*

Because here was the thing the Regent could not know — the thing no one alive knew, because he had learned it alone, in the dark of the archive, with his fingers on his ancestors' ink: the Great Binding was not merely a spell. It was a *sentence*. Written in the founders' language, sustained by their blood, structured like everything his family had ever produced — like ledger-copies, like contracts, like the debt-clauses that had governed his whole life. And every contract, every sentence, every binding ever written had within it the seed of its own unmaking. A termination clause. An escape hatch. His people had been scribes for nine generations, and scribes always, always left themselves a way out of the document.

He had spent three nights in the undercity writing it into himself. Not on paper — paper burned, paper was found, paper was read. Into memory, into muscle, into the cadence of his own pulse, so that when the moment came he would not need ink or light or time. He would need only his voice, and his blood, and the one instant in which the Regent believed it had already won.

Consent was the lock. But a lock turned by a scribe who knew the sentence better than its author could be turned *backward*.

He reached the plinth. Sera was shouting something — his name, over and over, ragged now, all her knight's composure gone — and the guards were unlocking her chains because the Regent had agreed, and the shadows were pulling back from the Stone like a tide making room, and everything was happening exactly as the hunger had planned.

Ivo laid his palm on the Binding Stone.

It was warm. Warm as skin, warm as a hand held out in the dark, and beneath his palm he felt the old covenant stirring, three centuries of his family's will compressed into glass and light, waiting for a founder's hand to open it.

"Now," the Regent breathed, from every wall at once, triumphant and vast. "*Now, founder's child. Give me the sky.*"

Ivo closed his eyes. Found the sentence. Found the clause his great-great-great-grandmothers had stitched into the heart of the covenant — *let the binder unbind, let the written end, let the debtor walk free* — and began, silently, in the old language, to speak.

He gave the Regent what it asked for. Or rather, he gave it the *words* it asked for — the surrender, the cooperation, the opening of the covenant — and threaded through every syllable, fine as spider silk, invisible as intent, ran the counter-weave. The unbinding. Not of the Stone. Of the *bargain*. Of the thing that had offered it. Clause by clause, thread by thread, he began unpicking the deal even as he appeared to seal it, and the Regent — drunk on victory, blind with hunger, three hundred years from hearing its own name spoken against it — never noticed that the contract it was accepting was eating itself as fast as it was signed.

On the far side of the vault, Sera got her hands free, and stopped shouting, and went very still.

Because she knew his hands. She had watched them for months — copying, cooking, mending, casting — and she knew what they looked like when they were working magic, and the man at the plinth was not surrendering.

He was *writing*.

Her heart slammed once, hard, and then she understood, and the understanding hit her like sunrise: he wasn't giving in. He had never intended to give in. He had let her believe — had let *it* believe — and she was on her knees watching the love of her life weaponize his own surrender, and she had never in her life been so furious or so grateful, and both feelings arrived at once and canceled each other out into something that had no name except *him*.

The guards were looking at the Stone. Everyone was looking at the Stone.

No one was looking at Sera Voss.

She rose off the glass without a sound. Six years of border discipline in every movement. There was a guard's knife at the nearest hip — she had counted blades the moment they dragged her in, because counting blades was what kept disgraced knights alive — and her wrists were raw and her eye was swelling shut and none of it mattered, because Ivo was holding the Regent's attention the way a lamp holds a moth, and moths, she had learned, were astonishingly easy to kill from behind.

The first guard dropped without a sound.

The second turned. Too slow. Much too slow.

And high above them all, in the walls, in the dark, the Regent felt the first thread of its bargain come apart — felt the clause slip loose like a stitch pulled from a seam — and its triumph curdled into something ancient and astonished and *afraid*.

"*What are you doing,*" it said.

Ivo opened his eyes. They were full of light.

"Reading the fine print," he said — and pulled.
