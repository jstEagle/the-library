## 19. The Devourer Unleashed

Sera Voss had stood in the presence of dying men before. She had held a comrade's head while the light went out of his eyes at Lantern Gate; she had knelt in the mud of the Ashwood beside soldiers who bled out whispering names of people they would never see again. She had thought herself finished with helplessness.

She was wrong. Helplessness had been waiting for her here, on the coronation dais, wearing the shape of everything she loved.

Ivo lay crumpled at the altar's foot, and above him the true king of Veyland wept like a child waking from a nightmare twenty years long.

"Kaelen." Sera dropped to her knees beside him, one hand pressed to Ivo's chest — his heart still beat, slow and stubborn, though his skin had gone grey as old parchment — and the other hovering uselessly over the king's shoulder, afraid to touch what remained of him. "Kaelen, look at me."

The king looked. That was the worst of it. The Hollow Regent had worn this face for two decades, smiling its wrong smile over a starving kingdom, and now that it was peeled away, what was left underneath was almost too human to bear: a man gaunt as a famine ghost, eyes sunk deep in bruised hollows, hands trembling against the marble. Twenty years imprisoned inside his own body, screaming behind glass no one could see.

"I heard him," Kaelen rasped. His voice was a ruin, rusted from disuse. "All these years, I heard him using my mouth. My voice. Signing my decrees with my hand." A shudder ran through him, and Sera saw that he was clutching something to his chest — a scrap of paper, soft with handling, edges furred. "And when your scribe pulled it off me—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Where did it go?"

Sera did not answer. She did not have to. The answer was arriving overhead.

* * *

It began as a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard — a low, vast groaning, like a glacier calving, like the world's oldest door being forced open. Then the light changed.

There had been no stars for twenty years. The sky above the capital had been a black lid, a ceiling, an absence everyone had learned to stop seeing. But now the absence itself was *moving*. Sera rose slowly to her feet and looked up, and her mind refused, at first, to assemble what her eyes reported.

The darkness was pouring upward.

It came off the palace spires first, off the lantern-glow the city hoarded so desperately, off every shadowed alley and shuttered window — threads of hungry dark streaming up into the wounded heavens like smoke from ten thousand fires. And where those threads gathered, the sky *bent*, folding inward around a point above the Glass Steppe far to the north, a wound in the world opening wider and wider, ringed by a corona of devoured light that had nowhere left to hide.

"It's not fleeing," Sera said, and her voice sounded small and absurd beneath the groaning sky. "It's not running from us."

"No." Kaelen had dragged himself upright against the altar, the letter crushed in his fist. He was looking at the sky with the expression of a man reading his own death warrant. "It's going home. To the place where the binding broke. It spent twenty years eating through the wards from inside the crown, and now the last anchor is gone, and there is nothing between it and the rest of the sky." He laughed — a terrible, cracked sound with no humor in it at all. "Nothing but us."

Below them, through the great windows of the coronation hall, the city was waking to the sight. Sera heard the first screams, then the bells — not the worship-bells of the starless faith, but alarm bells, real ones, rung by hands that remembered why bells existed. The borrowed light of the capital flickered as if the Regent were already drinking it from a distance.

She turned back to Ivo. He had not moved. His breathing was shallow, each rise of his chest a little less certain than the last, and around his fingers the faint traces of the counter-weave he had woven into his own surrender still glimmered and guttered like embers losing their heat. He had unbound a god from a king, and the act had taken nearly everything. What remained of him was barely enough to keep his heart arguing with the dark.

*Ivo.* She said his name silently, because saying it aloud would have broken something in her that she needed whole. *You clever, reckless, impossible man. You promised nothing, and I believed you anyway.*

* * *

They carried him — Sera took the shoulders, Kaelen the feet, a king and an oathbreaker bearing a scribe together down from the dais like pallbearers rehearsing their own funeral — into the royal library, because Kaelen said the heart of the Binding Stone could only be reached from there, and because it was the deepest room in the palace, warded by Ivo's ancestors against exactly the thing now devouring the sky.

The library doors sealed themselves behind them with a sound like a held breath released. For a moment, in the lamplit quiet among shelves rising into shadow, Sera allowed herself to pretend they were safe. It lasted four seconds.

Kaelen slumped against a reading table, and the scrap of paper fell from his hand. Sera picked it up before he could stop her.

It was a letter, written in a child's careful hand. *To whoever finds the kingdom after me,* it began. She read three lines and had to close her eyes. A boy-king, alone in a palace, writing to the future because no one in the present would listen. A boy who had sensed the thing circling his crown before anyone believed him. A boy whose warning had gone unread for twenty years while the Hollow Regent smiled with his smile.

"He gave me the authority," Kaelen said quietly. "Before it fully took me. The last authority of the crown. I've kept it hidden inside myself all this time — the one place it couldn't reach, because I never let myself hope enough for it to matter." He held out his trembling hand, palm up, and across it a sigil burned: the seal of Veyland's founding, the mark of the Binding covenant, rendered in light that hurt to look at directly. "The Binding Stone's heart answers to this. Only this. And I can't—"

He tried to lift his arm toward the hidden vault-door at the library's rear, and the arm failed him halfway, shaking so violently that Sera caught it. Up close she could see what twenty years of possession had truly cost: not just flesh wasted but *self* worn thin, a soul used as a scabbard until the blade had nearly cut through it.

"I can barely stand," Kaelen said. "I cannot weave. I cannot anchor. I cannot even open the door without falling." His ruined eyes found hers, then moved to Ivo's unconscious face. "But he can. If he wakes. If there's anything left in him to wake *to*."

"And if he does," Sera said, "you know what opening that heart means. You read the same letters we did. The binding needs an anchor. A willing life, given freely, forever." Her throat closed around the words. "You're asking me to wake him up so he can die."

"I'm asking you to let him choose," Kaelen answered. "It's the only thing anyone ever offered me. Let him have it too."

* * *

They laid Ivo on a reading table among the genealogies of his own bloodline, and Sera sat vigil again — the second vigil, she thought bitterly, and how many more would there be before the sky finished falling? She bathed his forehead with water from the library's ancient ewer. She held his cold fingers. She talked to him, low and steady, about nothing: the taste of the waystation stew, the ridiculous name of the mule they'd ridden out of the Ashwood, the way the undercity children had called him *the star-scribe* and followed him through the tunnels like ducklings.

His eyelids fluttered at *star-scribe*.

"Sera." Barely a breath. His eyes opened halfway, unfocused, finding her by voice alone. "Did it work?"

"You worked," she said. "The king is free. The Regent is—" She stopped. Outside the sealed windows, the darkness overhead had thickened visibly, a bruise spreading across heaven. "Gone north. To the steppe. To finish what it started."

Something moved through his exhausted face — grief, and guilt, and beneath both a terrible clarity. "Then we lost. All of it. The archive, the vault, the bargain, the counter-weave — it was all just buying time, and time's spent." He tried to sit up; she pressed him back down with a hand that shook more than his did. "Sera. The stone's heart. Tell me you didn't—"

"The king can't reach it. He's too damaged." She made herself say the rest plainly, because he deserved plainness. "Only you can. And you know what it costs. Ivo, listen to me — there has to be another way. There's always another way. We haven't searched the whole archive, we haven't asked the undercity, we haven't—"

"Sera." He reached up and touched her face, his fingertips cool and ink-stained and trembling. "When the valley's wards fell, do you know what I felt? Not terror. *Relief.* Twenty years behind walls I never chose, and the moment they broke, I finally breathed." A faint, crooked smile. "You told me once that belonging can be built instead of inherited. You built yours out of duty. Mine was built out of debt. Neither of us ever got to choose." His thumb traced her cheekbone. "This is the first thing I'll ever have chosen. Every line of it. Don't take that from me."

"You're talking about dying like it's a gift."

"I'm talking about *choosing* like it's a gift. They're not the same thing." His eyes drifted past her, to the king, to the bruised sky beyond the windows, and she watched the clarity harden into resolve. "How long do we have?"

Kaelen answered from the shadows by the vault-door, his voice flat with a king's arithmetic. "By the speed it's moving? Dawn. Perhaps less. When it reaches the steppe and pours itself into the wound, the remaining anchors fail everywhere at once. After that, no binding is possible. Not at any price."

"Then there's no time to waste on me lying here," Ivo said, and pushed himself upright despite both of them, swaying like a sapling in wind, grey-faced and grinning. "Your Majesty. Your authority. Give it to me."

* * *

The transfer took place at the hidden door, and Sera would remember it always as the strangest sacrament she had ever witnessed: a broken king pressing his burning palm against the forehead of a dying scribe, the founding seal flowing between them like molten gold poured from one vessel to another. Ivo gasped — the light sank into him, and for a moment his whole body arched with it, and the library's candles flared high, and somewhere in the walls the old wards of the Nakamura ancestors hummed a single deep note of recognition.

*Blood of the binders,* the stone seemed to say. *At last.*

The vault-door opened.

Beyond it lay a stair descending farther than lamplight could follow, and a cold upwelling from below that smelled of granite and centuries and, faintly, impossibly, of starlight — the preserved radiance of theBinding Stone's heart, hoarded like treasure in the dark. Ivo stood at the top of those stairs, swaying, lit from within by the crown's last authority, and turned back to look at them both.

"Sera." His voice was steadier now, which frightened her more than the weakness had. People grew calm when they had finished grieving for themselves. "If I don't come back up—"

"You'll come back up," she said fiercely. "That's an order. I'm still a knight of the realm, whatever my order says, and I am ordering you to come back up."

A laugh escaped him, soft and wondering. "Yes, ward-knight." He stepped toward the stair. Stopped. "Whatever happens down there — thank you. For arresting me on the border. For not letting go."

Then he was descending, and the dark took him one step at a time, and Sera stood at the threshold with her fists clenched so tightly her nails drew blood from her palms, hating the sky, hating the price, hating the nobility of it all, loving him with a ferocity that felt like a wound that would never close.

Behind her, Kaelen spoke quietly. "Ward-knight Voss. There is a stable yard. There are horses. The steppe is three hours' hard ride, and the Regent will arrive at dawn."

She turned. The king's ruined face held something new — not hope, exactly. Direction.

"If your scribe means to give himself to that stone," Kaelen said, "then someone should ride with him far enough to see it done. Someone who won't let the world forget what it cost."

Sera was already moving toward the doors.

Above the palace, the last shreds of the Regent's darkness streamed northward, and the sky groaned like a living thing being eaten alive, and dawn — the final dawn, perhaps, of the world as it had ever been — waited on the horizon like a held breath.

Three hours. One horse. One chance.

Sera Voss ran.
