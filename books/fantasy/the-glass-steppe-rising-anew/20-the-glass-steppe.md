## 20. The Glass Steppe

The sky was still empty when they reached it.

Ivo had imagined the place where the wards first fell a hundred times on the road from the capital — in fever-dreams, in the half-sleep of stolen camps, in the long grey silences when Sera rode beside him and neither spoke. He had pictured a crater, perhaps, or a scorched scar across the earth, some wound grand enough to match the sound he remembered: that shattering-glass cry the night the stars went out.

What he found instead was a plain of fused glass stretching to every horizon, smooth as a frozen lake and pale as old bone, reflecting back an unbroken blackness. No grass. No stone. No mark of any road. The Devourer's passage had melted a hundred miles of the steppe into one seamless mirror, and now the sky lay upon it like a second sky, so that Ivo walked between two darknesses with nothing above him and nothing below, and felt — absurdly, terribly — at home.

*You were born for this,* whispered something in his blood. *Your people wrote their names into the world here. You have only to write yours.*

"No," he said aloud, and Sera glanced over.

"No what?"

"It's talking to me again." He flexed his fingers inside his gloves. Beneath the leather, his hands still glowed faintly, the unbinding light leaking through his skin like dawn through shuttered windows. Since the vault, since the Regent had poured itself shrieking out of Kaelen and into the wounded fabric of the sky, the glow had not dimmed once. It was as if the thing he carried — the gift the valley's wards had spent three generations suppressing — had tasted its own purpose and refused to sleep again. "It keeps saying *write*. Like ink. Like the ledgers."

"Then don't listen," Sera said. Her voice was rough. She had not slept since the altar; none of them had. Behind them, Kaelen sat propped against his horse like an old man, the true king hollowed down to a whisper of himself, too damaged to stand but present — awake, weeping earlier, watching everything with eyes that had been prisoner in his own skull for longer than anyone knew. "Listening is how it got its hooks into him." She nodded back toward Kaelen without looking. "It starts as a voice offering things. Then it's a voice wearing your face."

"I know." Ivo did know. He had touched the king's hand in the royal library and seen the whole of it: the Regent coiled through Kaelen's soul like a taproot through loam, feeding slowly, patiently, on everything a man was. He knew exactly how it began. And he knew — because the archive beneath the mountain had told him, because Kaelen's childhood letters had told him, because his own ancestors' covenant told him in language older than any temple ledger — how it would end.

A willing anchor. Of the founder's blood. A life given freely, forever.

*Forever* was the word the letters underlined, in his ancestor's careful hand, the way a scribe underlines the terms of a debt.

He was, after all, a scribe. He understood debts better than most men.

* * *

They made camp at the edge of the glass where the steppe still remembered being land — a thin margin of grey ash-grass, brittle and starved, growing right up to the border where earth became mirror. There was no wood for fire. They ate cold rations and drank from a skin Sera filled at the last stream, and the darkness pressed close on all sides, total and absolute, the first true darkness Ivo had ever known. Even in the valley, even before, there had always been stars. Now there was only the Regent, vast and slow and patient somewhere in the black, digesting the sky.

Kaelen slept by the horses, if what he did could be called sleep — a shallow drifting, flinching at dreams. Sera spread her cloak near the ash-line and sat with her sword across her knees, though they both knew steel meant nothing now. The battle ahead would not be fought with steel.

Ivo took out the book.

It was not really a book anymore. In the deep vault, kneeling among the shards of the Binding Stone while the Regent's hunters closed in, he had done something no archivist would forgive and no scribe would forget: he had taken the original covenant — the Great Binding, written in his bloodline's hand across vellum gone brown with centuries — and he had read it until the words stopped being words. Then, with the last of his strength, he had *unbound* it. Peeled it apart glyph by glyph the way he peeled hunger-wards off wells and chains off hunted strangers. Not to destroy it. To understand it. To hold every clause of it open in his mind like a dissected flower.

Now the fragments lived in him. He could feel them arranged behind his ribs like type in a printer's tray, waiting for new composition.

"You're going to do it tonight," Sera said. Not a question.

"The Regent is feeding," Ivo said. "It just lost its vessel. It's gorging itself on the sky to heal, to finish the devouring before anything can stop it. That means—" He looked up, into the absolute dark. Somewhere up there, the thing that had worn a king's smile was spreading itself thin across heaven, glutted and careless. "That means it's distracted. And it means the wards are empty. The old binding failed because it was built on suppression — my family locked magic away in a valley and chained the sky with star-wards, and both were cages, and cages break. The Regent grew in the gaps. It *was* the gap."

Sera was silent a moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice she used for the hardest truths. "And the new one?"

"A door that locks from the inside," Ivo said. "Willingly."

She looked at him then, fully, in the light of his own hands, and he saw her understand — saw it land in her the way grief lands, all at once and forever. "How much of you goes into it?"

"All of it." He said it plainly, because she deserved plainness. She had confessed to his sleeping body during the fever — he had heard more than she knew, heard *I loved someone the order destroyed and I will not survive it twice* — and plainness was the only mercy left that wasn't a lie. "My freedom. My future. Whatever a person is, when you take away the moving-through-the-world part. The anchor doesn't die, exactly. The old texts say the founder's anchor 'walks no roads and sleeps no nights, but is not dead.' He becomes the hinge the door swings on."

"And you'd be alone out there." Her jaw tightened toward the glass. "On that. Forever."

"That was the plan I was going to tell you," Ivo said quietly. "But plans are just debts we owe ourselves, and I'm done with debts. All my life I've belonged to other people's clauses. The temple owned my hands. The debt-holder owned my years. Even my own bloodline owned me — wrote me into a covenant generations before I was born and called it heritage. So here's what I finally understand, Sera, standing at the end of every map:" He looked down at his glowing hands. "*Choice* is the only thing that was ever truly mine. So the binding will be made of choice or it will be worth nothing."

"And if it's worth nothing?" Her voice cracked on it. "If you pour yourself into that stone and the stars come back for one night and go out again—"

"Then at least the night was lit," Ivo said, "and everyone I love saw it happen."

Sera turned away. He heard her breathe, once, hard — the breath of a soldier deciding not to fight a battle she couldn't win. Then, without a word, she moved her cloak and bedroll to the space beside him and sat down against his shoulder, sword forgotten, and stayed there while the darkness breathed around them, and neither of them named what it was, because naming it would have made the leaving real, and there were hours yet.

* * *

Near midnight — or what midnight would have been, if night still had a middle — Ivo walked out onto the glass.

He went alone. That was the covenant's oldest term and the one clause he honored: the anchor must come freely, unbargained, unguarded. If Sera stood at his shoulder the choice would be tangled with her, and tangles were how the Regent fed. So he kissed her forehead at the ash-line — she let him, eyes closed, fists clenched at her sides, a knight holding formation against her own heart — and he stepped past the border where earth became mirror, and the glass rang softly under his boots like a struck bell that never quite finished tolling.

Halfway out, he stopped and knelt.

The surface was warm. That surprised him; he had expected cold. But the fused steppe held the memory of the Devourer's heat the way wax holds a seal, and when Ivo laid his palm flat against it, his blood answered. The unbinding light in his veins flowed downward instead of out, and far below — miles below, deeper than roots, deeper than stone — he felt the shattered heart of the Binding Stone, scattered fragments of his ancestors' work lying in the dark like a broken constellation buried in the earth.

*There you are,* he thought, and began to write.

Not with ink. With intent. With language. He reached down through the glass along the threads of the old covenant — he knew every clause now, had unbound them all into his keeping — and he gathered the fragments the way he had once gathered spilled letters off a temple floor, one by one, each shard of the Stone singing a different syllable of his family's name. *Nakamura.* Writer of walls. Keeper of the valley that forgot. The bloodline that had tried to protect the world by locking half of it away, and in locking, taught the dark where the seams were.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to ancestors he had never met. "You bound the world out of fear. Fear makes brittle things."

And he began the new covenant.

He wrote it the way he wrote everything — carefully, in a scribe's hand, with a craftsman's hatred of ambiguity. Where his ancestors had written *the blood of our line shall hold the wards*, he crossed it out and wrote *a soul that chooses shall hold them, and may be relieved by another who chooses.* Where they had written *forever*, he examined the word a long time — feeling the Regent stir somewhere above, feeling the vast attention of the hungry dark tilt toward him like an eye opening — and then, deliberately, he kept it. *Forever,* he wrote. *Because some promises should not be escapable. But chosen forever, renewed forever, a forever that is a gift and not a sentence.*

The difference was everything. The old binding was a wall. The new one was a vow.

The Regent came for him then.

It did not descend; it *arrived*, everywhere at once, the way cold arrives, pouring its attention down onto the glass plain until the mirror beneath Ivo's knees reflected not the empty sky but the thing itself — a shape like a crown wearing a storm, a hunger given geometry, vast beyond sense. It had no mouth and yet he heard it speak, in the voice it had borrowed from a smiling king:

*LITTLE SCRIBE. YOU UNBOUND ME FROM MY MEAT AND NOW YOU KNEEL IN MY TABLESCAR AND OFFER ME YOURSELF? I ACCEPT.*

"You misunderstand," Ivo said. His voice was small against the enormity, and he let it be small. Pride was a seam, and seams were how it fed. "I'm not offering myself *to* you. I'm offering myself *against* you. Every ward ever built was a cage, and you were born in the gaps of cages. But a vow has no gaps. A vow is solid all the way through, because nothing in it is unwilling." He pressed both palms to the glass. The light in his hands was blinding now, white as the stars it was meant to call home. "You want to devour the sky? Then swallow this."

He drove the new covenant downward, through the glass, into the buried heart of the Binding Stone, and gave himself to it.

It did not hurt. That was the last thing he expected and the truest thing he learned: surrender, done completely and without reservation, feels like coming home. Every wall he had ever chafed against — the valley, the temple, the debt, the fear of his own gift — dissolved not into nothing but into *everything*, because he stopped being a thing contained and became the containing. The light poured out of him and down, and the shattered Stone drank it, and the fragments rose through miles of rock and memory and fused themselves into the glass beneath his knees, and Ivo Nakamura felt himself become the hinge, the keel, the living root of a door that locked from the inside.

The Regent screamed.

It threw itself against the newborn binding with all the weight of a devoured heaven, and the binding *held* — not because it was strong, but because it was chosen, and there was not one unwilling fiber in it for the hunger to grip. The Regent had eaten stars and memories and kings, but it had never once been given anything freely, and it had no teeth for that. It scrabbled at the vow like a beast at glass, unraveling thread by thread, each strand of it dissolving wherever it touched the light of a freely-given soul.

And above him — oh, above him —

The first star reignited like an eye opening after long sleep.

Then a second. A third. They came back the way memories return to villages healed of the Devourer's feeding — hesitant, then certain, then joyful, constellations reassembling themselves out of the black like scattered pages finding their chapter. Ivo, spread now through the whole plain, felt each ignition as a small bell rung inside his chest. He counted them the way he had once counted ledger lines, and wept light, and kept writing, and the Regent unraveled against his vow until there was almost nothing left of it, a rag of hunger fleeing up past the returning stars, diminished, displaced, *not destroyed* — he felt it go, and filed the warning away in whatever remained of his scribe's mind, because even transcendence keeps good records—

—and then, at the edge of his vast new awareness, at the ash-line where earth remembered being earth, he felt a presence sit down and refuse to leave.

Small. Stubborn. Armored in exiled duty and ungovernable love.

*I'll build here,* Sera Voss said to the empty dark, to the plain of ringing glass, to him — knowing somehow that he could hear, or hoping it, which amounted to the same thing. *You said belonging can be built rather than inherited. Fine. Watch me build.*

Far below the returning heavens, anchored at the heart of the world's newest vow, Ivo Nakamura laughed — a sound that came out of the glass plain as faint chiming, like a bell learning joy — and began, with a scribe's patience and a free man's whole heart, to write her into the covenant's margins.

Not as a clause.

As a homecoming.
