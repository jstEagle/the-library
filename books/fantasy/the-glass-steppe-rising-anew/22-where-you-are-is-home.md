## 22. Where You Are Is Home

### Sera

The road to the glass steppe had no name on any map, and Sera Voss decided, somewhere past the third day of walking, that she liked it that way.

She had spent her whole life on named roads — the King's Way, the Wardens' Circuit, the long grey scar of the Border Road where disgraced knights were sent to rot. Named roads went places other people had already decided mattered. This one simply unwound eastward through country that was slowly remembering itself: fields where green had crept back over ash-grey soil, villages rebuilding their roofs with whatever timber the relief caravans brought, children who pointed at the sky each night and shouted the names of constellations as if greeting old friends returned from a long illness.

The stars had been back for four months. Sera still caught herself looking up at them the way she imagined a man looked at someone he'd loved and lost and miraculously recovered — with gratitude and terror in equal measure, because he knew now how easily they could be taken.

Tonight she stopped early, at the crest of a hill, because she could see it.

The glass steppe spread below her like a frozen sea of light. When the wards had first fallen — when the Hollow Regent had torn its way into the world through the wound in the sky — the discharge of raw magic had fused the plain to smooth, translucent glass, miles of it, rippled like water caught mid-laugh. By day it threw the sun back up in blinding sheets. But it was evening now, and the sun was going down behind her, and the steppe was beginning to do the thing that had made pilgrims weep and scholars abandon their careers.

It was beginning to shine from within.

And at its heart, faint as breath on a winter window, stood a figure made of starlight.

Sera sat down on the cold grass, pulled her cloak around her shoulders, and let herself look at him. She did this every night now, wherever she was, if the sky was clear enough and the distance short enough. Some nights he was only a smudge, a suggestion of silver against the deeper glow of the stone beneath his feet. Tonight, this close, she could see the shape of him clearly: Ivo Nakamura, standing exactly where his ancestors' covenant had once demanded blood and given only silence, rewritten now in his own hand, sustained by his own choice.

He didn't stand still, exactly. That was the first thing people never understood when she told them about it. He moved — slowly, endlessly, tracing patterns across the steppe that matched, if you watched long enough, the slow wheel of the stars above. He was the anchor, yes. The living heart of the Great Binding, the point around which every ward in the world turned. But an anchor, Sera had learned, was not a prisoner chained to one spot. It was a fixed point from which everything else was free to move.

He had chosen that distinction himself, in the last words he'd spoken before the light took him. *A binding sustained by choice,* he'd written across the shattered Binding Stone with fingers that bled light instead of blood, *not bloodline. Not debt. Choice.*

She wondered, not for the first time, whether he ever regretted it. Whether there were nights — and there must be nights; there were nights for everyone — when the weight of a whole sky pressed down on him and he wished he had let the Regent win, just to be done with it.

She would ask him tonight. She asked him most nights. And most nights he gave her the same answer, which was not really an answer at all but something better: he smiled, and the stars overhead brightened by a fraction, as if the whole sky were leaning down to listen.

Sera rose, shouldered her pack, and walked down toward the boundary line.

* * *

The boundary was easy to find even in the dark, because nothing grew beyond it. Grass ended in a clean curve, as if drawn by a careful hand, and past that curve lay the glass — warm underfoot even at midnight, humming very faintly, a sound you felt in your teeth more than your ears. Pilgrims came to the edge and pressed their palms to it and reported feeling forgiven, or healed, or simply less alone. Sera felt none of those things. What she felt, every time, was the specific warmth of a door left unlocked for someone expected.

Her house stood forty paces along the boundary, where the glass met a shelf of ordinary stone. She had built it herself over six weeks in high summer, with lumber hauled from the valley and help from villagers who refused payment and stayed anyway, hammering and arguing and singing badly, until the walls stood square and the roof held and there was a chimney that drew true. Kaelen's engineers had offered to send a crew from the capital. She had declined. Some things, she had written back, should be built rather than inherited.

That phrase had gotten around. She suspected the king found it funny.

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke and the rosemary she grew in a window box, the only plant stubborn enough to tolerate the strange seasons of the boundary air. She lit the lamp, set water to boil, and put out two cups out of habit — though it was not habit anymore, really. It was simply how the house was arranged. Two cups. Two chairs. A bed wide enough for two, though most nights it held only one.

Because he came down after dark. Every night. That was the arrangement, and the arrangement was the miracle, and Sera had stopped questioning miracles sometime around the moment she'd watched a scribe rewrite the covenant of heaven with his bare hands.

She was slicing bread when the light in the room changed.

It always changed the same way — a softening, a silvering, as if the moon had stepped inside without bothering to open the door. She turned, and there he was in the doorway, shaking glass dust from his hair, wearing the shape of himself: dark-haired, narrow-shouldered, twenty-three years old forever now, or so it seemed. His skin held a faint luminescence, like paper held up to starlight. His eyes were the worst and best part — they had gone the colour of the deep sky, and when he looked at her, she felt looked at all the way through.

"You're staring," he said.

"You're late."

"The Cassiopeia weave needed re-tensioning." He said it mildly, the way another man might say *the fence needed mending.* "There's a knot in the northern meridian that keeps pulling loose. I think it's my grandmother's work. Sloppy splice. I've been meaning to fix it for three months."

"Three months to fix a knot in the sky."

"I've been busy." He crossed the room, took the second cup she'd filled without being asked, and drank. Whatever he was now, tea still worked on him. Small mercies. "Also, I fell asleep standing up again. Somewhere over the Ashwood. Woke up and a whole flock of migrating birds had decided I was a roost."

Sera laughed — really laughed, the unguarded kind she'd thought the order had trained out of her years ago — and pulled him down into the chair beside hers. He came willingly. He always came willingly. That was the whole architecture of everything now: willingness, load-bearing, everywhere.

"How far can you feel tonight?" she asked. It was their evening litany, the question she asked the way other wives asked *how was your day.*

"All of it." He said it without pride, the way you'd say *the tide is in.* "Every ward from here to the Sunward Sea. The valley's borders are holding beautifully, by the way. Your temple elders finally stopped trying to rebuild the old isolation wards. They're planting orchards outside the wall instead."

"I heard. Third letter this month asking me to come home."

"Are you going to?"

She looked at him. He was watching her with that terrible gentle attention, the look of someone who had seen the inside of her — literally, once, during the counter-weave, when their minds had been braided together for nine minutes that felt like nine years — and loved what he'd found there anyway.

"No," she said. "I'm home."

### Ivo

Later, when the lamp was burned low and the dishes were done, Ivo stepped out onto the stone shelf behind the house and looked up.

This was his favourite hour, the one no one warned him about. During the day he was the anchor — working, weaving, holding ten thousand threads of ward-light taut between the world and the hungry dark beyond it. The work was vast and constant and, strangely, not heavy. That was the secret no one believed: it wasn't heavy, because he wasn't carrying it alone. Every person who slept safely under the stars was, in some tiny way, holding a thread. The binding ran on choice, and choice was everywhere now, multiplying. Farmers chose to leave their doors unlocked. Sailors chose to trust the navigational wards. Children in the capital chose, with the ferocious seriousness of children, to keep the little lantern-shrines Kaelen had commissioned, one for every star that had almost been eaten.

But at night — at night he got to stop being the anchor and just be a man standing behind a small house, looking at the sky he'd saved, seeing it not as a lattice of obligations but as what it had always been meant to be: beautiful.

Sera came out and stood beside him. Her shoulder fit under his arm the way it had since the first night she'd built the porch rail exactly to their combined height, which she had claimed was coincidence and which he had never believed for a second.

"The northern meridian," she said. "Tell me honestly. Does it hurt?"

He considered lying, out of habit — the old scribe's instinct, learned in a valley that taught its children to fold themselves small. Then he remembered that folding himself small was the one skill he had permanently unbound.

"A little," he said. "When I stretch it. Like an old scar in cold weather. But Sera — it's my scar. Do you understand? Before, the binding hurt because it was built on debt and blood and silence. People bound to things they never chose. My ancestors wrote a cage and called it protection. Mine runs on—" he gestured vaguely at the house, the lamplit window, her, "—this. It doesn't pull. It holds because everything in it wants to hold."

She was quiet for a while. Out on the steppe, the glass glowed softly around the place where he stood each day, and above it the stars wheeled in their slow, tensioned dance, every one of them accounted for.

"When I was a girl," she said at last, "the order taught us that duty and love were opposites. That the knight who loved was the knight who could be leveraged. I believed it for twenty years. I reported my own commander because loving him had made me hesitate one half-second too long, and I thought the hesitation was the corruption." Her voice stayed level, but he felt her hand find his and grip. "It took me until the Lantern Gate to understand the hesitation wasn't the sickness. The order was."

"And now?"

"Now I think duty is just love that's been given a job." She turned to face him, and the starlight in his own skin lit her features from below, softening the old scar at her jaw. "You have a duty. The biggest one anyone's ever had. And you come down off that steppe every single night to drink bad tea with me. That's not a contradiction, Ivo. That's just — a life."

"That's just a life," he agreed.

Above them, unprompted, a star flared briefly brighter — the one directly overhead, the one the archive texts called the Anchor's Eye, the one that had been dim and dying for a century before he rewrote the covenant. It happened sometimes, when he was happy. The sky, apparently, could tell.

They stood there until the cold drove them in, and the lamp went out, and the house at the boundary line went dark and warm and full.

* * *

### Sera

She woke near dawn to a sound she hadn't heard in months: hoofbeats on the boundary road.

Sera was out of bed with a knife in her hand before her mind fully surfaced — old habits, drilled deep, probably permanent. Through the window she saw a single rider coming up from the south at a hard gallop, royal courier blue whipping behind him, and she felt the particular dread of a woman whose peace has been too good for too long.

By the time she'd dressed, the rider had reached the boundary and dismounted, and she saw why he was pale. Everyone was pale, the first time they saw the steppe glow at dawn. He was young, and he saluted her with the crisp terror of someone who had been told exactly who lived in this house and had rehearsed the salute the whole ride.

"Warden-Captain Voss." Not a question. "Dispatch from His Majesty Kaelen, First of That Name Restored." He swallowed. "It's marked — um. It's marked *for both of you*, my lady. The king's instruction was explicit. He said—" the courier closed his eyes, reciting, "—he said, 'Give it to the anchor and the woman who taught me that crowns are just another kind of binding, and bindings can be rewritten.'"

Sera took the letter. The seal was genuine — the restored seal, the broken crown remade with a crack deliberately left visible, so no one would ever forget. Inside, Kaelen's handwriting, still shaky from what the Regent had done to him, but steady enough:

*Ivo. Sera.*

*The archive teams finished cataloguing the Deep Vault last week. You should know what they found beneath the shattered Binding Stone: a second stone. Older. Predating your ancestors entirely — predating the kingdom, maybe predating the valley. It is not broken. It is not dormant. It is warm.*

*And carved into its surface, in a script our oldest linguists say matches nothing human, is a single line, translated thus:*

***THE FIRST ANCHOR IS NOT DEAD. THE FIRST ANCHOR IS WAITING TO BE FOUND.***

*I do not know what this means. I know only that the moment I read it, every star in the sky flickered — once, all together, like a held breath. The archivists say it was coincidence. You and I have stopped believing in those.*

*Come to the capital. Both of you. Bring your ink, scribe, and your sword, Captain. Whatever wrote that line has been patient for a very long time, and patience, we have learned, is what hunger looks like from the outside.*

*— K.*

Sera read it twice. Then she carried it to the doorway, where Ivo stood in the grey dawn light, the morning star still bright above his shoulder, reading over her arm. She felt his breath go quiet against her hair.

Out on the steppe, the glass had begun to glow with the sunrise — and then, for just a heartbeat, it flickered. All of it, edge to edge, like a held breath.

"Well," Ivo said at last, and his voice was calm, and underneath the calm she heard the other thing, the thing that had carried him through a devourer and a broken sky and a covenant rewritten in his own blood: not fear. Readiness. "We'd better pack."

Sera looked out at the shining plain, at the ancient warmth sleeping beneath it, at the sky that had flickered like something waking up — and she went inside to fill the kettle, because whatever was coming, they would meet it the way they met everything now.

Together, at the boundary line, where duty had become home.
