## 1. Ink That Will Not Dry

Felix Brightwater had copied the same prayer four hundred and eleven times, and he knew this because he counted. Counting was one of the few pleasures permitted to a temple scribe in debt — that, and the smell of fresh ink, which he loved with a devotion he had never once directed at the gods themselves.

The prayer was called *The Litany of Patient Ears*, and it ran to three hundred words, none of which Felix believed. It asked the silent gods of the valley to hear the supplications of the faithful. In eleven generations, so far as any record showed, they had heard precisely nothing. The valley's gods were famous for it, in the way a stone is famous for not dancing. Pilgrims came anyway, mostly in autumn, mostly to leave offerings of bread and small carved animals at altars that never so much as warmed.

"They're listening," High Priest Ondrel liked to say, "they're simply *considering*."

Felix suspected the consideration had gone on long enough that the gods had either died, left, or were playing an extremely long game of some divine patience contest. But he said nothing. He copied. That was the arrangement: his family owed the temple a debt so old that no living person could name its origin, and until it was paid, Felix belonged to the ink pots and the drying racks and the long cold scriptorium where the light came through windows of thick green glass, making every morning look like the bottom of a pond.

He was on line two hundred and six of copy number four hundred and twelve when Brother Tamlin leaned over his shoulder.

"You've gone crooked," Tamlin said.

"I've gone *economical*. The margins are generous."

"The margins are sacred."

"The margins are empty, which is the same thing to a god who answers nothing." Felix kept writing. Tamlin was sixty, half-deaf, and the closest thing Felix had to a friend, mostly because Tamlin laughed at jokes he only half-heard, which meant he laughed at all of them.

"Careful," Tamlin said cheerfully. "The Quiet Hours punish blasphemy."

"Do they? I hadn't noticed them punishing anything."

Tamlin chuckled, patted Felix's head like a dog that had done something clever and slightly forbidden, and shuffled off to his own desk, where he was copying *The Litany of Patient Ears* for the nine thousandth time and had been for thirty years. There was a comfort in that, Felix admitted. Whatever else the valley took from you, it gave you back your days in identical pieces, like bread cut by a very careful hand.

* * *

That evening, as always, Felix locked away the day's work, banked the scriptorium fire, and walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone. The valley at dusk was beautiful in a way that made him vaguely sad, the way a photograph of someone you almost remember is sad. Glass flats beyond the town caught the last light and threw it back rose-gold, and the hills stood dark and patient around everything, like dogs waiting for a word that never comes.

His room above the cooper's shop was cold. His supper was barley and onions. His one luxury was a stub of candle he allowed himself exactly one hour of, during which he read whatever scraps of old script he could get his hands on — because if Felix Brightwater had a secret passion, it was not freedom, or wealth, or love, but *language*. Old script especially. The tongue the prayers had first been written in, before the temple translated everything into safe, modern, unambiguous Valley Common. Old script was full of knots and double meanings, words that leaned against each other. Reading it felt like eavesdropping on people who thought no one alive could understand them.

He fell asleep with a fragment of it under his cheek: a scrap he'd bought from a tinker for two coppers, some lines about doors, or maybe thresholds. Hard to say. Half the characters were worn away.

* * *

In the morning, the litany was wrong.

Felix noticed it before he was fully awake, which is to say he noticed it the way you notice a sound in another room — as a wrongness, a pressure, before understanding arrived. He had left copy number four hundred and twelve on his desk to dry, because the scriptorium racks were full and Brother Tamlin had a territorial relationship with the good ones. And now, standing barefoot in the grey light with sleep still in his eyes, Felix saw that the page was moving.

Not fluttering. Not stirring in a draft. *Moving* — the way a sleeping animal moves, from somewhere inside.

The ink was rewriting itself.

Line by line, in handwriting that was almost his but not quite — rounder, older, more certain — the words of *The Litany of Patient Ears* were being unpicked and re-stitched into something else. Where the litany had begged *hear us*, the new text said something else entirely. Felix leaned close, heart going oddly quiet, and read:

*We are awake. We have been awake for a long time. Who is writing?*

The candle stub was still on the desk. His hands, when he looked at them, seemed like other people's hands.

"No," Felix said aloud, to the page, to the room, to whoever was asking. "No, thank you."

The ink continued, serene, unhurried, in that rounder-than-his hand:

*You read the old tongue. Good. Few do. We will write slowly.*

And then, beneath it, smaller, almost shy:

*Please answer.*

Felix did what any sensible man would do. He dropped the scrap of old script, knocked over his water cup, swore in a manner that would have cost him a week's wages in fines if Brother Tamlin had heard, and stuffed the page into the bottom of his chest beneath three shirts and the winter blanket, where it could rewrite itself in the dark without an audience.

Then he sat on the edge of his bed and shook for a while, which he also considered sensible.

* * *

Here is what Felix knew about trouble, having been born inside it: trouble in the valley wore two faces, and both of them wanted paperwork.

The first face was the temple's. If the priests learned that a transcription had altered itself — that a prayer written in obedience had begun *disobeying* — there would be questions, then inquiries, then a tribunal, and tribunals in the Temple of Quiet Hours moved with the speed of continental drift and ended, historically, with someone's name being struck from the rolls. Struck names lost their debts, technically. They also lost their houses, their rights, and in two cases Felix knew of, most of their memories, because the striking rite was old and nobody fully understood it anymore. Nobody volunteered for it. Nobody was ever quite the same after it was done to them.

The second face was worse. The debt-collectors.

Felix's family debt was administered not by the temple directly but by an office in the capital called the Bureau of Quiet Accounts, whose clerks came through twice a year in grey coats, counting everything — candles burned, pages copied, hours slept, according to some formula no debtor had ever been shown. If the Bureau learned that temple property had begun exhibiting what their forms called *irregular animation*, Felix didn't know what would happen. That was the frightening part. In eleven generations, it had never once happened. There wasn't even a form for it.

Which meant, Felix reasoned, pulling on his boots with hands that mostly worked, that the correct course of action was simple: retrieve the page, burn it, report nothing, and spend the rest of his life copying litanies until he died warm, bored, and unremarkable.

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, a perfect plan.

He got as far as the chest lid.

Because here was the thing — the thing he would turn over in his mind for many nights afterward, the splinter that wouldn't go in any direction but deeper: *please answer.* Eleven generations of prayers had gone up like sparks into rain. And now something down in the dark of the world had picked up a scribe's pen and asked — politely, shyly, in beautiful old script — whether anyone was there.

Felix had spent his whole life being spoken *about*: listed, assessed, entered in ledgers, described in columns. No one had ever asked him a question that expected an answer. The gods certainly never had.

He closed the chest. He did not burn the page.

"I'm going to regret this," he told the ceiling, conversationally. The ceiling, in keeping with local tradition, said nothing.

* * *

At the temple, the morning proceeded with its usual holy indifference. Bells that rang themselves on a clockwork mechanism, because the gods could not be troubled to. Pilgrims shuffling in with their carved ducks and their stale loaves. High Priest Ondrel conducting the Hour of Consideration, in which the entire congregation sat in silence for one hour, considering, while Ondrel watched them consider and occasionally frowned at individuals whose consideration seemed insufficiently devout.

Felix sat in his pew and considered, with great devotion, the page hidden under his shirts.

Something was wrong with the valley. He'd known it the way you know weather is turning — not a fact, a pressure. The glass flats had hummed last night. He'd assumed it was wind over the flats; the flats often sang in autumn. But the song had been rhythmic. Almost syllabic. And this morning, three separate pilgrims had remarked to the door-wardens that their prayers had felt *warm* on the way out, which the wardens had logged, per procedure, under *Atmospherics, Miscellaneous*.

And Brother Tamlin — Tamlin, who had copied the same litany nine thousand times without incident — had come in late, pale, muttering that his ink pot had frozen overnight in a warm room, frozen *in patterns*, and that he'd thrown the whole pot down the well rather than look too closely at it.

"Patterns," Felix said carefully. "What kind of patterns?"

"Letters, boy. Letters that weren't mine." Tamlin laughed his half-hearing laugh, but it came out thin. "Old letters. I don't hold with old letters. Nothing good was ever written in old letters."

Felix looked at his own hands. They seemed ordinary. They felt, faintly, like borrowed pens.

That night he went home, barred his door, lit his hour of candle, and lifted the page from the bottom of the chest. The ink had continued in his absence. The bottom third of the sheet was now dense with that rounder, older hand, and the message had grown longer, more urgent, the way a letter grows when its writer fears the recipient means to stop reading:

*They sealed us with silence and called it peace. They sold a child's name to feed the lock and called it debt. You are the four hundredth pen we have tried. You are the first who reads.*

*The door remembers heat. Do you?*

Beneath the words, drawn small and precise, was a shape Felix recognized from his tinker's scrap — the worn half-characters he'd slept on for months. A threshold. A door.

And below the drawing, in ink that glistened as though freshly spilled, though the page had been dry for a thousand years:

*Someone is coming up the valley road. She knocks twice. Answer her, Brightwater. She is the first true thing to enter this valley in an age — and she carries fire.*

Felix sat very still. Outside, far off, on the road that wound down between the patient hills, he heard — or thought he heard, or perhaps the page simply wanted him to hear — the knock of a traveller's staff on stone. Twice.

He blew out his candle, and in the dark, the ink went on shining.
