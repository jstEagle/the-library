## 2. The Woman Who Knocked Twice

Felix Brightwater had copied four hundred and eleven prayers in his eleven years at the Temple of Quiet Hours, and he had a private theory that the gods of the valley were not silent so much as hard of hearing. He kept the theory to himself. The temple had strong opinions about theories.

He was grinding ink — the slow gray ink they used for transcriptions, nothing so bold as black — when he heard the knock. Two raps, brisk and unapologetic, on the temple's outer door. Nobody knocked twice at Quiet Hours. Pilgrims knocked once, timidly, and then waited so long that Felix usually opened the door out of mercy before their courage failed entirely. Debt collectors did not knock at all; they simply appeared in the doorway with ledgers under their arms like men carrying knives wrapped in cloth.

Two knocks meant someone who expected the door to be worth knocking on.

He set down the pestle, wiped his hands on his robe, and went to answer it.

The woman standing in the morning light was dusty from boot to collar, with wind-scoured cheeks and eyes the color of a forge seen through a keyhole — bright orange banked behind something darker. A long case was strapped across her back, wrapped in oilcloth, and she held it the way one holds something alive.

"I need," she said without preamble, "someone who can read this." She pulled a scrap of leather from her coat and thrust it at him. On it, in faded pigment, ran a line of the old script — the deep-cut letters that predated the valley's temple by a thousand years, if the temple's own crumbling histories were honest about it, which they mostly weren't.

Felix took the scrap. The letters seemed almost warm under his thumb, which he decided was his imagination, because everything in the valley was room temperature forever.

"Where did you get this?" he asked.

"Tracked it for six years," she said. "From the Ashfall ranges to the Salt Marches to here. It's a map. Or half of one. And I can't read it, which is why I've been walking in circles since spring."

"You walked *here*?" Felix said. "On purpose?"

The look she gave him suggested she was recalibrating her opinion of the entire valley and finding it wanting. "Is there another way in?"

"No," he admitted. "There's just the road, and the road is mostly an apology for a road." He turned the scrap over. "I'm Felix Brightwater. Temple scribe. I can read the old script."

"Can you read it well enough to guide me where it points?"

"That depends on where it points."

She smiled then, briefly, like a match struck indoors — quick, illuminating, immediately smothered. "That's fair. I'm Sera Ashfall. Dragon-keeper. And I'll pay you."

"I don't take coin," Felix said, because he didn't; every copper he touched flowed directly into the temple's ledger against his family's debt, and taking payment felt like being handed a shovel and told to dig himself deeper. "I take debt reduction. You pay the temple, they mark my ledger, everyone wins except me, but I'm used to that."

Something flickered across her face — not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. "How much do you owe?"

"More than you have. Less than the temple says." He folded the leather scrap carefully and handed it back. "Come inside. We shouldn't discuss maps on the doorstep. Brother Ollen thinks curiosity is a sin and he audits for it."

* * *

Sera had crossed nine borders in six years, and none of them had ever done what this valley's border did.

She'd felt it at the ridgeline, just past dawn: the flame-sense she'd carried since childhood going quiet all at once, like a hand closing around a candle. She'd stopped so abruptly that her pack-mule — a bad-tempered animal named Grudge — had walked into her back. Below her, the valley lay green and orderly and utterly still, terraced fields and whitewashed walls and a river moving without sparkle. Not dead. *Sealed*, some instinct said, though she didn't yet know why she knew the word.

Her magic hadn't come back since. Six years of tracking a dying fire across half the world, and now she stood in a country where fire apparently refused to be tracked, or perhaps refused to exist.

The innkeeper's wife fed her eggs and bread and asked no questions, which told Sera more than questions would have: people who don't ask questions are people who've been taught not to. The village was clean, polite, and quiet in a way that made Sera's skin itch. Nobody hummed. Nobody argued loudly. Even the dogs barked softly, as though volume itself were taxed.

And the scribe — the scribe interested her. Felix Brightwater had ink stains to his elbows and a face built for worrying, and when he'd taken the leather scrap his fingers had lingered on it a half-second too long, the way a man touches something familiar. He'd flinched at the warmth. She'd watched him decide not to mention it.

She found him after noon prayers, in the scriptorium, hunched over a transcription with the particular posture of a man hiding something small and terrible beneath a larger, safer task.

"The old script," she said, sitting down across from him without asking. "Teach me the alphabet. I'll pay in lessons of my own."

"What could you possibly teach me?"

"How to leave."

His pen stopped mid-stroke. "That's not a lesson anyone teaches here."

"Everything can be taught," Sera said. "Most things are just forbidden instead." She laid the scrap between them. "Start with this line. Read it to me."

Felix looked at the letters for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice dropped as though the room might overhear. "*Here sleeps the one who was put down like fire.*"

The silence afterward had weight.

"That's a strange thing to carve above a door," he added.

"Is there a door?"

"There's always a door," Felix said. "Ruins in the high hills. The temple calls them the Old Foundations and forbids us to go, which means everyone goes, once, as teenagers, and comes back disappointed. There's nothing there. Broken walls. Goats."

"Show me anyway."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and she owed him better than a lie. "Six years ago," Sera said slowly, "the last living dragon began to die. Not age — dragons don't die of age, they die of *ending*, and hers is ending. Her fire is guttering like a lamp out of oil, and I have followed the trail of it across three continents looking for whatever it came *from*. Fire doesn't come from nowhere, Brightwater. Every flame has a hearth. And every scrap, every shard, every burned fragment of the old script I've found has pointed here, into your beautiful deaf valley where my magic dies at the border." She tapped the leather. "This says *fire*. This says *sleeping*. I want to know what's sleeping, and whether it burns."

Felix sat back. She watched him weigh it — she was learning his tells already, the way his gaze went left when calculating, right when afraid. Right now it went right.

"If I guide you," he said, "and the temple finds out—"

"Then we go quietly."

"There's no quietly. There's only degrees of loud." But he was already pulling a fresh sheet toward him, sketching the hill trails from memory, and Sera hid a smile. Men said no with their mouths and yes with their hands. She'd known dragon-keepers the same way.

"One condition," Felix said. "We go at week's end. Market day. Half the priests will be drunk on festival wine and the other half pretending not to be."

"You've done this before."

"I've *imagined* this before," he said. "Imagination is free. Everything else gets billed."

* * *

They spent the next five days together, and it was worse than either of them expected, in the specific way that comfort is worse than discomfort.

Sera learned that Felix talked to his ink pots, apologized to furniture, and had memorized the temple's entire liturgy while privately believing most of it was filler. He learned that she counted everything under her breath — steps, coins, stars — a habit from years alone on the road, and that she laughed rarely but completely, like weather breaking. They bickered about pacing on the trail up to the orchards. They argued about whether goats had opinions (they did; Sera was right). Felix caught himself telling her a joke and was so startled by the sound of his own laughter that he had to sit down on a wall.

Neither of them mentioned any of this.

On the fourth evening, sitting on the temple steps while the valley went violet below them, Sera said, "Your gods never answer, do they. The prayers you copy."

"No."

"And that doesn't make you angry?"

Felix considered it. "Anger needs somewhere to go. Here, everything stays where it's put. Anger included." He glanced at her. "Why? Do yours answer?"

"My god died," Sera said. "Or slept. Or was eaten — the stories disagree. What's left is dragon-fire, and dragon-fire is going out, and I am the last keeper of a religion with one member and no miracles." She turned her hands palm-up, studying them. "So I suppose I understand keeping faith with silence."

"It's not faith," Felix said quietly. "Faith implies hope. What I have is more like... habit with incense."

But he said it smiling, and she caught it, and neither of them looked away quickly enough, and the evening got very slightly warmer despite the valley's best efforts.

* * *

On market day, they slipped out through the north gate before the festival drums started, Felix with a forged errand-pass (his forgery was excellent; forgery was, after all, adjacent to calligraphy), Sera with Grudge the mule and the oilcloth-wrapped case she still wouldn't open.

The high hills took a full day. As they climbed, Felix noticed something odd and tried very hard not to notice it harder: Sera's shadow had begun, faintly, to shimmer. Like heat-haze off summer stone, except it was autumn and cold and there was no heat anywhere in this valley, there never was.

"Sera," he said finally. "Your shadow."

She looked. Went still. Then she unwrapped the case with shaking hands, and inside lay a lantern of black iron, empty, its glass clouded — and within the glass, impossibly, a single ember glowed. Dim. Struggling. But glowing.

"It's been dark for two months," she whispered. "Dead dark. I carried it anyway because—" She stopped, swallowed. "Because giving up is also a habit."

The ember pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat remembering its work. And ahead of them, half-hidden in the crags, rose the broken walls Felix had dismissed all his life — the Old Foundations, the ruins with nothing in them but goats.

Except now, faintly, from beneath the ground, from under the glass-flat steppe beyond the hills, Felix heard — or felt, in his teeth, in his blood — a hum.

Like a door. Like something enormous, patient, and warm, waiting behind it.

And like ink beginning, very slowly, to dry in the wrong direction.
