## 3. What the Valley Forgot

Sera Ashfall had tracked the ember-scent across four kingdoms, two deserts, and one extremely rude mountain pass, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about following a dying fire: trust the wind, trust her own nose, and never, ever trust a map drawn by a man who had clearly never left his study.

The valley broke all three rules by lunchtime.

The wind here was wrong. It moved, technically — it pushed at the dry grass and tugged at the loose strands escaping her braid — but it carried nothing. No scent of rain coming over the ridge, no smoke from distant hearths, no mineral bite of stone. The air tasted like the inside of a clean jar. She kept turning in slow circles on the hill path like a dog trying to find a smell that wasn't there.

"Are you ill?" Felix asked behind her. He had the particular tone of a man who had been waiting politely to ask for ten minutes and could contain himself no longer. "You keep doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Rotating." He demonstrated with a small, apologetic turn of his shoulders, which was somehow the funniest thing she'd seen all week, though she refused to let him know it. "Like a compass needle that's given up on north."

"I'm reading the wind," Sera said.

"There's no wind."

"There's *almost* wind. There's the shape where wind should be." She held up a hand as if cupping water. "Every valley has weather moving through it. This one has weather going around it. Like a river splitting around a stone."

Felix considered this with the same careful attention he gave everything, his ink-stained fingers resting on the strap of his satchel. He'd packed for their expedition like a librarian preparing for war: spare quills, blotting sand, three different inks, a wax tablet, and — when she'd raised an eyebrow at it — an entire second robe.

"In case the first one gets dirty," he'd explained solemnly, as if this were obviously how expeditions worked.

Now he squinted up the hillside. "So the valley is... avoiding its own weather?"

"That would be my guess."

"And your guesses are usually right?"

"No," Sera said cheerfully. "That's why I keep making them. Eventually one sticks."

He laughed — a short, startled sound, as though laughter were another prayer the valley might punish him for — and then pressed his lips together like a man who'd sworn off sweets. She was beginning to collect these small suppressed reactions the way other travelers collected coins. Felix Brightwater, temple scribe, debt-bound, dutiful to the point of self-erasure, and apparently under the impression that joy required permission.

The self-writing prayer rode in her breast pocket, folded into oilcloth because Felix had insisted anything that rewrote itself should be treated like a venomous snake with excellent penmanship. Twice on the climb she'd felt warmth through the cloth. Not heat exactly. More like the memory of heat, the way a hearthstone stays warm long after the fire dies — except this valley had never had a fire to begin with.

She checked it anyway, crouched behind a boulder while Felix studied a lichen with scholarly suspicion.

The prayer had changed again. Last night it had been nine lines; now there were eleven, and the new ones weren't copied scripture anymore. They were directions.

*Up the sheep track past the split stone.*
*Where the grass forgets to grow.*
*Knock, and be answered.*

"Well," Sera said, showing him. "Your gods have stopped being subtle."

"The gods of the valley aren't *my* gods," Felix said automatically, and then paused, looking troubled, as though the sentence had come out wrong in his mouth. "They've never answered anyone. That's rather the whole point of them. We write the prayers, we file the prayers, we burn the prayers once a season in a ceremony that is mostly an excuse for the priests to argue about whose handwriting is worst."

"You burn them? You don't think burning prayers to silent gods might be slightly provocative?"

"It's traditional," he said, with the tone of a man defending a family recipe made entirely of ingredients he hated.

* * *

The split stone announced itself half a mile later — two slabs of granite leaning together like conspirators, tall enough that Sera had to tip her head back to see where they failed to meet. The sheep track wound between them and vanished into a fold of hills she would have sworn, from the valley floor, did not exist.

"Did you know about this?" she asked.

"Know about what?"

"This valley. The shape of it. The way the hills close up behind you." She turned. The pass they'd climbed through that morning was gone. Not hidden — *gone*, replaced by unbroken hillside, smooth as poured candlewax. Her stomach performed a slow, cold roll. "Felix. Look behind us."

He looked. His face went through several expressions in quick succession — confusion, disbelief, the specific blankness of a man checking whether he's dreaming — before settling into something quiet and frightened.

"That's not possible," he said. "I've lived here twenty-six years. There's a shepherd's hut right—" He pointed at empty grass. His hand stayed up for a moment, pointing at nothing, before slowly lowering. "There was a shepherd's hut there. Old Marta's. She used to give me honey cakes when I was small."

Sera touched his arm. Under the sleeve, his pulse was hammering.

"Hey. Brightwater. Look at me." He did. "This isn't your mind playing tricks. My magic died at the border, remember? Every spell I carry just... drains out of me like water through a sieve. I thought the whole valley was dead ground. But dead ground doesn't eat roads." She nodded at the seamless hillside. "Dead ground doesn't hide things. This place is *curated*."

"Curated," Felix repeated faintly.

"Someone decided what stays visible and what doesn't. Someone is still deciding." She pulled out the prayer again, and the warmth through the oilcloth was stronger now, insistent, like a cat pawing at a closed door. "And whoever they are, they want us to keep walking."

"That's exactly what worries me," Felix said. "Things that want people to keep walking usually want them walking somewhere specific. In every story I've ever transcribed, that somewhere is bad."

"Then it's lucky for both of us," Sera said, already moving, "that we're not in a story. We're in a field guide. And field guides have footnotes."

* * *

They came upon the ruins an hour past midday, and Sera knew immediately that they predated the temple — predated, possibly, the concept of temples.

Pillars lay toppled like felled trees, each one carved from a single piece of dark stone shot through with veins of something that glimmered dull red when the light caught it. The walls had been enormous once; even broken, they rose above her head. And everywhere — on lintels, on thresholds, on the shattered face of what might have been a statue — ran script.

Not the valley's current writing. Not any alphabet she knew from the dragon-roads, and she knew alphabets the way sailors knew coastlines.

But Felix knew it.

She watched him walk to the nearest wall and stop as if he'd walked into glass. His hand rose toward the carved letters without touching them.

"This is Old Vessel Script," he whispered. "It's in our oldest copies. Nobody's written it in — I don't even know. A thousand years? More? We only use it for..." He trailed off. Swallowed. "For the binding formulas. The ones that seal the prayer vaults."

"Read me something," Sera said. "Anything."

His finger hovered over a line of characters. His lips moved silently, translating, and she saw the color drain from his face line by line, like a tide going out.

"*Here slept the last unbroken thing,*" he read hoarsely. "*Here the fire gave itself willingly, so that the silence might hold.*" His finger moved to the next line. "*Let no flame wake it. Let no name call it. Let the valley forget, so that it may rest.*"

The wind — the almost-wind, the jar-clean air — went utterly still.

"Felix," Sera said carefully. "What's down here? What did your valley forget?"

"I don't know." His voice was very small. "I genuinely don't know. That's the horror of it. Whatever this is, someone took the knowing out of everyone's heads. Out of *mine*. There's a hole where the answer should be and I can feel the shape of it, the way you feel a missing tooth—"

The ground hummed.

Low, deep, below hearing and yet felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the soles of her boots. Sera had spent her life around fire. She knew every voice it had — the crackle of green wood, the roar of a forge, the whisper of banked coals at three in the morning. What shivered up through the ancient stones now was none of those. It was the voice fire would have if fire were asleep and dreaming, and the dream were vast, and the dreamer were starting to notice the dreamers standing on its eyelid.

Felix grabbed her hand. She didn't object. His palm was ice-cold and hers wasn't much better, and there are moments when dignity is a luxury item.

They followed the hum.

* * *

The door stood at the heart of the ruins, set into the hillside itself, and it was the most beautiful and most terrible thing Sera Ashfall had ever seen.

Thirty feet high, seamless black stone, worked with a relief so old its edges had softened like wax left in the sun — but the image was still legible. A great winged shape, curled around itself, sleeping. Around it, smaller figures knelt in rows upon rows, hundreds of them, arms raised, and from their hands poured lines of carved fire that flowed into the sleeping shape's body like rivers feeding a lake.

A door made of stone, in a land where fire cannot live.

And it was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not stone-stored-warm. Warm the way a living body is warm. Sera laid her bare palm against it and felt a slow, rhythmic pulse, steady as a heartbeat, patient as geology. Her eyes stung. Her throat closed. Because she knew this feeling — had chased its ghost across half the world, had watched the last dragon dim by degrees while she begged the sky for an answer.

This was fire. Real fire. Ancient fire. Imprisoned fire.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you're alive."

"Sera." Felix's voice cracked on her name. "Step back from it. Please."

But she was already pressing her forehead to the warm stone, and the truth was assembling itself inside her with terrible, beautiful clarity, each piece clicking home like bones setting. The magicless valley. The weather that detoured. The roads that forgot themselves. The silent gods who had never answered — because they weren't gods at all, or weren't only gods, or because answering was the one thing they could never afford to do. The self-writing prayer, climbing out of the dark like a hand reaching up from deep water.

The valley wasn't dead. The valley was a lid.

Everything flat and gray and god-forsaken about this place — the reason no sorcerer settled here, the reason her spells curdled at the border — wasn't a curse. It was a *seal*, maintained at the cost of every wild thing, every bright thing, every answered prayer, for longer than the temple had existed. Longer than names.

Something was buried beneath the glass steppe. Something the world had fought a war to put down here, using dragon-fire for chains and forgetting for mortar. And it had been lying there in the warm dark, breathing through the cracks, for a thousand years.

And it had just started knocking back.

"Sera." Felix's hand closed on her shoulder, gentle and trembling. "Whatever you're thinking — and I can see you thinking it, it's written all over your face like bad news — please remember that I am a coward, and I would like to leave."

She turned to look at him, and found her eyes wet and her mouth curved despite everything, because terror and hope, it turned out, felt identical from the inside.

"Felix," she said, "I don't think we're allowed to leave. Look."

He looked. She watched him look.

The prayer in her breast pocket had burned through the oilcloth — actually burned, though the valley permitted no flame — and lay open against her ribs, glowing softly gold. Eleven lines had become thirteen. And the newest words, written in ink that shimmered like embers, said only:

*You knocked. I heard you.*
*Come in.*
