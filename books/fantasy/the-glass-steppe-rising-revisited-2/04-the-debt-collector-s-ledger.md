## 4. The Debt Collector's Ledger

The summons came at dawn, folded into the slot of Felix's cell door like a knife slipped between ribs.

*Brother Brightwater. The High Priest requests your presence in the Ledger Room after morning offices. Come alone. Bring nothing.*

Felix read it three times, then a fourth, hoping the words might rearrange themselves into something less terrifying. They did not. In eleven years of service, he had never once been summoned to the Ledger Room. Scribes were summoned to the scriptorium. Novices were summoned to the chapel. The Ledger Room was where the temple's debts lived — the great iron-bound book that recorded every copper owed by every soul bound to the Quiet Hours, and where, it was whispered among the younger scribes, the Debt Collector himself kept his desk.

Nobody had ever seen the Debt Collector. Some said he was a priest who had taken vows of silence so absolute he no longer existed. Some said he was the god. Most agreed it was better not to wonder.

Felix dressed with hands that would not quite cooperate. His collar buttoned wrong twice. He caught his reflection in the water basin and made himself stop fidgeting.

"You're a scribe," he told his reflection sternly. "You copy prayers. You are profoundly boring. Nobody summons profoundly boring people to terrible things."

His reflection did not look convinced, and neither was he. Because there was the matter of the prayer hidden beneath his floorboard — the one that had rewritten itself overnight in ink that was not ink, in a hand that was almost but not quite his own. He had checked on it before dressing. It had grown by two lines since yesterday.

*The chains are old,* the new lines read. *The chains are old and they have my name on them, though they wear yours.*

He had put the floorboard back over it very quickly, the way one closes a door on a room where something is breathing.

* * *

The Ledger Room sat behind the sanctuary, down a corridor Felix had walked past a thousand times without ever seeing a door. Now the door stood open, exhaling air that smelled of dust and candle tallow and something else — something faintly warm, like sun on stone, which was absurd, because nothing in the valley was warm.

High Priest Ondrel sat at a desk that could have served as an altar, before a book the size of a tombstone. He was a spare man, dry as August grass, with eyes the color of weak tea, and he greeted Felix with a nod that contained no warmth whatsoever but also, notably, no anger. That frightened Felix more than anger would have.

"Brother Brightwater." Ondrel closed the ledger with a soft, final thump. "Sit."

Felix sat. The chair was cold. Everything here was cold, except for that impossible thread of warmth threading through the air like a hair caught in sunlight.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"No, Your Reverence." A small lie. Several large ones, actually, stacked neatly like copied pages.

Ondrel studied him for a long moment. "Eleven years ago, when you were brought to us, do you recall the terms of your bond?"

"Three hundred marks," Felix recited, because he had recited it ten thousand times, in fear and in hope and once, memorably, while drunk on festival wine. "Payable in labor at the standard rate of two marks per month, plus room and board deductions. I've paid down... some of it."

"One hundred and ninety-four marks remain," Ondrel said. "Which is curious. Because, Brother Brightwater, your family's debt was discharged in full four generations ago."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere far off, a bell rang the third hour, and its sound seemed to arrive late, as if even sound had to be careful in this place.

"I don't understand," Felix said.

"No," Ondrel agreed. "You wouldn't. Sit still. This is the part where most people weep, and I'd rather you waited until I finish."

* * *

What Ondrel told him, Felix would spend the rest of his life trying to unhear.

The valley had not always been silent. Once — before the temple, before the glass flats, before anyone now living — the gods of this place had spoken freely, and one of them had spoken *too much*, too loudly, too terribly, until the others had done the only thing left to do. They had buried him. Not in earth. In silence. And a seal built of silence requires feeding, the way a fire requires wood.

"The first currency was dragon-fire," Ondrel said. "When that failed, they turned to older fuel. Names. A name, freely surrendered, is a remarkable thing, Brightwater. It burns slowly. It burns for generations."

"My family—"

"Sold their name. Willingly. All of it — not just their surname, but the true name underneath, the one the world uses to know you. In exchange, the temple forgave their debts and guaranteed their line protection." Ondrel's tea-colored eyes settled on him. "Your line has been paying interest ever since. Every child born to your blood is born already owing. You were never working off three hundred marks, Brother. You were working off *yourself*. Two marks a month is simply what we deduct from what the seal takes regardless."

Felix discovered he was gripping the arms of the chair hard enough to whiten his knuckles. "Then what am I? What do I call myself, if — if my name is down there, in the dark, being—"

"You call yourself Felix Brightwater. That's the cruel genius of it. The name you use was crafted as a vessel — shaped to fit the stolen name like a sheath fits a blade. You answer to it. You think with it. But it isn't yours. It never was." Ondrel paused, and for the first time something that might have been regret crossed his face, quickly, like a cloud over water. "I'm sorry. Truly. I inherited this ledger, as my predecessor did. The arithmetic doesn't change because the accountant changes."

"And the prayer," Felix heard himself say. His voice sounded distant, someone else's voice, a voice belonging to a man whose name was being slowly eaten somewhere under the glass flats. "The prayer that rewrites itself. In my hand."

"Ah." Ondrel leaned back. "So you've seen it. Good. That saves an awkward conversation." He tapped the great ledger once. "The god below tests his chains, Brightwater. He pushes against them the way a prisoner counts his steps. And because your name is part of those chains — woven into them, generation upon generation — he can reach through you. Those self-writing pages aren't miracles. They're *letters*. He's writing through your blood, using your hand, because your hand is the only hand down here connected to him."

"What does he want?"

"What any prisoner wants." Ondrel smiled, and it was the worst thing Felix had seen all day, and Felix had seen his own life described as collateral in a debt ledger. "He wants out. Or failing that, he wants a key. And you, Brightwater, are looking more and more like a key with every page you write."

* * *

Felix didn't remember walking out. He found himself in the cloister garden, sitting on the low wall beside the herb beds, holding a sprig of rosemary he had apparently picked without noticing. The brothers moved past him on their errands, nodding, smiling their mild smiles, and Felix looked at each of them and thought: *do you know? Have any of you ever known? Was anything here ever real?*

The rosemary snapped in his fingers. He stared at it.

Eleven years. Eleven years of copying prayers to gods who never answered, believing his silence was piety and his patience was virtue, believing that someday the ledger would balance and he would walk out of the valley a free man with his name intact and his life ahead of him. And the whole time—

The whole time he had been kindling. Slowly burning. Fuel laid down by ancestors who had sold him before he existed, to keep a monster asleep, and nobody — not one person in eleven years — had thought him worth the truth.

"There you are."

Sera Ashfall dropped onto the wall beside him, close enough that her shoulder nearly touched his. She smelled of road dust and woodsmoke, an impossibility in a land where nothing burned. She had been asking questions again; he could see it in her face, the restless brightness of her, and normally that restlessness irritated him. Today it felt like the only warm thing in the valley.

"You look," she said, "like a man who's been having a very educational morning."

"You have no idea."

"Try me. I once watched a dragon sneeze itself inside-out. My standards for shocking are high."

Despite everything — despite the ledger and the floorboard and the letter breathing under his cell — Felix laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, a coin he hadn't known he was carrying.

"They sold my name," he said. "My whole family. Generations ago. To feed the thing your flame is leading you to. I'm not a debtor, Sera. I'm *fuel*."

Sera went very still. He watched her take it in — watched the dragon-keeper in her surface, the quick calculating mind beneath, and then, unexpectedly, something softer than either.

"Felix," she said quietly.

"That isn't even my real name. That's the point. It's a coat someone tailored to fit around the hole where my name used to be."

"It's the name of the man who showed me which roads flood and which herbs cure fevers and who cheats outrageously at cards," Sera said. "It's the name of the only person in this valley who walks like he's listening to something. So it's real enough for me." She hesitated, and her hand hovered near his, not quite touching. "The self-writing prayer. Is it still growing?"

"Two lines since dawn." His mouth was dry. "*The chains are old, and they have my name on them, though they wear yours.*"

Sera's breath caught. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, urgent, the voice of a woman who had spent years chasing a dying flame and recognized, at last, smoke.

"Felix. That's not a prisoner talking." Her eyes found his. "That's a prisoner who knows exactly which link to pull."

Behind them, unnoticed, the rosemary sprig in Felix's open palm had begun, very faintly, to smolder.
