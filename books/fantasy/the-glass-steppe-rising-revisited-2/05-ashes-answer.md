## 5. Ashes Answer

Sera Ashfall had told lies in better inns than this one.

She sat across from Felix Brightwater in the common room of the Drowsy Lantern, where the fire — a poor, apologetic thing, barely more than embers arranged to look hopeful — kept going out whenever she looked at it too long. The valley did that. It ate flame the way other places ate gossip: quietly, completely, and without any sense of shame.

Felix was copying something. Of course he was. He copied everything, the way other men drummed their fingers. His pen scratched along, steady as rain, and Sera watched him over the rim of her cup and thought: *I have tracked this man's handwriting across four hundred miles without knowing it.*

Because that was the truth she hadn't said yet. The prayer that rewrote itself in his temple — the ink that moved like something alive — matched, stroke for stroke, the script burned into the last dragon's shoulder scales. She had spent eleven years learning to read that script. She had watched her order dwindle from forty keepers to three, then two, then one, each of them dying with the same word on their lips: *find it, find it, find it.*

And here it was, wearing a scribe's robe and apologizing when he bumped the table.

"You're staring," Felix said, not looking up.

"I'm thinking."

"That's worse." He glanced up, and there it was again — that flicker of humor he tried to hide under all that debt-bound solemnity, like a coin hidden under a floorboard. "You've been thinking for three days now. Since the door. You think loudly, Sera."

"Then you know what I'm about to say."

"I know it's going to cost me money," he said. "Everything you're about to say costs me money. That's what happens when your entire existence is collateral."

*If only you knew how right you are.* She set down her cup. Outside, the valley lay under its blanket of nothing — no wind-magic stirring the grass, no charms keeping the lamps bright, just ordinary dusk and ordinary dark, so thick with silence it felt deliberate. A cage didn't rattle if the prisoner never woke. She understood that now. She'd stood in front of that humming stone door and felt heat coming through rock that hadn't known warmth since before her grandmother's grandmother learned to fly.

"Felix," she said. "The dragon is dying."

His pen stopped.

"The last one. Her name is Vessaneth, though she hasn't answered to it in years — she answers to *old girl*, mostly, because that's what I called her when I found her, and dragons forgive anything except being forgotten." Sera heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it went when feeling got too large for the room. "She was old when the war ended. Whatever the war was. She's been holding on ever since, and she's done holding on. I've watched her flame shrink every winter. Last month it guttered so low I could hold my hand above her throat."

"What does that—" He stopped himself. She watched him arrive at it, watched the color leave his face by degrees, like sunset. "God-fire. That's what you came for. Not the valley. Not ruins. The door."

"Not to open it blindly," she said quickly. "I'm not an idiot, whatever my order said about me. But god-fire is the only fire that rekindles a line. Dragon-fire comes from dragon-fire — that's the whole tragedy of us. We can't make more of ourselves. We can only keep what we were given. And everything we were given is sitting behind that stone door, burning in the dark, while your valley sits on top of it pretending to be a place where nothing has ever happened."

The innkeeper drifted past with bread and retreated fast; even here, in the dullest town in creation, people had learned when a conversation had teeth.

Felix set down his pen with exaggerated care, the way he did everything, as though the world might charge him for carelessness. "You want me to open it."

"I want *us* to open it. Together. Carefully. There are rites — my order kept fragments, half a hymn and a quarter of a warning—"

"And the other three-quarters?"

"Are why there's only one keeper left," she admitted.

He laughed — one short, startled bark, gone as soon as it arrived. Then he wasn't laughing. "Sera. Three nights ago I showed you a prayer that wrote itself. Ink moving against every law of this valley. And you looked at that door and felt *heat*, in a land where candles die. Do you understand what's asking to get out? Because I don't. And I copy prayers for a living. Silence is the one scripture I actually believe in."

"Then believe this instead." She leaned forward. Around them the common room murmured on — dice, dishes, a drunk singing softly about a miller's wife — and none of it mattered, none of it would matter, in ten years or fifty, when the last dragon was a story and the last keeper was a grave marker. "Vessaneth carried the wounded off the field at Cindervale. She burned herself hollow doing it. Every dragon who survived did the same — they gave their fire to build the cage, willingly, because someone had to, because the alternative was a god walking free with war in its mouth. They didn't lose the war, Felix. They *ended* it. And the payment was everything after."

His hands had gone still on the table. "You knew that at the door."

"I suspected. Now I know. I asked her, before I left. I sat with my head against her jaw and asked her what was under the glass steppe, and she said—" Sera's voice caught. She hated that it caught. Eleven years of discipline, undone by a dying dragon's honesty. "She said, *a brother who chose wrongly. Keep him sleeping, little keeper, unless the world needs waking more than it needs peace.*"

The fire between them sighed and went out. Neither of them moved to relight it. In the gloom, Felix's face was all shadow and stubbornness.

"So ask me properly," he said.

"What?"

"You've been circling it for three days. Ask me properly. What do you want from me?"

Sera Ashfall had faced border wardens, debt-lords, one genuinely terrifying customs goose, and the long slow death of everything she loved. None of it had made her heart do what it did now, which was attempt to exit through her ribs.

"I want you to help me open the door," she said. "And I want you to survive it. Both. In that order, if the gods are kind, and they aren't, but I've decided to be rude to them about it anyway."

"And if opening it frees a war-god? If your careful rites are wrong? If the thing under the steppe wakes up angry?" He shook his head. "Sera, my family sold my name to that temple. I am owned down to the syllables. If I break the seal, I break the only thing keeping the priests from collecting me like a bad harvest. If I *don't*—"

"—the dragon dies, and the fire dies with her, and the world gets smaller forever." She reached across the table. Her hand hovered near his, not quite touching; touching felt like a promise she hadn't earned yet. "I know. I've done the sums too. That's why I'm asking *you*. Not a soldier, not a priest. A scribe. Because whoever built that cage built it out of words, Felix — out of names and vows and written things. And you're the only person I've met in this whole buried valley who treats words like they're alive."

"They are alive," he said quietly. "That's rather the problem."

"Then let them live somewhere other than under a mountain."

For a long moment he said nothing. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged; the valley's small nightly noises went on, oblivious, and Sera thought absurdly that she would miss them — this dull, safe, magicless place, this man with ink on his fingers who apologized to furniture.

"If I do this," Felix said at last, "we do it my way. Slowly. I read everything first. Everything. If the door has instructions, I read them twice. If it has warnings, I read them until I've memorized the grammar of the warning." He held up a finger. "And if it goes wrong — if that thing starts waking — we close it. Even if closing it costs us. Even if closing it costs *me*. Swear it."

"Sera Ashfall swears nothing she doesn't mean."

"Then mean this one."

She looked at him — really looked, past the scribe's stoop and the debt-shadow and the careful hands, at the man underneath who had hidden a living prayer from priests because some part of him already knew the silence was a lie. Warmth, sudden and unhelpful, spread through her chest like brandy.

"I swear," she said. "We close it if it turns. On my order, on my life, on the last flame of my house. Happy?"

"Terrified," he said. "But yes."

She took his hand at last. His grip was ink-stained and warm and steadier than hers, and neither of them let go for longer than strictly necessary, and the innkeeper, wisely, brought no candles.

"One more thing," Sera said, because courage was a muscle and hers was already torn. "When this is over — whatever 'over' looks like — I'm taking you to see her. Vessaneth. Dragons remember everyone who helps them. It's the one vanity we never bred out."

"We?"

"Dragons and keepers. Family. Bad-tempered, hoarding, occasionally flammable family." She smiled despite everything. "You'll fit right in."

Felix exhaled slowly, looking down at their joined hands as though checking them for fine print. "Tomorrow," he said. "Tomorrow I go to the temple archive. There are records older than the priesthood — sealed folios, the ones they don't let scribes touch. If anyone wrote down what's under that door and how to bargain with it, it'll be there."

"And how exactly does a debt-bound scribe get into a forbidden archive?"

Felix Brightwater gave her a small, crooked smile that she would remember, later, in harder hours, like a coal kept in ash.

"I steal," he said, "very, very politely."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the temple bell rang the Quiet Hours — and for the first time since Sera had entered the valley, the silence that followed it sounded less like peace, and more like something below the earth turning over in its sleep.
