## 6. The God Beneath the Steppe

The stone door did not open so much as *admit* them, the way a housekeeper opens a door to guests she has been expecting all week and is mildly annoyed to see arrive late.

Felix had expected darkness, or fire, or some grand violence of light. What he got instead was a staircase — smooth, wide, worn in the middle by footsteps that could not possibly have been human — descending into a warmth that pressed against his skin like bathwater. Behind him, Sera's lantern hissed and guttered.

"It doesn't want the flame," she said quietly. "Look."

The lantern flame leaned toward the stairwell like a child tugging at a parent's hand. Sera unhooked it from its bracket, hesitated, then set it on the ground at the threshold. It stayed there, burning contentedly, as if it had come home.

"Right," said Felix, whose voice came out thinner than he'd intended. "We're leaving our fire with the door. That's fine. Completely normal. People do this sort of thing every day."

"Felix." Sera touched his elbow. In the dim glow bleeding up from below, her eyes were the color of banked coals. "You can still turn back. I mean it. Whatever your debt is — whatever this valley made you — you don't owe it your feet."

"That's just it," Felix said. "I think I do."

He started down the stairs before the cowardice in his legs could organize a formal objection.

* * *

The staircase went on far longer than any staircase had a right to. Felix counted four hundred steps before giving up, mostly because counting required thinking about how deep beneath the glass steppe they were, and thinking about that required imagining several thousand tons of sky pressing down on his head, and he had decided sometime around step two hundred that imagination was not his friend today.

Sera walked beside him, one hand trailing the wall. "It's warm here," she murmured. "Not hot. Warm. Like something breathing."

"Please don't say breathing."

"Like something *sleeping*, then."

"Better. Marginally."

The stairs ended at last in a cavern so vast that Felix's scribe-trained mind reached instinctively for comparisons and found only inadequate ones. It was like standing inside a cathedral built for giants, except no cathedral had walls that shimmered like cooling glass, or a floor that reflected their two small figures upward into a darkness above as though they were walking on the inside of a black mirror. And no cathedral — Felix had copied enough hymns to speak with authority — had a heart.

That was the only word for it. At the center of the cavern, half-buried in the glassy floor, lay an enormous shape: not flesh exactly, not stone exactly, but something between — a vast slow curve like the shoulder of a sleeping animal, veined with light that pulsed. Faintly. Patiently. Once every ten breaths or so, gold light would swell along those veins and fade again, and each time it did, Felix felt the pulse pass through the floor, through his boots, through his ribs, as though the cavern itself had a heartbeat and he had been invited, without being asked, to share it.

"Oh," Sera whispered. She had gone very still, the way she did around dragons — Felix had seen it once, at the valley's edge, when she'd spoken of the last of them. Reverence and terror holding hands. "Oh, you're real. You're *real*."

"I could have gone my whole life," Felix said faintly, "without learning that 'oh' could sound like that."

The light pulsed. And when it faded, something stood where nothing had stood before.

It was shaped like a person, roughly, if a person were drawn by someone who had only ever had statues described to them. Tall, robed in shadow that moved wrong, its face a smooth suggestion rather than a fact. But its voice — when it spoke, and it spoke directly into Felix's mind, warm as the air, gentle as a hand smoothing a page flat —

*You brought me a reader.*

Felix's mouth went dry. "I— we weren't—"

*Four thousand years,* the god said, and though it had no face, Felix heard something like weariness in it, ancient and bottomless. *Four thousand years of silence. Do you know what it is to be a story that no one tells? To be a prayer that no one remembers how to say? Your temple copies prayers to gods who cannot hear. I am the god who hears everything, and no one copies anything to me but dust.*

"You're the one rewriting my transcriptions," Felix said. It wasn't a question.

*Someone had to teach you the old script. Someone had to make you curious. Curiosity is the only prayer that travels.* The shape drifted closer — or didn't move at all, and simply was closer, the way dreams work. *And now here you are. The debt-child. The ink of my chains, come walking down my stairs on his own feet.*

"My what?"

"Felix." Sera's voice cracked across the cavern. She had stepped slightly in front of him, hand hovering near the knife at her belt, which Felix appreciated even though he suspected it was roughly as useful as threatening a mountain with a butter knife. "Careful. Gods bargain. That's what they *do*."

*I bargained once,* the god said, and the weariness curdled into something older and colder. *Ask her kind how that ended.*

* * *

It happened the way a memory happens when someone else is telling it — except no one was telling it, unless the cavern itself was, unless the pulse in the glass floor was a heartbeat and a voice at once.

Felix saw:

A sky full of wings. Not a few wings — a horizon of them, dragons in their hundreds wheeling over a world still young and green, and beneath them, walking calmly through the smoke of a burned city, shapes of light wearing robes, doing the calm terrible arithmetic of war. He saw the first treaty written in fire on a mountainside. He saw it broken. He saw gods learn that dragon-fire could unmake divinity, and dragons learn that worship could chain a dragon's heart like a leash, and both sides discover, with the enthusiasm of children finding matches, that the other could be made to suffer forever.

He saw the last battle. Not fought — *ended*. A council of gods and the eldest of dragons standing together over a defeated thing, one of their own, a god of dawn-fires who had burned too greedily and too widely, and choosing not to kill it because killing a god, they had learned, only made more war. Instead they buried it. They built a valley over it like a lid. They took the magic out of the land the way one empties a room of furniture, and they asked the dragons — asked, *asked*, the memory held the shape of a request, solemn and grieving — for a thread of living fire to weave into the seal.

And the dragons gave it. Willingly. Because a chain woven from willing fire holds better than any forged one, and because the eldest dragon had looked down at the buried god and said, in words Felix somehow understood across four thousand years:

*Sleep well, cousin. We will carry the cost so you don't have to wake to finish what you started.*

The vision folded shut like a book.

Felix found himself on his knees on the warm glass, Sera's arm around his shoulders, her heart hammering against his back fast enough that he could feel it through two coats.

"The dragon-fire," she was saying, half to him, half to the dark. "The seal runs on dragon-fire. That's why it's dying — why the flame I've tracked is dying — it's been *burning itself out* keeping this thing asleep, and no one—"

*No one remembered,* the god agreed softly. *That was the price of the seal, little keeper. A valley that forgets. A debt paid in names.* The faceless head turned toward Felix, and Felix felt its attention settle on him like a hand on his hair. *Do you understand yet, reader? Every Brightwater born in this valley has been a page in my prison. Your name was the first line. Your silence was the binding. And now the fire that holds me is guttering out, and when it dies, I wake — and everything your ancestors built burns with me.*

"Then what do you want?" Felix managed. His voice sounded like someone else's.

*What I have always wanted.* The god knelt — knelt, before a scribe, and the absurdity of it almost made Felix laugh hysterically — *To be remembered. To walk out of this hole under my own name instead of waking under none at all. You could carry me, Felix Brightwater. Not my body. My story. Tell the world what lies beneath its quietest valley. Let them choose: feed the seal, or free me properly. Either way, I stop being a secret. Secrets are the only chains that rust.*

"And my debt?" Felix heard himself ask, hating himself for asking. *Everything you want, at everyone else's cost,* whispered a sensible corner of his mind, and the rest of him told that corner to shut up. "If I carry your secret out — the ledger, the priests, the name they sold — all of it. Gone?"

*Gone,* the god said warmly. *Free as morning. No priest owns you. No ledger holds you. You would be the man who told the truth no one dared tell — honored in every city with a library.*

Felix's chest ached. Four thousand years of Brightwaters bent over ledgers, and here was the door out, offered politely, by the thing in the cage.

Beside him, Sera had gone rigid. He knew her well enough now — eight days of shared road and shared silences — to read the tension in her shoulders. She was doing the same arithmetic he was. *Tell the world. Let them choose. And while they deliberate for a generation, a war-god sits under a failing seal.*

"Don't answer yet," Sera said quietly. "Felix. Look at the floor. Look at the veins."

He looked. The golden light pulsed — and this close, this long, he could see what he hadn't before. The light wasn't steady. Between pulses there were flickers. Stutters. Little gaps where the glow failed to fill the whole vein, like a candle burning down inside a lantern of glass.

Like a fire running out of things to burn.

Above them — four hundred steps up, beyond the door, beyond the flats — somewhere at the valley's edge, a dragon was dying. The last one. The last thread of willing fire.

And Felix understood, with the awful clarity of a man finishing a sentence he'd been copying his whole life, that the god had shown him the truth of the war honestly — and had shown him nothing at all about what waking would cost. Honesty, in a cage, was still a bargaining tool.

"I need to think," Felix said aloud.

*Of course,* said the god, warm as banked embers, patient as debt. *But think quickly, reader. The fire is low. And I am not the only one who knows it.*

Far above them, muffled through four thousand years of stone and silence, Felix thought he heard — though it might have been his own heartbeat, though it might have been the seal groaning in its sleep — a bell begin to ring in the Temple of Quiet Hours.

They had left the door unlocked behind them.
