## 7. Prayers With Teeth

The trouble began with bread.

Felix noticed it on his third morning back from the hills — the morning after he'd told Sera, in a whisper that felt like tearing paper, everything the god beneath the glass had shown him. He was in the temple kitchen begging yesterday's loaf from Brother Anselm when old Marta from the tannery came pounding up the steps, breathless as a woman chased by bees.

"Brother Anselm," she gasped. "My son. The fever broke. Three nights it burned him and this morning he sat up and asked for *porridge*, the little wretch."

Anselm beamed. "A miracle of the Quiet Ones—"

"It's no miracle." Marta's face was gray. "I prayed for it at the roadside shrine. And now my hands—" She held them out. They were smooth as river stones, every callus gone, every scar, every mark of forty years' work erased to soft pink nothing. "I can't grip a hide. I can't hold a needle. I prayed for my boy and something took its payment out of my skin."

Felix stood very still by the bread shelf, and the loaf in his hands felt suddenly like evidence.

*Everything answered is taken,* the god had said, in that voice like warm honey poured over broken glass. *Nothing in this world is given. Only borrowed.*

He hadn't understood then. He understood now.

* * *

By noon there were four more stories. By evening, eleven.

The cooper's wife had prayed for rain for her garden; it rained, sweet and steady, and when she went to thank the sky her garden was fine but her memory of her wedding day had been scooped out clean as a melon rind. A shepherd boy who'd asked for his lost dog got the dog back — and lost three years of summers instead, standing in his own pasture weeping over memories that had gone to fog. The prayers were being answered, all across the valley, for the first time in living memory. And each answer came with teeth.

"They're not prices," Felix said to Sera that night, in the scribe's alcove where they'd begun sleeping in shifts, one always awake. "That's what I keep getting wrong. It's not a toll. It's a *feeding*. Every answered prayer is a thread pulled loose from someone's life and wound around the seal. The god isn't cruel. It's just — hungry the way a cage is hungry. It eats whatever touches it."

Sera sat cross-legged on the floor sharpening a knife she had no practical use for. "And your handwriting?"

He showed her. His transcription ledger lay open between them, and overnight the neat columns of copied prayers had rearranged themselves into words he hadn't written: *LET ME OUT AND I WILL PAY EVERY DEBT IN THIS VALLEY. I AM GENEROUS. I AM PATIENT. I AM SO TIRED.*

"Charming," Sera said. "It writes like a merchant."

"It writes like something that has been alone under a glass steppe for nine hundred years." Felix closed the ledger harder than he meant to. "Sera, I can't tell if I pity it or if I want to run until my boots wear through."

"You can do both. I recommend both. I've been doing both since I was nineteen." She set down the knife and looked at him with those ember-colored eyes that saw straight through him, which was either wonderful or unbearable depending on the hour. "But running's off the table, love. You know that."

He did know it. That was the problem.

Because the whispers weren't only in the ledger anymore.

* * *

They were in the well water, faintly, if you drank deep and held very still. They were in the wind off the flats at dusk, saying things like *your mother loved you more than your brother, didn't she? Ask me. I'll make it true.* They were in the dreams of anyone who'd ever knelt at a shrine and wished for anything, which was everyone, because wishing is the one prayer nobody thinks counts.

On the fourth day, the village turned on the temple.

It happened fast, the way rot happens — invisible and then everywhere. Felix was copying (or being written through) when the shouting started in the courtyard, and he looked out the window onto a scene he would remember even if the god ate the rest of him: two hundred villagers, pitchforks and torches and, in one memorable case, a goose held aloft like a standard. Old Marta at their head, her too-smooth hands wrapped in bandages.

"The Quiet Ones never answered!" she cried. "Nine hundred years of tithes and candles and silence, and the moment the true god stirs, these liars move to shut it away again! Open the door! Open the door and let it pay us what it promised!"

"We never promised you anything," High Priest Corvain said from the temple steps, and his voice was terrible and calm, the voice of a man who had spent decades being ignored and found it preferable. "You are being *farmed*. Can none of you feel the hook in your own mouths?"

The crowd answered with stones.

Felix watched the first window shatter and felt something in his chest go cold and clear, like water freezing into glass. This was what the god wanted. Not the door opened by force — it could wait centuries for force. No. It wanted chaos, fear, a valley eating itself, so that when the temple finally moved to reseal the breach, the people would *beg* for a sacrifice to be found. Any sacrifice. A convenient one.

A scribe with a debt.

"They're going to use me," he said aloud. He was surprised how steady his voice came out.

Sera appeared beside him at the window, soot already on her cheek from where she'd run through the courtyard smoke. "Then we leave tonight."

"And go where? The whisper's in the wells, Sera. It's in the *bread*." He laughed, a small unhinged sound. "There's nowhere in this valley it doesn't reach, and no way out that isn't through it."

"Then we don't run." Her hand found his, calloused against calloused, dragon-keeper's grip. "We stop running. We get ahead of it."

* * *

They didn't get ahead of it.

They got as far as the reliquary corridor before the lamps guttered and High Priest Corvain stepped out of the dark like a man who had been waiting there since before either of them was born. Behind him stood four acolytes, and behind *them*, laid out on a bier like a bride, was a slab of black glass that hummed the same note as the stone door under the steppe.

"Felix Brightwater," Corvain said.

"High Priest." Felix put himself half in front of Sera, an instinct so old it predated thought.

"I have read your family's ledger line. Four generations." Corvain's eyes were wet, which somehow made it worse. "Do you imagine I enjoyed knowing? Do you imagine I *slept*? Your name was sold to keep this valley breathing, boy, and the seal has grown thin, and the god has found a throat to speak through, and every hour we delay, some grandmother forgets her daughter's face." He lifted his chin. "The rite requires a name freely surrendered. Yours was purchased, which made it weak — why do you think the chains have been failing? But a name *offered*—"

"Offered by whom?" Sera's knife was out. Felix hadn't even seen her draw it. "You'd carve a man's self out of him and call it mercy?"

"I'd call it arithmetic." For a flicker, the old man's composure cracked, and underneath was just a tired keeper of a dying fire, exactly like her. "One name against ten thousand. One scribe against a war that burned the sky black. You've seen the ruins, girl. You know what woke means."

Felix did know. He'd seen it in the god's gift-vision: dragons like weather, gods like armies, the world a battlefield for so long that peace had to be built underground and locked.

And yet.

"I'll go," Felix heard himself say.

"*Felix.*"

"Not to the bier. Not carved like a ham." He turned to Corvain, and his heart was hammering but his voice, miraculously, held. "You said freely offered is stronger. Then hear my terms. Nobody drags me anywhere. Nobody touches Sera Ashfall. And I walk to that door on my own feet, at dawn, and I decide at the threshold what I give and how much." He swallowed. "Because there's a difference, High Priest, between paying a debt and letting a god write its own receipt. I'm a scribe. I know the difference better than anyone alive."

Corvain studied him for a long moment. Somewhere above, another window broke, and the goose honked defiantly.

"Dawn," the priest said at last. "The bier comes with us. If you falter, the choice stops being yours."

"He won't falter," Sera said.

It wasn't until the corridor emptied and her arms were around him, her forehead pressed hard against his collarbone, that he let himself shake.

"You're going to offer it something else," she whispered. "Something it wants more than your whole name. I've watched you bargain with ink your entire life. Tell me I'm right."

Felix looked down at her — the woman who'd knocked twice, who tracked dying fire across the world, who saw him truly even now, especially now — and he smiled with what he hoped was reassurance and suspected was terror.

"Get me to that door," he said, "and bring the last dragon. Because if my plan works, nobody gets sacrificed tomorrow."

He didn't add: *and if it fails, remember my face long enough to tell me about it later.*

He was fairly sure she heard it anyway.

* * *

That night, the ledger wrote one final line in his own handwriting, though his hands never touched the page:

*DAWN. BRING THE SCRIBE. I HAVE WAITED NINE HUNDRED YEARS. I CAN WAIT ONE MORE MORNING. PROBABLY.*

Below it, smaller, almost shy:

*I KNOW WHAT HE PLANS. I AM CURIOUS ANYWAY.*

Felix stared at the words until the candle died, and somewhere far below the glass flats, something ancient turned over in its sleep and smiled.
