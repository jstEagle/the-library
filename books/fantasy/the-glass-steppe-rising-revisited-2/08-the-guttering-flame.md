## 8. The Guttering Flame

Sera had spent eleven years chasing a rumor with wings, and in all that time she had never once imagined the last dragon would be *ugly*.

Not ugly, exactly. That was unkind. Worn. A dragon should have been a cathedral of scales and fury, and instead what lay at the valley's eastern edge — half-buried in the pale grass of the steppe like something the earth had tried to swallow and given up on — was old. Her hide was the grey of banked coals, cracked in long seams that glowed faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies. One wing lay folded wrong. Her breath came slow and vast, stirring the grass in waves, and each exhale smelled of woodsmoke and, strangely, of bread.

"Oh," Sera said, because she had rehearsed speeches for this moment for a decade and every one of them abandoned her at once.

The dragon's eye opened. It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of amber left too long in the sun, and it regarded her with an expression Sera could only describe as *unimpressed*.

"You're smaller than I expected," the dragon said. Her voice was gravel poured over honey, and it resonated up through Sera's boots, through her ribs, into her teeth.

"I'm — " Sera stopped. Started again. "You can talk."

"I can talk. You can gawk. Between us we make a whole conversation." The great eye slid half-closed. "Come closer, little ash-keeper. My eyes are not what they were, which is ironic, considering."

Sera climbed the low rise of the dragon's shoulder, her hands finding purchase in the warm seams between scales. Up close the heat was real — faint, guttering, but real — the first true warmth the valley had offered her since the border swallowed her magic at the crossing. She pressed her palm flat against a scale the size of a shield and felt it: a pulse. Slow as a season turning, but there.

*Emberfall*, she thought. The name from her grandmother's songs. The line that had carried fire across the world when the world was young and cold.

"You're dying," she said. Not a question. She'd known it from forty miles out, from the way the flame she tracked had thinned to a thread, a candle seen through fog.

"Everything is dying. It's rather the fashion." The dragon's laugh rattled like a landslide deciding against itself. "But yes. Soon. Which is why you're here, isn't it? I've felt you coming for years. Little spark walking across the world with a lantern in your chest and no idea how to light it."

Sera's throat tightened. "I know how to light it. That's why I'm here. There's god-fire under this valley — I've seen the door, I've heard it humming. God-fire can rekindle your line. Your eggs, your — whatever's left. The Ashfall clan has kept the rites for nine generations waiting for—"

"Stop." The word moved the grass for fifty yards. The dragon's eye opened fully now, and there was something in it Sera had not expected and did not want: pity. "Child. Sit down. This is going to be worse than you think, and you'll want to be sitting."

*

The dragon's name was Vessarath, and she had been alive when the valley was made.

She told it slowly, in the manner of someone who has told a story so few times that telling it hurts, and Sera sat on her shoulder with her knees drawn up and listened while the sun crawled westward.

"There was a war," Vessarath said. "Of course there was a war. There is always a war, eventually, between things that both believe they are the sky. The gods were new then, and hungry, and they burned bright enough to blind. We dragons were older. We burned slower. We thought that made us wiser." A rumble that might have been a sigh. "It only made us flammable."

"The histories say the gods destroyed you."

"The histories were written by priests, dear. Priests write what their gods tell them, and gods lie the way rivers run downhill." The seam-glow along Vessarath's flank flickered. "One god was different. Younger. Kinder, maybe, or just more frightened — frightened gods do the most damage. It saw where the war was going. It saw the world ending under us all. So it did the unthinkable. It surrendered. It let itself be sealed away, and its sealing took its fire off the board, and without its fire the war starved. Both sides put down their torches because there was nothing left worth burning."

"And the valley?"

"The valley is the seal. Every prayer unanswered, every spell gone flat at the border, every crop that grows stubborn and magicless — that's not a curse, child. That's a lock. And here is the part your songs got wrong, the part your grandmothers didn't know or wouldn't say." The amber eye turned to fix on her, and Sera felt very small and very warm. "Dragon-fire was the first chain. Not iron. Not stone. Fire. Freely given. A dragon lay down across the door of that prison and said *burn me instead*, and the seal drank her flame for a thousand years, and when she was spent another came, and another. Willingly. That was the pact. That was the price of peace."

Sera's mouth had gone dry. "And when the dragons ran out—"

"They ran out. All but me. And I—" For the first time, the vast voice faltered. "I ran. Eleven hundred years ago, when my turn came, I looked at the door and I could not do it. I told myself the war was over anyway, that one chain more or less would hold nothing. I flew until my fire was small enough to hide, and I hid, and I told myself I would come back." The glow in her seams dimmed, as if the memory itself cost fuel. "I never came back. And the seal began to starve. And the god beneath it began, very quietly, to dream."

The wind moved the grass. Somewhere behind them, far off toward the temple, a bell rang the hour, thin and toneless in the dead air.

"So the flame I've been tracking," Sera said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, small and far away. "The last living dragon-fire. It wasn't a treasure. It was a *chain*. And it's about to break."

"Now you understand why you found me."

"No." Sera stood up on the dragon's shoulder, because sitting suddenly felt impossible. "No, I don't, because I've spent eleven years — my whole family has spent *nine generations* — keeping the rites alive so that dragon-fire could be reborn. We sang to eggs that will never hatch. We kept a hearth lit in a house that burned down before my mother was born. And you're telling me the answer was never to find fire. The answer was to find a dragon willing to be *chained*."

"Yes."

"To burn as a prisoner. Again. Forever."

"For as long as the god sleeps, yes." Vessarath's voice was gentle, which made it unbearable. "That is what rekindling means, little ash-keeper. Not freedom. Not flight. A hearth that cannot leave its wall. I know the shape of the bargain. I am the one who broke it."

Sera laughed — a short, awful sound with nothing in it. "My grandmother used to sing about Emberfall rising. Wings over the world again. Fire coming home." She scrubbed a hand across her face. "She never once mentioned that home was a prison cell."

"Your grandmother sang what she needed to keep the faith. Faith is a fire too. It needs tending, and sometimes it needs kind lies." The dragon shifted, and the whole hillside of her moved, and Sera had to grab a scale-horn to keep her seat. "But hear me, Sera Ashfall. I did not send for you — no, don't look surprised, why else would a dying dragon let herself be found by the one hunter in the world still carrying our rites? I am done running. If a chain must be forged, let it be forged from me. Let my cowardice be the last thing it burns."

"That's not — " Sera's voice cracked. She hated it. "You're asking me to help you die badly. Slowly. Chained to a door in a valley where nothing grows right, for a thousand years, maybe ten thousand—"

"I'm asking you to help me stop being a deserter." The amber eye was very close now, and very old, and full of something Sera recognized from her own mirror on bad nights. "There are worse fates than a long penance, child. I've tried the alternative. It's called being yourself afterward."

*

They watched the sun go down together, which was a strange thing to do with someone who had watched eleven hundred of them alone.

Vessarath's breathing slowed as the light failed, each exhale longer than the last, and Sera found herself counting them the way she'd once counted her grandmother's coughs in the final winter. When the stars came out — duller here than anywhere else in the world, the valley even stealing brightness from the sky — the dragon spoke again, quieter.

"There is a boy," she said. "A scribe. He smells of ink and fear and something else. Something the god has touched. He came to the edge of my hearing two nights ago, following the writing."

"Felix." Sera's chest did something complicated. "He's — we found the door together. He's bound to the temple. Debt-bound. His family sold his name to feed the seal, though he didn't know it until this week, and he's been walking around ever since looking like a man whose floor turned out to be a trapdoor."

"Mm. And do you love him?"

Sera nearly fell off the dragon. "*What?*"

"I'm eleven hundred years old and dying. I'm allowed to ask blunt questions. Do you love him?"

"I've known him a fortnight."

"I knew my mate in an afternoon. We were at war; it concentrates the mind." The eye glinted with what was unmistakably, horribly, humor. "Well? You're allowed to not answer. But you've been holding his name in your mouth since you said it, the way you hold a coal you don't want to drop."

Sera looked out at the dark valley, at the distant smudge of the temple where Felix was — where Felix was probably being interrogated by a high priest who wanted to erase him, if the shouting she'd heard from the hills that morning meant anything. She thought of him reading the self-writing prayer by candlelight, terrified and meticulous, copying down a god's handwriting because someone had to and no one else could. She thought of the way he'd looked at her when she knocked twice on the temple door, like she was weather coming in.

"He sees me," she said finally. "Everyone else sees the last Ashfall, the flame-chaser, the errand. He looks at me like I'm a person standing next to an errand." She laughed, wetly. "And he's a terrible guide. He got lost going to the hills he grew up beside."

"A fine quality in a scribe and a fatal one in a guide."

"Don't change the subject back to me." She turned on the broad shoulder. "You said you sent for me. Why? If you already know what has to happen — if you're ready to lie down on that door — why drag me across the world first?"

Vessarath was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Sera thought she might have fallen asleep, or died, or both.

"Because a chain freely given holds better than a chain forced," she said at last. "That was always the secret of it. The first dragon who lay down on that door was not captured. She chose. And the god's prison has been weakening ever since the choosing stopped — ever since the last chains were made of debt and duty and sold names instead of consent." The glow along her flank pulsed once, slow, deliberate. "I need a witness, little ash-keeper. Someone who carries the rites, who knows what a willing fire looks like, so that when I burn, it burns as a gift and not a sentence. And I need—" the vast voice dropped, almost shy, an absurd register for a creature the size of a chapel, "—someone to remember that I came back. In the end. After eleven hundred years. I need one person in the world to know that."

Sera's eyes stung. "You ridiculous old coward," she whispered. "You brave, ridiculous old coward."

"Yes," Vessarath agreed comfortably. "Both. Now listen carefully, because I will only have strength to say this once, and then we must go to the door tonight — tonight, do you hear me, before the priest does something irreversible with your scribe. The rite of rekindling requires three voices: the one who burns, the one who tends, and the one who witnesses. You are two of those. But the third..."

The dragon's amber eye turned, slow and meaningful, toward the distant temple, where a single candle still burned in a high window.

"...the third must be someone the god knows by name."

Sera was already sliding down off the great shoulder, landing hard in the grass, her heart hammering out a rhythm that felt less like dread than like a decision arriving early.

"Hold on," she said. "Hold on. I'll bring him. Whatever it costs — I'll bring him to you."

She ran east toward the candle in the window, and behind her, faint as a hearth in the next room, the last dragon-fire guttered — and held.
