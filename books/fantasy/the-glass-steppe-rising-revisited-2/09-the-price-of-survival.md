## 9. The Price of Survival

The stone door was singing.

Felix had heard it hum for years in his dreams without knowing what it was — a low, patient note like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Now he stood before it with Sera's hand tight around his and the high priest's lantern throwing their shadows long across the glass flats, and the song was no longer patient. It was hungry.

"You cannot open it," High Priest Ondrel said. His voice was the voice Felix had obeyed since boyhood: dry, certain, worn smooth as a ledger page. "Child, you were never meant to stand here. You were meant to be *ink*. Come back to the temple. Let us do this properly, with ritual, with mercy—"

"Mercy." Sera did not turn around. Her other hand rested on the satchel at her hip, where the last ember of dragon-fire slept in its iron cradle, guttering like a candle in a draft. "You mean the mercy you showed his grandmother? The mercy of taking her name out of her mouth while she still breathed?"

"The seal must be fed," Ondrel said simply. "That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic."

Felix felt the god beneath them shift — a vast turning-over, like something asleep rolling toward a window. The ground under the glass flats was warm through his boots. It had been warm all day, and he understood now that it had been warm for six hundred years, warm as a body, warm as a held breath.

*Little scribe,* said the voice in his blood, gentle as ink spreading through water. *They come to spend you. She comes to spend the dragon. Everyone arrives at my door with someone else's price in their hands.*

"I know," Felix whispered.

Sera looked at him. In the lantern light her face was all sharp planes and exhaustion, ash still in her hair from the dragon's hollow at the valley's edge, and she was so beautiful to him in that moment that it hurt worse than anything the priest could say. "Felix. Whatever it's whispering—"

"It's not whispering." He laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "That's the terrible part. It's being *reasonable*."

*Tell her,* the god murmured. *Tell her what I offered. Freedom from your debt. Your name returned to you, whole, yours. All you must carry is my secret back into the world. Open the door. Let me rise. The war is old; the hatreds are cold; I have had six hundred years to reconsider.* A pause, silk over stone. *I have not reconsidered. But I say it beautifully.*

"He wants me to free him," Felix said. "In exchange, I get everything. My name. My debt erased. A life."

"And the world gets a war," Sera said flatly.

"And the world gets a war," Felix agreed. "Dragons against gods, fire against faith, and every valley between becomes a battlefield twice over." He looked down at his own hands — scribe's hands, ink-stained, trembling slightly. Hands that had copied ten thousand prayers to gods who never answered, because they couldn't. Because answering cost too much. "Ondrel. You knew. All those years, you knew what my family paid."

Ondrel's lantern dipped, just slightly. "I knew the Brightwaters kept the ledger. I did not know until this week what the ledger *was*." For one unguarded moment the old man looked his age, and older — a keeper of accounts who had finally read the fine print. "Felix, listen to me. The seal is failing. The prayers rewriting themselves, the door singing, the warmth under the flats — it is waking regardless. If we do not feed it a name soon, it will take the whole valley's. Every soul in Quiet Hours, every child in the orchard rows. One name against four thousand. That is the arithmetic."

"That's the arithmetic *you* were taught," Felix said.

"It is the only arithmetic there is!"

"No." Sera stepped forward, and the ember in her satchel flared, casting her shadow huge against the door. "There's another. Dragon-fire built the first chain. Willingly given — do you understand that, priest? The last dragon told me herself, before she told me anything else. The dragons didn't lose the war. They *ended* it. They gave their fire to bind him because no god would bind a god and no mortal could. And your temple took that gift and fed it names instead, generation after generation, because names were cheaper than gratitude." Her voice shook. "She's dying. She has been dying for centuries, holding a chain with her last breath, and none of you ever once said thank you."

The silence after that was very long. Even the god beneath them seemed to hold still.

"So there it is," Felix said quietly. "The priest offers the valley my name. The god offers me the world's ruin. And the dragon—" his throat closed, and he made himself finish, "—the dragon gets asked to burn again as a prisoner, because Sera came all this way to save her line and the price is another cage."

"Felix—"

"I'm not finished." He turned to the door. Up close, the stone was not stone at all but compressed writing — millions of lines of script packed so dense it had become mineral, prayers and bindings and bargains layered like sediment. Somewhere in that strata was his grandmother's name. His mother's. His, waiting. "Everyone keeps bringing prices to this door. So let me bring mine."

*Little scribe,* the god said, and for the first time there was something almost like caution in it. *What are you doing.*

"Reading," Felix said. "It's what I'm for."

He pressed his palm flat against the door and let his blood answer. It came easily now — it had been coming easily for weeks, the self-writing prayer, the ink that moved on the page. His blood rose to the skin of his palm and the door drank it, and the singing changed key, and Felix *saw*.

He saw the structure of the seal the way he'd seen the structure of a sentence: where the clauses joined, where the grammar had rotted, where six hundred years of borrowed names had patched a wall that wanted rebuilding. Names were ink. Ink was memory. Memory was *self* — and the seal didn't need a name torn from someone unwilling.

It needed a name given whole.

"Not taken," Felix said aloud. "Given. That's why it keeps failing. You've been *spending* people, Ondrel. Spending is not the same as giving. The chain was forged from willing fire, and it can be renewed by willing ink." He looked back at Sera, and his eyes were already wet, and he smiled anyway, because if this was the last thing anyone ever remembered of him he wanted it to be a smile. "A whole life. Not just a name on a ledger. Everything I am, poured in as new binding. The god sleeps for another age. The dragon doesn't burn. The valley wakes up magicless and safe and never knows how close it came."

"No," Sera said. Just that. Just *no*, flat and final, and then again, louder, cracking: "*No.* Felix, there has to be another way, there's always another way, we'll find it, we'll—"

"This is the way I choose." He cupped her face in both hands. His fingers were already going faintly numb at the edges, or perhaps that was terror. "You spent seven years tracking a dying flame across half the world because you refuse to let beautiful things end. I love that about you. I love *everything* about you, Sera Ashfall, and I need you to hear me: this isn't sacrifice. This is the first thing I've ever done that wasn't copying someone else's words. This one's mine. I'm writing it myself."

"You don't get to be noble alone," she said fiercely. Tears cut bright lines through the ash on her cheeks. "That's not how vows work. We said—"

"We said together. So be together with me. Hold on to me and don't let go, whatever happens. Can you do that?"

For a long moment she couldn't speak. Then she wrapped her arms around him, pressed her forehead to his, and said, "Yes. Yes. Always."

"Then I won't be alone at all."

*Wait,* said the god, and the patience was gone now, and underneath the beauty Felix heard at last the thing the valley had buried: fear. *Wait, little scribe, we can bargain, I can give you more, I can give you—*

"You can give me nothing," Felix said, "that I want more than this."

And he began to write himself.

* * *

It did not hurt, which was somehow the worst part. It felt like setting down a heavy load after carrying it so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

His name went first — not just *Felix Brightwater* but the weight of it, the debt coiled inside it, generations of quiet servitude unwinding like thread off a spool. He felt Ondrel cry out behind him; felt the old ledger, wherever it sat in the temple, burst into blank pages.

Then the small things. The smell of the temple garden after rain. The taste of burnt porridge, which he'd secretly preferred to proper porridge. The joke about the deacon's hat that he and Tomas had laughed at for a full year. Each memory lifted out of him clean and went into the door, and the door drank, and the singing deepened into something vast and settled and *final*.

He forgot his mother's face. He was sorry about that one, though he was already losing the reason why.

Sera held him the whole time. He sagged against her and she took his weight without complaint, her arms locked around him, her heartbeat steady against his ear — the one thing he held onto past all holding. When he forgot the temple, he kept her. When he forgot the valley, he kept her. When the god screamed beneath the earth, wordless and furious and fading, Felix forgot the god entirely, and that forgetting was the last nail, the final clause, the seal closing like a book whose story was finished.

The warmth went out of the ground. The door went silent. Cold, ordinary stone.

Felix blinked at it. It was a nice stone door, he supposed. Old. Someone had carved a lot of squiggles on it.

"Hello?" he said, to the woman holding him, because she was crying and he thought maybe he should say something. "Are you all right?"

She laughed — a broken, wet, disbelieving sound — and pulled back to look at him, searching his face the way you search a page you've read a hundred times for the line you're sure was there yesterday.

"Yes," Sera Ashfall said, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, and straightening, and lifting her chin the way she must have done across seven years of empty roads. "No. I will be. Ask me again tomorrow. And the day after that." She took his hand in both of hers, firm as a vow. "Come on. I'll make us some tea. And while we drink it, I'm going to tell you a story. It's about a scribe who couldn't write his own name anymore — and a woman who wouldn't let that be the end of it."

"A story?" Felix said, falling into step beside her, the glass flats glittering under the first honest stars he could ever remember noticing. "Is it a good one?"

Sera smiled through the last of her tears.

"It's the best one I know. And I'm going to tell it every night until you believe it."
