## 10. Rising, Remembered

The morning the valley woke, Sera Ashfall was standing in the temple garden holding a dead man's watering can.

Not a dead man — she corrected herself for the hundredth time — but the thought kept arriving anyway, uninvited, like a neighbor who didn't understand that grief wanted to be left alone. Felix Brightwater had walked out of the dark beneath the glass steppe with his eyes open and his heart beating and none of himself behind either one. He had looked at her with the polite blankness of a stranger at a market stall and asked if she needed help finding anything.

She had said yes. She hadn't been able to say what.

Now she watered the herbs he used to tend — *used to*, past tense, because the man who loved these plants was gone even though his hands still knew their work — and watched the magic come back to the valley the way dawn comes back: slowly, then all at once, then so gently you could almost pretend it had never left.

It started small. A spider's web near the garden wall caught the light and held it a half-second too long, silver thread singing faintly when the wind touched it. Then the well-water began tasting of cold stars. Then old Marta from the dye-house came running through the gates shouting that her mordant vats had turned colors that did not exist, and half the village followed her, and by noon the Temple of Quiet Hours was full of weeping farmers touching the walls like they were the flanks of animals they'd feared were dying.

The silent gods, it seemed, had not woken after all. They had only opened one eye, checked that the cage still held, and let out a long breath they'd been holding since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. That breath was what the valley called magic now. It moved through everything — the barley, the bells, the children chasing each other across the glass flats, leaving faint trails of light that faded like breath on a window.

Sera had expected joy to be simpler than this.

* * *

The dragon's name, it turned out, was Ember-of-the-Long-Quitting, which Sera privately shortened to Ember, because dragons choose their own names and no one had ever accused them of being concise.

She lay at the valley's edge in a hollow of warm stone, vast as a granary and thin as a promise kept too late. When Sera had first found her, weeks ago, her fire had been down to a coal no bigger than a thumbnail, guttering in a cavern of her own ribs. The last living dragon-fire in the world. The reason Sera had crossed three mountain ranges and sold two boats and lied to four kings' men about where she was going.

"You should know," Ember had rasped, when Sera knelt beside her with the god-flame cupped in a lantern of Felix's making — the last thing his hands had built while he still knew why. "You should know what you're asking."

"I know," Sera had said.

"You don't. Your lover learned. He paid so I wouldn't have to burn as a prisoner again." The great eye, gold-shot and ancient, had studied her. "He paid so the choice would come back to me."

And Ember — old, tired, furious and free in a way she hadn't been in nine hundred years — had chosen. She had opened her jaws over the god-flame and breathed it into herself willingly, roaring a sound that cracked the glass flats into rivers of shimmer, and her fire came back not as chains but as sunlight, as hearth-warmth, as something given rather than taken.

Now she slept in her hollow, growing fat on goats the villagers left her (the herdsmen had complained for exactly one day before realizing a dragon solved most of their wolf problem permanently), and every few days she cracked one eye open and said something cryptic and unhelpful.

"You smell of ink today," she told Sera on the third evening. "And stubbornness. You always smell of stubbornness. It's very tiring."

"Good. Stay awake then. I need advice."

"No, you don't. You need to stop circling the temple garden like a hawk that's forgotten how to land." The eye closed. "Go tell him the story, little ashfall. It's evening somewhere."

* * *

Felix was pruning the rosemary when she found him.

He worked the way he did everything — carefully, thoroughly, with the particular tenderness of a person who believed that small things deserved attention. His sleeves were rolled up. There was dirt on his chin. He did not remember putting it there, or the name Felix, or the word debt, or the night a woman knocked twice on the scriptorium door and changed the shape of his life.

But his hands remembered the rosemary.

"Good evening," he said, looking up. His smile was easy, unhurried. A stranger's smile, and also somehow exactly his.

"Good evening," Sera said. Her heart did its usual stupid thing, tripping over itself. "I brought bread."

"You bring bread every evening."

"I'm a creature of habit."

He laughed — and there it was, that laugh, low and startled, like the laugh itself surprised him. Some things survived the burning of a name. Some things lived deeper than memory, in whatever place a self is actually kept. She clung to that fact the way sailors cling to rigging.

They sat on the garden bench, which was really just an old altar stone the priests had repurposed during some long-forgotten budget crisis, and they ate bread, and the valley went gold and then violet around them, and then Sera took a breath and began.

"There was once a scribe in a temple where prayers were never answered," she said. "He had ink-stained fingers and a debt he didn't understand and a habit of talking to his manuscripts, which everyone pretended not to notice."

"I do talk to my manuscripts," Felix said. He frowned. "Did I tell you that?"

"You'll see." She smiled. "This scribe copied out a prayer one night — a prayer for rain, from a farmer named Odo, who was not a good farmer but was trying — and when he came back the next morning, the prayer had rewritten itself. In his own handwriting. Which he hadn't written."

"That would be alarming."

"It was extremely alarming. He hid the page under his mattress like a boy hiding a love letter, which tells you something about how lonely he was."

Felix's ears went slightly pink. Sera filed that away with fierce, private delight. Every reaction was a coin. Every coin was proof of a man still buried under the forgetting, waiting to be excavated.

"Then a woman arrived," she continued. "A dragon-keeper. Tall. Handsome. Excellent taste in boots. She'd tracked a dying flame across the world and it led her here, to a valley that swallowed her magic at the border like a thief swallowing stolen coins. She needed a guide who could read the old script."

"And the scribe agreed?"

"He bargained shamelessly. He made her pay triple the standard rate and then felt guilty about it and threw in a free tour of the herb garden."

"I would never charge triple."

"He absolutely would," Sera said solemnly. "I have witnesses."

* * *

She told him a piece of the story every evening, and she had learned to ration it, the way you ration water in a desert crossing. Too much at once and he only got headaches and a hollow feeling he couldn't name; she'd learned that the hard way, watching him press his palms against his temples and apologize, *apologize*, as if his missing self were a discourtesy he was committing against her.

So instead: one scene per night. One coin dropped into the well, listening for the splash.

The night she told him about the ruins above the valley — the ones older than the temple, older than the gods' silence — he stopped chewing mid-bite.

"The stone door," he said slowly. "Humming. In a land without fire."

"Yes."

"Why do I know that? Why does my chest hurt when you say it?"

"Because you stood in front of it," Sera said, "and you put your hand flat against the stone, and you said—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, because he needed to hear things true, not tidy — "you said, *it's warm. All this time, it's been warm.* And then you understood what the valley was, before I did. You've always been faster than me at the terrible realizations."

"I can't imagine being fast at anything," Felix said, but he was staring at his hands, turning them over, as if they might confess something.

"They're good hands," Sera said. "They wrote a prayer once that saved a valley. They built a lantern that carried a god's fire. They held mine when I was more frightened than I have ever been, including the time I fought a river-serpent, which I mention often, because I earned it."

"A river-serpent," Felix repeated, delighted despite himself. "Is that in the story too?"

"Chapter six. It's very heroic. Mostly I screamed."

* * *

Some evenings were harder.

The night she described the price — his name, his memories, offered line by line into the seal — she got through exactly four sentences before her voice failed, and Felix, this kind stranger wearing her beloved's face and habits, reached over and took her hand without hesitation.

"I don't know who this man was," he said quietly. "But he must have loved you very much."

"He did," Sera managed. "He does. That's the whole problem. That's the whole *point*."

"Tell me the rest tomorrow," Felix said. "The happy parts. Were there happy parts?"

"So many," she said. "There was a part where he danced badly at a harvest festival. There was a part where he argued with a dragon. There was a part—" she steadied herself— "there's a part happening right now, actually. We're inside it. It's my favorite."

* * *

On the forty-first evening, something new happened.

She had reached the scene beneath the glass flats — the imprisoned god, vast and patient and terrible, offering Felix freedom in exchange for carrying its secret — when Felix suddenly set down his bread and said:

"The bargain was a trap."

Sera went very still. "What?"

"It was a trap." He was frowning, working at it, a man hauling on a rope from the bottom of a deep well. "If he carried the secret out, the world would remember the god, and remembering is a kind of waking — isn't it? Memory feeds it. That's why the seal needed a name. Not just any name. A name that would be *remembered*, so the god could ride the remembering out." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and frightened and utterly, unmistakably his. "That's why it wanted him specifically. Because someone would tell his story."

The garden was silent. Somewhere beyond the wall, Ember's distant breathing stirred the air like weather.

"Felix," Sera whispered.

His brow creased. "Sorry — did I say something wrong? I don't know why I know that. I don't know how I know any of it."

"You said your name."

"Did I?" He touched his own mouth, wonderingly, like a man discovering a door in a wall he'd walked past for years. "Felix," he tried again, slower. "Felix... Brightwater." Nothing followed the surname. No debt, no family, no history. But the name itself sat there in the evening air, solid, alive, refusing to evaporate.

"The seal doesn't need all of you," Sera said, and her voice broke and reassembled itself around hope. "Ember told me — a seal takes what's freely given, and it keeps what's freely given. But stories aren't the seal's property, Felix. Stories are *mine*. I've been telling you yourself, one piece each night, and each piece goes into *you*, not into the cage. The god sleeps. The valley remembers. Both things. You found the answer nobody else found — you're doing it *right now*, just by sitting here and letting me love you out loud."

Felix stared at her. And then — slowly, tentatively, the way dawn comes back, the way magic comes back, the way everything worth having comes back — he smiled.

"Sera Ashfall," he said, testing the shape of it. He knew her name. Of course he knew her name; she'd told him the first night, and names spoken aloud to strangers were never part of the seal's ledger. "The dragon-keeper. Who fought a river-serpent."

"Screamed at. Screamed *at* a river-serpent."

"And loved a scribe," he said. "Who is apparently learning himself back, one evening at a time." He looked at his hands, then at her, and something in his face shifted — not memory exactly, but the place where memory would grow, like green shoots through cracked glass. "Tell me tomorrow what happens next. Tell me every tomorrow there is."

"I intend to," Sera said. "Starting with chapter eleven. In which the scribe discovers that his debts were forgiven, his garden is famous throughout the valley, and a dragon has requested — and I quote — *more goats and fewer visitors*."

Felix laughed, that low startled laugh, and reached for her hand, and his fingers fit hers the way they always had, the way they always would, no matter how many times she had to teach him why.

Above them the first stars came out, and the valley — awake, gentle, humming with returned magic — held them both, and kept the story safe, and waited, with infinite patience, for tomorrow.
