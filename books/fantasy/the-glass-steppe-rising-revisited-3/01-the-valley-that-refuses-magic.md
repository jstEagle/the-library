## 1. The Valley That Refuses Magic

The dead of Serrado Valley were terrible gossips, and Paloma Reyes loved them for it.

"Left," murmured old Marta from her grave behind the chapel, where she had been buried for sixty-one years and had not once stopped offering unsolicited advice. "You're holding it too flat. Left, girl."

"It's *bone*, Marta," Paloma said, tilting the small rib toward the window light anyway. "It doesn't have a left."

"Everything has a left. You just haven't met it yet."

Paloma smiled and adjusted her grip. The workshop smelled of beeswax and juniper smoke and the faint mineral sweetness of cured bone — a smell she had been told was strange by everyone who wasn't born to it. Outside, the morning came down soft and gold through the junipers, and somewhere a neighbor's goat was screaming its opinion about breakfast. Ordinary sounds. Valley sounds. She let them settle around her like a shawl.

On her workbench lay the reason for today's carefulness: a rib bone from a valley ox, long and pale as a river stone, and beside it a list written in her own neat hand. *For Tomás Aguilar, aged six years. Drowned in the mill pond, Tuesday last.* Below that, the things his mother had asked for: *he liked frogs. He laughed like a door hinge. He was afraid of the dark but only a little.*

That last one mattered most. It always did.

Memorial bones were the oldest craft in the valley, older than the chapel, older than the Reyes name, and they worked on one principle only: the dead could not speak here, but they could be spoken *to*, and if you carved their name into bone with enough patience and enough love, the living would have something to hold when the silence got too big. Paloma did not pretend the bones did anything magical. Magic couldn't do anything in the valley at all. That was the whole point of the place.

But she could hear the dead. Had heard them since she was four years old, sitting in the churchyard, asking a headstone why it was so cold. Not ghosts, exactly — no floating sheets, no rattling chains, nothing so theatrical. Just voices, low and constant, like wind through wheat, rising strongest near the boundary stones at the valley's mouth where the ground went strange and glassy.

And the voices never crossed the stones either. Whatever the boundary kept out, it also kept something in.

"Tell me about the boy," she said to the room, because talking helped, and because the dead of Serrado had known the Aguilars since before the Aguilars had a roof.

"He climbed everything," said Marta. "Terrible climber. His mother aged ten years every summer."

"He was kind to the miller's dog," offered someone else — a man's voice, gravelly, one of the founders buried up-slope whose name even Paloma had half-forgotten. "Shared his bread with it. Dog followed him everywhere. Followed him right down to the water, poor thing. Been howling since Tuesday."

Paloma wrote *kind to animals* on her list and underlined it twice.

* * *

By midday she had the first pass done. She worked slowly, the way her grandmother had taught her — *the bone remembers hurry, mija; carve like you're writing a letter you want read slowly* — scoring the rib with fine lines that would catch candlelight at the funeral: reeds along the lower curve, a frog mid-leap near the top, and beneath them, in letters small enough to require leaning close, the words *HE WAS AFRAID OF THE DARK BUT ONLY A LITTLE.*

She'd learned early that the honest details were what broke people open in the good way. Anyone could carve *BELOVED SON*. But *afraid of the dark, but only a little* — that was a sentence a grieving father could touch with his thumb and feel his son again, specific and true.

"You're good at this," said Marta, which was high praise, since Marta considered Paloma's grandmother merely adequate and everyone else in the valley's history a waste of good bone.

"Thank you, Marta."

"Don't thank me. Thank your grandmother. And eat something. You get thin when you work and then you complain about being thin, and I died before I ever complained about anything, so I don't want to hear it."

"I don't complain about—"

"Constantly. Like a kettle."

Paloma laughed, and the sound bounced off the workshop walls, and for a moment the loneliness that lived in her chest — the one she kept fed and groomed and dressed in routine so it wouldn't look like loneliness — went quiet.

Because here was the truth she didn't say aloud, not to Marta, not to the neighbors, not to the dead: she was twenty-six years old, and she had never once left the valley. Nobody born inside the boundary stones ever wanted to. Why would they? Beyond the stones, magic existed — real magic, wild and hungry, the kind that had eaten kingdoms once upon a time — and any valley-born soul who stepped past the boundary would carry nothing out with them but their own unprotected skin. The stones kept magic out. They kept the valley safe. Safe, and small, and sealed like a jar.

And the dead? The dead couldn't cross either. Which meant that Paloma's whole world — every voice she loved, every whisperer and gossip and gravedigger's ghost — ended precisely where the glassy stones began. If she walked out, she would walk into silence. Total silence. The first true silence of her life.

Other people dreamed of leaving. Paloma dreamed of quiet, and found the dream terrifying.

"Eat," Marta repeated, with the finality of the deceased.

* * *

She ate bread and honey on the step, feet bare in the dust, and watched the light move down the valley. Serrado was shaped like a cupped hand: orchards in the palm, the town in the middle, and the fingers closing toward the north where the boundary stones stood in their ancient ring — twelve of them, tall as two men, smooth and faintly translucent, like teeth of some enormous patient animal. Children dared each other to touch them. Children always regretted it. The stones were warm in summer, cold in winter, and humming, very faintly, all year round — a hum you felt in your back teeth more than heard.

Her grandmother had taken her there once a year, every year, until she died. *This is the edge of everything, mija,* she'd say, hand heavy on Paloma's shoulder. *Everything worth having is behind us. Remember that.* And Paloma, eight years old, pressing her palm to the humming glass, would hear the murmur swell — hundreds of voices just beyond, tangled and urgent, like a crowd outside a locked door — and she would nod and believe her, mostly.

Mostly.

"The boy's mother is coming tomorrow," said the gravelly founder-voice, drifting after her as she carried the rib back inside. "Bring the bone finished. She won't survive another day of waiting. Some of them need it quick, carver. You know this."

"I know it." She did. Grief was a fire and a memorial bone was the only water the valley had.

She worked through the afternoon. The frog gained legs. The reeds gained shadow. Somewhere in the middle of the third hour, without quite deciding to, she began adding a tiny second figure beside the frog — a dog, sitting, waiting, ears up — because the miller's dog had howled since Tuesday and grief belonged to animals too, whatever the old formulas said.

Marta approved. The founder grumbled that it wasn't traditional. Paloma kept it.

* * *

Evening came down violet and cool, and that was when the voices at the edge changed.

She noticed it while washing her tools — a wrongness in the background murmur, the way you notice a clock stopping. All day the dead had been their usual selves: gossiping about the Aguilar funeral arrangements, arguing about whether the new well was dug crooked (it was), complaining that nobody visited the north graves anymore. Comfortable noise. Familiar noise.

But now, underneath it, from the direction of the boundary stones, something else. A hush moving through the voices like a wave through wheat. One by one, the whispers nearest the north ring were going quiet — not fading, not sleeping, but *stopping*, the way people stop talking when someone important walks into a room.

Paloma set down her tools. Wiped her hands. Stood very still in the blue dusk of her workshop and listened with everything she had.

At the far edge of her hearing, beyond Marta, beyond the founders, beyond even the oldest voices, something at the stones was... listening back.

It had happened before, rarely — once or twice in her life, always at dusk, always gone by full dark. Her grandmother had called it "the Steppe turning over in its sleep" and had never once elaborated, which was very like her. The murmur would flatten, the hum in the stones would deepen, and Paloma would feel, for a few breathless minutes, like a mouse under the gaze of something vast and glassy-eyed.

Tonight it lasted longer than usual. Tonight, at the very bottom of the hush, she thought she caught a word — or the shape of a word, worn smooth as sea glass:

*...carver...*

Then it was gone. The murmur resumed its gossip. The well was still crooked. Marta was still complaining that Paloma's candle smoked.

Paloma stood in her doorway a long time, arms wrapped around herself, watching the north ring of stones catch the last red light like a row of held breaths.

"Silly," she said aloud, to hear a living voice, even her own. "Silly woman. Carve your bone. Sleep. Tomorrow there's a funeral."

She went in. She lit the candle properly. She finished the little dog.

And she did not sleep — not really, not deeply — and near midnight, when the moon cleared the eastern ridge and laid a silver road straight across the valley floor toward the boundary stones, Paloma heard the dead go silent all at once, every voice in the valley, a hundred years of whispers stopping between one heartbeat and the next.

Into that enormous silence came a sound she had never heard before: footsteps on the glass, past the stones, stumbling closer — and a man's voice, cracked with exhaustion and pain, saying clearly, impossibly, her name.

"*Paloma Reyes.*"

She reached for the poker by the hearth, heart slamming, and told herself firmly that no one knew her name, no one crossed the stones, no one *could* —

The door of her workshop rattled.
