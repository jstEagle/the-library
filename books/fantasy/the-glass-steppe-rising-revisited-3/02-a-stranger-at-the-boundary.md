## 2. A Stranger at the Boundary

The dead had been restless all week.

Not the valley dead — they were a well-behaved congregation, murmuring their small grievances and old loves from beneath the boundary stones, content to be remembered in bone and beeswax. No, it was something else. Something *outside*, pressing against the edge of Paloma's hearing like a thumb against a drumhead. For six nights she had woken with her hands aching, as if she'd been gripping a chisel in her sleep, and each morning the murmur at the valley's rim was a little louder, a little more urgent, a little more like arguing.

She mentioned it to no one, because there was no one to mention it to. That was the arrangement she'd made with her life: herself, her workshop above the tannery, the honored dead of the valley, and a standing order of memorial bones that kept her fed and housed and pleasantly occupied until such time as she died and became someone else's work.

It was a good arrangement. She reminded herself of this often, the way one reminds oneself that a soup is not too salty.

On the seventh evening, she was walking home along the boundary path with a wrapped haunch of mutton under one arm and sawdust still clinging to her sleeves, when the stranger came through the stones.

She heard him before she saw him — which was itself impossible, because nothing crossed the boundary. That was the whole point of the boundary. The valley sat in a pocket where magic could not follow, a fact every child learned before they learned their letters: *past the stones, the world is wide and wicked; inside the stones, the world is only wide.* Whatever waited beyond was kept out by the same law that kept the valley's own small wonders in check. Nothing bled through. Nothing slipped past. In ninety years of her grandmother's life and twenty-six of her own, the rule had held without exception.

And yet here was a sound coming through the stones like water forced backward through a sieve — a thin, high keening, the sound a bone makes just before it cracks, and then a man stumbled between two of the great gray markers and fell face-first into the bracken on the valley side.

Paloma stood very still.

He was tall, or he would have been if he'd been standing, dressed in travel-ruined grays and browns that might once have been fine. A satchel was strapped across his back, and one hand was locked around its strap with the desperate grip of a drowning man. His other arm was flung out before him, palm down against the earth, and —

And he was bleeding light.

There was no better way to say it. From his sleeve, from the seam of his glove, from a tear at his shoulder, light seeped out of him the way blood seeps from a wound — slow, viscous, gold-white, pooling in the bracken and sinking into the soil with a faint hiss, like rain on a hot stone. Where it touched the ground, tiny white flowers opened and immediately closed again, confused about the season.

"Well," said Paloma, to nobody. "That's new."

The man groaned and pushed himself up onto his elbows. He got one knee under himself, swayed, and looked up at her.

His eyes were the color of the sky just before snow, and they were fixed on her face with an expression she couldn't at first parse — relief? terror? Both, braided together, the way grief and love are braided at funerals.

"Paloma Reyes," he said.

Her name. Her whole name, spoken in an accent she didn't recognize, soft around the vowels, careful with the R, as though he'd practiced it. As though he'd been carrying it a long way.

Every hair on her arms rose. The dead went silent — truly silent, for the first time in her life, the entire murmuring congregation of the valley holding its breath at once, which was so unprecedented that it frightened her more than the bleeding man did.

"That," she said slowly, "is not possible."

"I know." He tried to stand, failed, and settled for sitting back on his heels, breathing hard. Up close she could see he was younger than she'd first thought — late twenties, maybe, with dark hair stuck to his forehead with sweat and a jaw shadowed by several days without a razor. "I've read the accounts. I know what's supposed to be impossible. I'm finding the list is shorter than advertised."

"You're bleeding."

"I'm aware."

"Not blood."

"I'm *aware*," he said again, with a note of desperation that almost made her laugh. Almost. She did not laugh, because laughing felt like an invitation, and invitations were how trouble got comfortable.

Instead she crouched down, keeping a careful four feet between them, and studied him the way she studied a piece of raw material before committing the first cut. Bone-carving had taught her patience and a certain unsentimental eye: everything told you what it was, if you looked long enough and didn't flinch. This man was exhausted to the marrow. Frightened underneath the exhaustion. Determined underneath the fright. And hurt in some way that had nothing to do with the light leaking out of him — hurt somewhere structural, the way a house is hurt when its foundation shifts.

"You came through the stones," she said. "Nothing comes through the stones."

"I know that too." He managed something that was nearly a smile. It was a good smile, ruined by exhaustion, the way a good frame is ruined by a cracked canvas. "You're the third person to tell me what I know tonight. The first two were dead, though, so you're already my favorite."

"The dead don't talk here."

"No," he agreed. "I noticed. It's very restful. I can see why your family liked it."

Her hands went cold. "What do you know about my family?"

Everything about him went carefully, deliberately still — the stillness of a man who has stepped on ice and heard it speak. "Perhaps," he said, "we should start over. My name is Ilya. I've traveled eleven weeks to reach this valley, most of them on foot after the horses were — " he waved vaguely at his shoulder, at the light, " — it's a long story involving the horses. I was sent by people who are mostly dead now. And I brought you a book."

"I didn't ask for a book."

"No," he said. "But it asked for you."

* * *

She should have walked away. She knew that even then, some sensible chamber of her mind filing the observation neatly away: *you should have turned around, gone home, bolted the door, and let the valley deal with whatever this was.* The valley had dealt with things for centuries. There were wards older than the chapel, customs older than the wards, and a general agreement among the living that anything strange was best handled by pretending, firmly and collectively, not to see it.

But the dead had gone silent, and their silence was a kind of scream, and Paloma Reyes had spent her whole life listening to the dead, and she found she could no more walk away from that silence than she could walk away from a child crying in the night.

So instead she said, "You're getting blood — light — whatever that is — all over the boundary path, and Mrs. Okonkwo will have opinions about the flowers," and hauled him up by his good arm.

He was heavier than he looked, and lighter too, in some way she couldn't name, as though part of his weight had leaked out along with the light. He leaned on her more than he probably wanted to, and she pretended not to notice, and together they made a slow, ridiculous procession up the path toward the village, a bleeding scholar and a bone-carver with a mutton haunch, while the boundary stones behind them hummed a low, uneasy note that she had never heard them make before.

"What are those?" he asked, nodding at the stones as they passed.

"Boundary markers. They're older than the valley records. Nobody knows who set them." She glanced sideways at him. "You came through one. You should tell me how."

"I walked," he said. "And then there was a moment where walking became falling, and then I was here, and you were looking at me like I was a fish that had come ashore to discuss theology."

"That's a fair description of how you looked."

"It's how I *felt*," he said solemnly, and despite everything, despite the silence of the dead and the humming stones and the light still seeping through his coat, she felt the corner of her mouth betray her.

They passed the tannery, where old Sefu was shuttering his windows, and he tipped his hat to Paloma and gave the stranger the particular look villagers reserve for things they intend to gossip about thoroughly but acknowledge minimally. They passed the chapel, where the evening bell rang, and the stranger flinched at the sound in a way that told her he hadn't heard bells in a long time, or perhaps had heard too many of a different kind.

"My workshop's above the tannery," she said. "You'll take the chair by the stove. You'll tell me who sent you and what's in the book. And you will not bleed on the floorboards; they're oak and they're older than both of us put together."

"A reasonable list of terms," he said. "I accept all of them."

"And you'll explain the flowers."

"The flowers?"

"Wherever your — whatever-it-is — touches the ground, flowers open. White ones. Out of season. Mrs. Okonkwo keeps the boundary path and she counts everything, and I mean *everything*. If there are forty-one unseasonal white flowers on her path tomorrow, she will find whoever is responsible, and she will make them wish the Hollow — " she stopped. "Whoever is responsible will regret it."

Something flickered across his face at the words she'd swallowed — *the Hollow Wind*, the phrase that had risen unbidden from somewhere below her memory, from some inheritance she hadn't known she carried — but he only said, "I'll rake the path myself if I have to."

* * *

The workshop smelled of linseed oil and bone dust and the lavender she burned to keep the dead polite. She got him into the chair by the stove, unwrapped the mutton and set it to cook — hospitality was hospitality, even for impossible men — and then knelt beside him with her sewing kit, because the tear in his shoulder needed closing and she was the steadiest pair of hands in the valley, a fact of her trade.

Up close, the wound was worse and stranger. It wasn't a cut, exactly. It was more like a place where he had been *unmade* slightly — the skin parted, and beneath it there was no flesh, only a soft glow, layered like mother-of-pearl, pulsing gently in time with his heartbeat.

"Don't stitch it," he said quietly, watching her face. "It doesn't work like that. It closes when — " He stopped. "It closes. Eventually. Under the right conditions."

"Which are?"

"Conditions I'd rather discuss after the mutton."

She threaded a needle anyway and closed his sleeve instead, because a torn sleeve was a torn sleeve regardless of metaphysics, and while she worked she felt him watching her hands with an intensity that made her want to hide them, which was absurd, since her hands were the plainest thing about her.

"You're a carver," he said. Not a question.

"Bone-carver. Memorials, mostly. The valley brings me their dead — well. Their dead's bones. I carve the names, the dates, sometimes a line of what they loved. It helps them settle." She tied off the thread. "It helps the living more, honestly. But don't tell them that."

"You hear them," he said. "The dead. That's why the book names you. *The carver who hears.*"

The needle nearly slipped. She set it down with exaggerated care, the way her grandmother had taught her to set down tools when her hands wanted to shake — *the tool remembers your fear, child, and bone remembers everything.*

"Say that again," she said.

He reached for the satchel, and she watched him hesitate — actually hesitate, his fingers resting on the buckle, some argument happening behind his eyes. Then he drew out a book.

It was not a large book, but it was dense the way river stones are dense, with the heft of something that has absorbed years. Its cover was leather gone black with age, and its pages, when he opened it, were vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, covered in a cramped, beautiful hand. Ink faded to the brown of old tea. Diagrams in the margins — circles, lines, and shapes that made her eyes slide off them, that made the lavender smoke bend toward the book as if leaning in to listen.

"The order that kept this book was called the Wardens of the Turning Year," Ilya said. "They were founded to maintain something called the covenant — the covenant of the Glass Steppe. Most of what they knew is lost now. Most of *them* are lost now. When I left, there were four of us. I've had no word since, and I've stopped expecting any." He turned the pages with a gentleness that undid her a little; men who handled books like that had usually been broken by losing one. "This is the record of the covenant. Its terms. Its history. Its — schedule."

"Schedule."

"Prophecy, if you like the word. The Wardens didn't believe in foreseeing the future. They believed in *bookkeeping*. Certain things, once set in motion, must arrive on time, and a century ago a Warden named Aldessa Vane wrote down what would arrive, and when." He found a page near the end and turned the book toward her. "Read it."

She read it.

The handwriting was precise and increasingly hurried, as though the writer had been racing something. The entry was dated — and the date was today, this exact day, written a hundred years before she was born. Below the date, in that same hurrying hand:

*A messenger crosses the stones at dusk, bleeding light, bearing the book. He will be met by the carver who hears. His death is written on the final page. God forgive us all for what we wrote and what we did not.*

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog began to bark at nothing, or at something only dogs could see.

"'His death,'" Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Whose death?"

Ilya closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

"Mine," he said. "According to the book, I die soon. Quite soon. On a date that hasn't happened yet, written by a woman a hundred years dead." He looked up at her, and the firelight caught the snow-colored eyes, and she saw that beneath the exhaustion and the fear there was something else, something that looked almost like apology. "I'm sorry to introduce myself this way. I rehearsed a much better version, with more charm and considerably less bleeding."

Paloma sat back on her heels. Around her, the dead of the valley — her quiet, well-behaved, lifelong companions — remained utterly, pointedly silent, and in that silence she heard for the first time what the silence itself was saying. It was not emptiness. It was *withholding*. It was a hundred years of held breath.

And beneath it, faint as a finger tapped on a far-off window, came a single word in a voice she knew better than her own — her grandmother's voice, dry and warm and gone these ten years:

*Steppe.*

Paloma closed her eyes.

"All right," she said to Ilya, without opening them. "Start from the beginning. And eat your mutton. Dead men tell no tales, and neither, apparently, do the dead in this valley, so it looks like it's up to you."

When she opened her eyes again, he was smiling — that ruined, exhausted, real smile — and reaching for the plate.

And outside the window, above the boundary stones, the moon was rising: full-faced, patient, and very slightly wrong, as if it had already begun counting toward a thirteenth turn that no almanac admitted existed.
