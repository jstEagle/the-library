## 3. The Book That Names Tonight

There is a particular kind of nausea that comes from reading your own name in a book you have never opened before. I can describe it precisely, because I spent that first evening in Paloma Reyes's workshop doing nothing else.

The book sat between us on her workbench like a confession neither of us wanted to make. It was old — old the way river stones are old, worn smooth at the edges by a century of hands — and its cover was leather gone the color of weak tea, stitched with thread that might once have been red. The order had called it *the Ledger*. Not a prophecy book, never that; prophecies were for charlatans and poets, and the Order of the Quiet Page considered itself neither, mostly. A ledger. A record. As if naming it something bureaucratic would make the contents behave.

It did not make the contents behave.

"Read it again," Paloma said. She was standing by the window with her arms crossed, which I was learning was her posture for *I do not believe you, but continue anyway.*

"I've read it four times."

"Then you'll survive a fifth." She nodded at the page. "Slowly. Out loud. And don't skip anything this time. You skipped something the last time — I saw your eyes jump."

My eyes had jumped because my stomach had dropped, but arguing about it seemed unwise. I smoothed the page flat. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, though the script itself remained unnervingly crisp, as if whoever had written it had pressed hard enough to leave grooves in the paper, as if they'd wanted to be sure no one could claim later that they'd misread.

I cleared my throat.

"*On the night when the moon begins her thirteenth turn,*" I read, "*a messenger will cross the boundary stones into the valley where magic cannot follow. He will come bleeding light instead of blood, for his wounds were given in the Steppe, and the Steppe does not forgive.*"

Paloma's jaw tightened. My ribs, beneath their bandages, gave a sympathetic twinge. When I'd tumbled through her boundary stones that morning, the gash along my side had been leaking a soft gold shimmer rather than anything so ordinary as red, and she had looked at it the way a woman looks at a spider in her bathwater: with horror, then fascination, then the brisk practicality of someone who decides the situation is now her problem.

"*He will carry the Ledger,*" I continued, "*and he will seek the carver who hears.*"

"There," Paloma said. "That's the part you keep rushing past. *The carver who hears.*"

"You're a carver. You hear things. Dead things, specifically, if what you told me on the walk up here is accurate."

"What I told you on the walk was that I carve memorial bones and that sometimes the dead are chatty. That is not the same as—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. "Go on."

"*He will arrive half-dead and wholly frightened,*" I read, "*which is the traditional condition of messengers.*"

"That's almost funny," she said suspiciously.

"The order's founder wrote most of it. She apparently had a sense of humor. It died before she did, by all accounts." I turned the page, and there it was — the part my eyes kept jumping past. I made myself look at it directly, the way you look at a bill you know you can't pay.

"*On this date*"—and the date written there was today's date, in a calendar system I had only learned existed three years ago, matching down to the day—"*the messenger Ilya Vaschenko will stand in the house of bone above the river bend, and he will understand that he has never once made a free choice in his life.*"

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. Somewhere outside, the valley's evening sounds went on without us — water over stones, an owl clearing its throat, wind moving through grass that had never known a single spell and was smug about it.

"Well," Paloma said finally. "At least it didn't say anything embarrassing."

"It said I've never made a free choice."

"It also called you a messenger, which is a job title. Jobs aren't choices, technically. Ask anyone who's ever been employed." She crossed to the bench and looked down at the Ledger with an expression I couldn't quite parse — respect, maybe, or the particular wariness of a craftswoman examining someone else's shoddy joinery. "Who wrote this?"

"Sister Amaranth. Founder of the Order of the Quiet Page. She wrote the whole thing in one sitting, allegedly, in a trance she described as 'deeply inconvenient.'" I rubbed my eyes. "A hundred years ago. Before either of our grandparents could walk."

"And the order sent *you*."

"The order sent whoever was left." I heard how it sounded the moment it left my mouth and winced. "That came out wrong."

"No," she said slowly, "I think it came out exactly right. Sit down, Ilya Vaschenko. You look like a man who's been holding himself upright by argument alone, and I want the whole story before I decide whether tonight's dinner includes you."

* * *

Her house smelled of bone dust and beeswax and, faintly, of the rosemary she grew in a cracked pot on the sill. I sat on a stool that creaked with the dignity of long service while she lit two lamps and set out bread, hard cheese, and a jar of pickled something that I elected not to investigate too closely.

I ate. I talked. The two activities competed for my mouth and mostly cooperated.

"There were thirty-one of us when I joined," I said. "Eleven years ago. The Order of the Quiet Page — we kept the records of the covenant. All the treaties, all the terms, every amendment the signatory ghosts ever demanded. We were archivists, basically. Librarians of the apocalypse."

"Librarians of the apocalypse," Paloma repeated. "Is that on your letterhead?"

"Our letterhead was a stamp and a leaky roof. But yes, spiritually."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, barely a movement, but I noted it the way you note the first green shoot after a hard winter — cautiously, and with a hope you're embarrassed by.

"Over the years, members left. Died. One retired to the coast to raise goats, which we all agreed was the sanest decision anyone in the order ever made. By this spring there were four of us. Then two. Sister Odile passed in her sleep in the autumn, peacefully, surrounded by unfinished filing. That left me and Brother Anselm, who is ninety-one and convinced the covenant broke in 1840 and everything since has been a hallucination." I paused. "Anselm may yet be proven right about several things."

"And the book chose you? Is that how it works?"

"The book doesn't choose. The book just... is. It was written, and now it happens, and everyone near it spends their lives feeling like furniture in someone else's room." I turned my cup in my hands. "When it became clear someone had to go — someone had to find the carver who hears, deliver the warning, try to stop the thirteenth turn — Anselm couldn't walk to his own gate. So."

"So you volunteered."

"So I volunteered," I agreed, and took a bite of bread I suddenly couldn't taste.

Paloma watched me with those steady dark eyes that seemed to take inventory of whatever they landed on. Carver's eyes. I imagined they assessed everything — people included — for grain, for flaws, for what could be shaped and what should be left alone.

"You're not telling me everything," she said. Not accusing. Observing.

"I'm telling you everything relevant."

"That sentence has a hole in it big enough to drive a funeral cart through." She leaned back. "But fine. Relevant first. What happens if the covenant fails? Actually fails. Not in ledger-language. In words a person can feel."

I set down the bread. This part I had rehearsed the whole journey, and it still came out of me like a stone pulled from deep water.

"The Glass Steppe isn't another world, exactly. It's the world's other draft. The one that got cut. Everything magic ever touched and then abandoned ended up there — cities, forests, whole peoples, folded flat into glass. For a thousand years the covenant held it separate, like a lid on a pot. Thirteen signatories, thirteen turns of the moon to renew it, every cycle. And the renewal..." I hesitated. "The renewal requires certain conditions. Conditions that stopped being met a hundred years ago."

"Which is why your order started dying."

"Which is why the edges started fraying. The boundary stones. The rules. Magic can't follow into this valley — that's the last clause still holding, the last knot your ancestors tied before they ran. But knots slip. And when this one goes, the lid comes off. The Steppe pours through. Everything it touches unravels back into magic, and magic—" I gestured vaguely at my own glowing wound. "Magic doesn't care what it unmakes. A wall becomes sand. A promise becomes noise. A person becomes..."

"A person becomes what?"

"I genuinely don't know. No one who's seen it happen has been able to describe it coherently. Brother Ferro watched a village unravel during the last breach, forty years ago. He wrote eleven pages about it and the last six are just the word *no* repeated."

Paloma absorbed this with the composure of someone whose daily colleagues were dead. "And the moon's thirteenth turn is when?"

"Tonight began the count. We have until the turn completes. Weeks, perhaps. Fewer, if the fraying accelerates." I smiled, and I'm afraid it wasn't a good smile. "So. Librarian of the apocalypse, reporting for duty. I found the carver who hears. I delivered the warning. Chapter complete."

"But the book says more than that." She tapped the Ledger without opening it. "You flinched twice tonight. Once at the date line. Once somewhere else. Carvers notice flinching — it's how we tell where the crack runs before the bone splits."

I looked at her. She looked back, patient as sediment.

"It says I die here," I said. "In the valley. Tonight's date, again. Two lines below the entry about arriving." I laughed, and it came out steadier than I felt, which is the main trick I know. "Sister Amaranth was thorough."

* * *

Paloma didn't say anything for a long moment. She rose, banked the fire, moved around her workshop touching things — a finished carving here, a half-formed one there — the way other people touch talismans. I understood. It was a shop full of proof that she made things that lasted. I envied her that.

"How does it say it?" she asked at last. "Exactly. Word for word. If I'm going to spend the night deciding whether to believe a hundred-year-old book, I want the ugly parts verbatim."

"*The messenger Ilya Vaschenko will end upon this ground,*" I recited, "*as all messengers end, having served the page.*"

"'As all messengers end,'" she repeated. "How many messengers have there been?"

"One."

The word sat between us. I picked up my cup to give my hands something honest to do.

"Brother Tomas," I said. "Sent eighty years ago, when the first signs of fraying appeared. He carried a copy of the relevant pages, since the order wasn't willing to risk the Ledger itself. He crossed into a valley very much like this one, looking for a carver who hears. He was never heard from again." I turned the cup. Turned it back. "Before I left, Anselm gave me a small bundle. Bone fragments. He said they'd been recovered from a boundary region decades ago, and that if I met the carver, I should give them to her, because 'the dead prefer to go home through their own trade.' I have them in my pack. I didn't mention it earlier because—"

"Because you were hoping they weren't what they obviously are," Paloma said quietly.

"Yes. Because hope is load-bearing and I couldn't afford to remove it."

She exhaled through her nose — not quite a laugh, but related to laughter the way a cousin is related to you: shared blood, different temperament. "You're strange, Ilya Vaschenko."

"I'm a librarian. Strangeness is in the job description, right under 'leaky roof.'"

But she was looking at me differently now, and I recognized the look because I'd been wearing it myself all evening, aimed inward. It was the look of a person recalculating. The book had named me. Named my arrival, named my wounds, named my fear, named my death with the tidy finality of a clerk closing a file. And sitting in that lamplit workshop, watching a stranger weigh whether my existence was evidence or coincidence, I felt the dread I'd been carrying since the day I first read the Ledger rise fully into my throat:

Not the fear of dying. I'd made a rough peace with that somewhere on the road; you can't volunteer for a fatal errand without renegotiating terms with yourself. The real fear was smaller and stranger. The fear that I had never been a person at all. That I was a sentence someone else had written a century ago, and that every choice I remembered making — joining the order, studying the archives, volunteering for this — had simply been the sentence unfolding, grammar masquerading as will. That when people looked at me they saw not Ilya Vaschenko, uncertain and fond of cheese and afraid of horses, but a function. A delivery mechanism with a name attached for convenience.

"Do you think it's true?" I asked her. I hadn't planned to ask. The question escaped like a bird from a house whose door was open a moment too long. "The book. Do you think I'm — following a script?"

Paloma considered this with the seriousness it deserved, which was, to her credit, complete.

"I think," she said slowly, "that I've carved two hundred memorial bones, and every single one of those dead people believed they were writing their own story right up until they weren't. The dead are terrible forecasters and excellent liars, mostly to themselves." She picked up the Ledger, weighed it in her hands like a piece of stock she hadn't decided on. "But I'll tell you what I know about bones, and books, and anything else that gets carved. A shape isn't a fate. The same blank can become a flute or a knife. It depends on the hands." Her eyes came up to mine. "Someone wrote you into this story, messenger. That doesn't mean you're done being written."

Outside, the moon was rising over the valley — fat, silvered, beginning its slow deliberate turn toward the number thirteen. And as I watched it through her window, the Ledger in Paloma's hands gave a sound I had never heard it give in three years of carrying it.

It hummed.

Low, resonant, like a wet finger circling a glass rim — and Paloma's head snapped toward it, and her face went pale in a way that suggested she recognized the note.

"That sound," she whispered. "I know that sound. That's the sound my grandmother's chest makes when I open it."
