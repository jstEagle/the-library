## 4. What the Dead Remember

The dead of Hollow Creek Valley were, as a rule, terrible gossips.

Paloma had learned this before she'd learned her letters, sitting cross-legged on the cold floor of the ossuary while old Marta's ghost complained for the four-hundredth time about the quality of her funeral bread. *Too dense,* Marta had said, in the voice of dry leaves and river-stones that all the valley dead shared. *I waited forty years to die and they gave me bread like a roof tile.* And then she had told Paloma everything — who owed whom money, which farmer's second son was not actually the farmer's, why the miller's wife wore gloves even in summer.

The living kept secrets the way jars keep preserves. The dead poured themselves out.

Which was why, on the morning after the stranger with the bleeding light collapsed in her spare bed, Paloma walked down the hill with a basket of fresh-baked offering bread and a question she did not want answered, feeling very much like a woman about to ask a room full of chatty aunties whether they had been lying to her their entire lives.

*They haven't,* she told herself. *There's no reason for them to lie. They're dead. What do the dead have to hide?*

The thought was meant to be comforting. It arrived instead wearing the exact expression of doubt that her grandmother used to give the weather.

* * *

The ossuary of Hollow Creek stood where the valley pinched narrow, a low stone building older than any of the families it served, its walls packed with niches like a honeycomb built by very tidy bees. Each niche held bones wrapped in linen, each bundle labeled in a carver's hand — not names, never names, but small carved marks: an anchor for the fisherman, a lark for the singer, a crooked little dog for the boy who had once stolen half the village's sausages and been beloved for it forever.

Paloma's craft lived here. Not the hearing — the hearing came free, unwanted, like freckles — but the carving. When someone died in the valley, you gave them a bone marked with what they had loved, so that when they lingered, as the dead of Hollow Creek always lingered, they would remember themselves.

She set the bread on the offering shelf, broke the first loaf open so the smell could rise, and said, in the formal cadence her grandmother had taught her before she could walk:

"Honored ones. A carver asks."

The murmur that rose from the walls was familiar as rain. Hundreds of voices layered over one another, soft and constant, the sound the valley made when it thought no one was listening. Old Marta was already complaining that the bread was better than last year's, which from Marta was a love letter. The sausage boy was asking after the miller's dog. Someone was singing. There was always someone singing.

"I have a question," Paloma said. "And I need a true answer."

The murmur dipped, the way a market square goes quiet when a stranger walks through it. Not silent. Just... attentive.

"A man crossed the boundary stones last night," she said. "He shouldn't have been able to. Nothing magic crosses those stones — that's the whole point of them, that's what we tell our children, that's what I carve into the memorial stones myself." She heard her own voice sharpen and made herself gentler. "He carried a book. It spoke of a covenant. It named tonight's date, and his death, and — me. 'The carver who hears.'"

Silence.

Not the ordinary pause between questions and answers. Silence like a door closing. The murmur didn't fade; it *stopped*, hundreds of voices cut off at once, as if someone had dropped a bell jar over the entire ossuary.

Paloma stood in the ringing quiet with her hands going cold around the bread knife.

"Old Marta," she tried. "You've never once in your death kept your mouth shut about anything. Tell me about the covenant."

Nothing.

"Sausage boy. Lark-singer. Anchor-fisherman. Anyone." Her voice cracked upward, embarrassingly young. "Someone say something. You talk more than the river. You talked through my mother's funeral. You talked through the *sermon*. One word about a covenant."

The silence stretched. She counted her heartbeats against it the way she counted stitches in a carving — one, two, three — and somewhere around forty she understood, with a slow sick certainty, that this wasn't confusion or ignorance.

This was discipline.

This was a hundred years of practice.

"You *know*," she whispered. "You all know. You've known the whole time."

The dead of Hollow Creek Valley, who had held her hand through every grief of her life in their murmuring, murmuring way, who had taught her which herbs eased pain and which men to avoid and how to carve a crooked little dog so it looked loved rather than merely fed — the dead said nothing at all.

She walked out of the ossuary into the bright indifferent morning and discovered she was shaking.

* * *

She tried the churchyard next, because surely it was only the ossuary crowd, the old guard, keeping some old rule. The churchyard dead were younger, chattier, closer to living memory.

"Hello, hello," said the schoolmaster, before she'd even finished the formal greeting, because he was rude and wonderful that way. "Is it true there's a man sleeping in your house? Widow Ansel's granddaughter swears she saw light coming out of him. Light, Reyes! Out of his *elbow!*"

"It is true," Paloma said. "And he brought a book about a covenant. Do any of you know—"

The schoolmaster stopped mid-word. So did the drowned ferryman. So did the two sisters buried side by side who had spent ninety years bickering about whose turn it was to haunt the orchard.

Silence again. The same silence. Practiced, coordinated, total.

Paloma stood among the leaning headstones and felt something in her chest tilt, like furniture shifting in a house that had always seemed solid.

They had organized. Her gossips, her grumblers, her sweet rude chorus — they had *organized*, a century ago, and agreed on a story, and kept it, and let her grow up inside the lie like a seed inside a shell.

"Fine," she said aloud, to all of them, to none of them. "Fine. Keep your secret. I'll just go ask the man bleeding light in my kitchen, shall I? Since apparently I take my history from strangers now."

Still nothing. But underneath the silence, if she strained, she felt something else — not words, not answers. A pressure. A leaning. Like a hundred hands hovering near her shoulders, wanting to touch, not daring.

*We are sorry,* the pressure said, though no voice said it. *We cannot.*

"Well," Paloma said, blinking hard at the sky, "you could have *said* that part."

* * *

By late afternoon she had exhausted the churchyard, the barrow-field, and the memorial cairn by the falls, and had received the same beautiful, infuriating nothing from every honored dead in the valley except one category: the recently dead. The newly arrived ghosts knew nothing — of course they didn't; whatever the secret was, it predated them, and the elder dead had clearly instructed them in silence along with everything else. Even the newest ghost, a shepherd who'd died three winters back, only looked at her with a ghost's version of apologetic eyes and said, *Ask Marisol,* and then clamped his own mouth shut with visible effort, like a man biting down on a scream.

Marisol.

Her grandmother.

Who had been, in life, the valley's bone-carver before her, the keeper of the ossuary, the woman who had taught Paloma every formal cadence and every informal shortcut, who had died eleven years ago in her sleep with clean hands and a closed chest at the foot of her bed, and who had lingered afterward the way all the valley dead lingered — but quietly. Quieter than any of them. Paloma had always assumed it was dignity. Grandmother Marisol had been a dignified woman; she'd have found old Marta's eternal bread complaints undignified in the extreme.

Now Paloma wondered if the quiet had been something else. Now she wondered about the closed chest.

She climbed the path behind her cottage as the sun went long and gold, up to the family plot on the shoulder of the hill where three generations of Reyes carvers lay under stones Paloma had carved herself. Evening birdsong. Smell of thyme crushed underfoot. All the ordinary machinery of a valley that had never once, in her whole life, frightened her.

"Grandmother," she said. "It's Paloma."

The presence gathered slowly out of the evening air — a warmth, a weight, the smell faintly of linseed oil and dried lavender, which had been Marisol's carving-kit smell and remained her ghost-smell now. Paloma sat down on the grass beside the headstone, knees drawn up, and did not ask about the covenant. Not directly. She'd learned that much today.

"I met a man," she said instead. "Well. I *found* a man. He fell over my boundary stones leaking light like a cracked lantern, and then he said my name, which I hadn't given him, and now he's asleep in my good bed with a book that knows things it shouldn't." She pulled a blade of grass, stripped it, abandoned it. "Everyone else has gone quiet on me, Grandmother. Every dead soul in this valley has gone quiet as a locked room. Even the sausage boy, and that boy once narrated his own funeral from the grave because he thought the eulogy was inaccurate."

The warmth beside her did not stir.

"I'm not angry," Paloma said, which was true in the way that a storm cloud is not yet raining. "I just — I've given my whole life to the dead. Every carving. Every listening hour. Every complaint about bread taken seriously and answered with better bread. I thought we had an arrangement, the living and the dead of this valley. I thought we were honest with each other because nobody else would put up with either of us." Her voice wobbled and she hated it and let it anyway. "You were the one who taught me the hearing was a gift. You never said it was also a leash."

Evening settled. The birdsong thinned. The warmth of her grandmother hovered at the edge of speaking — Paloma could feel it gathering the way she could feel a carving deciding what it wanted to be under her knife — and then, just when Paloma had decided the silence would win, Marisol moved.

Not toward her. *Away.* The presence slid along the hillside, westward, toward the boundary stones at the far end of the valley, and paused there, at the invisible line where the world of Hollow Creek ended and the world that magic couldn't enter began.

"Grandmother?" Paloma got to her feet, heart hammering. "What's out there?"

The presence pressed against the boundary like a hand laid flat against glass. Held there a long moment. Then it turned, and came back to her, and did the thing no valley ghost ever bothered to do, because words were cheap and murmurs were easier: it shaped one single word, carefully, deliberately, in the voice of dry leaves and river-stones, and set it down in front of Paloma like a key on a table.

"*Steppe.*"

Then the warmth folded itself away, and her grandmother's plot was just grass and stone and thyme, and the evening was just an evening, and Paloma Reyes stood alone on the hill holding one word and understanding exactly how little one word was worth against a book full of dates and deaths.

*Steppe.*

She turned it over as she walked home in the blue dark. The Glass Steppe — the name every child in the valley learned as a warning, the great shimmering waste beyond the mountains where magic ran wild and unfenced, where the covenant — *there was that word again* — was said to hold the wild places apart from the mortal world. A fairy-tale geography. A place that had never touched her life and never would, because nothing from beyond the stones could follow her here.

Except, apparently, it could send a man.

Except, apparently, her grandmother knew its name well enough to spend her one spoken word on it.

Paloma let herself into her cottage. The stranger was still asleep in her good bed, breathing shallowly, the book shut on the table beside him with its brass corners catching the firelight. On its cover, tooled into leather gone brown as old bone, was stamped a design she hadn't examined closely before — too busy yesterday with blood and panic and the sheer logistics of a man who bled light.

Now she looked. Circles within circles, lines radiating from a central point, and at the center, small and precise, a sigil: a stylized figure like a hand, or a root system, or a river delta — and she knew it. Knew it the way you know a face in a dream. Her grandmother had doodled it in the margins of recipe books. Had scratched it absently into candle wax. Had worn it, once, on a cord around her neck that she'd stopped wearing the year Paloma turned seven and never explained why.

Paloma stood in her own kitchen, fire crackling, stranger breathing, dead outside the window keeping their hundred-year silence, and felt the ground of her whole careful life shift beneath her like ice deciding whether to hold.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would wake the stranger, read the book, demand answers from the living since the dead wouldn't give them.

But tonight, she took the book gently in both hands — and it was warm, warmer than a book should be, warm as a living thing — and opened it to the page that named tonight.

And found, tucked into the binding's spine, a corner of parchment in a handwriting she knew better than her own.

Her grandmother's.
