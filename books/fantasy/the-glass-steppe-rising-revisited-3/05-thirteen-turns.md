## 5. Thirteen Turns

Ilya had rehearsed the explanation eleven times on the road. He'd rehearsed it in the voice of Master Oren, grave and deliberate; in the voice of Sister Anthea, brisk and unsentimental; even once in the voice of the last messenger, whose diary he carried and whose cadences he'd come to know like a scar. All eleven rehearsals assumed a listener who would nod thoughtfully and ask intelligent questions.

What he got instead was Paloma Reyes holding a carving knife.

"Start from the beginning," she said, "and don't use any word you can't carve."

He considered this. "That rules out most of my vocabulary."

"Good. Start smaller."

They were sitting on her workbench stools — well, she was sitting; he was perched at the very edge of his, aware that bone dust clung to everything in this house like a second family. Outside, the valley did its evening work: someone's goat complaining about something eternal, woodsmoke standing up straight in the windless air, the boundary stones catching the last light so they glowed faintly amber, like teeth in an old jaw. Inside, it smelled of marrow-oil and cedar shavings and, underneath it all, of the particular silence that magic made when it pressed its face against a window and was not let in.

"The beginning," Ilya said, "is a hundred years ago."

"Slower."

"A hundred years ago," he said again, "there were thirteen people who didn't want the world to end."

Paloma turned her knife over in her fingers but didn't set it down. He decided to take that as encouragement.

"They were smiths and singers and one very stubborn shepherd, according to the records, and what they wanted was simple enough: two worlds, kept apart. The mortal realms — your world, my world, the world of bread and taxes and bad knees — and the Glass Steppe, which is..." He searched for a word he could carve. "A place. A big one. Older than ours and hungrier than ours, and full of things that don't understand why we draw lines."

"And the covenant is the line."

"The covenant is thirteen lines," Ilya said. "Thirteen promises, each one signed by a living soul who gave their name into the glass. That's what a signatory *is*. Not a ruler. A hinge. The Steppe stays on its side of the door, our side stays on ours, and the hinge holds as long as—" He stopped.

"As long as what?"

"As long as the moon agrees." He said it plainly because there was no other way to say it. "The old rite tied the covenant to the lunar calendar. Twelve turns of the moon, one for each... no, that's not right either. Twelve turns for the twelve living signatories, and the thirteenth turn belonged to the covenant itself. Every century, the moon makes a thirteenth turn — a turn that doesn't belong to any month, a turn the calendars pretend not to see — and during that turn, the hinges are supposed to be re-greased. Re-sworn. Someone stands at the council-chasm and speaks the words, and the glass remembers, and everyone goes home."

"And if nobody speaks the words?"

Ilya looked down at the book in his lap. It was old enough that its leather had gone the color of tea left too long, and heavy enough that carrying it for four hundred miles had rearranged his spine into a shape he suspected was permanent. He opened it to the page he'd been avoiding since the boundary stones, the page with tonight's date written in faded iron-gall ink by a hand dead a century.

"Then the door doesn't lock behind the thirteenth turn," he said. "It opens. All the way. And the Steppe comes through."

* * *

Paloma was quiet for long enough that he could hear the goat outside finally winning its argument with somebody.

"Floods," she said at last. "You said floods. There's no water out there. I've heard the dead talk about the Steppe my whole life — fragments, mutters, things that drift up against the stones — and none of them ever mentioned water."

"It's not water." Ilya rubbed his eyes. "That's the part I keep getting wrong when I explain it, so forgive me. Think of it less like a flood and more like... dye. You drop one thread of red wool into white cloth and the whole bolt goes pink eventually. Magic is like that. It doesn't push. It *soaks*. If the covenant lapses, the Steppe won't march across the valley with banners. It will just start being true here. And everything here that isn't built to hold it—"

"Unravels."

"You've seen it?"

"I've carved bones for people who saw the edges of it." Her voice went flat in a way he was learning meant she was protecting something. "Travelers who came through the stones by accident, years ago. One man swore his shadow walked ahead of him for three days before it came back. A woman brought me her daughter's hairbrush and asked me to burn it because the daughter had started hearing it hum. We burned it. The humming moved to the daughter's teeth." She looked at him. "The valley refuses magic. That's not a story we tell children. That's the whole architecture of the place. The stones aren't decoration."

"No," Ilya agreed. "The stones are the last stitch. When the order built the valley's warding — and yes, before you ask, the order helped build this entire valley, that's a conversation for a night with more wine — the boundary stones were designed as a failsafe. A pocket of refusal. Somewhere the dye couldn't reach." He tapped the book. "But a pocket isn't a wall. It holds against seepage. Against stray things. Against a traveler who stumbles through bleeding light." He gestured at himself with rueful apology; his forearm still glowed faintly through the bandage, like a lantern wrapped in linen. "It does not hold against the entire Steppe arriving at once with a century of backed-up appetite. Nothing holds against that. The stones will crack like sugar glass, and then—"

"Then the humming moves into everyone's teeth."

"Yes."

* * *

Here is what Ilya did not say, sitting on the edge of that stool in a bone-carver's kitchen while a woman he'd known for one day sharpened a knife with the patience of a glacier:

He did not say that the order had known about the thirteenth turn for thirty years and had argued about it for twenty-nine of them. He did not say that the argument had been between those who wanted to find a new carver — because the prophecy spoke of a carver, always a carver, *the carver who hears* — and those who believed the whole enterprise was doomed and the kindest thing was to spend the last century teaching people how to say goodbye. He did not say that the doomsayers had been winning until the dying started.

And he especially did not say that the reason he, specifically, a junior archivist with a bad lung and a talent for being overlooked, had been chosen as messenger was that the senior members had drawn lots and he had volunteered before the lots were drawn, because a man with a year to live spends it differently than a man with fifty.

All of that could wait. Some of it could wait forever.

What he said instead was: "There's a deadline."

"How long?"

"The thirteenth turn began six nights ago."

Paloma's knife stopped moving. "Six nights. And you've been walking for—"

"Four hundred miles. Yes. I know." He held up a hand before she could say the next thing, because he'd already said it to himself every night by the fire, in all eleven voices. "I know what you're going to ask. Why send a man on foot? Why not a bird, a spell, a letter, anything faster? Because the covenant doesn't recognize birds or letters. It recognizes *bearers*. A living person, carrying the book, speaking the words at the chasm. That's the mechanism. The founders built it that way on purpose — they wanted whoever renewed the covenant to have to *walk there*, to see the Steppe, to understand what they were swearing to. No one should renew a promise they haven't sweated over." He smiled without much humor. "The founders were sentimental about suffering. Most people who build permanent things are."

"So the words get spoken at the chasm by the bearer. By you." She frowned. "Then why am I in the prophecy? The book names me. 'The carver who hears.' If you're the messenger, what am I?"

"I don't know."

"You don't—"

"I don't know," Ilya repeated, and found that saying it twice made it no more honest but slightly more bearable. "The final pages are damaged. Water damage, or fire, or someone's thumb over a hundred years of handling. The rite itself — the actual wording — is partially legible, and the parts that are legible reference a second voice. A witness. An ear." He looked at her hands, at the fine pale scars crosshatching her knuckles like a map of small decisions. "The order spent years looking for someone who could hear the dead clearly enough to serve. Do you know how rare that is? Most mediums are radios in a storm. The records describe you the way sailors describe a lighthouse."

"I'm not a lighthouse. I'm a woman in a valley who talks to grandmothers."

"Grandmothers," Ilya said, "are the hardest audience there is. Believe me. I've presented to councils."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth that on another face would have been the beginning of a smile. On hers it was more like a crack in ice — brief, structural, quickly patched. But he counted it. He was keeping a tally, though he'd have denied it under torture.

* * *

"Say the rest," Paloma said. "The part you're circling."

"There's no part I'm—"

"You've checked your book four times since you sat down. You keep touching the cover like it's a wound. Say the rest."

Ilya exhaled. Outside, the light had gone fully blue, that hour when the valley seemed to hold its breath between day and dark, and the boundary stones had brightened from amber to a low white heat. He watched them and told the truth, because apparently this woman extracted truth the way other people pulled splinters: quickly, and with an expectation that you'd thank her after.

"When the covenant lapses fully — when the thirteenth turn completes without renewal — the unraveling starts at the edges of the world and works inward. The order's models gave the mortal realms perhaps a season. Maybe two. But the valley—" He hesitated.

"The valley has the stones."

"The valley has the stones," he agreed, "and the stones are woven into the covenant itself. They're a knot tied into the same rope. When the rope frays, the knot doesn't survive it. Your protection isn't separate from the thing that's dying, Paloma. It's a limb of it. When the Steppe floods through, the valley doesn't get spared. The valley gets *dissolved first*, because it's the piece of the mortal world most saturated with covenant-work. Everything the stones have refused for a hundred years — everything pressed up against that boundary, waiting — comes through at once." He met her eyes. "Every dead thing that's ever murmured at the edge of your hearing. All of them. At once. With nothing between you and them."

The knife in her hand had gone very still.

"That's why you came to me first," she said quietly. "Not for help. To warn me."

"I came to you for help," Ilya said. "The warning came free."

* * *

She stood then, and paced, and he let her, because he'd learned on the road that people process catastrophe at different speeds and the worst thing you can do is walk beside them narrating. She went to the window. She looked at the boundary stones glowing in the dusk like a ring of banked coals. She said something under her breath that he chose not to hear, out of courtesy and a little fear — because he'd gathered that when Paloma Reyes spoke softly to the air, sometimes the air answered.

When she turned around, her face had settled into something he recognized from the order's war rooms: not courage exactly, but the decision to arrange your features as if courage were present until it took the hint.

"Say I believe you," she said. "Say I believe all of it — the covenant, the turn, the flood. What is it you actually want from me? Walk to a chasm in a haunted glass desert and hold your book open while you read? Because I'll tell you now, Ilya-of-the-bleeding-arm, I have never left this valley. Not once. My whole life is in a half-mile radius, and I like it that way, and the dead I know here are *mine*, and everything past those stones is a rumor I've spent thirty years declining to check."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you talk like a man delivering a bill."

"My sister used to say that." The words escaped before he could vet them, and he felt them land somewhere between them, warm and awkward. "She said I had the manner of a tax collector with good intentions."

"Was she right?"

"She usually was." He closed the book and set both hands flat on it, grounding himself. "Here's what I want. Honestly. Not the mission statement — the want. I want to not do this alone. The prophecy needs a carver who hears, and I need—" He stopped, restarted, chose smaller words. "I have walked four hundred miles talking to a diary. The last messenger walked this same road alone, and I've read how it ended for her, and I would rather fail with company than succeed the way she succeeded, which was completely, and then die anyway, alone, in a canyon, with her final entry being a complaint about the weather."

Paloma looked at him for a long moment.

"What was the weather complaint?" she asked.

"'Wind from the east smells of rust. Suspect this is ominous.'"

Despite herself — he watched it happen despite herself — she laughed. It was short and rusty, a laugh that had been stored rather than used, but it was real, and it changed the whole geometry of the room. The knife went down on the bench. Not surrendered. Set aside, the way a negotiator sets aside a document.

"If I come with you," she said, "it's not because of your prophecy. Prophecies are just gossip with better posture. I'll come because the dead at my boundary have been murmuring for a hundred years, and I always assumed they were lost travelers looking for directions. If they're signatories — if they're *waiting* — then I owe them more than directions." She picked up a scrap of bone from the bench, turned it, set it down. "And because if the valley dissolves, everyone I've ever carved a memorial for dissolves with it, and I did not spend my life keeping their names for nothing."

"That's the best recruitment speech anyone's given me all century," Ilya said, "and I wasn't even giving it."

"Don't get comfortable. I haven't said yes."

"You set the knife down."

"I set the knife down," she agreed, "because if I'm leaving this valley, I'm deciding which tools come with me, and that takes longer than one evening." She glanced toward the back of the house, where he knew from his one tour of the premises that her grandmother's chest sat under a floorboard she'd pretended not to look at twice today. Something crossed her face — a shadow with a family resemblance to dread. "Tomorrow," she said. "Tonight I sleep on it. Tonight you eat soup, because you look like a strong wind could file you under 'deceased,' and tomorrow morning—"

The boundary stones flared.

Both of them saw it through the window — the ring of white heat stuttering, dimming, flaring again, like a lamp running low on oil. The goat outside stopped mid-complaint. Even the woodsmoke seemed to lean.

Ilya was on his feet without remembering standing, the book already open in his hands, his heart doing arithmetic he didn't want finished. Six nights into a turn that should have been seamless. Six nights, and the stitches were already pulling.

"That's early," Paloma said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles were not. "That's early, isn't it."

"Yes," Ilya said. "Pack tonight."

* * *

Later — much later, when the soup was eaten and the lamps were out and he lay on her pallet of guest blankets listening to a house settle around two insomniacs — Ilya heard her speak through the wall. Softly. To the air, or to whatever waited on the far side of it.

"One word," she whispered. "Give me one word."

And the house was silent, and the valley was silent, and the stones burned their low anxious white, and Ilya lay in the dark understanding that he had just acquired a partner who talked to silence the way other people prayed — and that sometime before dawn, the silence was going to answer her, and neither of their lives would survive the answer intact.

He reached for the book in the dark and rested his hand on its cover, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you didn't entirely trust.

Outside, the moon was rising. Thirteen turns, he thought. Eleven nights left, if the stitching held.

Twelve, if it didn't.
