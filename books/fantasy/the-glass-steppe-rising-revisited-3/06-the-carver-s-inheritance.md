## 6. The Carver's Inheritance

The chest sat at the foot of Paloma's bed for two days before she could bring herself to open it.

It was not a beautiful thing. It had never been a beautiful thing — a squat box of valley cedar, iron-banded, its lid scarred where her grandmother had set a hot kettle down one too many times and then lied about it with tremendous dignity ("That was the frost, child"). It smelled of camphor and old linen and, underneath all that, faintly of bone dust, the way everything in Paloma's house did.

She had dusted around it her whole life. That was, she realized now, the point.

Ilya sat across the kitchen table with the prophecy book open between them, and he had the particular patience of a man who had learned that pushing Paloma Reyes was like pushing a river uphill. He'd tried once — *open it tonight, we're losing turns* — and she had looked at him with such flat valley calm that he'd actually apologized to his tea.

So instead they waited out the second day together, and Ilya filled the silence the way he seemed constitutionally incapable of not doing.

"You know what I find funny," he said, turning a page as though the page might bite him, which, given the book's track record, was a reasonable concern. "In the order's library, this text has eleven commentaries. Eleven. Scholars wrote entire treatises on whether 'the carver who hears' is one person or a lineage. A lineage! There's a footnote war. Footnote wars, Paloma. Two men stopped speaking to each other over a comma."

"Did either of them think to just come ask?"

"They thought the valley was a metaphor." He smiled, tired. "Most of the order thinks most things are metaphors. It's how you survive reading prophecy for forty years without screaming."

"And do you scream?"

"Only on feast days." He closed the book gently, fingers resting on its cover. "You don't have to open the chest because I'm here. You know that."

"I know." She ran her thumb along the edge of the table, feeling the grain. "I'm opening it because my grandmother spent sixty years telling me stories about our family, and every single one of them ended with *and so we came to the valley, thank the stones*. Not one story about before. Not one. You don't leave out a hundred years unless you're hiding something, and you don't hide something from your own granddaughter unless—" Her voice snagged. She smoothed it flat. "Unless you're ashamed."

"Or protecting you."

"Those wear the same coat," Paloma said, standing. "But only one of them fits."

The dead murmured their agreement from the corners of the house — a low rustle like wind through wheat, the sound they made when they were being tactful. Even the honored dead knew when a family was about to have an argument with itself across three generations.

* * *

The lock was no lock at all. It hadn't been for years; the little iron tongue had rusted into a decorative suggestion sometime before Paloma was born. What kept the chest closed was habit, reverence, and the unspoken law of grandmothers everywhere: *this is mine, and you will wait until I am gone.*

Her grandmother had been gone four springs.

Paloma knelt and lifted the lid, and the smell rose up to meet her — camphor, wool, dried lavender gone gray, and beneath it that older mineral note. Bone. Not finished work. Raw bone, waiting.

The top layer was innocent enough. Winter shawls, folded with the aggressive precision of a woman who believed wrinkles were a moral failing. A bundle of letters tied in string that Paloma already knew contained nothing but recipes and complaints about the weather, because she'd read them as a girl and been bored to tears. A christening gown nobody had worn in living memory. Small domestic archaeology. The sediment of an ordinary life, deliberately arranged to look like the whole of it.

Paloma lifted the shawls out anyway.

Beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay the tools.

She knew bone-carving tools. She'd inherited her own set — fine gravers, scrapers, a burnisher of river stone, all lovingly maintained. These were different. These were heavier, stranger, older. The handles were wound in wire gone black with age. The largest graver's blade wasn't steel at all but something pale and glassy that caught the lamplight wrong, bending it, the way the boundary stones bent the moon.

And cut into each handle, in a script she did not recognize but her hands, absurdly, wanted to trace: sigils. The same sigils. Over and over, like a signature practiced ten thousand times.

"Ilya," she said quietly.

He was beside her before she finished the word. He didn't touch anything. He'd learned that much about her craft — you didn't reach for another carver's tools uninvited, and you especially didn't reach for a dead woman's.

"That matches," he said, and his voice had gone careful, scholarly-careful, the voice he used when he was frightened and dressing the fear in footnotes. "Page thirty-one. The covenant diagrams. The smith's marks." He opened the book with hands that weren't quite steady and turned to a plate near the middle — a drawing of a great curved bone, covered edge to edge in interlocking symbols, and along its base a row of small marks that the commentary called *the maker's hand*.

The marks on the page matched the marks on the handles. Exactly.

Paloma sat back on her heels. The floorboards pressed cold against her knees. Somewhere in the walls, the dead had gone very quiet again — that listening quiet, the hundred-year quiet, the one she was beginning to hate more than any noise.

"There's more," she said, because there always was. There was always more. That was the shape her life had taken since a bleeding stranger fell through the stones: *there's more.*

* * *

Under the tools, under another layer of oilcloth, lay the bone itself.

Half-finished.

It was a long curve of something dense and yellowed, wider than her palm, and even unfinished it *hummed* — not sound, exactly, but pressure, the way a struck bell holds its note in the wood of the frame. Paloma had carved memorial bones since she was nine years old. She had held the femurs of saints and the finger bones of liars and the tiny perfect ribs of drowned children, and none of them, none, had ever pushed back against her hands like this.

The carving covered half its length. The other half was bare, sanded smooth, waiting — abandoned mid-sentence, if bone could speak a sentence, and Paloma was increasingly afraid that this one could.

"It's the covenant," Ilya breathed. "Or — part of it. A copy? A draft?" He hovered, wanting to look and knowing better than to grab. "The book describes thirteen signatories sealing the terms into a single anchor-bone. This is—"

"A forgery would be complete," Paloma said.

He stopped.

"A practice piece gets finished, even badly. An heirloom gets polished and displayed and lied about at festivals." She turned the bone slowly in the lamplight, and the half-done sigils shifted like fish under ice. "This stops. Mid-stroke. Look — see the last line, how it trails off, the groove getting shallower? She put the tool down in the middle of cutting. Nobody does that. Your hand finishes or it ruins the work. Every carver knows it. It's the first thing they teach you." Her jaw tightened. "She stopped anyway. On purpose. Which means she wasn't practicing, and she wasn't preserving. She was *refusing*."

The word dropped into the room and rang.

Paloma heard herself say it and felt the anger arrive behind it, slow and enormous, like weather coming over the ridge. Not hot anger. Valley anger — patient, deep-rooted, the kind that grew through rock.

"She was a smith," Paloma said. "A covenant-smith. One of *them*. The book names the founding bloodlines, doesn't it? Read me the names."

Ilya hesitated, then found the passage. "'And the terms were graven by the hands of the Smith-kin: Vess of the Salt Court; Oren of the Ashes; Marisol of the Glass Steppe—'"

"Say it again."

"*Marisol of the Glass Steppe.*"

"My grandmother's name was Marisol." Paloma laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Everyone called her Sol. Little sun. I thought it was because she liked the mornings. She got up before dawn her whole life, said sleeping past sunrise was for people with nothing to do." The bone trembled slightly in her grip, or perhaps her grip trembled in the bone. "She was named for the place she helped bind. And she ran from it. Ran here, past the stones, into the one place magic can't follow, and she raised my mother to raise me to carve little memorial trinkets for farmers, and she never — not once — not *once* told me."

"Paloma—"

"The dead whisper to me, Ilya. My whole life. And I asked her, when I was young — I asked her why me, why our family hears them, and she said, *because someone has to, child, and the gift lands where it lands.*" Paloma set the bone down on the folded shawls with exaggerated gentleness, because if she didn't handle something carefully right now she was going to throw it through a wall. "*The gift lands where it lands.* Like it was rain. Like it was luck. It wasn't luck. It was inheritance. It was a job she quit and left on my doorstep."

Ilya crouched down to her level. He was good at that — making himself smaller, making himself a listener rather than a lecturer. "Maybe she thought she was saving you."

"From what? Being useful? Being *needed*?" Paloma's voice rose and the dead in the walls rose with it, a sympathetic stir, and she reined them and herself both back down. "There are people out there who need a carver who can hear. There's a whole Steppe full of arguing ghosts and a covenant coming apart like wet paper, and my grandmother knew — she must have known, she *carved half the anchor*, she'd have known better than anyone what was fraying — and instead of teaching me, she taught me to stay small. Taught me the valley rules. Stay inside the stones. Don't go looking. Be content." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, furious at the fury. "I built my whole life inside a fence she put up and never explained."

"Grandmothers are allowed their mysteries," Ilya offered, weakly.

"Grandmothers are allowed mysteries about *lovers* and *lost fortunes* and why there's a dent in the kettle lid. This isn't a mystery. This is dereliction." She stood, knees cracking, forty-two years old and suddenly feeling every one of them plus several borrowed centuries. "And the worst part — the truly unforgivable part — is that she was probably kind about it. Wasn't she? She was kind. She taught me my first graver strokes on a soup bone. She held my hands steady. She told me I had a talent. She loved me." Paloma stared at the half-finished bone glowing faintly in the lamplight. "And she loved me *instead of telling me the truth*, and now some stranger bleeds light on my doorstep and tells me the world ends in thirteen turns, and the woman who could have prepared me is four years in the ground and I can't even ask her why."

The silence stretched.

Then, from the corner by the stove, where the oldest of the honored dead tended to gather — the ones who'd died in the valley before anyone could remember — came a murmur. Not words. Just a tone. The tone people used at funerals when they meant *there, there* and knew better than to say it aloud.

"Don't you dare comfort me," Paloma told the corner. "Not tonight."

The murmur retreated, chastened. Even the dead, apparently, feared a Reyes woman mid-reckoning.

Ilya gathered the tools back into their oilcloth with the tenderness of a man handling someone else's grief, which was precisely what he was doing. "For what it's worth," he said, "she didn't finish refusing, either. Half a refusal. She stopped the rite and she stopped the telling, and both halves are sitting in this room right now." He glanced up at her. "You could be the half that talks."

Paloma looked at him for a long moment. "You're infuriatingly quotable for a man who apologizes to tea."

"It's a burden."

Despite everything — despite the ache behind her sternum and the bone humming on the bed like a struck bell and the hundred-year silence pressing against the windows — she huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then she picked up the half-finished covenant-bone, wrapped it in the cleanest shawl, and held it against her chest the way you hold a child, or a verdict.

"We cross tomorrow," she said. "At dawn. Before I lose my nerve, or before the dead talk me out of it, or before I find a third secret in this chest and decide to burn the whole house down."

"Tomorrow," Ilya agreed softly.

But that night, as she banked the fire and listened to the house settle, Paloma noticed something she should have seen hours ago. The bottom of the chest — beneath the false floor her grandmother had surely installed herself, beneath the tools and the bone and the folded lies — was lined in lead. Lined thick, and fitted tight, and old.

Whatever Marisol of the Glass Steppe had hidden down there, she had hidden it from the dead.

And the dead, Paloma realized, her heart climbing into her throat, had been silent about the chest all evening. Not tactful.

*Silenced.*

She knelt, pried up the false floor, and found the letter.
