## 7. Crossing the Stones

Paloma had spent her whole life learning the exact weight of silence.

The valley kept it the way a good coffin keeps a body: snugly, respectfully, with an understanding that nothing inside was going anywhere. Magic could not follow past the boundary stones — everyone knew this, the way everyone knew the river ran south and the bread needed salt. It was not discussed. It was simply the shape of their lives, and Paloma had fitted herself to that shape so long ago she'd forgotten there had ever been a time before fitting.

Now she stood at the stones with a satchel of carving tools, her grandmother's half-finished sigil wrapped in wool against her chest, and a stranger who bled light watching her with the particular patience of a man who has already read how this scene ends.

"You don't have to do this," Ilya said, for the fourth time.

"I know." She said it for the fourth time too. There was a certain comfort in ritual, even a small one.

"The valley will not take you back the same."

"I heard you the first three times."

"And yet you keep standing here instead of walking through, which suggests some part of you is still negotiating." He smiled when he said it — a crooked, tired smile, the kind that apologized for itself even as it arrived. "I only mention it because I've spent a century's worth of other people's prophecies learning that people like to be told the truth twice and then decide anyway."

Paloma looked back down the slope, at the valley spread out below in its evening grays and greens. The smoke from Widow Hark's chimney. The bell rope hanging slack at the shrine. The little mound behind the meeting house where, tomorrow morning, someone would bury the memorial bone she'd finished two nights ago — the drowned child's name cut fine and true into sheep femur, every letter a small door the dead boy could open whenever he wanted company.

She would not be there to see it buried.

That was the thing about leaving, she was discovering. It wasn't one grief. It was ten thousand small ones, each patient, each waiting its turn.

"They'll wonder," she said.

"They'll grieve," Ilya corrected gently. "It's not the same thing, though it wears the same coat."

"You're very wise for a man leaking light."

"It's mostly borrowed wisdom. The book does most of my thinking." He patted the satchel where the prophecy rode against his hip. Then his face did something complicated — the smile folding inward, the humor going out of it like a candle pinched between wet fingers. "Paloma. Before we cross. If what's written in that book is true — if your family were covenant-smiths, if the Steppe has been waiting a hundred years for a carver who hears — then on the other side of these stones, you are not going to be who you were this morning."

"I'm aware."

"No, I don't think you are. Nobody is. That's rather the point of becoming someone new: it happens to you, not with you."

Paloma turned the sigil over in her hands through the wool. Her grandmother's knife-marks. Her grandmother's cowardice, or her grandmother's mercy — she still hadn't decided which word fit, and suspected she wouldn't until she'd walked a long way in the old woman's absence.

"Then let's find out who it happens to," she said, and stepped past the boundary stone before her feet could file another objection.

* * *

Ilya watched her go first, which surprised him. He had expected argument, negotiation, perhaps tears — he had budgeted for all three, the way a messenger learns to budget for everything. Instead she simply stepped through, shoulders square, chin tucked like a woman leaning into weather, and vanished into the space between the stones as though it were a doorway and not a wound in the world.

He followed, because following her was now the entire job description.

Crossing never felt like anything from the outside. From the valley side, the boundary stones were just eleven gray teeth in a ring of grass, unremarkable, the sort of thing farmers leaned against while eating lunch. But passing between them —

Passing between them was falling upward.

The world tilted without moving. Ilya felt the familiar lurch, the sensation of being poured from one container into another, and then the light changed — thickened, went amber and strange — and the grass underfoot became glass underfoot, and the air itself woke up.

Behind him, Paloma made a sound he would remember for the rest of his life, however long the book intended that to be.

It was not a scream. It was closer to the noise a person makes when cold water hits skin they didn't know was numb — shock and relief braided into one involuntary note. She stood three paces into the Glass Steppe with her arms slightly away from her body, fingers spread, and Ilya watched sixty years of silence come off her all at once.

"Oh," she said. Then, smaller: "Oh no."

"Paloma—"

"They're *loud*." Her voice had gone thin and bright, stretched over something enormous. "Here — here they're *loud*, why are they loud, in the valley they whisper, they whisper, I thought that was just how the dead *were*—"

"That's the valley," Ilya said. "The valley doesn't refuse magic, exactly. It muffles it. Your ancestors built it that way — a quiet room, so the smiths could rest between workings." He hesitated. "I should have warned you better."

"You said magic rushes back."

"I said it returns. I failed to convey—" he gestured helplessly at her, at the air around her, which seemed to Ilya to be practically vibrating, "—the volume."

Paloma laughed. It came out cracked and wild, halfway to sobbing, and she pressed both palms flat against her ears as though that had ever once worked in her life. "It's like standing inside a bell. It's like standing inside *every bell*. Who are they? Why are there so many? The Steppe is supposed to be empty, everyone says the Steppe is empty—"

"The Steppe is the opposite of empty. The Steppe is where emptiness went to be forgotten." Ilya moved to her side, close but not touching; he had learned that the newly-hearing needed proximity without pressure, a hand offered but not taken. "Can you sort them? Voices from voices?"

Her eyes were closed. Her lips moved faintly, counting, cataloguing, the bone-carver's habit of naming things asserting itself even now, even here. "There's — a woman arguing about a fence line. A child asking if anyone's seen her dog. Hundreds of — no. Thousands. Thousands, Ilya, they're layered like sediment, like — like rings in bone, each year pressed down on the last—"

"Yes."

"—and underneath all of them, something *old*. Something that isn't talking. Something that's *listening*." Her eyes opened, and Ilya saw that they had changed. Not the color — the depth. As though someone had added a room to a house overnight. "What's listening?"

"The Steppe," Ilya said quietly. "Or what's left of it. Or what it owes. Depending on which signatory you ask, and they will all fight you about it."

Paloma looked back.

Ilya watched her look back, and understood, in the way he understood most things now — secondhand, pre-written, a man reading over fate's shoulder — exactly what she was seeing. The boundary stones still stood behind them, gray and ordinary. And beyond them, impossibly, the valley: green and small and close, close enough to touch, close enough that he could see the shrine bell catch the last light. The crossing bent distance like a straw. One step back and she'd be home. One step back and the silence would fold over her again, warm and total, and all of this would become a strange vivid hour she could spend the rest of her life politely not mentioning.

The valley looked beautiful. The valley looked like a held breath.

And then, as they both watched, the light on the far side dimmed — not sunset, something else — and the valley shrank. Not gradually. All at once, the way a name shrinks when you learn what the person bearing it really did. The green pulled back. The details went soft. In the space of a heartbeat it went from *home* to *a place* to *a memory of a place*, and the boundary stones that had guarded it for generations suddenly looked less like teeth and more like a fence around a garden whose gardener had died.

Paloma made the cold-water sound again, quieter this time.

"There it goes," she said.

"It doesn't go anywhere. It's still there. It will always be there."

"You know what I mean." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she probably shooed flies — no ceremony, no permission asked. "My whole life I thought the valley was protecting me. From magic. From the dead. From — all this." She turned in a slow circle, taking in the glass plain, the amber light, the horizon that broke and reformed like a sentence being rewritten. "But it wasn't protection, was it. It was a *muzzle*. They muzzled me and called it peace."

"Your grandmother bought that peace," Ilya said carefully. "However she bought it."

"With a broken rite and a hundred-year loan." Paloma's jaw set, and Ilya recognized the expression from the night she'd shown him the sigil — the anger that was really grief wearing armor. "And loans come due. You told me. Thirteen turns."

"Thirteen turns," he agreed.

"Then we'd best not stand here admiring the view." She hoisted her satchel higher on her shoulder and faced out across the Steppe, away from the shrinking valley, away from everything shaped like safety. "Which way?"

Ilya blinked. He had prepared speeches for refusal, for panic, for collapse. He had, in a moment of professional thoroughness, even prepared one for her turning around and walking home, which the book insisted would not happen but which he had felt prudent to draft regardless. He had prepared nothing for *which way*, delivered like a woman asking directions to market.

"That way," he said, pointing east, toward the broken horizon, toward the salt courts and the chasm and everything the book promised with its terrible tidy handwriting. "Toward the Salt Court. Two days' walk, if the mirages behave."

"Do mirages behave?"

"Ours haven't been informed of the question."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her — brief, startled, real. She took one step, then another, and Ilya fell in beside her, and together they walked out onto the Glass Steppe while the dead rose around them like heat shimmer, thousands strong, murmuring, arguing, singing, waiting.

Half a mile on, Paloma stopped abruptly.

"What?" Ilya said, hand going to his knife. "Hunters? Mirages?"

"Worse." She was staring at her own hands, turning them over in the amber light. "I can hear my own bones."

"...I beg your pardon?"

"My bones. They're *talking*. Well — humming. Like struck tuning forks." She flexed her fingers, frowning with fierce concentration. "They're saying something. It's faint. It sounds like — counting?"

Ilya felt the bottom drop out of his stomach, because he knew — he had read it a dozen times, in a dead language, by candlelight, hoping he was mistranslating — what lived in the bones of a covenant-smith's bloodline. What the Steppe put there. What it was doing right now, presumably, checking its inventory.

"Paloma," he said, as evenly as a man can say anything with his heart hammering, "how good are you at ignoring things?"

She gave him a look that suggested the answer was *not very, and you knew that before you asked.*

"Counting," she repeated softly, closing her fingers into a fist, as if she could hold the sound inside. "Down from thirteen."

Ahead of them, far across the glass, something answered — a low tone rolling beneath the plain like a bell rung underwater, and every ghost within earshot went silent at once, all thousand voices hushing in the same instant, the way a forest goes quiet when it decides, all together, to watch.

Paloma lifted her chin toward the sound.

"Well," she said. "I suppose it knows we're coming."
