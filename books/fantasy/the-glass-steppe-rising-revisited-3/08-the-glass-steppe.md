## 8. The Glass Steppe

Ilya had crossed into the Steppe eleven times in his life, and every time the first sight of it knocked something loose in his chest that took days to settle back into place. He'd assumed it would be different this time. He was dying, after all; he was a messenger on a final errand; he was accompanied by a bone-carver who heard the dead and who had just discovered that her entire family history was a lie stitched together from cowardice and good intentions.

Surely, he thought, watching Paloma Reyes take her first step onto the glass plain, surely this time the wonder would belong to someone else.

It did not.

The Steppe opened before them like a held breath finally released. Glass — not the green-brown glass of bottles or the cruel glass of windows, but something older and stranger, fused sand stretched flat to a horizon that didn't quite meet the sky. The horizon *broke*, that was the word the order used. It broke like a sentence interrupted, and beyond the break there was nothing so much as a suggestion of more, a pale smear of almost-color that made your eyes water if you looked too long.

And beneath their feet, beneath the whole impossible plain: cities.

"Careful where you step," Ilya said, because it was the only useful thing he knew how to say.

Paloma wasn't listening. She stood three paces onto the glass, her satchel of carving tools clutched against her chest like a shield, and she was staring down at her own feet with an expression Ilya would spend the rest of his life — however much of it remained — trying to describe.

Beneath the surface, dim and deep as fish under winter ice, moved the mirages. Towers. Streets. A bridge with lamps along its spine, all of it suspended somewhere far below the glass, tilted at angles no city should tolerate. As they watched, a spire drifted past beneath them, slow as a cloud, and inside it — impossibly, horribly — tiny figures at tiny windows, going about whatever business the drowned dead of the Steppe still went about.

"They're *under* us," Paloma said. Her voice came out flattened, pressed thin by the scale of everything.

"Under, around, occasionally through." Ilya crouched and rapped his knuckles on the glass. It rang — a low, resonant note that traveled outward in a visible ripple, shivering the mirage-cities like reflections in a struck pond. "The order's teaching is that the Steppe was a sea once. Then the covenant was signed, and the sea agreed to hold still. Everything that ever lived in it got frozen mid-motion. Cities, ships, people. All of it preserved in the pause."

"That's not preservation," Paloma said quietly. "That's a held breath."

"Yes," Ilya said. "That's rather the point of everything we're doing here."

She looked up at him then, and he saw the moment the awe curdled — saw it happen in real time, the wonder draining out of her face and leaving behind something colder and more honest. He recognized it because he'd worn the same expression on his first crossing, at nineteen, holding the order's primer and feeling suddenly like a man who had been handed a beautiful book and told, only afterward, what it cost.

"The dead here," she said. "I can hear them already. Not whispering. Not like the valley."

"No," Ilya said. "They don't whisper here."

* * *

They walked for most of the day, following a route Ilya knew from maps that were themselves older than most nations. The Steppe did not reward wandering. There were places where the glass thinned to a membrane over deeper hollows, and there were things that lived in the hollows, and the order's primer devoted an entire chapter to them under the cheerful heading *What Not To Do, Comprehensively.*

Paloma walked beside him in silence for the first hour. He let her have it. Some silences were doors you had to leave closed until the person behind them chose to open them, and Paloma Reyes struck him as a woman with many doors and strong opinions about which ones stayed shut.

It was she who broke first, naturally. He was beginning to learn that about her.

"Your order," she said. "The one that sent you. How many are left?"

"Nine," Ilya said. "Seven, now, counting the two who died getting me to the boundary stones. We were forty when I was chosen."

"Chosen how?"

"Ah." He laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle as the ground they walked on. "You'll love this. They drew lots."

"Lots."

"Straws, technically. Elder Vashem carved them himself out of a saint's fingerbone, which the younger members thought was very holy and the older members thought was extremely morbid, and both factions were correct." He stepped carefully over a seam in the glass where two ancient panes of the world had fused imperfectly. "I drew the short one. There was a moment of terrible silence, and then Brother Anselm put his hand on my shoulder and said, 'Well. Someone had to,' which remains the least comforting sentence ever spoken to me."

Paloma snorted. It was a small sound, quickly suppressed, but Ilya caught it and treasured it. Two days of knowing her, and that was the first laugh he'd gotten, even a broken one.

"You volunteered," she said. Not a question.

"I'll tell you why someday," he said. "Not today. Today I'm going to teach you the signs, and you're going to complain about them, and we're both going to pretend the horizon isn't broken, and it will be a lovely afternoon."

"It's morning."

"It's a lovely morning, then."

* * *

The signs, as the Order of the Last Page taught them, were less a language than a set of courtesies. The Steppe was inhabited — by ghosts, by mirage-things, by the wind itself, which Ilya assured Paloma was a topic they would discuss later and which made her eyes narrow in a way that suggested she had already met it.

"First sign." Ilya held up his right hand, palm out, fingers spread, then folded the middle two down. "This means *living*. You show it to anything that addresses you, before anything else. Ghosts here are old, and old ghosts forget what living people look like. Some of them get upset when reminded. This tells them gently."

"And if they get upset anyway?"

"Then you show the second sign." He reversed his hand, knuckles out, thumb tucked. "*Passing*. It means you mean no claim on them, their territory, their memories, or their grudges. Grudges are — significant, here. Some of these dead have been nursing the same grievance since before my order existed."

"What do they have grudges *about*?"

"The covenant," Ilya said. "Mostly. What it promised them. What it took. Which faction got the better terms." He hesitated, then decided honesty was the better part of self-preservation. "Which is, incidentally, exactly the argument we're walking into. The signatories' ghosts split into factions a long time ago. Thirteen signatories, and depending on whom you ask, anywhere from four to nine surviving factions."

"Thirteen ghosts can't agree with each other."

"Thirteen ghosts have had a hundred years to disagree with each other. It compounds. Like interest."

Paloma practiced the signs as they walked — *living*, *passing*, and then the third, which Ilya demonstrated with some reluctance because it always felt theatrical: both hands cupped together, as if holding water, offered forward. *Asking*. It meant you wished to speak with the dead directly, and it committed you to hearing the answer.

"That one," Paloma said slowly, turning her hands over, "I've been doing my whole life without meaning to."

"Then you're ahead of the curriculum."

"There's a fourth, isn't there. You keep skipping it."

Ilya kept his eyes on the horizon. "There's a fourth."

"Ilya."

"*Refusing*," he said. "Fist closed, drawn across the throat. It means *no*. And before you ask — yes, it works on the dead, and no, they don't like it, and yes, sometimes it's the only thing that works. The order teaches it last because half the novices who learned it first never learned anything else. You give a frightened person a way to say no to a ghost, and they say it to every ghost, forever, and then they learn nothing and go home and write bitter memoirs."

"Do you have bitter memoirists in your order?"

"We have Brother Anselm. Same thing."

That got the second laugh, fuller than the first, and Ilya felt absurdly proud of it, like a man who had coaxed a flower out of stone.

* * *

They stopped at midday in the lee of a formation the maps called a Whale — a vast blister of glass bulging up from the plain, opaque and faintly blue-green, big as a barn. Inside it, if you pressed your ear to the surface, you could hear the sea that used to be. Ilya had done it once, at nineteen, and heard waves, and gulls, and something enormous breathing, and had not done it again.

He unpacked the food — hard bread, dried fruit, a wax-wrapped cheese that Elder Vashem had sworn was blessed, though it tasted mostly of wax — while Paloma wandered a few paces off and stood looking down into the glass.

"There's a market down there," she said. "A whole market. Stalls. People carrying baskets." A pause. "One of them just looked up at me."

"Don't wave."

"I wasn't going to wave."

"Everyone wants to wave. It's the single most common cause of novice incidents. You wave, they see you, they start *rising*, and then you spend six hours talking a drowned fishmonger back down to the bottom of the world while he explains, in great detail, everything that was taken from him." Ilya bit into the bread. "Brother Anselm has done it four times. He's written a pamphlet."

"He sounds delightful."

"He's the reason I know how to talk people down off ceilings, metaphorically speaking. Come eat. And then—" he hesitated, because the next part of the lesson was the part he dreaded, the part that turned awe into arithmetic— "then I should show you the map of where we're going, and how long it takes, and you should hear the numbers sitting down."

Paloma came and sat. She ate her bread and looked out at the broken horizon, and Ilya unrolled the map — a sheet of treated hide, inked a century ago, annotated by a dozen messengers in a dozen hands. Their route ran diagonally across the Steppe: boundary stones to the Salt Court, Salt Court to the Mirrored Ford, Ford to the council-chasm. On foot, in good conditions, with the Steppe in a cooperative mood: nineteen days.

"And the moon turns thirteen times in how long?" Paloma asked.

"Twenty-six nights. But the turns aren't even. They compress. The closer the covenant comes to failing, the faster they fall." He tapped the margin, where a previous messenger had scrawled, in a hand gone shaky: *the last three turns came in four days.* "Best case, we arrive at the chasm with five turns left. Worst case—"

"Worst case we arrive and it's already over."

"Worst case we arrive and it's nearly over, which is worse, because then we'll know exactly how much we missed it by."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. Down in the glass, the market went about its frozen commerce. Somewhere far off — miles, maybe tens of miles — something sang. One long note, rising, patient. Ilya didn't explain it. There were some things you let people discover in their own time, and the singers of the Steppe were one of them.

"My valley," Paloma said at last. "I spent thirty-one years thinking it was the whole world. Or — no. That's not true. I spent thirty-one years pretending I thought it was the whole world, because the alternative was wanting something I couldn't have." She turned the bread over in her hands without eating it. "And now I'm standing on top of a drowned ocean full of arguing ghosts, and the strangest thing — the genuinely strangest thing — is that some part of me is *homesick*. For this place. A place I've known for half a day."

Ilya understood that completely, and it frightened him how completely.

"It's the glass," he said. "Elder Vashem's theory — she had a theory for everything, mostly wrong — was that the Steppe remembers being a sea, and seas are homesick by nature. Every tide is a failed attempt to return to the moon. So the whole plain aches, constantly, toward something it can't reach, and anyone who stands on it catches the ache like a cold." He smiled ruefully. "The other elders said that was nonsense. The other elders also refused to walk the Steppe, so I've always weighted Vashem's vote heavily."

"A sea trying to go home." Paloma looked out at the broken horizon, and her expression did something complicated — grief and recognition tangled together. "I know the feeling."

* * *

They walked again through the long afternoon, and Ilya taught, and Paloma learned with a speed that unsettled him slightly, the way a gifted student always unsettles their teacher. By evening she could run the three courtesy signs without thinking, could identify a thin-glass hollow by the faint oily rainbow at its rim, and had asked seventeen questions he could answer and three he couldn't.

The third unanswerable one came as the light went amber and long.

"The last messenger," she said. "The one before you. What happened to him?"

The question landed in Ilya's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He kept walking. He counted his steps — one, two, three, four — the way he did when the truth needed a moment to arrange itself into something survivable.

"He reached the chasm," Ilya said finally. "He completed his errand, as far as the order ever knew. And then he didn't come back." Another four steps. "We don't know what happened to him. His bones were never found."

All of which was true, in the way that a locked door is true. What Ilya did not say — what he had been ordered never to say, and had privately resolved never to say regardless of orders, because some truths changed hands too early and shattered — was that the order knew exactly where the last messenger's bones were.

They were in a sealed case in the order's reliquary. And a portion of them — the finger bones, the ones Elder Vashem hadn't carved into lots — traveled in the satchel of every messenger sent after him, because the order believed, with a hundred years of evidence behind the belief, that the dead messenger guided the living ones. That his bones grew warm near danger. That they hummed, very faintly, when the right path lay ahead.

They were humming now. They had been humming since noon.

Ilya adjusted his satchel strap and said nothing, and the bones sang their small warm song against his hip, and ahead of them the first stars came out over the broken horizon, and somewhere in the deepening dark, the thing the bones were warming toward began — softly, patiently — to sing back.

Paloma stopped walking.

"Ilya," she said. "Do you hear that?"

He did. Of course he did. And from the look on her face — the carver who hears, hearing something new, something that was not a whisper and not an argument but a melody, deliberate and directed, aimed at *them* — he understood that the easy part of the journey had just ended.

"That," Ilya said, closing his hand around the satchel strap, "is either a welcome or a warning. And in my experience, on the Steppe, it is very rarely both."

The singing grew louder.
