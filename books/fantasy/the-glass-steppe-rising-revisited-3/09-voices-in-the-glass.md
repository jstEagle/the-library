## 9. Voices in the Glass

Paloma had spent her whole life believing she understood silence.

The valley taught it to you early. The dead there spoke in murmurs, in the hush of a mother humming over a cradle two hundred years empty, in the patient rustle of grandmothers checking on gardens they no longer owned. Even at the boundary stones, where the whispers thickened like fog, they were still *whispers* — polite, mournful, apologetic. Forgive us for intruding. We know you are busy with the living.

The Glass Steppe did not whisper.

It *argued*.

Paloma knew this within four steps of the ridge, before Ilya's outstretched hand even reached hers. It came up through the soles of her boots and settled behind her teeth like a struck tuning fork — a dozen voices, no, a hundred, layered and overlapping and *interrupting each other*, the way only people who have known one another far too long can interrupt.

"—the terms as written, as *signed*, I will not see them—"

"—written by cowards who flinched at the ninth clause—"

"*Flinched?* FLINCHED? You were not even present at the—"

"—she hears, do you feel that? She *hears* —"

"—quiet, quiet, all of you, you'll frighten—"

"She is a Reyes," said a voice near the bottom of the pile, low and gravelly, like a stone rolled downhill. "We don't frighten."

Paloma sat down. Not gracefully, not with intention — her knees simply resigned their post, and she found herself sitting in the glass sand with her hands flat against the ground, breathing the way she'd been taught to breathe when a dying man wanted his last words taken down and couldn't bear to be rushed.

"Paloma?" Ilya crouched beside her. His light-blood had dried to a faint gold shimmer along his forearm, which was somehow more alarming than open red would have been. "What is it? What do you hear?"

"Everything." She laughed, and it came out slightly unhinged. "Ilya, I hear *everything*. In the valley it's like listening through a wall. Here it's like—" she groped for it, "—like being handed a room full of strangers mid-quarrel and being told it's your fault."

"It might be," said the glass beneath her palms.

She yanked her hands back.

"I apologize," the voice added, sounding genuinely contrite. It was an old woman's voice, dry and precise, with the particular crispness of someone who had once held office. "That was unkind. But we have waited a long time, carver, and we are not practiced at courtesy anymore. Courtesy requires hope, and hope requires practice, and we have had neither."

Paloma looked at Ilya. Ilya looked at Paloma. Somewhere behind them, the sun was doing something wrong to the horizon — bending light off the fused plain so that the whole Steppe glowed from underneath, like a lamp under a bedsheet.

"You could have mentioned," she said, "that the ground talks."

"The ground doesn't talk." Ilya rubbed the back of his neck. "The *dead in* the ground talk. There's a difference. Technically."

"Technically."

"The order's texts were very specific about it."

"And your order's texts," said the old woman's voice, now coming from everywhere and nowhere in particular, "were written by men who visited once, took notes, and left. Sit down, messenger. You too, carver. If we are to negotiate the survival of two worlds, we will do it properly, seated, and you will learn our names."

"We're negotiating *now*?" Paloma's voice cracked upward. "I've been here half a day! I don't even know what the covenant *says* yet!"

"Then you are better prepared than most," said another voice — younger, sharper, amused. "Vess will tell you otherwise, because Vess believes preparation is a moral virtue, but Vess also believes salt is a food group."

"That is slander," said the old woman.

"That is *accuracy*," said the young one, delighted.

* * *

They made camp that night — or what passed for night, the sky deepening to a violet so dark it seemed to drink the stars — in the lee of a glass ridge shaped like a frozen wave. Ilya built a fire from twisted black shrubs that burned green and smelled, inexplicably, of rain. Paloma sat cross-legged with her bone-knife across her knees, the way she always did when she needed to think, and tried to sort the voices into something like a seating chart.

There were factions. That became clear within the hour. She thought of them, privately, as the way she'd once thought of the valley's quilting circle: three groups united by common purpose and divided by everything else.

The first faction spoke slowly and used words like *as written* and *lawful succession* and *the sanctity of the original signatures.* They were led — though none would admit to leading — by a ghost named Oren, who had apparently died defending a border no one remembered existed, and who treated every sentence as if it might be entered into evidence.

The second faction interrupted constantly. They wanted renewal, immediately, on any terms, because the moon was turning and cities were waking up inside the glass and screaming. Their loudest member was the sharp young voice, a girl named Sefa who had died at nineteen and had clearly decided that eternity was no excuse for patience.

And then there was Vess's faction, which did not argue so much as *wait* — a handful of voices that listened to everything, asked precise questions, and gave away nothing. Paloma recognized the type. Every village had one: the woman at the well who never gossiped, which meant she already knew.

"You're counting us," Vess observed, after a long stretch in which Paloma had only listened.

"I'm listening," Paloma corrected. "Counting comes after."

"Ahh." A sound like satisfaction. "Your grandmother counted first."

The fire crackled green. Ilya went very still, the way he did whenever anyone mentioned the word *grandmother*, which he had learned — in three days — was a door best left closed unless invited.

"My grandmother," Paloma said carefully, "is buried in the valley. Under an apple tree. She's been very quiet for eleven years."

"Yes," said Vess. "Quiet. That is one word for it."

"What does that mean?"

"It means, carver, that when your grandmother crossed these stones sixty years ago — younger than you are now, angrier than you will ever manage to be — she stood where you are standing, and she heard us, and she told us—" Vess paused, and Paloma had the sudden vertiginous sense of a ghost choosing her words with a jeweler's care, "—she told us she would *fix what we broke*, and then she walked back to your little valley, and she sealed it, and she never came back. And we have spent sixty years wondering whether she succeeded, or whether she simply decided we weren't worth the trouble."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the plain, a city-mirage flickered in the dark — towers of trapped light, briefly visible, briefly *sounding* — and fell silent again.

"I don't know anything about that," Paloma said.

"No," Vess agreed. "You don't. Which is why you're useful."

"Vess," Ilya warned quietly.

"I am being *honest*, messenger, which is more than your order ever managed." The old voice turned its attention fully onto Paloma, and it felt like being measured for a garment. "Here is what you must understand tonight, girl. Thirteen of us signed that covenant. Thirteen names, thirteen prices paid, one law holding two worlds apart by a thread thinner than your carving-wire. Eleven of us remain — sleeping, mostly, dreaming in the glass, waking only when the moon turns. And every one of those eleven has spent a century forming opinions about how the thread should be re-tied. Do you understand what that means?"

"That everyone wants a say."

"That everyone has had a *century* to want a say," said Oren gravely. "It compounds. Like interest."

Sefa snorted — an actual audible snort from a disembodied voice, which Paloma found herself absurdly comforted by. Dead people who could snort were, whatever else was true, still people.

"They will each come to you," Vess continued. "One by one. They will make their cases. Some will lie. Some will believe their own lies, which is worse. Two of them, I suspect, will try to frighten you. And you, carver, will need to decide whose words get carved into whatever new law you intend to write — because the messenger's book gives you the *when*, but only a smith gives the *what*."

"I'm not a smith," Paloma said. "I carve memorials. Names on bones. Little birds for children. I've never—"

"Never what?" Sefa's voice was suddenly close, right at her ear, gentle now. "Never mattered? Never been needed? Carver, we can smell it on you — the valley got into your bones, all that careful smallness, all that *staying out of the way*. Out here, staying out of the way means the world ends. So." A breath Paloma was fairly sure ghosts didn't need. "Be overwhelmed. Be frightened. We'll wait. But be needed while you're at it. It's non-negotiable."

Paloma stared into the green fire. Her eyes were stinging, which was ridiculous, because she was exhausted and cold and the smoke was genuinely irritating, that was all.

"For the record," she said finally, when she trusted her voice, "in the valley, nobody argues with me. They hand me a commission and they cry and they thank me and they leave. It takes ten minutes. I could do it in my sleep. Nobody has ever—" she gestured helplessly at the darkness full of waiting voices, "*needed* me to be right about anything."

"How lovely," said Oren.

"How *terrible*," said Sefa, at exactly the same moment.

They glared at each other in the manner of the immortally bickering, and Paloma laughed despite herself — a real laugh, surprised out of her, the kind she hadn't managed since before the drowned child's bone.

"There," said Vess, and there was something almost warm in it. Almost. "Now you sound like family."

The word landed like a dropped stone.

* * *

Later — much later, when Ilya slept with the book under his head like a pillow and his brow furrowed even in dreams — Paloma sat alone at the edge of the firelight and let the voices come to her one at a time, the way she'd done her whole life, the way that was the only thing she knew how to do.

Oren came first, formal and sad, wanting the borders restored exactly as they were, because "a law that bends is a law that breaks, and I have watched enough things break." Paloma took down his testimony on a scrap of bark, because taking things down was what her hands were for, and she saw him steady himself on the ritual of it — the relief of a man finally being *recorded*.

Sefa came second and wanted nothing formal at all. "Just save the cities," she said. "I grew up in one of them, the one in the eastern glass, before it sank. Save them first and argue about clauses later. Clauses don't scream." Paloma wrote that down too, and underlined it.

The others came in ones and twos. A farmer-ghost who wanted the rivers returned. A soldier who wanted forgiveness, specifically and by name, from a signatory who refused to wake. A voice that only wept, whom Paloma sat with in silence for a long time, because some testimonies aren't words.

And then, as the false dawn greyed the east, the last presence arrived — slow, deliberate, settling beside her like someone taking a seat they'd reserved.

"You count well," it said, "for a beginner."

The voice was old and dry and gravelly, like a stone rolled downhill, and Paloma's hands went still on her knife.

"You," she said. "The one who said *we don't frighten*."

"I did. It remains true." A pause. "You want my name, carver. All the others gave theirs freely. Mine I have kept, because mine is a complicated thing to say aloud in mixed company."

"Say it anyway."

Another pause. Out on the plain, the eastern glass caught the first light and began, very softly, to glow — the whole world lit from below like a lamp under skin.

"In life," the voice said at last, "I was called Marisol Reyes. In death, most here call me the Eleventh Chair. Your grandmother called me Tía Mari. And I have been waiting sixty years, niña, to ask her granddaughter a single question."

The fire had gone to embers. Paloma realized she was gripping her bone-knife hard enough to hurt.

"Ask it," she whispered.

"When your grandmother sealed that valley and left us to rot," said the ghost who bore her own name, "did she send you *instead* of herself? Or did she send you *in spite* of herself?"

Out beyond the firelight, the glass plain stretched away toward a broken horizon, glowing, patient, full of cities and arguments and the sleeping dead — and somewhere among them, Paloma understood with terrible clarity, were answers her grandmother had spent a lifetime keeping from her.

She stood up. Her legs shook. She stood up anyway.

"Take me to the other chairs," she said. "All of them. Tonight if we can."

Behind her, Ilya stirred, and reached instinctively for the book.
