## 10. The Order of the Last Page

Ilya had never been a good liar, which was why he'd become an excellent one.

It was a distinction the Order of the Last Page had taught him in his first week, back when he still flinched at the word *Order* and thought it sounded like something old men shouted across courtyards. Brother Cassian — who was not a brother in any religious sense but refused to answer to anything else, on the grounds that "Cassian alone sounds like a man about to be arrested" — had explained it over cold tea.

"You lie badly," Cassian said, "because you believe lying is a performance. It isn't. Lying is carpentry. You don't perform a table. You build it, joint by joint, until it holds weight. Then you put your cup on it and forget it's there."

Ilya had thought about that for eleven years now, mostly while lying.

He was thinking about it tonight, watching Paloma sleep with her back against a shelf of wind-carved glass, her satchel tucked under one arm like a child she didn't trust anyone else to hold. In that satchel, wrapped in oilcloth beside the prophecy book, were bones. She'd told him they were working stock — blank pieces, material for her craft. He hadn't argued. Carpenters carried lumber.

But he knew what those bones were. He'd helped carry them out of the Chapterhouse himself, though she didn't know it, and would never know it if he could arrange the world to his liking, which so far he had failed spectacularly to do.

The moon hung above the Steppe like a coin someone had bitten to test. Eleven turns left. Plenty of time to keep lying.

He almost believed it.

* * *

The Order had found him in a fever-ward in Vhalsk, which was fitting, because the Order found most of its messengers in places where death had already begun the paperwork.

He remembered the ward: forty beds under a ceiling of stained plaster, the smell of camphor and boiled linen, and Ilya nineteen years old, coughing into a rag and watching the rag come away spotted red, thinking, with the strange calm of the very ill, *so that's the shape of it, then.*

A woman sat down on the edge of his bed without asking. She was perhaps sixty, dressed like a traveling clerk, with ink stains up both forearms and a face like a closed ledger. She set a small brass instrument on his blanket — a lens on a hinge, the kind jewelers use — and looked through it at him for a long moment.

"Interesting," she said.

"I'm dying," Ilya offered. "In case the interesting is good news."

"The interesting is always good news. Good news is just interesting news that's finished." She folded the lens away. "My name is Magistra Orenne. You have a condition the physicians here call 'wasting' because they are poets of despair. We call it a thinning. The border between you and elsewhere has gone porous. Things leak through."

"What things?"

"Light, mostly. Occasionally weather." She glanced at the window, where snow was falling upward past the glass, unhurried, as though it had somewhere better to be. "You've noticed."

Ilya had noticed. He'd also noticed that no one else in the ward ever seemed to see it, and that when he mentioned it, the nurses smiled the way nurses smile — kindly, and while reaching for something sedating.

"We can't cure you," Orenne said. "We can give the remainder of your life a shape. A purpose. A very long walk." She produced a book from her coat — not the prophecy book, but its humble cousin, a ledger bound in grey leather — and opened it across his knees. "Or you can stay here and be interesting at these nurses until spring."

He should have asked more questions. He wants it noted, wherever such notes are kept, that he knows he should have asked more questions. But he was nineteen, and spotted-red-ragged, and the snow outside was falling upward, and someone had finally said *we see it too.*

"What's the walk?" he asked.

Orenne turned the ledger toward him. On the page was a map of the mortal realms with a valley marked near the center — a small green place ringed in stones — and beyond it, drawn in lines so faint they might have been breath on glass, a plain with no name.

"A delivery," she said.

* * *

They trained him for three years, which everyone agreed was two years longer than the last messenger got.

The Order of the Last Page occupied a former monastery in the hills above Vhalsk, and it consisted, at its height, of nine people, a goat named Doctrine, and a library of one book — the prophecy book, the *Codex of the Thirteenth Turn*, written a century before by a signatory's hand that no living member could identify. Nine people guarding one book. Ilya had once suggested, gently, that this was a poor ratio.

"It's an excellent ratio," Cassian snapped. "One book, nine people. Do you know how many people we'd need if we had two books? Eighteen. We can barely feed the goat."

Doctrine, at the sound of her name, ate a page of Cassian's notes. This happened often enough that the Order's entire institutional knowledge existed in duplicate, or in Doctrine.

They taught him the signs — the hand-marks by which members of the scattered orders recognized each other: thumb to sternum for *I carry*, two fingers crossed over the wrist for *the road is watched*, palm flat against the earth for *the covenant weakens*. They taught him the geography of the Glass Steppe as best anyone knew it, which was poorly, from accounts written by people who had mostly died shortly after writing them. They taught him the salt courts and their etiquette (*never refuse the first cup; always refuse the second; the third is a test and there is no correct answer, simply choose with confidence*).

And they taught him, in the last month, about the messenger before him.

His name had been Anselm. Ilya learned it the way you learn the name of a storm — all at once, from everyone, in the tone people use for things they've already grieved.

Anselm had left the Chapterhouse thirty-one years ago carrying the same book Ilya now carried. He had been older — fifty, a lifelong member, steady as a doorframe — and he had made it as far as the boundary stones of the valley before the Hollow Wind's hunters took him. Not killed him. That was the part Cassian made Ilya hear twice, standing in the archive room with the lamp turned low.

"They took him apart," Cassian said. "Slowly. Because the Order's protection lives in the whole body — every bone signed, every joint anointed. Break the body and you break the safe-conduct. They wanted the book, and they wanted the Order to know they had it, and they wanted time to do both." He paused. "The valley folk found what was left. Buried him outside the stones, because nothing dead may cross, and carved him a marker. Their bone-carvers do that. It's a kindness their whole culture is built around."

Ilya had felt sick, and young, and suddenly very aware of his own skeleton sitting inside him like a passenger.

"Why tell me this now?" he'd asked. "The night before I leave?"

"Because you'll want to know it eventually," Cassian said, "and eventually out there comes at inconvenient hours." He'd put a hand on Ilya's shoulder, briefly, the way he did everything — efficiently, and as if embarrassed by it. "Also because the Order voted to send you his bones."

* * *

That was the part Ilya had never said aloud, and it sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone, heavier each turn of the moon.

The Order had recovered Anselm's remains — ransomed them, actually, from a scavenger-clan in the Steppe's outer margins, at a cost that bankrupted them for a decade. And then Magistra Orenne, practical as winter, had made a decision that Ilya had argued against and lost.

"A messenger's bones carry safe-conduct," she'd said. "It's in the old law. The dead who served the covenant may be borne by the next messenger, and their passage opens doors. Anselm earned that right. We'll not waste it on a grave."

"He earned a grave," Ilya had said. "He earned to be *done*."

"And when you die on the Steppe," Orenne had replied, without cruelty, only with the terrible arithmetic she was famous for, "would you rather be done, or useful?"

So they had cleaned the bones, and signed them in the Order's ink, and packed them in oilcloth, and Ilya had carried Anselm across half the mortal realms on his back, and the bones had opened doors — literal ones, once, in a customs-house in Marrow Ford that had no business opening — and Ilya had talked to them sometimes, on the road, out of loneliness, apologizing.

*I'm sorry. I'll bring you home after. After the delivery, I swear it. You'll rest.*

And then he had reached the valley, and bled light through the boundary stones, and fallen at the feet of a bone-carver who heard the dead — and in the chaos of that first hour, bleeding, terrified, watched by a woman whose eyes missed nothing, he had let her take the satchel. Had let her assume. Had said nothing when she later remarked, kindly, that his "working stock" was fine-grained and well-kept.

She was carrying Anselm. She had been carrying Anselm for weeks. She slept with her arm around him.

And the worst of it — the part that made Ilya press the heel of his hand against his eyes now, in the dark, under the bitten coin of the moon — was that it wasn't only concealment. It was worse than a lie. It was that some cold, practical corner of him, the corner Orenne had trained, had looked at Paloma Reyes and thought: *a carver who hears the dead will treat these bones well. Better than I could. This is efficient.*

He had used her tenderness as a packing method.

* * *

Paloma stirred. He went still, the way you go still around sleeping animals and armed people, though she was neither, or was both.

"Ilya." Her voice was rough with sleep. "You're doing the thing where you think loudly."

"I don't think loudly."

"You think like a dropped trunk." She sat up, pushing hair from her face, and squinted at him in the moonglass light. "Third time tonight. What is it?"

He built the table. Joint by joint. *Nothing. Watching the horizon. Thinking about the turns.* He had the materials right there, cut and ready.

Instead he heard himself say: "Can I tell you something about the bones?"

Her whole body changed — not alarm exactly, but the readiness of someone who had grown up listening for the dead and had learned that the living were capable of worse surprises. "Which bones."

"In your satchel. The working stock." His mouth was dry. Carpentry, Ilya. Build it true. "They're not mine. They're not blank. They belonged to a man named Anselm, who was the messenger before me, and the Order sent him with me because his bones open doors, and I let you believe—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "I let you carry a dead man and never told you who he was. I'm sorry. I've been sorry for weeks. Sorry isn't the point. The point is you should know, and you should be angry, and I should stand here for it."

Silence. The Steppe ticked and settled around them, glass cooling in the dark like a hearth going out.

Paloma reached into the satchel — slowly, deliberately, the way she did everything with bone — and drew out the oilcloth bundle. She unwrapped it in her lap. Long bones, small bones, a skull with the Order's faded sigil inked at the temple. She looked at them for a long moment.

Then she pressed two fingers to the skull's brow, the way she touched everything she meant to listen to, and closed her eyes.

Ilya waited. Somewhere far off, a mirage-city chimed its false bells.

"He's quiet," she said at last. Her voice had gone soft and strange. "Not gone. Quiet. Waiting." She opened her eyes and looked at him, and there was anger in it, yes, banked and real — but underneath the anger, something that undid him more thoroughly than rage could have. "He's been listening to us this whole time, Ilya. Every campfire. Every argument. Every time you almost told me and didn't." She paused. "He says you apologize in your sleep."

"Oh no," said Ilya.

"Quite a lot, apparently." Something at the corner of her mouth twitched — not forgiveness yet, but the ground where it might grow. "You're going to introduce us properly. Now. His name, his story, everything the Order told you. And then—" she lifted the skull, gently, the way she'd lift a drowned child's memorial, he realized, the way she lifted everything, "—then I'm going to ask him what he wants. Because he's not cargo, and he's not a key, and he's certainly not yours to spend."

"No," Ilya said. "He isn't."

She began wrapping the bones back into the oilcloth, careful as prayer, and then stopped, head tilting east, toward the dark line of the horizon where the glass gave way to pale spires rising like teeth against the stars.

"Ilya," she said quietly. "There's someone coming. Or something standing. Out past the dunes — white, tall, a lot of them, in rows." Her fingers tightened on the bundle. "And the dead in my satchel just said one word."

He was already reaching for the book. "What word?"

"*Court.*"
