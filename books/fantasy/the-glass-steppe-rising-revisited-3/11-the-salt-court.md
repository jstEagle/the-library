## 11. The Salt Court

The pillars announced themselves an hour before we saw them.

Ilya felt it first — he'd been walking with his head cocked like a heron all morning, listening to something I couldn't hear yet. Then the dead began to change their tune.

In the valley, the dead murmured. That was the only word I'd ever had for it: a low, constant susurrus, like wind through wheat, like a house settling, like a hundred conversations happening just under the floorboards of the world. I'd lived my whole life inside that sound and called it silence.

Out here on the Steppe, the dead didn't murmur. They *debated*.

And as we walked east toward the broken horizon, the debate began to organize itself into factions, and the factions into choruses, and one chorus in particular grew louder and sweeter than all the rest — a sound like bells being rung underwater, like a choir singing through a mouthful of honey.

"They know we're coming," Ilya said.

"Who's *they*?"

He didn't answer, which was answer enough. Three days on the glass and I had learned that Ilya's silences were not empty; they were full to bursting, like jars sealed too soon.

The salt pillars rose out of the plain like the ribs of some god who had lain down here to die and been too proud to finish the job. They were white — not bone-white, which I knew intimately, but salt-white, a color that seemed to give off light rather than receive it. There were hundreds of them, maybe thousands, each one taller than the chapel steeple back home, each one carved — and this stopped me mid-stride — *carved*, in the way that water carves, in the way that patience carves, spirals and flutes and faces worn so deep they might have been growing there since the world was young.

"Vess's court," Ilya said quietly. "If she still holds it."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then we're standing in someone else's parlor."

We stood in someone's parlor for a long moment while I decided whether that frightened me more or less than everything else that had happened since I crossed the boundary stones. I decided it was roughly equal, and kept walking.

* * *

The path between the pillars was swept clean. That detail unsettled me more than anything else — more than the faces in the salt, more than the way my reflection multiplied in them until I was a crowd of myself. Someone *swept*. Somewhere in this forest of mineral spires there were hands, or the memory of hands, doing housework.

At the center of the court, where the pillars ringed an open circle of packed white earth, a table waited.

Not a metaphorical table. An actual table, long and low, set with cups and a steaming pot, and behind it sat a woman who could not have been more than twenty when she died, because that is what she was — dead, unmistakably, gloriously dead, though death had clearly not touched her vanity. She wore robes the color of storm-light, layered like the petals of some impossible flower, and her hair was pinned up with needles of black glass. Her skin had the faint translucence of sea ice. When she smiled at us — and she smiled at us immediately, warmly, as if we were expected, because we were — her teeth gleamed like pearls in a shell.

"The carver," she said, "and the messenger. Sit. You've been walking on my nerves for two days."

"You can see us?" I asked. "From here?"

"I can see everything that touches my salt." She gestured to the seats across from her. "Sit. The tea is honest. It's the last honest thing you'll encounter today, so drink deeply."

I looked at Ilya. Ilya looked at the tea. The tea steamed, innocently, smelling of something between chamomile and rain.

"It's poisoned," I said.

Vess laughed — a real laugh, delighted, ringing off the pillars until the faces in them seemed to laugh too. "Oh, I *like* her. Yes, child, of course it's poisoned. Everything offered by the dead is poisoned; that's what offering *is*, out here. Poison is simply hospitality with consequences." She poured three cups without asking. "The question is never whether the cup is poisoned. The question is whether the poison is meant for you."

"And if it isn't?" Ilya asked.

"Then it's a compliment." Vess pushed a cup toward each of us. "Drink, messenger. You look like something the Hollow Wind chewed and spat out."

"We haven't met the Hollow Wind yet," Ilya said carefully.

"No," Vess said, and her smile did something complicated at the edges. "You haven't. Drink."

I sat. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the Steppe keeps of foolish decisions: Paloma Reyes, bone-carver of the valley, sat down at a ghost-queen's table and drank the tea.

It tasted like winter ending.

* * *

Here is what I learned over the next hour, sitting in the salt court while shadows wheeled overhead in patterns no living sky would make:

Vess had been a signatory. One of the original thirteen who bound the Glass Steppe to the mortal realms a thousand years ago, before the covenant was rewritten, before the century-old sabotage my grandmother committed, before any of it. She spoke of those days the way old soldiers speak of wars — with nostalgia and fury in the same breath, often in the same sentence.

"We were thirteen," she said. "Thirteen voices, thirteen deaths freely given, one law. And the law held. Do you know how rare that is, carver? A law that *held*? Kings would trade their crowns' weight in blood for a law that held for a hundred years, and ours held for nine hundred."

"And then?" Ilya asked.

"And then your grandmother happened." Vess turned her translucent gaze on me, and for the first time the warmth in it went cold at the bottom, like a river freezing from underneath. "Tell me, carver. Did the honored dead of your little valley ever mention her work? No? They went silent when you asked about the covenant? A hundred years of silence?" She sipped her own tea — hers, notably, was a different color from ours. "That silence has a name. It's called *shame*."

"She wasn't ashamed of anything," I said, and heard my own voice come out harder than I expected. "She broke the rite because it demanded a living smith be bound into the glass every century. A *child*, the first time. She refused. She bought your covenant a hundred extra years with her own hands and everyone here treats her like a criminal."

The court went quiet. Even the debate of the distant dead hushed, the way a tavern goes quiet when a fight starts.

Vess set down her cup. Slowly. Precisely. The click of porcelain on porcelain sounded, in that silence, like a lock turning.

"A child," she repeated softly. "Yes. The first anchor was a child. My daughter."

I felt the ground tilt under me, though the ground was not moving.

"Her name was Sefa," Vess continued, in a voice that had gone smooth as glass over something jagged. "Eleven years old. She volunteered — do you understand? Nobody forced her. She was *brave*, and she loved this place, and she lay down in the chasm and let us bind her, and the covenant held for a hundred years exactly as designed. So do not stand in my court and tell me my daughter's sacrifice was a crime. Your grandmother stole the replacement. Every year past the twelfth turn, she stole from Sefa's grave, and now the whole Steppe is coming apart at the seams because one woman a thousand years your senior decided her conscience mattered more than—"

"Than a child's life?" I said.

"Yes," Vess snapped.

"Yes," I said, "exactly. Than a child's life."

For a moment neither of us breathed. Ilya had gone very still beside me, the stillness of a man watching weather turn.

And then Vess — Vess *laughed* again, though this laugh had knives in it, or grief; with the dead it was often hard to tell the difference. "Oh, you are *so* much like her," she said. "The letters don't lie. The stubbornness. The jaw." She reached across the table, quick as a striking snake, and caught my chin in fingers cool as well-water. "Do you know why I let you walk into my court, carver? Why my salt didn't swallow you at the edge like it swallows the hunters?"

"Because you need me," I said through her grip.

"Because I need you," she agreed, and released me, and the warmth returned to her face like sun after cloud, seamless, total, terrifying. "The covenant must be renewed. On that we agree. But there are terms, carver, and terms are written by whoever holds the pen. Your grandmother held the pen once. Now it's your turn — and I should very much like to advise the hand that holds it."

"You mean control it."

"I mean *advise*." She refilled our cups. "I am a generous ally, Paloma Reyes. Shelter, provisions, safe passage through territories that would crack your bones like kindling. In exchange, when you rewrite the covenant, certain clauses will favor my faction. The eastern reaches. The drowned cities. My people, who have waited nine hundred years for recognition." Her eyes glittered. "Signatories get forgotten. Queens do not."

"And if I refuse your advice?"

Vess spread her hands, gracious, wounded, magnificent. "Then you leave my protection, and the Steppe does what the Steppe does." Behind her, far off between the pillars, something made a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. "But that would be foolish. You need allies. Everyone needs allies. Ask the messenger — his order needed allies too, once. Where are they now, Ilya? All those learned men and women with their prophecy books?" She clicked her tongue. "Dust. Dust and one boy sent out to die alone."

Ilya's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"He hasn't told you that part either, has he," Vess said sweetly, watching him. "What the book says happens to him. Oh — forgive me. Was that a secret?" She pressed her translucent hand to her heart in mock contrition. "How careless of me. Well. Secrets are such heavy things to carry across the Steppe. Consider this my first gift: I'm helping you put some of them down."

* * *

They gave us a chamber for the night — a hollow in one of the great pillars, floored with salt crystals that glowed faintly amber, warm as a hearth. Ilya took the corner nearest the entrance and sat with his knees drawn up, turning his cup of honest tea in his hands, saying nothing, which from Ilya was practically a shout.

"So," I said, settling across from him. "The book says what happens to you."

"Paloma—"

"Don't. Don't do the thing where you protect me from information like I'm made of sugar. I crossed the boundary stones knowing what I was giving up. I sat through dinner with a dead queen who wants to use me as a pen. I can handle a sentence out of a book." I held out my hand. "Show me."

He didn't move. In the amber light his face looked young and exhausted and scared, and I realized — really realized, in the way that lands in the chest like a dropped stone — that he had been carrying this since the boundary. Since before the boundary. Carrying it alone, politely, with his terrible gentle manners, all this way.

"It's one line," he said finally. "Page ninety-one. 'The messenger falls at the rite's completion, his purpose spent.'" His mouth twisted. "*Purpose spent.* Like I'm a candle."

"That's the prophecy's reading," I said slowly. "Prophecies are written by people, Ilya. People who hold pens. Vess told us that herself, tonight — she just thought she was teaching me a lesson about her faction." I leaned forward. "My grandmother held the pen once too. She rewrote the ending of a thousand-year covenant with a chisel and sheer stubbornness. If she could break a rite, I can unbreak a line."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the salt court sang its underwater-bells song, and somewhere in it, I was almost sure, a voice claimed my grandmother's name among its own.

"You believe that," he said wonderingly. "You actually believe that."

"I'm a carver," I said. "Nothing I make comes out the way it was planned. Why should prophecies be different?" I pulled my kit toward me and took out the half-finished sigil, my grandmother's work, and set it between us in the amber light. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we negotiate with a queen, and I intend to be insufferably well-rested when I tell her no."

Somewhere above us, soft as snowfall, Vess's laughter drifted down through the pillar — and beneath it, quieter, another voice, one I hadn't heard before, whispering a single word that smelled of salt and old grief:

*Sefa.*

I did not sleep quickly. But I slept.
