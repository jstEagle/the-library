## 12. Terms and Thorns

The Salt Court smelled the way old promises smell — like rain that fell somewhere else, a hundred years ago.

Ilya had been in courts before. The dying order kept him three years in their house at Vell's Crossing, and even there, among scholars who measured their tea leaves before brewing them, he had learned that architecture was always an argument. A tall hall said *we are permanent*. A narrow door said *you are not welcome*. And a court built entirely of salt pillars, each one weeping slow white tears into the glass below it, said *we have been crying for so long that our grief has become load-bearing*.

He kept this observation to himself. Paloma was already overwhelmed; he could see it in the way she held her elbows, as if her own arms were strangers who might bolt. She had spent one day on the Steppe and she flinched at everything now — the light, the silence between gusts of wind, above all the dead, whose voices here did not whisper but *debated*. He'd watched her press her palms over her ears twice that morning, uselessly, because the dead did not travel through ears.

So when the pillars of Vess's court parted to admit them, Ilya did what he had learned to do in sickrooms and scriptoriums alike: he made himself pleasant, unremarkable, and observant.

"Guests," said Vess.

She stood at the center of her court beneath an arch of fused salt, and Ilya revised his first impression of her. In the mirage where they'd first seen her she had seemed vast, a queen rendered in fog and glare. Here she was smaller — or rather, more precisely sized, the way a knife is precisely sized. Her gown moved like water remembering it used to be snow. Her face was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful: you could see everything through it, including the dark.

"You honor us," Ilya said, and bowed exactly deep enough to be polite without being submissive. He had practiced that bow for a week before leaving Vell's Crossing. It was, he reflected, possibly the most useful thing the order had taught him.

"Honor." Vess tasted the word. "A living man and a bone-carver walk into my court uninvited, carrying a book older than my death, and call it honor. Sit."

There were no chairs. There were, instead, low shelves of salt, worn smooth in the shapes of people who had once sat there and been, presumably, slowly seasoned. Ilya sat. Paloma sat beside him with the rigid grace of someone sitting on ice.

Vess circled them once. He felt her attention like weather.

"The messenger," she said. "And the carver who hears." Her eyes settled on Paloma, and something moved behind the lake-ice of her face — recognition, or its counterfeit. "You have her hands. Did you know that? The last smith who stood in this court had those exact hands. Wide palm, short fingers, thumbs like little stubborn animals."

Paloma went very still. "You knew my grandmother?"

"I knew *a* grandmother." Vess smiled, and it was not a kind smile but it was not entirely unkind either, which Ilya was learning was the local dialect of courtesy. "The Steppe remembers hands. Hands made us. Hands broke us. But come — you didn't cross my glass to discuss ancestry. You came about the covenant. You want it reforged before the thirteenth turn completes. You want to save your valley, your worlds, your pretty mortal arrangements."

"That is broadly the shape of it," Ilya admitted.

"And you want my help."

"We hoped," Paloma said carefully, "for shelter first. Terms after."

"*Terms.*" Vess laughed — a sound like a chandelier deciding against itself. "Oh, carver. Everyone wants terms. That is all the covenant ever was. Terms. Thirteen signatories, thirteen sets of terms, argued over forty years and sealed in a single night of blood and glass. You think magic has laws? Magic has *contracts*, and contracts have prices, and everyone who signed believed someone else would pay." She drifted closer, and the salt around her wept faster. "Shelter you shall have. Food you shall have — such as food is here. And tomorrow, when you've slept, we will discuss terms."

* * *

The guest-chamber was a hollowed pillar, dry and white and lit by nothing Ilya could name. Beds of woven salt-grass had been left for them, which raised uncomfortable questions about how recently other guests had needed beds.

Paloma did not sleep. He heard her moving in the dark, and then heard her voice, low and urgent, and realized she was talking to the honored dead of her valley — the ones whose echoes she carried like coins in a pocket. He caught fragments.

*"...you could have told me. Any of you. A hundred years of murmuring at the boundary stones and not one of you said 'oh, by the way'..."*

Ilya stared at the ceiling and let her grieve. Some conversations required privacy from the living even when they included the dead.

But near midnight — if midnight meant anything on a plain without clocks — her voice came again, closer, and he opened his eyes to find her sitting on the edge of his bed with her knees drawn up, silhouetted against the pale glow of the walls.

"I can't stop hearing them," she whispered. "The signatories. They're under the whole court. Under the whole *Steppe*. They argue in their sleep, Ilya. Like dogs dreaming. And one of them keeps saying numbers. Prices. Over and over." She hugged her knees tighter. "What price? What are they counting?"

He should have told her then. He had read the book cover to cover four times on the road, and though the final page was damaged — torn, half-legible, maddening — he had pieced together enough of the marginalia to know what the signatories counted. He opened his mouth to say something honest.

What came out was: "Get some sleep. Negotiations go better rested."

She gave him a long look — the look of a woman who carved bones for a living and therefore knew exactly what things looked like just before they broke — and lay down on her own bed without another word.

He lay awake and hated himself with the efficient, familiar ease of long practice.

* * *

Morning in the Salt Court arrived as a brightening of the walls. Vess received them in the open center of her court, seated now upon a throne that was clearly also a salt pillar, still growing, still weeping, so that her rule appeared to rise out of her own accumulated sorrow. Two attendants stood at her shoulders — ghosts, Ilya guessed, or something adjacent to ghosts, translucent as breath on cold glass. They served tea in cups of salt that stung slightly to hold, which Ilya suspected was intentional.

"Now," Vess said. "Terms. Let us speak plainly, because the thirteenth turn does not wait for diplomacy. You wish the covenant renewed. Renewal requires a rite. The rite requires the full wording — which, I note, your precious book no longer possesses." Her eyes flicked to the prophecy volume in Ilya's satchel, and he tightened his grip on it involuntarily. "Yes, we know about the torn page. The Hollow Wind tore it, a century ago. Or your grandmother did. Accounts differ. Ghosts gossip terribly."

Paloma leaned forward. "Then how do we renew anything?"

"Because *I* remember the wording." Vess lifted her chin. "I was there. I am one of the thirteen. My signature is in the glass under your feet, carver, along with twelve others. The order sent your messenger with a book, but books burn, drown, tear. Flesh remembers longer. So: I will give you the rite. Every word. Every gesture. Complete, correct, guaranteed."

Ilya's heart performed a small hopeful somersault, which he immediately distrusted. "In exchange for?"

"There it is." Vess looked almost fond. "The scholar asks the right question. Very well — plainly, as promised. My faction holds four signatures. Four voices out of thirteen. When the covenant is remade, I want terms favorable to the dead of the salt flats: passage rights, jurisdiction, recognition. We have been *forgotten*, messenger. A century of being a footnote while the living world forgot it owed us anything. I want my people written back in."

"That seems reasonable," Paloma began —

"It is reasonable," Vess agreed. "That is what should worry you. The unreasonable demands announce themselves. The reasonable ones get signed." She set down her cup, and the salt of it sighed. "But there is a condition that is not mine to waive. It belongs to the covenant itself. The original terms. And since you must know them to renew them honestly, I will tell you what your book's torn page once said."

The court went quiet. Even the weeping pillars seemed to listen.

"The covenant binds two worlds with one anchor," Vess said. "Not a symbol. Not a stone. A *person*. Once each century, a living smith — a maker, blood of the founding line — is bound into the glass. Alive. Their craft, their years, their heartbeat, given to the Steppe to keep both worlds stitched shut. Not killed. Worse than killed. *Kept.*" Her voice softened into something almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing she had done yet. "There are smiths in that glass, carver, who have been aware for nine hundred years."

Paloma did not move. Ilya watched her not move with a completeness that frightened him — no breath, no blink, her hands flat on her knees like objects set down carefully.

"The founding families knew," Vess continued, merciless as tide. "Each generation raised a child to the craft knowing the child might be chosen. Lots were drawn. Sometimes volunteers stepped forward — the Steppe honors willingness, discounts it from the price, though never to nothing. And one family, three generations into the burden, drew the lot for a girl of nineteen—" the lake-ice face turned fully toward Paloma, "—and fled. Crossed into a valley where magic could not follow, where the covenant could not reach, and hid behind boundary stones for a hundred years. They took the knowledge with them and called it safety. They took the *duty* with them too, whether they liked it or not. Blood doesn't file paperwork."

"Her name," Paloma said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "The girl. Nineteen. Whose lot was drawn."

"Marisol," Vess said. "Marisol Reyes."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Ilya to count his own heartbeats and arrive at an unwelcome total.

"My grandmother's name was Marisol," Paloma said finally.

"Yes."

"And the sigil in her chest. Half-carved. The diagrams matching the book."

"Yes."

"She wasn't running from magic." Each word came out separately, like stones placed on a grave. "She was running from a *draft notice*. From being fed to the ground. And she took her daughters and her daughter's daughter's daughter and raised us in a hole where nothing magical could ever reach us, and she let the whole covenant rot for a century, and she never—" Paloma stopped. Her jaw worked. "She let me think the dead were just neighbors. She let me think the silence at the stones was peace."

"What else would you have had her tell you?" Vess asked, and for one moment the mockery dropped away and something ancient and tired looked out. "That her grandmother's grandmother had been bound screaming into glass? That the family trade ended in a pit? Children run from less."

"She should have told me *anyway*," Paloma said, standing. "You don't get to decide a person's inheritance for her. That's — that's the whole point. That's what the covenant got wrong in the first place!" She was shaking, Ilya saw, but her voice held, and he thought: *there she is. The woman the book meant by 'the carver who hears.' Not the hearing. The holding.*

Vess regarded her for a long moment. Then, softly: "Careful, carver. Righteousness is expensive here."

"Everything is expensive here," Paloma said. "That appears to be the entire architecture."

Ilya stood too, because standing seemed suddenly important, and because he wanted to be between Paloma and the throne and could not say why without revealing more than he wished to. "We'll need time to consider the terms," he said smoothly. "All of them. Including the original price."

"Take until moonrise." Vess rose; the pillar beneath her grew a visible inch, weeping. "And messenger — a kindness, since I am feeling generous." Her eyes glittered. "Ask your book why the order chose a dying man to carry it. Ask what happened to the last one. The Steppe remembers hands, I said. It also remembers *bones*. Some of them are in your companion's bag already."

They walked out through the parting pillars. Paloma said nothing the entire length of the court. It was only at the threshold, in the raw wind off the glass dunes, that she stopped and turned to him, and her face was wet, and her voice was steady, and both of those facts together terrified him more than anything Vess had said.

"One century," she said. "One child every hundred years. My family ran, and bought themselves a valley, and the bill just... waited. It waited for *me*." She looked at her own hands — wide-palmed, short-fingered, stubborn-thumbed. "It was always going to be me, wasn't it? That's why the book names me. Why the dead went silent. Why my grandmother left a half-finished sigil instead of a finished one. She couldn't do it. She hoped I'd never have to choose."

Ilya opened his mouth to deny it, to comfort, to lie gently the way he had been lying gently since midnight.

Before he could, the horizon flickered. Far across the glass, a city-mirage shuddered — and for three full seconds became real: towers, windows, and from within them a sound that neither of them would ever successfully describe afterward, a sound like a thousand voices remembering they were drowning.

Then it was gone. The Steppe settled. Somewhere beneath their feet, patient as arithmetic, the signatories resumed counting.

"First turn marker," Ilya said quietly. "Twelve remain."

Paloma wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, decisively, the way she probably wiped dust from bone. "Then we don't have time for me to fall apart," she said. "Show me the book's margins, Ilya. Everything you haven't told me. *Everything.* Tonight."

And Ilya, whose talent had always been for surviving other people's stories, understood that his own had just begun to demand terms of him.
