## 13. Night of the First Turn

The Glass Steppe did not have a night in any way Paloma had been taught to expect. The sky above it darkened, yes — but the dark came up from below, rising out of the fused glass like heat off a summer road, so that by full dark they were walking on a faint amber glow, as though the whole plain were a hearthstone someone had forgotten to bank.

"It's warmer after sundown," Ilya said, for the third time that day. He seemed to find this delightful. "In the valley, everything cools at night. Here the glass gives back what it took during the day."

"Everything here is backwards," Paloma said, shifting her pack. "I've noticed."

She had been on the Steppe for six days now, and she was keeping count not because she wanted to but because her hands kept doing it without her permission. Six days of walking across a plain that showed them things that weren't there. Six days of Ilya teaching her hand-signs from his dying order — *water ahead*, *danger behind*, *I am sorry* — and six days of the dead beneath the glass growing louder with every mile, until she had taken to humming while she walked, an old valley tuneless tune, just to give her ears something else to lean against.

Tonight they made camp in the lee of a ridge of glass that had buckled upward like a frozen wave. Ilya built no fire — "nothing burns here, or rather, everything does, briefly and unhelpfully" — and instead arranged three smooth shards around their bedrolls, which he claimed held warmth. They did hold warmth. Paloma was trying very hard not to think about where that warmth came from.

"You're counting," Ilya said.

"I'm not."

"You're moving your lips in groups of seven. You do it when you're counting bones. You counted the vertebrae in that ribcage mirage yesterday and then told me it was wrong."

"It *was* wrong. Nobody builds a skeleton with fourteen thoracics. Even a mirage should have some self-respect." She wrapped her arms around her knees. Above them, the moon hung fat and white and patient. "It's tonight, isn't it."

Ilya was quiet for a moment. When he answered, all the lightness had gone out of his voice, the way water goes out of a sponge when you squeeze it. "The first turn marker passes at moonset. According to the book, the Steppe will... test itself. See if the covenant still holds. Like a man poking a bruise to see how deep it goes."

"And when it finds out how deep?"

"Then we'll know," he said, which was not an answer, and they both knew it was not an answer, and he said it anyway because sometimes people need to say something with their mouths while their stomachs are saying something else entirely.

Paloma looked at the moon. In the valley, she had never thought much about it. It came up, it went down, it pulled the tides in the river and the moods in her aunt. Here it hung like a coin about to be spent.

"The dead are restless," she said. "Beneath us. All day they've been..." She searched for the word. Whispering was wrong. The valley dead whispered. These dead *argued*, and today the argument had stopped being words and become pressure, a headache with opinions. "They've been holding their breath. All of them at once. Do you know what that sounds like? A hundred thousand ghosts, all inhaling together and refusing to let go?"

"No," Ilya said honestly. "What does it sound like?"

"Like my grandmother before she told me my father wasn't coming home."

He reached over and put his hand over hers on her knee. His fingers were long and ink-stained — he'd been copying passages from the prophecy book onto his forearm, having lost his inkwell crossing the stones, and he looked increasingly like a man who had been tattooed by accident. "We could walk through the night. Put distance between us and—"

"Between us and what? There's nowhere to be *not*. It's the whole Steppe, Ilya. You can't stand outside a room to avoid what's happening in it."

"You can leave the room," he offered.

"Yes, and look where leaving got me." She hadn't meant it to come out sharp. It came out sharp anyway. He took his hand back, and she immediately regretted the shape of the silence that followed. "I'm sorry. That's not — you didn't drag me. I crossed those stones on my own legs."

"I know."

"But I keep thinking about the boundary stones getting smaller behind me, and how the valley is sitting there inside its little fence of quiet, trusting me, and I lied to it, basically, by walking away, and—" She stopped herself, pressed her palms flat against the warm glass. "My mother used to say I apologize like a hinge. Squeak, swing, squeak. Sorry, change of direction, sorry."

Ilya smiled at that, a small real smile. "For what it's worth, I don't think the valley feels betrayed. I think valleys can't feel anything. That's why we have to feel things *for* them." He tipped his head back against the glass ridge. "That sounded wiser than I intended. Don't quote me."

"I'm going to embroider it on something."

"Don't you dare—"

The dead let go of their breath.

There was no other way Paloma could describe it. One moment: the held silence, vast and terrible, pressing up through the glass into her teeth. The next moment: release, everywhere, all at once — and the Steppe *shuddered*.

Not shook. Shuddered, the way a horse shudders, the way a sleeping dog shudders chasing something in a dream. The amber glow under them flickered like a lamp in wind. Somewhere far off — or near, distance meant nothing here — glass sang, a low ringing note that traveled up through the ground and into the marrow of Paloma's shins and set every healed fracture she owned humming in sympathy. Her left wrist, broken at nine years old falling from the pear tree; two ribs, cracked by a runaway cart; the small bones of her right foot, crushed under her grandmother's chest when it fell. All of them woke up and remembered being broken.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's — Ilya—"

He was already up, already pulling her to her feet, and his face in the flickering light was the face of a man watching his own dread arrive on schedule. "The marker. It's passing. Get your pack, get—"

The horizon broke open.

Paloma would spend years afterward trying to describe what happened next, and she would fail every time, but she would always begin the same way: *there was a city.*

It rose out of the glass plain perhaps half a mile distant — no, it didn't rise, it *surfaced*, the way a drowned thing surfaces, pushed up by gases of its own decay. It was one of the mirages, one of the trapped cities they had seen shimmering inside the plain all week, except that this time the glass between it and the air thinned, stretched, went clear as window-glass — and then the city was simply *there*, standing on the Steppe as if it had always stood there, towers and bridges and a great domed hall with thirteen spires, and it was screaming.

Every soul in it. At once.

The sound hit Paloma like a wall. She had heard the dead her whole life — murmurs, mutterings, the occasional cross old woman complaining about her headstone's spelling. This was nothing like that. This was a city's worth of voices discovering, simultaneously, that they existed again, and that existing again *hurt*. Windows lit up street by street. Figures appeared on balconies, leaning out, mouths open. And beneath the screaming, beneath even her ability to parse it, Paloma caught fragments — names, pleas, accusations — and one voice, closer than the others, crying out in a language she somehow understood anyway:

*"The smith! Where is the smith? We were promised a smith!"*

"They can see us," Ilya shouted over the din. He had his hands clamped over his ears, which was useless and human and made her love him a little, in the way you love people for being exactly as frightened as you are. "Paloma, they can *see* us—"

A tower leaned. Slowly, terribly, like a drunk deciding to sit down. Its shadow swept toward them across the glowing plain, and where the shadow touched the glass, the glass went black and brittle and began, quietly, to flake away into nothing.

"We have to run," Paloma said. It seemed important to say it out loud, to make it a fact in the world rather than a scream inside her.

They ran.

The Steppe fought them. Not maliciously — she was almost sure it wasn't malicious — but the shudder-quake rolled on beneath their feet in waves, and the amber glow guttered, and the city screamed louder the closer they came to its edge of clarity, as though their running were a rope dragging it further up out of the glass. Behind them, the domed hall split along its thirteen spires with a sound like a bell breaking. Ahead, the flat plain heaved up a ridge of glass directly in their path, smooth and sheer as the side of a dropped plate.

"Left!" Ilya gasped. "There's a seam—"

There was a seam, a crack in the world's biggest pane of glass, wide enough for a person and a person's regrets and not much else. Paloma went in sideways, pack scraping, and Ilya came in after her, and they wedged themselves into the crack's narrow throat while the city behind them reached the peak of its screaming and —

stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, all at once, mid-cry, the way a door stops a conversation. The light went out of the windows. The figures on the balconies sagged and blurred and sank back down into the plain, and the city dimmed from *real* to *mirage* to *memory* in the space of ten heartbeats, until it was only a shimmer underfoot again, a suggestion, a rumor.

The shudder passed. The glass under them cooled from hearth-warm to merely strange. Far overhead, indifferent and enormous, the moon continued its slow slide toward the horizon.

Paloma realized she was gripping Ilya's sleeve hard enough to tear it, and let go, and discovered her hands were shaking, and put them under her arms like a child, and hated herself for it, and did it anyway.

"It went back under," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "They were *right there*. I heard them. Ilya, I heard them, they were asking for — they said *smith*. They said they were promised a smith." She turned to look at him in the blue-black dark of the seam. "Your book. Does your book say what happens on the last turn? Thirteenth turn. Do they stay up?"

He didn't answer fast enough.

"Ilya."

"If the covenant lapses," he said carefully, "everything bound in the Steppe comes up. Permanently. Every city. Every soul. All of it, loose in the mortal realms at once, with no law to hold any of its edges." He swallowed. "The order called it the Unbinding. The old texts call it the Flood. My teacher called it Tuesday, eventually, because he drank."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed softly. "It isn't. But I've found that if I don't make it slightly funny, I stop being able to talk about it at all, and then I'm just a man standing in a crack in the world not talking."

Paloma laughed — a short, broken, involuntary sound, half sob, entirely necessary. Then she pressed her forehead against the cool glass beside her and breathed, and let the shaking move through her and out the way her grandmother had taught her to let pain move through carved bone: don't fight it, don't feed it, just give it a channel.

"They knew me," she said into the dark. "Or they knew what I am. 'Promised a smith.' My family, Ilya. Whatever my grandmother ran from, whatever she broke — they've been waiting a hundred years down there in the dark, and tonight they got close enough to ask for her, and she still wasn't there, and I was."

"She sent you instead," Ilya said. "Maybe that counts for something."

"Maybe it counts for nothing. Maybe it counts worse than nothing." She lifted her head. Through the seam's narrow mouth she could see the moon, nearly touching the horizon now, huge and white and halfway done with whatever terrible arithmetic it was performing. "One turn down. Twelve to go. And each one is going to be worse, isn't it."

"The book says the turns accelerate."

"Of course it does. Books always say the worst possible thing. It's how you know they're honest."

They stayed in the crack until true dark settled and the Steppe's amber glow returned, steadier now, like a fire that has burned itself back down to coals. When they finally crawled out and gathered their bedrolls, neither of them suggested sleep. Sleep felt obscene, some-how, like laughing at a funeral — and yet Paloma's body insisted, the way bodies do, dragging her down within the hour despite everything, into dreams full of thirteen spires and a voice calling *smith, smith* in her grandmother's cadence.

She woke once, near dawn, to a sound she couldn't place. Ilya sat propped against the glass ridge, the prophecy book open on his knees, tracing lines of text with one ink-stained finger. His lips moved silently. And on the page, in the grey pre-dawn light, Paloma saw — or thought she saw — the ink shifting. Rearranging itself. Adding a line that had not been there when the sun went down.

*Ilya,* the new line seemed to read, in letters fine as hairline cracks, *and the carver, at the ford of mirrors—*

"Ilya," she whispered.

He looked up. Followed her gaze down to the page. Went very still.

"Has it ever done that before?" she asked.

"No," he said. "The book has never once changed since it was written. That was the whole point of it. That was the whole *horror* of it." He closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a room where something is sleeping. "Until tonight."

Far to the west, past the buried cities and the singing dunes, something answered the closing cover with a single soft ring of glass — and Paloma, who heard the dead, heard beneath it a new voice joining the argument below, thin and cold and amused, asking the Steppe a question she could not make out, but which made every ghost who heard it fall suddenly, completely silent.

* * *

They walked at first light, and neither of them spoke about the book, and both of them thought about nothing else.
