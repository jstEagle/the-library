## 14. The Bone That Sings

Paloma woke with a splinter of glass in her palm and no memory of how it got there.

She lay in the lee of a salt ridge, wrapped in Ilya's second-best cloak — he had three, which struck her as two more than any honest messenger needed — and stared at the wound without much alarm. A thin line of red, already clotting. In the valley, a cut like this would have been an annoyance. Here, on the Glass Steppe, where the ground itself was the fused memory of everything that had ever burned, she found herself wondering if the land had done it on purpose.

*Don't be foolish,* she told herself. *Land doesn't have intentions.*

The dead had corrected her on this point twice before breakfast yesterday.

She sat up carefully. Ilya was still asleep a few paces off, flat on his back, one arm flung over his eyes like a man shielding himself from a sun that wasn't there yet. He talked in his sleep. Mostly numbers. Sometimes apologies. Once, memorably, a very firm *no, that's the wrong chalice*, which Paloma had decided not to ask about, because some mysteries were load-bearing.

Above them, the moon hung fat and pale, four turns along its strange thirteenth orbit. Nine to go. She did the arithmetic every morning now, the way other people said their prayers.

Her knife roll lay beside her, and among the tools sat the piece she'd been working: a length of femur from the last messenger's remains, cleaned and smoothed, half-carved with the first tentative lines of — well, she still didn't know what. That was the trouble. Every craft she'd ever learned had begun with knowing what the thing was *for*. A memorial bone for a drowned child was for the mother's grief. A name-plate for a grave was for the living to find their way back to the dead. But this bone, this messenger's bone, was for nothing she could name yet. She'd been carving it the way you worry at a loose tooth: compulsively, half-hoping it would come free and tell you what it wanted.

She picked it up.

And the Steppe shivered.

Not the wind-shiver she'd learned to expect, the dry rattle of salt grains skittering across glass. This was deeper. A hum, low and resonant, like a wet finger circling the rim of a bowl. It came up through her knees, through her seat-bones, through the marrow of her own skeleton, and it was coming *from the bone in her hand*.

Paloma went very still, the way she did when a ghost first spoke — because moving too fast, she'd learned, was how you lost them.

The hum swelled. Beneath it, faint but unmistakable, something that was almost a voice. Not words. Not yet. More like the feeling of words, the way you can hear someone talking through a wall and know whether they're angry or laughing without catching a single syllable.

"Hello," she whispered, feeling immediately ridiculous.

The Steppe hummed back.

*Ilya,* she thought, and then, immediately, *no.* Not yet. This was hers. Whatever was happening under her hands, in this grey hour before dawn, belonged to her alone, and she had been alone so long that sharing the first moment of it felt like tearing a page out of a book mid-sentence.

She turned the bone over. The lines she'd carved — simple guide-marks, the kind she'd made ten thousand times — were glowing. Faintly. A pale gold light seeping up out of the grooves like warmth out of fresh bread.

"Oh," she said.

Then, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter and her grandmother had raised her to test things twice: "Oh?"

She pressed her thumb against one of the glowing lines. The hum changed pitch — rose, brightened, became for one impossible second something very like a chord — and across the dark plain, thirty feet away, a vein of light flickered through the glass itself. Following the line of her carving. Mirroring it. As if the ground were reading over her shoulder.

Paloma Reyes, who had spent twenty-nine years believing her gift began and ended at the ears, sat on cold glass in a dying world and watched the land listen to her.

* * *

By full light she had conducted eleven experiments, and Ilya had woken up in time to witness the last one, which was unfortunate, because it involved her holding the bone aloft and shouting "AGAIN!" at a plain of empty glass like a general addressing troops made entirely of echo.

He stood there with his cloak half-buttoned and his hair doing something structurally ambitious, and said, "Should I ask?"

"The ground answered me."

"I'd guessed that part."

"It *answered me*, Ilya." She was aware she was grinning and couldn't stop. Her face felt unfamiliar with the expression, like a borrowed coat. "I carved a spiral last night — just testing, just a practice spiral — and when I ran my thumb down it, the glass lit up in the same shape. All the way out." She pointed east, where, if you knew where to look, a faint gold thread still traced its way across the plain toward the horizon. "Thirty feet. Maybe forty. The land heard me."

Ilya crouched beside her work-roll, examining the bone with the careful neutrality of a man trying not to hope too loudly. "The dead here," he said slowly, "aren't separate from the Steppe. That's what the order taught. The signatories didn't die *on* the glass — they died *into* it. Their bones are the substrate. Everything you're walking on is—"

"Is a graveyard," Paloma finished. "Yes. I'd worked that out. It's rather hard to miss, sleeping on it."

"But if your craft works on the dead—" He looked up at her, and she saw the thought arrive in him the way weather arrives over a flat plain: all at once, visible from a long way off. "Paloma. If your craft works on the dead, and the dead are the land—"

"Then my craft works on the land." She let it sit between them for a moment, enormous and fragile as a soap bubble. "I've been carving memorials my whole life. Little ones. Names on bone so grief has somewhere to put itself down. And this whole place is one uncarved memorial the size of the sky." Her voice did something embarrassing on the last word, and she cleared her throat and pretended it hadn't. "Nobody's ever asked it what it wants to remember."

Ilya was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "My grandmother used to say that about people. That most folk go their whole lives unasked."

"Was your grandmother wise or just talkative?"

"Both, tragically. She died mid-anecdote. We suspect she planned it that way for dramatic effect."

Paloma laughed — actually laughed, out loud, on the Glass Steppe, nine turns from the end of the world — and the sound startled a shimmer out of the glass beneath them, a ripple of light running away in all directions like a pond disturbed by a thrown stone. They both froze and watched it go.

"Did you do that?" Ilya asked.

"I laughed."

"We should probably note that down somewhere. *Subject's laughter produces measurable seismic phenomena.*"

"Don't call me subject."

"Messenger's habit. We logged everything." He dug in his pack and produced the prophecy book, that dreadful old thing with its century-dead ink, and she tensed — but he only used the back board as a writing surface, scratching notes with a stub of charcoal. "All right. From the beginning. What exactly did you feel, the first time?"

Paloma considered lying, purely out of habit — she'd kept her gift private for so long that honesty still felt like undressing in a market square — and then told the truth instead, because apparently that was who she was becoming out here. "Like being listened to," she said. "Properly. The way I listen to the dead. Except reversed." She turned the bone in her fingers. "In the valley, I spend all day hearing. Everyone's grief, everyone's unfinished business, pouring into me, and me pouring back whatever comfort I can carve. But nobody — nothing — ever *heard* me. There was no one to hear. The dead take; they don't give back. That's not a complaint. It's just the shape of the work." She paused. "This morning something gave back."

Ilya wrote that down too. Underneath it, she saw, he'd drawn a small sketch of the spiral, and next to the spiral, after a moment's thought, a small star.

"What's the star?"

"Order notation. It means 'anomalous hope.'" He glanced up. "We don't get to use it often. There's some debate about whether we're allowed to use it at all. Old Master Corvin said hope wasn't data." He shrugged. "Old Master Corvin also died falling into his own soup, so I've adjusted my views accordingly."

* * *

They spent the day working, and it was the best day Paloma could remember having in years, possibly ever, which seemed ungrateful toward the whole rest of her life and true anyway.

She carved. That was the whole of it. She sat cross-legged on the glass with the messenger's femur across her knees and she carved the way water runs downhill, without deliberation, following the grain. And the Steppe followed *her*. Every line she cut sang up through the bone and out into the land, and the land answered in light, tracing her patterns across its face in slow golden script, and beneath it all ran that deep chord, that almost-voice, growing clearer by the hour.

By midday she could distinguish tones in it. Grief, mostly — vast, sedimentary grief, compressed over centuries into something that had stopped being a feeling and become a geology. But threaded through it, other notes. Defiance. Tenderness. One bright recurring tone that made her think, absurdly, of a child running through shallow water.

"There were cities here," she murmured, working. "Before the glass. Weren't there? I've seen them in the mirages."

"The covenant's founding flooded them," Ilya said quietly. He'd given up pretending to take notes an hour ago and was simply watching her work with an expression she was choosing not to examine too closely. "Thirteen signatories gave themselves to bind the Steppe away from the mortal realms, and the binding took everything with it. Whole lives. Markets. Arguments. Somebody's laundry, presumably, mid-wash." He gestured at the horizon, where the broken edge of the world shimmered. "Everything they were is still in the glass. That's why the mirages scream, when the turns pass. It's not torment, exactly. It's — being remembered wrong. Or not at all."

Paloma's knife stilled.

"That's what the dead always say," she said. "In the valley. The first thing, every time. *Remember me right.* Not *avenge* me, not *free* me. Remember me *right*." She looked down at the bone, at the pattern emerging under her hands — spirals nested in spirals, and at the center, taking shape without her quite deciding it, the outline of a gate. Or a door. Or perhaps simply an opening. "Maybe that's the covenant's problem. It was written as a lock. Nobody ever carved it as a memorial."

Ilya went very still. "Say that again."

"A memorial?"

"The other part."

"*It was written as a lock. Nobody ever carved it as a memorial.*"

He fumbled for the charcoal, knocked the book off his knee, retrieved it, and wrote, tongue between his teeth, muttering: "*Covenant-as-lock versus covenant-as-remembrance — reframe — oh, oh, this is why they sent for a carver, this is why the book says carver and not smith or priest or soldier—*" He stopped. Looked up. His eyes were shining in a way that would have embarrassed him deeply if he'd noticed. "A hundred years ago, the order tried to renew the covenant with scholars, soldiers, priests. All of them failed. All of them read the old terms and tried to satisfy them. But you're not reading the terms. You're listening to the *land*."

"Well," Paloma said, modestly, and then ruined it by adding, "someone had to. You lot were busy dying of soup."

"One death. One. It was a tragedy and we mourned him."

"You drew a diagram of it."

"For *training purposes*—"

But she was laughing again, and the glass rippled gold around them, and for one afternoon the Steppe felt less like a tomb and more like a workshop — a great unfinished commission waiting for the right pair of hands.

* * *

Dusk came early out here, or perhaps dusk simply came honestly, without the valley's soft gradients. The light went from gold to violet to a deep bruised blue, and the moon rose for its fifth turn, and Paloma felt the shift in the land the way you feel a storm front in an old scar.

But tonight, for the first time, she also felt something else.

She held the bone up against the dark, and the carved lines glowed steady and warm, and the Steppe's great chord gathered itself around the little gate-pattern at the bone's heart like water finding a channel. Not screaming. Not arguing. For a few breathless moments, the whole plain was simply *singing along*.

Hope, she discovered, was a physical thing. It had weight. It sat in the chest like a swallowed coal and radiated outward, and it made you reckless.

"We can do this," she said. "Ilya. We can actually do this. I don't need the old rite. I don't need the old terms. I can carve new ones — a covenant the way I'd carve a memorial, clause by clause, name by name, asking instead of binding. If the land will sing with me—"

"It's singing with you now."

"Then we—"

The chord broke.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next: the deep harmony fractured into discord, the gold light guttered, and far to the north — so far it should have been invisible, except that everything on the Steppe carried, the way sound carries over ice — something *howled*. Not wind. Wind didn't hate. This was a long, rising, deliberate cry, like a voice stretched thin on purpose, and underneath it came a rhythm she felt through her knees before she understood it:

Footsteps. Many. Running.

Ilya was on his feet with his hand at his knife, all the softness gone out of him. "Hollow Wind," he said. "That's their horn-call. Paloma — they know. Somehow they already know what you can do, and they've come to stop it."

Paloma closed her hand around the singing bone and felt its warmth pulse against her palm, once, twice, like a heartbeat answering hers.

"Then they'd better run fast," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it.
