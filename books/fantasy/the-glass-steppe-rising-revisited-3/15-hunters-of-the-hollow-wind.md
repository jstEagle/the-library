## 15. Hunters of the Hollow Wind

I had been a messenger long enough to know that the worst part of any journey is not the danger. The worst part is the moment when you realize you've stopped watching for it.

I realized this at dawn on the fourth day of the Steppe, when I caught myself humming.

Not just humming — humming badly. Some half-remembered tune from the order's dormitory, the one Brother Anselm used to whistle while mending his boots, off-key in exactly the way that made novices laugh into their sleeves. I was walking beside Paloma Reyes through a country of fused glass, the sun coming up like a struck match along the eastern dunes, and I was *humming*, which meant some traitorous corner of my mind had decided we were safe.

The dead knew better. They'd been restless all night — not the arguing factions from the salt flats, but something thinner, more nervous, skittering at the edge of my hearing like wind through a keyhole. Paloma had noticed too. She kept turning her head as she walked, the way a deer does, and she hadn't slept more than an hour at a stretch.

"You hear them?" I asked now, quietly.

"I hear *something*," she said. "It's not words yet. It's like — you know when a house settles? That creak before the crack?" She glanced at me. "You're humming."

"Am I." I stopped humming with the dignity of a man who has never hummed in his life.

"You were. Badly."

"It was a hymn."

"It was a crime against whatever god wrote it." But there was a small crease of amusement at the corner of her mouth, and I found — to my considerable alarm — that I would do a great deal to keep that crease there. This was new information about myself, and I filed it away next to the other things I'd learned since crossing the stones: that bone-carvers walk with their weight on their heels; that Paloma talked to the dead the way other people talk to difficult relatives, patiently, with occasional exasperation; that I had crossed half a continent carrying a book that named my death, and had never once been afraid the way I was afraid now, which was not for myself.

That was the trouble with fear, I was learning. It came in different grades. There was the fear of dying, which I'd had plenty of time to make peace with — a coughing, blood-spotted peace, negotiated over three years in an infirmary bed. And then there was this other kind, the newer kind, the one that arrived when I imagined the hunters finding us and thought first not *I don't want to die* but *she doesn't deserve what they'll do if they catch her.*

The Hollow Wind didn't kill messengers quickly. The last one, Serafim, had taken eleven days to die, and the order's records of it filled four pages, most of which I'd been shown as instruction and all of which I'd dreamed about since.

"They're close," I said. "The dead are nervous because the Wind is moving. It moves like weather. You feel it before you see it."

Paloma looked out at the dunes — great rolling waves of green-grey glass, translucent near their crests, so that the morning light passed through them and pooled in the hollows like spilled honey. Beautiful country. Murderous country. Out here there was no cover taller than a man, no water, and nothing to hide behind except distance itself.

"How far to the ford?" she asked.

"The Mirrored Ford? Two days, if we push. Three if we're careful."

"And if we're hunted?"

"One and a half," I said, "and then only briefly."

She snorted. It was, I had come to understand, her highest form of praise.

* * *

We pushed.

By midday the nervousness in the dead had sharpened into something with edges. Paloma stopped twice to press her palm flat against the glass, listening with her whole body the way she did, her braid slipping over her shoulder, her lips moving faintly around words she wouldn't share until she understood them. The second time she stood up slowly and brushed glass-dust from her knees.

"There's a dead woman under this dune," she said. "Old. Older than the covenant, maybe. She says the wind changed direction twice in one hour."

"Winds do that."

"Not here they don't. Not according to her. She says the wind here has blown one direction for a thousand years, and yesterday it turned, and today it turned back, and both times it turned *toward* us." Paloma's dark eyes fixed on me. "Ilya. What hunts by wind?"

"The Hollow Wind," I said, because there was no point being clever about it. "It's not a wind, exactly. It's — think of it as the order's shadow. Everything the order believes, the Wind believes the opposite, with the same devotion and better funding."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be. Reassurance is how they get you." I checked our backtrail out of habit — a line of bootprints pressed into glass dust, absurdly legible, pointing at us like an accusation. "The order keeps the covenant because it believes the world survives on law. The Wind broke away generations ago because it decided the world would be kinder ending than continuing on those terms. Annihilation as mercy. Their founder — well. You'll meet her eventually, if the stories are true, and I'm beginning to suspect every story on this Steppe is true."

"So they want the covenant to lapse."

"They want the thirteenth turn to pass quietly. No rite, no renewal, no anchor. The Steppe floods the mortal realms, everything magic touches unravels, and the whole painful experiment of existence gets a clean ending." I shrugged. "It's not stupid. That's the worst part. I argued with their doctrine for six years and never once caught them in a lie. Only in a conclusion."

"And the messenger?"

"Is the loose thread." I touched the book where it rode against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sleeping cat. "Kill the messenger, lose the book or bury it, and the rite dies of neglect. They don't even have to fight the signatories. They just have to make sure nobody arrives."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. Then: "Serafim," she said. "The last messenger. You said his name once, when you were feverish after the salt court. You said *I should have carried it myself.*"

"I say a lot of things feverish."

"You also apologized to a salt pillar for forty minutes."

"In my defense, it was a very judgmental pillar." But she wasn't letting go, and I hadn't expected her to. She had a carver's patience; she could worry at a thing for days without tiring. "Serafim was my teacher," I said finally. "Before the cough. Before they chose me. He taught me the signs, the routes, the safe-houses. He volunteered for the delivery when the order realized how bad the timing was — the thirteenth turn falling inside his lifetime. He was older. Experienced. The obvious choice."

"But he didn't go."

"He started. Made it as far as the ash country. The Wind's hunters took him on the third day." I kept my voice level, which cost more than shouting would have. "Eleven days. The order recovered what was left. What they couldn't recover—" I stopped. Paloma's hand had gone, very slightly, to the satchel at her hip, where she kept her carving bones wrapped in felt. Among them, though she didn't know it yet — or perhaps she did; she heard the dead, and the dead talk — were two fingers' worth of bone that the order had given me in a small cedar box, to deliver to the Steppe for burial, because the Wind had left nothing else of him to send home.

Her eyes went soft and terrible at once. "Ilya—"

"Don't," I said. Gently, but firmly. "Not now. Maybe not ever. Right now we need to walk faster."

* * *

We saw them at dusk, and I will give the Hollow Wind this much: they understand theater.

They came over the western dunes in a line, five figures, silhouetted against a sky the color of a bruise healing. They did not run. They did not call out. They simply appeared along the crest, evenly spaced, like notes on a staff, and stood there while the light died behind them, and let us do the arithmetic ourselves.

Five of them. Two of us. A mile of open glass between.

"Hollow Wind?" Paloma breathed.

"Hollow Wind." My mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the Steppe's thirst. "They're wearing the grey. See how they don't quite — bend? When they move?"

She saw it. Her breath caught. The Wind's hunters wore robes of woven ash-cloth that held their shape like armor, so that when they moved it was less like walking and more like statues being rearranged by someone with a cruel sense of composition.

"What do we do?"

"We run," I said, "and we get clever later."

We ran.

The glass dunes are treacherous going at the best of times — the surface squeaks underfoot like packed snow, and the slopes give way in sheets, and the light plays tricks, showing you solid footing where there's a hollow full of powdered glass deep enough to swallow a horse. We went down the backslope of our dune at a controlled fall, Paloma ahead of me because she was lighter and faster and, I suspected, because some instinct in her had already mapped the ground the way she mapped everything, by touch and attention. Behind us, without hurry, the five figures began to descend.

They didn't chase. That was the horror of it. They *advanced* — the same unhurried rearrangement of statue-limbs, eating the distance at a pace no living runner could sustain for long, because they didn't tire, and we did.

"Left!" I shouted, as the dune's shoulder crumbled. "There's a field of standing shards ahead — broken glass, big as men! If we can get among them—"

"Why?"

"Because they hunt by resonance!" I gasped it out between strides. "The ash-cloth — it reads heartbeat, breath, the vibration of a living body through the glass. In open country we're bells ringing. Among the shards, the whole ground rings. We drown in noise!"

Paloma didn't waste breath answering. She cut left, and I followed, and behind us I heard — felt, rather, in my teeth — a single low tone begin, like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup. The hunters had started to sing.

* * *

The shard field saved us, in the way that terrible places sometimes save you from worse ones.

Imagine a forest where every tree is a splinter of ancient catastrophe — blades of green and violet glass thrust up from the plain at drunken angles, some tall as watchtowers, thin enough to sing in the wind. The dead were thick here, crowded into the reflections, and when Paloma plunged among the shards she made a sound I'll remember on my own deathbed: half a sob, half a laugh, the sound of a woman walking into a room full of voices after years of quiet.

"They're everywhere," she panted. "Ilya, they're *everywhere*, they're pressing against me—"

"Let them press." I caught her arm, pulled her deeper between two towering blades. "Tell them why we've come. Tell them what the Wind intends. These are Steppe-dead, Paloma — covenant-dead. If the thirteenth turn passes, they unravel with everything else. Nobody wants that less than they do."

She stared at me for one heartbeat — and I watched her arrive at the same realization I'd been carrying since the valley: she had spent her whole life hearing the dead as a duty, a grief, a burden borne alone. She had never once considered that the dead might *fight for her.*

She closed her eyes. She laid both palms flat against the singing glass. And she spoke aloud, in her clear carver's voice, pitched to carry:

"People of the glass. I am Paloma Reyes, daughter of the valley, bone-carver, hearer. The Hollow Wind comes to unmake the covenant that holds your names. I am going to the chasm to remake it. Help me."

The field answered.

I cannot describe it honestly, so I will describe it inaccurately and ask your forgiveness. Every shard began to ring — not the hunters' hunting-tone, cold and single, but a chord, dozens of voices, hundreds, a sound like a cathedral full of bells agreeing on something. The hunters' song stuttered. Somewhere behind us, one of the grey figures made a sound I had never heard a hunter make, and have not heard one make since: hesitation.

"They're confused," Paloma whispered, eyes still closed, tears cutting tracks through the glass dust on her face. "They can't find our heartbeats. The dead are — Ilya, they're *holding our hands.*"

"Then hold on," I said, "and walk."

We walked. Through a forest of ringing glass, blind in every ordinary sense, guided by the pressure of the dead — left here, right there, down, wait, *now* — while the hunters' tone circled us like sharks around a lantern. Twice a grey figure loomed out of the reflections close enough that I could see the seams of its ash-cloth robe and the pale oval where its face should have been, and twice the dead roared up through the glass beneath its feet in a wave of dissonance that drove it reeling back.

The third time, we were already past.

* * *

We came out of the shard field under the first stars, on a long shallow slope of dark glass that ran down toward a basin glowing faintly with trapped moonlight. Behind us the hunters stood at the field's edge in a silent row, and I understood, looking back, that they were not defeated — merely recalculating. The Wind was patient. The Wind had waited a hundred years. It could wait out two exhausted travelers and one impossible girl.

But they had not counted on the impossible girl.

Paloma stood with her hands still trembling at her sides, glass dust in her hair, catching her breath in great whooping gulps that gradually steadied. Then she turned to me, and in the starlight her expression was one I hadn't seen before — not the wary reserve of the valley, nor the overwhelmed wonder of the early Steppe, but something settled and fierce, like a blade finding its groove in a whetstone.

"They told me their names," she said. "All of them. While we walked. Hundreds of names. They haven't been able to give anyone their names in a century — the old covenant locked them down to whispers. But I'm a carver. Carving is permission. So they gave me their names, and I—" her voice cracked, and she laughed at herself for it, furious and delighted, "—I memorized every single one."

"Of course you did," I said.

"They also told me something else." The delight drained out of her, leaving the fierce part behind. "The hunters aren't following us anymore. They're splitting. Three are peeling north — toward the chasm. Toward the council. They're not trying to kill us anymore, Ilya. They've decided killing us is taking too long. They're going to wake the signatories themselves — or silence them forever — before we can ever reach them."

I felt the bottom drop out of the evening. All our careful arithmetic — days to the ford, days to the chasm, the race we thought we were running — redrew itself in an instant, and the numbers came up wrong.

"How far ahead of us are they?"

"A night," she said. "Maybe less. They move like the wind, remember?"

I remembered. I looked north, where the stars came down to meet the broken horizon, and I thought of thirteen ghosts asleep in glass, and three grey shapes descending on them through the dark, and the book against my ribs that named tonight's date a century ago and had not, so far, named a single thing I could prevent.

Paloma slung her satchel higher on her shoulder and started down the slope without waiting for me.

"Well?" she called back over the glass, and there was that crease of amusement again, sharp as a chisel now. "Are you coming, messenger? Or do I have to memorize your name too?"

I ran to catch up.
