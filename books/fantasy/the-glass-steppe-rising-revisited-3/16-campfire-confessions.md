## 16. Campfire Confessions

The fire was Ilya's idea, and Paloma suspected it was less about warmth than about giving them both something to look at that wasn't each other.

They had made camp in the lee of a half-buried archway — some ancient structure the glass had swallowed to its shoulders, its carved lintel still visible above the surface like a drowning man's hand. The Steppe at night was not dark, exactly. The glass held the day's light the way a miser holds coins, and it gave it back slowly, grudgingly, in a faint amber glow that rose from the ground itself. It made everything look preserved. Pickled. As though they were two flies who had wandered into amber and might be there a very long time.

Ilya built the fire anyway. Old habits, he said, from a country of ordinary dirt and ordinary darkness. He fed it with twisted stalks of glasswort that burned green and smelled, absurdly, like mint tea.

Paloma sat across from him with her knees drawn up and her knife across her lap, working a sliver of bone into something she hadn't yet decided to name. It was easier to talk when her hands were busy. She had learned that long ago, over graves.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm watching you work." He said it simply, without embarrassment. "In the order's archives there were descriptions of covenant-smiths. They always sounded like weather reports. *The smith carves; the covenant holds.* Nobody ever mentioned the hands."

"What about the hands?"

"They move like they're listening." He turned his palms up. "Which I suppose they are."

Paloma looked down at her fingers, at the small white crescents the knife had pressed into her thumb over years of this. "My grandmother said a carver's hands should be quiet even when the rest of her isn't. That the dead can tell the difference between a hand that's working and a hand that's afraid."

"Was hers afraid? Ever?"

"I used to think no." She shaved a curl of bone away and watched it fall. "Now I think she was afraid for a hundred years and hid it so well I mistook it for calm. There's a difference between peace and a very well-carved mask. I should know. I've made enough of them."

Ilya laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped before he could stop it. "You make jokes about masks."

"I make jokes about everything. It's how you survive carving children's memorial bones for thirty years without weeping into the grain." She glanced up. "You'd know that too, wouldn't you. The dying order. All those last pages and final entries."

Something crossed his face then — not quite a flinch, but the shadow one makes on a road when passing a doorway where someone once stood. He fed another glasswort stalk to the fire and watched it flare green.

"Yes," he said. "I would know."

* * *

The thing about the Glass Steppe at night, Ilya thought, was that it refused to let you lie comfortably. In the valley — he'd only seen it for an hour, bleeding light onto the boundary stones, but he'd understood it immediately — the dark came down soft, like a blanket pulled up by a careful grandmother. Here the ground glowed, the sky glittered with too many stars arranged in constellations no mortal astronomer had ever named, and every direction offered a horizon sharp enough to cut yourself on. There was nowhere for a secret to hide. Not even your own.

He had been carrying his for eleven days now. Longer, if you counted honestly, which the Steppe seemed determined to make him do.

"Ask me," he said.

Paloma's knife paused. "Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking since the salt court. You have a face for it. Very well carved, like you said. But I've spent a year learning to read the faces of people deciding whether to tell me bad news." He smiled, and hoped it didn't come out crooked. "Yours has been saying *ask him why he really volunteered* since the night we met."

She set the knife down across her knee. The fire popped. Somewhere far off across the glass, one of the dead began a low argumentative murmur — the Steppe never entirely slept, and neither did its ghosts — and Paloma tilted her head toward it the way other people tilt toward music, listening despite herself.

"All right," she said. "Why did you volunteer? A dying order sends a messenger across the world with a book that names his death, and he goes. That's either faith or despair, and you don't strike me as a man of faith."

"No," Ilya agreed. "Not faith."

"So?"

He picked up a pebble of glass, turned it in his fingers. It caught the firelight and threw a tiny green spark against his knuckle.

"The cough started three winters ago," he said. "The order's physicians were good ones — the best left anywhere — and they told me the truth, because the dying deserve truth more than anyone. Something in the lungs, growing slow but certain. Two years, they said. Maybe three if I was gentle with myself." He huffed. "I have never once been gentle with myself. So: two."

Paloma had gone very still. Not the stillness of shock — he'd learned to tell that apart — but the stillness of a carver reading grain, finding the flaw, understanding exactly where it ran.

"When the order found the prophecy book," he went on, "and read the messenger's part in it — the crossing, the walking, the dying at the end — everyone else saw a horror. A sentence. And I sat there in the archive thinking, *how strange, that sounds like my life already.* The book just wrote down what my lungs already knew." He turned the pebble over. "So I volunteered because it wasn't a sacrifice. It was a choice about *where*. I could die in a bed in the archive hall, or I could die having seen the Glass Steppe. Having done the one thing worth doing. It wasn't bravery, Paloma. It was shopping."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "That is the most depressing way anyone has ever described courage."

"And yet here we are." He tossed the pebble gently back toward the fire, where it landed with a sound like a struck bell. "Eleven days past the second turn. Still breathing. My cough's even quieter out here, did you notice? The Steppe air, Vess says, remembers being alive. Perhaps it lends a little of that to the rest of us."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness he'd come to recognize as her most honest register — the voice she used when feeling had gotten ahead of words and she was reining it in by force.

"You let me carve protection charms for you," she said. "Back at the ford. Three nights of work."

"I know."

"You knew you were dying anyway, and you let me spend three nights—"

"I let you," he said, "because you needed to do it. Because your hands were shaking after the hunters and I knew the only thing that would steady them was work, and the only work you trust is work for someone else. You can't carve for yourself. Have you noticed? You've never once made anything for Paloma Reyes."

Her mouth opened. Closed. She looked, for the first time since he'd known her, genuinely caught — a woman surprised mid-thought by her own reflection.

"That's not fair," she said finally.

"It's completely fair. It's the fairest thing I've said all week."

* * *

The fire burned down to embers, and the green smell of the glasswort smoke settled over them like a second blanket. Paloma drew her coat tighter and considered, with the detached interest of a scholar, the fact that she was about to say something true.

It was his fault. He'd gone first. There was a craft to confession, same as any other — you couldn't ask someone to hold still under the knife unless you'd shown them your own scars first. Her grandmother's rule. *Never cut deeper than you've bled.*

"I never wanted to leave the valley," she said.

Ilya nodded slowly, waiting. He was good at waiting. It occurred to her that a man who had spent a year walking toward his own death had probably gotten excellent at it.

"But that's the smaller truth," she went on, before her nerve could fail. "The bigger one is — I never wanted to be alone in it. Those aren't the same thing. Everyone in the valley thinks I stayed because I love the place, and I do, I do love it, the way you love a room you've been shut in. But the real reason—the reason I never carved into any stone—is that leaving meant becoming someone the valley couldn't protect. And worse—" her thumb found the crescent scar on her knuckle and pressed it, an old habit, a private counting—"worse, staying meant I got to keep being useful instead of happy. Useful is safe. Useful is *needed*. Nobody questions why you're alone if you're needed."

The dead murmured far off across the glass. She listened to them without meaning to, the way you listen to rain.

"My whole life, the dead needed me and the living tolerated me, and I told myself that was enough. A full life. A *carved* life." She laughed, and it came out rougher than she intended. "And then you fell through the stones bleeding light and said my name — my *name*, before I'd given it — and do you know what my first feeling was? Not fear. Not wonder. It was relief. Horrible, selfish relief. Because someone had finally come through the wall, and whatever happened next, I wouldn't have to pretend the wall was a home anymore."

Ilya had not moved. But something in the quality of his attention had changed — the way a listener becomes a witness.

"I'm angry about that," she said. "I'm angry that it took a stranger and a dying order and a breaking covenant to get me out of a prison I built myself. I had everything I needed to walk out. I had legs. I had a grandmother who clearly wanted me to. I just—" She spread her hands, helpless, and hated how young the gesture felt. "I just never believed I was allowed to want things. Wanting felt like ingratitude. The dead gave me purpose, the valley gave me shelter, and I repaid them by never asking for a single thing more. Thirty years of gratitude used as a lock."

"Your grandmother broke the rite," Ilya said quietly. "Refused to bind a child. Sabotaged the covenant rather than pay its price. Everything she did was for mercy — and it trapped her granddaughter in a valley for a lifetime." He shook his head. "Mercy does that sometimes. It builds beautiful cages."

"Don't defend her."

"I'm not. I'm defending *you*. There's a difference." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the hollows of his face. "You asked me why I volunteered, and I told you the ugly truth — that I was shopping for a better death. Now you've told me yours. So here's what I see, from outside, where you can't see yourself: a woman who spent thirty years serving the dead faithfully, who walked out of her sanctuary to save a world that had lied to her family for a century, and whose worst crime is that she wanted company on the road." He smiled. "If that's a prison, Paloma, the bars are made of tissue paper."

"Tissue paper," she repeated.

"It's a metaphor from home. Very sturdy tissue paper."

Despite herself — despite everything — Paloma laughed. It startled her, the sound of it, bright and uncarved, bouncing off the glass plain and coming back changed. Across the Steppe, the murmuring dead went briefly silent, as if listening. Then, softly, one of them laughed too — a dry rustling sound, like wind through winter reeds — and passed the laughter along, ghost to ghost, until somewhere in the distance a whole chorus of the departed was chuckling at nothing, at everything, at the sheer improbable comedy of two mortals sitting on a burning plain confessing their prisons to each other.

"They like you," Ilya said.

"They like *gossip*," Paloma corrected. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

She threw a pebble at him. He caught it, grinning, and set it carefully beside the fire like a treasure.

Later, when the embers had gone gray and the amber glow of the Steppe rose up around them like a tide, Paloma lay back against the drowned archway and stared at the unnamed constellations. She heard Ilya settle nearby — closer than the first nights, not so close as to presume — and heard his breathing, and noticed, with a carver's precision, that the faint whistle in it had grown quieter again. Quieter than it had been in the valley. As if the land itself were lending him breath, one borrowed hour at a time.

*I let you carve for me because you needed to,* he'd said. But that wasn't all of it. She was almost sure it wasn't all of it. There had been something in his face at the ford, watching her work — something that wasn't pity and wasn't patience but was warmer than either, and she had filed it away unexamined, the way she filed all her own wants.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would examine it.

She was nearly asleep when Ilya spoke, so quietly she might have dreamed it.

"Paloma. The book — I never showed you the marginalia. The notes in the margins, the ones the original scribe added after the main text." A pause. "One of them isn't about the rite at all. It's a list. Names, dates. And the last entry—" his voice did something complicated— "the last entry is dated tomorrow."

She opened her eyes. Above them, one of the unnamed constellations flickered — or perhaps it moved — rearranging itself, star by patient star, into a shape she almost recognized.

A hand. Open, palm-up.

As if the sky itself were asking to see what she was holding.
