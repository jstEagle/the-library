## 17. The Second Turn

Paloma felt the turn coming the way she felt weather in old bones that weren't yet old enough to complain about it — a pressure behind her ears, a hush in the air, and every ghost within a mile going quiet at once, like children told to sit still for a photograph.

"Is it soon?" Ilya asked. He was crouched over their little fire of glass-shards and dried steppe-grass, which burned with a pale green flame and gave off no heat whatsoever. The Glass Steppe's idea of kindness, apparently: light without warmth, plenty without comfort.

"Tonight," she said. "Maybe sooner. The dead have stopped arguing."

"They've been arguing since we got here."

"I know." She almost smiled. "That's how I know something's wrong."

They had been walking for six days since the first turn — six days across dunes that chimed faintly underfoot like a thousand struck wineglasses, six days of mirage-cities rising on the horizon and dissolving when approached, six days of Ilya teaching her the order's hand-signs while she taught him, in exchange, how to tell which of the Steppe's dead were worth listening to and which were simply lonely. He was an earnest student. He had the particular earnestness of a man who had spent his whole life preparing for one errand and was now discovering that the errand had opinions of its own.

"The sign for *water*," he said now, raising two fingers, "is also the sign for *mercy*. Which tells you something about the order."

"That they were thirsty?"

"That they were honest," he said, "in the way only dying institutions can afford to be."

The moon was already climbing, fat and wrong-colored, a bruised silver that no valley sky would have permitted. Thirteen turns. They had watched the first one pass from the shelter of a fossil-rib archway while the ground screamed beneath them. Now the second was coming, and this time there was no archway, no shelter, just a shallow bowl of glass with a ring of salt-bushes around it, and the two of them, and the dead.

Paloma sat down beside the cold fire and took out her carving.

She had been working on it for days without quite admitting what it was. A length of leg-bone from the last messenger — the poor man whose remains Ilya had carried across half the world in a satchel, guilt folded up small alongside his provisions — smoothed and hollowed and slowly, patiently, being given a shape. Not a sigil. Not yet. More like a question posed in bone. When she held it near the glass, the Steppe hummed against it, low and curious, the way a horse hums when you press your palm flat to its neck.

"You're talking to it again," Ilya said.

"It's not talking back. That would be rude of it."

"You're smiling, though."

"Am I?" She turned the bone in her fingers. In the valley, carving had been a service rendered downward: she spoke to the dead on behalf of the living, grief made orderly, names made permanent. Here it was something else. Here the dead outnumbered the living so completely that she wasn't a messenger anymore. She was a translator at a parliament. And the parliament, God help her, was starting to recognize her as such. Ghosts drifted toward her campfire at night the way moths did, except these moths had grievances, and factions, and — in one memorable case — a written petition, scratched into a shard of glass, demanding restoration of a border that had been dissolved four hundred years before Paloma's grandmother was born.

"I've been thinking," Ilya said, "about what happens if we fail."

"Don't."

"Humor me. If we fail — if the covenant lapses and everything unravels — do you think we'd know? Or would we just stop, the way a dream stops, without ever getting the chance to be sad about it?"

Paloma considered the horizon. Somewhere far behind them, beyond days of walking, lay the boundary stones, and beyond them the valley, where her tools were, and her house, and the graves of everyone who had ever loved her, all of it sealed behind a rule that magic could not follow past the stones — a rule that had kept her safe her whole life and small her whole life, and which she was beginning to suspect had never been a rule at all but merely a promise, and promises, unlike rules, could expire.

"We'd know," she said. "The dead would know first. And they'd tell me. They never shut up about anything else; why would they spare me that?"

He laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, the kind she'd learned to prize because they came maybe twice a week and always slightly against his will. "You make it sound almost bearable."

"Grief is bearable. It's everything else that isn't."

And then the moon reached the top of its arc, and the second turn began.

It started, as before, with silence. Every ghost within hearing went still — not gone, but *held*, like breath. Then the glass under them shivered. Not the chime of footsteps this time but a deeper note, subterranean, the plain clearing its throat. Far off to the east, one of the mirage-cities rose all the way up out of the glass and stood there, real as a wound: towers, bridges, banners, windows lit gold, and from it a sound that Paloma felt in her teeth rather than heard, a vast exhalation, ten thousand voices remembering themselves all at once —

— and then it sank. Slowly, gracefully, horribly, the city settled back into the plain like a body lying down, and the light in its windows went out window by window, and the note under the world faded to nothing, and the ghosts resumed their murmuring as though nothing had happened, because for them nothing had. This was simply what the Steppe did now. It dreamed itself awake for a moment each turn, and then forgot.

Ilya had both hands pressed flat against the glass. His face was wet. She pretended not to notice, because he would have wanted her to.

"There were people in there," he said quietly. "Not ghosts. People. Caught mid-turn. That city's been sinking for a hundred years, Paloma. Every time the moon marks a turn, they get one breath of real, and then —"

"I know."

"How do you stand knowing? How do you hear all that every day and still carve little names and drink your tea and —"

"Because the alternative," she said, "is to lie down in the glass too, and I've met the people down there, and let me tell you, they are *terrible* conversationalists."

He looked at her. She looked steadily back until the corner of his mouth gave up and twitched.

* * *

It was three hours later — the moon beginning its long slide down the other side of the sky — that she saw the horizon change.

At first she thought it was another mirage. But mirages rose; this was falling. On the northern edge of the world, where they had come from, where the boundary stones stood in their patient ancient row, something pale was collapsing. Even at this distance she recognized the shape of it, the way you recognize your own front door in a nightmare. The stones were crumbling. Not fast — nothing on the Steppe did anything fast — but certainly, grain by grain, like sugar held too long in water. A fine dust hung above the place where they had stood for a thousand years, catching the moonlight, glittering.

"No," Ilya said. He'd seen it too. He was standing now, walking three steps toward it as if three steps could matter across forty miles of glass. "No, that's not — the covenant hasn't failed yet, we still have eleven turns, the valley should hold until the thirteenth —"

"The valley holds as long as the stones believe it does," Paloma said. Her voice came out very level, which surprised her. Inside, something was tearing along a seam she hadn't known was there. "And the stones are old, Ilya. Older than my grandmother. Older than the book. Whatever the first turn shook loose — they're tired. They're done believing."

She had grown up thinking of those stones as furniture. That was the truth of it, the small ugly truth she'd never said aloud to anyone: the boundary markers were as fixed and unremarkable in her mind as the stove, the well, the crooked fence post. She had walked past them ten thousand times. Children dared each other to touch them. Old men leaned on the smallest one to catch their breath. And all the while they had been holding back an ocean, silently, without thanks, and she had never once — not once — thought to carve their names.

Now she watched them die from forty miles away, and she couldn't even hear them go. That was the cruelest part. She was a woman who heard the dead, and the distance stole even that from her. They were passing somewhere behind that glittering dust-cloud, and she was getting silence.

"Paloma." Ilya's hand on her arm. Gentle. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You've stopped blinking."

"So have you."

A ghost drifted close — one of the petition-drafters, a thin gray woman with the posture of someone who had spent several centuries composing formal complaints — and hovered at the edge of the firelight, uncertain. Then, awkwardly, she bowed. Not to Ilya. To Paloma. And behind her, others were gathering: dozens of them, then scores, the Steppe's dead rising softly out of the glass like frost forming on a window, all facing north, all facing the dust, all silent. An honor guard. A vigil.

"Oh," Paloma whispered. Her throat closed. "Oh, they *liked* the stones."

"They were signed under those stones," Ilya said. "Every one of them. That's where the oaths were spoken."

The vigil lasted until the dust-cloud thinned and the horizon showed nothing where the markers had been but a low smooth absence, a gap in the world's teeth. Then, one by one, the ghosts sank away, and the plain was ordinary again, chiming underfoot, dreaming its drowned cities.

Paloma sat back down by the dead fire. Her hands were steady. She noted this with a kind of distant clinical interest, the way you'd note that a house continued standing after the earthquake. Her hands were steady, and her eyes were dry, and somewhere inside her a door that had been open her entire life — the door home, the door back, the door that said *you can always return to who you were* — swung shut with a click she felt in her spine.

No retreat. She had known it intellectually since the first turn. Knowing it intellectually and watching the proof crumble on the horizon were different animals entirely. There was no version of this story now in which she went back to the valley and took up her old commissions and complained cheerfully about the mayor's funeral urn. That woman was gone. That woman had been gone since she crossed the stones, honestly, and the crumbling was just the world catching up to the fact.

"You could still hate me," Ilya offered quietly. "For bringing you into this. I'd understand it. I think I'd respect it, actually."

"If I hated you, you'd know. I'm from a long line of grudge-holders. We have traditions."

"What are the traditions?"

"Mainly the grudges."

He smiled, then sobered. "What are you thinking?"

She looked down at the bone in her lap — the messenger's bone, half-carved, humming faintly against the glass. She thought about doors. She thought about how her grandmother must have stood somewhere on this same plain, a century ago, watching the same horizon, carrying the same terrible arithmetic, and chosen to break the rite rather than feed it. She thought about how breaking things was also a kind of craft, if you did it lovingly enough.

"I'm thinking," she said, "that the valley didn't protect us from magic. It protected us from *deciding*. My family ran, and the stones let them run, and the stones kept the door shut so nobody ever had to choose anything harder than what to carve next. And now the door's shut from our side, and for the first time in a hundred years, somebody from my family has to actually finish what my grandmother started." She turned the bone over. "I used to think exile was the worst thing that could happen to a person. I'm starting to think it might be the first honest thing that ever happened to me."

"That's either very wise or very exhausted."

"Those are the same thing after midnight."

The moon touched the western rim of the world. Below its last sliver, far to the south — south, where the Salt Court lay, where Vess reigned over her pillars of poisoned hospitality — a light appeared. Not mirage-light. Not city-gold. Something smaller and more deliberate, a lantern moving in a straight line toward them across the glass, unhurried, unmistakably *aimed*.

Ilya saw it too. His hand went to the satchel where the prophecy book rode. "Nobody walks that straight on the Steppe unless they already know where they're going."

"Or unless they want us to think they do."

The lantern bobbed nearer. Around it, faintly, Paloma caught the smell of salt on the wind, and beneath the salt something sweeter and worse — the scent of a feast laid out in an empty room.

"Well," she said, standing, bone knife loose in her grip, heart hammering in a way she chose to call readiness rather than fear. "It seems the queen has noticed her guests left without saying goodbye."

The lantern stopped a hundred paces out. And from the dark around it, in a voice like sugar dissolving in wine, Vess called: *"Little carver. We need to talk about your messenger."*
