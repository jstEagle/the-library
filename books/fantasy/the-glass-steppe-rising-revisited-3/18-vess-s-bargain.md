## 18. Vess's Bargain

I had learned, in my years with the Order of the Last Page, that the worst news never arrives looking like bad news. It arrives looking like a gift. It arrives with a smile. It arrives, in this particular case, riding a palanquin of salt pillars carried by forty ghost-soldiers who had clearly spent the morning practicing their synchronized marching and wanted very much for someone to notice.

"They're showing off," I murmured to Paloma.

"I can see they're showing off."

"The left rank is slightly ahead of the right."

"Ilya." She didn't look at me. Her eyes were fixed on the palanquin as it crested the glass dune, salt banners snapping in a wind that smelled of old promises. "If you narrate the enemy's parade one more time, I'm going to carve your name into something unflattering."

"That's fair," I said. "Though I'd point out that everything down here is already unflattering. It's a plain of broken glass haunted by the arguments of the dead. There's no up."

That was the thing about the days after the first turn passed — the ones we could still count as days. The Steppe shuddered on its schedule now, the moon-turns ticking past like the slow closing of a door, and between the shudderings there was nothing to do but walk, and argue, and wait for Vess to make her next move. We both knew she would. Queens of salt do not take refusal gracefully. They take it personally.

The palanquin stopped twenty paces from our little camp — two bedrolls, a cold fire, and the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth like a wound that hadn't decided whether to heal. Vess descended without touching the ground, which was, I gathered, her version of good manners. She wore mourning-white and a crown of fused salt that caught the light of the trapped cities and threw it back in pieces.

"Carver," she said, inclining her head to Paloma. "Messenger." To me she gave only a glance, the way one acknowledges furniture one intends to replace.

"Queen of Salt," Paloma said evenly. "You've brought an escort."

"I've brought *an army*." Vess let the word sit in the air between us, fat and gleaming. "Four thousand of the Steppe's honored dead. Every soldier who ever fell defending the covenant's borders. They have been waiting a hundred years for someone to give them orders again." She smiled, and it was a lovely smile, the kind that has been practiced on mirrors made of ice. "And I am prepared to give them to you."

Paloma's hands went very still at her sides. I had learned to read those hands the way sailors read weather. Stillness meant calculation. Stillness meant the bone-carver was already working the problem, feeling for the grain.

"In exchange for what?" Paloma asked.

"For so little." Vess drifted closer, her hem whispering against the glass. "The book is clear, carver. You've read it. The covenant requires an anchor — a living soul bound into the glass, holding the door shut from inside. The rite names the messenger." Her gaze slid to me with the tenderness of a butcher selecting a cut. "He is dying anyway. The Hollow Wind told you as much, did they not? His lungs are full of the order's incense smoke. He has months, perhaps weeks. Why waste a whole life when a half-spent one will do?"

"*Why waste—*" I started, and Paloma's hand found my arm. Not gripping. Just resting. A hand saying: let me.

"You want me to bind him," Paloma said. "Carve him into the covenant. Alive."

"As the book instructs. As the law demands. As every signatory before you agreed." Vess spread her hands, magnanimous. "Do this, and my four thousand march with you to the chasm. The Hollow Wind's hunters will break against them like rain on stone. You will reach the rite alive, perform it properly, and save the valley, the Steppe, and every mortal realm besides. One man's last months, traded for millions of lives. Carver, you are a woman who weighs things for a living. Weigh this."

The silence that followed was long enough for me to notice several things I wished I hadn't. That the ghost-army behind Vess stood in perfect formation, waiting, their hollow faces turned toward us with something horribly like hope. That Paloma's thumb had moved, very slightly, against my sleeve — a small unconscious stroke, the way one touches a thing one is afraid of losing. And that some treacherous part of me, the part that had volunteered for this errand precisely because I was dying, was doing arithmetic of its own.

Months for millions. Even I could carry that sum.

But then I looked at Paloma's face, and I saw that she wasn't doing arithmetic at all.

She was remembering.

I knew what she was remembering because she'd told me around a campfire three nights ago, in the low voice she used for confessions: her grandmother's letter, hidden in the sigil, explaining why the old rite had been broken. *They bound a child,* the letter said. *Every century, a living smith, chosen by lot or by lie, carved screaming into the glass while the signatories watched and called it duty.* Paloma's family hadn't fled the Steppe. They'd fled the *price*. And Paloma had spent the entire journey since learning that letter by heart, mouthing it in her sleep, carving fragments of it into scraps of bone the way other people pray.

"Vess," Paloma said quietly. "When the last anchor was bound — a hundred years ago — how old were they?"

Vess's smile flickered. "Nineteen."

"And did they go willingly?"

"The covenant does not require willingness. It requires *presence*."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer the law permits."

Paloma nodded slowly. Then she stepped forward, away from me, away from the shelter of my arm, until she stood close enough to Vess that the queen's cold radiance lit her from below like footlights. My stomach dropped. In my experience — and I had, unfortunately, extensive experience — when a quiet person steps toward power instead of away from it, the next words are going to be expensive.

"Here is what I weigh," Paloma said. "I weigh a girl of nineteen, screaming, while ghosts watched and called it duty. I weigh my grandmother, who broke the world rather than bind another child, and bought us a hundred years with her sabotage. I weigh every clause of the old covenant, and I find them all written in the same ink." She lifted her chin. "Blood. Coercion. Fear dressed up as necessity. You offer me an army to write the same sentence again with a different name at the end of it."

"The alternative is annihilation."

"The alternative," Paloma said, "is that I am a bone-carver, and I have not yet begun to work."

Something moved through the ghost-army then — a ripple, shoulder to shoulder, four thousand dead soldiers shifting their weight as though a wind had passed through ranks of wheat. Vess felt it too. I watched her feel it. Her crown of salt chimed softly, and for just a moment her composure cracked along a fault line a century old, and underneath it I saw not malice but *hunger* — the hunger of a woman who had signed the old covenant herself, who had helped bind that nineteen-year-old, who needed the old law to be righteous because otherwise she had been a monster for a very long time.

"You would gamble everything," Vess said, "on craft. On *carving*."

"On willing bone," Paloma said. "Yes."

"There is no such rite. It has never existed. The covenant cannot be remade on consent — the founders tried gentler laws before the sacrifice, and the Steppe itself rejected them. The land demands blood, carver. It always has."

"Then the land," said Paloma Reyes, in the mild tone of a woman discussing the weather, "is about to learn some new manners."

I fell in love with her a second time, right there, which was inconvenient, because I had promised myself I wouldn't do that until after the apocalypse, and even then only briefly.

* * *

Vess did not storm off. Queens of salt do not storm; they *subside*, the way tide goes out, leaving things stranded and exposed. She regarded Paloma for a long moment, and then she laughed — a short, genuine, terrible laugh, as if Paloma had surprised her into honesty.

"A hundred years," Vess said, "and the first smith to stand in this place refuses the book on its own terms. Do you know what the Hollow Wind said of you? *She will bend. They all bend. Give her a deadline and a dead man and she will choose the dead man's use over his dignity.*" She turned. "I hoped she was wrong. I am sorry to learn she isn't, because I liked you, carver, and I am about to become your problem."

"Vess—" I began.

"The army marches with the Wind now," she said simply. "Not because I love her cause. Because the old law is the only law I know how to enforce, and a queen who cannot enforce law is only a woman standing in a field." She paused at the edge of her palanquin's light, and her voice dropped, and for one unguarded instant she sounded tired beyond the reach of death. "Bind him willingly, carver — truly willingly, his choice freely given — and perhaps the land will accept it. Perhaps consent is a door the founders never thought to knock upon. But if you fail, and the thirteenth turn comes, everything unravels together. Remember that I offered you the certain road."

Then she was gone, and the army with her, four thousand pairs of ghost-boots receding across the glass in perfect time, and the dunes were empty except for us and the wind and the distant, patient screaming of a city-mirage trapped under the horizon.

Paloma stood very still until the sound faded. Then her shoulders came down from around her ears, all at once, like a drawbridge lowering.

"Well," she said. "That went badly."

"That went *terribly*," I agreed. "We now have no army, a deadline measured in moon-turns, and a queen's grudge. On the bright side—"

"Is there a bright side?"

"We're alive, the book is intact, and you just told a four-thousand-year-old ghost-queen that the land needs new manners. I intend to bring that up at every available opportunity for the rest of your natural life." I hesitated. "However long that is."

She turned to look at me then, and whatever she saw in my face made her frown. "Ilya. What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. Something's *complicated*." I sat down heavily on my bedroll, because my legs had opinions about standing. "She offered you a bargain where I die and everyone else lives. Clean math. And you refused it without blinking, and I don't know whether to be grateful or horrified, because Paloma — I *volunteered* for this. Dying is the one part of the job I was already qualified for. When you refused her, you didn't just refuse the army. You refused the easy ending. For both of us. Now we have to earn one the hard way, and I've seen the hard way, and it has hunters in it."

Paloma crouched in front of me. She took my hands — the scholar's soft hands, the smoker's ruined hands — and turned them over, studying the palms as if reading a text.

"When my grandmother broke the rite," she said, "she didn't ask anyone's permission either. Not the signatories'. Not the valley's. Not mine, and I wasn't born yet, and I've spent my whole life paying interest on her loan." Her thumb traced a line across my palm, deliberate, the way she touched bone before cutting. "I'm not refusing to let you die, Ilya. Death comes for everyone; I hear it coming for most of them. I'm refusing to let a *book* decide how. Or a queen. Or a hundred-year-old law written by frightened people." She looked up. "Whatever ending we get, we're going to write it ourselves. Badly, probably. With jokes. But ours."

"That's the most romantic threat I've ever received."

"I'm a carver. Threats are basically commissions."

I laughed, and it hurt, and I laughed anyway, because some things are worth the cost.

* * *

We broke camp within the hour. Vess's departure had changed the geometry of the Steppe — I could feel it in the way the dead went quiet along our route, the arguing voices in the glass pulling back like spectators before a fight. Word traveled fast among the dead. By nightfall, everything that haunted this plain knew that the carver had refused the queen, and that the queen's answer was coming.

It came at moonrise.

We heard it before we saw it: a sound like a glacier clearing its throat, rolling across the dunes from the direction of the Salt Court. Then the horizon behind us lit white — not dawn-light, nothing so gentle — and I turned to see the glass itself rising. Not breaking. *Rising*. Sheets of it peeling up off the plain in vast translucent blades, catching the moonlight and throwing it in shards, and through the glass I could see shapes moving. Soldiers. Vess's soldiers, or what remained of them once the Wind's whispers had gotten hold of their hearts — hollow-eyed now, salt-crusted, marching in formations that bent at angles geometry would disown.

"Hollow Wind's taken the court?" Paloma breathed beside me.

"No." I watched the wave-front of risen glass crawl toward us, slow as judgment. "Worse. Vess kept her word. She said war would follow us out." My mouth had gone dry. "She wasn't threatening us, Paloma. She was warning us. The Wind doesn't need her permission anymore — the moment her army wavered, it walked in and took the *soldiers*. And now it wants the book, and the carver, and the messenger, and it wants us before the second turn."

"How long?"

I looked east, where the moon hung low and swollen, already leaning toward its next mark. "Two nights. Maybe less."

Paloma slung her pack — grandmother's tools, the sigil, the scraps of carved bone that were becoming, piece by piece, something none of the old books had imagined. She looked at the wall of risen glass bearing down on us, and at the dark road ahead, and she did the thing I was beginning to understand was simply who she was: she reached into her pocket, took out a small blank bone, and began to carve as she walked.

"What are you making?" I asked.

"A complaint," she said, "to file with management."

Ahead of us, far across the plain, the ground shimmered — a thin silver line winding through the dunes, catching the moonlight like a seam of mercury. A river, out here, in the middle of the Glass Steppe, where no water had flowed since the world was young.

I had read about it in the order's oldest texts, in the chapter the archivists called *inadvisable*.

"Paloma," I said. "I think that's the Mirrored Ford."

She glanced up from her carving, took in the silver line, and frowned.

"And here I was hoping," she said, "for a nice ordinary bridge."
