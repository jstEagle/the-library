## 19. The Mirrored Ford

The river announced itself before we saw it — a sound like a hundred small bells being argued over by the wind.

I had been walking since dawn with my hood up and my thoughts down, which is to say I was thinking about Ilya again and pretending I wasn't. Three days since Vess's war had chased us out of the salt country. Three days of his jokes getting worse as his limp got better, and mine getting quieter as my hands got itchier. A carver's hands itch when they want to work. Mine had been itching since the chasm, and there was nothing in this whole glass-burned Steppe worth carving except, apparently, a covenant, and I wasn't ready to think about that yet either.

So when the bells started ringing out of nowhere, I was almost grateful for the distraction.

"What is that?" I asked.

Ilya stopped. He did that thing he did — the scholar's pause, where he tilted his head like a bird deciding whether a worm was worth the effort. "Either music," he said, "or a trap."

"You've been very helpful on this journey. Has anyone told you that?"

"Constantly." He grinned, then winced, because grinning still pulled at the bruised ribs from the dune chase. "It's coming from over the rise. The book mentions a ford somewhere in this stretch — the Mirrored Ford. It's marked as neutral ground."

"Marked by whom?"

"By everyone, apparently. That's what made it neutral." He said it lightly, but his hand found the strap of the satchel where the prophecy book rode against his back, and I noticed he'd taken to walking with it on the far side from strangers. Old habit. Sensible habit. The last messenger had died carrying it, and I carried some of those bones in my pack now, wrapped in linen, and I tried not to think about how light they were.

We crested the rise together, and I forgot every clever thing I'd ever planned to say.

The river ran silver — not water-silver, not moonlight-silver, but mirror-silver, a ribbon of polished surface maybe thirty feet wide, flowing between banks of pale reeds that chimed when the wind moved them. That was the bell-song. The river itself made no sound at all. It flowed in perfect silence, and its surface didn't reflect the sky above us.

It reflected other skies.

I knelt at the bank before I'd decided to kneel, the way you lean toward a fire on a cold night without asking your legs' permission. In the glass-still water I saw a valley — *my* valley, unmistakably, the boundary stones and the terraced gardens and the smoke rising straight up from chimneys because magic couldn't follow past the stones to bend it sideways. But the reflection was wrong in one detail, and the wrongness went through me like a hook through soft flesh.

There was a child in the reflection. Small, dark-haired, sitting on my doorstep carving something clumsy out of wood, tongue poking out the corner of her mouth in concentration. And behind her, framed in the doorway, a woman stood wiping flour off her hands.

The woman was me.

* * *

"I wouldn't," Ilya said quietly, behind me. "Not until you know what it does."

"It shows you what you could have been." My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "Is that right? Is that what it does?"

"The order's records call it the Ford of Unlived Hours. Pilgrims used to come here to grieve. Also pilgrims used to come here and never leave, so eventually the order stopped recommending it." He crouched beside me, careful not to touch the water. "Look at the surface. Really look."

I looked. Now that he said it, I saw: the river wasn't showing me a memory or a wish. It was showing me a *life*. The image moved. The little girl finished her clumsy carving and held it up, and reflected-me laughed — a laugh I knew, my own laugh, but rounder somehow, less careful. The seasons turned in fast-forward. The girl grew. There was a man in the doorway too, broad-shouldered, laughing at something out of frame, and I felt an absurd, piercing jealousy of a person who did not exist.

"That's not me," I whispered. "That's the me who never heard the dead. Who never had to sit alone with everyone's grief because nobody living wanted to share it."

"Yes."

"How do you make it stop?"

"You don't. You walk away." He said it simply, but his jaw was tight, and I realized he hadn't looked at the water once. Not once since we'd arrived. "Paloma—"

"Look at yours first," I said. "Fair's fair. You've seen mine."

He hesitated. Then, because Ilya was constitutionally incapable of refusing a fair challenge, he leaned over the bank.

The river showed him a library.

Tall windows, dust motes turning gold in the light, shelves climbing past the top of the frame. Reflected-Ilya sat at a desk piled with books, and he was *whole* — no limp, no bruised ribs, no gray undertone to his skin that I'd learned to watch for without admitting I was watching. He was arguing with someone off-frame about a footnote, gesturing with a pen, alive in the specific way of a person whose body has never once betrayed him. The reflected life rippled forward: publications, students, a name known in three cities. A chair by a window that he'd earned instead of borrowed.

I watched Ilya look at it for exactly four seconds. Then he shut his eyes and sat back hard on his heels.

"Well," he said. "That's humiliating."

"Why? It's beautiful."

"Because it's true, isn't it? That's the trick of this place. It doesn't show you fantasies. It shows you the road not taken, accurately rendered." He rubbed his face. "I was supposed to be that. Before the order found me, before the wasting sickness — I was a student. An actual student, with an actual thesis, about covenant law, which is the most boring sentence I've ever said aloud. I was good at it. And now the river's showing me the version where I stayed good at it instead of becoming a dying errand boy with a limp and a satchel."

"You're not dying anymore," I said. "You're — better. Since the second turn. Since—" I stopped, because neither of us liked finishing that sentence, the one about how whatever the Steppe was doing to him seemed to be holding the sickness at bay, and what would happen when we left, if we ever left.

"Since," he agreed. He glanced at the water sidelong, the way you glance at an ex-lover across a market square. "Doesn't mean I don't miss him. Footnote-Ilya. He seems like he'd have been insufferable. I'd have liked him enormously."

Despite everything — despite the dead murmuring at the edge of my hearing all day, despite the turns counting down somewhere in the moon's slow arithmetic — I laughed. It startled both of us.

"There," Ilya said, pointing at me with great satisfaction. "Record that. The carver who hears can also laugh. The river clearly has no idea what it's missing."

* * *

We should have walked away then. We were both tired, and tired people should not be offered alternate lives, any more than hungry people should be handed knives and told to juggle.

But the ford was the only crossing for miles — the book's map showed the river bending away into country we couldn't afford to detour around — and crossing meant wading in, and wading in meant the reflections rose up around your legs like hands.

I went first, because Ilya's ribs were bad and I'd decided, privately and without consulting him, that I went first at dangerous things now. The water was cold but not cruel, knee-deep, and the moment my boots broke the surface the river gave me everything.

The valley-mother life unfolded around me in panels, like pages turning. The daughter — she had a name in the reflection, a name I felt rather than read, *Marisol*, and grief hit me sideways for a child I'd never had — grew up learning to carve from a mother who only carved for the living. No ghosts at my elbow. No dead murmuring their unfinished business into my ears at midnight. In the reflection I baked bread on festival days and my hands smelled of yeast instead of bone-dust, and when Marisol cried at night I could hold her without also hearing the drowned child of some other family whispering through the walls.

It was a good life. That was the unbearable part. It wasn't a fantasy; it was a *plausible* life, the one I'd have had if the blood in my family had run quiet instead of loud, and the river rendered it with a craftsman's care, every detail lovingly finished. In the third panel I was old, old and soft-handed, teaching Marisol's daughter to carve, and I was *happy*, and I stood in the freezing water of the Mirrored Ford and mourned that woman as though she had died, because in every way that mattered, she had. She'd never existed, and she'd never get to exist, and the river was the only place she lived, and I hated it, and I could not look away.

"Paloma." Ilya's voice, from behind me, strained. "Keep moving. Don't stand still in it."

"I'm mourning," I snapped. "Let me mourn."

"Mourn while walking. That's the rule. I'm serious — the pilgrims who never left are the ones who stood still. The river will keep showing you panels forever. It has a hundred years of material and no sense of an ending."

So I walked. Each step forward drowned one panel and raised another, and I made myself look at each one just long enough to grieve it properly, because I had spent my whole life doing grief-work for others and I would be damned if I'd shortchange myself. Goodbye, bread-smelling hands. Goodbye, Marisol. Goodbye, festival days. Goodbye, the me who was loved easily and simply, without prophecies attached.

Halfway across, a panel rose that stopped me mid-stride.

In it, I was still myself — bone-carver, hearer-of-the-dead — but Ilya was there. Not footnote-Ilya, not scholar-Ilya. *Our* Ilya, limping and joking and alive, standing in my workshop in the valley, arguing cheerfully with my grandmother's ghost about the proper angle for a memorial groove. The dead were there and welcome. The valley was there and open. Both lives braided into one, impossible, and the river offered it up like a merchant offering the one thing you can't afford, priced at exactly everything.

"That one's a lie," Ilya said hoarsely, behind me. Close — he'd caught up. "That one's not even a road not taken. That's a road that was never paved. Don't—"

"I know." My eyes were burning. "I know it's a lie. Let me look at it anyway. Some lies you need to see clearly so you can bury them properly. I'm a carver. I know how to bury things."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "All right. But I'm looking at mine too, then."

And his, when it came, was worse than the library. His was the two of us on the Steppe after the covenant was remade — after *some* covenant, any covenant — walking back through open boundary stones, both of us free, both of us whole, his sickness gone and my exile undone, and the river rendered it so tenderly that I heard him make a small sound, low in his throat, like something breaking politely.

"We can't have any of it," I said. "Can we."

"No." He said it steadily, and I loved him for the steadiness, and I hated the river for making me catalog the things I loved about him at knee-depth in unlived hours. "But knowing what we can't have — seeing it, naming it — that's different from never having looked. My order had a saying. Grief is the tax on love, paid in advance."

"Your order sounds exhausting."

"They were. Deeply. I miss them." He put his hand briefly on my shoulder — a touch that lasted two seconds and cost us both more than the whole river — and said, "Walk."

* * *

We came out the far side soaked to the thigh and silent, and sat on the bank wringing out our stockings in the pale afternoon light, and the silence was the good kind, the kind that comes after a storm has passed through a room and rearranged nothing but the air.

"She had a name," I said finally. "The girl. Marisol. Did yours have names too?"

"My students. Two of them. One of them had terrible handwriting and defended it as a philosophical position." He smiled at his own wet boots. "I'll miss him. Them. All of it. Properly, I mean — I think I just did, back there. Paid the tax."

"Good. Because I've decided something." I pulled my second stocking on, grimacing at the clamminess. "When this is done — the rite, the covenant, all of it — I'm going to come back to this ford. On purpose. And I'm going to look at every panel, all the way to the end of the road, and then I'm going to thank the river and leave. That's the difference between us and the pilgrims who never walked out. They came to drown in what they lost. I'm going to come to finish grieving it. There's a distinction. It matters."

Ilya looked at me for a long moment, water dripping off his hair, and something in his expression shifted — the way light shifts on glass when the angle changes.

"You know," he said, "the book has a passage about this ford. I skipped it, because it was in verse and the verse is always nonsense. But now I'm wondering." He swung the satchel around and drew the prophecy book out, and my stomach tightened the way it always did — the book that named tonight, that named his death, that had been right about everything so far with the smug patience of a century-old author.

He flipped to the marker he kept there — a strip of salt-cloth from Vess's court, which we no longer spoke about — and scanned the page. And I watched his face do something complicated.

"What," I said. "What is it."

"It's the verse." He read aloud, slowly:

*"Where the hours flow unlived, the carver shall see clear:
what she mourns, she may mend; what she mends, she must carve.
The ford is the first door. The chasm is the last.
Between them, the bone remembers what the river forgives."*

Silence, except for the chiming reeds.

"'What she mourns, she may mend,'" I repeated. My hands had gone cold, and not from the river. "Ilya. What does 'mend' mean? In covenant language. Precisely."

He looked up at me, and I saw the scholar and the errand boy and the dying man all arrive at the same thought at the same moment, and none of them liked it.

"In covenant law," he said carefully, "'to mend' means to restore something broken to its original terms. Or—" he swallowed, "—or to rewrite them. It's the root word of the whole rite, Paloma. *Mendare.* The founding verb. The ford isn't just showing us what we lost."

The reeds chimed. Downstream, far off, thunder muttered — or a city-mirage screamed, these days it was hard to tell the difference.

"It's telling you," Ilya said, "that the grief itself might be the material. That what you saw in that water — every life, every loss — the rite may want all of it carved in."

I looked back at the river. Somewhere in its silver silence, a woman with flour on her hands was laughing in a doorway, and a girl named Marisol was carving something clumsy and perfect, and they were waiting — patiently, the way the dead wait — to find out whether I would give them a shape, or let them stay unmade forever.

"Then we'd better reach the chasm," I said, standing, and slinging my pack over shoulders that suddenly felt like they belonged to someone else entirely — someone new, someone mid-carving. "Because I have a great deal of mourning to work with. And I intend to use every hour of it."
