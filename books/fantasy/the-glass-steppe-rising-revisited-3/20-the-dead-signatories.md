## 20. The Dead Signatories

The chasm announced itself long before we saw it.

I felt it first as a change in the ground's hum — a low note beneath my boots like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. Then Paloma stopped walking, tilted her head in that way she did when the dead got loud, and said, "It's arguing."

"Arguing?"

"The glass. The dead in it. They're not whispering anymore." She frowned at her own feet as if they'd betrayed her. "They're *debating*. Something about... whether to let us in."

"That seems rude," I said. "We've come such a long way."

"They're also," she added, with the particular dryness she reserved for moments when her gift and her common sense were having a disagreement, "arguing about you specifically."

"Wonderful."

"One of them thinks you look underfed."

I considered this. "One of them is correct."

She almost smiled — one of those quick, reluctant things she doled out like a miser counting coins — and then the ground dropped away.

* * *

The council-chasm was not a crack in the earth so much as a wound the earth had decided to keep. It opened before us like a mouth a mile wide, its walls sheer planes of fused glass descending into a blue-green dark, and every surface caught the light of the thirteenth-turn moon and threw it back wrong — bent, doubled, ancient. Looking down into it was like looking down into someone else's memory.

And there, suspended in the walls themselves, were the thirteen.

They slept in the glass the way flies sleep in amber, though no fly had ever looked so monumental. Each signatory stood or sat or knelt within their own column of glass, preserved mid-gesture: a woman with her hands raised as if catching something; an old man seated cross-legged, his chin on his chest; a figure whose armor had melted into the glass around them until metal and mineral were indistinguishable. Thirteen shapes, thirteen columns, arranged in a ring around the chasm's throat like stones around a hearth.

Paloma made a sound I'd never heard from her before. Not grief exactly. Recognition.

"I know them," she whispered. "Not their names. Their *voices*. I've been hearing fragments of them since the Salt Court — the arguing dead, the ones who claimed my grandmother's name. They're here. They've been here the whole time."

"The founders," I said. "The original signatories. The order's records call this place the Council-Chasm, but none of the records agree on where it is. Vess's maps put it three hundred leagues north." I looked at the impossible geometry of the horizon behind us, which had been doing increasingly creative things all day. "The Steppe moved us here. Or moved itself."

"Why would it do that?"

"Because," I said, and the words came out heavier than I intended, "it wants what we want. Or it wants what it was built to want. The covenant is a contract, Paloma. And contracts have terms. And terms have enforcement."

She gave me a look. "You could just say things plainly sometimes."

"I'm a messenger. Plainness is above my pay grade."

"You aren't paid."

"*Precisely.*"

But the joke landed soft between us and died there, because the chasm was humming louder now, and the thirteen shapes in the glass had begun — very faintly, very terribly — to glow.

* * *

We descended by a stair that shouldn't have existed: steps cut into the glass wall, each one singing a different note under our feet, so that going down sounded like someone playing a broken scale. The air grew warmer as we went, then cooler, then warm again, cycling through seasons in the space of a breath. The dead pressed close around us — not the valley's polite murmurs, not even the Steppe's argumentative factions, but something older and denser, a crowd at the door of a house that had been closed for a century.

Paloma walked with her hands over her ears for the first fifty steps. Then she took them away.

"It's too much to block," she said quietly. "So I'm letting it in instead." Her jaw was set in the way I'd learned meant she was frightened and refusing to be. "Tell me about the waking rite. All of it. Before we reach the bottom."

I told him — told *her*, I mean; my thoughts were already tangled — I told her everything the order had given me, which was less than I'd have liked.

"There are two parts. The first is location and approach — done, apparently, since the Steppe delivered us. The second is the Waking Question. The covenant's founding question has to be asked anew, aloud, at the foot of the thirteen. But it can't be asked by just anyone." I hesitated. "It has to be asked by a voice the dead trust."

"And the book says—"

"The book doesn't say. The book assumes. There's a line — I memorized it, because it kept me up nights — '*and the carver who hears shall ask, for the dead have waited a hundred years for ears.*'" I watched her face as it landed. "It's been you since page one, Paloma. Maybe since before page one. The order didn't send me to find a hero. They sent me to find *you*."

The stair-note under my boot cracked. Just a hairline fissure, singing flat. But Paloma had gone very still, the way she did when a ghost said something she didn't want repeated.

"A hundred years," she said. "That's how long my grandmother's been dead. That's how long the silence lasted." She looked down into the blue-green dark, where thirteen lights were brightening like coals being breathed upon. "They weren't silent because they couldn't speak. They were waiting for someone who could listen properly. And my grandmother — she broke the rite, she sabotaged the covenant, she bought the valley time — but she never came back to face them. She ran home and hid behind the boundary stones and raised a family that forgot everything." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. It went flatter. "And now the debt comes due, and it lands on me, because I'm the only one left with ears."

"Paloma—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me it isn't fair. I know it isn't fair. Fairness is for people with options." She started down the stairs again, faster now. "Come on, messenger. Let's go wake up thirteen ghosts and find out exactly how much trouble my bloodline is in."

* * *

The floor of the chasm was a lake of glass so clear it might have been air, and standing on it was like standing on nothing. Below our feet, far down, shapes drifted — older dead, deeper dead, sediment of centuries. Above us the thirteen columns rose in their ring, and now that we stood among them I could see details the distance had hidden: the woman with raised hands had bone beads braided through her hair, each one carved with a sigil I recognized from Paloma's grandmother's chest. The cross-legged man wore a carver's apron. Another figure — small, young, heartbreakingly young — held a half-finished bone in one hand, frozen mid-cut.

"They were smiths," Paloma breathed. "All of them. Look — tools, aprons, work-benches melted right into the glass. The covenant wasn't signed by kings. It was signed by *craftsmen*."

"Thirteen smiths," I agreed. "One from each realm that bordered the Steppe. The order's oldest text calls them 'the Hands.' Not rulers. Hands. The ones who did the work."

"And the price—" She stopped herself. We both knew about the price. A living smith bound into the glass each century. The reason her family fled. The reason the last messenger's bones rode in her pack.

The nearest column flickered.

It was subtle at first — a shimmer along the glass, like heat off a road — but then the woman inside it turned her head. Not quickly. With the terrible slow patience of someone who has waited a hundred years and intends to take another minute if she pleases. Her eyes found Paloma through six inches of solid glass, and her lips moved, and although no sound crossed, Paloma answered as if she'd heard every word.

"She says," Paloma translated, and her voice shook only slightly, "*'You have your grandmother's hands.'*"

"Do you?" I asked. "Have her hands, I mean. Literally."

"Everyone keeps asking me that." But some of the terror had gone out of her face, replaced by something steadier. "Yes. These are her tools. This is her craft. I suppose these are her hands in every way that matters."

Around the ring, one by one, the other twelve began to stir. The cross-legged man lifted his chin. The armored figure's gauntlet flexed inside its glass shroud. The young one — the child-smith — pressed one palm flat against the inner surface of their column, and where the palm touched, the glass fogged like a window breathed upon.

Paloma stepped forward into the center of the ring. I stayed at the edge, because this was hers, and because some animal part of me understood that the circle had a shape and a meaning and I was not one of its points.

"What do I ask?" she called softly, to all of them, to none of them. "The founding question. What is it? Nobody wrote it down?"

The woman with the bone beads smiled — a slow, sad, ancient smile — and spoke without sound, and Paloma listened, and listened, and then laughed once, short and disbelieving.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, of course. It's not written down because it's different every time." She turned to look at me, and the moonlight through the glass columns laid thirteen bands of light across her face. "There is no fixed question, Ilya. The question is whatever the moment demands. The thirteen ask it of whoever stands here, and the answer becomes the new covenant's first clause. My grandmother was supposed to answer it a hundred years ago. Instead she ran." She drew a breath. "Now it's mine."

"That's not pressure," I managed. "That's a guillotine with extra steps."

"Gentle humor, Ilya. Where's the gentle humor?"

"I'm saving it. I may need it more than you do."

But she wasn't listening anymore. She'd turned back to the thirteen, and she'd raised her hands — her carver's hands, her grandmother's hands — palms open, the way you approach something wild that you mean no harm to. The glass around her had begun to sing, all thirteen columns together, a chord so low I felt it in my teeth, in the old fracture in my ribs, in the messenger's brand on my wrist that had burned silently for a thousand miles and now, suddenly, went cool.

The dead of the whole Steppe had gone quiet. Every faction, every argument, every voice in the glass — silent. The entire world holding its breath around one woman in a ring of sleeping founders.

And the woman with the bone beads pressed her hand to the inside of her column, directly across from Paloma's raised palm, separated by a finger's width of glass and a hundred years of silence — and the glass began, slowly, to melt.

Not break. Melt. Softening like tallow, like ice in spring, opening outward from the point where the two palms nearly met. The other twelve columns followed, blooming open petal by petal, and the smell that came out was stone dust and woodsmoke and dried flowers and something underneath it all like rain on a roof — the smell, I realized, of a hundred years of held breath finally let go.

The first signatory stepped free of her glass.

Her boots crunched on the chasm floor as if she'd never stopped walking. She was tall, gray-haired, wearing the faded remains of a smith's coat, and her eyes — her eyes were the exact color of the deep glass, green-blue and lit from within. She looked at Paloma for a long moment. Then past her, at me, and her expression suggested she knew precisely what I was, precisely what I'd carried, and precisely whose bones were in Paloma's pack.

"You brought the book," she said. Her voice was rust and honey. "And the bones. And the heir." She turned back to Paloma. "Child. Before you ask anything, before you promise anything — there are things the book does not say. Things we could not write down, because paper can be stolen." Her gaze flicked, sharp and sudden, toward the chasm rim, toward the darkening sky beyond it, where a wind had begun to move that carried no scent at all.

"Hollow Wind," Paloma said.

"Coming," the signatory confirmed. "Faster than we hoped. Slower than we feared." She extended one weathered hand, palm up — an offer, or a summons, or perhaps simply the gesture of a craftsman asking to see another's work. "So. Carver who hears. You stand where thirteen stood. The question will come to you when it comes, and not before, and you will not get to choose it."

Paloma set her jaw. "And if my answer is wrong?"

The old woman's smile was gentle and utterly without comfort.

"Then we will all have a great deal of time," she said, "to discuss it."

Behind her, the twelfth and thirteenth columns opened, and the youngest signatory — the child with the half-finished bone — stepped out onto the glass, blinking, and looked up at Paloma with eyes a hundred years old.

"We heard you coming," the child said. "For three days. Through the soles of the world." A pause, and then, with the devastating directness of the very young: "Are you really going to change the ending this time?"

The wind at the rim of the chasm rose to a howl.

Paloma reached into her pack, and closed her hand around her grandmother's sigil, and said, "Watch me."
