## 21. Thirteen Ghosts, One Question

The chasm breathed.

That was the only word for it. The air rose and fell in long slow swells, warm and mineral-sweet, as though the whole council-chasm were the chest of something sleeping — which, I supposed, it was. Thirteen somethings.

Ilya stood at my shoulder, close enough that I could feel him not-quite-touching me, the way he'd learned to stand near a bone-carver who spent her life listening to things other people couldn't hear. He didn't say anything. He was getting better at that too.

Below us, set into the glass floor of the chasm like flies in amber, lay the signatories. Thirteen figures, each sealed in a column of fused glass taller than a house, each one shaped mid-gesture — one with hands raised as if in blessing, one curled small as a child hiding from thunder, one standing so straight and severe she might have been carved rather than born. Their faces were closed. Their eyes, when the light moved through the glass just so, were open.

"They're waiting for you," Ilya said quietly. "They've been waiting a hundred years."

"Everyone keeps telling me that lately." I flexed my fingers inside my gloves. My grandmother's gloves, worn soft as moth wings at the fingertips. "It's very flattering and I hate it."

He laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed. But he laughed. That mattered. Down here, where the dead argued loud enough to shake grit off the glass ledges, laughter felt like carrying a candle through a windstorm.

I climbed down.

* * *

The glass underfoot sang as I descended the switchback path cut into the chasm wall. Not the way valley stone hums under a mason's chisel — this was older, deeper, a note held so long it had forgotten it was a note. With every step the thirteen shapes grew, until by the time I reached the floor they loomed over me like a forest of frozen lightning.

The dead of the Steppe had followed us down. They crowded the upper ledges in their hundreds — Vess's salt-pale courtiers, the ragged ghosts of drowned cities, the thin ones who had been arguing since we arrived about whose fault everything was. They had gone quiet now. All of them. A silence so total I could hear my own heart doing its foolish mortal work.

I took out the bone.

It was the half-finished sigil from Grandmother's chest, the one that matched the book's diagrams, and I had worked on it every night since the ford — adding grooves, deepening lines, learning its grain the way I'd learn any piece before carving. It was warm against my palm. Bones usually cool to the touch. This one refused.

"I don't know the rite," I said aloud, because it seemed important to be honest with thirteen people who had died for their honesty. "The book's last page is torn. Half the words are gone. My grandmother—" My voice caught on her name, the way it always did now, anger and forgiveness tangled together like two threads I could no longer separate. "My grandmother made sure of that. So if you're expecting someone who knows what she's doing, you've waited a century for the wrong woman."

The glass columns did not answer.

But the light inside them changed. It thickened, went gold, then white, and the first figure — the one with raised hands — opened her mouth behind the glass, and the chasm filled with a voice like a bell struck underwater.

**"Carver."**

Every ghost on the ledges flinched. So did I.

"You woke us," the voice said. "The covenant is broken, the moon turns, and the land itself has begun to die. Before anything else — before rite or renewal or sacrifice — the question must be asked again. As it was asked at the founding. Answer it wrong, and no covenant will hold, however cleverly carved. Answer it right, and even a broken law can be mended."

I looked up at the shape in the glass. Her face was kind and terrible, the way faces are when they have outlived everything soft about them.

"What question?" I asked, though some part of me already knew. Some part of me had been hearing it in the murmur of the valley dead for years, underneath all their small requests — *carve my name, remember my dog, tell my daughter I forgave her.* Underneath everything, the same question, asked and re-asked by everyone who had ever died.

The thirteen spoke together, and the chasm rang:

**"What is survival worth?"**

* * *

Silence again. The kind that waits.

I am a bone-carver. People bring me their grief and I make it into something they can hold, and in eleven years of practice I have learned that the worst thing you can do with a hard question is rush it. So I stood there in the breathing dark and let the question settle into me like sediment into water.

And the first ghost answered before I could.

She was third from the left, the one curled small as a child, and when she spoke her voice came out cracked and young. **"Nothing,"** she said. **"Survival is worth nothing. I know because I bought mine with my sister's name. When the plague came to my city, the covenant needed a death freely given, and I wrote *her* name instead of mine, and I lived ninety more years, and every one of them tasted of ash. I signed the covenant to bind that bargain forever, so that no one could ever do what I did again without paying. Survival bought by another's ruin isn't survival. It's just a slower way of dying."**

The ghosts on the ledges murmured. Somewhere above, Vess's voice said something sharp and low, and the murmuring stopped.

The second column lit — the tall severe woman, standing straight as a blade. **"Everything,"** she said simply. **"Survival is worth everything, and I will not apologize for saying so. I was a farmer. The Steppe was drowning in the first breaking, and the old law demanded my harvest, my herds, my children's inheritance — all of it, thrown into the glass to keep the sky stitched shut. I gave it. I gave it gladly, and then I came here and gave myself, because a world with nothing alive in it is worth precisely nothing at all. You cannot carve a covenant out of principles, carver. You carve it out of the stubborn animal fact of being alive and wanting to stay that way."**

"That's not an answer," I heard myself say. "Those are opposite answers."

**"Yes,"** said all thirteen, and the sound of it made my teeth ache.

* * *

They came one after another after that, and I understood slowly that this was the rite's true beginning — not words in a book, but this: the question, and the answering, and the weighing.

The fourth ghost had been a ferryman. His column showed him mid-stroke, oars forever pulling. **"Worth one crossing,"** he said. **"No more than you'd pay for any passage. I carried souls between the living shore and the glass for sixty years, and I learned that people pay too much for survival and too little for arrival. Worth one crossing. Pay it and move on. Don't stand on the bank bargaining your whole life away for a boat ride."**

The fifth had been a mother; I knew it before she spoke, the way I always know. Her answer was four words: **"Whatever my children need."** And then, softer, almost embarrassed: **"Which is sometimes less than I wanted to give and more than they wanted to take. Worth exactly that. No formula survives contact with your actual child."**

Despite everything, despite the dead and the dying sky and the thirteenth turn bearing down on us like a wave, I smiled. She sounded like Mrs. Okonkwo from the valley, who used to say love was mostly logistics with occasional miracles.

The sixth ghost had been a soldier, and his column was scarred, pitted like old armor. **"Worth less than honor,"** he said, **"and I died learning it too late. Ask yourself instead what survival is *for*. A thing kept past its purpose rots. A thing given its purpose endures. Keep the world alive, carver, but ask it what it's alive *to do*."**

The seventh had been a singer. Her voice, when it came, was the most beautiful thing I had ever heard, and it was ruined — hoarse, broken, gorgeous the way a cracked bell is gorgeous. **"Worth one song,"** she rasped. **"One true one. I traded my voice to the glass to seal the second clause, and I have regretted it every day for a hundred years, and I would do it again tomorrow. Both things are true. Hold both. Anyone who tells you sacrifice comes clean wasn't paying attention while it happened."**

The eighth was the one who frightened me. His column was empty-looking, his shape faint as breath on a window, and his voice was barely there at all. **"Worth forgetting,"** he whispered. **"I was the archivist. I wrote down every term, every price, every name. And I found, at the end, that the ones who survived best were the ones who let themselves forget what it cost. I could not forget. It is why I am so faint. Do not build your new law on memory alone, carver. Build in mercy for those who need to put the burden down."**

I thought of the valley dead, murmuring their small requests, asking to be remembered — and how some of them, the oldest ones, only ever asked to be *left alone*. I had thought it strange. I didn't anymore.

* * *

By the ninth ghost, my legs had gone numb and Ilya had crept down the switchbacks to sit beside me on the cold glass, and neither of us mentioned it.

The ninth had been a twin. **"Worth sharing,"** she said. **"My brother and I signed as one soul in two bodies. The old law accepted it. Remember that, carver, whatever else you forget — the law itself once allowed the price to be divided. It has been done. It can be done again."**

Something in my chest turned over. I thought of a prophecy naming a man's death, and a woman who had been quietly, desperately looking for a loophole for weeks, and I did not look at Ilya. I looked very carefully at the tenth column instead.

The tenth had been a liar — she said so herself, cheerfully, the first honest liar I'd ever met. **"Worth a good story,"** she declared. **"I cheated death three times with nothing but a quick tongue, and when the fourth bill came due I paid it laughing, because a story where everyone lives forever isn't a story, it's a ledger. Make sure whatever you build leaves room for people to surprise you. Especially the dead. *Especially* the dead."**

Vess, on the ledge above, made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

The eleventh had been a healer, and her answer was the longest. **"Worth measuring,"** she said. **"Not in coin or glory but in mornings. How many mornings does the price buy? For whom? I have watched healers burn themselves to ash saving one patient while a hundred wait, and I have watched them hoard their strength and lose them all. There is no clean arithmetic. But refuse to do the arithmetic at all, and you will spend everything on the loudest grief instead of the greatest need. Count, carver. Count, and then forgive yourself for the counting."**

The twelfth had been young. Younger than me. His column still had the awkward angles of a boy not finished growing, and his voice cracked exactly the way living boys' voices crack, which nearly undid me. **"I don't know,"** he said. **"Is that allowed? Everyone else got these wise answers and I just — I didn't want to die. I was scared. I signed because they needed a thirteenth and I was there, and I have spent a hundred years wondering if that makes me a coward or just a person. Maybe survival is worth being afraid of losing. Maybe that's the whole answer. Maybe anyone who says otherwise is showing off."**

The soldier's column flickered, affronted. The singer's rasped something that was probably a laugh.

And then the thirteenth column began to glow.

* * *

I had saved it for last without meaning to — or perhaps I had meant to, some carver's instinct keeping the hardest piece for the end, the way you leave the finest detail until your hand is steadiest. The thirteenth signatory stood in her glass with her arms crossed and her chin lifted, and even sealed in a hundred years of silence she radiated the specific energy of a woman who has been interrupted.

Her voice, when it came, was dry as old paper and twice as sharp.

**"Worth renegotiating,"** she said. **"Every generation. Every turn of the moon, if necessary. I was the youngest of the original drafters, and I watched my elders write terms they assumed would be eternal, and I told them — I *told* them — that no law survives unchanged, because the people under it change. They called me impatient. Then they all died sealing their perfect eternal covenant, and here we are, a hundred years later, undone because the world moved and the law did not."** Her eyes fixed on me through the glass, and I swear the dead woman smirked. **"You are going to break our beautiful law and write a worse one, carver. It will have flaws we cannot imagine and your grandchildren will curse you for. Good. That means it's alive. A covenant that cannot be argued with is a cage. Write a door into yours."**

The chasm exhaled. All around us, the thirteen lights dimmed to embers, and the hundreds of lesser ghosts on the ledges stirred like wind through wheat.

"The question," the first signatory said, gentler now, "has been asked and answered. Thirteen answers, none of them agreeing, all of them true. That is the founding condition, carver. Not consensus. *Disagreement, held together.* The old covenant failed because it demanded one price from everyone. Yours must hold many prices in one hand without crushing any of them."

"And if I can't?" I asked. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "If I can't hold all of it?"

"Then the moon finishes its turn," said the boy, the twelfth, the honest one, "and everything unravels, and honestly? At least we'll all be equally embarrassed."

A ripple of ghostly laughter ran through the ledges — real laughter, rusty and startled out of them, the first I'd heard from the Steppe's dead that wasn't argument. Even Vess's courtiers cracked. Above us, the chasm's rim glowed faintly against the bruised sky, where the moon hung one turn short of complete.

I looked down at the sigil-bone in my hands. Thirteen answers sat in it now, or would, once I carved them — *nothing, everything, one crossing, whatever they need, less than honor, one song, forgetting, shared, a good story, measured in mornings, I don't know, renegotiate.* A law built out of disagreement. A door written into the walls.

"Well," I said, standing on legs that shook only a little, "I should get started. It's a lot of carving and I only have the one pair of hands."

"You have ours," said the singer, hoarse and warm.

"All of them," said the ferryman.

"Metaphorically," the liar added. "We're ghosts. Mostly useless for manual labor."

I was still smiling at that — actually smiling, down in the belly of the world with the dead all around me — when Ilya touched my elbow.

"Paloma." His voice had gone carefully flat, the way it did when he was frightened and didn't want me to know. "Where's the book?"

I turned. The satchel where we kept it — the prophecy book, the torn page tucked safe in oilcloth, the thing three hunters and a ghost-queen and a storm-witch had failed to take from us — lay open on the glass.

Empty.

And somewhere far above us, at the rim of the chasm, a shape slipped away into the dark, moving with the particular silence of someone who had been sitting among us, listening to every answer, for a very long time.
