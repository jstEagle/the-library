## 22. The Third Turn and the Treachery

Ilya had learned, in the weeks since crossing the boundary stones, to read the Steppe's moods the way a sailor reads weather. The glass sang differently before each moon-turn — a high, thin keening like wet fingers on a bowl's rim — and tonight it was singing early.

He sat up in their camp beneath the ribs of a fallen mirage-arch, some drowned city's gate frozen mid-collapse in the glass, and listened. Paloma slept with her back against his pack, her carving knife still in her hand even now, even in sleep. She'd told him once that she'd never once put it down since they crossed. *You don't stop listening to the dead just because you're tired,* she'd said. *They take it personally.*

The keening rose. Fell. Rose again.

"Third turn," he said quietly, to no one, and felt the words settle into his chest like stones dropped into a well.

Three turns gone. Ten remaining. The turns were compressing — the first had taken a full week of walking, the second four days, and this one, by his count of the moon's position through the arch's broken crown, had come in barely two. The Steppe was running out of patience. Or perhaps it was simply that everything magical ran faster as it approached its ending, the way a candle gutters brightest just before it drowns in its own wax.

Paloma stirred. She didn't wake — not fully — but her lips moved, and he caught the shape of a name in them that wasn't his. Her grandmother's, maybe. Or one of the thirteen. She talked in her sleep now, more every night. The dead of the Steppe didn't whisper politely at the edges of her hearing the way the valley's honored dead had. They *argued*, and apparently they argued right on through until morning.

He should have woken her for her watch an hour ago. He hadn't. He'd been reading the book again.

*The Book That Names Tonight.* He'd started calling it that in his head because its true name — the *Codex of the Thirteenth Signatory* or whatever the order's scholars had called it — was too grand for what it actually was: a water-stained, thumb-worn thing that smelled of tallow and old panic. A century ago, someone had written down everything: the dates, the turns, the rite, the price. And him. His arrival, dated to the day. His death, dated too.

He knew where the page was. He'd known since the Salt Court, since Vess had smiled at him with teeth like frost and recited his fate back at him as though offering him tea. He'd read it eleven times since. He could recite it. He could probably carve it from memory, if he had Paloma's hands, which he did not, which was rather the point of her.

*Ilya Vaschenko, messenger of the Order of the Last Page, dies at the rite of renewal, his blood the ink, his breath the bellows—*

He'd stopped reading past that line after the fourth time. Not because it frightened him. Because he'd volunteered knowing, and there was a kind of cowardice in flinching at your own signature.

But tonight he'd read further. Tonight, with the third turn keening overhead and Paloma murmuring names in her sleep, he'd turned past his own death — past it, the way you step over a body on a road because looking would slow you down — and read the rite itself. All of it. Every clause, every invocation, every word the carver must speak while the bone takes the covenant's weight.

And he had discovered something that made his stomach drop through the glass and keep falling.

The rite was long. Not long the way a sermon is long. Long the way a river is long — branching, recursive, folding back on itself, clauses nested inside clauses like the rings of a tree. No one could hold it in memory. No one was meant to. The order had assumed the book would travel with the carver, open on her knee, consulted line by line as the moon completed its turn. It had never occurred to whoever wrote this that the book might be anything less than eternal.

Ilya looked at the sleeping woman who was supposed to speak those words, and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only at the worst possible moments, that they were all going to die of a footnote.

* * *

He woke her gently, which is to say he said her name twice and then, when that failed, held the water skin under her nose until she surfaced swearing.

"You let me sleep through my watch."

"The dead kept you company. I didn't want to interrupt."

"They're terrible company." She sat up, rubbed her face with both hands, and then went still, the way she went still when one of them spoke to her. "What?"

"Nothing."

"Ilya." She used his name like a scalpel. "Your face is doing the thing it does when you've read something you haven't told me about."

"My face doesn't do things."

"Your face has been doing things since the Salt Court. I hear the dead, remember? But I don't need to. You're loud enough for both of us." She held out her hand. "Give me the book."

And here is where Ilya Vaschenko, messenger of the Order of the Last Page, who had walked nine hundred miles bleeding light instead of blood, who had outargued a ghost-queen and outrun hunters made of hollow wind — here is where he hesitated.

Not long. A heartbeat. Two.

But a heartbeat is a very long time when you are being watched by someone who has spent her whole life reading what people carry underneath their words.

"That's what I thought," Paloma said softly, and took the book out of his pack herself.

She read. He watched her read, and watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out. When she reached the rite, her finger stopped moving down the page. When she reached the end of the rite, her finger lifted off the paper entirely, as though the words had burned it.

"It can't be memorized," she said.

"No."

"How many clauses?"

"Two hundred and six. Some of them contradict each other. There's a section in the middle that's written backwards — the order's scholars think it has to be spoken in reverse, but nobody's sure whether you reverse the letters or the word order, and the man who wrote the footnote died before clarifying."

"He *died* before clarifying."

"He was a hundred and forty years old, Paloma. He clarified most of it."

She closed the book and pressed it flat against her chest, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the glass keened above them, patient as arithmetic.

"We'll manage," she said finally. "We'll copy it. Carve the clauses into bone as we walk — my bones, spare stock, whatever I have. If the words are in the covenant-bone already, maybe the rite will—"

"Maybe."

"Don't 'maybe' me. Everything we've done since the stones has been 'maybe.' Maybe the sigil opens the canyon. Maybe Vess keeps her word. Maybe the dead will sign. We are a traveling festival of maybes, Ilya, and we are still alive, so forgive me if I add one more."

It was such a Paloma speech — fierce and practical and shot through with a humor she deployed like armor — that he almost laughed, and almost told her everything. About the page. About the date. About how the prophecy named his death not as a possibility but as a *clause*, itemized, load-bearing.

He didn't. Because she was smiling at him, tired and real, and because he had exactly one piece of good news left to give her — *the rite can be rewritten; the carver's hands make the law* — and he intended to save it for the moment she needed it most.

Later, he would understand that this was the moment. This was the exact moment. And he would hate himself for it with a thoroughness that surprised even him.

* * *

The hunter came at dawn, or what passed for dawn on the Steppe — the hour when the trapped cities inside the glass brightened from grey to gold, their mirage-windows catching light that had nowhere else to go.

Ilya woke to birdsong.

That was the wrongness, though it took his sleep-drugged mind a moment to find it. There were no birds on the Glass Steppe. There had been no birds since the covenant sealed the plain in glass. The Hollow Wind's hunters didn't sing — they *whistled*, a sound like wind through a keyhole, like the last breath of something that had faked its death a century ago and never stopped running.

But this was birdsong. Sweet, rising, three notes and a trill. And it was coming from the ridge above their camp, where a small brown bird perched on a shard of glass, cocking its head at him with an expression of tremendous friendliness.

Paloma was gone.

No — not gone. Her bedroll was empty, her pack still there, her knife — her knife was *there*, lying in the dust beside her bedroll, which she had not done once, not one single night since they'd crossed the stones. She'd gone to relieve herself, or to speak with the dead privately, or—

The bird sang again. Three notes and a trill. And Ilya, whose order had taught him the signs of the Steppe until they lived in his marrow, finally heard what the song actually was.

A counting song. An old one. Children in the mortal realms still sang it skipping rope.

*One for sorrow, two for mirth—*

He was moving before the verse finished, snatching up his pack, and his hand found the place where the book rode against his spine, the place it had ridden for nine hundred miles, the place it had ridden *this morning* when he'd checked it at the changing of the watch, because he always checked it, because checking it was the last thing he did before he slept and the first thing he did when he woke—

Except last night he hadn't. Last night he'd read the rite instead, and then he'd woken Paloma late, and then he'd lain down with the book still in his hands, and somewhere between the second clause and the birdsong, he had slept.

The pack was light.

The bird on the shard of glass spread its wings, and the wings were wrong — too wide, too pale, feathers like strips of fog — and it sang the counting song one more time, all the way through, ending on the verse no children ever reached:

*—and none for the messenger, who slept.*

Then it folded inward like a closing hand and was gone, and the whistling came from everywhere at once, fading northward, unhurried, mocking.

Ilya stood in the middle of their camp holding an empty pack, and screamed. Not words. Just sound. It came out of him raw and animal and it echoed off the mirage-arch and came back to him sounding, horribly, like laughter.

* * *

Paloma found him like that. To her credit, she didn't ask what happened. She looked at the empty pack, then at his face, and said, "How long?"

"I don't know. An hour. Less. I fell asleep with it in my hands, I was going to check it, I always check it—"

"But you didn't."

"But I didn't."

She sat down on the glass. Not gracefully. She just sat, the way people sit when a floor gives out, and put her face in her hands, and for a long time the only sound was the keening of the third turn winding down toward silence, and the distant, dwindling whistle of something carrying a century of prophecy north to the woman who had faked her death to escape it.

"Two hundred and six clauses," Paloma said at last, into her hands.

"Yes."

"Backwards section."

"Yes."

"A footnote written by a dead man."

"Yes."

"And the Hollow Wind has the book." She lowered her hands. Her eyes were dry, which somehow was worse than tears. "Ilya. Tell me you copied some of it. Tell me you at least—"

"I know the opening invocation. Forty-one clauses. I read them enough times that they're—" He tapped his temple. "In here. Imperfectly. Out of order, possibly."

"Forty-one."

"Forty-one."

"Out of two hundred and six." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, bright and cracked, the laugh of someone standing on a ledge deciding whether it was funnier to jump or to sit down. "One-fifth of a river. We have one-fifth of a river and the rest of it is in the claws of a bird made of fog, flying toward the person who *broke the covenant in the first place*—"

"She didn't break it. Her grandmother broke it. Broke the *rite*. The Hollow Wind just... benefited."

"Oh, thank you. Thank you for that distinction. That makes me feel *so much better*—"

"Paloma—"

"No. You don't get to 'Paloma' me right now." She stood up, and there it was, the anger, arriving the way it always arrived with her — late, enormous, and aimed with terrible precision. "Because here's what I keep circling back to, Ilya. Here's the thing. You've known about the rite for weeks. You've known it couldn't be memorized. You've known — you *told me yourself*, at the ford, at the campfires, all those nights — that the book was the whole plan. So why didn't we copy it? Why didn't you say, on day one, *carver, sit down, we're spending three days carving these clauses into bone*? Why am I only hearing about the backwards section *now*?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question. Because the answer was the page. Because the answer was that he'd rationed the truth like a miser rationing bread, saving his confession for the moment of maximum usefulness, and in doing so had saved nothing at all.

"Because I was afraid," he said. It wasn't the whole truth. It was the part of the truth he could bear to stand inside. "Because if I told you the whole rite, you'd start asking about the price, and the price is—"

He stopped.

Paloma waited. The Steppe waited. Even the keening seemed to lean in.

"The price is named," he finished quietly. "In the book. Specifically. By name."

"Whose?"

He looked at her, and she looked at him, and he saw the moment she worked it out — saw it land, saw her decide not to ask again, saw her file it away in that relentless ledger she kept behind her eyes. Not now. Later. When it could be faced.

"Later," she agreed aloud, which frightened him more than shouting would have. Then she picked up her carving knife from the dust, turned it over once, and pointed it north, toward the dwindling whistle, toward the canyon country where the fossil giants slept and the Hollow Wind nested.

"Forty-one clauses," she said. "That's what we have. So here's what happens next. We go get the other hundred and sixty-five. And Ilya?" She sheathed the knife with a snap. "When we find whoever stole it — the bird, the wind, the woman, whichever face it's wearing — you stand behind me. Because I have spent my entire life being polite to ghosts, and I am *done*."

She started walking north without waiting for him, and the glass rang under her boots like a struck bell, and Ilya stood alone for one more moment among the ruins of his careful plans, holding his guilt the way he'd held the book — close, and uselessly, and much too late.

Then he followed her, because following her was the only plan he had left.

Behind them, faint and fading, the third turn finished its keening. Seven remained. And somewhere ahead, in a canyon of fossil giants, something that had once been a signatory opened the stolen book to the last page, and began — patiently, hungrily — to read.
