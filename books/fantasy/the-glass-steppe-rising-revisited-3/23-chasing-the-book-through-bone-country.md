## 23. Chasing the Book Through Bone Country

The thief left tracks in the glass, which was either arrogance or stupidity, and Paloma had learned by now that the Hollow Wind was neither. It was invitation.

"Look at this," she said, crouching over a print pressed into the fused plain — a boot-shaped depression ringed with frost, though the morning was warm enough that Ilya had rolled his sleeves to the elbow. "Frost. In summer. On glass."

"That's not frost," Ilya said, kneeling beside her. His voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was about to say something he'd rather not say. "That's *absence*. The Hollow Wind eats warmth as it passes. Where it walks, the world forgets heat ever touched it."

"Wonderful. So we're following a trail of forgetting."

"We're following a trail of *theft*," Ilya corrected, and then winced, because it was his theft too, technically. The book had been under his cloak when the hunter came into their camp wearing Vess's salt-gray face and asking for water. Ilya had given the water. He'd also, in his own words, "been an absolute idiot about it," which was the most honest self-assessment Paloma had heard from anyone in years, living or dead.

She didn't blame him. She'd told him so twice, once sincerely and once at volume, and both times he'd accepted it like a man accepting a sentence he believed he deserved, which annoyed her more than the theft did.

The tracks led north, toward a line on the horizon that wasn't a line at all but a wall of ribs.

Paloma had seen them from a distance for two days and assumed they were cliffs. Up close, they were unmistakably ribs — curved arcs of bone taller than the boundary stones back home, bleached white-gold, rising out of the glass plain in a row that stretched farther than her eye could follow. Between them hung smaller bones: vertebrae the size of cottages, jaw fragments like fallen gates, a skull so vast that a whole village could have sheltered in its shadow and complained about the draft.

"Fossil giants," Ilya said quietly. "The first things that ever lived on the Steppe, before it was glass. When the covenant was struck, everything old enough to remember a world without magic was... preserved. Not killed. *Kept.*"

"Kept where?"

He pointed up. Paloma followed his finger and saw, threaded through the rib-canyons, shapes moving against the sky. Long, slow shapes. Like kites made of smoke, if kites could sulk.

"The Hollow Wind nests there," Ilya said. "In the canyon of the kept-dead. Which is exactly where we're going."

"Good," said Paloma.

Ilya looked at her. "Good?"

"You heard me." She stood, brushing glass-dust from her knees, and felt something settle in her chest that she recognized from her workbench at home — the particular steadiness that arrived when a difficult piece finally showed her how it wanted to be carved. For three days since the theft she had been helpless, furious, hollowed out. The rite's wording lived in that book. Without it, the thirteenth turn would arrive and find them standing in a field holding good intentions and nothing else.

But helplessness, she was discovering, was mostly a habit. And habits could be broken the same way anything else could: with the right tool, applied at the right angle.

"The Hollow Wind wants us to come," she said. "It sent one hunter; now it's leaving footprints wide enough for a funeral procession. That means it thinks it has already won. And people who think they've won get careless about who walks through their front door."

"You're planning to walk through its front door."

"I'm planning to *negotiate* through its front door," she said. "There's a difference. One gets you killed. The other gets you killed politely, after tea."

A sound escaped Ilya that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed between them lately. "You know," he said, "when the order chose me, they told me the carver who hears would be gentle. Patient. A quiet woman from a quiet valley."

"I am extremely quiet," Paloma said. "Ask anyone. Ask the dead. They complain constantly that I don't talk enough."

* * *

The entrance to the canyon was a gap between two ribs wide enough for four people abreast, and the moment they stepped into its shadow, the temperature dropped as though they'd waded into a river of winter. Paloma's breath fogged. The dead of the Steppe, who had been murmuring around her like wind through wheat since they'd crossed the stones, went suddenly, deliberately silent — the way a room goes silent when someone important enters.

"They're afraid," she murmured.

"Of the Wind?"

"Of *here*." She laid her palm against the nearest rib. It was warm. That was the wrongness of it — everything else in this frozen place was cold, but the bone beneath her hand held a deep, patient warmth, like a hearth banked for the night. And through that warmth she felt them: presences so old and so large that calling them ghosts was like calling the ocean a puddle. They weren't whispering. They were *dreaming*, and their dreams moved slowly as glaciers, and somewhere inside those dreams was a memory of grass.

"They were alive before the covenant," she breathed. "Before magic was bound at all. They're not signatories. They're older than signing."

"Don't wake them," Ilya said, very softly. "The order's records say the giants dream the Steppe's shape. If one of them wakes mid-dream—"

"—the Steppe changes shape. Yes. Thank you. I do occasionally read the terrifying documents you carry." She took her hand off the rib with exaggerated care, the way she'd lift a half-carved femur off a client's table. "Which way?"

The hunter's tracks led inward, deeper, past rib after rib, each arch framing a view of more bone beyond it, until Paloma lost all sense of the canyon's shape. It wasn't a canyon, she realized. It was a *body*. They were walking through the chest cavity of something that had died — or been kept — so long ago that geography had grown over it. Frost ferns bloomed on the inner walls. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm almost like speech.

Almost like speech. She stopped.

"It's talking," she said.

"What is?"

"The dripping. Listen." She closed her eyes and let the sound resolve the way she let whispers resolve at home — by leaning her attention into it without grabbing. The drips separated into syllables. The syllables arranged themselves, grudgingly, into words:

*—turn back — turn back — turn back—*

"A warning?" Ilya asked.

"Or directions," Paloma said. "Depends how you read it. Turn back from what? Or turn back *to*?" She opened her eyes. "Either way, someone here bothered to learn drip-language just to talk to visitors. That's hospitality. Rude hospitality, but hospitality."

"You have a very generous definition of rude."

"I grew up with the honored dead of a valley that pretends nothing happened a hundred years ago," she said. "My standards for rudeness are extremely well calibrated."

They followed the dripping. It led them down a side passage — a space between two vertebrae, ceilinged by the arch of a spinal column — and opened without warning into a chamber where the frost stopped.

Not melted. *Stopped*, in mid-bloom, every ice-fern hanging complete and motionless in air that no longer moved. In the center of the chamber, on a shelf of bone worn smooth as a pew, sat the prophecy book.

Just sitting there. Open. Waiting.

"Oh, that's a trap," Ilya said.

"Obviously it's a trap."

"It's such an obvious trap."

"Yes," Paloma agreed, walking toward it. "And yet."

"*Yet?*" He caught her sleeve. "Paloma. The last time someone walked toward an obvious thing in this canyon, according to my order's records, they were never recorded again. There isn't even a record of there being no record. That's how thorough the not-recording was."

She looked at his hand on her sleeve, then at him. "Ilya. Think about what the Wind actually wants. It wants the covenant to fail. It wants me to give up, or die, or turn around. It does *not* want me clever. So when it leaves the book sitting in the open in the middle of its own home—" she tapped her temple— "it's betting I'm too frightened or too proud to take it. Frightened, because traps. Proud, because taking bait feels like losing."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong in interesting new surroundings," she said, "instead of right in a freezing hallway. I've spent my whole life choosing the second option. I'm due."

She picked up the book.

The chamber exhaled.

Every suspended ice-fern shivered at once, and the cold rushed back in like a held breath released, and from somewhere above them — far above, up through the vertebrae and the ribs and the dreaming dark — came a sound that Paloma felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears. A voice. Not the Wind itself, she thought. Something that served it. Something that lived here, among the kept-dead, and had opinions about guests.

"*Who carries the smith-mark into the house of the kept?*"

The words came from everywhere, in a voice like dry leaves settling. Ilya went pale. Paloma looked down at her own hands, at the fine white scars across her knuckles from thirty years of carving, and understood.

The sigil. Her grandmother's sigil, the half-finished bone medallion she'd carried against her sternum since the chest in the valley. It had warmed when she entered the canyon — she'd noticed, filed it away, kept walking. Now it burned like a coal.

"She did," Paloma said aloud, to the chamber, to whatever listened. "My grandmother. Sera Reyes. Covenant-smith. And I'm her granddaughter, and I've come to finish what she started."

Silence. Long enough that Ilya's breath fogged eleven separate times.

Then the leaf-voice again, closer now, and with something in it that might have been curiosity, or hunger, or both: "*Sera of the valley. The runaway. The breaker.*"

"The letter-writer," Paloma said. "The refuser. The woman who wouldn't bind another child to your glass." She lifted the sigil from its cord and held it up, and the half-finished lines of it caught the strange light and threw them back doubled. "She broke your rite and bought a hundred years, and in a hundred years none of you thought to finish the argument. So I'm here to finish it. But I'll be honest with you, because I find honesty saves time: I can't finish anything while your hunters are stealing my books and your master is sending storms. So here are my terms."

Ilya made a small sound beside her, the sound of a man watching someone juggle knives he hadn't known were in play.

"I want passage through this canyon to wherever the Wind keeps its council," Paloma said. "Unmolested. My companion untouched. In exchange, when I rewrite the covenant — and I will rewrite it, that part isn't negotiable — the kept-dead of this canyon get a clause. Their names in the new law. Recognition. Whatever a dreaming giant counts as respect, they'll have it in writing, in bone, signed."

More silence. Then, impossibly, she felt it — the attention of the canyon shifting, the way a room shifts when an old argument is reopened. Through the warm bone under her feet, faint as a pulse, the giants' dreams turned toward her.

"*Terms,*" the leaf-voice repeated. It sounded almost amused. "*The runaway's blood comes back offering terms.*"

"Not blood," Paloma said. "Craft. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire point."

Another silence. Then the ice-ferns along the walls began, very slowly, to melt — not all at once but in sequence, a path opening through the chamber and onward, deeper into the canyon, marked by falling water that spelled, in drip-language, a single word over and over:

*—come — come — come—*

"That's either an invitation," Ilya whispered, "or a menu."

"With my luck lately," Paloma said, slinging the book's strap over her shoulder and setting off down the melting path, "probably both. Keep up."

They walked for hours through the warming dark, past rib-arches where frost gave way to a pale moss that glowed faintly, past alcoves where smaller fossils curled like sleeping dogs — creatures kept so long they'd become furniture for the canyon's ghostly household. Once, something enormous shifted overhead, and a rain of ancient dust sifted down, and both of them froze until the dreaming bulk settled again. Once, Paloma caught Ilya smiling at nothing and asked him about it, and he said, "You negotiated with a canyon," with the tone of a man cataloguing wonders, and she pretended not to be pleased and was pleased anyway.

Near what she judged must be late afternoon — the canyon kept its own weather and its own clocks, and both were lies — the path opened onto a ledge overlooking a vast hollow at the canyon's heart. And there, at last, she saw them: figures gathered on a floor of black glass below, robed in moving absence, a dozen of them, still as furniture. And above them, coiling down through a wound in the canyon's roof, the Hollow Wind itself poured into the chamber like smoke returning to a bottle.

At the center of the gathering, on a throne of interlocked vertebrae, sat a figure that did not move with the wind but against it. As Paloma watched, the figure raised its head — and though the distance was impossible, though the light was wrong, she felt its gaze land on her with the weight of a hand.

The leaf-voice spoke one final time, directly into her ear, gently, almost kindly:

"*She has been waiting a hundred years to meet the runaway's granddaughter. Do try to say something clever.*"

Paloma squared her shoulders, touched her grandmother's sigil for courage, and started down the slope toward the thing that had faked its own death to escape the sacrifice — rehearsing, under her breath, several openings, none of which were clever, and one of which was probably going to start a war.
