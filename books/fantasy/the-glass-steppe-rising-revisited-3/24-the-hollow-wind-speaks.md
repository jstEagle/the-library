## 24. The Hollow Wind Speaks

I had imagined meeting the Hollow Wind a hundred times on our march across the Steppe, and in none of those imaginings did she offer us tea.

Yet here we stood — Paloma, myself, and the ghost of a salt-queen's grudge trailing behind us like a bad smell — in a hollow of black glass at the canyon's mouth, and before us, seated on a throne of fused vertebrae that might once have belonged to something enormous and patient, was a woman pouring tea from a pot carved of smoky quartz.

"Sit," said the Hollow Wind. "You've walked three days through Bone Country. Your feet are lying to you about how much more they can do. Mine lied to me for sixty years, so I know the sound."

She was not what I expected. The hunters who served her moved like weather; I had assumed their mistress would be a storm given a face. Instead she was small, gray-haired, with laugh lines around her eyes that no villain should have earned honestly. She wore the robes of a signatory — I recognized the cut from the murals in Vess's court — but hers were scorched at every hem, as if she'd walked out of a fire and decided the fire had been right about everything.

Paloma did not sit. My brave, stubborn carver stood with her thumb hooked in her belt the way she did when a corpse was saying something foolish, and said, "You stole the book."

"I borrowed it," the Hollow Wind corrected pleasantly. "There is a difference. A theft implies I intend to keep it. I intend to destroy it, which is really more of a public service." She gestured with the teapot toward a second stool, then a third. "Ilya of the Order of the Last Page. Yes, I know your order's name; yes, it is a ridiculous name; no, I will not apologize for saying so. And Paloma Reyes, the carver who hears. Sit down, both of you. The tea is not poisoned. Poisoning guests is Vess's hobby, not mine. I find it wasteful. Dead people argue so much less usefully than live ones."

"That," Paloma said, "is the first sensible thing anyone on this Steppe has said."

And because my feet *were* lying to me, and because Paloma sat first — which I could tell surprised her more than it surprised me — I sat too.

* * *

The tea tasted of iron and honey, which I would learn later was simply what tea tasted like when brewed over glass heated by old grief.

"You want to know why I took your book," the Hollow Wind began, "and you're both too polite to ask, so I'll tell you. But first, introductions done properly." She set down her cup. "My name was Sera Vane. I was the thirteenth signatory. The youngest. I signed the covenant when I was nineteen years old, because I was told it was an honor, and because everyone I loved was signing, and because nineteen-year-olds are idiots."

Paloma made a small sound. I glanced at her. She was staring at the scorch marks on Sera's hems with an expression I couldn't read — or perhaps didn't want to.

"I faked my death at the fourth turn," Sera continued. "A century ago now. It wasn't difficult. The signatories were already half-glass by then; who counts the ribs on a ghost? I let them bury an empty shroud and I walked north until the screaming stopped, and I have spent every year since making certain the covenant dies quietly, without ceremony, without another child fed into the glass to keep it alive." She smiled, and it was a warm smile, and that was the worst thing about her. "So. Now you know the monster. Ask me anything."

"Why?" Paloma asked. "If you wanted it dead, why wait a hundred years? Why not break it yourself?"

"Because breaking it loudly kills everyone in your valley, carver. Everything magic touches unravels — your messenger knows the verse. A slow lapse lets the Steppe seep back gently. Rain falls upward. Stones crumble. Strange dreams. People adapt. A sudden rupture is a wound." She refilled my cup unasked. "I am many things, but I am not cruel. I am merely honest, which people often mistake for cruelty, because honesty arrives uninvited."

"Honesty about what?" I asked.

Sera Vane looked at me for a long moment, and I felt like a specimen pinned under glass — fitting, on the Glass Steppe.

"About mercy," she said.

* * *

Here is what she told us, while the wind moved through the fossil canyon above us and made the giant ribs hum like a struck harp.

"The covenant does not keep magic out of your valley," she said. "That is the lie your grandmother's generation believed, and the lie the Order still teaches its messengers. The covenant keeps magic *in*. It is a dam, and the mortal realms are the dry valley below it, and every century some well-meaning smith re-seals the stones so the flood doesn't come. Fine. Noble, even. But ask yourselves: what is the water?"

Neither of us answered.

"The dead," Sera said softly. "The dead are the water. Every soul the Steppe has ever caught — every ghost bound into the glass, every signatory sleeping in the chasm, every nameless whisperer at your boundary stones, Paloma Reyes. The covenant holds them here. It has held them here for a thousand years. They cannot move on. They cannot rest. They cannot *end*. They persist, in the dark under the glass, aware, waiting, forever, so that the living can have clean skies and tidy graves and the comfortable fiction that death is a door."

The wind hummed through the bones above us.

"I died," Sera said, "or nearly. I lay in the glass for one night before I clawed free, and in that one night I heard all of them. All of them, carver. Thousands of years of them. Do you know what they sound like, all together? Neither do I. No one does. Because they never stop, there is never silence, and so there is nothing to hear *against*." Her hands were steady around her cup. "Annihilation is mercy. Letting the covenant lapse doesn't unleash monsters on the living. It lets the dead finally, truly die. It lets the Steppe forget itself. And the living — the living lose a little wonder, a little color, a few impossible cities. In exchange, every trapped soul in this prison gets to end. That is the trade. I have had a hundred years to find a flaw in it, and I have not found one."

* * *

I want to be honest, since honesty apparently arrives uninvited: she nearly won me.

Not with rhetoric. Rhetoric I could handle; I'd been raised by an order whose entire liturgy was rhetoric. What undid me was smaller. It was the way she said *they cannot end*, with the flat certainty of someone who had checked the lock herself. It was the memory of the last messenger — Aldric, whose bones rode in Paloma's pack, whose death I had caused by surviving — and the thought that Aldric had been sent to preserve a prison. That everything I had bled for, everything the Order had spent generations guarding, was a jailer's key polished and passed down like a holy relic.

I looked at Paloma. She was listening the way she listened to the dead — completely, without flinching, letting every word land where it wanted.

"You hear them," Sera said to her suddenly. "The boundary dead. The ones at your stones. Tell me, carver — in all your years, did even one of them ever ask to stay?"

Paloma was quiet for a long time.

"They don't ask for things," she said finally. "They murmur. Names, mostly. Fragments."

"Murmuring," Sera said, "for a hundred years, at the edge of a valley that refuses magic, unable to cross, unable to leave, unable to sleep. You built your whole gentle life on top of their murmuring. You carve their names in bone and call it service. But a name carved in bone is still a cage with prettier bars." She leaned forward. "I am offering them the only gift left to give. Silence. True silence. An ending. And you two are marching to the chasm to reforge the lock."

"The alternative," I said, and my voice came out thinner than I wanted, "is the flood."

"The flood is a story the living tell to justify the prison," Sera said. "I have watched the turns pass, messenger. The Steppe leaks, and nothing unravels. A city-mirage became real for eleven minutes at the first turn — did it destroy the world? It screamed, yes. It screamed because it remembered being alive. That scream is the sound of a thousand years of captivity, and you heard it for one night and fled. Imagine hearing it always."

"She's right that it screamed," Paloma said quietly, to me. Not to Sera. To me.

"Paloma—"

"I'm not agreeing with her. I'm being honest, which apparently arrives uninvited." She turned to Sera. "You're right that they suffer. I've thought so since I was six years old, listening at the stones. You're right that the covenant is a dam and the dead are the water. Here's what you're wrong about." She set down her cup with the deliberate care of a woman placing a chisel. "You've decided that because the suffering is real, the only cure is the end. That's not mercy. That's despair wearing mercy's coat. I've met despair. It lives in valleys and refuses to let anyone leave."

Sera's smile flickered — just at the corner, just for a moment.

"And your alternative?" Sera asked. "Vess tells me you have one. Carved bone instead of bound flesh. Willing signatures instead of a sacrificed child. How lovely. How *craftswomanly*. And when your new covenant holds the dead here for another thousand years — gently, consensually, oh so kindly — what will you tell them? That their prison is legitimate now because they agreed to it? Consent does not make a cage a home, carver. It makes the cage harder to break."

"It makes it a door they chose to hold shut," Paloma said. "For a while. Until something better is carved. Covenants can be rewritten. That's rather the point of me."

"For a while," Sera repeated, tasting the words. "And in the meanwhile, they wait. They always wait." She rose, and the vertebrae of her throne shifted with a sound like distant surf. "I didn't take your book to hurt you. I took it because the final page contains the rite's binding clause, and as long as that page exists, someone will always rebuild the dam. Your grandmother understood. She tore the rite apart a century ago and bought your valley a hundred years, and everyone called her a deserter. I call her the only honest smith your family ever produced."

Something crossed Paloma's face — grief and fury tangled together — and I reached for her hand and found it already a fist.

"Keep the page," Paloma said. "Burn it. We'll write our own."

"No," Sera said, almost sadly. "You won't. Because you'll fail, and failure at the thirteenth turn means rupture, and rupture means I was right after all, just by accident." She snapped her fingers, and somewhere in the dark of the canyon, wings beat. "But I am not cruel. So I'll make you an offer, the same one I make everyone: walk away. Both of you. Go south, go anywhere, live your strange long lives. When the moon completes its turn, the Steppe goes quiet, and your valley loses its magic-proof walls and gains, in exchange, an ordinary world where the dead are allowed to finish dying. Everyone ends. Everyone rests. Isn't that what you carve, Paloma Reyes? Rest?"

"I carve remembrance," Paloma said. "Not oblivion."

"Remembrance," Sera said, "is what the living call it when they're the ones who can't let go."

* * *

We walked out of the hollow as the light failed, and the hunters let us go, which frightened me more than an attack would have. Predators only release prey they believe is already caught.

Sera's last words followed us up the canyon path, thrown casual as a stone:

"Ask your ghosts, carver! Tonight, at your fire — ask them if they want to rest! Ask them honestly! If even one says yes, you'll come back to me, and I'll pour the tea!"

Paloma didn't speak for an hour. Neither did I. The fossil giants loomed overhead, their rib-cages arching across the stars like the naves of drowned cathedrals, and the wind sang through them, and I could not stop hearing *they cannot end* in Sera's warm, terrible voice.

It was full dark when we made camp in the lee of a fallen femur the size of a ship's keel. Paloma unpacked Aldric's bones — wrapped in cloth, carried faithfully since the valley — and laid them out with a carver's tenderness. Then she knelt, put her palms flat against the cold glass, and closed her eyes.

"What are you doing?" I whispered.

"Asking," she said. "Honestly. Like she said." Her jaw tightened. "Because if I don't ask, she wins anyway."

I sat beside her and did the only thing I could, which was listen.

The dead of the Steppe came to her voice like moths — I felt them as a pressure against my ears, a crowd gathering behind a door. And Paloma asked them, in a voice that shook only a little: *Do you want to rest? Truly rest? Tell me true.*

Silence. Long enough that hope and dread traded places in my chest twice.

Then, faint as frost forming, the answer rose — not one voice but many, layered, arguing, exactly as the dead of the Steppe argued:

*Rest. Rest. No — not yet — rest — wait — my name first, say my name first — rest —*

Some said yes. Some said no. Some said *wait*, which was its own kind of answer, and possibly the most important one.

Paloma opened her eyes. They were wet, and furious, and more certain than I had ever seen them.

"They're divided," she said. "Which means nobody gets to decide for them. Not her. Not the old law. Not even me." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and looked at me, and despite everything — despite the stolen page and the beating wings in the dark and the doubt sitting in my chest like swallowed glass — she almost smiled. "Which means we need the book back, Ilya. The rite, the wording, all of it. Because if the dead get a vote, they need something to vote *on*."

"And if we can't get it back?"

Paloma looked north, toward the deep canyons where the Hollow Wind nested, and her hand went to the half-finished sigil at her throat — her grandmother's work, the deserter's work, the honest smith's work.

"Then," she said, "we do what my grandmother did. We break into Bone Country and steal it back."

Above us, in the singing ribs of the fossil giants, something with wings began to laugh.
