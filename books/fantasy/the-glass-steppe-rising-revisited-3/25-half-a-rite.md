## 25. Half a Rite

The Hollow Wind's nest smelled of old rain and older grudges.

Paloma had expected a fortress. What she found instead, at the heart of the fossil canyon where the ribs of giants arched overhead like the vaulting of some drowned cathedral, was a nest in the most literal sense — a great bowl of woven storm-stuff, twined from wind and static and the shed feathers of things that had never been birds, and in its center, half-buried in the downy dark, the prophecy book.

It lay open. Of course it lay open. Books stolen by storms always lay open; she was fairly sure it was a rule.

"There," Ilya breathed beside her, pressed flat against a rib of something that had been enormous when the world was younger. His voice had gone thin. "That's it. That's — Paloma, that's the whole book."

"I can see it's the whole book."

"No, I mean—" He swallowed. "The last page. It's *there*. Torn, but there. The hunter didn't destroy it. It just took it."

"Then we take it back." She said it more steadily than she felt. Her hands were not steady at all; they hadn't been since the campfire, since the shadow-thing had come through their fire like smoke through a keyhole and lifted the book off the stone where Ilya slept with a tenderness that had somehow been worse than violence. "Books don't fight."

"You haven't met this one."

* * *

Getting to the nest was the work of an hour and the cost of several years of Paloma's life.

The canyon of fossil giants was not empty. The Hollow Wind had left sentries — or rather, the canyon itself had grown sentries, because in the Steppe the difference between weather and will had always been negotiable. Pale shapes moved between the ribs: hunters made of hollow air, wearing the suggestion of limbs the way a scarecrow wears a coat. They did not walk so much as *arrive*, over and over, in small increments.

Ilya counted them under his breath. "Four. Maybe five. The fifth might be a trick of the glass."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we're very popular."

They went the way Paloma's grandmother's sigil told them to go — which was to say, they went nowhere sensible at all. The bone sigil, held up to the canyon's strange light, warmed in her palm and tugged her leftward whenever a hunter drifted near, like a compass needle that had opinions. Twice it pulled her into crevices barely wide enough for her shoulders. Once it pulled her directly beneath a hunter that stood motionless on a rib above them for the length of ninety heartbeats while Ilya's breathing went shallow and quick against her ear.

"Why is it stopping?" he whispered.

"Because it's listening."

"For what?"

"For us to be interesting." She kept her voice flat as valley slate. "So don't be."

When the hunter finally moved on, Ilya exhaled against her neck and said, "You are the calmest person I have ever been terrified next to."

"I carve bones for drowned children," she said. "Calm is the trade."

* * *

The nest received them the way a mouth receives food.

There was no other way to describe it. They climbed the last stretch of woven storm and the rim of the bowl parted around them — not violently, but with a slow, deliberate courtesy that was infinitely more frightening — and then they were inside, standing on a floor of compressed lightning-scar, and the entrance sealed behind them like a decision.

The book lay three strides away.

Between them and it, coiled loosely around the pages like a dragon around gold, lay the torn final page itself — no. Paloma blinked. No. The page was *on* the book, laid across the open text, and pinned beneath one corner of it was something small and pale.

A bone. Carved.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She knew that carving. She knew the stitch-pattern of the gouges, the particular way the maker had worried one edge smooth — she had learned her letters on that edge, tracing it as a child while her grandmother pretended not to watch. It was a practice-bone. A child's first attempt at a covenant-clause, all wrong in the ways first attempts are wrong, kept for a hundred years in a chest at the bottom of a valley.

Except the chest was behind them now, days behind them, broken open on a mountainside.

"It has her things," Paloma said. Her voice came out very small. "The Wind has my grandmother's things."

"Paloma—"

"It *took* them. From the chest. When the boundary stones crumbled, when everything spilled out — it went through her chest like a—" She stopped. Breathed. The dead had taught her that much: breathe before you speak, or your words come out as someone else's grief. "It went through her things."

Ilya touched her elbow, lightly, the way you touch a person standing on ice. "We're here for the page."

"Yes."

"Just the page."

"*Yes.*"

But her feet carried her toward the little carved bone first, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

* * *

The moment her fingers closed around the practice-bone, the nest spoke.

It did not speak in words. It spoke in pressure — a vast attention settling over her like snowfall, like the weight of the whole sky deciding to notice one small warm thing. The walls of woven storm leaned in. Somewhere far above, thunder cleared its throat.

And then, threaded through the pressure, faint as a voice through a wall: laughter. Dry, papery, familiar.

*Oh, you stubborn girl.*

Paloma went absolutely still.

"Grandmother?"

*Not her,* said the pressure, and the laughter curdled into something else — amused, yes, but with teeth in it. *Merely what she left behind. Merely the shape her leaving pressed into things. You carry her craft, carver. Craft leaves fingerprints.*

"The Hollow Wind," Ilya whispered. He had gone gray. "It's — it's talking through—"

"Through her leavings." Paloma looked down at the practice-bone in her hand. The wrong little clause, carved by a child's hand. And beneath it, visible now that she looked, worn nearly smooth by a century of handling: a second set of marks. Older. Deeper. An adult's hand, worrying the same edge, over and over, the way you worry a wound.

Or a guilt.

"You've read her things," Paloma said to the storm. Not a question.

*Every word,* the Hollow Wind said, and its voice was everywhere now, in the walls, in the floor, in the space between her heartbeat and the next. *Every letter. Every confession. Do you know what she wrote, carver? Do you know what your precious grandmother did?*

"We know she broke the rite," Ilya said quickly. "We know she tore the final page—"

"She did not tear it." The laughter came back, and this time it was genuinely delighted, which was worse. *Tearing is easy. Any coward tears. Your grandmother was no coward. Ask yourself, messenger-boy: why would a woman who wanted the covenant dead leave the last page intact? Why leave the weapon lying about for anyone to pick up?*

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "Because she wanted it found."

*Because she wanted it finished,* said the Wind. *By someone with better hands than hers.*

* * *

The page came free of the book easily. Too easily — it slid out from under the storm-coil like a gift being given, and Paloma distrusted every inch of that ease, but her hands were already moving, because her hands had always been braver than the rest of her.

She knelt with the page spread on her knee, and Ilya knelt with her, and together they read the ending of a rite begun a century ago.

It was not what either of them expected.

"It's incomplete," Ilya said at once, scanning. "No — wait. It's complete. It's *complete*, every line of the binding is here, the anchor-clause, the sealing-words, all of it — but Paloma, look at the margins."

She looked.

The margins of the final page were full of writing in a hand she knew better than her own. Small, cramped, furious handwriting, crowding the formal script of the original signatories like weeds through pavement. Corrections. Objections. Whole passages struck through and rewritten in the margin in her grandmother's voice:

*This clause binds without asking. Strike it.*
*This price is theft dressed as law. Strike it.*
*A smith who consents is worth ten who are taken. FIND ANOTHER WAY. I did not. You must.*

Page after page after page. A century of margin.

"She didn't sabotage the covenant," Paloma heard herself say. The words came out strange, distant, like someone else's voice heard through water. "She sabotaged herself. She tore the page out so no one could perform the rite *as written* — and then she annotated it. She rewrote it in the margins. She spent her whole life—" Her throat closed. Opened. "She spent her whole life trying to fix it and she died before she could, and she left it here, in the one place the Wind would keep it safe, because the Wind hates the covenant too, and—"

"And she gambled," Ilya said softly, "that someday someone with your hands would come looking."

The nest was silent. Even the thunder had gone quiet, listening.

Paloma stared at the margins until they blurred. Rage came first — it came hot and total, rage at her grandmother for hiding this, for the chest with its false bottom, for the letter she'd apparently written and the letter she'd apparently *not* written, for making Paloma cross a world to find her homework. Rage at the dead woman for dying with the work unfinished. Rage at the whole rotten architecture of inheritance, the way the old ones always handed down their failures wrapped in silence and called it protection.

She let it burn. She'd learned that from the dead too: let the fire take what it takes, and see what's left standing after.

What was left standing was the page.

And the page was good. Gods, it was *good*. Her grandmother's marginal rite was rougher than the original, less elegant, stitched together from objection and refusal — but it ran on consent the way the old one ran on blood. Clause by clause, strike by strike, a woman in a valley that refused magic had spent fifty years building a covenant that magic could live inside honestly.

"She got almost all the way there," Ilya murmured. He was tracing the margin-notes with one finger, careful not to touch the ink. "Look — clauses one through nine, rewritten clean. Ten and eleven, drafted twice. Twelve..." He stopped.

"What?"

"Twelve is blank." He turned the page over, held it to the strange light. "There's a heading. *On the price, and how it may be shared.* And then nothing. Just — blank space. Half a line of ink where she started and stopped."

Paloma looked at the aborted stroke. One downward mark, wavering, abandoned. She thought of her grandmother sitting somewhere in the valley with this page on her knees, pen poised, and the sentence refusing to come — because the sentence required an answer she didn't have. *How do two people share one death?* The question had defeated her.

It had not defeated Paloma. Paloma had stood in a chasm with a man who stepped forward to die for her, and she had answered the question with a knife and her own healed fractures, and the answer had *held*.

"She stopped where I started," Paloma said.

Ilya looked at her.

"She couldn't finish clause twelve because she'd never—" Paloma laughed, and it came out wet and fierce. "She'd never loved anyone enough to want to split the price. She fled alone, she hid alone, she worked alone, she died alone. She built a covenant of consent and had no one to consent *with*. But clause twelve doesn't need her answer anymore." She tapped the blank space, once, hard. "It has mine."

* * *

They burned nothing and broke nothing on the way out. The nest opened for them again, that same terrible courtesy, and as they climbed down into the fossil dusk the Hollow Wind's voice followed them, unhurried, almost kind:

*Take it, carver. Finish her work. And when the covenant stands remade on your willing bone — remember that I told you the truth about your family, when no one else would.*

"Why help us?" Paloma called back, because she was tired of not asking.

The pause stretched long enough to hold a century.

*Because annihilation is mercy,* the storm said, *and your grandmother's mercy failed. Prove mine won't.*

Then the canyon was only a canyon, and the hunters were only shadows, and the two of them walked out under the ribs of giants with half a rite in Paloma's pack and the whole of it in her head.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen vertebra the size of a chapel, and Paloma did not sleep.

She sat with the page on her knees and her carving knives laid out in their worn roll of leather, and she read her grandmother's margins until she could recite them, and then she began, very quietly, to work.

Clause twelve needed a vessel. The old rite had used a living smith bound into the glass; her grandmother's draft had left the question open; Paloma's answer already slept against her heart in the form of a covenant-bone carved from her own fractures and a dead messenger's remains. But the *wording* — the wording had to be perfect. Wording was load-bearing. Her grandmother's notes said so three times in increasingly agitated handwriting: *THE WORDS ARE THE WALLS. DO NOT BUILD WITH CRACKED STONE.*

"You're muttering," Ilya said. He was supposed to be on watch. He was instead lying on his back watching her with that expression he got — the one that was half scholar, half stray dog.

"I'm working."

"You're muttering at a dead woman."

"I'm *collaborating* with a dead woman. There's a difference."

"There is," he conceded. "For the record, she'd be proud of you."

"For the record, she'd be insufferable about it." Paloma blew bone-dust off the page's margin, where she'd penciled — pencil, the one luxury the Steppe allowed, graphite being honest about its mistakes — the first draft of clause twelve. *Let the price fall where love has already divided it.* "She'd correct my grammar and then cry about it privately for a decade."

"That sounds like someone I know."

"Sleep, Ilya."

He smiled and closed his eyes, and for a while there was only the wind doing its ordinary, unhaunted work among the fossil ribs, and the scratch of her knife, and the page warming slowly under her hands as though the ink remembered being alive.

Near midnight, she noticed it.

A new line in the margin. Faint — faint as breath on glass — in handwriting that was not hers and was not her grandmother's and was not any living hand at all:

*Clause twelve, amended by the undersigned. We were there when she began it. We will be there when you end it. — Thirteen voices, waiting.*

Paloma sat very still for a long time.

Then she woke Ilya, and showed him, and watched his face do the thing it did when hope arrived carrying paperwork.

"They signed it," he said hoarsely. "The council. They've *pre-signed*—"

"They're not waiting for the rite." Paloma looked east, where the horizon had begun, very faintly, to glow the color of a bruise — the direction of the chasm, the direction of home-that-was-never-home, the direction of the sixth turn. "They're waiting for *us*. And something's coming to meet us before we get there. Look at the light. That's not dawn."

Ilya followed her gaze, and the humor drained out of his face entirely.

"No," he said. "That's Vess. Vess's salt burns green when she marches."

The glow brightened. Beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an army of the dead keeping perfect time.
