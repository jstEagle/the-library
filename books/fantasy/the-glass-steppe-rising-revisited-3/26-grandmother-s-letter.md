## 26. Grandmother's Letter

The sigil did not want to open.

Paloma had spent the better part of an hour turning it over in her hands — that half-finished crescent of bone she'd pulled from her grandmother's chest a lifetime and five moon-turns ago — looking for seams, hinges, tricks of joinery. Bone-carvers know bone the way masons know stone, and this piece should have shown its construction plainly. It didn't. The grain ran wrong, spiraling inward toward some hidden heart, as though the whole thing had grown rather than been made.

"You're going to wear a groove in it," Ilya said from across the fire. He was rewrapping his hands where the glass dunes had chewed them raw. "Or it's going to wear one in you."

"It's hiding something."

"Everything your grandmother owned hides something. I'm beginning to suspect the woman was a locked box with legs."

Despite everything — the stolen book, the torn page, the canyon of fossil giants groaning somewhere behind them like a sleeping congregation — Paloma laughed. It came out rusty. She noticed Ilya noticing, and pretended not to notice him noticing.

She turned the sigil toward the firelight again, and there it was: not a seam but a *scar*. A hairline where the grain knitted itself closed over something hollow. Her grandmother had carved the break shut — deliberately, lovingly, the way you close a wound you intend to heal rather than hide.

"She wanted someone to find it," Paloma said quietly. "But only someone who knew how to look."

"And that's you."

"That's me." She picked up her smallest knife — the one with the handle worn to the shape of three generations of palms, hers the latest — and set the blade against the scar. "Hold on to something."

"What's going to hap—"

She pressed. Not hard. Just true, the way you tap a chisel into a line the bone has already agreed to. The sigil sighed open along its hidden seam like a book that had been waiting a very long time for its reader, and inside, folded into a space no larger than a sparrow's egg, lay a scrap of leather wrapped around a thin sheaf of paper gone amber with age.

Paper. Real paper, valley-made, mulberry-fibered, the kind her grandmother had used for lists of tallow and thread. Not prophecy-vellum. Not covenant-glass. Just paper, the ordinary kind, the kind you write to family on.

Paloma's hands were suddenly not steady at all.

"Do you want me to—" Ilya started.

"No." Then, because that wasn't fair: "Sit close, though. In case."

He came around the fire and sat shoulder-to-shoulder with her, close enough that she could feel the strange faint warmth he carried, the warmth of a man who bled light. She unwrapped the leather. Inside were four pages, written in a hand she knew — knew the way you know a voice through a wall. Firm strokes at the start of lines, trailing off uncertain at their ends. Her grandmother had never finished anything gracefully. Not letters. Not goodbyes. Not, apparently, covenants.

*If you have opened this,* the letter began, *then you are a carver, and you are past the stones, and I am sorry.*

Paloma read it aloud. She didn't decide to; the words simply insisted on air, the way names do, the way apologies do.

---

*If you have opened this, then you are a carver, and you are past the stones, and I am sorry.*

*Sorry first for the chest with its false bottom and its honest-looking junk. Sorry for teaching you to carve memorial bones and never telling you what the first ones were for. Sorry most of all for the silence you will have met on the other side when you asked the honored dead about the covenant. That silence is mine. I bought it. I paid for it with a hundred years of their disapproval, and I would pay it again, which is why I need you to hear the whole of it before you judge me, because you will want to judge me, child. You come by your stubbornness honestly.*

*Here is what they will have told you, or refused to tell you: the Glass Steppe is bound by a covenant, and the covenant requires a smith, and every hundred years the smith must be bound into the glass — living, willing or otherwise, the old law does not much care which — to hold the mortal realms and the magic realms apart. Thirteen signatories made that bargain when the worlds were new and frightened of each other. It worked. It worked beautifully. Nobody asked what it cost, because the ones who could answer were busy being anchors.*

*I was the smith. My mother before me, and hers before her, back to the beginning. We told ourselves we were honored. We told ourselves the children went willingly. And then it was my turn to choose the next, and I looked at the list of names, and every name on it belonged to somebody's daughter, and I thought: how many generations of us held our daughters' hands and called it duty?*

*There was a girl. I won't write her name here — names travel badly on paper, and she has suffered enough of being written about. She was eleven. She sang while she worked the bone. Everyone said what a fine smith she would make. Everyone said it in front of her, the way people say things about children, as if children are furniture that will someday be useful.*

*I took her to the edge of the glass, and I stood there with the rite in my hands, and she asked me if it would hurt, and I opened my mouth to say the thing we all said — that it was an honor, that the Steppe would hold her gently — and what came out instead was the truth: yes. Yes, it would hurt, forever, in a way that never quite becomes memory because memory requires an ending.*

*And then I broke it.*

*Not the girl. The rite. Do you understand the difference? I want so badly for you to understand the difference. I did not fail my duty out of cowardice. I committed a different duty, harder and worse, on purpose. I had spent thirty years carving covenant-bone, and I knew its grammar the way you know yours, and I found the flaw the founders had built in and never spoken of: the rite requires completion. A broken rite doesn't half-work. It doesn't work at all — but neither can it be replaced until its term runs out. I snapped the final clause like a wishbone and buried the pieces, and the covenant began to die, slowly, the way a fire dies when you seal the flue, and I gathered our family and we ran for the valley, where magic cannot follow, and I let everyone believe we fled in fear.*

*We did not flee in fear. We fled in protest.*

*A hundred years, the breaking bought. A hundred years for someone wiser than me to find a better way. I spent sixty of them looking for that way myself and failed — the old law is a knot tied by frightened gods, and I could loosen nothing further without unraveling everything. So I did the last thing left to a smith who refuses: I taught my granddaughter to carve, and to listen, and I waited to see if the dead would trust her more than they trusted me.*

*They will not have trusted me. I heard them stop speaking the day I broke the rite, all of them, even the ones who hated the sacrifice, because a broken promise offends the dead more than a kept one, and I accept that. But you — you mend things. You sat with drowned children and gave their mothers bones to hold. If anyone can make the dead believe that refusal can be a form of love, it is you.*

*The sigil in your hands is half a key. The other half is not an object. The other half is the answer to the question the thirteen will ask you, and I cannot give it to you, because I never found mine. Perhaps that is my true failure, and perhaps it is my true gift: I leave you the question instead of the answer.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop talking, which my mother always said I was constitutionally incapable of doing.*

*You will meet a messenger. The order always sends one when the term nears its end. They will be young and frightened and carrying a book that has already decided everything about them, and you will be tempted to treat them as the book treats them — as ink, as instrument, as a line item in a prophecy. Don't. The last messenger was a boy named Tomas who deserved far better than the two sentences history gave him. Whatever the book says about the one you meet, remember: books are also carved things. And anything carved can be recarved.*

*That is the whole of my inheritance to you, child. A broken rite, a buried question, and permission.*

*Permission is the part nobody ever thinks to ask for. Take it anyway.*

*— Your grandmother, who loved you in the only language she trusted, which is to say, badly, and constantly, and in bone.*

---

The fire had burned down to a red murmur by the time she finished. Neither of them spoke. Somewhere above the canyon rim, the moon was climbing toward its ninth turn, patient as a creditor.

Paloma became aware, distantly, that she was crying — not the dramatic kind, just the quiet leak of a thing long dammed finally finding its channel. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, annoyed at herself.

"I spent my whole life being angry at her," she said. "For the lies. For the silence. For making me the kind of person who ends up out *here*." She gestured at the dark, the glass, the whole impossible world. "And the whole time she was—"

"Trying to hand you a way out," Ilya said.

"No." Paloma shook her head. "That's the thing. She wasn't handing me a way out. She says it right there — she couldn't find one. She handed me a *question*, and permission, and then she had the nerve to die before finding out whether any of it worked." A wet, surprised laugh escaped her. "She left me homework. From beyond the grave. That is the most her thing I have ever heard."

Ilya was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful gentleness he reserved for the parts of the story that touched him too. "The messenger she mentions. Tomas. The bones you carry —"

"I know." Paloma touched the satchel at her hip, where the last messenger's remains rode against her own. She had known since the Salt Court, known and not said, and now the letter had said it for her, and somehow that was a relief — grief shared with a dead woman was still grief, but it was less lonely. "She knew about him too. A hundred years ago, and she knew, and she still wrote *deserved better*." She looked into the coals. "Everyone in this story keeps trying to save people they'll never meet. It's absurd. It's the most absurd family tradition imaginable."

"And yet here you are," Ilya said, "carrying it on."

"Here I am." She smoothed the letter's pages against her knee, careful of the old folds. "The question, though. She says the other half of the key is the answer to what the thirteen will ask. *What is survival worth.*" She said it slowly, tasting it. "She never found her answer. Sixty years and she never found it."

"Maybe she did," Ilya said. "Maybe her answer was the breaking itself. Maybe refusing to spend a child *was* what survival was worth — to her."

Paloma considered that. The fire popped. Out in the dark, the fossil canyon breathed its long mineral breath, and very faintly — softer than she had ever heard them, here where the dead usually shouted — the dead of the Steppe seemed to lean in.

"She'd hate that I'm crying over her letter," Paloma said. "She'd say tears warp the bone."

"Did she actually say that?"

"Constantly. Usually while crying over her own carvings." Paloma laughed again, fuller this time, and folded the letter with the reverence due to both scripture and family embarrassment. "I forgive her," she said, mostly to the fire, partly to the dead, entirely to the woman. "Not because she was right about everything. Because she was wrong about enough of it, and scared the whole time, and did the hard thing anyway. That's not a saint. That's just — family."

Ilya put his hand over hers, briefly, warmly, and said nothing, which was exactly right.

It was Paloma who saw it first — or rather, felt it, the way she felt everything now that the valley's deafness had been stripped from her. The letter's last page, against her knee, had begun to *hum*. Faintly. Familiarly. The same resonance she'd felt when her carving first sang against the Steppe itself.

"Ilya."

"I feel it." He was already leaning in. "The letter — it's resonating?"

"Not the letter." She lifted the page. Beneath it, where it had lain against the open sigil, the old bone's spiral grain had begun to turn — slowly, visibly, like a lock acknowledging its key. And rising out of the hollow heart of her grandmother's half-finished work, in a hand Paloma recognized from a hundred memorial bones, new words were surfacing as though carved by a patient ghost working from the inside:

*Clause the last, restored: the price shall be named twice, and paid by none unwilling.*

Paloma stared at it. Her grandmother hadn't just broken the rite a century ago. She had been *drafting* — all those years, secretly, in the only vault no one could search — a replacement. Half a rite. An unfinished sentence, waiting a hundred years for a carver with steady hands and a reason to finish it.

"Oh," Paloma whispered. "Oh, you stubborn, magnificent woman. You didn't leave me a question after all."

The bone glowed softly in the dark, and far away across the glass, something vast turned over in its sleep — and began, very slowly, to wake.
