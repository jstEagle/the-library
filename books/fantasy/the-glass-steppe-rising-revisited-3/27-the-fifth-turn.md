## 27. The Fifth Turn

The moon had stopped behaving like a moon.

Ilya noticed it first as a wrongness in the light — the way the thirteenth sliver hung in the sky like a fingernail paring, too bright, too close, and then, three nights later, appeared again from the *other* side of the sky, as if it had gotten bored of its usual route home. He sat up in the lee of a glass ridge with the prophecy book open on his knees and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for two days.

The first turn had taken a month. The second, closer to three weeks. The third had passed while they were still arguing with Vess's salt-court heralds about the proper way to address a dead queen (bow twice, never thrice; the third bow was for funerals, and Vess took a dim view of premature ones). The fourth turn had come and gone in nine days, marked by a shudder through the Steppe so deep that the fossil canyons to the north had groaned like a ship changing its mind.

And now, watching that pale sliver slide backward across the stars, Ilya understood: the turns were compressing. Whatever patience the covenant had left was being spent faster and faster, like a candle burning down its own wick.

"Paloma," he said. "You should see this."

She was asleep sitting up, which she'd gotten good at lately — a bone-carver's trick, she claimed, though Ilya suspected it was just what happened to a person when the dead argued all night in her skull. She woke the way she always did: instantly, completely, one hand already finding the carving knife at her belt.

"The moon's going backward," he said.

She looked up. Watched. Her face did the thing it did when she was translating dread into craft — a small tightening around the eyes, a settling of the jaw, the expression of someone measuring a bone before deciding where to cut.

"How long until the fifth?" she asked.

"If the pattern holds? Six days. Maybe five."

"And after that?"

"I don't want to do that math."

"Do it anyway."

He did it anyway. "Three days. Then two. Then—" He closed the book. "Then the turns stop being turns at all. They'll blur together. The covenant won't lapse at the thirteenth turn, Paloma. It'll unravel somewhere around the tenth or eleventh, because there won't be enough *time* left inside the turns for anything to hold its shape."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. Somewhere behind them, out on the plain, a city-mirage flickered inside the glass — spires and domes and tiny lit windows, a whole civilization pressed flat like a flower in a book. It had been screaming less often lately. Ilya wasn't sure whether that was hope or exhaustion.

"So we walk faster," she said.

"We walk faster."

She stood, brushed glass-dust off her knees, and shouldered her pack with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had decided something and saw no reason to announce it. But as she turned toward the north road — the long white scar across the Steppe that led, if the ghosts were honest with them, to the council-chasm — she paused.

"Ilya." She didn't look back. "When this is over. If it's over. Do you think the valley will still be there?"

He thought about lying. He'd gotten better at not doing that, mostly because she could hear lies — not in his words, but in the dead's reaction to him, a faint static of disapproval that followed every falsehood like a dog following a butcher's cart.

"No," he said. "I think the valley will be *different*. Which might be worse. Or better. I've given up predicting."

"Hm," said Paloma, in the tone of a woman filing that answer away for later inspection, and walked north.

* * *

They made the salt flats by midday, and that was where they saw the rain fall upward.

It started as a shimmer on the southern horizon — a gray column where no column should be, standing over the distant hills that hid the boundary stones and, beyond them, the valley. At first Ilya took it for dust, or heat-haze, or one of the Steppe's mirages leaking sideways. Then Paloma stopped walking, and he knew.

"It's raining," she said. "Over the valley."

"Yes."

"It's raining *upward*, Ilya."

It was. Even at this distance they could see it — drops lifting off the earth in slow silver threads, climbing into the clouds instead of falling from them, the whole sky drinking the valley like a man gulping water after a long fast. The Steppe wasn't waiting for the covenant to lapse. It was already reaching across, pulling the mortal world's moisture, its warmth, its *loose magic* — everything unbound and undefended — back into itself.

Paloma watched for a long time. Her hand had gone to the carved sigil at her throat — her grandmother's half-finished work, the thing that had opened the Hollow Wind's canyon to them, the thing that had cost them the torn final page and nearly their lives. She touched it the way other people touched saints' medals.

"My mother used to say rain was the valley's way of keeping secrets," she said. "Softly, so you couldn't hear yourself cry. Now it can't even keep its own water."

Ilya searched for something comforting and found nothing that wouldn't be a lie. So instead he said, "Vess's scouts reported the Hollow Wind moving south. Toward the chasm. If we're quick, we reach it first."

"Quick." Paloma laughed — a short, dry sound with no humor in it, but not without warmth either, somehow. That was the trick of her, Ilya had learned. Even her despair came packaged with a kind of stubborn tenderness. "You know what my grandmother would have said about 'quick'? She'd have said the dead have been waiting a hundred years. They can wait six more days."

"She'd also have sabotaged the rite," Ilya pointed out. "So perhaps we don't take all our grandmother's advice."

That earned him a look — the sharp one, the one that meant he'd crossed some invisible line between *funny* and *too soon*. He was getting better at finding the line. Not perfect. Better.

"Too soon," she confirmed, and walked on.

But a few paces later, without turning around, she added: "Thank you. For saying it anyway. Someone has to remember what she actually did, or the forgiveness doesn't mean anything."

Ilya carried that sentence with him for the rest of the day, turning it over like a stone with a warm side.

* * *

They camped that night in the shadow of a fallen signatory — one of the thirteen, a figure of fused glass the size of a cathedral, lying on its side in the plain as if it had simply lain down mid-journey centuries ago and been too tired to get up. Its face was smooth, featureless, worn by wind into something almost peaceful. Travelers' offerings cluttered its base: ribbons of woven grass, cups of salt, small carved bones left by people who had never known its name.

Paloma went among the offerings and read them like letters. She did this every night now — moved through the little shrines with her fingers hovering an inch above each carved token, listening. Ilya watched her work and felt the old ache in his chest, the one that had nothing to do with the prophecy.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm admiring. There's a difference."

"Mm." She lifted a child's carving — a lopsided horse, all legs and courage — and held it a long moment. "This one's fresh. Carved within the last season. There are still people out here, Ilya. Families. Little enclaves living in the cracks of the Steppe even now."

"The order thought everyone had fled south generations ago."

"The order thought a lot of things." She set the horse down gently, exactly where it had been, aligned with its neighbors. "These people stayed. Nobody protected them. Nobody wrote them into any covenant. They just... stayed, and kept making things." She looked up at him then, and in the moonlight her face was very serious. "That's who we're doing this for. Not the valley. Not the Steppe. The ones who stayed and kept making things."

Ilya nodded, and meant it, and privately filed the image away next to the warm-sided stone: Paloma Reyes, kneeling in glass dust, straightening a child's crooked horse by a millimeter so it faced the same direction as the others.

* * *

On the second day, the turns announced themselves early.

They felt it as a tremor underfoot at noon — not the deep groan of the fourth turn but something sharper, quicker, a hiccup in the world's swallowing. The sky flickered. For three heartbeats, the plain around them became a different plain: greener, wetter, threaded with rivers, dotted with tents and herd-beasts and smoke from cookfires. A memory of the Steppe alive, before the glass. Then it snapped back, and the silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

"That was the fifth," Paloma said quietly. "Wasn't it."

Ilya checked the sky. The moon hung there, thin and wrong, already beginning its next impossible journey. "Early. By two days." He did the new math aloud, because she'd asked him once to stop hiding numbers from her. "If it keeps accelerating — sixth turn in four days. Seventh in three. Eighth in two. After that—"

"After that we're counting in hours." She flexed her fingers, the way she did before difficult carving work. "Then we'd better not need sleep."

"We'll need sleep."

"Then we'd better learn to argue with the moon."

Despite everything — the upward rain now visible behind them like a silver pillar accusing the sky, the flickers coming more often, the horizon to the north darkening with a storm-front that was not weather — despite all of it, Ilya laughed. It startled him, the laugh, coming up out of him rough and rusty. Paloma glanced sideways at him, one eyebrow raised.

"What?"

"Arguing with the moon. You make it sound like a thing a person could win."

"My grandmother argued with a covenant," Paloma said. "And won. For a hundred years." She resumed walking, northward, into the darkening distance. "I'm her granddaughter. I intend to be worse."

* * *

They walked through the flickers. That was the only way to describe the next day: walking through a world that couldn't decide what it had been. Sometimes the glass underfoot was glass. Sometimes it was grass, soft and real enough that Paloma knelt once to touch it, and when she raised her hand her palm was wet with dew that vanished before Ilya's eyes. Once, for nearly a full minute, they walked through a market — stalls, voices, the smell of bread — and Ilya heard a vendor call a price in a language that predated every language the order had ever recorded, and then the market was gone and they were alone on the white scar with their own footprints and the taste of bread neither of them had eaten.

Paloma didn't speak much during those hours. But Ilya noticed she had begun talking to the dead as they walked — not the frantic consultations of the early days, but conversation. Casual. Almost companionable. She'd tilt her head, listen, snort occasionally.

"What are they saying?" he finally asked.

"Complaints. Mostly." She ducked under a shard-arch that had once, perhaps, been a gate. "The signatories are waking up faster than expected. All thirteen stirring now. They can feel the turns compressing, same as us. And they're..." She hesitated.

"They're what?"

"Afraid," she said. "Thirteen ghosts who bound a world with their deaths, and they're afraid. Of the Hollow Wind. Of the rite. Of me, a little." She smiled thinly. "Of me a lot, actually. Apparently the last carver who stood where I'm standing was my grandmother, and they remember how that ended."

"Do you resent that? Being measured against her?"

Paloma considered it with the seriousness she gave everything, weighing the question like a bone in her hand.

"No," she said at last. "Resentment is heavy, and I'm carrying enough. But I'll tell you what I feel, since you asked." She stopped at the crest of a low dune and looked north, where the storm-front had resolved itself into something unmistakable: a wall of hollow wind, a rolling darkness eating the horizon, and somewhere inside it, the thing that had once been a signatory and had chosen instead to become an ending.

"I feel like the whole story has already been written," she said. "By the prophecy book, by my grandmother, by the Hollow Wind, by thirteen ghosts with opinions. Everyone's written their version. And every version ends with somebody paying." She looked at him then, and the gentleness in her face was fiercer than anger. "I'm going to write a different ending. Not because I'm sure I can. Because someone has to try, and I'm the one holding the knife."

Ilya had prepared, over the course of their journey, a number of speeches for moments like this — reassurances, encouragements, the polished consolations of a trained messenger. He discarded all of them. What he said instead was:

"Then let's go be uncertain together. It's faster with two."

Paloma laughed — really laughed, brief and surprised at herself — and the sound carried out over the glass, and somewhere beneath them, Ilya would have sworn, the Steppe listened.

* * *

That night, the rain over the valley reached them.

Not as water. As sound. A vast, soft hush from the south, like the world's largest crowd inhaling at once — the valley's rains rising in reverse, drawn up and up into a sky that was learning to drink. Paloma stood at the edge of their camp with her face turned south, toward a home she had already chosen to lose, and Ilya stood beside her without speaking, because some silences are the truest company.

"The boundary stones," she said finally. "Can you see them from here? With your order's eyes."

He looked. Far south, past the silver pillar of reversed rain, past the hills — yes. Faintly. The old stones still stood, but their glow was guttering, a candle-flame cupped in a hand growing careless.

"They're dimming," he said. "Not broken. Dimming. The covenant isn't gone yet, Paloma. It's spending itself."

"Spending itself on what?"

"On staying alive long enough for us to finish." He paused. "Or on staying alive long enough to fail beautifully. The book is unclear on which."

"The book," said Paloma, "has been unclear on everything since page one. I'm starting to think that was deliberate. Whoever wrote it wanted readers who'd argue back."

"Your grandmother tore out the last page."

"Exactly. First argument in a hundred years." She turned from the south at last, and her profile against the rising moon was calm, resolute, tired in the way of mountains rather than travelers. "Sixth turn comes in four days. We'll be at the chasm's rim by then, or we'll be nowhere at all. Come on, messenger. Walk with me."

They walked. Behind them the valley's rain climbed into a hungry sky; ahead of them the Hollow Wind's darkness rolled slow and patient as a closing hand; beneath them the glass hummed with thirteen waking dreams and a hundred years of unpaid debts.

And Ilya, dying slowly in the ordinary way that had brought him here in the first place, felt something he hadn't felt since before the order found him: not certainty, not hope exactly, but *readiness*. The readiness of a man who has stopped asking whether the bridge will hold and simply begun to cross.

Beside him, Paloma's knife whispered against a scrap of bone as she walked — practicing, always practicing, carving the shape of a future nobody had written yet.

At dawn, the scouts found them.

Not Vess's salt-soldiers, and not the Hollow Wind's hunters either — these wore ash-gray cloaks and carried no weapons at all, and the eldest of them, a woman with eyes like banked coals, looked at Paloma's knife and then at Ilya's face and said, in a voice that cracked like dry salt:

"The chasm has opened early. Thirteen are awake. And one of them is asking for the carver by name."
