## 28. Allies of Salt and Ash

The salt pillars of Vess's court had been leaning for three days.

Paloma noticed it the way she noticed everything about bone — by the angle of the break waiting inside it. Salt was not bone, but it remembered pressure the same way. Each pillar in that impossible court had once stood straight as a held breath, and now they tilted inward, like old men huddled over a fire, like mourners closing ranks around a grave. The Hollow Wind's storm had passed this way, or was passing still, and even a ghost-queen's architecture knew fear.

"She's watching us from the tallest one," Ilya murmured. He stood at the edge of the salt flats with his hood up against the grit, looking less like a messenger of a dying order and more like a man who badly wanted to sit down. "She's been watching since dawn."

"I know." Paloma turned the half-finished covenant-bone over in her hands. It had become a habit, this turning — thumb along the carved groove, feeling for the places where the dead had already signed their voices into it. Six clauses so far. Six ghosts who had agreed, freely, to be part of something new. "I can hear her thinking. Ghosts think loudly when they're worried. It's all *what if* and *but then* and *if only I had*."

"What is she thinking right now?"

"That she should have killed us when she had the chance," Paloma said, "and that she's very sorry about the poisoned wine."

Ilya snorted. "Is she?"

"No. But she wants us to think she is, which in my experience is most of an apology."

They had come back to the Salt Court because there was nowhere else left to go. That was the truth of it, unglamorous as a bruise. The Fifth Turn had come and gone while they walked, and time on the Steppe had begun to behave like water running through cupped hands. The moon markers that had once taken weeks now passed in days. Behind them, the boundary stones of her valley were dust on the horizon. Ahead of them, the council-chasm waited, and between here and there lay the Hollow Wind's territory — a storm given ambition, a dead signatory given a century to nurse her grudge against living things.

And Vess, queen of salt, keeper of grudges of her own, sat in her tilting court with an army of pillar-wights and nothing left to lose but the shape of her losing.

"You're smiling," Ilya said suspiciously.

"I'm nervous. It looks like smiling. My grandmother said the two ran in the family."

"Your grandmother," Ilya said carefully, "also sabotaged a covenant that held the world together."

"Yes, well." Paloma tucked the bone into its sling against her ribs. "Nobody's grandmother is only one thing. Come on. If we keep the queen waiting, she'll invent new ways to be insulted, and I'd rather she used the old ones. I've learned their antidotes."

* * *

Vess received them in the open, which was itself an omen. Once, she had held court beneath the great central pillar, enthroned on a dais of crusted brine, veiled in the shimmer that ghosts wore instead of silk. Now she stood among her leaning soldiers with her veil torn away, and Paloma saw for the first time what lay beneath: a woman's face, or the memory of one, worn smooth as sea glass. Death had been kind to Vess the way the tide is kind to a shipwreck — patiently, and without asking permission.

"Carver." Her voice came out of the ground as much as out of her, resonant, mineral. "You have nerve, walking back into my court after what you refused me."

"You mean the bargain where I bound Ilya into the glass forever?" Paloma kept her voice pleasant. "Yes. I do have nerve. It's the same nerve that lets me stand here now."

A ripple went through the pillar-wights — those tall figures of compacted salt that served as Vess's honor guard and army both. They had no faces to read, but they had posture, and their posture said they remembered the last negotiation had ended with war following them out the door.

"I offered you an army," Vess said.

"You offered me a leash with an army attached."

"And now I offer you the same army," Vess said, "without the leash."

Silence. Somewhere far off, the Hollow Wind moaned across the glass dunes — a sound like a door that would not stay shut, like grief that had learned to travel. Paloma felt Ilya shift beside her, felt him not-quite-touch her elbow, the way he did when he wanted to warn her and didn't dare.

"Say more," Paloma said.

Vess drifted closer. Up close, the queen smelled of old rain — the rain that fell upward now over the valley, the rain that belonged to no sky anymore. "The Hollow Wind has taken the Fossil Canyon," she said. "Her hunters grow bolder. Three of my pillars fell last night. Do you understand what that means, carver? We are salt. She is wind. When she blows, we scatter, and scattered salt is *nothing*. I have ruled this court for nine hundred years, and in nine hundred years I never imagined kneeling to anyone. And yet." She spread her smooth hands. "Here I am, about to kneel."

"To me?" Paloma asked, startled.

"To the idea of you." Vess's smile was thin and sharp as a flake of mica. "Don't preen. You're a means to an end, and I am honest enough to say so, and you are clever enough to prefer an honest enemy to a flattering friend. Am I wrong?"

"No," Paloma admitted. "That's the most flattering thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

Behind her, Ilya made a small strangled noise that might have been a laugh being murdered before it could cause trouble.

* * *

They negotiated in the ruins of the feast hall, because Vess insisted on protocol even now — especially now, Paloma suspected, because protocol was the last thing standing between the queen and the admission that her world was ending. The long table of salt had cracked down the middle. They sat on either side of the crack, which struck Paloma as either very funny or very true, and she couldn't decide which, so she decided it was both.

"Tell me your plan," Vess said. "All of it. I will know if you lie. I have heard ten thousand dying confessions, carver. Lies have a texture."

"Willing bone," Paloma said. She laid the covenant-bone on the table, in the crack, where both sides could see it. "The old covenant was forged on a sacrifice. A living smith, bound into the glass every hundred years. That's why my family fled. That's why your covenant is breaking — not because the magic failed, but because the *price* was always theft, and theft rots."

"The old signatories agreed to it," Vess said.

"The old signatories were thirteen desperate people staring down the end of the world, and one of them faked her death to escape the price, and became the thing hunting us now." Paloma let that sit. "You want to know what I've learned on this Steppe, Vess? Every ghost I've met who signed the old law regrets it. Not one of them says *we saved too much*. They say *we paid too dearly*, and they say it in their sleep, if ghosts sleep, and I think they do."

"They will ask your price again," Vess said. "At the chasm. The covenant demands an anchor. Bone and blood and consent — fine words. But consent to *what*, carver? Something must hold the new law in place. Something must be spent."

"I know." Paloma's hand found the bone at her ribs, an unconscious gesture she'd stopped fighting weeks ago. "I've been carving myself into it since the beginning. My healed fractures. My knuckle-bones. Whatever the rite takes, it takes from me willingly, and Ilya too — we split the price between us. Two anchors instead of one victim. Shared weight bends; single weight breaks. Even salt knows that."

For a long moment Vess said nothing. The pillar-wights in the doorway leaned, listening, though they had no ears.

"You would bind yourself," Vess said at last, "to the Steppe. Forever. As I am bound."

"If it comes to that. Yes."

"And you call this better than the old way."

"I call it *honest*," Paloma said. "The old way lied to everyone. It told the signatories the price was noble. It told the smiths' families their children were honored. It told the valley it was safe, when really the valley was just someone else's prison wall. My plan tells the truth: the world needs holding, and holding costs, and the ones who pay get to choose it." She turned the bone so the carved clauses caught the light. "Six ghosts have already signed. Freely. They each gave a clause — a promise the new covenant keeps. One promised the rain falls downward. One promised the dead may speak and be answered. One promised—" she hesitated, then finished— "one promised that no child is ever again named as payment. That clause is carved deepest. It took the longest. Some promises are harder to make than others."

Vess looked at the bone for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had lost some of its throne-room resonance and gained something smaller, older, almost human.

"My clause," she said. "If I join you. What would you carve for me?"

Paloma had thought about this. She'd thought about little else since the Fifth Turn, lying awake under a sky that was coming apart at the seams, listening to six ghosts murmur their hopes into her craft. But she hadn't expected the question to land in her chest the way it did.

"You tell me," she said. "It has to be yours. That's the whole point. I can't give you a promise you didn't make."

Vess was quiet for so long that one of the pillar-wights shifted nervously. Then the queen laughed — a dry, cracking sound, like ice on a pond in early spring, like something frozen beginning to move.

"Nine hundred years," she said, "and no one has ever asked me what I wanted. They asked what I demanded. There is a difference. I am only now learning how wide it is." She straightened, and even her ruined court seemed to lean a little less. "My clause is this: *that hospitality be true*. No poison in the welcome cup. No knife behind the guest-right. Let every threshold on the Steppe mean what it says." Her smooth face twisted wryly. "Yes, carver. I am promising to stop being exactly what I have been. That is what a clause is, isn't it? A promise that costs."

"It's the best kind," Paloma said softly. "May I carve it?"

"Do it before I regain my senses."

* * *

Carving Vess's clause took until dusk, and it was the strangest work Paloma had ever done. Usually the dead spoke to her from a distance — murmurs at the edge of hearing, arguments in the glass, whispers in the marrow of things. But Vess stood beside her while she worked, close enough to touch, dictating her promise word by word like a woman writing a will she intended to survive.

"*No poison in the welcome cup,*" Vess repeated, watching the knife move. "Deeper. Deeper, carver. If it isn't deep, the wind will find it and wear it away."

"Nothing wears bone that's cut true," Paloma said. "My grandmother taught me that. Well — her tools taught me. She wasn't much for talking by the end."

"Your grandmother." Vess's voice changed. "Mara Reyes."

Paloma's knife stopped. "You knew her name?"

"I knew her *work*." For the first time, the ghost-queen looked uncomfortable, which Paloma would have paid good money to see under other circumstances. "A century ago, when the rite broke, the Steppe screamed for a year. We ghosts felt every crack. And then, one night, someone walked the boundary stones and sang them quiet — a living woman, alone, with a bag of carved bones. She bought your valley its hundred years. We wondered ever after who she was and why she ran instead of staying to finish the job." Vess's smooth eyes fixed on Paloma. "Now I know. She couldn't finish the job, because finishing it meant binding another child. So she built a wall instead of a bridge, and she went home, and she taught her granddaughter to carve."

"You're saying that like it's a good thing."

"I'm saying it like it's a *thing*," Vess said. "Walls and bridges are made of the same stone, carver. The difference is which way you're facing." She nodded at the bone. "Finish the clause. We have a storm to outrun and a chasm to reach, and I have an army to embarrass myself in front of."

* * *

The oath-taking happened at moonrise, on the salt flats before the leaning court, and it was — there was no other word for it — a wedding of sorts. Vess spoke her clause aloud before her pillar-wights, her voice carrying flat and clear across the glass: *no poison in the welcome cup, no knife behind the guest-right.* Paloma carved the final stroke, and the bone *sang* — that low, resonant hum she had first heard in the canyon country, the sound of the Steppe itself acknowledging a signature. Seven clauses now. Seven ghosts committed.

Then Vess turned to her army and did the thing Paloma would remember for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be.

She knelt. A ghost-queen, nine centuries old, kneeled in the salt before a bone-carver from a valley that refused magic, and said, in a voice pitched to carry: "I have demanded obedience for nine hundred years. Tonight I am asking for it. Will you follow a queen who follows?"

And the pillar-wights — faceless, ancient, loyal the way mountains are loyal — leaned forward as one, and the sound they made was like a thousand hearthstones settling, and it meant *yes*.

"Well," Ilya whispered at Paloma's shoulder. His voice was doing something complicated. "That's seven clauses, an army of salt, and a truce with a woman who tried to have us killed. In my order we called that a productive week."

"In my valley we called it Tuesday," Paloma said, and then, because his voice had done that complicated thing and hers was doing one too: "Are you crying?"

"Wind," said Ilya. "Grit. Very common on the Steppe."

"Mm-hm."

"It's *very* gritty."

She let him have it. There would be time later to tell him that she'd cried too, quietly, the moment Vess knelt — not from relief exactly, and not from hope exactly, but from the strange specific ache of watching someone choose to be smaller than their title so that something larger than any of them could live.

Seven clauses. An army. A chance.

It was more than she'd had a month ago, when a stranger bled light onto her doorstep and said her name like he'd read it somewhere.

It was maybe not enough. The Hollow Wind's storm had begun to gather on the northern horizon, black and patient and vast, and the Sixth Turn would pass within days, and beyond the chasm waited twelve sleeping ghosts and one question that had broken the world once already.

But tonight, for the first time since she'd crossed the stones, Paloma slept without the bone clutched to her chest. She set it beside her in the salt, where it hummed gently to itself like a cat, seven promises deep and growing.

She woke before dawn to Ilya shaking her shoulder, his face gray in the starlight.

"Paloma," he said. "The scouts are back. The Hollow Wind isn't gathering in the north anymore."

"Then where—"

"It's moving toward the chasm," he said. "It's going to wake the signatories first. And kill them before you ever get the chance to ask your question."
