## 29. The Carving Begins

The bone did not want to be carved at first.

Paloma learned this on the second morning of the work, when her burin skated off the femur's surface three times in a row and left a white scratch like a scar that healed itself before her eyes. She sat back on her heels in the salt-workshop Vess had grudgingly cleared for them — a hollow beneath a leaning pillar, floored with woven ash-grass, lit by lamps of trapped dawn — and addressed the bone directly, as she would have addressed any stubborn client back home.

"You were a man," she said. "You walked on this leg for forty years, give or take. You have opinions. I respect that. But we are on a deadline."

The bone said nothing, because bones never do. It was the dead who answered, and the dead had been answering all night.

*Ask him what he wants to be,* murmured the drowned child's voice, the one she knew best, the one that had followed her out of the valley like a duckling that had imprinted on the wrong mother. *Everything else you've carved wanted to be remembered. This one might want to be something new.*

Paloma turned the femur over in her hands. It was the last messenger's — the man whose name had been worn away by a century of carrying, whose bones Ilya had borne across the Steppe in a satchel against his spine without ever telling her what they were. She had forgiven him for that. Mostly. The forgiveness was still cooling on a windowsill somewhere, and she took it down to look at it from time to time.

But the bone was here now, and it was honest in the way only dead things are honest: completely, and about everything at once.

"All right," she told it. "What do you want to be?"

And the dead around her — the signatories sleeping in their glass below, the salt-court ghosts drifting at the edges of the lamplight, the hundred small voices of the Steppe that had begun, since the rite's first clauses took root, to sound less like arguing and more like a very large family at a very long dinner — all went quiet, so that whatever answer came could be heard alone.

It came as warmth. Not words. A feeling in the marrow, which is where bones keep their diaries: the sensation of being carried. Of being useful after a long uselessness. Of traveling toward something instead of away.

"Good," Paloma said, and her voice cracked slightly, and she pretended it hadn't. "Then we agree on the important part."

She picked up her burin and began.

* * *

Her grandmother's tools fit her hands the way old songs fit a voice — imperfectly, with room to grow into them. There were eleven of them, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the chest that had crossed the boundary stones strapped to Paloma's back, and each had a name scratched into the handle in the old valley script. *Patience.* *Mercy.* *The One That Argues Back.*

She was using The One That Argues Back now, because the covenant-bone required argument. That was the whole point.

The plan — if you could call something a plan when it had been invented at three in the morning by a bone-carver, a dying messenger, and a ghost-queen who kept trying to add riders to every sentence — was this: the old covenant had been written in bound flesh. A living smith, chained into the glass each century, screaming at the seam between worlds so the seam would hold. Her grandmother had broken that rite rather than feed it another child, and the hundred years bought by that sabotage were nearly spent.

So Paloma would write a new one. Not in flesh. In bone. And not bound bone — *willing* bone, given freely, clause by clause, each clause contributed by a ghost who chose to sign.

"It will not hold," Vess had said, the first time she heard it. The salt queen had a way of pronouncing doom the way other people commented on weather. "A covenant of volunteers. Like an army of poets."

"Poets hold lines," Ilya had said, and then looked so pleased with himself that Paloma had thrown an ash-grass cushion at his head, which he caught, which made it worse.

But the dead had not laughed. The dead had gone quiet in that listening way they had, and then the drowned child had said, softly, *I'll sign first,* and everything after that was craft.

Craft, Paloma could do. Craft was the one language she had never needed magic to speak.

* * *

The first fracture was from her seventh year, when she fell from the mill roof chasing a cat named Duchess who had never once deserved the loyalty. The bone had healed crooked, and for a season her wrist ached before rain, and her grandmother had held it in both hands and said nothing, which Paloma now understood had been its own kind of grief.

She could not take the bone out of her arm. But bone remembers, and memory can be asked to give.

She sat cross-legged under the lamp with her own forearm laid bare on the cloth, the burin hovering, and felt profoundly ridiculous.

"This is either going to work," she informed the assembled dead, "or we are all going to pretend it didn't happen."

*I once signed a covenant with my whole skeleton,* said a voice like dry leaves — Signatory Nine, the one they called the Drowned Queen's Rival, though she preferred to be called Marta. *You are being very modest.*

Paloma pressed the burin's tip to the skin above the old break and thought, not of cutting, but of *asking*. That was the difference, she realized — the thing her grandmother must have known, the thing the old order had forgotten in their hunger for certainty. You did not carve the covenant out of someone. You carved it *with* them, and the body, like the dead, got a vote.

Her wrist warmed. The ache that lived there — seven years old, rain-forecasting, hers — rose up through the skin like a bubble through honey, and where it broke the surface, the burin moved on its own, or she moved it without deciding to; the distinction had stopped mattering days ago. A line appeared. Then a curve. Then a clause.

She couldn't read it yet. The covenant-script wrote itself in the grammar of the dead, which was not letters but agreements — each mark a promise shaped like a shape. She would learn to read it the way she'd learned to hear the dead: slowly, humbly, and mostly by being wrong in interesting ways.

"That's mine," she whispered. "That clause is mine." It was the ache itself, she understood suddenly — the rain-sense, the crooked healing, the whole history of the fall. She was signing her broken places into the law of the world. *Let what is damaged be load-bearing.*

Somewhere behind her, Ilya stopped pretending to catalog salt-pillar acoustics and just watched, his mouth slightly open, the way he watched everything she made — as if craft were a magic greater than any he'd been raised on, which, she was beginning to suspect, it was.

"Don't stare," she said. "It's rude and also I'm working."

"I'm not staring. I'm witnessing. There's a difference. Witnessing has dignity."

"Witnessing has dignity," she agreed, "if the witness makes tea."

He made tea. He was learning the valley way — too much leaf, boiled too long, bitter enough to strip paint — and she drank it anyway, because some things you accept as they are, and some people you love as they're becoming, and these turned out to be the same lesson wearing different coats.

* * *

By the fourth day, the workshop had developed a rhythm, and the rhythm had developed opinions.

Mornings belonged to the messenger's bones. Paloma worked the femur in the grey light while the last messenger's ghost — thin, patient, worn smooth as river stone — stood beside her and dictated. Not words. *Terms.* He had carried the prophecy book across half the world and died within sight of the valley stones, and his death had taught him things about distance and duty that no living throat could say properly.

*Clause:* *that no message shall cost more than the messenger chooses to pay.* His voice was barely a breath, but Paloma carved it deep, because shallow promises break first.

"Noon belongs to me," Vess announced, arriving each day with the punctuality of tide and the graciousness of a landslide. The salt queen had, over the weeks, migrated from predator to ally to something almost worse: a collaborator with taste. "Your clause structure is peasant work, carver. A covenant should have *proportions*. Symmetry. A sense of occasion."

"A covenant should hold," Paloma said, not looking up.

"Holding *is* an occasion. You simply lack style about it." Vess drifted around the workbench, trailing cold like a draft with ambitions. "Here. Your third clause ends on an open curve. Unfinished. Sloppy."

"It's not unfinished. It's *open*. It means the terms can be renegotiated by future generations instead of waiting a century to fail catastrophically."

Vess was silent for a long moment. When Paloma glanced up, the ghost-queen was looking at the open curve with an expression that on a living face would have been called wistful, and on Vess was merely called Tuesday.

"My sister and I fought for a century," Vess said, "over whether the covenant should be a wall or a door." She touched the curve without touching it. "Neither of us thought of a hinge."

Then she swept out, and Paloma carved the word *hinge* into the margin of her notes, because sometimes even queens contribute by accident.

Afternoons were the hardest and the best. Afternoons were when the signatories came up from the chasm — not all thirteen, not yet, the waking cost them dearly — but two or three at a time, rising through the glass floor like reflections choosing to precede their owners. Each brought a clause drawn from their death, as the council had promised, and each death was a different country, and Paloma had to learn its language before she could carve its border.

Signatory Four had died of thirst on the Steppe when it was still grassland, and his clause was about *thirst* — about wanting so much it hollows you, and how a law should leave room for wanting without punishing it. Paloma carved his clause in spirals, tightening, and then, at the center, leaving a space that filled nothing and held everything.

Signatory Eleven had died old, in bed, surrounded by grandchildren, and her clause embarrassed everyone by being simple: *that the ending be allowed to be ordinary.* The other ghosts grumbled. Paloma carved it twice.

And the drowned child — her first client, her north star, the small voice that had taught her the trade — contributed the clause that made her put down the tools and walk out into the salt wind until she could see straight again.

*Clause:* *that no one be asked to stay who wishes to go, nor sent away who wishes to remain.*

"That's just kindness," Paloma had said, when she came back in, red-eyed, daring anyone to comment.

"Yes," said the child, serene. "Laws forget it. Someone should write it down."

* * *

On the sixth evening, her grandmother arrived.

Not as a ghost — the honored dead of the valley could not cross the stones, and perhaps that was its own mercy. She arrived the way grandmothers arrive in workshops everywhere: through the work itself. Paloma was fitting the messenger's radius-bone against her own carved clause when she found, already present in the grain, a hairline channel that she had not cut — a maker's mark, the double-spiral of the Reyes line, pressed into the bone before it was ever bone, in the marrow, in the blood, in the inheritance.

Her grandmother had been here the whole time. In the tools. In the hands. In the instinct that said *ask, don't take* — an instinct Paloma had assumed was hers alone and recognized, with a jolt that was equal parts fury and gratitude, as a lesson taught so early and so gently it had felt like personality.

"You could have written a letter," Paloma muttered to the empty air. "Oh, wait."

*There is a letter,* said the drowned child helpfully. *In the sigil. Has been the whole time. I assumed you knew.*

Paloma set down her tools with the exaggerated care of a woman defusing her own temper, retrieved the half-finished sigil from her pack, and held it to the lamp.

And there, in channels too fine for any eye that hadn't spent six days learning to read agreements-shaped-like-shapes, was writing. Her grandmother's hand. A letter, folded into the bone itself, waiting for someone with the eyes to find it — waiting, Paloma understood with a chill that was not entirely unpleasant, for exactly the carver her grandmother must have prayed she'd become.

She did not read it that night. Some letters you have to grow into, like your mother's coat, like your own name.

She wrapped the sigil back in its cloth, set it beside the growing covenant-bone — messenger's femur and her own signed fractures and thirteen clauses and one open curve and the word *hinge* — and stepped out into the salt night, where Ilya was sitting on a pillar watching the moon make its slow, counting climb.

"Nine turns down," he said quietly. "Four left. How's it going in there?"

"The dead are co-authoring a legal document with me," Paloma said, sitting beside him. "One of them died of thirst four thousand years ago and has strong feelings about desire. Another thinks laws should permit boring deaths. Vess wants more symmetry. The child wants kindness. And my grandmother hid a letter inside a bone I've been carrying for weeks like a sentimental idiot."

Ilya smiled — that smile of his, the one that had started as wariness and had become, without either of them filing paperwork, something she checked the horizon for. "And you?"

"What about me?"

"What does the carver want the covenant to say? Your clause. The one nobody dictated."

Paloma watched the moon. Thirteen turns, and it had taken her twenty-nine years and one apocalypse to notice that she had never once been asked what *she* wanted — not by the dead, not by the valley, not by the story that had apparently been written about her a century before her birth.

"That no one's life be written before they live it," she said. "That's mine. I'm saving it for last."

"Good clause," Ilya said.

"You haven't read it."

"I don't need to. I know your hand."

She was still deciding what to do with that — with him, with the warm ridiculous fact of him — when the covenant-bone, alone in the workshop behind them, began to sing.

Not metaphorically. Not the resonance-thrum she'd discovered on the glass dunes. It sang — a low, sweet, three-noted phrase, repeating, patient, unmistakably *waiting for an answer* — and every ghost on the Steppe went silent at once, the way a household goes silent when the letter finally comes.

Paloma stood up so fast she knocked the tea over.

"That's not one of our clauses," she said. "Ilya. That's not one of ours. Something's answering us. Or—" she swallowed— "*asking*."

The song rose again, sweeter, closer, coming now not from the workshop but from the dark beyond the salt pillars — from the direction of the chasm, where thirteen sleepers lay in glass, and where something older than all of them had just woken up and wanted, very badly, to talk.
