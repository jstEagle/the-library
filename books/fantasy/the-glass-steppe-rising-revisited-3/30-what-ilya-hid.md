## 30. What Ilya Hid

The trouble with confessing a lie, Ilya had learned over the course of one very long autumn on the Glass Steppe, was that there was never a good moment for it. There were only less catastrophic moments, and most of those got eaten by emergencies.

He had been carrying the truth since the Salt Court. Longer, really — since the night he first read the book in a dying order's scriptorium, by candlelight, with his hands shaking so badly he'd had to press them flat against the table to read at all. *The messenger dies at the rite,* the final page said, in a hand that had gone careful and small, as though the writer had hoped that writing it gently would make it gentler. *The messenger dies at the rite, and this is not a warning but a schedule.*

A schedule. As if his death were a train arriving.

For three hundred miles he had told himself he would tell her tomorrow. Tomorrow was a remarkably durable country. It had survived hunters, salt courts, mirrored rivers, a battle, two moon-turns, and one genuinely humiliating incident involving a mirage of a bakery that had turned out to be solid glass when he'd tried to buy a roll from it. Tomorrow had survived all of it. Tonight, apparently, it was going to die.

Because Paloma was holding the book.

She had taken it from the satchel while he slept — no, he corrected himself honestly, while he'd pretended to sleep, which was its own small sin — and she sat across the fire now with the last page open on her knees, reading by the light of the coals. Her face did what her face always did when she worked: nothing. Carvers' faces. She could hold a chisel against a dead man's femur and look like she was deciding what to have for dinner.

But her thumb had stopped moving down the page. That was how he knew.

"The messenger," she said quietly, "dies at the rite."

"Paloma—"

"And then there's a second line." Her voice didn't rise. That was worse. He had heard her shout at ghosts; he had heard her argue with Vess until the salt pillars rang. This was the voice she used for bones that had been broken a long time and healed wrong. "There's a second line underneath, scraped almost away, and someone wrote over it. Someone wrote *he will understand why* over the top of it."

"I can explain."

"Can you." She looked up. The firelight caught the fine scars on her hands, the ones she'd told him about at the campfire — every fracture she'd ever healed, the map of a life spent being careful. "Because I've been sitting here for the length of one candle doing arithmetic, Ilya. And the sum keeps coming out to *you knew*. You knew before you crossed the stones. You knew at the boundary when you bled light all over my memorial stones. You knew when you taught me the order's signs, when you told me about the last messenger, when—" Her breath caught, and she mastered it, because of course she did. "When we sat at that fire and you told me you volunteered because you were dying anyway. You let me think that was the whole of it."

"It was mostly the whole of it," he offered.

"*Mostly.*"

"There was also," he said, "a secondary portion."

She closed the book. Gently. That was somehow the most frightening thing she could have done. She set it down beside her like a surgeon setting aside an instrument she would need again later, and stood, and walked around the fire toward him, and he discovered that a man who had faced the Hollow Wind's hunters without flinching could still feel his stomach attempt to leave through his heels when a five-foot-two bone-carver walked toward him with her arms folded.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'm already sitting."

"Good. Stay accurate. It'll help you."

* * *

Here is what Ilya Petrov had learned about Paloma Reyes in the months since the boundary stones: she was not angry the way other people were angry. Other people's anger was weather. Hers was geology. It moved slowly, applied pressure evenly, and reshaped everything it touched permanently.

So he sat, and she stood over him, and the Steppe breathed around them — that faint glassy sigh it made at night now that the covenant-bone was half-carved and the land had begun, tentatively, to trust them.

"You want to know why I didn't tell you," he said.

"No," she said. "I want you to tell me why you didn't tell me. Those are different things. The first one is you performing an explanation. The second one is you telling the truth. I carve bones for people who can't lie anymore, Ilya. I know the difference."

"That's an unfair advantage."

"Yes," she agreed. "It is. Start talking."

He started talking. It came out worse than he'd rehearsed, the way confessions always do — rehearsed versions are smooth like river stones, and real ones come out jagged and embarrassing.

"I found the book eleven years ago," he said. "No — I was *given* it eleven years ago. I read the whole thing in a week. Every date, every name, every turn of the moon. And the last page says the messenger dies at the rite. Not might. Not *if things go poorly*. Dies. It's written the way the rest of the book is written — the way your arrival was written, the way tonight's date was written a century before either of us was born. It isn't a prophecy in the sense of a warning. It's a record. Whoever wrote it was taking minutes of a meeting that hadn't happened yet."

"And you decided," Paloma said, "to attend anyway."

"I decided the meeting needed a quorum."

"Don't be clever. I'm asking you not to be clever right now."

"I'm sorry." And he was; he could hear how the cleverness sounded from outside, how it had always sounded — the little lacquered box he put his fear in so it wouldn't rattle. "You're right. Here's the truth without the varnish. I was twenty-three years old and I had a sickness the order's physicians called *a long goodbye*, and I had just read a book that said my death had a purpose, a date, a place, a meaning. Do you know what that is like? Everyone else gets to rage at the unfairness of dying. I was handed a receipt. My death was *purchased* and *scheduled* and somebody upstream considered it fair payment. And some shameful part of me was relieved. Because it meant I wasn't wasted. It meant the thing eating me from the inside was at least eating me *toward* something."

Paloma had gone very still. Not carver-still. Listener-still. He knew the difference now; he'd learned it watching her work with the dead. One stillness was control. The other was attention.

"So when the order chose me," he went on, "I said yes before they finished asking. And when I read the page properly — the second time, sober, with better light — I understood something the first reading hadn't given me. The prophecy doesn't say *the messenger dies at the rite* because death is required. It says it because that's what happened *in the version where nobody fought it*. The book records the road as it lies. It doesn't account for a carver who rewrites covenants out of her own healed fractures. It doesn't account for willing bone. As far as the book knows, none of that exists yet. So far as the book is concerned, I walk into that chasm alone and I don't walk out."

"But you didn't tell me that."

"No."

"Why not?" And here her voice finally cracked, just at the seam, one hairline fracture, and he hated himself with a completeness that felt almost restful. "Why not at the stones? Why not at the ford, when I was crying over the mother I'll never be? Why not at the campfire, Ilya, when you asked me to be honest and I *was*, when I told you I never wanted to be alone in that valley, when I gave you the truest thing I own?"

"Because you would have stopped."

"I would have *what?*"

"You would have stopped moving." He made himself say it plainly, no lacquer, no box. "You would have done what you've wanted to do since the mirrored ford — find another way, any way, a way that costs only me and not you. You'd have spent our nine remaining turns trying to unwrite one sentence in a century-old book instead of carving the covenant. And the valley would have drowned in magic, and the Steppe would have unraveled into the mortal world, and everyone we've marched with would have gone to war over the corpse of a chance we traded for one man's extra year. I did the arithmetic. I chose. I chose the world over telling you the truth, and I'd choose it again, and I know that makes me a monster in at least one story, and I am so tired, Paloma. I am so tired of being the sentence in the book."

The silence afterward was long enough that the fire settled twice.

Then Paloma crouched down in front of him, so their eyes were level, and took his hands — the scholar's soft hands, the messenger's scarred palms — and held them the way she held bones she was deciding how to mend.

"You're not a monster," she said. "You're a man who loved people in the stupidest possible way, which is silently, and from in front, so they couldn't see your face." Her thumb moved over his knuckles. "But Ilya. Listen to me. Listen with the whole of yourself, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to be furious again for a while. You don't get to decide what I can carry. That's not love. That's just another person writing my story for me, and I have had *enough* of that. A dead grandmother did it. A prophecy book did it. A ghost queen did it. I will carve my own name into my own covenant before I let the man I—" She stopped. The word hung there, uncarved, unfinished, between them like a chip of bone neither dared lift.

"The man you what?" he asked, very softly.

"Don't you dare use my own technique on me."

"It's a good technique. I learned it from a master."

"Ilya."

"Paloma."

She exhaled through her nose — the sound she made when a ghost was being difficult — and finished it anyway, glaring at him as she did, as if the words were being extracted under protest: "*Love.* The man I love. There. Are you happy? You made me say it during an argument, which is the worst possible craftsmanship, and I hope you're ashamed."

"I'm not," he said. "I'm going to remember it forever. I'm going to be insufferable about it."

"You're going to be *dead* about it, if you keep hiding pages from me." But her grip on his hands hadn't loosened, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain, all three at once, which he suspected was simply her face now and would be forever. "Here's what happens next. You don't get to die on schedule. That book recorded a world where you walked into the chasm alone, and that world is gone — I broke it, I'm breaking it further every day, every clause the dead sign is a clause the old prophecy never saw coming. Your death isn't a schedule anymore. It's a *draft*. And I am the editor."

"The book has been right about everything else."

"The book has been right about everything *except the parts I changed*," she said. "That's the whole point of me. That's the inheritance they ran from and the gift they left. We are not bound by what was written. We are bound by what we agree to. So here is my agreement, and you will sign it or I will carve your objection into a spoon and make you eat with it: no more hidden pages. No more protective arithmetic. If the covenant asks for a price, we hear it together, and we answer together, and if the answer is terrible then at least it will be terrible in stereo. Can you live with that?"

And Ilya — who had spent eleven years being a sentence, a schedule, a receipt, a road laid down by a stranger's pen — felt something in his chest come loose that had been set there so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

"For however long that turns out to be," he said. "Yes."

"Wrong answer format," she said, and kissed him, briefly and fiercely, like stamping a document. "Try: yes, Paloma, I will stop lying to the woman I love."

"Yes, Paloma. I will stop lying to the woman I love."

"Better. Witnessed." She glanced pointedly at the darkness beyond the firelight, where the Steppe's dead murmured in their glass. "They heard it. The dead are terrible gossips. You're bound now."

"I was bound before. This is a better contract."

* * *

They sat together after that, shoulder to shoulder, and she showed him the page properly, tracing the scraped-under line with a fingernail. Beneath *he will understand why*, in the older hand, nearly erased: *unless the carver unbinds him.*

"She knew," Ilya said slowly. "Whoever wrote this. They saw both endings."

"Or she hoped hard enough to leave a door." Paloma tilted the page toward the coals. "My grandmother destroyed the rest of the rite rather than bind a child. But she left this. One scraped line. A crack in the wall for someone to widen later." Her jaw set. "I'm going to widen it into a gate."

"You realize," he said, "that if you save me from a prophecy, the book becomes wrong, and a century-old order built its entire theology on that book, and Vess will claim it proves covenants can be rewritten whenever convenient, and the Hollow Wind—"

"Ilya."

"Yes?"

"You're doing the clever thing again."

"...I'll stop."

Above them, through the split and mending sky, the moon slid onward — thinner now, hurrying, nine turns behind it and four ahead, and as they watched, it shuddered once, like a wheel with a bent axle, and the whole plain of glass sighed and shifted beneath them. Far off toward the council-chasm, a light kindled that had no business kindling: cold, white, patient.

Paloma stood, pulling him up with her, and slung the satchel over her shoulder with the book inside it — closed, at last, for both of them.

"Come on," she said. "We've got a gate to finish. And you're going to tell me every single thing you've been carrying, start to finish, in order, while we walk. Consider it a commission. I charge by the confession."

"How much for the first one?"

"Everything," said Paloma Reyes, and started walking toward the chasm, and the light, and the thirteenth turn, and — for the first time in eleven years — Ilya followed her without checking the book to see what happened next.
