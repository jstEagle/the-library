## 31. The Ninth Turn

The ninth turn came at dawn, and it came rude.

Paloma felt it before she saw it — a shudder in the glass beneath her bedroll, like the Steppe clearing its throat to say something unpleasant. The sky over the eastern horizon went the color of an old bruise, and somewhere far off, one of the trapped cities screamed its brief, terrible scream and went quiet again.

She had not slept. That was not surprising. What was surprising was that she had spent the sleepless hours not weeping, which she considered a personal triumph, but sharpening her carving knives, which Ilya would have called "concerning" if he'd been awake to call it anything.

He wasn't awake. He lay three feet away, breathing shallowly, his fever broken sometime in the small hours but his face still gray as old tallow. The prophecy's words sat between them like a stone neither of them could pick up: *the messenger dies at the rite.* He had known for weeks. He had carried it across half the Steppe with that ridiculous crooked smile of his, letting her believe they were walking toward survival when really they were walking toward his funeral.

*You lied to me,* she had said last night, when Vess's runner brought the torn page and the truth together.

*I omitted,* he had corrected, from his bedroll, with the audacity of a dying man. *Lying is when you say something false. I simply failed to say something true. There's a legal distinction. The Salt Court has whole treatises on it.*

*You let me carve your name into the covenant-bone thinking you'd live.*

*I let you carve my name into it because you were going to anyway, and because if you'd known, you'd have stopped.* He had paused then, and his voice had gone quiet in a way she'd rarely heard it. *And I couldn't let you stop. Not for my sake.*

That had been the worst part. Not the lie — the lie she could almost forgive, because she understood it, because she had told smaller versions of it her whole life (*I'm fine, Grandmother, the voices don't bother me, I don't mind being alone*). The worst part was that he'd been right. If he had told her, she would have stopped. She would have found some other way or no way at all, and the world would have come apart around her stubbornness, and she would have called it love.

So she had done what she always did when she couldn't argue: she had picked up a knife and worked.

The covenant-bone lay across her knees now, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. It was beautiful. She allowed herself to think so, because false modesty was just another kind of lie and she'd had enough of those this week. She had carved it from her own healed fractures — the wrist she'd broken falling out of the plum tree at nine, the ribs from the ice, the finger snapped by a slipping chisel — and from the last messenger's remains, the small bundle of bones she'd carried unknowingly since the boundary stones, since before she'd known what he was or what he'd paid. Each ghost who had touched the work had left a clause in it, a line of script finer than spider silk, and when she held the bone up to the light the clauses caught fire like frost on a window.

It was missing something, though. She'd known it for two days and hadn't wanted to name it.

The bone needed an author's mark. A signature. And every signature she knew how to make was a valley signature — the little spiral her grandmother used on memorial bones, the knotwork of the Reyes line, all of it inherited, all of it someone else's hand guiding hers.

She didn't want that anymore. She was beginning to suspect that was the whole point of everything.

* * *

Ilya woke mid-morning, which for him lately counted as a miracle, and immediately tried to sit up, immediately failed, and immediately pretended he hadn't tried.

"Don't," Paloma said, without looking up from her knives.

"I was just stretching."

"You were just sitting up."

"Stretching is sitting up with ambition." He settled back down, wincing, and studied her sideways with those scholar's eyes that missed nothing even when the rest of him was failing. "You're angry."

"I'm furious."

"Ah. Good. For a moment there I worried you'd gone soft on me." He tried the smile. It didn't land the way it usually did; fevers take the polish off a man. "Paloma—"

"You should have told me."

"Yes."

"That's it? Yes?"

"What would you like me to add? An apology? I have one, it's sincere, it's yours. A justification? I have one of those too, but I suspect you've already built a better one than I could, because you always do." He folded his hands on his chest, the picture of a man surrendering to a superior force. "I hid it because I was afraid. Not of dying. Of you stopping. And that was cowardice dressed up as strategy, and I'm not proud of it, and I'd probably do it again, which makes it worse, doesn't it? Honesty about one's own dishonesty. The scholars' curse."

Paloma put down her knife very carefully, because the alternative was throwing it.

"Do you know what I did last night?" she said. "While you were snoring like a landslide?"

"I do not snore. I breathe with conviction."

"While you were breathing with conviction," she said, "I sat here and I thought about turning back. Or stopping. Or finding some cave to hide in until the thirteenth turn comes and the world ends quietly without anyone blaming me for it. I thought about it for hours. It was very restful, imagining giving up. Like a hot bath."

"And then?"

"And then I realized something that made me so angry I sharpened all four of my knives." She held one up so it caught the bruise-colored light. "Every story I've ever been in was written by somebody else. My grandmother wrote the story where I stay in the valley and never ask questions. The dead wrote the story where I'm the carver who hears and nothing more. The prophecy book wrote the story where you die and I save the world and everyone lives miserably ever after according to plan. Even you—" she pointed the knife at him, gently, "—you wrote a story where you're the noble messenger who dies on schedule, and you didn't even leave room in it for me to object."

"That does sound like me. I edit ruthlessly."

"Well." She set the knife down. "I'm done reading other people's drafts. If I keep going — and I haven't decided yet, don't interrupt —"

"You've decided. You've been decided since dawn. You rewrapped the covenant-bone twice and you only do that when you've made up your mind and want your hands to know it too."

She stared at him. "I hate that you notice things."

"It's my single redeeming quality. Well. That and the hair."

"The hair is a mixed blessing," she said, and was annoyed to hear her own mouth twitching. This was the trouble with Ilya: even furious at him, some traitorous part of her kept laughing. "Here is what I've decided, since you're going to be smug about it either way. I'm not doing this to save the world."

He blinked. "You're... not?"

"No. The world can look after itself for five minutes; it's managed this long. Vess wants the covenant saved for her faction. The signatories want it saved for their memory. The Hollow Wind wants it unsaved entirely. Everyone has a reason, and every reason is bigger than me, and I am so tired of being the smallest person in every room." She pulled the covenant-bone into her lap and unwrapped it, and the morning light caught the ghost-script and made it burn. "I'm doing this because it's mine. My grandmother broke the rite, so the fixing belongs to my family. My bones are in this thing. My dead are in this thing. And nobody — not a prophecy written a hundred years ago, not a ghost-queen with a salt army, not a wind with opinions, and not you, Ilya, especially not you — gets to write my ending for me. If you die at the rite, it will be because the rite takes you, not because a book said so. Books are paper. Paper can be argued with."

For a long moment Ilya said nothing. Then, softly: "You know that's not how prophecy works."

"I know exactly how prophecy works. I've been listening to the dead my whole life, and do you know what they are? They're people who finished their sentences. That's all a prophecy is — a sentence somebody finished before you got a word in. So I'm going to get a word in." She looked down at the bone, at the empty space near its base where the author's mark belonged, and her voice steadied. "There's room here. One clause left unclaimed. Every ghost gave theirs, and the last messenger gave his, and there's one space left, and I've been saving it."

"For whom?"

"For whoever's brave enough to sign last." She met his eyes. "Signatures go in order of willingness, Grandmother's notes say. The first signer risks least — everyone else has already committed. The last signer risks most. They're the one who says yes knowing exactly what it costs." Her throat tightened, and she made herself keep going. "I'm asking you to be last. Not because the book says you die — because I refuse to let a book decide whether you do. Sign it yourself. In your own hand. Make it your sentence, not the prophecy's."

Ilya looked at her for a long time. The fever had burned him down to essentials, and what was left underneath was younger than she'd thought, and frightened, and braver than the fear.

"If I sign," he said slowly, "and I still die—"

"Then you'll die having chosen it, which is the only death I've ever been able to make peace with. I carve memorials, Ilya. Do you know the difference between the ones that haunt me and the ones that don't? It's never how they died. It's whether the dying was theirs." She held out the carving knife, handle first. "So choose. Out loud. Where I can hear it."

He took the knife. His hand shook — fever, exhaustion, or feeling, she couldn't tell and didn't try to. He turned the covenant-bone over twice, reading the ghost-clauses the way he read everything, thoroughly and slightly aloud under his breath, and then he laughed, a small cracked sound.

"There's a clause here from the drowned child," he said. "From your valley. The one whose memorial you were carving when I arrived."

"She signed first. Before anyone asked her. She said—" Paloma's voice caught. "She said she'd been waiting a hundred years for someone to fix the door she drowned trying to get through."

Ilya pressed his palm flat against the bone, closed his eyes, and said, clearly, in the voice of a man making a formal declaration rather than a confession:

"I, Ilya Voskresen, last messenger of a dying order, sign last and willingly. Not because a book commands it. Because a carver asked me. And because—" he opened his eyes and looked straight at her, and did not look away, "—because I would rather write one true sentence with her than a hundred accurate ones alone."

The bone took his words. Paloma felt it happen — a warmth under her palms, a hum like a struck bell, and a new line of script bloomed along the base in a hand she didn't recognize, angular and hurried and unmistakably alive. The ghost-script flickered gold once, twice, and settled.

Somewhere far away, the Steppe exhaled.

"That," Ilya said faintly, "was not supposed to happen. I think. Possibly. My order's texts were vague on the subject of signing while feverish."

"The bone accepted you." Paloma was grinning and couldn't stop. "Not the prophecy. Not the rite. The bone. Do you understand what that means? The covenant isn't waiting for the thirteenth turn to judge us anymore. It's already listening. We're not performing the old rite at the chasm — we're bringing it a finished document and asking it to countersign."

"Is that allowed?"

"Absolutely not. Which is why it might work."

* * *

They broke camp within the hour, because the ninth turn had passed and the tenth was coming faster now — days apart instead of weeks, the moon stumbling toward its finish like a drunkard hurrying home. The Steppe itself seemed to feel the hurry. The glass dunes had begun to shift overnight, rearranging themselves into long ridges that all pointed the same way, like compass needles, like fingers.

Toward the council-chasm.

Vess's outriders found them by noon — salt soldiers, glittering and silent, carrying a banner of white ash-cloth. Their captain, a hollow-eyed woman named Sereth whom Paloma trusted approximately as far as she could throw a salt pillar, delivered her message in the flat tone of someone reciting terms they didn't endorse:

"The Queen of Salt sends word. The Hollow Wind has taken the northern passes. Its storm-front moves on the chasm ahead of you. She offers her host to march beside yours — such as it is, a carver, a dying scholar, and whatever ghosts still answer you." Sereth paused, and something almost human crossed her face. "She also sends a private message. To the bone-carver."

"Go on."

Sereth leaned close. "She says: *Your grandmother cheated them all, girl. Cheated the Steppe, cheated the sacrifice, cheated me. I hated her for a century. Then I watched you sharpen four knives rather than cry, and I thought — ah. There's the cheating blood. Good. Use it.*" The captain straightened. "That's all."

Paloma stood very still for a moment, holding the covenant-bone against her chest, feeling its new signature warm against her heartbeat.

"Well," said Ilya, shouldering his pack with a wince he disguised badly. "Your grandmother: saboteur, deserter, and posthumous flatterer. She really did think of everything."

"She really did." Paloma looked north, where the horizon had begun to darken with something that was not weather, and felt the fear arrive right on schedule — cold, enormous, entirely reasonable. She let it sit for a moment. Then she set it down beside all the other things she was carrying, next to the grief and the fury and the strange bright weight of being believed in, and she picked up her feet and started walking.

Behind her, Ilya scrambled to follow, calling out, "For the record, when historians write about this march, I'd like it noted that I walked most of it."

"Noted," Paloma said. "In your own hand."

Ahead, the dark on the horizon grew wings.
