## 32. March on the Chasm

I had marched in armies before. Well — *army* is generous. I had once walked three days behind a column of salt-soldiers who were mostly enthusiasm and borrowed boots, and I had learned then that no one in a marching host ever feels as brave as they look from a distance. Up close, an army is coughing and blister-plasters and someone quietly asking whether anyone else is terrified.

This one was no different, except that half of it was dead.

The salt-host of Vess came down out of the pillar-courts in ranks of gleaming white, their banners made of pressed ash that never quite settled. Behind them walked the ash-folk of the burned cities, gray and patient, carrying their dead between them like lanterns. And at the center, where any sensible commander would put the supply wagons, we put Paloma.

She had objected to this arrangement for two full days.

"I'm not fragile," she'd said, carving as she talked, because she always carved while she talked now, her hands restless as birds. "I'm the one doing the rite. Shouldn't I be at the front?"

"You're the only person alive who can end this," Vess had answered, gliding alongside with the particular serenity of a queen who has recently discovered humility and is still getting used to the fit of it. "The front is where things get broken."

"Then I'll carve myself back together," Paloma said, and Vess had looked at me, and I had shrugged, because what was I going to do? Argue with either of them?

In the end we compromised: Paloma marched in the center until the last mile, and then she would walk to the chasm on her own feet, unguarded, because the rite required her to arrive unbloodied and unbowed, and also because — though she'd never say it — she wanted the Hollow Wind to see her coming.

Now, on the morning of the twelfth turn's eve, I rode at the head of the column on a horse made of compacted wind, which sounds impressive until you learn that riding one is like sitting on an argument. It kept trying to have opinions about our route.

"Not through the pass," it hissed into my ear. "The pass smells wrong."

"The pass is the fastest way."

"The pass smells like *her*."

I pulled the horse up short and looked back down the line. Ten thousand ghosts and perhaps four hundred living volunteers — valley folk who had crossed the stones when word went out that the boundary was failing, farmers and millers and one extremely determined cheesemaker named Odile who had announced that if the world was ending she would at least die somewhere with better views than her cellar. They stretched back along the glass dunes like a river deciding which way to break.

And above us all, on the horizon, the storm waited.

It had been waiting for a day and a night, coiled over the council-chasm like a black hand over a wound. The Hollow Wind did not need to march. She simply stood at the place we had to reach and let the weather do her talking.

"She knows we're coming," said Vess, materializing beside me without the courtesy of footsteps. Ghosts could be so smug about physics.

"She's known since the ford," I said. "Possibly since the Salt Court. Possibly since before I was born — the book does go in for that sort of thing."

"And still you walk toward her."

"That's generally how battles work, yes." I squinted at the storm. Lightning moved inside it in patterns too regular to be weather. Words, maybe. Or teeth. "Vess, can I ask you something honest?"

"I would be devastated if you didn't."

"When this is over — if it ends well — what happens to your faction? The terms you wanted. Binding the messenger, keeping the old law's shape. You've given all that up. Why?"

For a long moment the ghost-queen said nothing, and I watched the ash-banners ripple behind her, and I thought she wouldn't answer. Then:

"Because I died for a covenant that ate children," she said, "and I told myself for nine hundred years that it was necessary. Necessary things should not require that much lying." She turned her pale gaze on me. "You are necessary, messenger. That is why I offered to bind you. And that is why I will stand in front of you tomorrow instead. I am tired of confusing the two."

I didn't have anything clever to say to that, which for me is nearly unprecedented, so I just nodded and kicked my argumentative horse onward toward the storm.

* * *

We made camp within sight of the chasm's edge, close enough that at night the sleeping signatories glowed faintly below us, thirteen shapes under the glass like coins at the bottom of a well. Paloma sat apart, working by lantern-light on the covenant-bone, and I brought her tea that Odile had somehow produced from a war camp, because apparently cheese-people are resourceful people.

"How is it?" I asked.

"It sings," she said simply. She held it up. The bone was long as a forearm now, white shot through with threads of gold where each ghost's clause had been set into it — Vess's law of shelter, the ash-folk's law of memory, the drowned child's law, gods help me, of *listening*, because Paloma had asked the little ghost what the old covenant most lacked and the child had said, "Nobody asked us anything." "Every time I think it's finished, another voice comes. Last night it was a shepherd who died four thousand years ago. He wants a clause about fences."

"Fences?"

"He says good boundaries make good neighbors. He was very firm about it." She smiled down at the bone, and the lantern caught the scar-tissue smoothness of her own healed fractures worked into its length, and I felt the familiar ache in my chest that I had been carefully not naming for weeks.

"Paloma," I said. "Tomorrow—"

"Don't." She didn't look up. "Don't do the speech where you tell me something important in case you don't survive. I hate those speeches. Everyone in stories gives those speeches and then survives anyway, and then it's awkward."

"What if I don't survive?"

"Then I'll carve your name so beautifully the dead themselves will be jealous, and I'll never let you hear the end of it." Now she looked up, and her eyes were steady and furious and kind. "Say nothing important. Drink your tea. We'll both be insufferable survivors together."

So I drank my tea and said nothing important, and if my hand shook slightly on the cup, the lantern-light was dim enough to grant me mercy.

* * *

The storm hit us an hour before dawn.

Not at dawn, as honor might suggest. An hour before, when the camp was thickest with sleep and the watch-fires low. The Hollow Wind had never promised honor; she had only ever promised arithmetic — fewer deaths if everyone simply stopped.

The first warning was the horses. The wind-horses screamed as one, ten thousand voices of compressed air crying out, and then the sky came down.

It did not come as rain. It came as *memory* — the storm flung the Steppe's own past at us, mirages torn up out of the glass and hurled like boulders. A city fell on the eastern pickets whole, towers first, screaming with the voices of everyone who had ever lived in it. A forest of fossil-trees crashed across the southern line, petrified branches shattering into shrapnel. And through it all moved the hunters, the hollow-eyed ones, running along the wind itself, their feet never touching ground.

"To the chasm!" Vess's voice cut through everything, vast as a landslide. "The host advances! We do not hold ground — we *give* it! Every step backward is a step toward the rite!"

It was, I will admit, brilliant. Retreat reframed as advance. The salt-ranks turned in perfect order and began falling back toward the chasm's rim, drawing the storm after them, funneling the Hollow Wind's fury toward the one place on the Steppe where thirteen sleeping gods lay under glass.

Which meant we were leading the apocalypse directly to the altar. But the alternative was dying scattered in the dunes, so.

I lost Paloma in the third minute of the fighting, which remains the worst minute of my life, and I include the day I learned what the book said about me. One moment she was beside me with the covenant-bone strapped across her back like a sword, and the next a thrown mirage — a wedding feast, of all things, frozen mid-celebration, guests of glass shrieking soundlessly — crashed between us, and when the shards cleared she was gone.

"PALOMA!"

The battle swallowed my voice. All around me the ash-folk were doing their terrible quiet work, catching hunters out of the air and holding them, holding them, until the wind went out of them and they crumbled into ordinary gusts. Odile the cheesemaker was standing over three fallen salt-soldiers swinging a wheel of aged rind like a mace, roaring something in her valley dialect that I chose not to translate even internally. The storm screamed. The glass sang. Somewhere beneath it all, I swear, the sleeping signatories had begun to hum.

I found her at the rim.

She had been driven there — or had fought her way there, with Paloma it was impossible to say which — and she stood at the very edge of the council-chasm with the storm boiling above her and three hunters closing in, and she was not afraid. That was the thing I couldn't look away from. She had her grandmother's sigil raised in one hand and the covenant-bone in the other, and she was saying something to the hunters, actually *talking* to them, the way she talked to every dead thing she'd ever met, and the hunters were faltering, because no one had ever spoken to them as if they were people rather than weather.

"—and whoever you were before," she was saying as I arrived at a dead run, "whoever she took you from — you don't have to do this. Put it down. Rest. I'll carve you a name. Any name you want. I'm told I'm very good—"

One hunter lunged. I got there first, which surprised us both, taking the blow across my shoulder and going down hard on the glass with the hunter's blade skidding off the order-signs branded into my coat. Paloma's sigil flashed. The hunter froze mid-strike, shuddered, and came apart into a breeze that smelled, briefly, impossibly, of some childhood summer.

The other two fled upward into the storm.

"Are you hurt?" She was kneeling over me, hands quick and warm, cataloguing damage.

"Bruised. Dramatic. Otherwise fine." I sat up. Below us, the chasm yawned, and the humming from the glass was louder here, thirteen voices rising like a tide. "They know," I breathed. "Paloma — the signatories. They're waking up on their own. The book said they needed your voice, but the storm — the battle — it's calling them early—"

Above us, the Hollow Wind gathered herself.

I had seen her once before, at a distance, a figure of torn cloud and old grief. Now she poured herself down out of the sky in a column a mile wide, and every ghost on the field — salt, ash, hunter, all of them — stopped fighting at once, because some things command attention past the reach of any war.

Her voice, when it came, was everywhere at once, patient and terrible.

"*Carver.*"

Paloma rose to her feet at the chasm's edge, the covenant-bone held against her chest, and did not step back.

"You may still walk away," the storm said. "Take your messenger. Take your little army of the forgiving. Return to your valley, such as it now is, and live out your small warm life. I will spare every soul on this field. Every one. You have my word, and my word has kept for nine hundred years." A pause, and the wind gentled into something almost tender, almost like the voice of a tired mother. "*Just walk away, child. You were never meant for this. Ask yourself whose story put you here.*"

And I saw it land. I saw the temptation move through Paloma like a current through water — the valley, the quiet, the bench by the window and the bones and the murmuring dead, the life she had mourned on the mirrored ford. Her shoulders softened. For one heartbeat, two, she wavered at the edge of the chasm, and the whole battlefield held its breath, ten thousand ghosts and four hundred living souls all waiting on the answer of one bone-carver who had never wanted any of this.

Below her feet, the glass of the council-chasm began, very softly, to glow.

Thirteen lights. Rising.
