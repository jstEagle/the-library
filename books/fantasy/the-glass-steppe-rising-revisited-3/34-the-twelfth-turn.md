## 34. The Twelfth Turn

The moon had been wrong all day.

Ilya noticed it first as a smudge of color at its lower edge, like a thumbprint on a lamp-glass — a bruise of green that deepened as the afternoon wore on. By the time the allied host had gathered itself from the wreckage of the morning's fighting and begun the slow, limping march toward the council-chasm, the bruise had spread across half the moon's face, and the sky around it had begun to do something Ilya's order had only ever described in the careful, distancing language of scholars who hoped never to see it themselves.

*The firmament divides,* Brother Anselm had written, a hundred years dead. *As a curtain parts. As a wound opens.*

He had been right about everything else in that book. Why not this.

"It's splitting," Paloma said beside him. She said it the way she might have announced that bread was ready, or that a splinter needed drawing — flat, practical, unhysterical. That was one of the thousand things he loved about her and could not yet say aloud: she met catastrophe with the same steady hands she used on bone. "The sky is actually splitting."

"Technically," Ilya said, "it's the membrane between the Steppe and everything else. The sky is just where we can see it happening."

"Thank you. That's much less frightening."

"I thought so."

She elbowed him, gently, in the ribs — the uninjured side, because she was attentive that way even now, even with glass dust in her hair and her grandmother's sigil hanging at her throat like a small cold sun. He would remember that later, he decided. When there was a later. He filed the elbow away with all the other small treasures: the way she hummed when she carved, off-key and unbothered; the way she talked to ghosts as if they were neighbors who'd let their hedges grow too long; the way she had looked at him last night across the dying fire and said, *I refuse to let a book finish this sentence for us.*

One turn remained. The twelfth marker had passed an hour ago with a sound like a struck bell heard through water, and every ghost on the Steppe had felt it go through them like a shiver through a sheet. Somewhere above, behind the wounded sky, the thirteenth turn waited — and with it, either a covenant remade or a world quietly, thoroughly unraveled.

Ilya walked at the head of what remained of their army and tried not to count how few of them there were.

* * *

They were outnumbered perhaps four to one, though numbers meant little when your soldiers were made of salt and memory. Vess's host marched in ranks that shimmered — pillar-ghosts and salt-wights and the drowned courtiers of a kingdom that had been glass before it was ever salt, all of them bound now to Paloma's alternative by nothing sturdier than a promise she had carved into her own healed ribs. Behind them came the ash-folk of the burned cities, slower, quieter, carrying their dead like bundles. And scattered among them, thin as thread, the free dead — the ones who owed no faction anything, who had simply heard that a carver was coming to the chasm and had chosen, for the first time in a century, to hope out loud.

Against them: the Hollow Wind.

It did not march so much as *arrive*, in gusts and pressures, a storm wearing the shape of a woman who had faked her own death to escape being fed to the glass. Where it passed, the fused plain groaned and remembered being sand. Its hunters moved ahead of it like splinters off a broken blade, and three of those splinters had found Ilya's army this morning.

Three. He counted his wounds the way his teachers had taught him — clinically, gratefully, as evidence he was still alive to be wounded. A gash along the left forearm where a hunter's edge of compressed wind had opened him to the bone; he'd bound it with a strip of Vess's salt-cloth, which stung like honesty. A burn across his right shoulder where the sky-splitting light had come down sideways during the worst of the fighting and touched him through a gap in the storm. And deeper, under the ribs on the left side, a bruise the size of a hunter's fist that ached with every breath but had not, despite his private suspicions, cracked anything vital.

"You're favoring the left," Paloma said.

"I'm favoring the left *elegantly*."

"You're walking like a man carrying a full bucket on that side."

"A full bucket of dignity, yes."

She gave him the look — the one that had learned, over these long terrible weeks, to be fond and exasperated in equal measure — and then, without asking, took his arm and set it over her shoulders. She was smaller than him by a head and should not have borne any of his weight, but she bore it anyway, the way she bore everything: as a commission. Something given into her hands to be worked.

"The healers—" he started.

"Are salt-ghosts, Ilya. They can mend a memory but they look at blood like it's soup gone off." She adjusted her grip. "Besides. You'll want both arms working for whatever you do at the bottom of that chasm. Standing there looking prophetic, presumably."

"That is most of my job description, yes."

"And the rest?"

"Holding the book." His hand went to his satchel, out of habit, and found the shape of it — the prophecy that had named tonight's date a century before either of them drew breath, torn and mended and missing its final page until Paloma had won it back out of Bone Country. "Holding the book and hoping the carver knows what she's doing."

"She does," Paloma said. "Her grandmother taught her. Badly, and with lies, but thoroughly."

Ahead of them, the ground began to change.

* * *

The council-chasm did not open so much as *reveal* itself. One moment the glass plain stretched flat and mirage-haunted to the broken horizon; the next, the light bent, and there it was — a wound in the world a mile wide, descending in terraces of fused and colored glass, down and down into a darkness that was not empty. Thirteen shapes slept in that darkness. Ilya had seen them once, from the rim, and had dreamed of them every night since: the original signatories, each sealed upright in a column of glass like insects in amber, like saints in a cathedral window waiting for the light to pass through them correctly.

Now the whole host halted at the rim, and the silence that fell was the kind armies make only when everyone in them understands, without a word being spoken, that some of the people standing here will not be standing anywhere tomorrow.

Vess drifted forward. Even diminished — a third of her pillars shattered in the morning's fighting, her crown of salt melted crooked — the ghost-queen managed to make halting look like a coronation. Her voice came out layered, many-mouthed, the sound of a court speaking in unison.

"This is where our part ends, carver," she said to Paloma, and then, grudgingly, including him: "*Messenger.*"

"Your part ends?" Ilya asked. "You're holding the line above us. That's rather a large part."

"We hold the line above you," Vess agreed. "Which means we hold the door. Which means, messenger, that whatever happens below, no more of the Wind gets past us to reach you — and also that if the rite fails, we will be the first to learn what unraveling feels like." Her many faces arranged themselves into something almost like a smile. "We agreed to this. Do not make us regret having standards."

Paloma stepped forward and did the thing that still astonished him after everything: she bowed to a ghost-queen of poisoned hospitality, formally, carver to patron, as if Vess were a client commissioning a memorial and not a predator who had spent weeks trying to bend the covenant toward her own faction. "When it's done," Paloma said, "your name goes in the bone. First clause. Witnessed and willing."

Something moved behind Vess's salt features — quick, quickly hidden, but Ilya saw it. A hundred years of being forgotten, and this bone-carver with dust on her hands had promised her remembrance in a covenant written on consent, and the queen of the Salt Court had nearly wept in front of her entire army.

"See that you do," Vess said, rough-voiced. "And carver —" the layered voice dropped to something single and small — "the girl who hears. Come back up. Dead queens are poor company. I should know."

* * *

They descended alone.

That had always been the terms — the rite allowed no army below, no queen, no host. Two living souls: the messenger and the maker. Everyone else could watch from the rim, and the watching, Ilya thought as the terraces took them down, might be worse than fighting.

The glass sang under their feet. It had begun doing that an hour ago, as the twelfth turn settled in — a low harmonic rising from the whole plain at once, the sound of a held breath, of thirteen sleeping voices dreaming in unison. With each terrace it grew richer, stranger, until Ilya could pick out individual notes and knew, with a scholar's helpless thrill, that each note was a signatory. The drowned king. The star-reader. The smith who had built the first boundary stones and then, in shame or foresight, written herself into the covenant to guard against its misuse.

And one note that was wrong. Off-key. Absent, almost — a gap in the chord shaped exactly like a woman who had faked her death and fled. The Hollow Wind's place in the music, empty and aching.

"Do you feel that?" Paloma whispered. She had stopped walking, one hand pressed flat to the glass wall of the terrace, her eyes closed. "There's a hole in it. In the song. Like a tooth knocked out."

"The twelfth signatory. The one who ran."

"No." Paloma opened her eyes, and there was something in them he hadn't seen before — not fear, not wonder. Recognition. "It's not empty, Ilya. It's *straining*. Like a voice that's been singing someone else's part for a hundred years and has forgotten its own." She shook her head slowly. "Whatever she tells herself, some piece of her never left this chasm. It's been down here the whole time, waiting to be sung home."

"Can you do that? Sing her home?"

"I can carve it," Paloma said. "If there's anything left of her worth carving." Then, catching his expression: "Don't look at me like that. Hope isn't a promise. It's just a direction."

"Is that a valley saying?"

"It's a *grandmother* saying. Apparently the lying old woman was occasionally wise." But she smiled when she said it, small and real, and took his hand, and they went down together into the dark that was not dark — lit now from above by the splitting sky, whose wound had widened enough to show what lay beyond it: not stars, but *threads*. Millions of them, silver and taut, running from the Steppe up into the mortal world and back again, the covenant's ancient stitching laid suddenly bare. Some of the threads had frayed. A few had snapped already, curling loose like cut harp strings, and where they dangled, Ilya knew, rain was falling upward over a valley that had believed itself safe for a hundred years.

* * *

Two terraces from the floor, the sky finished breaking.

There was no thunder. That was the terrible part — Ilya had braced for thunder, for apocalypse in the loud register, and instead the wound in the heavens simply parted with a sound like a page turning, and the light that came through changed. It stopped being light and became *attention*. Everything it touched stood still and considered itself. The glass terraces gleamed like they were being read. The sleeping signatories in their columns brightened, each face turning — slowly, terribly, beautifully — toward the surface, toward the thirteenth turn hanging overhead like a door left ajar.

And far away, at the rim, the battle for the door began. Ilya heard it as pressure and flicker: Vess's salt ranks meeting the Hollow Wind's storm in a collision that made no noise at all, because sound, like everything else, was busy reconsidering itself.

"Paloma." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Look at me a moment."

She looked. Glass-light and split-sky-light on her face, bone-dust in the lines of her palms, and underneath all of it — underneath the exhaustion and the grief and the inherited weight of a family's century-old flight — the calm. Not serenity; he'd never mistake her for serene. The calm of a woman at her workbench who has finally, after long preparation, picked up the right tool.

"When this is done," he said, "there's a thing I need to tell you. About the prophecy. About me."

"I know what it says, Ilya."

His heart stumbled. "You—"

"I've known since the ford. Since the mirrored river showed me a life where you weren't dying, and I understood why you flinched away from your reflection." She squeezed his hand, hard, her grip a carver's grip, certain and warm. "You hid it to keep me moving. It was a stupid, kind, infuriating thing to do, and I am going to be angry at you properly later, when there's a later. But right now—" she nodded upward, at the turning moon, at the threads, at the thirteen sleeping faces beginning to wake— "right now I need you to know that I have already decided the book doesn't get the last word. It never did. My grandmother tore its ending out, and I'm going to write a better one, and you are going to be alive to complain about my phrasing. Those are the terms. Sign here."

She held out her other hand, palm up, the gesture of a craftsman closing a bargain.

Ilya laughed — a cracked, wet, entirely undignified laugh — and put his hand in hers. Around them the sky hung open, the moon turned its final mile, the storm broke against the salt line above, and somewhere below them in the glass, thirteen hearts that had not beaten in a hundred years began, faintly, to knock.

"Then let's go ruin a prophecy," he said.

Together, they took the last steps down to the floor of the chasm — where the glass had already begun to open for them, and where something vast and patient was rising through the terraces to ask, at last, its founding question:

*What is survival worth?*
