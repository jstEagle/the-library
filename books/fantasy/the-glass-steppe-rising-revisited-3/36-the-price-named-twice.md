## 36. The Price Named Twice

The bone was singing.

I had heard it hum before — in the Salt Court, on the dunes, that low note like a wet finger circling a glass rim. But this was different. This was the sound of something being *finished*, and I stood at the edge of the chasm with my hands empty and my heart hammering against my ribs like a prisoner who'd finally learned the guards' schedule, watching Paloma carve the last clause into the covenant-bone while thirteen ghosts leaned close around her like students around a beloved teacher on the last day of term.

She had been carving for six hours. The battle above us still roared — Vess's salt soldiers clashing with the Hollow Wind's hunters along the chasm's rim, glass shattering into music, someone's war-horn moaning a note that made my teeth ache — but down here, in the council-chasm where the original signatories slept in their glass shrouds, there was only her voice. She narrated as she worked. She always did now. Not for me, exactly. For them.

"—and this clause is for you, Maravel," she said to the ghost of a woman who had drowned in a river that no longer existed, "because you said the water should be allowed to remember its dead without charging them rent."

Maravel laughed, a sound like pebbles rolling downhill. "I said it more elegantly than that."

"You said it drunker than that. I'm improving it."

I loved her. That was not news. I had known it since the mirrored ford, maybe since the campfire, maybe — if I was honest, which the prophecy book insisted I be — since the moment she looked at me bleeding light at the boundary stones and said, *"You're dripping on my memorial work."* Love arrives when it arrives; the book had simply gotten the date wrong by a season, which I chose to find encouraging rather than damning.

But here is what else I knew, standing there with empty hands: the covenant demanded an anchor. A living soul bound into the glass, willing or otherwise, to hold the new law fast. The old rite had taken a child every hundred years. Our rewritten rite asked only for consent — but it still asked. The glass itself had told us so, three nights ago, in a voice like a bell cracking: *A covenant must be anchored in flesh. Name your anchor.*

And the prophecy book, which had named tonight's date a century before either of us drew breath, named its anchor too.

*Ilya of the Last Page dies at the rite, his blood the ink, his body the stone.*

I had hidden that page from her for eleven days. She had found out four nights ago, in the worst argument of our lives, which considering we'd survived a chase through Bone Country and a war at the chasm's rim was genuinely impressive. She hadn't forgiven me yet. She also hadn't left. I had decided not to examine that too closely, because examination might ruin it.

Now the moon hung above the chasm's mouth, swollen and strange, one turn from completing its thirteenth revolution. The sky had split at the edges like old varnish. And Paloma set down her knife, blew bone-dust from the finished sigil, and said:

"It's done."

* * *

The signatories woke fully then — all thirteen, rising out of their glass shrouds like figures surfacing through ice. I had seen them wake once before, during the rite's beginning, but this was different. This was the difference between a door opening and a door opening *onto you*.

They arranged themselves in a circle around the covenant-bone, which Paloma had mounted on a plinth of fused glass at the chasm's center. Each ghost spoke their clause aloud, voice by voice, and each time they spoke, the bone flared a little brighter. Maravel's clause about water. Old Hask's clause about debts forgiven at death rather than inherited. The twins, Lirren and Lirra, who had died holding hands and whose single shared clause required that no covenant ever again separate the living from those they loved — which, I noted with the grim humor that had kept me alive across two thousand miles of hostile glass, was precisely the clause currently being violated by the covenant's demand for an anchor.

Paloma knew it too. I watched her jaw tighten as the twins finished speaking.

Then the thirteenth signatory stepped forward — the First Smith, the one whose name had been carved away so long ago even the other ghosts called him only *Older Brother* — and he looked at Paloma with eyes like banked coals and said the words we had both been waiting for and dreading.

"The clauses are spoken. The bone is willing. Now the covenant names its price." His gaze moved from Paloma to me, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. Only the terrible fairness of old law. "One anchor. Living. Willing. Bound into the glass until the next reforging."

"I know," I said.

My voice came out steadier than I expected. Small mercies.

Paloma turned to look at me, and everything in her face was a locked door. "Ilya—"

"Don't," I said gently. "Please. We've argued this. We've argued it magnificently. You called me a coward with a martyr complex, which stung mostly because it was accurate. But the book names me, Paloma. It has always named me. The order sent me because I was dying anyway — the hollow-fever, remember? Six months, the physicians said, and that was eight months ago. Whatever the glass takes from me tonight, it's taking a debt, not a life." I smiled, because if I didn't smile I would say something true and unbearable. "Besides, think of the symmetry. The book says I die at the rite. If I don't, the book is wrong, and I have wanted to prove that book wrong since the day it ruined my life."

"That book saved your life," she said. "It led you to me."

"And look how well that's going."

Above us, something exploded — a salt pillar, by the sound, coming down in a long crystalline sigh. Dust sifted into the chasm like snow. Vess's voice rang along the rim, hoarse and furious: "*Hold the line, you beautiful brine-brained bastards, HOLD IT—*" and I thought, absurdly, that I would miss her.

Paloma crossed the space between us. Up close her face was streaked with bone-dust and dried blood and the particular exhaustion of someone who has been carrying a world on her shoulders and has recently discovered the world weighs more than advertised.

"Eight months past six months," she said quietly. "You're still here. Maybe the fever was wrong too."

"Maybe. Or maybe the Steppe's magic has been keeping me upright, and the moment I step out of it—" I shrugged. "Either way. It's me or no one, and 'no one' means the covenant fails and everything unravels. Your valley. My order's memory. Every city-mirage trapped in this glass, screaming every turn of the moon. I've heard them, Paloma. I can't walk away from that. You taught me that much — you, who walked away from safety to walk *toward* the screaming."

Her hand came up and gripped my collar. For one wild moment I thought she would kiss me, and I confess I braced for it the way a man braces for a wave.

Instead she said, "Step back from the plinth."

"...What?"

"Step. Back. From the plinth." Her voice had gone very calm, which anyone who had spent five weeks with her knew was the most dangerous weather she owned. "You keep talking about the book like it's scripture. Like it gets a vote. Well, I've read it cover to cover, Ilya, every surviving page, and do you know what I noticed that you didn't?" She released my collar and turned to the circle of ghosts. "Signatories! Honored dead! A question of law!"

The thirteen ghosts inclined their heads. Even Older Brother.

"When a covenant is forged," Paloma said, and her voice took on the cadence of recitation, the rhythm of someone who had memorized the old rite the way other people memorize prayers, "and the rite requires an anchor of living flesh — does the law specify *one* anchor?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

Then Maravel began to laugh — that pebble-roll laugh, building. "Oh," she said. "Oh, she's going to do it. She's actually going to do it."

"Answer the question," Paloma said.

"The law specifies *an* anchor," Older Brother said slowly, and I could hear him turning the words over, testing them for traps, finding none. "A covenant must be anchored in flesh. It does not... it does not specify the number."

"Because it never occurred to anyone," Paloma said, "that more than one person would volunteer. Because the old rite *bound* its anchors. It never asked. It never needed two." She turned back to me, and her eyes were bright and fierce and absolutely terrifying. "You've been reading the prophecy like a sentence. I've been reading it like a first draft."

"Paloma." My mouth had gone dry. "What did you do?"

* * *

Here is what she had done.

While I had slept after the battle's first day — actually slept, for the first time in a week, because she had drugged my tea with valerian root and later admitted it without a shred of remorse ("You were insufferable. You kept reorganizing the book pages by candlelight and sighing.") — she had taken up her knife and carved a second name into the covenant-bone.

Hers.

Not instead of mine. Beside it. Two anchors, side by side, the way the twins Lirren and Lirra had died, the way they had written their clause: *let nothing sever what love holds together.* She had carved herself into the bone with the same steady hands that carved memorial bones for drowned children, and she had told no one, because — as she informed me now, in front of thirteen ghosts and a crumbling sky — "If I'd told you, you'd have argued, and you argue like a scholar, which is to say at length and with citations."

"I—" I said.

"Also," she continued, "the price splits. That's how willing bonds work — ask any of them." She gestured at the ghosts. "A bound sacrifice pays whole. A willing one shares. Two anchors, half a burden each. The glass doesn't get a whole person. It gets half of me and half of him, and frankly, half of him is the better half, so it should count itself lucky."

"That is not—" I started.

"Maravel," Paloma said. "Legal opinion."

"Splitting is lawful," Maravel said promptly. "Rarely attempted. Usually because volunteers are scarce and martyrs are plentiful." She looked pointedly at me. "Present company included."

"Hask?"

"Lawful," the old man rumbled. "Unprecedented in my memory, which is long enough to make 'unprecedented' mean something."

"Twins?"

Lirren and Lirra spoke together, as they did everything: "Let it be so."

Older Brother had not moved. He stood looking at Paloma the way a man looks at a lock he has stared at for a century and suddenly sees the keyhole. "The old law will fight this," he said. "The glass remembers the old terms. When the anchor is claimed, it will pull. It will pull *hard*. One soul, it knows how to take. Two souls sharing — it may refuse. It may try to take one of you whole anyway."

"Then it can try," Paloma said, "while I explain to it, in detail, exactly which clauses I just carved and why they apply. I've spent five weeks learning to argue with the dead. The glass is just a very large dead thing."

And I stood there, in the center of the chasm, with the moon one turn from full completion and the battle thundering overhead and the woman I loved having quietly, calmly, without consulting me, signed herself into my death — and I felt something crack open in my chest that was not despair.

It was relief. Ugly, enormous, undeserved relief. Because I had been prepared to die alone, and I had told myself that was noble, and underneath the nobility it had simply been *lonely*, the way my whole life had been lonely — chosen by an order, sent by a book, dying on schedule. And this impossible, stubborn, bone-dusted woman had looked at the loneliest thing in the world and said *no, we're doing it together.*

"You drugged my tea," I said, because someone had to say something and my higher functions had resigned.

"Yes."

"You carved yourself into a covenant-anchor without telling me."

"Yes."

"That's the same crime you nearly killed me over."

"Mm." She tilted her head. "Yes. I've been thinking about that. I've decided the crime isn't lying to protect someone. The crime is deciding alone that someone else's life belongs to you." She held out her hand. "So. Your life is yours. Mine is mine. Do you consent to share the price — half and half, willingly, neither whole?"

The moon touched the rim of the chasm. Above, the sky tore a little wider, and through the tear I could see — briefly, horribly — the mortal world, upside down, rain falling upward over a valley I had never seen but knew instantly, the way you know a place from a lover's stories. The valley that refused magic. Her valley. It was leaking. Everything was leaking.

I took her hand.

"For the record," I said, "if we survive this, I am going to be insufferable about the tea for the rest of my life."

"If we survive this," Paloma said, "you'll have the rest of your life to be insufferable about anything you like. That's rather the point."

We stepped onto the plinth together, back to back, her spine against mine, her heartbeat tapping through her shoulder blades into my own — quick, stubborn, alive. The covenant-bone rose before us, glowing, every clause lit like windows in a house at dusk. Thirteen ghosts raised their voices. Overhead, Vess's army screamed and held. The Hollow Wind howled at the rim of the world.

And the glass reached for us.

It came up through the plinth like cold water, like the boundary stones crossing in reverse, like everything the valley had ever kept out arriving all at once. It found my name in the bone and pulled — and found hers beside it, and pulled — and found that the two pulls tangled, caught, *held each other fast*, two anchors fouled together like ships in a harbor storm, neither able to be taken without dragging the other, and the old law shuddered and strained and could not swallow either of us whole.

Half. It got half. It got the fever, burning out of me like a lamp running dry. It got the loneliness, drawn out of her in long silver threads. It got pieces I couldn't name and wouldn't miss — and it gave something back, though I didn't understand that until later: a thread, thin as spider silk, running from my chest into the glass, and another from hers, and the two threads braided where they met.

The moon completed its turn.

Light poured down the chasm like a struck bell.

And through the roar, through the singing bone and the shouting ghosts and the sky knitting itself closed above us, I heard Paloma laugh — really laugh, breathless and disbelieving, pressed against my back — and say the words I would carry out of that chasm and into whatever came next:

"*It worked. Ilya — it actually — oh, don't faint, you absolute scholar, open your eyes, you have to see this—*"

I opened my eyes.

The glass beneath the plinth was changing. Spreading outward from the covenant-bone in slow rings, the fused mirror-surface of the Steppe was softening — not breaking, *softening* — cities stirring inside it like sleepers turning over, the first city-mirage opening its eyes.

And every ghost in the circle went silent at once, heads turning toward the chasm's mouth, where the battle had stopped — truly stopped — and something was walking down the switchback path toward us, trailing storm-light, wearing the face of a signatory who had died a thousand years ago.

Older Brother's coal-eyes widened.

"That," he said, "is impossible. She's dead. *I attended her funeral.*"

The figure stopped at the edge of the light, and smiled, and said:

"Hello, little brother. I believe we need to talk about what you've just done to my Steppe."
