## 37. Thirteenth Turn

**Paloma**

The bone was singing.

Not the way her carvings usually sang — a hum in the wrist, a whisper against the ear — but the way a bell sings when it has decided to stop being metal and become sound instead. Paloma knelt at the bottom of the council-chasm with her palms flat on the covenant-bone, and the bone sang through her arms, her ribs, her teeth, until she could not tell where the carving ended and the carver began.

*Hold,* said the dead, all of them at once, thirteen voices braided into one. *Hold. Hold.*

"I'm holding," she muttered. "You don't have to nag."

Above her, at the rim of the chasm, Vess's salt army held the line against the Hollow Wind's storm — a wall of white pillars and stubborn ghosts, glittering like a wedding cake that had survived a war. Paloma could hear the battle only distantly, the way you hear rain from inside a warm house: wind screaming, glass shattering, Vess's voice cutting through it all with the imperious clarity of a queen who had never once doubted herself in four hundred years of being dead.

*The moon,* said the thirteenth signatory — the old one, the first one, whose name had been worn smooth as a river stone by centuries of being spoken. *Watch the moon.*

Paloma looked up. The sky above the chasm was split open along its seams, light bleeding through like yolk from a cracked egg, and through that wound hung the moon — swollen, silver, caught mid-turn, trembling on the last inch of its arc like a coin balanced on its edge.

One turn left. One breath.

Beside her, Ilya knelt in the glass, one hand pressed over the place where the covenant-bone met his chest, because the price had been named twice now — once by prophecy, once by choice — and split between them like bread at a table. She could feel his half of it humming against her half, two debts sharing one ledger.

"You're doing the thing," Ilya said. His voice was ragged but steady. "Where you talk to the dead like they're difficult relatives."

"They *are* difficult relatives."

"Fair." He winced as the moon trembled again. "Paloma. If this doesn't—"

"It holds."

"If it doesn't—"

"Then we'll be very embarrassed," she said, "in front of an audience of thirteen ghosts and a storm. And I refuse. I spent thirty years refusing things quietly in a valley. I'm not starting again at the end of the world."

Something moved through the glass beneath them — the old law, the sacrificial law, rising up through a century of resentment. It came as cold, came as hunger, came as a voice made of every smith who had ever been bound into the Steppe screaming *this is how it has always been done.* The glass rippled. The bone shuddered under her hands. For one terrible moment Paloma felt the old rite reaching for Ilya the way a drowning man reaches for a rope, felt it measuring him, weighing him, finding him sufficient.

"No," she said — not loudly, not dramatically, just with the flat finality of a woman who had carved ten thousand names and would not carve his. "No. You've had your century. You're done."

She pressed her will into the bone the way she pressed her burin into antler: patiently, precisely, along the grain. *This is what you are now,* she told the covenant, clause by clause, line by line, each one signed in a ghost's voice. *Consent. Witness. Rest. No more children in the glass. No more smiths bound screaming. Only this: willing bone, willing voice, willing memory.*

The old law fought her. Of course it did. Laws always fight hardest when they are dying.

But bones know something laws do not. Bones know that nothing holds forever, and that this is not a tragedy — it is simply the shape of things, and the shape can be carved anew.

The moon completed its turn.

*

There was no thunder. Paloma had expected thunder. There should have been thunder; there were certainly enough storms standing by.

Instead there was silence — a great soft rolling silence, like snowfall, like the moment after a fever breaks, like the pause between the last note of a song and the applause. The split sky closed along its seams with a sound like a book shutting gently. The moon settled into its proper orbit, whole and ordinary and silver, looking for all the world as if it had never once considered doing anything strange.

And the Glass Steppe went quiet.

Truly quiet. Not the held-breath quiet of a land waiting to die, not the strained quiet of ghosts biting their tongues for a hundred years, but the deep, easy quiet of a place that has finally been allowed to sleep. Paloma felt it settle over her like a blanket pulled up by careful hands.

The thirteen signatories sighed — one long exhale that moved across the Steppe like wind through wheat — and then, one by one, they lay back down into their glass beds. But differently now. Before, they had slept the way prisoners sleep, guarded and guarded against. Now they slept the way the honored dead sleep in her valley: trusted, tended, expected to be remembered.

*Thank you, carver,* murmured the oldest one, already half-dissolved into rest. *Tell them our names. That is all we ever wanted. Tell them our names.*

"I will," Paloma promised. "Every one. In order. With correct pronunciation."

A ghostly chuckle, fading. Then stillness.

Ilya slumped sideways against her shoulder, and for a moment she thought — her heart stopped, actually stopped, a full missed beat — before she heard him breathing. Just exhausted. Just human. Just alive, against all sense and all prophecy, because the price had been paid twice and therefore, by some arithmetic older than either of them, forgiven.

"We did it," he whispered.

"We did it."

"The book was wrong."

"The book," Paloma said, "was written by someone who assumed nobody would bother to argue with it." She looked down at the covenant-bone, glowing faintly now, its work done, its clauses signed. "Never assume that about a carver. We argue professionally."

*

**Ilya**

Later — how much later he could not say; time on the Steppe had gone soft as candle wax — Ilya climbed out of the chasm on legs that complained in three languages, and stood blinking in the new quiet.

The battlefield was gone. Or rather, it had been *unmade*, gently, the way frost unmakes itself on a window in the morning. Vess's salt soldiers stood down in loose formations, their weapons dissolving into harmless drifts of white. Above them, the Hollow Wind's storm was coming apart — not defeated, not destroyed, but *unraveling*, thread by black thread, and within the unraveling Ilya could see her.

The former signatory. The one who had faked her death to escape the sacrifice, who had spent a hundred years arguing that annihilation was mercy, who had nearly been right.

She was smaller than she had seemed in the storm. That was the first thing. The second thing was that she was crying, or doing whatever ghosts do instead, and the third thing was that she was looking at the sleeping signatories in their glass with an expression Ilya recognized from his own mirror, from the worst year of his life: the look of someone who has been alone so long they've forgotten loneliness was ever a choice.

Paloma climbed up beside him. Together they watched the Hollow Wind come apart.

"She could stop it," Ilya said quietly. "If she wanted. She's strong enough."

"She doesn't want to." Paloma's voice was soft. "Listen."

And Ilya listened, and heard — beneath the dissolution, beneath the wind losing its edges — a single thread of the Hollow Wind's voice, repeating something. Not a curse. Not a warning.

An apology. Over and over, in a language dead four hundred years, to the twelve ghosts sleeping in the glass. *I left you. I left you. I left you.*

"She ran," Paloma said. "Same as my grandmother. Same as my whole family. We all ran from the same thing, and she just... ran longer, and turned into weather on the way."

The last threads of the storm thinned. The Hollow Wind's shape grew translucent, then bright, then — and Ilya would swear this to anyone who asked, though no one ever would — she smiled. Small. Tired. Relieved.

Then she was gone, and the wind with her, and the Glass Steppe lay open to the horizon under an honest moon, quiet as a held breath finally let go.

"Well," said Vess, materializing beside them in a swirl of salt and wounded dignity. There was a crack running through her nearest pillar, and she carried it like a medal. "That was adequately dramatic. I've seen better endings, but I've certainly seen worse. The siege of Saltmere, for instance, which ended with everyone exploding."

"Vess," Paloma said, "you held the line."

"Of course I held the line. I am extremely holdable." She examined her cracked pillar with satisfaction. "Though I'll note, for the historical record, that I was promised favorable terms in the new covenant, and instead received *consent, witness, and rest*, which is the least flattering trio of words ever committed to bone."

"You also received," Paloma said, "an entire army of ghosts who follow you because they choose to, rather than because you trapped them. Which, if you think about it, is worth more."

Vess opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"Hm," she said, in the tone of a queen discovering an argument she couldn't win and deciding to pretend she'd never entered the debate. "Hm. Yes. Well. Don't let it go to your head, carver." And she swept away toward her soldiers, radiating the specific dignity of someone who has just been emotionally outmaneuvered by a woman holding a stick of carved bone.

Ilya laughed. It hurt to laugh — everything hurt, honestly; he felt like a sock that had been washed too hot — but he laughed anyway, and Paloma laughed with him, and the sound went out over the quiet Steppe and nothing answered it angrily, and that, more than anything, was the miracle.

"Do you feel it?" she asked after a while.

"What?"

"The quiet. Really feel it." She closed her eyes. "I've heard the dead my whole life. Whispering, muttering, arguing, begging. There was never silence. Not once. Even in the valley — especially in the valley, they were just polite about it." She opened her eyes, and they were wet, and she was smiling. "It's quiet, Ilya. They're resting. All of them. They're actually *resting*."

Ilya reached over and took her hand — the one with the burin calluses, the one that had rewritten the world. She let him.

"So what does a bone-carver do," he asked, "when the dead finally stop talking?"

Paloma considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, which was to say, completely, except when making jokes, at which point also completely but in the other direction.

"Listens harder," she said. "Obviously. Quiet has a lot to say."

*

They made camp at the rim of the chasm, among the salt soldiers, who had begun — tentatively, the way strangers at a party begin — to talk to one another. Four hundred years of war does not end in an evening, but it can end in a conversation, and there were many conversations happening, low and wondering, ghost to ghost, soldier to soldier, everyone checking that everyone else was real.

Vess sent over a salt-crust loaf that tasted like mineral and victory. Ilya ate his portion lying flat on his back, staring at the moon, which continued, obligingly, to behave.

"One turn," he said. "It finished the turn. The book said I'd die at the rite, and instead I'm eating terrible bread under a law-abiding moon."

"The book was a draft," Paloma said. She was turning the prophecy volume over in her hands, frowning at it the way she frowned at badly cured antler. "First edition. Full of errors. Someone should annotate it."

"Someone should burn it."

"Someone should *annotate* it," she repeated firmly, "because burning books is how you get worse books. Trust me. My family burned a page once and it cost a hundred years." She smoothed the cover. "Besides. It got us here. Wrong in every particular, and right in the only way that mattered — it put a carver who hears in front of a covenant that needed one. Maybe that's all prophecy ever is. A letter that arrives late, addressed to whoever's stubborn enough to open it."

Ilya watched her in the moonlight — this woman who had crossed the stones for him, who had split a death in half rather than pay it whole, who was already, visibly, mentally, beginning to sketch annotations in the margins of reality.

"Paloma Reyes," he said. "What happens now?"

She looked out over the Steppe — the glass plains gone gentle, the mirage-cities resting peacefully in their depths instead of screaming, the horizon whole, the world, for the moment, mended.

"Now?" she said. "Now we sleep. Tomorrow we walk back to the boundary stones. And somewhere between here and there—" she patted the covenant-bone, tucked under her arm like a beloved and slightly alarming child, "—I'm going to start learning the names of every ghost on this Steppe. Thirteen signatories. Two armies. A queen with a cracked pillar and a secret heart of gold she'd deny under torture. It's going to take years."

"Years," Ilya said. He tried the word out. It fit strangely, in the mouth of a man who had volunteered to be a messenger because he was dying anyway, and who was now, apparently, not dying, and would therefore need something to *do* with all this unexpected future. "I suppose I could help. I know the order's records. Names, dates, proper forms of address for the deceased. I'm very good at formalities."

"You're very good at hiding things, you mean."

"That too. But mostly formalities." He grinned. "Consider it my penance. You saved my life; I'll alphabetize your ghosts."

Paloma laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she must have kept locked away for thirty years in a silent valley — and lay back against the warm glass, and above them the moon rode on, steady and silver and finished with its turning.

Below, in the deep of the chasm, the thirteen slept. And in the morning, when they rose, the Steppe would still be quiet — but not empty, never empty, only resting, the way a story rests between its chapters, waiting for the next hand to pick it up.

Far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the world, at the place where glass became grass and the mortal realm began — the boundary stones were waiting.

And so, though neither of them knew it yet, was home.
