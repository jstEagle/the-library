## 38. Aftermath at the Stones

The boundary stones looked smaller than Paloma remembered.

That was absurd, of course. Stones did not shrink. They had stood in their crooked ring for longer than anyone in the valley could count, gray and patient as old sheep, and they were exactly the size they had always been. It was she who had changed — stretched thin and rewoven across thirteen turns of a broken moon, salted by Vess's hospitality, scarred along her forearms where she had carved herself into living covenant-bone, and carrying somewhere behind her ribs a quiet hum that had not been there before.

The new covenant, singing its slow consent-song through the glass beneath her feet.

She stopped at the edge of the ring and did not step through. Not yet.

Behind her, the Glass Steppe lay flat and luminous in the late afternoon light, and for the first time in a hundred years it was *quiet*. Not silent — she could still hear the dead, would always hear them now, a murmur like wind through wheat — but quiet the way a house is quiet when everyone in it has finally stopped arguing and settled down to supper. The mirage-cities slept honestly in the glass instead of screaming themselves briefly real. Somewhere far off, a pillar of salt that had once been Vess's court glittered contentedly, its queen having dissolved into what Paloma could only describe as a smug sort of peace.

"Are you going to stand there all evening," Ilya said, "or are you going to go home?"

"I'm deciding whether it still is one."

He came to stand beside her, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers. He was thinner than when he'd stumbled through these same stones bleeding light, and he moved carefully, the way people do when they've been told their heart is now partially made of glass and shared property. But he was alive. The prophecy book, closed forever in her pack, had been wrong about him in the only way that mattered — or rather, it had been right about everything except the ending, because endings, Paloma had learned, were not written. They were carved. And you could carve them differently if you were willing to put your own hands on the knife.

"The valley will feel different," she said. "The protection is gone."

"The protection is *replaced*." Ilya ticked the points off on his fingers, an old scholar's habit he'd never quite shaken. "No more magic-proof border. Instead: anyone who crosses is bound by the terms. Consent, witness, fair dealing with the dead. Honestly, it sounds better than most mortal laws I've read."

"You've read very few laws."

"I've read *some* laws." He paused. "Also, technically, I'm bound to the Steppe's rhythms now, so if anyone's going to feel different coming home, it's me. I'll be wanting to lie down on warm glass at noon like a lizard. Your neighbors will have opinions."

Paloma laughed — a real laugh, surprising her on its way out. It had been so long since anything in her chest had moved freely. "They'll have opinions about you regardless. You're a stranger who arrived bleeding light and stole our bone-carver away for a season."

"I didn't steal—"

"You absolutely did. That's how it will be told. 'The stranger with the book.' Every story needs a villain or a fool, and you're too polite to be a villain."

"I contain multitudes," Ilya said with dignity. "Some of them are villains."

* * *

She stepped through the stones.

She had braced for pain, or vertigo, or some great tearing sensation — the reverse of the cold-water flood that had hit her crossing out. What she felt instead was nothing at all, and that nothing was the strangest thing of all. For her whole life, the valley had been a held breath around her, a place where the dead went muffled and magic went nowhere. Now the valley breathed. She could feel the covenant's terms settling over the fields like a new kind of weather — gentle, watchful, *asking* rather than forbidding.

And the dead of the valley — her dead, the ones she'd served with needle and file for twenty years — were suddenly, unmistakably *present*.

"Oh," Paloma said softly.

The churchyard two fields over. The drowned child whose memorial bone she'd been carving the night Ilya arrived — little Tam, who had been a faint tickle at the edge of her hearing for years, valley-muffled to almost nothing. Now his voice came clear as a bell across the grass: *Is it done? Is the loud place done shouting?*

"It's done, Tam," she murmured. "You can rest properly now. Or talk. Whatever you like. That's rather the point."

*Can I talk?* A pause, wonder-struck. *Nobody ever asked.*

"That," Paloma said, and had to stop, because her throat had gone tight. "That is exactly the point."

Ilya watched her with the expression he got when he was witnessing something he'd only read about — reverence fighting with the urge to take notes. "You can hear them here now. All of them."

"All of them." She turned slowly. The whole valley lay below them, gold-green in the lowering sun: the rooftops, the mill, the long strip of the river where Tam had drowned. And threaded through all of it, voices. Generations of them. Farmers and grandmothers and one extremely chatty miller who had apparently been waiting ninety years to complain about his successor's technique. The silence she had thought was the valley's nature — the silence she had built her whole careful, lonely life inside — had just been a wall. And the wall was gone.

She had spent her life serving the dead in whispers, half-deaf, carving names she could barely hear. Now the whole choir was singing and she was standing in the middle of it.

It was the most wonderful thing that had ever happened to her.

It was also, she realized, standing very still, the end of everything she knew how to be.

* * *

Her cottage sat where she'd left it, which felt like a small miracle. The door stuck the way it always stuck; she shouldered it with the familiarity of twenty years and stepped into air that smelled of bone dust and dried lavender and the particular staleness of a room no one has lived in for a season.

Everything was exactly as she'd left it. The carving bench by the window. The tools laid out in their worn leather roll — grandmother's tools, grandmother's handles polished dark by three generations of palms. The half-finished memorial for Tam on the bench, the child's name only half-emerged from the femur, waiting.

Half-finished. Like everything else she'd left.

Ilya hovered in the doorway, which was polite of him, and also transparently a way of avoiding committing to entering a dead woman's house. "I could wait outside."

"You could, or you could come in and help me decide which of my belongings I own now that I'm a different person."

"That's a heavy errand for a guest."

"You're not a guest. You're the reason my house smells like this." She picked up a shirt off the chair, sniffed it, and threw it in the corner for burning. "See? Consequences."

He laughed and came in, ducking under the low lintel, and then stood in the middle of her small room turning slowly, taking it in — the narrow bed, the single shelf of books, the row of finished memorials on the high shelf, each one a name she'd coaxed out of bone for a family that mostly thought of her as a necessary oddity. The carver who hears. Useful at funerals. Slightly frightening at markets. Kept at arm's length by everyone, because that was the arrangement, because that was what the valley's silence required, because Paloma had never known any other way to be.

"This is where you lived," Ilya said quietly. "All those years. Hearing them through wool."

"Muffled. Like being underwater." She ran her finger along the bench. "I used to think the muffling was mercy. The valley protecting me from them. Now I know it was just — a locked door. And I was on both sides of it."

"Do you regret it? Crossing?"

She considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because lying to Ilya had stopped working somewhere around the mirrored ford. "I regret that my grandmother had to break a covenant alone in the dark and carry the guilt of it to her grave. I regret every year the signatories screamed and nobody came. I regret—" she touched the scars on her forearms, old healed fractures that had given themselves willingly to the new law, "—that it took me until I was thirty to learn I was allowed to want things."

"And the crossing itself?"

"The crossing itself was the first morning of my life." She said it simply, and watched him absorb it, and added, because gentle humor was how she kept from weeping: "Also I got a war, a ghost-queen's poisoned soup, and a chase through fossil giants out of it, so the value for suffering exchanged was frankly excellent."

"The fossil giants were a highlight," Ilya agreed solemnly. "For me. Less so for you, I recall."

"You negotiated passage with a canyon of bones using my grandmother's sigil while I hung upside down from a rib. I am allowed to have complicated feelings."

* * *

Toward dusk, they walked down into the village proper.

Paloma had rehearsed this on the walk — several speeches, actually, ranging from dignified to defensive — but all of them evaporated when she saw the crowd already gathered at the lane's end. Half the valley seemed to be there, clustered nervous and curious, and at the front of them stood Marta the miller's wife with Tam's little sister on her hip, and old Fen the headman, and behind them a dozen faces she had known her whole life and never once been close to.

They were looking at her the way people look at someone returned from a country they'd thought was mythical.

"You're back," Fen said, stating it like a legal fact, buying time.

"I'm back."

"And the—the moon thing. The sky thing. The rain that fell upward last week." He gestured vaguely at the heavens, which were currently behaving in an entirely conventional manner. "That's finished?"

"The covenant is remade. On new terms. Nobody gets sacrificed, nobody gets bound against their will, and the Steppe stays on its side of the horizon — provided we deal fairly with the dead, which we are now able to hear, because the old silence is gone."

A ripple went through the crowd. Someone whispered. Someone else whispered louder. And then, from the churchyard direction, a chorus of voices rose — audible, Paloma realized with a lurch, to *everyone*, not just her, because the new covenant's terms included witness, and the dead of the valley had apparently decided that after a hundred years of enforced quiet, they had *opinions*.

Great-aunt Hesper, dead forty years, addressed the crowd in a voice like a cracked bell: *About time. Also, whoever planted a fence through my grave, move it.*

The crowd made a collective sound like a kettle taken off the heat.

"They can hear them," Marta whispered. "Everyone can hear them now?"

"Everyone," Paloma confirmed. "That's the term. Witness. The dead speak, the living answer. It's in the clause I carved myself, so if you have complaints, I'm afraid you'll have to take them up with my forearm."

There was a pause. And then Tam's sister — four years old, fearless in the way of the very young — wriggled down from her mother's hip, toddled straight past Paloma, and shouted at the churchyard: "TAM! TAM, ARE YOU THERE?"

*I'm here,* said a small bright voice, delighted beyond measure. *Did you get taller?*

"I'm GROWING!"

And the whole strange, terrified crowd broke — not into panic, but into the other thing, the thing underneath the fear, the thing Paloma had spent her life watching people suppress at funerals: relief. People began talking to their dead. Tentatively at first, then in a flood. Old Fen, gray and formal, removed his hat and said, "Father Aldous. I owe you an apology regarding the tithing records." Somewhere a woman was laughing and crying at once at whatever her mother had just said about her husband. The valley, sealed for a century, opened like a fist unclenching.

Nobody was looking at Paloma anymore. Which was, she understood, the entire point of everything she had carved.

Marta lingered, though, shifting the empty hip where her son had been. "Tam's bone," she said. "The memorial. Is it—"

"Unfinished. I'll finish it tomorrow. Properly, this time. With him telling me what he wants it to say." Paloma smiled. "He has requests. There's a dog involved."

"He always wanted a dog."

"He wants one carved chasing his name around the border. He's very specific. He gets it from your side."

Marta laughed wetly, gripped her hand, and went to join her children — both of them — in the churchyard.

Paloma stood in the middle of the lane and felt the truth settle over her, gentle and irreversible as snowfall: she could never again be the woman who stayed. The woman who stayed had been necessary, and brave in her quiet way, and desperately lonely, and she was gone. Whoever walked back up that hill tonight would be someone else — a woman with glass under her skin and a whole chorus in her ears and, possibly, terrifyingly, a future that hadn't been written a century before she was born.

She found she didn't miss the old woman. She mourned her, the way you mourn a version of yourself that served its purpose. And then she let her go.

* * *

Ilya found her at the boundary stones at full dark, because of course he did; she'd known he would. The moon was up — one moon, ordinary, keeping honest time again — and the Steppe beyond the ring glimmered faintly, breathing its slow consent-song.

"So," he said, sitting down beside her on the cold grass with a grunt. "What comes next?"

Paloma looked at him. At the man the book had named for death, alive and asking questions. At the valley behind her, glowing faintly with ten thousand conversations finally allowed. At the Steppe ahead, quiet and vast and hers now too, in the way of things you've bled for.

She reached into her pocket and brought out a fresh piece of bone — a good one, dense and pale, salvaged from the last messenger's remains with his ghost's cheerful blessing — and a knife.

"Next," she said, setting the blade to the surface, "we find out."

The first cut sang.

Somewhere past the stones, in the young quiet of the remade world, something new stirred to answer it.
