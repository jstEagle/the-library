## 39. The Carver Who Hears

Ilya woke before dawn because the Steppe told him to.

That was the arrangement now — the covenant's small courtesy for the man who had almost been its anchor. His blood carried a thread of the glass plain in it, and the plain spoke through that thread the way a tuning fork hums when its twin is struck across a room. Some mornings it was a low chord of contentment, like a cat purring in another room. Some mornings — this morning — it was insistent, a tapping at the ribs: *up, up, there is work.*

He lay a while longer anyway, watching the light come up over the workshop Paloma had built against the boundary stones themselves. It was a strange architecture, half valley and half Steppe, as if she'd decided that since she could no longer belong wholly to either world, she would simply build a door and live in the hinge. The east wall was valley granite, cool and gray. The west wall was fused glass, and at sunrise it caught the first light and threw it back in long amber ribbons across the floor, so that waking here felt like being gently set on fire by someone who loved you.

Paloma was already up. She was always already up. He suspected she had made some private pact with the dawn years ago and saw no reason to break it just because the world had ended and been remade.

He found her at the workbench, sleeves rolled, a curl of bone pinned under her left hand and the finest of her grandmother's chisels in her right. Her shoulders had the particular stillness he'd learned to read as *deeply occupied* — not unfriendly, but a stillness you knocked on rather than walked through.

"You're humming," he said.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's the fourth line of the covenant. You hum it when you're concentrating. Vess does it too, which is somehow worse, because a ghost-queen humming your own handiwork is a very specific kind of flattery."

"It's not flattery if it's true," said Vess, from the corner.

Ilya did not jump. He was proud of that. A year ago he would have jumped; a year ago he had jumped off a cliff into a river of reflections, so his standards had shifted somewhat. He simply turned and inclined his head to the salt-pillar silhouette standing in the amber light, translucent as sea foam and about as easy to argue with.

"Good morning, Your Majesty."

"Your Majesty," Vess repeated, savoring it. "You see, carver? This one has manners. I told you the messenger orders trained them well, even the ones who volunteer out of morbid self-interest."

"That was one time," Ilya said, "and I was dying, and it seemed efficient."

"And now look at you." Vess drifted closer, peering at him with the frank appraisal of someone who had spent three centuries judging the living and found them mostly wanting. "Alive. Bound to a plain of glass. Sleeping in a house built on a border. Efficient indeed."

Paloma set down the chisel and looked up at him properly then, and Ilya felt the familiar small rearrangement in his chest that had stopped surprising him around the second turn of the moon and had never quite settled into something he could ignore. Her eyes were tired in the good way — the way of someone who had slept and woken with purpose waiting for her like a dog at the door.

"The Steppe woke you," she said. Not a question.

"It tapped me on the ribs at first light. Politely. It's learned manners too." He came to stand beside the bench. "What are we carving?"

"A name." She turned the bone toward him. It was a forearm-length piece, pale and dense, already roughed into the shape of a tablet. At the top, in her precise, patient lettering, half-finished: **MAREN OF THE SALT COURT**.

Ilya went quiet.

Maren. One of Vess's oldest retainers, a ghost who had held the eastern marches of the Salt Court for two hundred years without complaint, who had fought at the chasm during the storm, who had stood in the shield-wall of voices while Paloma carved and the old law screamed through the glass. Three weeks ago Maren had come to Vess and said, in the flat tone of someone reporting weather, that she was finished. Not fading — finished. Full. Like a cup that had finally been filled to the brim, and all she wanted was to be poured out gently instead of left standing until she evaporated.

"She asked for you specifically," Ilya said.

"She asked for my hands," Paloma corrected. "There's a difference. The dead don't want *me*. They want someone who'll take the time." But her ears had gone faintly pink, and Ilya, who had spent a year learning to read her the way she read bone — grain, flaw, hidden strength — knew exactly what the difference meant to her.

*Hope as daily practice.* That was what she called it, though she'd never said those words aloud to him. He'd pieced it together from watching: the way she opened the workshop every morning whether or not anyone came; the way she kept the kettle hot for ghosts who couldn't drink but liked the smell; the way she treated each name as if it were the only name, because to the dead whose name it was, it was.

"So," he said, pulling up the second stool — his stool, worn to the shape of him now, which still astonished him on slow evenings — "what comes next?"

Paloma looked at him over the half-carved name. "You ask me that every morning."

"Because every morning you have an answer, and I find I've grown fond of the ritual. A man bound to the rhythms of a glass plain takes his pleasures where he can."

Her mouth twitched. "Next, we finish Maren. Then there's the ferryman at the Mirrored Ford — he wants his boat's name restored, apparently it mattered terribly to him. Then the Hollow Wind's people."

That last one landed softly now, where once it would have landed like a knife. The hunters who had chased them across the dunes, who had stolen the book, who had very nearly ended everything — after the thirteenth turn, when their mistress dissolved into rest, they had simply... stopped. Stood in the glass dunes like abandoned puppets, then begun, one by one, to wander toward the workshop and stand outside it in the rain of upward-falling light, waiting. Not asking forgiveness. Asking names. Most of them had died so long ago they'd forgotten their own.

"They're queueing again," Ilya said. "There were nine of them at the stones when I walked in. One of them brought flowers. Well — he brought a mirage of flowers, trapped in a shard of glass, which is either very romantic or very confusing depending on how you feel about botany."

"Which one?"

"The tall one. The one who nearly put a spear through my shoulder at the ford."

"Halvard." Paloma picked the chisel back up. "He apologized last week. In his way. He stood outside for six hours and then said 'the messenger bleeds light' and left. I think that was the apology."

"It was better than most apologies I got from the order," Ilya said, and was surprised to find he could say it lightly now. The order. The dying brotherhood that had pressed a prophecy book into his hands and sent him walking toward his own death with a blessing and a pack of dried figs. He missed them the way you miss a fever — grateful it's gone, aware it shaped you.

The chisel whispered along the grain. *M.* *A.* *R.* Outside, the morning deepened, and the amber ribbons on the floor climbed the wall, and somewhere beyond the west wall the Steppe hummed its great contented chord, and Ilya sat in the middle of all of it feeling a thing he had no clean word for. He had been a character in a book once — a name on a page, a death scheduled a century in advance. Now he was a man on a stool watching a woman carve letters, and the difference between those two things was, he had come to believe, the entire point of existence.

* * *

Midmorning, the dead began to arrive.

This was normal now. That was perhaps the strangest sentence Ilya owned: *the dead arriving is normal now.* Before the rite, the dead had whispered or argued or screamed, always at the edges, always through veils. Now they walked up the path from the Steppe side in the full daylight, translucent and unhurried, and waited their turn like patients at a village clinic, and Paloma heard them plainly, and answered, and sent them off lighter than they came.

A child ghost arrived first — a boy of maybe eight, drowned a hundred and forty years ago in a river that no longer existed, who came every third day just to sit near the kettle and smell the steam. Paloma had carved him a small bone boat, and he carried it everywhere, and today he reported gravely that the boat had survived a storm in the shallows of the Ford.

"Did it?" Paloma said, with the seriousness the report deserved.

"Big wave. Halvard saw it. He says it's seaworthy."

"Halvard knows boats?"

"He was a fisherman." The boy shrugged, the universal gesture of children explaining adults to other adults. "Before."

"Then it's seaworthy," Paloma agreed, and the boy glowed — literally, faintly, the way the newly peaceful dead did when they were pleased — and drifted off toward the Ford to tell Halvard the verdict.

Ilya watched her watch him go. "You know," he said, "a year ago you'd never spoken to a living stranger who wasn't buying a memorial bone."

"A year ago I'd never spoken to a living stranger at all. You don't count."

"I'm wounded."

"You bled light all over my workshop floor and knew my name before I gave it to you. You count as a natural disaster, not a stranger." She said it dry, but her hand found his wrist as she reached past him for the honing stone, and stayed a moment longer than reach required. "Besides. If you hadn't stumbled through my stones bleeding prophecy, I'd still be in the valley carving other people's griefs and hearing nothing but murmur at the edge. So. Natural disaster. Best one I ever hosted."

Vess, from her corner, made a sound like wind through salt pillars, which was her laugh. "Hosted. As if he weren't still here. A year, messenger. When do you stop being a guest?"

"When the Steppe stops telling him when to wake," Paloma said, and there was something underneath the joke, something careful, and Ilya recognized it because he'd been carrying the same thing himself for months: the question neither of them asked directly. *What is he, now? What am I? Where does a man sleep when he belongs to a plain of glass and a woman belongs to a hinge between worlds?*

But that was a question for an evening with wine, and it was not yet noon, and there was a name to finish.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the rhythm they'd fallen into without ever discussing it. Paloma carved; Ilya prepared the bones — cleaning, soaking, selecting, because the dead were particular about their medium the way the living were particular about coffins. Maren's bone had come from the Salt Court itself: a pillar fragment Vess had surrendered from her own court, which had caused, per Ilya's understanding, roughly the equivalent of a constitutional crisis among the salt ghosts, resolved only when Vess declared that if her marshal was to be remembered, she would be remembered in royal material.

By late afternoon the letters were done, and Paloma began the part Ilya never tired of watching: the listening.

She laid both palms flat on the carved name, closed her eyes, and went still in the way that was different from her working-stillness — deeper, older, the stillness of a woman standing at a door between rooms. The workshop quieted. Even Vess drew back. Even the Steppe, Ilya felt through his ribs, lowered its voice.

And then Maren's voice came through the bone — not a whisper anymore, nothing like the murmurs of the old days, but clear as a bell rung underwater: *Yes. That is my name. That is how it sounded when my mother said it.*

Paloma repeated it aloud, once, giving it back, and the bone warmed under her hands, and the letters filled with a soft inner light, and somewhere beyond the west wall a marshal of the Salt Court, two hundred years dead, sighed the sigh of a soldier finally allowed to take off her armor.

"There," Paloma said quietly. "Done."

She lifted the tablet and carried it to the window ledge that faced the Steppe, where the finished names went to wait for the dusk ceremony — because the newly peaceful dead liked to have their names sung into the open air at sundown, all together, a chorus of remembrance rolling out over the glass plain like a tide. There were eleven tablets on the ledge now. By week's end there would be twenty. The queue, Ilya had observed, did not shrink; it only reorganized itself, patiently, hopefully, the way hope does.

He stood beside her at the window. Below, the first of the evening ghosts were gathering on the path — Halvard tall and awkward with his glass-flower, the boy with his boat, a scatter of others, and far off, catching the low light, the towers of the Salt Court gleaming like candles.

"What comes next?" Ilya asked again, softer this time. Not the morning ritual question. The real one.

Paloma considered the horizon. "Tomorrow? More names. The ferryman. Halvard, probably — he's worked up the courage, I can tell, he keeps practicing standing still. Next year?" She turned the finished tablet in her hands, watching the light move through the letters. "More names. There will always be more names, Ilya. That used to feel like a burden. Grief without end, and me alone at the bottom of it." She glanced at him, and the pink was back at her ears. "Now it feels like a practice. Like the kettle. You keep it hot because keeping it hot is the point."

"And the valley?" he asked, because it needed asking sometimes, gently, the way you touch a scar to check it's healing. "The rain still falls upward over it. The protection's gone. Your mother's people are living inside the covenant now, same as everyone."

"They're fine," she said. "Better than fine. Marta sends word through travelers — the valley's trading with the Steppe villages now. Glass tools for valley wool. Children dare each other to touch the boundary stones." A pause, and then, with the small fierce tenderness that was, Ilya thought, the truest thing about her: "My grandmother broke the old covenant to buy them a hundred years. We rebuilt it to give them forever. I think she'd have liked the ledger balanced that way."

The sun touched the broken horizon. Out on the plain, the trapped cities lit up inside the glass — not screaming anymore, not since the rite, just glowing, like lanterns in deep water, like memories allowed to be beautiful again.

"Dusk," Ilya said. "Time to sing the names."

They went out together onto the path, and Paloma raised the tablet of Maren of the Salt Court, and began the chorus, and eleven voices rose from the ledge to meet hers, and the Steppe hummed beneath it all, contented, whole.

It was Halvard, afterward, lingering awkwardly with his impossible flowers, who changed the shape of the evening. He cleared a throat he no longer had, looked at his feet, and said:

"Begging pardon. Carver. Messenger." A pause, long enough that Ilya braced himself for another six-hour silence. "There's a ghost coming up the Steppe road. Been walking three days. Old one. Older than us." Halvard's brow furrowed, and for the first time since the war ended, Ilya saw fear move through the big fisherman's translucent face. "She says her name is Reyes. Says she's family. And carver — she says she's not here for a name."

Halvard swallowed.

"She says she's here to deliver one."
