## 40. Rising, Revisited

The boundary stones had learned to be doors.

That was how Paloma thought of it now, watching from her workshop window as a caravan of wool merchants came up the valley road and passed between the old markers without so much as a pause. A year ago those stones had stood like teeth in the valley's jaw, humming with refusal. Now they simply stood — gray granite softened by moss, carved at Paloma's own hand with small open circles instead of the old closed wards. Travelers crossed freely. Some of them even waved.

One of them waved now: a round-bellied woman driving two mules loaded with salt fish, lifting her hand toward the workshop as if greeting an old friend she'd never actually met. Paloma lifted hers back. The woman would stop by later, probably. They all did eventually. Word had spread past the valley's edge that the bone-carver who hears would take your name down properly if you asked — not the name you were born with, but the one you'd earned, or the one you wished to be remembered by — and that she charged fair prices and better conversation.

"Paloma." The voice came from the corner of the workshop where no living person sat. "The fish woman's grandmother is following her again. She wants to know if the mule on the left is eating enough."

"Tell her the mule on the left has been stealing oats from the mule on the right all morning," Paloma said, not looking up from her carving. "She can see that perfectly well from wherever she is."

A pause. Then Marisol's ghost laughed — a bright, crackling sound like ice settling in a spring pond — and drifted off through the wall to deliver the news. The dead of the valley had gotten chatty since the covenant was remade. That was the thing nobody had warned her about: peace was loud. Not the desperate murmuring at the valley's edge she'd grown up hearing, but actual voices, opinions, complaints, jokes. The dead spoke in daylight now, and the living answered, and half the time the conversations were about nothing at all, which Paloma had come to consider the finest miracle of the age.

She turned the memorial bone in her hands. It was a small commission — a shepherd's dog named Bristle who had died defending his flock from a wolf and had, according to his grieving boy, been "the bravest animal ever born anywhere." Paloma had carved the whole story into the femur: the wolf coming down the hillside, Bristle standing his ground, the flock scattering safe behind him. At the end, where the story ended, she'd left a smooth blank space, because Bristle's ghost had asked for one.

"What will you put there?" she'd asked him.

"Nothing," the dog had said, in the way dogs say things, which is mostly feeling shaped into words. "I want somewhere to put my nose when I visit."

So the blank space stayed blank, and every few days the boy came and held the bone while his dog pressed his ghostly nose against it, and both of them were content.

This was the work now. This was what her grandmother's tools had become.

* * *

"You're holding the gouge like it insulted you."

Tomas looked down at his hands, then back up at her with the particular dignity of a fourteen-year-old being corrected. "You said firm grip."

"Firm, not vengeful. The bone remembers everything you do to it. If you carve angry, it stays angry." She reached over and adjusted his fingers — his knuckles still soft, unscarred, a carver's hands at their very beginning. "Loosen your wrist. Let the tool find the line. You're not forcing the story out. You're uncovering it. It's already in there."

"It's already in there," he repeated, skeptical, and then made his cut, and the curl of bone that came away was clean and fine, and his face did the thing she privately called the first-light face — the moment a new carver realizes the material is talking back. She'd worn that same face once, over a memorial sparrow, in this same room, with these same tools.

Her grandmother's tools. The chest had long since given up its other secrets — the letter, the sigil, the torn page she kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards — but the tools themselves had become something new in her hands. The little paring knife with the worn horn handle. The gouges wrapped in red thread. Her grandmother had used them to break a covenant; Paloma used them to write names. Same steel. Different story. She liked to think the old woman would have approved, though knowing Grandmother Reyes, she'd have made some dry remark about Paloma taking her sweet time about it.

"She does approve," said a voice from the doorway, and Ilya leaned there with two cups of cider and the expression of a man who had absolutely been eavesdropping through the bond. "I heard her say so last night. Well — she said 'finally,' which from her is practically a parade."

"You don't get to narrate my dead relatives to me."

"I'm bound to the Steppe's rhythms, Paloma. Your dead are adjacent rhythms. It's very hard not to overhear." He handed Tomas a cup too, because Ilya had never once managed to enter a room without feeding someone. "How's the dog?"

"Bristle?" Tomas brightened. "He visited again this morning. Señora Reyes says he likes the blank part best."

"Señora—" Ilya raised his eyebrows at Paloma. "Your grandmother lets the apprentice call her señora?"

"He's polite," Paloma said. "Unlike some messengers."

"I delivered a prophecy across a continent and rewrote the laws of two worlds. I think I've earned a nickname."

"You have a nickname. Vess calls you 'the little clerk.'"

"I have decided to be flattered by that."

Tomas giggled into his cider, and outside the window the fish-woman's cart creaked past again on its way home, and the afternoon went on being ordinary, which was, Paloma thought, the entire point of everything they had bled for.

* * *

Later, when Tomas had gone home and the light had gone amber, she and Ilya walked up to the stones. They walked up most evenings. It wasn't pilgrimage exactly, and it wasn't habit either. It was more like checking on a wound that had healed — pressing gently, just to feel that it didn't hurt anymore.

The Steppe shimmered beyond the last stone, glass plains catching the sunset and throwing it back in colors that had no mortal names. A year ago the horizon had been broken, jagged, screaming. Now the fused glass had softened at its edges, growing lichen the color of amethyst, and the trapped cities inside it had gone quiet — not dead, but sleeping honestly, dreaming whatever cities dream. Once a month a mirage rose and walked the plain like a gentle giant, and the signatories' ghosts rode along inside it, waving at travelers.

"They're arguing again," Ilya said fondly. "Can you hear them? Third pillar council. Something about whether the moon-turn festivals should include dancing."

"The dead argue about everything now. It's wonderful. It means they're staying."

"That's the technical term, yes. Staying." He bumped her shoulder with his. "Vess sent word through the salt lines, by the way. She wants to commission a court chronicle. Carved bone, full history of the Salt Court, 'in the hand of the Smith Who Split the Price.' Her words. She included a threat about what happens if we refuse, which from Vess is basically a hug."

"Tell her yes. Tell her I'll charge double because she'll demand seventeen revisions."

"She'll respect that enormously."

They stood a while in the quiet. Somewhere behind them the valley was lighting its lamps, one by one, and somewhere ahead the Steppe was doing whatever the Steppe did at dusk — Paloma had stopped trying to fully understand it and had settled for loving it, which took less time and hurt less.

"Do you ever miss it?" Ilya asked. "Being the woman who stayed?"

She considered the question seriously, because he always asked serious questions seriously. "I miss her," she said finally. "The woman who stayed. But I don't miss her life. She was a locked room, Ilya. Beautiful furniture, no windows." She turned the little paring knife over in her pocket — she carried it everywhere now, the way other people carried prayer beads. "My grandmother locked that room trying to protect everyone in it. And it worked, for a hundred years. But a room isn't a life. A door isn't a wound. I know that now." She smiled. "Also, the rain falls the correct direction here, which I remain grateful for daily."

"One time. It fell upward one time."

"It fell upward for three days, and a fish landed on the mayor."

"A flying fish is a gift."

"It was a carp, Ilya."

They were both laughing when the first star came out, and under the laughter, faint and constant as a heartbeat, Paloma felt the covenant hum — the willing-bone covenant, the one signed in voices instead of blood, the one anchored in two living souls who had split the price between them and found the halves lighter than any whole. Some nights she could feel every clause like a warm coal. Tonight it was just a low contented note, the sound of thirteen ghosts and one reformed storm and one salt queen and one whole world agreeing, for once, to keep going together.

* * *

Back at the workshop, before bed, she did what she did every night: opened the prophecy book to check, and closed it again.

It sat on its shelf now behind glass Tomas had helped her fit, more museum piece than menace. The pages had stopped writing themselves the moment the thirteenth turn completed — the book had finished its sentence, so to speak, and gone silent. But she checked anyway. Old habits. And every night the last written page said the same thing it had said for a year:

*And the carver who hears remained, and taught, and the names were not lost.*

Below it, in fresh ink that had appeared exactly once, on the morning after everything, in handwriting nobody recognized and everybody suspected:

*— end of the account. begin yours.*

Paloma ran her thumb along the glass cover, said goodnight to the book, and blew out the lamp.

In the dark, Marisol's voice drifted through the wall, soft and pleased: "The fish woman's grandmother says thank you. And the mule is fine."

"Tell her I said you're welcome," Paloma murmured, already half asleep, her grandmother's tools laid out neat and waiting for tomorrow, for the next name, for the next story already resting inside the bone — because it was always already in there, and all a carver ever had to do was listen.
