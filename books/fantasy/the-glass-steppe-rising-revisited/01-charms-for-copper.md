## 1. Charms for Copper

The smugglers' market of Corrow woke before the sun did, the way all dishonest things do—quietly, and with an eye toward the door.

Felix Kavanagh had been awake for two hours already, kneeling on the cold flagstones of their stall in the Undercroft, tying luck-knots by candlelight. The candle was a luxury they permitted themselves exactly one hour a day; after that, their fingers knew the work well enough to do it blind. Over-knot, under-knot, tuck the red thread twice, whisper nothing at all. Ten years of practice had worn the magic down to something so small it barely qualified as magic anymore—a warmth in the fingertips, a faint hum behind the teeth, like a word remembered but never spoken aloud.

That was the trick of surviving the Prohibition. You didn't stop being what you were. You just made yourself small enough that no one thought to look.

The knots went into the basket: luck-knots for gamblers and new mothers, fever charms braided from river-reed and dried yarrow, a half-dozen "blessed" buttons that were only buttons, sold to the superstitious at three coppers each because superstition, unlike magic, was perfectly legal. Felix priced everything in copper. Copper didn't draw eyes. Silver drew Wardens.

Above them, the Undercroft groaned awake. It was a vaulted cellar beneath the old tannery district, ceiling black with a century of smoke, lit by hanging lanterns that swayed whenever the trains rattled past on the viaduct overhead. By midmorning it would be a crush of bodies—fence-men and spice-runners, unlicensed physickers, women selling charms that were honestly just knots and knots that were honestly just charms—and every one of them would be watching every other one of them, the way fish watch fish in a shrinking pool.

Felix liked it here precisely because nobody looked at anybody. Invisibility was a craft, and Corrow's underworld was its guildhall.

They set out the wares with the economy of long habit: knots in the left tray, charms in the right, buttons front and center where a casual glance would land and find nothing worth a second look. The awning was patched in three places. The sign—KNOTS & CORDS, MENDED—was hand-painted and deliberately dull. Everything about the stall said *I am poor and boring and I have been here forever*, which was true in two cases out of three.

The third case—the *forever*—was the dangerous one. Because Felix Kavanagh had not always been a charm-seller in a hole under the tanneries. There had been a house once, on Lantern Row, with blue shutters and a mother who hummed while she worked and a sister who—

Felix's hands stilled over the basket.

*Don't,* they told themselves, the way they did every morning. *Not today. Not any day.*

Ten years since the Proclamation. Ten years since the dragons went silent beyond the Glass Steppe and the Chancellor's men came through the cities with iron badges and lists of names, and the names on those lists stopped coming home. Felix had survived by becoming no one: no surname spoken above a whisper, no craft practiced past the threshold of deniability, no attachments that couldn't be burned in a brazier inside a minute's notice. They had gotten very good at it. They had gotten so good at it that some mornings they woke and couldn't remember the color of their mother's humming, only that there had been one.

The first customers arrived with the lantern-light: a dockhand wanting a knot for his brother's voyage, a girl of maybe nine buying a fever charm with coins counted out one at a time from a sock. Felix took the girl's copper and pressed the charm into her palm and closed her fingers over it, and felt—for one treacherous heartbeat—the old warmth rise, the hum behind the teeth, the thing that used to be called *oath-work* back when oaths were legal.

"Keep it dry," Felix said. "And don't tell anyone where you got it."

"I got it from nobody," the girl said solemnly, with the fluency of a Corrow child, and ran off into the smoke.

*From nobody.* Felix almost smiled. That was me, little one. I'm nobody. I've been nobody so long I've nearly made it true.

* * *

It was past noon when the courier came.

Felix noticed him the way they noticed everything in the Undercroft—not by looking, but by listening. The market had a rhythm, a wash of haggling and boot-scrape and the tanner's vats breathing their sour breath up through the floor grates, and this man broke the rhythm. His footsteps were wrong. Too fast, too uneven. A runner's stride collapsing into something else.

Then the crowd's murmur changed pitch, the way water changes sound when something large moves beneath it, and Felix looked up despite themselves.

He came through the press of bodies like a man swimming against a current that wanted him dead. Tall, gray-cloaked, dust to the knees—Steppe dust, pale and glittering, the kind Felix hadn't seen in ten years and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a scar. He was holding his left side, and between his fingers, dark and spreading, was blood.

The market parted around him the way it always parted around trouble: everyone suddenly very interested in their own goods, everyone suddenly deaf. A runner bleeding in the Undercroft meant Wardens, or worse than Wardens, and the oldest law of Corrow was *see nothing, know nothing, sell nothing to either side.*

But he wasn't looking at the market. He was looking at the stalls. Reading them, one by one, with the desperate focus of a man searching a wall of faces for a single word.

His eyes found Felix's sign. *KNOTS & CORDS, MENDED.*

Something in his face broke open—relief and terror in the same expression, which is a thing Felix had seen exactly once before, ten years ago, on a different face, in a different life.

He crossed the floor in six lurching strides and collapsed against the stall, and his weight came down on the planks hard enough to scatter the button-tray, and his hand shot out and closed around Felix's wrist with a grip that had nothing left in it but will.

"You're the speaker," he rasped.

Every hair on Felix's arms stood up. *Speaker.* No one had used that word aloud in the Undercroft in ten years. People said *charm-seller*, *knot-wife*, *cord-man*, anything soft and dismissive. *Speaker* was the old word. The forbidden one. The word from before, when oath-speakers walked openly and the dragons answered them across the Steppe like thunder answering lightning.

"You're mistaken," Felix said quietly, in the flat tone they'd rehearsed for a decade. "I mend cords."

"He said you would say that." The courier coughed, and the cough was wet and terrible. "Ovid said—no. I'm Ovid. I'm—" He laughed, a cracked, airless sound. "I've been running so long I introduced myself to myself." His grip tightened on Felix's wrist. "Listen to me. There isn't time. They killed my partner at the salt gate. They'll have my trail. I need you to take it—take it *east*—"

"Whatever you're carrying, give it to the Wardens. They'll—"

"The Wardens are why I'm bleeding." His eyes were gray, steppe-gray, and fever-bright, and they held Felix's with a ferocity that made argument impossible. "You're Kavanagh. You're *the* Kavanagh. Sera said if the fire ever began to fail, find the last speaker, and she described you, she described your mother's stall, she said *they'll be hiding in plain sight, selling small magics for copper, because that's what survivors do*—"

The name went through Felix like a blade finding the gap in armor.

*Sera.*

No one had said that name to Felix in ten years. Felix had not said it themselves. It lived in the sealed room at the back of their heart, behind a door they'd welded shut with grief and habit, and this stranger—a bleeding, dying stranger—had just kicked the door open with one word.

"My sister is dead," Felix heard themselves say. Their voice sounded very far away. "She died with my parents. Everyone knows this."

"Everyone knows what the Proclamation told them to know." Ovid fumbled at his cloak, at the inner pocket, and his fingers were slick with blood and shaking. "Please. Please. I carried this four hundred miles. I buried it in a hollow reed and swallowed river water and ate things I won't describe. I am *not* going to fail at the last door."

He pulled it free.

A map. Rolled tight, wrapped in oilcloth gone gray with age—and scorched, scorched badly along one edge, as though it had been pulled from a fire, or had *been* the fire, or had passed through something hotter than fire ought to be. Even rolled, even half-crushed in the courier's failing grip, it seemed to Felix that the thing gave off heat. Or perhaps that was the blood. Perhaps that was only their own pulse, suddenly loud in their ears.

"Take it," Ovid whispered.

"I can't—"

"*Take it.*" And then, softer, with the terrible gentleness of the dying: "It points to the Emberhold. You know what that is. Of course you know. Your family kept the old roads in their heads like other families keep recipes." His breath hitched. "The fire's guttering. Do you understand? The covenant-fire. It's been failing for years and now it's days, maybe. And when it goes—" He shuddered. "You've seen the edges of the Steppe. You've seen what leans against the barrier, waiting. When it goes, everything behind it comes through, and there is no army, no Warden order, no chancellor's wall that will hold it. Only the fire. Only a speaker."

Felix's hands had risen without their permission. They were holding the map. They did not remember taking it.

"This is a death sentence," Felix said. "For both of us. For anyone who touches it."

"Yes," Ovid agreed, and smiled, and it was the strangest smile Felix had ever seen—peaceful, exhausted, almost amused. "Most true things are."

Boots. Somewhere across the Undercroft, boots—many of them, in unison, the disciplined cadence that no smuggler ever managed. The market's murmur died all at once, the whole cellar holding its breath, and in the sudden silence Felix could hear the ring of iron-shod heels on stone, descending the tannery stairs.

Wardens. Six at least. Here, now, following the blood-trail.

Ovid heard it too. Something resolved in his face, some final arithmetic completed. He pushed himself off the stall, straightened as much as his ruined body allowed, and turned to face the stairwell—putting himself, deliberately, between the boots and Felix, the way a wall stands between a storm and whatever lies behind it.

"Run when I fall," he said, conversationally, as if discussing weather. "Not before. Before draws the eye. After, everyone runs, and you're just another body in the flood." He glanced back at Felix, and his gray eyes were calm. "Sera believed you'd do this. She said, *my sibling hides well, but they were never a coward, they only learned to look like one.*"

"You knew her," Felix said. The words came out broken. "You *know* her. Is she—"

The boots reached the bottom of the stairs.

Iron voices: *"In the Chancellor's name! Nobody move!"*

Ovid squared his shoulders. The last thing he said was not loud, and it was not for the Wardens. He said it over his shoulder, quick and low, a courier delivering his final message:

"She's alive, Kavanagh. She's alive, and she's waiting, and the fire is dying. *East.*"

Then he drew a short blade from his belt, turned to meet the iron line of Warden shields pouring across the Undercroft floor, and shouted something defiant and unrepeatable that Felix would carry to their grave.

What happened next happened fast, and it happened in pieces. The crash of the charge. A lantern knocked swinging, shadows wheeling across the vaulted ceiling like great slow birds. The wet, awful sound the short blade's fight made, and then the sound of it ending. Someone screaming about a fugitive, someone else shouting *check the stalls, check every stall*—

Felix did not run when Ovid fell. Felix ran one heartbeat after, in the flood of panicking bodies, with the scorched map crushed against their chest and the name *Sera* ringing in their skull like a struck bell that would not, would not, would not stop.

Behind them, under the swinging light, the courier lay still among the scattered luck-knots, and the red thread of every knot on the ground was running dark with his blood, as if the charms themselves had finally been paid the price they'd always secretly promised.

* * *

Felix did not stop until the river.

They came out through the culvert they'd mapped years ago and never needed—crawled through filth and old rain-water into the grey afternoon under the viaduct, and stood gasping in the shadow of the tannery pilings while the brown water slid past, thick and patient, carrying its secrets to the sea. Their hands were shaking. They watched them shake, distantly, as though they belonged to a stranger.

Slowly, with fingers that would not quite obey, they unwrapped the oilcloth.

The map opened like a wound.

It was drawn on dragon-hide—that much Felix knew at a glance, knew the way a baker knows bread—pale and tough and faintly iridescent, and it was scorched along the eastern edge as though the fire that marked the Emberhold had reached up out of the ink itself. Roads crossed it in faded silver. The Glass Steppe spread across its middle like a frozen sea. And there, in the far east, circled in a hand Felix had not seen in ten years but would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the ocean—

There was a cipher. A small, looping notation in the margin, letters folded into each other like hands in prayer.

Felix knew that hand. They had watched it write birthday messages in steam on winter windows. They had watched it copy out their mother's charm-recipes, tongue between teeth, at a kitchen table with blue shutters and a view of Lantern Row.

The world tilted. The river roared and did not roar. Ten years of careful, painstaking, daily nothing—the small magics, the copper prices, the dull sign, the welded door at the back of the heart—all of it stood up at once and walked away, and Felix Kavanagh stood alone on the riverbank holding a piece of their sister's handwriting and understood, with a dread so complete it was almost calm:

The past had found them.

It had taken ten years, four hundred miles, and a dying man's blood. But it had found them. And it was pointing east.
