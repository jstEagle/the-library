## 2. The Warden's Knock

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock at all.

It was the sound of the smugglers' market dying—shutters slamming, awnings folding like wounded birds, a hundred small economies collapsing into silence all at once. Felix Kavanagh had lived in Corrow for ten years, and they knew every sound the market made: the wet slap of fish on the brine-boards, the clink of charm-wire in copper pans, old Maret's cough that meant the tide was turning. This sound was none of them. This was the sound of people remembering they had something to lose.

Felix was already moving before their mind caught up with their hands. The stall came apart in practiced pieces: the luck-knots swept into a canvas roll, the fever-charms into their false-bottomed scale-box, the little brass weights that looked so innocent scattered over the boards to look like abandonment rather than flight. Ten years of careful living, and it still came down to thirty seconds of muscle memory.

Through the gap where the tarpaulin hung, Felix saw them come.

Wardens moved through the Saltmarket the way water moves through a drowned house—everywhere at once, unhurried, inevitable. Grey cloaks, iron badges stamped with the closed eye of the Prohibition, and the long white birch-staves they carried instead of swords, because swords were for soldiers and Wardens were for *people*. There were twenty of them at least, fanning out from the harbor stair in pairs, and at their center walked a woman with a slate-grey sash who held a scrap of paper as though it were a live coal.

They were asking questions. Felix couldn't hear the words, but he could see the shape of them—the way the paper was thrust toward faces, the way heads shook. They were looking for someone. Someone connected to—

To the courier. To the man who had bled out across Felix's counter not six hours ago, whose blood Felix had scrubbed at until his knuckles were raw, whose body the harbor-watch had carried away wrapped in sailcloth while Felix stood in the crowd with everyone else and wore the face of a stranger watching a stranger die.

*Ovid*, the dead man's partner had said, when the watch asked around. *Steppe-runner. Came up the salt road two days ago, half-dead already.* That was all anyone knew. That was all Felix had let himself know, because knowing was dangerous, because knowing was the thing that got families killed—

Felix's hand went to their coat pocket, where the scorched map lay folded against their ribs, and stopped there.

*Half-dead already.* He had been running from something on the Steppe. And now the Wardens were in Corrow asking after him, which meant whatever he ran from had long grey arms.

"Felix." Maret's voice, low, from behind the fish-stall. The old woman didn't look up from her knives. "Grey-cloaks took Dossan already. Asked him who sells working charms in this row. He gave them your corner."

Dossan. Who owed Felix eleven coppers and a grudge. Felix closed their eyes for half a heartbeat. "Which way did they go?"

"Toward you. You've got maybe—" Maret finally glanced up, and her eyes were old and kind and terribly tired. "Go, child. Don't be clever. Clever gets people buried."

"I'm never clever."

"You're the cleverest liar on this coast," Maret said, "and I've loved you ten years for it. Go."

* * *

Felix went, but not the way Maret meant.

The front of the row was closing—Felix could hear the Warden-pairs working stall to stall, methodical as bookkeepers. So Felix went up, the way the market's children went up, along the rafter-path beneath the warehouse eaves where the roof-beams made a bridge of shadows. It was a route built for smaller bodies and lighter consciences, but Felix had been crossing it since they were nine years old, and grief and fear both make a person thin.

From the beams, Felix could see the whole grey machinery of the raid below. And could see, too, the moment it turned from a hunt into something worse.

They had reached the spot where Ovid had died. The boards had been scrubbed, but the Wardens found what scrubs leave behind—a dark seam in the grain, a stain gone down between the planks. The woman with the sash knelt, touched it, and said something to the men around her. Two of them broke off immediately and began walking the row with a different posture—not searching anymore. *Closing.*

And then one of them stopped at the foot of Felix's own ladder.

"Charm-seller works here," the Warden said, voice carrying flatly up through the morning air. "Luck-knots. Fever-braids. The kind that work."

"The kind that work," the woman repeated, softly, to herself, and Felix felt those four words settle over them like a shroud. In the Prohibition's arithmetic, a charm that worked was a crime. A charm-seller who survived ten years of selling them was either invisible or protected, and the Wardens had just been told which stall on this row sold *real* magic.

*Burn it,* Felix thought, with a clarity that felt almost calm. *Burn everything.*

There was one charm left. One. The last of the batch Sera's letters had taught Felix to fold, back when letters were things sisters sent each other—veil-work, concealment, a trick of folded intention that made the eye slide off its bearer. Felix had kept it for ten years without using it, the way a drowning man keeps a plank he's too proud to grab. It was the last piece of the old life Felix owned that wasn't memory.

Felix burned it anyway.

The veil-work took hold the way it always did—not invisibility, but unimportance. For the length of one incense-stick's burning, no eye would find Felix worth finding. Felix dropped from the rafters into the mouth of the fish-row, walked through the closing net of Wardens with a porter's slouch and a porter's blankness, and passed within an arm's reach of the woman with the sash. She looked directly through Felix. Her eyes were the pale grey of weather coming.

Then Felix was past, and down the harbor stair, and into the tangle of streets below the Saltmarket where the city forgot itself in alleys and stairwells and the smoke of dye-works. Behind, faintly, Felix heard the shout go up—*the stall's been cleared, she's run, search the rows*—and the sound of boots scattering like spilled coins.

The charm guttered out three streets later, and Felix felt it go: a coldness in the chest, a sudden nakedness, as if a lamp had been blown out inside them. That was the last of it. Ten years of craft, and Felix Kavanagh now carried nothing magical except a dead man's map and a name they'd spent a decade trying to bury.

Felix leaned against a dye-works wall and let the shaking come, because it would come regardless, and it was better done here than on the road.

* * *

The safehouse was a cooper's loft above the tanneries, rented under a name that belonged to a man who had died in the flood-year, and Felix climbed to it by the outside stair with the map pressed so hard against their ribs it might have bruised bone. Lock the door. Bar the door. Lamp low, shuttered, the light squeezed down to a coin-sized circle on the floor.

Then, and only then, Felix unfolded the map.

Up close, in honest light, the scorching told its own story. The burn hadn't come from a campfire—it had come from above and behind, a single sweeping stroke, the mark of dragonfire at distance or something wearing its shape. The map's edges were curled and blackened; its face had been saved by the leather tube it rode in, and even so, one corner was eaten away entirely. Whatever had chased Ovid down the salt road, it had nearly taken the map with him.

But the face of it—ah, the face of it.

It was the Glass Steppe, drawn in the old surveyor's style, the style from before the Prohibition when maps were allowed to remember what lay east of the salt flats. Felix traced the inked line of the salt road with a fingertip. The glass plains beyond it, rendered in fine crosshatching that caught the lamplight like frost. And at the map's heart, drawn in a hand steadier than any surveyor's, a symbol Felix had not seen in ten years and had never once stopped seeing in dreams:

A circle, broken at the top. A flame seated in the break.

The sign of the Emberhold. The sign of the covenant.

Felix sat very still.

Below the symbol, marching in a neat column across the Steppe's width, ran a line of small dense characters—ciphers, dozens of them, worked into the map's margins like a border of stitching. And Felix knew that hand. Not the cipher itself, though the cipher was familiar too, familiar the way a scar is familiar—but the *hand*. The slight forward lean of the letters. The way the crossbars dipped left, as if the writer were always leaning into wind.

Sera had written her cipher left-leaning because she'd learned to write sitting on a doorstep with the harbor gale in her face, and she'd refused, all her childhood, to turn her back to the sea.

"Sera," Felix said aloud, to the empty loft, and the word cracked down the middle.

Their sister. Dead—had been dead for ten years, officially, in the records and in Felix's heart and in the grave-marker Felix had never visited because visiting meant believing. Dead in the breaking of the covenant, dead in the night the dragons fell silent and the Prohibition came down and the Kavanagh household ended in fire and iron. Felix had built a life on top of that death the way Corrow built on its pilings—everything resting on the certainty that the ground below was solid and sunken and would not move again.

Now the ground moved.

Because the cipher wasn't decoration. Felix's fingers were moving before their grief finished arriving, working the margin characters the way Sera had taught them at that same doorstep, *lean into it, little fox, the code leans too.* The first cluster resolved slowly, rustily, like a gate opening after winter. Then the next. Then the next, faster, the cipher waking in Felix's hands like a language spoken in sleep.

*Emberhold. Fire fails. Find the last speaker. She says he'll know the flame. Trust the runner. —O*

And beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*If I don't come back, tell my sister the fire remembers her. —S*

Felix read it four times. The fourth time was the worst, because by then the words had stopped being words and become a door, and behind the door was everything Felix had spent ten years nailing shut.

*Tell my sister.* Written by Sera's hand, in Sera's living, leaning, wind-blown script. Which meant that when these words were written, Sera Kavanagh was alive. And the runner had carried them east-to-west across the whole broken Steppe, hunted and burned and bleeding, to find a last speaker—who else could that be but Felix, last of the swordspeakers, the oath-bound line that ended in a cellar on covenant-night?—and deliver them to Felix's own hands.

*Sera is alive.*

The thought arrived like weather. It did not ask permission. It filled the whole room, and Felix discovered that a person can drown in hope as thoroughly as in seawater, and that hope, unlike seawater, leaves you wanting more.

And underneath it, colder, the second thought, the Warden-thought, the survivor-thought: *then why do the Wardens want the map badly enough to close a market district at dawn?*

Wardens did not mobilize for a dead Steppe-runner. Wardens did not kneel over bloodstains and send pairs into the alleys for a charm-seller of no rank. Unless the map was not a curiosity to them. Unless the symbol in its heart was not, to the grey-cloaks, a dead religion's relic—but a threat. Or worse: a *loose end*, ten years tied off, suddenly fraying.

Felix thought about Ovid again. *Half-dead already.* Hunted across the Steppe by something that burned, and then hunted into Corrow by something that wore grey. The poor man had been squeezed between the two great teeth of the world, and he had spent his last strength choosing which hands his message died in.

He had chosen well. Felix only wished he'd had the chance to know it.

Felix sat back on the floorboards, the lamp-flame doubled in their eyes, and did the arithmetic of their life. Stay in Corrow: the Wardens had Dossan's testimony, the stall's location, and patience. Flight to the southern ports: a fugitive with a hunted map and no charms, among strangers, forever, running. Or—

Or east. Across the salt road, onto the glass, following a dead man's map and a living sister's cipher to the Emberhold, where the covenant had broken and where, if the cipher told true, it could perhaps be mended. Toward the thing that burned. Toward the Wardens' own teeth.

Every rational fiber of Felix's careful, invisible, ten-years-in-the-weaving life voted *no*.

But Felix thought of Maret saying *don't be clever, clever gets people buried,* and thought: yes, and hiding got my family killed anyway, didn't it? Careful got them killed. Invisible got them killed. The Prohibition came down like a lid and the Kavanaghs were under it, and no amount of smallness had saved a single one of them. Ten years of being no one had purchased exactly nothing—except the skill of vanishing, which was, Felix realized with a strange cold laugh, precisely the skill a fugitive needed.

Grief had been a stone in Felix's chest for a decade. Now, for the first time, it sharpened to an edge.

Felix rose, and began to pack. Not the market-stall way—there was no market-stall anymore—but the road way: hard bread, oilskin, whetstone, the plain sword that had never once been an oath-sword since the night oaths became contraband, coin sewn into the coat seams. Last of all, the map, rewrapped in oilcloth, and Ovid's cipher-page copied twice—one copy sewn into a boot, one committed to the only vault that Wardens couldn't raid.

By the time the lamp burned down, the plan had a shape, if not yet a road. Out of Corrow before the net tightened. East along the salt flats while the weather held. And somewhere between here and the horizon, Felix would need help, because the Steppe killed solo travelers as surely as Wardens killed speakers, and Felix had spent ten years forgetting how to need anybody.

A problem for daylight.

Felix pinched out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the tannery yards below—the ordinary sounds of an ordinary city that did not yet know its oldest lie was fraying. Somewhere out there, Wardens were knocking on doors. Somewhere far east, a beacon was burning that Felix had thought was ash. And somewhere between the two was a sister, alive, writing *the fire remembers her* in a hand that leaned into wind.

Felix put their shoulder to the loft's shutter and looked out at the black line of the eastern hills, barely visible past the rooftops, where the Glass Steppe began its long bright nothing.

"Hold on, Sera," they said quietly. "I'm coming. And I'm bringing the whole rotten truth with me."

Below, faint but distinct, came the sound Felix had dreaded all night: boots on the outside stair. Measured. Unhurried. Already climbing.

The Warden's knock—and no time left to answer anything but the window.
