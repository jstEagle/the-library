## 3. The Thief in the Rafters

Wren had been watching the charm-seller for three weeks, and in that time she'd learned exactly four things, which was three more than most people managed to learn about anyone.

First: the charm-seller—Felix Kavanagh, though nobody in the smugglers' market knew the name, and Wren only knew it because she could pick a lock on a records office as easily as she picked a purse—arrived at the stall each morning before the lamps were doused and left after the last of the night-market crowd had thinned to drunks and beggars. Second: the charms were real. Not the painted-bone nonsense the other stalls sold, not dried rat hearts wrapped in red thread, but actual working luck-knots, actual fever charms that cooled a brow within an hour. Third: Felix never used magic where anyone could see. The knots went home with customers and worked their quiet miracles far from the stall, which meant no one connected them to anything but good fortune. And fourth—the thing that mattered most to Wren—the charm-seller kept a small tin box beneath the counter's false bottom, and in that box were the rejects. The mis-tied knots, the cracked fever-stones, the charms that worked only half or only sometimes or only on Tuesdays, if Tuesday was a thing a charm could care about.

Wren had stolen from that box eleven times.

She told herself it wasn't really stealing, which was a lie she'd gotten very good at telling, because the alternative was admitting she was a thief, and she'd promised herself at nine years old, huddled under a cart in the Warden-camp outside Corrow while the snow came down gray as ash, that she would never be what they said she was. But her little brother Tam needed fever charms the way he needed bread—more, some weeks—and half-working charms were still half-working, and the charm-seller charged three coppers for things that took ten minutes to tie, and Wren had exactly zero coppers on a good day.

So. Eleven thefts. She knew the rhythm of the stall now better than she knew the rhythm of her own heartbeat. Which was why, when the charm-seller abandoned the stall mid-morning—mid-morning, with a full tray of knots still displayed and the day's best selling hours stretching out ahead like an unguarded purse—Wren didn't think *opportunity*. She thought *wrong*.

She thought it again when she saw the charm-seller come back twenty minutes later moving fast through the crowd, too fast, with a look on their face that Wren recognized from funerals and fires and the one time the Wardens had swept the camp and dragged off the man who taught her to juggle. It was the look of someone carrying something heavier than their arms could hold.

Wren followed them. Following people was the second thing she was good at, right after lying to herself.

* * *

The charm-seller cut through the tannery row, down the stair between the dye-houses where the air went purple and reeking, along the canal path where the water moved slow and green. They checked over their shoulder four times in as many minutes, and each time Wren melted into whatever the street offered—a doorway, a stack of hides, the shadow of a leaning wall—and each time she felt the small hot thrill she always felt, the one that made stealing feel less like survival and more like a game she was winning.

Then the charm-seller turned into the rookery behind the salt-merchants' warehouses, and Wren made her mistake, because she got greedy. There was a gap between two buildings, a drainpipe running up the brickwork like a black vein, and above it the flat rooftops where a person carrying something precious might hide. Wren wanted to see where they were going. She wanted to know what they carried. Curiosity was her third-best skill and her worst habit, and it put her in the open just long enough for the charm-seller to stop walking, turn around, and say, in a voice flat as slate:

"You can come down from there, or I can keep walking and let you explain to the next person why you're skulking."

Wren froze on the drainpipe, one hand and one boot hooked onto brick, feeling exactly as graceful as she looked, which was not at all. "Skulking? Me? I live here."

"You live on a drainpipe."

"Rent's cheap." She dropped lightly to the ground, landing in a crouch, and looked up at the charm-seller properly for the first time at conversational distance. Up close, Felix Kavanagh had eyes the color of old pewter and hands that were nicked and scarred in the way of someone who worked with cord and blade both, and a face that was trying very hard to be nothing at all. "Fine. You caught me. I'm a great admirer of your work. Especially the tin box."

Something flickered across that careful face. "The tin box," Felix repeated.

"The rejects. Half-charms. Cracked stones." Wren spread her hands, all innocence, which fooled no one including herself. "Eleven times. You count?"

"I count everything." The pewter eyes narrowed. "You've been taking my castoffs for three weeks. Why haven't I caught you before?"

"Because I'm better at this than you are at noticing." It came out with more edge than she'd intended, and Wren heard it land, saw the charm-seller's chin lift slightly—not offense, she realized. Assessment. Recalibrating. This was a person who measured threats quickly and filed them away. "Anyway. That's not why I stopped you."

"It isn't? You stopped me."

"I did." Wren glanced up and down the rookery, then stepped closer, dropping her voice. "And here's how this goes. Option one: you walk away, I go find a Warden sergeant, and I tell him there's a charm-seller in the smugglers' market selling real magic, and I describe your face so well his grandmother could pick you out of a lineup. Prohibition carries a pyre, doesn't it? Or at least the deep cells. I hear the cells under the Chancellery don't have windows."

The silence that followed had weight to it. Somewhere above, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"That's blackmail," Felix said finally.

"That's option one." Wren held up a second finger. "Option two: you take me with you."

"Take you—" For the first time, the flat composure cracked, and underneath it was something almost humanly baffled. "Take you where? Who says I'm going anywhere?"

"Everyone's going somewhere today." Wren ticked it off on her fingers, enjoying herself despite everything, despite the fear sitting cold in her stomach that she was gambling her whole life on a read of a stranger's face. "You abandon your stall mid-morning. You come back looking like your own ghost. You check every shadow twice. And—" she leaned in, savoring it, "—you've got paper. Rolled up, oilskin-wrapped, inside your coat, left side, against your ribs. Nobody carries paper like that unless the paper matters more than their lungs."

Felix's hand moved toward the coat and stopped. Wren grinned.

"And here's the part you're going to love," she said. "I've seen maps. Sold a few I lifted, traded a few back when the owners got violent. And I've seen the shape of the one you're hiding, because it pressed against your coat when you bent to check that shadow by the tannery stairs. Coastline's wrong for anywhere east. Too much open curve. Too much blank." She let the grin fade into something steadier. "That's west. That's Steppe country. And I was born beside the Glass Steppe, charm-seller, in a camp you've probably walked past a hundred times without seeing, and I know those glass plains the way you know your knot-work. You need a guide. I need out of Corrow before the Wardens finish tearing apart the market and start pulling in everyone who ever bought a lucky rabbit's foot from the wrong stall. So. Option two."

* * *

Felix didn't answer right away. They stood in the rookery's narrow light—just a strip of pale sky between the warehouse roofs, dust drifting through it like slow snow—and Wren watched them think. She was good at reading faces; hers had to be readable, growing up in a camp where the wrong expression at the wrong time could get your ration card torn up. And what she read on the charm-seller's face was grief being wrestled into a locked room, and fear pacing outside the door, and under both of them something stubborn as bedrock.

"Who died?" Wren asked quietly.

The question landed harder than any threat she'd made. Felix's jaw tightened. "Why do you think someone died?"

"Because you walk like the world got rearranged and you haven't found where to stand yet." Wren shrugged. "I've seen it. When the Tide took the eastern camps—everyone walks like that after. Like the floor might be missing next time they step."

"The courier who brought me the map was killed." The words came out clipped, rationed. "This morning. In front of me."

"And the map's worth killing for."

"The map is worth dying for. Possibly several times over." Felix studied her, and Wren resisted the urge to fidget, which was another skill she'd mastered young. "You understand that coming with me means the Wardens will hunt you too. Not chase—hunt. There's a difference. Chasing ends. Hunting doesn't."

"I've been hunted since I was born, more or less. Being hunted by professionals is just a promotion." Wren crossed her arms. "Here's what I bring. One: I know the Steppe. Water holes, safe paths, where the glass gets thin enough to crack and drop you into old fire-vents. Two: I can get into, out of, and through anything with a lock, a latch, or a lazy guard. Three: I'm small, fast, forgettable, and I lie beautifully. Four—" she hesitated, and for a moment the performance dropped and there was just a girl of maybe nineteen with chapped hands and hollow cheeks and a brother waiting somewhere she couldn't afford to think about, "—I'm useful alive, and I know it, and I'll act like it. I won't do anything stupid. Stupid people die on the Steppe, and I intend to not die. Very firmly."

Felix was silent a long moment. Then: "Your brother. You mentioned him just now in your head—I could see it. He stays in Corrow?"

Wren blinked. "How did you—that wasn't out loud."

"You looked toward the river district. People look toward what they're leaving." A pause. "If you come with me, you can't send for him. Not yet. Letters get read."

"I know how letters work." The snap in her voice surprised her. "He's safer invisible. I've kept him invisible for six years. I can do it from farther away than most."

Something passed over Felix's face at that—invisible, kept safe, family—and Wren catalogued it, filed it, added it to the growing dossier of things the charm-seller wasn't saying. Everyone in this city was carrying a locked room. Hers just happened to be smaller than most.

"One condition," Felix said.

"Name it."

"You steal from me again—charms, coin, the map, anything—and we're done. Not angry-done. Done-done. I leave you wherever we are and you find your own way back across the Steppe."

"Harsh."

"Honest."

Wren considered. Then she spat into her palm and held it out, because some agreements were too serious for handshakes and too important for lies. "Spit's binding in the camps," she said. "Break a spit-promise and even the Wardens won't trade with you after."

Felix looked at the offered hand for a moment—this grim, careful, grieving stranger with a map against their ribs and death on their heels—and then, to Wren's lasting surprise, spat into their own palm and clasped it. Their grip was dry and strong and scarred.

"Sera," Felix said, so quietly Wren almost missed it.

"What?"

"The name. On the map. It's written in my sister's cipher, and my sister has been dead for ten years, and this morning a dying man whispered her name to me in the middle of a market full of people who never notice anything." Felix's pewter eyes fixed on hers. "So understand what you're signing up for. I'm not going to the Steppe for adventure. I'm going because the dead are talking, and I've spent ten years learning not to listen, and I can't anymore."

The wind came down the rookery, smelling of salt and tar and distant rain. Wren found, to her irritation, that her throat had gone tight. She covered it with a shrug.

"My mother used to say the Steppe remembers everybody," she said. "Glass holds what falls on it. Maybe it holds her too."

Felix looked at her differently then—not as a blackmailer, not as a thief, but as something adjacent to a person. "What was the camp called? Where you were born?"

"Emberlight Camp. Eastern edge. Before the Tide came through and—" Wren stopped, waved a hand vaguely at the concept of annihilation. "It's not there anymore. Nothing's there anymore. Just glass and grass fighting over the ruins."

"Emberlight," Felix repeated, and something in their voice made Wren's spine prickle, some note she couldn't place, like hearing your own name spoken in a foreign language. "Then you'd better show me the way, thief. And I'd better teach you which end of a sword is which before the Wardens catch up. Because they will catch up."

"They always do." Wren grinned, and this time the grin was real, sharp-edged, alive. "Good thing I run faster than they march. Come on, charm-seller. Salt gate closes at dusk, and I know a fence who owes favors bigger than yours."

She set off down the rookery at a jog, and after a moment—after one last glance back at the stall she'd tended for ten years, one last touch of the map through the oilskin, one last breath of the life that was already over—Felix Kavanagh followed.

Behind them, in the market they'd left, the first Warden horns began to sound.

* * *

They made the river district by late afternoon, Wren leading them through a maze of leaning tenements and rope-bridges that Felix clearly distrusted on principle. Twice they ducked into cellars to let Warden patrols pass, boots ringing on cobbles, and twice Wren watched Felix go still and silent and terrible in the dark, hand resting on the sword at their hip like it was the last true thing in the world.

At the third cellar, waiting out a patrol that was taking its sweet time, Felix finally spoke.

"The charms you stole. The fever ones. Who were they for?"

"Told you. My brother."

"Fever charms don't cure fevers. They cool them. Buy time." Felix's voice was careful. "Six years is a lot of time."

Wren stared at the dark wall opposite, at the water seeping green through the stones. "Tam was born sick. Heart like a bird's, the camp healer said. Small and quick and liable to stop. The charms keep him level. Keep the fevers from cooking him." She hugged her knees. "Half-charms, mostly. Your rejects. But half is better than nothing, and nothing is what we could afford."

Silence. Then, softly, the sound of cord being worked—Felix's fingers, quick and sure in the dark. When they finished, they held out a luck-knot, tied tight and perfect, humming faintly with a warmth Wren felt before she touched it.

"This one's not a reject," Felix said. "Give it to him before you go. Tonight. Tell him it's from the market, no names."

Wren took it. It sat in her palm like a small sleeping coal, and she found she couldn't speak for a moment, which was unacceptable, so she cleared her throat and said, "This doesn't buy you out of the spit-promise terms."

"No. It buys nothing. That's what makes it different."

Above them the boots faded at last, and Wren tucked the knot into her shirt, against her skin, where Tam's fever charms had lived for six years, and led the charm-seller up into the fading light.

One more night in Corrow. One more night of locks and shadows and saying goodbye to a boy who couldn't be told goodbye. Then the salt gate, and the Steppe, and whatever the dead sister's cipher wanted from the living.

The horns sounded again, farther off now, but patient.

Wardens were patient. Wren ran anyway.
