## 4. What the Cipher Says

The safehouse was a coffin with a skylight.

Felix had chosen it years ago for exactly that reason: a decommissioned dye-works loft above Tanner's Row, reachable only by a ladder through a false roof-panel, forgotten by everyone except the rats and one very old woman who still believed she owned it. Dust hung in the light like something suspended mid-fall. The vats below had gone to rust and resin-smell, and the whole place hummed at night with the river's slow complaint beneath the pilings.

Felix climbed up with the map pressed flat against their chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and did not light a lamp until every shutter was drawn and every seam checked twice. Then, finally, a single stub of tallow candle, cupped inside a chipped dye-bowl so its glow would show through no crack.

The map came out of its wrapping slowly, the way one handles a wound.

Scorched along one edge, soft as cloth from handling, it was drawn on dragon-parchment—the real thing, not the dyed calfskin the smugglers sold to tourists at three coppers a foot. Felix had seen true dragon-parchment only once before, ten years ago, in a house that no longer existed. It held ink strangely, drinking it deep so that lines seemed to float *beneath* the surface rather than on it. This map had been made before the Prohibition. Before everything.

And across its lower corner, small enough to be missed as mere watermark, ran a cipher in a hand Felix would have known blindfolded, drunk, dead.

*Seraphine.*

Their sister had written left-slanting when she was being careful and right-slanting when she was showing off. This was left-slanting. Careful. Hidden. Which meant whoever drew this map had known it might be seized, and had hidden what mattered where only family would think to look.

Felix sat down on an overturned dye-crate and let the grief come, because there was no one here to see it and because they had learned—ten years of learning—that grief refused was grief doubled. It came the way it always did: not as tears first but as cold, a coldness starting behind the sternum and spreading out to the fingertips, as if some old charm were working backward through them. Their mother's voice saying *count to ten, little spark, then count again.* Their father's laugh from the workshop. Sera, seventeen and furious and brilliant, arguing politics at supper while the stew went cold.

Then the night itself. The knocking. The fire that burned too blue to be honest fire.

Felix counted to ten. Counted again. The cold receded to its usual low ache, the one they carried like a stone sewn into the lining of everything they wore.

*Work,* they told themselves. *Grief is for people who have time.*

They took out their tools: a sliver of polished horn, a needle fine as an eyelash, a scrap of charcoal, and the one luxury they permitted themselves—a lens ground by a Corrow optician who owed them more favors than he could count. By candlelight, the cipher resolved into its architecture. Sera's system was a child's game grown teeth: letters displaced along a spiral keyed to a word only Kavanaghs would know. Felix tried the obvious ones first. *Mother.* No. *Ember.* No. *Home.*

Then, hands steady in the way that meant they were not steady at all: *Luck-knot.*

The word their mother had used for the charms she wove for neighbors, free, never sold, back when charm-craft was a kindness and not a crime.

The spiral opened.

The first decoded line was coordinates—not the crude latitude of Warden charts but the old covenant-grid, degrees measured from the Emberhold itself, which no human cartographer had been permitted to use since the Breaking. The second was a phrase that stopped Felix's breath:

*The fire gutters. Ovid runs. If you read this, you are the last speaker, and I am sorry.*

*I am sorry.*

Not *I miss you.* Not *I survived.* An apology, laid down like a debt acknowledged before it was even named—and underneath it, in smaller characters, almost squeezed into the margin as if the writer had hesitated:

*They keep me where the fire is. Do not come for me. Come for the fire.*

Felix read it four times. On the fourth reading their vision blurred, and they realized they were crying without any of the usual ceremony, tears simply falling onto the dragon-parchment and vanishing into it, drunk deep like the ink.

Sera alive. Sera alive for ten years, alive through every anniversary Felix had mourned her, alive through the mass grave and the sealed house and the name they had taught themselves never to say aloud in case the walls of Corrow had ears. Alive, and kept—*kept*—where the fire was, which meant the Emberhold, which meant the heart of the Steppe, which meant a journey of nine hundred miles across country where magic died and Wardens hunted and the Hollow Tide gnawed at the edge of the world.

Hope arrived then, and it was exactly as dangerous as Felix remembered. It did not feel like warmth. It felt like a hook set behind the breastbone, pulling toward a horizon, indifferent to whether the flesh holding it tore.

*Do not come for me. Come for the fire.*

"You know me better than that," Felix said aloud, to a sister ten years silent, and their voice cracked on the second word.

* * *

They worked until the candle was a puddle.

The full decode took hours because Sera had layered it—coordinates in the spiral, but route-notes in a second key beneath, keyed to the private language they'd invented as children, the one about dragons and thieves and the salt-god of the harbor. The route-notes were terse and practical and terrifying in their implications. *Avoid the Warden road at Vell's Crossing; they post Tide-lures now.* *Water at the glass seeps is drinkable but the glass remembers; walk where it is cloudy, not clear.* And once, uncharacteristically personal, in the margin beside a sketch of the Emberhold's beacon-tower:

*It sings at night. Did you know that? It sounds like Mother humming.*

Felix put the lens down and pressed the heels of both hands against their eyes.

The city outside was going about its evening. Through the shutter-seams came the sounds of Corrow at dusk: the fish-bells, the argument of gulls over the rendering yards, a Warden drum marking the change of patrol on the Processional—two beats, rest, two beats, the rhythm every child in the city learned before they learned letters. Somewhere below, Tanner's Row was lighting its lamps, and families were sitting down to suppers that did not include luck-knots or fever-charms or any of the hundred small magics that had once stitched a neighborhood together, because stitching was illegal now, and fear was cheaper.

Ten years Felix had built this life. Not a good life—an invisible one, a life shaped like a shadow, all corners and caution. They had a stall in the smugglers' market under a name that wasn't theirs. They had a landlady who thought they repaired umbrellas. They had routines worn smooth as river stones: market at dawn, decode nothing, speak to no one of anything, sleep with the ladder pulled up. A careful life. A buried life. The whole architecture of it had one purpose—to make sure that what happened to the Kavanaghs never happened to anyone again, least of all Felix.

And now a dying courier's blood was drying on their sleeve and a dead sister's handwriting was telling them to cross the world.

*Come for the fire.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the Warden drums changed pattern—three beats now, rest, three. Night patrol. Felix knew the rhythms of Corrow the way other people knew their own pulse, and three-beat meant the sweep patrols, the ones that worked door to door with warrant-chains and truth-sniffers, the ones you did not outwait.

They began, methodically, to burn the safehouse.

Not with fire—with water and a knife. The decodes went into the dye-bowl and dissolved to grey ghosts. The lens went into a wall-crack behind a loose brick, a cache that might outlive them. The maps of Corrow's drains, the lists of fences and friendly doors, the ten-year archive of a careful life—all of it fed to the river through the floor-drain, ink blooming and unraveling like smoke underwater. Last of all Felix sat with the scorched map itself, Sera's cipher now naked to their eye, and thought about what carrying it meant.

If the Wardens caught them with it, it would not be a cell. Cells were for smugglers and charm-sellers. This was covenant-material, dragon-parchment, the name of a traitor's network written in a traitor's hand. This was the kind of thing that got your whole building questioned, your neighbors' neighbors questioned, your grave dug before your body was in it.

The rational choice—the choice ten years of care had trained into their bones—was to leave the map. Copy what mattered into memory, destroy the original, vanish deeper into the city's folds. Let the fire gutter. Let the barrier fail. Let someone else be the last speaker.

But hope had set its hook, and Felix found they could no longer remember how to want a small life. That was the cruelty of it. For ten years they had wanted nothing, and wanting nothing had kept them alive, and now a single line in a dead woman's living hand—*I am sorry*—had undone the whole discipline of survival, and Felix understood that they had not been living all this time after all. They had been waiting. Preserved, like the map, like the oath-sword wrapped in sacking under the floorboards, against a day they had pretended would never come.

They pried up the board.

The sword lay where it had lain for a decade, wrapped in wool and waxcloth, its hilt bound in leather gone stiff as bark. It had been their father's, and his mother's before him, and it had not sung since the night the covenant broke—oath-blades woke for covenant-work, and there had been no covenant to work for. Felix unwrapped it anyway, half expecting dust and disappointment.

The blade was clean. Ten years in the dark, and it was clean, and along its fuller ran a faint warmth-colored shimmer, like coals remembered under ash.

Felix's hands shook. They rewrapped it, slung it across their back beneath a fishmonger's coat, and told themselves it was only metal. It weighed nothing at all. It weighed everything.

* * *

They were halfway down the ladder when the knock came.

Not on the safehouse door—the safehouse had no door worth knocking on—but below, on the street door of the dye-works, heavy and official, the knock of people who expected answers. Then voices, muffled by floors: two of them, maybe three, and the particular clipped cadence of Warden procedure. *By authority of the Proclamation. Open in the name of order.*

Felix froze on the ladder, one hand on a rung slick with old dye, and listened to their careful life end.

There was a back way—they had made sure of that years ago, a rope-and-pulley drop into the tannery yard behind—but the yard backed onto Cutpurse Lane, and Cutpurse Lane at night was Warden territory on any patrol rotation Felix had ever tracked. Which meant the rotation had changed. Which meant someone had talked. The fence at the salt gate, maybe, or the optician, or the old woman who still believed she owned the building—someone whose memory of Felix's mother had proven cheaper than their silence.

That was the arithmetic of hiding. Every year the ledger of people who knew your face grew longer, and the price of your secret grew smaller, until one day someone sold you for less than a luck-knot.

Above, through the skylight, boots crossed the loft Felix had just vacated. A lantern-beam swept the seams of the shutters. Someone said, quietly, the words Felix had spent ten years dreading and somehow always known they'd hear:

"She was here. Smell the tallow. Fresh."

Felix went down the rope into the dark of the tannery yard, the map against their chest and the sleeping sword across their back, and stood for one moment in the stink of hides and lime looking up at the small square of sky above the safehouse, where the lantern-light moved like a slow search of everything they had been.

Goodbye, they thought. Not to the building. To the life. To the umbrella-mender and the careful routines and the discipline of wanting nothing. Goodbye to the version of them that had believed burial was safety.

Then they pulled their hood up and walked into Cutpurse Lane, past the mouth of the alley where a Warden patrol's lanterns bobbed closer with every heartbeat, and turned—not away from the lane, as instinct screamed, but into it, because the one direction hunters never watched was the one that led through their own shadow.

Behind them, faint through the walls, the drums changed to four beats. Alarm.

Ahead, somewhere past the salt gate and the gray flats beyond it, the Glass Steppe waited, blinding and vast, with a guttering fire at the end of it and a sister who sang to herself in a tower because she thought no one was coming.

Felix counted to ten. Counted again.

Then they ran.
