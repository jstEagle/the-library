## 5. Out the Salt Gate

The fence's shop smelled of tallow and old iron, the way it had ten years ago when Felix's mother used to bring her charms here to sell. Doss Marlow had not changed the smell, only added to it—layers of candle smoke and lamp oil and the particular sourness of coins handled by too many desperate hands. He sat behind his counter like a toad on a stone, fat fingers laced over a belly straining at a vest that had once been fine, and he looked at Felix for a long moment before he said anything at all.

"You have her eyes," he said.

"I have my own eyes."

"You have her eyes in them." He unfolded himself from the stool with a groan that suggested the stool was winning their long war, and shuffled to the back of the shop, moving bolts of "cloth" that were really bundles of contraband under honest-looking fabric. "Sit. Both of you. You look like you've run from Corrow to Corrow."

Wren perched on a crate near the door, which was not sitting so much as staging herself for flight, and Felix understood the instinct. Every instinct in their own body was screaming it too. *You are in a fence's back room with a stolen map and a thief and the Wardens are burning the smugglers' market behind you.* The thought arrived with the taste of ash still in their mouth—the concealment charm's last flare, the alley, Wren's hand yanking them sideways an instant before a lantern-spear swept the wall where they'd been standing.

"Marlow," Felix said. "I need the salt gate open tonight. The postern, if the main gate's watched. And I need it quiet."

"And I need my youth back and a river of wine, and neither of us is getting what we want by asking pretty." He came back with a clay jug and three cups, poured without asking, and pushed one across the counter. "Drink. You're white as salt yourself."

Felix didn't touch it. "Do you know what's happening in the Lowmarket?"

"I know Wardens don't burn a market for one dead runner unless the runner was carrying something worth more than the market." Marlow's small eyes were shrewd and unhurried. "I know they've closed the river stairs and doubled the watch on every gate but one. And I know your mother saved my daughter's life with a fever charm the year the grey cough came through, and never took a copper for it, and told me—" he paused, and something moved behind the shrewdness, something older and wearier, "—she told me that if ever her children needed a door, I'd know which door she meant."

The words landed like a hand pressed flat against Felix's sternum. Their mother had been dead ten years. Ten years of careful invisibility, of selling luck-knots that were only knots and fever charms that were mostly willow bark and belief, of being no one—and here was her voice coming out of a fat old fence's mouth as if she'd left it in escrow.

"She meant this door," Felix said quietly.

"She meant this door." Marlow drank, wiped his mouth. "The salt gate. It's the worst gate in Corrow—that's why it's the best one. Half-fallen since the quake, and the Wardens keep a token watch because nothing comes through it but salt-haulers and fools. There's a culvert beneath it, old drainage, runs out under the wall to the flats. My man Hesk keeps the toll-booth there. Tell him the toll is paid in full and show him—" Marlow reached into his vest and produced a small disc of green glass, worn smooth, "—this. He'll know it."

Felix took the disc. It was warm from his body, and lighter than it should have been, and it trembled slightly in their fingers because their fingers were trembling.

"Why?" The question came out rougher than intended. "Ten years, Marlow. Why now? Why me?"

The fence looked at them for a long time. Outside, distantly, there was the sound of shouting—not close, not yet, but the city had a new texture tonight, a grain of fear running through it like a flaw through glass.

"Because I'm old," he said finally, "and because I've spent thirty years buying and selling other people's secrets, and I can smell it when a secret is going to eat the world. Whatever's on that map, whatever name that dying man gave you—" Felix flinched; Marlow saw, and nodded slowly, "—it's already eating. I can hear its teeth. So take my door, Kavanagh. Take it and don't come back through it, because I don't think there'll be a door after tonight."

* * *

They went down through the tannery district as the bells began.

Not alarm bells—Corrow hadn't sounded true alarms since the Prohibition—but the curfew bells, rolling out quarter by quarter, and each peal fell on Felix's spine like a dropped stone. Curfew meant the Wardens sweeping street by street with lantern-spears and hound-charms, and it meant the culvert under the salt gate would be watched or it wouldn't, and there was no way to know which until they were inside it.

Wren moved through the dark streets like water finding its level. She'd been silent since Marlow's shop, which worried Felix more than talk would have; the girl's silence had a coiled quality, a held breath. Twice she stopped them with a raised fist and pulled them into doorways while patrols passed, and twice she chose hiding places before Felix had even registered the patrol's approach.

"You've done this before," Felix whispered, the second time.

"I've done everything before." Wren's voice was flat. "Hiding's the first thing they teach you in the camps. Before letters. Before prayers." A pause, and then, grudgingly, almost against her will: "Your fence friend. He knew your mother."

"He did."

"And he just... gave you the way out. For free."

"For a debt."

Wren was quiet for three steps. Then: "Nobody in this city does anything for a debt. Debts are what people say when they want something later."

"My mother would have said you're right," Felix said. "She'd also have said that some people pay their debts early so they don't owe anything later. Maybe Marlow's one of those."

"Maybe your mother was a fool."

"Maybe." Felix kept walking. "She died anyway. Foolishness doesn't save you. But sometimes it saves someone else, years later, when you can't see it." They heard Wren chew on that in silence, and did not look back to see her expression, because some things could not survive being seen directly.

The salt district announced itself by smell—brine and mineral rot, the ghost of the flats beyond the walls where the sea had retreated a generation ago and left its salt crust like a scab. The buildings grew lower and meaner, roofs sagging, walls patched with whatever the tide of poverty had washed up. And then the wall itself rose ahead of them, vast and black against the bell-lit sky, and set into its base like a rotten tooth: the salt gate.

It was worse than half-fallen. The great arch had cracked diagonally, one side slumped, the whole structure shored up with timber braces that looked like a beggar's crutches. Beside it squatted the toll-booth, a box of planks and lamplight, and in the booth sat a shape that might have been a man or might have been a heap of sacks wearing a hat.

"Hesk," Felix breathed, and stepped forward—

—and Wren's hand clamped on their arm, hard enough to bruise.

"Wait." Her voice was barely air. "Look at the booth. Look at the lamp."

Felix looked. The lamp burned steady. Too steady. No flicker, no guttering, no moth-shadow dancing across it. A lamp that burned that still was either enchanted or freshly trimmed, and neither boded well for a lonely toll-keeper on curfew night.

And then the shape in the booth stood up, and it was too tall for Hesk, and the hat came off, and lantern-light caught the pale grey of a Warden's cloak.

"No," Felix whispered. The word came out of them like something broken. "No, Marlow wouldn't—"

"Everyone would," Wren said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible arithmetic of the camps in her voice. "Everyone would, for a pardon. Now move."

But there was nowhere to move *to*. Behind them, the curfew bells; ahead, the trap; around them, low buildings and dead ends. Felix's mind, trained by ten years of careful smallness, ran through options and found them all ending in irons or worse—and then found one more, a bad one, a desperate one, and hated it even as their feet started moving.

"The culvert doesn't start at the booth," they said. "Marlow said drainage. Drainage has outfalls. Come on."

They ran along the base of the wall, crouched below the sightline of the ruined arch, Wren a shadow at their shoulder. Fifty yards, a hundred. The salt crust crackled underfoot like thin ice. And there—half-hidden behind a collapsed shed, a black mouth in the wall's foundation, breathing out cold air that smelled of deep stone and stagnant water. An outfall. Old, choked with silt at the lip, but open.

Felix was on their knees at its edge, mapping the crawl in their head, when the light found them.

"Lantern-spears!" Wren's shout and her shove came together; Felix went sprawling into the silt as white light seared the space where their head had been. Boots. Many boots, ringing on the salt crust, fanning out from the gate with the drilled precision Felix had spent a decade learning to avoid. A voice—amplified by a speaking-charm, flat and terrible:

"Fugitives at the wall. Take them living. The map is the priority."

*The map.* Not them. The map. Something about that order—its cold priorities, the way it valued paper over persons—hardened something in Felix that had been soft and frightened all night. They rolled, came up running, and dove for the culvert mouth with Wren a half-step behind.

The tunnel swallowed them into blackness and the roar of their own blood.

They crawled. It was not dignified and it was not fast. The culvert was shoulder-width, its ceiling pressing down like a hand, its floor a channel of old drain-slime and brackish trickle. Somewhere behind them light bloomed at the entrance—lantern-spears probing the dark—and a voice called into the tunnel, distorted by echo, offering terms. Pardon for the map. Pardon for cooperation. The word *pardon* bounced off the stones again and again until it lost all meaning, became just a sound, a bird-cry, nothing.

"They won't come in," Wren panted beside Felix, her breath hot against their ear. "Too narrow. They'll seal the far end."

"Then we'd better be past the far end."

The tunnel bent, and bent again, and the air changed—sweeter, colder, tasting of open night—and then the culvert spat them out onto the salt flats under a sky so wide Felix made a sound they'd never heard themselves make before.

* * *

Later, they would not be able to say how long they lay on the salt crust, side by side, gasping like landed fish. The wall stood behind them, a black seam across the world, and torchlight crawled along its top like lice. Ahead—ahead was everything else.

The Glass Steppe began where the salt ended, and it did not begin gently. It began like a second dawn lying on its side: a plain of fused sand stretching to the horizon, catching the moon and the stars and throwing them back in sheets and rivers of pale fire. Where the dragon-fire of the old covenant had swept the land, it had turned sand to glass—not in patches but in whole provinces, mile upon mile of it, rippled like a frozen sea, veined with darker flows where the heat had run deepest. And everywhere, everywhere, the grass: stubborn, miraculous steppe-grass growing straight up through the glass in cracks and seams, silver-green in the starlight, bending in a wind that came off the plain smelling of distance.

Felix had seen the Steppe from the city walls their whole life—a glimmer on the horizon, a story, a scar. They had never imagined *this*: that it would be beautiful. That the wound in the world would shine.

"Oh," Wren said. Just that. One syllable, and in it Felix heard the camps, and the walls, and every narrow ceiling the girl had ever hidden under, cracking a little.

"It remembers," Felix said. Their voice came out strange. "The glass. It's what the fire did, ten years ago, the last night of the covenant. The whole Steppe burned, they say—dragons flying low, the oath-fire pouring off them—and when it cooled, this. People say the glass still holds the shape of the flames. That if you dig, you find sand underneath that never burned. Unchanged. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"I don't know." Felix stood. Their legs shook. Their hands shook. Everything in them that had spent ten years learning to be small was now standing in front of an ocean of light, and the vertigo of it was physical—a tilting, a falling-without-falling, as if the ground had quietly become the sky. "Maybe waiting for us."

Behind them, muffled by distance and stone, a bell began to ring again. Different bells now. Faster.

Wren sat up sharply. "That's not curfew."

"No." Felix shaded their eyes and looked back at the wall. Along its top, at the salt gate, lights were massing—not searching now, gathering. And away to the south, faint but unmistakable, dust hung above the flats road: riders. Moving fast. Toward the gate, or toward the flats, toward *them*, and there was no way to know which except to assume the worst, because Marlow's face was in Felix's memory saying *take my door and don't come back through it*, and because the word *pardon* was still echoing in the culvert behind them, and because a debt was exactly the kind of thing a man could pay with other people's blood.

"He sold us," Felix said. Flat. Final. Grief and fury in the same breath, because the betrayal hurt precisely because the kindness had been real—both things true in the same old man's hands, and maybe that was the worst part, that there had never been a lie at all, only a man who meant his debt and feared his masters and chose his neck.

"Course he did." Wren was already on her feet, brushing salt from her knees, and when Felix looked at her the girl's face had gone hard and bright as the plain itself. "Question isn't whether he sold us. Question's whether we're still here to be collected." She jerked her chin at the shining immensity ahead. "Out there, no gates. No walls. No corners to get cornered in." A pause, and then, with the ghost of her crooked grin: "Also no roofs, no markets, no idea what's edible, and I heard the Steppe eats people. But mostly the first things."

Felix laughed. It startled them—the sound tearing loose before they'd decided to make it, ragged and disbelieving, the first laugh in longer than they could measure. Perhaps it was terror. Perhaps it was the vertigo, the whole careful architecture of their hidden life collapsing at once, leaving nothing between them and the open plain but night air. Ten years of being invisible, and here at the end of it they stood in starlight bright enough to cast shadows, exposed to the whole turning world, and it felt—God help them—it felt like breathing after drowning.

They turned their back on Corrow. On the bells, the riders, the wall, the grave of their mother's debt and their own careful decade. The map rode against their chest, scorched edges and Sera's cipher, heavier than paper had any right to be. *Sera may be alive.* The thought was a coal they carried carefully, afraid to breathe on it, afraid to let it go out.

"Stay off the dark veins in the glass," Felix said, stepping down onto the plain proper, where the first blades of steppe-grass brushed their ankles like fingers. "Marlow's maps say the deep flows hold the heat even now. And watch the horizon every hundred counts. If the Wardens ride onto the glass, we'll see them a mile off."

"And if the Tide comes instead?" Wren asked, falling into step. She asked it lightly, the way camp children asked about hunger—as though the answer couldn't matter, since it would arrive regardless.

Felix looked east, where the plain ran on and on toward a darkness on the horizon that was not night. A darkness with an edge. A darkness that the starlight refused to touch.

"Then we run," they said, "and we don't stop until the Emberhold."

They walked out onto the Glass Steppe together, two small figures on an ocean of frozen fire, and behind them the bells rang on, and somewhere on the dark road dust was rising, and ahead—far ahead, at the edge of seeing—a single point of light burned on the horizon, red as a coal, red as an ember, and even at that distance Felix could have sworn it flickered.
