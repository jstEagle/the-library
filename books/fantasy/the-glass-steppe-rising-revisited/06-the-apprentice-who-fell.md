## 6. The Apprentice Who Fell

The summons came at the fourth bell of the morning watch, and Ilya Vosk had already been awake for two hours, standing at the window of his cell in the Wardenhouse with his hands folded behind his back, watching frost crawl up the glass. He had learned to sleep like a soldier—lightly, briefly, on command—but the order that had come down from the capital three days ago sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, and this morning it would not let him rest.

*Find them. The courier's accomplices. The map is to be recovered intact or destroyed. No exceptions.*

No exceptions. He turned the phrase over as he dressed in the grey-and-silver of an apprentice second class, buckling the prohibition-ward at his hip with fingers that knew the motion too well to need thinking. Ten years in the Wardens, four of them as an apprentice under Commander Aldric Voss, and he could still recite the Prohibition's founding catechism in his sleep: *Magic is a wound in the world. The speaker is the blade that made it. The Warden is the hand that closes it.*

He had believed it the way he believed in the sunrise. Completely, without examination, because examination had never seemed necessary.

Now the stone in his chest rolled over, and he wondered.

* * *

Commander Sef Halvane was not Aldric Voss. That was the first thing Ilya had thought when Voss died two winters ago—a fall from the Emberwatch cliffs, they said, though no one had ever shown Ilya the body—and it was the thing he kept returning to now, standing at attention before Halvane's desk while the commander read a report with lips slightly pursed, as though the words tasted faintly of vinegar.

Halvane was a careful man. Voss had been a blunt one. Voss had taught his apprentices to ask questions because a Warden who didn't understand what she enforced would enforce nothing worth having; Halvane taught his to obey, because obedience was cheaper than understanding and lasted longer.

"The courier," Halvane said without looking up, "was named Ovid Marsh. A Steppe-runner, licensed out of Corrow until the license was revoked six years ago for smuggling." He set the report down. "You knew him."

It was not a question. Ilya kept his face still. "I arrested him once. Three years ago. Charm-carrying, small amounts. He was released for lack of holding space."

"And he spoke to you."

"He tried to bargain. They always try to bargain."

"With what?"

"Information he didn't have. Names he'd invented. The usual."

Halvane studied him then, and Ilya felt the study like a lamp held close to the skin. The commander was a narrow man, dry as old paper, with eyes the color of dishwater and a habit of stillness that unnerved new apprentices. Ilya had stopped being unnerved by it a year ago. He had almost stopped noticing it at all.

Almost.

"There were letters on the courier's body," Halvane said. "We recovered most of them. Burned some—the fire took the satchel before we got there." A pause, thin as a knife laid flat. "What we recovered confirms he was carrying correspondence between parties in Corrow and parties beyond the Steppe. The map he passed is believed to be part of that correspondence. It bears a cipher seal we have not yet broken."

"You want me on the hunt detail."

"I want you on the market detail today. There will be raids through the smugglers' quarter beginning at noon bell. Your squad takes the tannery row and the charm-stalls east of the well." Halvane finally looked at him fully. "You have a reputation among the traders, Vosk. They trust your face more than most. Use that. Someone sold that courier supplies, or luck-knots, or silence. Find who."

"And if I find them?"

"They come to the Wardenhouse for questioning." Halvane returned his gaze to the papers. "They are witnesses, Vosk. Not criminals. Not yet."

*Not yet.* Ilya saluted, about-faced, and walked out into the corridor where the morning light came through the high windows in long dusty bars, and only when he was three doors down did he allow himself to think the thought that had been circling him since the fourth bell:

*Witnesses. Not yet. And what happens when they are?*

Voss would never have used that phrasing. Voss had drawn lines so bright you could walk them blindfolded. Halvane drew lines in pencil and erased them when convenient.

Ilya told himself it meant nothing. Commanders differed. Men grew cautious or careless. It did not mean the order itself was rotten.

The stone in his chest did not agree.

* * *

The raid went wrong at half past the noon bell, and afterward Ilya could never quite untangle which moment had been the true beginning of the end—whether it was the bleeding runner in the charm-market, or the seal on the map, or the look on the face of the stall-keeper whose luck-knots he'd bought every winter solstice since he was nineteen years old.

His squad swept tannery row clean: two unlicensed charm-sellers, both elderly, both weeping, both marched off gently enough because Ilya insisted on gentle when he could get away with it. Then the market east of the well, stalls shuttering like startled eyes as the grey-and-silver moved through in pairs. Ilya worked his side of the lane with the practiced courtesy that had earned him his reputation—papers shown, questions asked, threats implied but never spoken aloud.

At the third stall from the corner, he found blood on the boards.

Fresh. Still tacky at the edges. A smear leading away toward the alley behind the stalls, and beside it, ground into the grit, the wax stub of a message-seal cracked open, its impression half-legible in the dirty light.

Ilya crouched. He should have called it in immediately—evidence protocol, flag the site, wait for the recorders. His hand was already moving to the whistle at his collar.

Then he saw the seal.

It was pressed deep, the mark of someone who had stamped in haste or pain, but the design was unmistakable to anyone who had ever stood honor-watch outside a commander's door: a mountain split by a sword of light, crowned with seven stars arranged in the old covenant pattern—one for each of the sworn realms, five human and two dragon.

Aldric Voss's personal seal.

On a smuggler's courier. In a smuggler's market. Two years after Voss fell from the Emberwatch cliffs.

Ilya stayed crouched for a long time. Around him the raid roared on—boots, shouts, a woman's voice raised in protest, the wet slap of confiscated charms being thrown into lead-lined sacks. None of it reached him. He was nineteen again, standing night-watch outside Voss's study, hearing the commander argue with someone behind the closed door—arguing about the Steppe, about *the fire*, about a word Ilya hadn't known then and had only later found in the restricted archives: *covenant*. He remembered Voss coming out with his jaw set like a man who has decided to die in a particular direction, and clapping Ilya on the shoulder, and saying, *Keep asking questions, boy. It'll cost you everything, someday. Ask anyway.*

Two months later, Voss fell from the cliffs.

Ilya picked up the wax stub with a hand that was not entirely steady, and slipped it inside his gauntlet instead of the evidence pouch.

* * *

He spent the evening telling himself a dozen reasonable stories.

The seal could be forged. Forgers copied commander's seals all the time—it was practically a cottage industry in the border towns. The courier could have stolen it, bought it, found it in a dead man's effects. Wax survived. Things coincided. The world was full of loose threads that meant nothing, and a good Warden knew how to leave them alone.

But at the second bell of night, unable to sleep, he went down into the records vault where he still had access as an apprentice of senior standing, and pulled the file on Aldric Voss's death.

It was thin. That was the first wrong thing. A commander's death file ran to dozens of pages—witness statements, site surveys, correspondence logs. Voss's ran to nine. The witness statements contradicted each other on basic points: the weather, the hour, whether anyone had seen him climb. The site survey noted scuff marks consistent with *struggle* and then, in a later hand, in different ink, the notation had been amended to read *consistent with misstep*. The amending hand belonged to Chancellor's Office.

And clipped to the back, half-hidden by the cover sheet, a single page in Voss's own cramped script—an inventory, apparently, of documents he intended to deliver somewhere. Ilya read it twice. The last line read: *Seal and cipher-key enclosed for K——, who will know the pattern. If I do not return from the Emberhold, tell her the fire was never theirs to smother.*

*K.*

Kavanagh. The name surfaced from some corner of Ilya's memory—the raid reports, the market interviews, the stall-keeper with the careful hands and the invisible presence, the one the informants said had been seen speaking with Ovid Marsh the day the courier died. Felix Kavanagh. Charm-seller. Unregistered, unremarkable, invisible by every account.

Except one account. One informant, a fence on salt-row, had mentioned—in passing, in the bored tone of a man trading scraps—that the Kavanagh family had died in the Purge of the Breaking. All of them. Except, rumor said, one child nobody could confirm.

One child nobody could confirm. And a dying courier pressing a sealed map into careful hands, a map bearing a cipher the Wardens couldn't break, sealed with the mark of a commander who had died asking questions about fire.

Ilya stood in the cold of the vault with the page in his hands and felt something crack—not loudly, not all at once, but with the deep structural sound of ice on a river in late spring, the kind of sound that means the crossing is no longer safe.

The Prohibition had a catechism. *Magic is a wound. The speaker is the blade.* But Voss had written *the fire was never theirs to smother*, and Voss had been the straightest blade Ilya had ever known, and Voss had fallen from a cliff with struggle-marks on the stone and a chancellor's pen amending the truth.

He thought of the elderly charm-sellers weeping in the lead-line wagon. He thought of Halvane's voice: *witnesses, not yet.* He thought of the Hollow Tide reports he'd filed and filed and filed, the villages beyond the barrier marked *containment acceptable*, the evacuations that only ever seemed to run toward the capital. He thought of ten years of believing, and what the believing had been built on, and who had built it, and why.

*Ask anyway.*

He put the page back exactly as he'd found it, closed the file, shelved it, signed the access log in his own name because forgery suddenly seemed like the smaller sin, and walked out of the vault a deserter in everything but fact.

* * *

By dawn he had made his arrangements with the mechanical calm of a man dismantling his own life: coin gathered, ward-belt left folded on his bunk—if they came for him, let them find the uniform surrendered, not abandoned—his grandmother's iron ring on his finger and nothing else worth taking. At the last, standing in the doorway of the cell he'd occupied for six years, he looked at the grey walls and the narrow window and felt almost nothing, which frightened him more than grief would have.

The gate sentry nodded him through without question. Apprentices came and went. It was the commanders who watched the gates.

Corrow at first light smelled of fish and coal-smoke and the particular sourness of a city pretending yesterday hadn't happened. The smugglers' quarter was shut tight, shutters drawn, the market burned to ash and scraped earth. But salt-row still woke early, and Ilya had spent three years learning its rhythms, and one of those rhythms was a fence named Doss Marlowe who owed debts in every direction and paid them in information.

Doss took one look at him out of uniform and poured him tea without being asked.

"You're either here to arrest me or to disappear," the old man said. "Tea works for both."

"The Kavanagh one. The charm-seller." Ilya wrapped his hands around the cup. "Where are they?"

"Now why would I tell a Warden that?"

"Because I'm not a Warden anymore. As of roughly four hours ago." He turned the cup slowly. "Because the map they're carrying has my dead commander's seal on it, and the file on my dead commander's death was rewritten by the Chancellor's Office, and I need to know if what I'm starting to believe is true before I decide what to do with the rest of my life."

Doss studied him over the rim of his cup for a long, unhurried moment, the way old traders study anything they might buy.

"They went out the salt gate before midnight," he said at last. "Headed east, onto the Steppe, with a thief girl who's been robbing charm-stalls since she had teeth. Word is they're making for the glass country." He refilled Ilya's cup unasked. "That's a killing road, apprentice. Nothing out there but glass and grass and things the Tide has left hungry."

"I know."

"Do you? You've worn a uniform since you were fifteen. You don't know hunger, boy. You don't know what the Steppe does to people who think rules will protect them." Doss leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost kind. "Out there, nobody cares what you believed. Only what you can carry and who stands beside you."

Ilya looked down at the tea, at his own reflection warped in its dark surface: a young man in plain clothes with a Warden's posture and a traitor's certainty, holding the wax impression of a dead man's seal in his pocket like a relic.

"Then I'd better find out who stands beside me," he said.

* * *

The salt gate was a scar in the city wall, and beyond it the Glass Steppe began without ceremony—one moment cobbles, the next a plain of fused sand stretching to the horizon, pale gold and blinding in the climbing sun, glittering as though the whole world had been vitrified by a single breath of fire. Which, Iyla supposed, remembering the histories, it had.

He walked east. He had no plan beyond the walking: follow the salt-road until it forked, take the fork the caravans avoided, watch for sign of three travelers moving fast and traveling light. He was a tracker by training and a believer by habit, and even with the belief cracked through, the tracking still held. Tracking was honest work. Tracking asked only that you pay attention.

He found their camp the second evening, in the lee of a shattered waystone, smokeless fire, three bedrolls, and a girl with a knife already in her hand sitting up in the dark like she'd been born mid-alertness.

"Don't move," she said.

Behind her, two figures rose out of the shadows—one tall and careful, one slighter, hooded, and when the taller one stepped forward into the starlight with a hand resting on the pommel of an old sword, Ilya saw the family resemblance to a name in a ten-year-old casualty list and understood that everything he had done in the last two days was correct.

"I'm not here to arrest anyone," he said. He knelt slowly, deliberately, and placed the wax seal on the ground between them, and pushed it across the glass with two fingers. "My name is Ilya Vosk. I was Aldric Voss's apprentice. I believe the Wardens killed him, and I believe your map is the reason why—and I'd like to help you carry it."

The girl's knife did not waver. The hooded figure had gone very still. But the tall one—Felix Kavanagh, charm-seller, last of a murdered family—only looked at the seal for a long moment, and then at Ilya, with eyes that had clearly already weighed a hundred strangers and found them wanting.

"That's a bold story," Felix said quietly. "Give me one reason not to gut you where you kneel."

Ilya reached up, unpinned the prohibition-ward from his belt—he had kept that much, the weight of it, to surrender rather than abandon—and laid it on the glass beside the seal.

"Because I just gave up everything I had," he said, "to kneel on frozen sand in front of three strangers and beg. And because out here—" he echoed the old fence, and heard how true it sounded— "nobody cares what I believed. Only what I can carry, and who stands beside me. Let me stand beside you."

The Steppe wind moved over them, carrying the faint mineral smell of ancient fire, and far to the east—so far it might have been imagination—something on the horizon flickered, once, like a heartbeat.

Like a beacon, guttering.
