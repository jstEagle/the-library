## 7. Glass and Grass

The Glass Steppe did not begin all at once. That was the first thing that surprised Felix.

They had walked out of the salt hills for two days, through scrub country where the wind combed dry grass into silver waves, and Felix had almost convinced themselves the stories were exaggerated—that *glass* was just a poet's word for hardpan and glittering stone, the kind of flourish mapmakers loved because it cost them nothing.

Then they crested a low rise on the morning of the third day, and the world turned to light.

The plain ahead stretched to the horizon like a frozen sea. Not sand, not rock—glass, true glass, fused smooth and pale green where the sun caught it, deep blue in the hollows, threaded with veins of amber that glowed faintly even in daylight, as if the ground itself were holding embers under its skin. The grass grew in the seams: stubborn tufts of steppe-grass pushing up through fractures in the fused surface, bending together in the wind with a sound like whispering.

Felix stopped walking without deciding to.

"First time?" Wren said, coming up beside them. She had her hood back, her dark hair whipping loose, and she was grinning the way she only grinned at other people's misfortune or wonder—usually both at once. "Everyone does that. The standing-still-and-forgetting-to-breathe thing."

"It's—" Felix began, and found no word adequate, which was an unfamiliar and unpleasant sensation. "It's beautiful."

"It's a graveyard," Wren said cheerfully, and started down the slope. "Beautiful graveyard. Keep up. The sun off the flat will cook your eyes raw if you squint at it too long."

Felix followed, and Ilya brought up the rear, his Warden grey traded two days ago for a drover's coat that hung wrong on him, making him look less like a fugitive and more like a man wearing someone else's life—which, Felix supposed, was exactly what he was.

* * *

By midday, Felix had learned the first lesson of the glass: it lied about distance.

The plains were so flat and so clear that a ridge three miles off looked close enough to touch, and the heat rising off the surface bent everything above it into shimmer. Wren navigated by none of these things. She navigated by the grass.

"The old growth follows the water veins," she explained, crouching to tap a seam of amber where a tuft of grass grew thick and dark green. "See how it's greener here? There's a seep under this stretch, maybe six feet down. Where the grass thins out yellow, the glass is getting thin underneath—you don't camp there. And where there's no grass at all—" she pointed to a patch of black glass ahead, glossy and bare, "—you go around. Wide."

"Why?"

Wren glanced at Ilya, then back at Felix, and something in her expression shifted—the thief's swagger settling into something older and more careful. "Because the Tide came through here once. Maybe not the big Tide, maybe just a finger of it, reaching out when the barrier was weak. Grass doesn't grow where the Hollow touched. And the glass there..." She hesitated. "It drinks warmth. You sleep on it, you wake up cold in a way that doesn't stop."

Felix looked at the black patch again. Up close, he could see that the glass wasn't smooth there—it was pitted, scarred, as though something had gnawed at it.

"You know the Steppe well," Ilya said quietly. It was not quite an accusation. He'd been circling that question for two days, polite as a cat around a bowl.

"I told you. Born beside it." Wren stood and brushed off her knees. "Camp settlements ringed the edge, before the Prohibition made refugees of everyone who couldn't pay the relocation fees. My mother's people ran the waystations along the southern road." She said it lightly, but her jaw was set, and she didn't elaborate, and Felix—who knew better than anyone what it meant to have a past you carried like broken crockery—didn't ask.

Instead Felix asked, "How do you find water when the grass fails?"

That earned him a real look—surprise, quickly hidden. Wren recovered fast. "You listen," she said. "At dusk. The glass carries sound along the seams. Put your ear to it where two amber veins cross, and if there's water within half a mile, you'll hear it moving. Like a whisper under a door." She smirked. "Course, you could also just steal a water-finder's charm off some rich pilgrim, but those are rare as honest Wardens now."

"They were never common," Ilya murmured, and Wren snorted, and Felix felt something loosen in their chest that they hadn't realized was tight.

They walked on. The glass sang faintly underfoot—a high, glassy chime at each footfall, millions of footsteps' worth of music scattered across the plain—and Felix thought, not for the first time, that the Steppe remembered. That was the old belief, the one from before: that when the dragons burned the covenant into the world, the fire had fused the sand into glass so the promise would never wash away. *Written in the floor of the world.* Every vein of amber was a line of the oath. Every chime underfoot was a syllable of it, still speaking, ten years after the speakers were outlawed.

Felix's oath-sword rode quiet against their back. It had been quiet for ten years. But out here, on the glass, Felix thought they could feel it listening.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a shattered waystation—an old dragon-road marker house, its stone walls cracked but standing, its roof long gone. The stars over the Steppe were ferocious, closer than stars had any right to be, and no moon. Wren built the fire small, in a dug pit lined with broken glass shards that caught the light and threw it upward in little constellations, and cooked steppe-hen she'd snared that afternoon with a skill that bordered on insulting.

"Where did you learn the snares?" Felix asked.

"Same place I learned everything." Wren turned the bird on its stick. "Being small and hungry teaches you faster than any school. You watch what survives and you copy it." She glanced sideways at Felix, firelight catching the sharp planes of her face. "You've never camped a day in your life, have you? City-charmer. Luck-knots and fever charms. You walk like the ground owes you money."

Felix opened their mouth to object and closed it again. They had, in fact, walked into a seam-crack twice today, drunk from a seep Wren found while Felix stood uselessly holding the waterskins, and built a fire pit that smoked like a chimney until Wren rebuilt it.

"I'm learning," they said with dignity.

"You're dying slowly and I'm enjoying it." But Wren passed them the first cut of the hen, which was not what a person did with someone they were merely tolerating, and Felix ate it and let the silence be friendly.

Ilya sat apart, as he usually did, mending a strap on his pack with careful soldier's hands. After a while he said, without looking up, "Tomorrow we cross the Searoad flats. Open glass for a full day, no cover. If the Wardens are running sweep patrols along the old road, we'll be visible for miles."

"So we don't take the old road," Wren said.

"The old road is the only route with water at both ends."

"Then we take it at night and sleep through the day under shade-cloaks. Which means," she added, pointing her stick at Felix, "our charming companion has to learn to sleep on glass without freezing solid, and you—" the stick swung to Ilya— "have to stop looking at the horizon like it's going to apologize."

Ilya's mouth twitched. It was the closest thing to humor Felix had seen from him since he'd deserted; it suited him badly and entirely.

Sleep came hard for Felix anyway. They lay on their bedroll with the glass chiming faintly beneath them whenever the wind moved the grass, staring up at stars, thinking about Sera's cipher, and Ovid's scorched map, and the name whispered by a dying man that had cracked ten years of careful silence open like an egg. *Sera lives.* Hope was a splinter under the skin. You couldn't dig it out. You could only wait to see what it festered into.

"Kavanagh." Wren's voice, low, from the far side of the fire. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The lying-there-being-loud-about-it thing. Your thoughts have a sound. It's awful." A pause. "Can't sleep either?"

"No."

Wren rolled onto her side. In the ember-light her eyes were wary in a new way—not sizing him up as a mark anymore, but weighing something. "Ask you something?"

"You'll ask regardless."

"True." She fed a twig to the fire. "Back in Corrow. The fever charms you sold. The luck-knots. I bought one off you once—did you know that? Two years ago. Winter market. Cost me a whole copper I'd stolen off a fishmonger." Her voice stayed light, but her hands didn't; they were very still. "It worked. Not luck, exactly, but—I slept that night. First time in weeks. Just... slept, warm, like something was sitting between me and the dark." She looked up. "That was magic, wasn't it? Real magic. And you sold it to me for a copper."

Felix was quiet for a moment. "It was a cantrip," they said finally. "A warming-ward, woven into the knot. The simplest thing I know." The simplest thing they were *allowed* to know, now—the last scrap of a craft they'd spent a decade burying alive inside themselves. "It shouldn't have worked that well. You must have been cold enough to meet it halfway."

"I was always cold enough." Wren stared into the fire. "Here's the thing I can't get straight in my head. The Wardens say magic is what broke the world. What called the Tide. Everyone says it. You say it in your sleep, practically—it's in the prayers they make us say in the camps. But you're *made* of the stuff, aren't you? Charms and knots and whatever's humming in that sword of yours. And I've known you"—she counted—"eleven days. And in eleven days you haven't broken anything except my opinion of city people. So either you're the exception—" her eyes came up, sharp— "or the story's a lie."

The wind moved the grass. Underneath them, the glass sang one long soft note, like a held breath.

"It's a lie," Felix said.

Saying it aloud felt like stepping off a ledge. Ten years of silence, and it took four words, spoken to a starving thief by a dead fire in the middle of the world.

Wren didn't gloat. Felix had expected gloating. Instead she went still, the way she went still watching a lock, absorbing every detail. "Tell me," she said.

"Not tonight." Felix sat up, cross-legged, heart hammering in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with the strangeness of choosing to open a hand they'd kept closed for a decade. "Tonight I'll show you something instead. If you want."

"What kind of something?"

"A cantrip. Like your luck-knot." Felix held out one palm. "The warming-ward. It takes about a minute to learn the shape of it. Less to feel it. Whether you can ever *do* it is another matter—but feeling it is enough, for tonight."

Wren looked at the offered hand like it might bite. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, startled sound. "You're serious. You're going to teach *magic*. To me. Out here. Where any Warden within fifty miles would—" She stopped. Looked at Ilya, asleep-or-feigning-it by the wall. Looked back. "You trust me not to sell you."

"I trust you not to sell me," Felix said, "because you already know what the Wardens do to people who know things. And because you slept warm one night two winters ago and you've been chasing that night ever since. I recognize the look. I invented the look."

Something moved through Wren's face—too quick to name, gone before it landed. She shifted forward across the glass until she knelt opposite Felix, knees nearly touching, and held out her hand palm-up, mimicking his. It was, Felix thought, the most vulnerable posture he'd ever seen her take, and she wore it like a knife she wasn't sure how to hold.

"Now what?"

"Now you stop performing and breathe."

"I am breathing."

"Like a sparrow in a snare. Slower. From here." Felix touched their own sternum. "The warming-ward isn't a spell you say. It's a shape you hold. Everything I ever wove started the same way: you find the cold place—in yourself, or in front of you—and you *cup* it. Like water. Like you're going to carry it somewhere warmer without spilling."

He reached for the craft the way you reach for a language you dreamed in as a child, terrified it would be gone. It was not gone. It rose in him like water finding an old channel: the sense of the cold gathered in his cupped hands, the air between his palms thickening, warming, taking on weight. Faint gold light kindled in the space between his fingers—barely a coal's worth, barely anything, and yet it lit both their faces, and Wren forgot entirely to perform.

"Oh," she breathed.

"Don't grab at it," Felix said softly. "It's not a purse. Hold your hands near mine. Feel where the warmth sits. It pools in the low places—that's the whole secret. Magic isn't force. It's *shelter*. Something cold comes to it and it holds the cold gently until the cold remembers it used to be warm."

"That's not what they taught us," Wren whispered. Her hands hovered over his, trembling slightly, and Felix couldn't tell if it was fear or hunger or both braided together. "In the camp lessons. Magic is hunger. Magic takes."

"Magic is what the hungry made it look like," Felix said, "after they took everything else from the people who had it." He turned his hands, letting the little gold warmth spill upward into hers—deliberately, freely, the first time in ten years he had given the craft away instead of hiding it in knots and calling it craftwork. The warmth settled into Wren's palms like a bird landing.

She made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost nothing else at all. Tears stood bright on her lashes in the gold light, and she didn't wipe them away, and she didn't pretend they weren't there, and Felix understood that this was the bravest thing he'd ever watched anyone do.

"It doesn't hurt," she said. "Why doesn't it hurt? They said it burns you. They said—"

"Whoever told you that wanted you afraid of it. Fear keeps hands in pockets." Felix let the ward fade, gently, the way you bank a fire. "Fear is cheap. It's the cheapest tool there is. That's why they reach for it first."

Across the dead fire, Ilya stirred. Felix tensed—but Ilya only turned over, and whether he had heard, Felix couldn't say, and decided not to care. Some truths stopped being dangerous the moment they were shared. Others became bearable for the same reason. He was done sorting which was which.

Wren flexed her fingers, staring at her own empty palms as though the warmth might still be visible there. "Teach me the rest," she said. Not begging. Stating terms, the way she stated everything—but her voice cracked in the middle of it, and the crack undid her completely.

"One thing at a time," Felix said. "Tomorrow: shade-cloaks, night-walking, and how not to die on the Searoad flats. The next night, another knot. We've got a long way to go, and the Steppe's a better classroom than Corrow ever was." He paused. "And Wren? When we find whoever's at the end of this map—when we find out what happened ten years ago—there's a place in that reckoning for everyone the lie was told to. That includes you."

Wren wiped her face briskly with her sleeve, businesslike, rebuilding herself piece by piece. "Sentimental city-charmer," she muttered. But she lay down facing him across the ashes, and her breathing, when it finally slowed toward sleep, was the easy breathing of someone warm.

Felix lay back and listened to the glass sing beneath them, all those buried syllables of the old covenant still speaking their patience into the dark. Somewhere east, under the horizon, the Emberhold's fire was guttering. Somewhere behind them, the Wardens were sharpening their fear. But here, in the wreck of a waystation, on hostile ground that remembered better days, something small had taken root: two strangers by a dead fire, sharing warmth on purpose.

He was almost asleep when Wren spoke again, drowsy and slurred:

"Kavanagh. The grass. It's pulling back from the east seam."

Felix opened his eyes. Along the eastern edge of their shelter, where the amber veins ran thickest, the tufts of steppe-grass were bending—away from the horizon, all together, like a crowd flinching from something coming.

The glass under Felix's palm had gone cold.
