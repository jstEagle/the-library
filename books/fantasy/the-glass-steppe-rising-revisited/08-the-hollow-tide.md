## 8. The Hollow Tide

Ilya had been taught that the Steppe was empty.

It was one of the first lessons at the Warden academy, delivered by a grey-bearded instructor with a pointer and a wall-map: *Beyond the salt line lies the Glass Steppe—uninhabited, uninhabitable, a scar left by the dragons' treachery. No settlement is sanctioned. No settlement survives.* The lesson came with diagrams of heat shimmer and cracked glass plains, with casualty figures from the first expeditions, and with the quiet implication that whatever lived out there deserved its emptiness. The Steppe was the price of the dragons' betrayal, and emptiness was simply what justice looked like from a distance.

Three days out from the salt gate, walking behind Felix Kavanagh and the thief called Wren, Ilya was beginning to understand that he had been taught a great many things that were simply lies wearing uniforms.

The Steppe was not empty.

He saw it in the small things first. A cairn of stacked glass shards beside the trail, deliberate as a signature. A windbreak built from the ribs of some enormous beast, lashed with wire gone green with age. A child's toy—a horse carved from steppe-glass, its mane a row of sharp translucent spikes—lying half-buried where someone had dropped it, or set it down, or been made to let go of it. Each time, Wren noticed before he did. She would slow, touch the object or almost touch it, and say nothing, and her silence had a texture he was learning to read: not indifference but recognition.

"People live here," he said on the third evening, as they made camp in the lee of a fused dune that gleamed dull bronze in the failing light.

Felix didn't look up from the charm they were mending—a fever-knot, the thread frayed from too much handling. "People have always lived here."

"The Wardens' maps show nothing."

"Then the Wardens' maps are doing what maps do for the people who pay for them." Felix bit the thread clean and held the knot up against the last of the sun. It caught fire-colored light and threw it back, and for a moment Ilya thought of the beacon-frescoes in the academy chapel, the old covenant rendered in gold leaf: dragon-fire given freely, human oaths given freely, two hands clasped over a burning heart. He had knelt under that fresco every morning for six years. He was only now noticing that in every rendering he'd ever seen, the human hand wore a chancellor's ring.

Wren crouched by the cookfire, feeding it dried dung-cakes she'd gathered without being told, with the unselfconscious competence of someone raised where fuel didn't grow on trees. "There's a village ahead," she said. "Half a day. Glassmelters—they trade with the fringe camps. My mother sold them salt." Her voice did something complicated on the word *mother* and flattened itself again. "If they're still there."

"If," Felix said gently.

Ilya said nothing. But that night, lying awake under stars so dense they seemed less like lights than like weather, he found himself praying—not the formal prayers of the Order, which were mostly oath-adjacent recitations the Prohibition had hollowed into nonsense, but the raw kind, the kind you weren't supposed to need if your faith was sound. *Let them be there. Let them be alive. Let there be one thing in this whole rotten architecture that isn't a lie.*

He would think about that prayer later, and about how precisely it had been answered, and in what currency.

* * *

They crested the ridge an hour past dawn, and the world ended.

Ilya had seen the Hollow once before, from the walls of Corrow, on the night they drilled the evacuation protocols into the second-years. It had been far away then, a smudge of wrongness on the northern horizon, like a hole cut in a painting of the sky. His instructor had stood beside him and said, *That is what we hold back. That is why the Prohibition exists. Every charm you confiscate, every speaker you take off the streets, keeps that wall standing.* And young Ilya, seventeen and certain, had believed it the way you believe in gravity.

The thing below him now was not a smudge.

It was a tide in truth—a slow, rolling front of absolute absence, maybe half a league across, advancing over the glass plain toward the village of the glassmelters with the patience of something that had never once needed to hurry. Where it passed, the ground did not break or burn. It simply stopped. Color drained out of the glass like blood from a face; the land went grey, then went *less than grey*, then went nothing at all—not darkness, because darkness was still something, still had edges and depth, but a smooth and total negation that his eyes slid off the way fingers slide off ice. Above it, even the light bent wrong. Even the wind died a hundred yards short of it, as if the air itself refused to touch it.

And the village lay directly in its path.

"No," Wren breathed beside him. She had frozen mid-step, one hand out toward the valley, and her whole body had gone taut as a snare-wire. "No, no—it wasn't supposed to reach this far south, it's supposed to be *north*, everyone says—"

"How far?" Felix asked. Their voice was flat, controlled, and Ilya heard the cost of that control in every syllable. "How long does the village have?"

"I don't—" Wren's voice cracked. "I don't know. It moves different speeds. Sometimes it sits for months. My camp—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "We moved twice when I was small. You pack everything you own and you run until the scouts say the edge has stalled. Usually you get days."

Below, the village was waking. Even at this distance Ilya could see smoke rising from cooking fires, could see the tiny moving specks of people hauling water, opening kiln-sheds, living their morning. Someone was leading goats down toward a well. Someone else had hung laundry—white shirts, a red blanket—on a line strung between two glass-poled houses, and the red blanket snapped cheerfully in the wind that would soon stop existing.

"We have to warn them." Ilya was already moving down the slope before he finished saying it, gravel skidding under his boots. "Now. Right now. If we run—"

"And say what?" Felix caught his arm. Their grip was iron. "Look at me, Ilya. Say what?"

"That the Tide is coming! That they have to evacuate!"

"With what authority? With what route? With wagons they don't have and roads that don't exist?" Felix's eyes were terrible—not cold, but burning with something colder than anger. "You've served the Order six years. Tell me the protocol for a civilian Tide-warning outside the capital. Tell me it exists."

Ilya opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the answer rose in him unbidden, in his instructor's voice, in the exact cadence of the classroom: *Tide-response assets are concentrated per the Continuity Doctrine. Civilian evacuation prioritization follows administrative tier: capital first, garrison towns second, unsanctioned settlements are not designated.* Unsanctioned settlements are not designated. He had copied those words into his notes at nineteen, in neat academy script, and had never once asked himself what they meant when you subtracted the neatness.

"They know," he said. His voice came out strange, thin. "Felix—the Wardens know. The Continuity Doctrine—it's not a secret document, it's core curriculum, I just never—" His knees felt unreliable. He sat down hard on the glass scree without deciding to. "The Order has known for years the barrier was failing. We trained for it. There are Tide-lure units, evacuation corridors, grain reserves sealed under the Citadel. All of it for Corrow. For the capital. The provinces get a garrison warning if there's time. And the Steppe settlements—" He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "The Steppe settlements aren't on any map. Officially there's no one out here to save. Officially the Steppe is empty."

Below them, faint on the wind, the village bell began to ring. Not the alarm pattern—he knew alarm patterns, they'd drummed them into every recruit—but the morning call, the one that said *come to the well, come to the kilns, the day is starting*. Somewhere a dog barked. The red blanket snapped and snapped.

"They're not going to run in time," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She had gone very still, the way she went still before a pickpocketing, all her wildness compressed into a single point of focus. "Even if we scream ourselves dead running down there. Where would they even go?"

"There's a corridor," Ilya whispered. "A marked corridor east of the salt line. If they reached it—if they had carts—" He heard himself, heard the pathetic arithmetic of it, and hated every word. Half a league of devouring nothing. A village of glassmelters with kilns and goats and laundry on lines. A corridor three days' walk away through terrain they'd never survive at speed.

Felix stood looking down at the valley, and Ilya watched them make a decision he couldn't see the shape of. Then Felix said, quietly, "Show me exactly how fast it moves. Watch a landmark. Count."

So they counted. Felix timed it against their own pulse, an oath-keeper's habit, and Wren picked a shattered spire of glass at the Tide's near edge and tracked it against a crack in the ridge. The numbers, when Felix spoke them, were worse than hope: the front was advancing perhaps forty paces in a hundred heartbeats, and accelerating as the ground beneath it grew flatter.

"Two hours," Felix said. "Maybe three. They can outrun it if they start now, if they go southeast, if—" They stopped themselves. Looked at Ilya with an expression he would carry for the rest of his life, whatever the rest of his life turned out to be. "Go down. Tell them. Don't tell them what you are. Just tell them what you saw."

Ilya ran.

* * *

He had given warnings before. As a Warden apprentice he'd stood in market squares announcing confiscation schedules, curfew changes, the quarterly reminder that harboring charms was punishable by branding. He knew how to make a voice carry. None of it prepared him for the glassmelter village, for the smell of woodsmoke and hot sand and frying onions, for the woman at the well who listened to his shouting with her hands still wet, for the way the whole place turned to look at him—the stranger, the armed stranger, the man in road-scuffed leathers with a Warden's training in his stance—and waited for him to make sense.

"Tide," he gasped. "The Hollow Tide. It's crossed the deep glass—it's coming from the northwest—you can see it from the ridge—it will be here in two hours, maybe less."

An old man with kiln-scarred hands squinted northwest and spat. "Scout said the edge stalled at the Black Fords till thaw."

"It hasn't stalled. I'm looking at it right now. Please—" He grabbed the man's forearm, felt the old scars there, the glass-burns of a lifetime's honest work, and something in his chest tore along a seam he hadn't known he had. "Please believe me. Get your people southeast. Leave everything. Everything."

For one bright moment, they believed him. He watched belief move through the village like fire through dry grass—the woman at the well dropping her bucket, the goat-boy turning to run, doors opening, voices rising, a child's cry cutting through the morning noise. People began to move. Not well, not in order, but they *moved*, harnessing the two carts the village owned, throwing kiln-tools and salt-sacks and a screaming cat aboard, an old grandmother arguing fiercely that the household icons must come before the water barrels.

And then the man came out of the longhouse with the Warden seal on his collar.

Ilya saw him and his stomach dropped through the floor of the world. A census-warden—an old one, half-retired, the sort they stationed at the edge of nowhere to count heads and collect the loyalty-tithe. The man looked at Ilya's bearing, at the fugitives visible on the ridge above, at the panic spreading through his charge, and did the math the Order had taught him.

"You're no runner," the census-warden said loudly, over the din. "You're Order-trained. And those two on the hill—" His voice hardened into the register Ilya had heard a thousand times, the voice that made proclamations. "These people have evacuation doctrine. There is no corridor within a week's march. There is no sanction for movement during survey season. This is panic-mongering, likely by fugitives, and it will kill more people than the Tide ever—"

"The Tide is *right there*." Ilya's voice broke across the square like a whip. He pointed, and a ripple of villagers turned, and from the western rise—for the village sat low, in a shallow bowl of glass—they could see it: the grey absence crawling down the far slope, patient as winter, eating the horizon.

The square went silent. Then it went mad.

What happened next took eleven minutes—Ilya counted, because counting was the only thing keeping him sane—and he would relive all eleven minutes for years. The census-warden shouting doctrine while his own people ignored him. Carts overloaded until axles screamed. A family refusing to leave a grandfather too frail to walk, until Ilya lifted the old man bodily onto a cart and the family wept and followed. The goat-boy sobbing because the goats couldn't all come. Wren appearing out of nowhere—he never learned how she'd covered the distance so fast—seizing a lost toddler by the collar one-handed and sprinting. Felix at the southeastern edge of the bowl, finding the path, calling directions in a voice that carried like a war-horn.

And Ilya himself, running house to house, kicking open doors, dragging out the ones who'd frozen—the old, the stubborn, the ones who'd decided to die with their kilns because the kilns were all they had. In the last house he found a young mother hiding under a bed with her baby, both of them shaking, because hiding from danger was the oldest animal wisdom and no one had ever taught her that the Tide could not be hidden from. He got them out. He got nearly all of them out.

Nearly.

The Tide reached the village as the last cart cleared the eastern rim. Ilya, looking back from the ridge, watched it happen with the clinical horror of a man reading his own autopsy. The grey absence rolled into the bowl and touched the first house, and the house did not fall—it *unbecame*, color and shadow and substance draining away into that smooth nothing, and then the well, and then the longhouse with its council benches where the census-warden still stood, alone, refusing to run, holding his seal up before the advancing void like a man defending a door that no longer had a wall around it. The laundry line went. The red blanket vanished mid-flutter. The bell rang once more, though afterward Ilya could never say whether he'd truly heard it or whether his mind had supplied the sound the way a wound supplies pain.

Then the village was a smooth grey bowl of nothing, and the Tide rolled on after the carts, slower now, climbing, and the people of the glassmelters ran southeast across the glass with everything they had ever owned reduced to what two carts could carry, and Ilya ran among them because stopping was unthinkable.

* * *

They lost it at dusk. The Tide slowed where the glass grew rough, and the carts reached broken terrain the void-front couldn't cross quickly, and by full dark the survivors were scattered across three miles of steppe, huddled around fires they barely had fuel for, counting themselves and their children and coming out even, except for the census-warden, except for old Marta who'd gone back for her icons, except for the village itself, except for everything.

Ilya sat apart from the survivors, because he could not bear their gratitude. An old glassmelter woman had pressed a shard of colored glass into his hand—a kiln-reject, blue-green, worthless—and said, *You came. Nobody comes.* He was holding it now, turning it over, and it caught the firelight and threw a small blue coin of brightness onto his knee, and he wanted to smash it and couldn't.

Felix sat down beside him without asking. For a long time neither of them spoke. Behind them, the refugees murmured; Wren moved among the fires with her pockets full, and Ilya realized distantly that she was giving things away—dried meat, a needle, a knife—everything she'd stolen and hoarded for survival, handed out to strangers with a fierce scowl that dared anyone to thank her.

"The Doctrine," Felix said at last. Not a question.

"Continuity Doctrine," Ilya said. "Written eleven years ago, after the breaking. Capital-first evacuation. Sealed reserves. Tide-lure research—weapons, Felix. They've been building weapons that herd the Tide." He heard his own voice shake and let it. "I thought it was contingency planning. Prudence. I memorized it. I *praised* it in my third-year examinations." The blue coin of light trembled on his knee. "They knew the barrier was dying. They've known for years. And they chose—who to save. They drew a circle around the Citadel and everything outside it became—" He gestured back toward the west, toward the smooth grey bowl where a village had been, where a red blanket had snapped on a line in the morning wind. "That. Became that. Not on purpose. Just by arithmetic. Just by deciding some lives were real enough to map and others weren't."

"And the Prohibition?" Felix asked softly. "Where does that sit in your arithmetic now?"

Ilya looked at his hands. Six years of confiscations. Six years of taking luck-knots and fever charms from frightened people in markets, six years of escorting speakers to assessment, six years of believing that every charm seized was a brick in the wall that kept the Tide out. And now he had seen the wall's true shape from the inside: not a barrier but a *ledger*, maintained by men who had starved the fire that fed it and were saving themselves with the change.

"The chancellors broke the covenant," he said. He didn't know how he knew it—only that the day's horror had burned away everything soft in him and left the load-bearing truth exposed, plain as bone. "Ten years ago. Something happened at the Emberhold that they blamed on the dragons, and they used it to outlaw magic, and they've been using the fear ever since. And now the fire's guttering, and the Tide is eating villages, and the Order's answer is a doctrine with the word *continuity* in it." He looked at Felix, and found his eyes were wet and did not care. "I swore an oath to protect the realms from the Hollow. All of the realms. I meant it. I still mean it. Which means I can't be a Warden anymore, because they've decided most of the realms are acceptable losses."

Felix studied him for a long moment. Whatever they saw in his face seemed to settle something. "Ovid thought so too," they said quietly. "The courier. He was Order-trained once—I found the marks on his effects. He left, same as you're leaving. Same reasons, I think." They paused. "There are letters, Ilya. In Ovid's pack. Letters I haven't been able to make myself open since the gate. I think tomorrow I will. And I think you should be there when I do."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only one of us who knows how the Order thinks. And because—" Felix looked west, where the Horizon glowed faintly with that wrong, sourceless light, the Tide settling over the ruins of the world like a lid. "Because whoever wrote those letters knew my sister survived the breaking. And if Sera is alive, then someone out there has been fighting the lie for ten years. And I want a witness who used to wear the uniform when I find out what she knew."

Far off in the dark, a refugee child began to sing—a thin, wavering steppe-lullaby, the kind sung to children born beside the glass—and around the fires, exhausted voices joined it one by one, the survivors of the glassmelters singing their village into memory.

Ilya closed his fist around the blue shard and made his choice complete.
