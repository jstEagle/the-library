## 9. Ovid's Letters

They buried Ovid at dawn, in the manner of the Steppe-runners—or as close to it as Felix could manage with a shovel borrowed from the safehouse's absent owner and a patch of hardpan behind the ruined dovecote.

There had been no time for ceremony on the road. The three of them had run through the dark hours with the Hollow Tide's cold breathing somewhere behind them and the Wardens' lantern-signals stitching the hills ahead, and only when the grey light came did they dare stop. Now Ilya stood at the head of the grave with his Warden coat turned inside out, so that the grey lining faced the sky instead of the silver badge, and Wren had laid a stolen copper charm—*one of mine*, Felix realized, one of the luck-knots she'd lifted from the market stall a lifetime ago—on the mound of turned earth.

"He carried me two miles," Wren said. "When his horse went down. He didn't complain once. I kept waiting for him to complain." Her voice was rough. She kicked at the dirt, angry at it. "People don't do that."

"People like him do," Felix said.

It wasn't much of an epitaph. But Ovid had been a man who moved quietly through a loud world, and perhaps quiet words suited him best.

Felix knelt a last moment and pressed their palm flat against the earth, the way their mother used to press her palm against Felix's forehead when fever burned—*there, now, I have you.* The ground was cold. It would warm by noon. That was all the mercy the Steppe offered anyone.

Then they went back inside, because the dead could wait and the living could not, and there were things in Ovid's saddlebags that needed reading before the light got good enough for Wardens to see smoke from a chimney.

* * *

The safehouse was a smuggler's cache dressed up as a shepherd's hut: one room, one window shuttered with a slate, a hearth that drew badly, and walls lined with false bricks behind which the real owner—some fence named Marrow whom Wren swore she'd met twice and trusted once—kept goods too hot to sell in Corrow. There was dried mutton, a jar of pickled something-or-other, a crate of cheap wine, and, folded into a false brick behind the hearth, a packet of letters tied with waxed cord.

They weren't Marrow's. They were addressed in a careful, slanted hand to *O. Vess, runner, third bell post*, and every one of them bore a small burnt-orange seal stamped with a shape Felix knew better than their own heartbeat.

A knotwork loop. A circle closed over a flame.

Sera's mark. The cipher-sigil she'd invented at eleven years old, doodled in the margins of her schoolbooks, carved into the doorframe of the house that no longer existed, stamped into the wax of every letter she'd ever sent home from her first year of covenant-service at the Emberhold.

Felix stood holding the packet for a long time without breathing.

"Felix?" Ilya said, from the doorway. He'd been checking the sightlines, because he was still half-Warden even with his coat inside out, and old habits made him useful. "What is it?"

"Letters," Felix managed. "From my sister."

Wren looked up sharply from where she'd been picking the lock on the wine crate out of pure habit. "The dead one?"

"The one we thought was dead," Felix said, and sat down heavily on a stool, and broke the cord.

* * *

There were nine letters. The earliest was dated four days after the Breaking—the night the sky over the Emberhold had gone black, the night the Proclamation had come down from the Chancellors' hall naming dragonkind traitor, the night Felix's family had died in a fire whose origin no one ever satisfactorily explained, though everyone understood without saying that *Wardens investigating covenant-loyalists* explained it well enough.

Felix read the first letter standing up. By the third they were sitting. By the sixth they had stopped noticing the cold, or the others, or anything but the slanted hand marching across the page like a woman walking fast through dangerous country.

*Ovi—*

*I don't know if this will reach you or if you'll believe it if it does. Everything they're saying is a lie. I was there. I stood in the covenant-hall when the Chancellors sealed the doors and turned the rite backward. The dragons didn't break faith—they came to renew it, and the Chancellors feared what renewal meant: that power would be shared, truly shared, and their seats would be worth less than a dragon's shadow.*

*I ran. I'm not proud of how. There are others who ran with me. We call ourselves loyalists—not to the old order, but to the oath itself. The fire at the Emberhold still burns. It's small and it's hungry and it needs feeding, and I need people who can move unseen between here and the cities. You always said you could carry anything across anything. Prove it.*

*Burn this.*

*S.*

Felix read it twice. Then a third time, mouthing the words, as if the second and third readings might reveal them as forgery, as grief-dream, as anything other than what they plainly were.

"She was there," Felix said aloud. Their voice sounded strange, scraped thin. "She was *in the hall*. She saw it happen."

Ilya had come to stand behind them, reading over their shoulder with the particular stillness he adopted around painful things. "That matches what my commander believed," he said quietly. "Before he died. Before—" He stopped himself. His hand went briefly to the map in Felix's satchel, to the scorched edge where the commander's seal had been. "He spent his last years trying to prove exactly this."

"And they killed him for it," Wren said. Not unkindly. Just flatly, the way she stated facts about weather and prices and which fences would cheat you. "And they killed Felix's family for it. And they've been hunting anyone who says otherwise for ten years." She pried the wine crate open at last and pulled out a bottle, then thought better of it and set it down unopened, which for Wren was roughly equivalent to a funeral observance. "So the whole world's been living inside a lie with a Warden standing on every corner to keep the lid on."

"The whole world," Ilya agreed, "or enough of it."

Felix wasn't listening anymore. They were opening the second letter.

* * *

The letters traced a decade the way a river traces a canyon—slowly, invisibly, cutting deeper every year.

The second letter, six months after the Breaking: Sera alive in the high Steppe, learning the dragon-roads, learning which of the scattered drakes still answered to the old names. The third: word of a safehouse network, runners and hedge-witches and one disgraced Chancellor's clerk, all keeping the ember of the covenant from going out entirely. The fourth and fifth: harder years. Purges. The Wardens' lists growing longer. Two loyalist names struck through in Sera's hand, mourned in a single line each—*we buried Tomas under the white stones; he laughed at the end, which was like him.*

The sixth letter changed its weather.

*Ovi—*

*The fire is failing. I don't know how to write this except plainly, so: the beacon at the Emberhold has dimmed by half since midwinter, and the Hollow presses harder at the barrier every week. We thought the fire fed on the land itself, on the old dragon-magic sunk into the mountain. It doesn't. Or it doesn't only.*

*It feeds on oaths. On spoken, freely-given, bound-in-blood oath-magic—the kind the speakers used to make at every covenant-renewal, the kind that hasn't been spoken in ten years because speaking it is death by Proclamation. The chancellors didn't just break the covenant. They starved it. Every year the lie stands, the fire eats more of itself.*

*We are looking for a speaker. Not a hedge-charmer, not a knot-tier—a true oath-speaker, blood-bound, trained before the Breaking. There can't be many left. There may be one.*

*You know who I mean. I've said the name to no one in ten years. Find them, Ovi. Tell them—*

*tell them Sera kept the flame. Tell them she never stopped writing letters to a brother who wouldn't answer, because hope is a discipline and she has practiced it daily.*

*Burn this.*

*S.*

Felix put the letter down very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off.

*Brother,* it said. *A brother who wouldn't answer.* And beneath the words, in the space where meaning lived rather than ink, the thing Sera could not have known: that the letters *had* been answered, once, in the only currency Felix had—silence kept like a vow, a name buried so deep that even grief couldn't dig it up, because to speak of Sera Kavanagh in the Corrow markets was to die, and Felix had chosen to live long enough to be invisible.

Ten years of silence, and she had counted it as hope.

"I need a moment," Felix said, and walked out into the cold, and stood behind the dovecote with their hands flat against the wall until the shaking stopped.

* * *

It was Wren who found them there, some unknowable time later. She didn't say anything at first. She just leaned on the wall beside them, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and looked out at the Steppe doing its morning trick of turning from grey to gold to a blinding, glassy white.

"She's alive," Wren said eventually.

"She's alive."

"That's good, yeah?"

Felix laughed—a cracked, awful sound. "It should be. Ten years I've—" They stopped. Started again, slower. "I lit a candle for her every year. In the cellar, where nobody could see. Mourning-candle, the old rite. You burn it for the dead so they can find their way." They stared out at the glass plains. "And she was alive the whole time. Writing letters. Keeping a fire lit. Practicing hope like it was a trade."

"So be glad."

"I *am* glad." And they were—that was the terrible thing, the joy was real, it was enormous, it filled their chest like floodwater. But underneath the joy, cold and rising, was the rest of it. "Wren. Read the last letters. Really read them."

Wren took the packet back inside. Felix followed, and watched her read, and watched the expression on her sharp face travel from curiosity to dawning understanding to something older and warier.

The seventh letter: the fire dimming faster. The eighth: the barrier failing along the northern reach; the Hollow taking a village called Ashenmere in a single night; the loyalists evacuating whom they could. The ninth—the last, undated, the ink hurried:

*Ovi—*

*No time. The fire gutters. Weeks, maybe days. If it dies, the barrier dies with it, and everything the Hollow touches it keeps. I am going to bind myself to the Emberhold's heart—to hold the flame with what I am, for as long as I am. It may buy the years we need. It may kill me. Either way I need the last speaker brought here BEFORE the fire fails, because a binding alone won't reignite it. Only a freely-given oath can do that. Only an oath-speaker.*

*Find Felix. I know they think I'm dead. I let them think it—I ordered it, after the fire, to keep them safe, and it is the one cruelty of my life I'd commit again. But the time for safety is done.*

*Tell my sibling: I'm sorry about the candle. Light it for yourself instead.*

*Burn this. Burn everything. Run.*

*S.*

Silence held the room. Outside, wind worried the dovecote's broken slats.

"She bound herself to the fire," Ilya said at last, and his voice had gone hollow. "As fuel. Like the old kings in the founding-stories. Like—" He stopped. Everyone in the civilized realms knew the stories; they were told to children as warnings about the price of magic. "She's been *burning*. For however long. To hold the barrier."

"To hold it while waiting for me," Felix said. "For the last speaker." They heard their own voice as if from a distance, level and strange. "She sent Ovid to find me. Ovid bled out at my stall to get the map to me. And she's been burning alive in the dark for—how long? How long since that letter?"

Wren checked the dates, lips moving. "Two months. Maybe three."

Three months of Sera, poured out like lamp-oil.

The hope that had been flooding Felix's chest curdled as they stood there—did not vanish, nothing so clean as vanishing, but thickened, darkened, turned into something with weight and edges. Because this was no longer the story they had set out to tell themselves on the road out of Corrow. It was not even, strictly, the story the map promised. Felix had crossed the salt gate telling themselves: *the world is ending, the fire must be relit, the covenant must be reforged.* Noble words. Large words. Words big enough to hide inside.

But the letters made the story small and sharp and unbearably personal. Not *save the world.* Save *Sera*. Pull your sister off a pyre she climbed onto willingly, out of love, out of duty, out of ten years of practicing hope—and do it before the oil runs out.

The world could wait on the world's own time. Sera could not.

"We leave within the hour," Felix said. "The Emberhold is eight days hard across the glass. Maybe six if we take the dragon-road the map shows, and I'll take six over eight with a fire dying at the end of it."

"You heard the letter," Wren said, already moving, sweeping the supplies together with a thief's economy. "'Run,' it says. Running toward seems wrong."

"It's Sera's handwriting," Felix said. "She always did give directions like the opposite of what she meant. When we were children she'd say *don't follow me* and stand there waiting until you did."

That earned a ghost of a laugh from Ilya—then cut off. He was at the window, at the gap in the slate shutter, and his whole body had changed. Felix knew that change. Every hunted creature knows it.

"What?"

"Dust," Ilya said quietly. "North ridge. Three riders, maybe four, moving in formation. Grey coats." He turned, and his face was the face of a man watching his old life come over a hill with drawn swords. "Wardens. They're not searching. They're *tracking*. Straight line, no sweep pattern." His jaw tightened. "Someone gave them our route."

*Marrow,* Felix thought, and filed the betrayal away in the place where griefs waited their turn. There was no time for it now. There might never be time for it.

"Back door," Wren said. "There's always a back door. Marrow's caches always have one—it's how he stays alive."

"Take the letters," Felix said, folding the packet into their coat, over their heart, where the map already rode. Nine letters and a scorched map: the whole weight of a resurrected sister, carried against the ribs. "And Wren—if we're separated—"

"Don't finish that sentence. Bad luck." Wren grinned, fierce and thin, and kicked open the false brick behind the hearth, revealing a crawl-space and darkness and, faintly, the smell of the open Steppe beyond. "After you, ambassador."

Felix went into the dark with Sera's letters burning against their chest like a second, smaller Emberhold, and behind them the Wardens' dust rose higher over the north ridge, and ahead—eight days, six if they dared, five if the gods were generous and the Hollow merciful, neither of which had ever been true—a guttering flame waited, and a woman who had practiced hope daily was feeding herself into it.
