## 10. The Warden's Net

Wren smelled the Wardens before she saw them—oiled leather and cold iron riding the wind off the glass, a smell that had followed her through half her life in the camps. She was on watch, perched in the crooked rafters of the waystation's hay loft with a knot of dried steppe-grass between her teeth, when the wind shifted and brought it to her: six riders, maybe seven, coming up the old drover track at an easy trot. Not hurrying. They didn't need to hurry. The waystation sat alone on a sea of fused glass, and everything that moved toward it moved along one of three roads.

"Company," she said, dropping from the loft without bothering with the ladder. "Wardens. Coming up the south track."

Felix looked up from the map they'd been studying by the light of a shuttered lamp, and something in their face went still and flat, the way it always did when the past reached for them. Ilya was slower. He'd been cleaning his boots by the cold hearth, and he kept doing it for two full seconds before his hands stopped.

"How many?"

"Seven. Maybe eight." Wren crossed to the window slit and put her eye to it. Dust hung over the south track like a smudge of breath on glass. "They're not rushing. That means they think we're already here or already gone, and either way they can afford to be polite about it."

"They could just be changing horses," Felix said, but their voice said they didn't believe it.

"The waystation master flies no colors," Ilya said quietly. He'd risen now, boots forgotten. "But this is a relay point. If there's a signal post—" He stopped himself. He'd been stopping himself all week, catching the habits of ten years of service before they could finish forming words.

"There's a signal post," Wren said. "Top of the well housing. I saw it when we came in yesterday. Chain and pennant." She turned to look at him, and let herself enjoy, just slightly, the grim set of his jaw. "So. Options. We run for the north road and hope the glass doesn't cut the horses' feet, we hide in the cellars and hope nobody thinks to look, or—" she grinned, because grinning was how she kept her hands steady, "—we walk out the front door and say good morning."

"Wren," Felix began.

"I'm serious. Look at what we've got." She counted on her fingers, quick and sharp. "One genuine Warden uniform, currently being worn by a genuine deserter who hasn't been reported yet—or wasn't as of three days ago. One pair of travelers' papers I lifted off a merchant at the last ford who won't miss them till spring. And one of me." She spread her hands. "I've talked my way past worse than a road checkpoint with less than this. We don't run. Running is what guilty people do, and guilty people get chased. Chased people get caught."

Felix stared at her. There was something complicated moving behind their eyes—worry, yes, but under it, unmistakably, a kind of reluctant admiration that Wren had been carefully cultivating since the salt gate. It was nice to see it bloom.

"And if you're wrong?" Ilya asked.

"Then I'm wrong loudly, and you two are already out the back with the horses while they're busy arresting me." She said it lightly, but she watched it land on Felix like a stone, watched them flinch, and felt a small hot coal of satisfaction that had nothing to do with pride and everything to do with the fact that someone in this world would mind if she were arrested. That was new. She was still deciding what to do with it.

"We're not leaving you," Felix said. Flat. Final. The same tone they used about the map, about Sera, about everything that mattered.

"No," Wren agreed. "You're not. Because my plan works." She pulled the stolen papers from inside her coat and slapped them against Ilya's chest. "Congratulations, Sergeant. You're escorting two pilgrims to the shrine at Vael's Rest. Try to look like you hate your job."

"He won't have to try," Felix murmured, and even Ilya's mouth twitched.

* * *

They gave themselves an hour. Long enough to eat, to rub dust into their clothes so they looked like they'd been on the road for days instead of hours, to coach Ilya on the name of his fictional posting until he could say it without flinching. Wren braided her hair back tight and plain, the way camp girls wore it, nothing worth stealing showing anywhere on her. Felix wrapped the map in oilcloth and buried it in the bottom of their pack beneath a change of shirt and a bag of fever charms—the real ones, the ones that worked, which Wren had been forbidden to sell and had been strongly tempted to anyway.

The waystation keeper, a leathery woman named Brakka with a burn-scarred forearm and the incurious eyes of someone who had survived the Steppe by never asking questions, took their coin for stabling and asked nothing. But when Wren passed her on the way to the yard, the old woman's hand shot out and closed around her wrist, quick as a trap.

"You've got Wardens coming up the south track," Brakka said, low. "And a pennant chain hanging loose on my well. You want it to stay hanging loose?"

Wren blinked. Then she smiled her best market smile. "What would it cost to keep it loose?"

Brakka's eyes flicked to the yard, to Ilya standing stiff-backed by the horses in his gray-and-iron, to Felix loading packs with careful, invisible hands. Something in her face softened by a degree too small for anyone but a thief to measure.

"Your mother's charm-work," she said. "The fever knots. I had one, years back, from a woman in Corrow. Saved my son." Her grip tightened once, then released. "Keep it loose yourself. I saw nothing."

She walked away, and Wren stood in the yard with her heart doing something strange and painful in her chest. Everyone on the Steppe, it seemed, had been touched by Kavanagh hands somewhere in the dark of the last decade. Everyone owed a debt they'd never been able to pay. She climbed the well housing, unhooked the pennant chain, and dropped it down inside where no rider would think to look, and told herself the heat in her face was just the wind off the glass.

* * *

They came up the track at mid-morning, seven riders in Warden gray, and Wren stood beside the horses in the yard with her thumbs hooked in her belt and her face arranged into the dull patience of a traveler waiting out a delay. Beside her, Felix held the papers loosely, eyes down, the very portrait of a person hoping to be overlooked. Ilya stood apart, mounted, in the shade of the station eave, wearing boredom like armor.

The lead rider reined in and swept the yard with the particular gaze of a man who had done this a thousand times and found nothing nine hundred and ninety of them. His badge showed a silver net over crossed keys—Third Company, capital-posted, which meant money and ambition and no patience for provincial duty.

"Papers," he said.

Felix handed them over. Wren watched the sergeant's eyes move across the names, the seals, the stated purpose—pilgrimage, Vael's Rest, the little shrine that had somehow survived the Prohibition because nobody important remembered it existed. She watched his lips move slightly, checking. She watched him get bored.

"Purpose of travel?"

"Pilgrimage," Felix said. Their voice came out rough and humble, pitched perfectly. "My sister and I. Our mother made a vow, before she passed. We're finishing it for her."

Something flickered across the sergeant's face—not suspicion, but the tired discomfort of a man reminded that people still did things like make vows. "Vael's Rest is eight days north. You're carrying little enough for eight days."

"We carried little enough for ten years, sir," Wren said, before she could stop herself, and then laughed—a camp-girl laugh, self-deprecating, disarming. "Sorry. Habit. We're used to less."

The sergeant almost smiled. Almost. And then his gaze slid past her, up to the mounted figure in the eave's shadow, and the almost-smile died.

"Sergeant." He said it the way you'd poke a suspicious object with a stick. "Identify yourself."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. She'd known—I'd known, some cold practical part of her had known—that the uniform was a coin with two faces, that the same gray that opened gates could close them. But she'd banked on the distance, on the capital man not knowing every face in the provincial companies, on luck. And luck, she thought bitterly, was exactly the thing she'd been selling other people for years and never managed to buy for herself.

Ilya brought his horse forward at a walk. Calm. Too calm. "Sergeant Ilyan Vosk, Fourth Provincial. Escort detail."

"Fourth Provincial." The sergeant turned his horse to face him fully, and now the whole patrol was watching, seven riders going quiet in the way soldiers go quiet when they smell something wrong. "Fourth Provincial's been recalled to Corrow. Standing order, five days ago. Every company, every man, muster at the Salt Gate." He paused. "You didn't hear?"

"I've been in the field," Ilya said.

"In the field." The sergeant's eyes narrowed. "On whose warrant?"

Silence stretched. Wren counted heartbeats—one, two, three—and watched Ilya sit his horse with his hands loose on the reins, and understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever happened in the next ten seconds would happen whether anyone chose it or not. He could lie. He was good enough, maybe, cool enough, if the sergeant was lazy enough. Or—

Or he could stop lying. She saw the moment he decided. It was in his shoulders, actually; they came down, just slightly, like a man setting down a load he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten its weight.

"My warrant was Commander Drevna's," Ilya said. "Before she died asking questions the Chancellery didn't want asked. I'm not mustering at the Salt Gate, Sergeant, because I've seen what's east of here, and I know why the Third Company is really riding the drover tracks. Ask your captain what's in the supply crates marked 'survey equipment.' Ask him why the Tide-lures are being issued to line patrols." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it—it stayed level, almost gentle, the voice of a man reporting facts. "Ask him who gave the order to evacuate the capital and leave the rest of the Steppe to the Hollow."

The yard went absolutely still. Even the horses seemed to hold their breath.

The sergeant's hand moved to his sword hilt—not fast, not angry, just inevitable, like weather. "Sergeant Vosk. You are under arrest for desertion and sedition. Disarm and dismount."

"No," Ilya said.

It was such a small word for such an enormous thing. Wren heard it and felt it go through her like a bell-strike. No. Ten years of service, a commander's trust, a uniform, a name, a whole architecture of belonging—and he stood in a dusty waystation yard and said no to all of it, quietly, in front of seven witnesses, for two fugitives and a thief.

The sergeant drew. So did two of his riders.

And Wren moved, because that was the only thing she knew how to do when the world went to pieces: she moved toward whatever was loosest and most valuable. In this case, the sergeant's saddlebag, gaping open on his left side where he'd reached for his sword. Her knife was out and under the flap and back before the blade cleared his scabbard—papers, a sealed dispatch case, a heavy purse of capital mint. She spun away with all of it clutched to her chest as the first shout went up.

"Hold! Hold, you idiot girl—"

Felix was already moving too, cutting the picket lines, slapping the horses into motion—not theirs, the Wardens', sending seven mounts bolting in four directions across the yard in a tangle of screaming leather and flying hooves that turned the patrol's neat arrest into a scrum of cursing men fighting their own animals. Wren had seen Felix fight exactly twice, both times with the oath-sword, and both times it had been like watching something ancient wake up. This was different. This was a person who had spent ten years surviving smugglers' markets, and it was somehow just as impressive: elbows, timing, a thrown feed bucket, a horse led deliberately through a rider's path. Nobody got hurt. Everybody got chaos.

"North road!" Felix shouted. "Now!"

They ran for it—all three, horses abandoned as too slow to catch, sprinting for the gap in the station's north fence with the dispatch case banging against Wren's ribs and the purse jingling like a festival. Behind them, the sergeant's voice cracked across the yard, hoarse with fury:

"Vosk! You traitor! You're branded! Do you hear me? Branded! There's no company in the provinces will take your name!"

Wren risked one glance back. Ilya had stopped—stopped, the fool, the brave stupid honorable fool—at the fence line, and turned, and faced the seven of them across forty feet of trampled dust. He didn't draw his sword. He just stood there, in his gray-and-iron uniform, and let them look at him, and let himself look at them, and Wren understood that he was saying goodbye to something. To the whole shape of his life.

"I heard you," Ilya called back. His voice carried clear across the yard, level as ever. "Find out what's in the crates, Sergeant. That's all I ask. Find out, and then decide who the traitor is."

Then he turned and ran, and they ran together, three figures and a stolen purse and a dead woman's cipher, out onto the blinding white-gold of the glass.

* * *

They didn't stop until the waystation was a smudge behind them and the land had folded twice between them and the south track. Then Felix called a halt in the shadow of a ridge of twisted glass—dragon-fire glass, Wren reminded herself, though it still looked like frozen lightning to her—and they stood in the blue shade of it, breathing hard, and nobody said anything for a long time.

Finally Wren sat down on a shelf of warm stone, dumped the purse out, and started counting. Capital mint, heavy coins, probably a month's wages for the whole patrol. "Well," she said, when she couldn't stand the silence anymore. "We're rich, we've got a sealed dispatch case I haven't picked yet, and we're officially the most wanted people on the Steppe. All things considered, a productive morning."

Neither of them laughed. She'd expected that from Felix. She hadn't expected it from Ilya, who stood facing back the way they'd come, one hand resting on his sword hilt—not gripping it, just resting, the way you'd touch the shoulder of an old friend you were about to leave.

"They'll hang me if they take me," he said. Conversationally. Like a man noting the weather. "Desertion in the face of the enemy, sedition, theft of official property—" he glanced at Wren, and there was, unbelievably, dry humor in it, "—well. That last one's yours."

"Shared custody," Wren said. "I'll split the purse."

He turned around then, and looked at the two of them, and the humor drained out of his face and left something rawer underneath. "Ten years," he said. "I believed in it. The Prohibition. The Wardens. I believed we were the wall between the realms and the fire, and that the wall was what kept everyone safe. My commander believed it too, right up until she started reading the archives, and then she believed something else, and it killed her." His hand tightened on the hilt now. "This morning I had a chance to keep the lie. It was easy. It was sitting right there. One lie, one smooth answer, and I'm still Sergeant Vosk with a pension and a place to sleep." He shook his head slowly. "I couldn't do it. I don't know when that changed. Somewhere between the village and the letters, I think. Somewhere between watching the Tide eat a town and reading what Sera Kavanagh wrote to a courier about the truth." He looked at Felix. "Your sister died for this. Or lived for it—I don't know which yet. Either way. I'm in. All the way to the Emberhold, whatever's there. Whatever it costs."

The wind moved over the glass plains and made them sing, faintly, the way they always sang—that thin crystalline note, like a wet finger on a bowl, like the memory of fire. Wren hugged the purse to her chest and felt, uncomfortably, like she was intruding on something. This was the second time in a week that someone had declared themselves to Felix Kavanagh in terms that had nothing to do with copper or survival, and the second time she'd found herself standing nearby, holding the loot, wondering when exactly she'd stopped being a thief traveling with strangers and started being part of whatever this was.

Felix stepped forward and did something Wren had never seen them do: they took Ilya's hand off the sword hilt and clasped it, bare, skin to skin, the old gesture—the oath-gesture, the one from before the Prohibition, the one that used to mean something binding.

"Then you're not branded," Felix said quietly. "Not among us. Among us, you're sworn."

Ilya's throat worked. He nodded once, unable, apparently, to find words, and Wren looked away to give him privacy and busied herself with the dispatch case instead, working her knife into the seal.

It came open under her fingers like it had been waiting for her. Inside: orders, written in a chancellor's own hand. Supply requisitions for Tide-lures, dozens of them, issued to line patrols across the eastern Steppe. A schedule of evacuations—capital districts only, dates running through the season. And at the bottom, in the same firm hand, a single line that stopped Wren's breath and made her hold the paper out with a hand that wasn't quite steady:

*"Let the barrier fail where it may. The Emberhold garrison will hold the fire until the succession is secured. Nothing else matters."*

"Succession," Felix read aloud. Their voice had gone very soft and very dangerous, the voice Wren had heard exactly once before, at the salt gate, when they spoke their sister's name. "Secured. They're not trying to save the fire. They're trying to own it."

"And the Tide-lures," Ilya said grimly. "They're not just weapons. They're herding it. Pushing the Hollow away from the capital and letting it feed everywhere else." He met Felix's eyes. "The Emberhold. Your sister. If Veyne's people are securing a succession—"

"Then Sera isn't just a prisoner," Felix said. "She's an heirloom."

Nobody said anything after that. The glass sang its thin note. Far to the northeast, past the folded horizons, Wren thought she could see—though it might have been heat-shimmer, though it might have been imagination—a smudge of darkness on the sky where no darkness should be, moving slowly, patiently, the way hunger moves.

She shouldered her pack, heavier now by one purse and one terrible piece of paper, and fell into step as they turned north, toward the dragon-road, toward the guttering fire, toward whatever waited at the end of the world's oldest lie.

Eight days to Vael's Rest, the papers had said. Wren wondered idly, counting coins in her pocket, whether any of them would live to see a shrine.
