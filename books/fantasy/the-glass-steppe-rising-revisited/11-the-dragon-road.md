## 11. The Dragon-Road

The glass sang at dawn.

Felix had learned to read the Steppe's small voices in the six days since the salt gate—the tick of cooling panes as night's cold bled away, the whisper of grit skittering across fused sand like rice poured over a drumhead—but this was different. This was a note that rose from beneath their boots, low and sustained, a hum they felt in their back teeth before they heard it with their ears.

They stopped mid-stride and crouched, pressing a palm flat against the glass.

Warm.

Not sun-warm. The morning was still gray, the light thin as broth, and yet the pane under Felix's hand carried heat like a hearthstone hours after the fire had died. And beneath the warmth, faint but unmistakable, something pulsed. A rhythm. Slow, vast, patient.

A heartbeat.

"Felix." Wren had stopped ten paces back, her weight on her heels in the way she did when she'd scented trouble before she could name it. "You feel that too, or have you finally cracked?"

"Feel it," Ilya said behind her. He said little these days—his Warden training lived in his body still, in the way he checked their backtrail every quarter hour, in the way he slept with his boots on—but when he spoke it was always worth hearing. He knelt beside Felix without touching the glass, hovering his hand above it the way one hovers over a sleeping animal. "It's not natural heat. It's *directed*. Something underneath is pushing warmth up through the stone."

"Not stone," Felix said. Their voice came out strange to them, half-swallowed. "Glass. It's all glass out here, if you dig. Fused sand. The old stories say—" They stopped. The old stories were dangerous things; they'd spent ten years burying them alongside everything else.

"The old stories say what?" Wren asked, and there was no mockery in it now. There hadn't been for days. Not since the Tide took the village on the flats, not since Ovid's letters, not since Sera's name had passed between them like a coal too hot to hold.

Felix drew a breath and let the story out of the cellar where they'd kept it. "That the Steppe wasn't always glass. That when the covenant was forged, the dragons' fire swept the plains—not as war, but as witness. That the sand melted and remembered. That if you knew how to listen, the ground would tell you what was sworn here."

Wren looked out across the plain—the endless silver-white expanse running to every horizon, glittering where the weak sun caught it, beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful, which is to say: with an undertone of menace. "And you believe that."

"I believe my sword hasn't made a sound in ten years," Felix said, "and it started humming three days ago, getting louder the further east we walk." They rose, and their hand found the hilt at their hip without deciding to. The oath-sword. Plain steel by every test the Wardens' assessors had ever run—that was the only reason Felix had kept it through the Prohibition years, the only reason it hadn't been confiscated and rendered down with all the other oath-bonded blades. It had been dead metal since the night the covenant broke. Since the night their family died. A keepsake, nothing more. A piece of a life that no longer existed.

But now, resting against their palm, it hummed. And the hum matched the pulse in the glass exactly.

"It knows where we are," Ilya said quietly. "Or it knows where we're near. My commander—" He stopped, jaw working. Even now, weeks after his faith had cracked open like river ice, he handled the memory of Commander Aldric carefully, as if rough treatment might shatter it entirely. "Aldric had papers he never showed anyone. Maps of the Steppe with lines drawn under the surface. He told me once, drunk, that the Wardens didn't patrol the deep Steppe because there was nothing to patrol. He laughed when he said it. It wasn't a true laugh."

"Under the surface," Wren repeated. Her eyes had gone bright and narrow, the thief's look, the look that mapped value. She spun in a slow circle, scanning the plain—and then stopped, facing east-southeast, toward the smudge of mountains where the Emberhold waited somewhere beyond the haze. "There. Look. The glass is different there. Smoother. Like a road."

Felix saw it then, once she'd pointed: a band of glass perhaps thirty feet wide, running straight as a plumb line toward the mountains, its surface unmarked by the ripples and stress-fractures that webbed the rest of the plain. It looked less like terrain than like architecture. Less like accident than intention.

"A road," Felix said. "Under the glass, or *of* the glass."

"Only one way to find out," Wren said, already walking.

* * *

They followed the smooth band for two hours before the ground opened.

No warning—one moment the road ran flat ahead, the next the glass simply ended at a wide oval mouth, descending into the earth at a gentle grade, its edges worn round as a riverbank. Warm air breathed up out of it, carrying a smell Felix's body recognized before their mind did: hot stone and iron and something older, mineral and alive, like the air inside a forge that has never fully cooled.

Wren hung back. For all her bravado, she had a healthy animal respect for holes in the world. "It's a tunnel."

"It's a door," Felix said. The sword at their hip had gone from hum to song, a thin clear tone they could no longer pretend not to hear. They drew it halfway from the sheath and watched the light along its edge—not reflected light. Emitted. A faint gold shimmer, like sunlight through honey, running the length of the blade.

Ilya's breath caught audibly. "That's oath-light. I've only seen it in engravings. In the *old* engravings, the ones they don't put in training halls anymore."

Felix looked at the blade and felt something move in their chest that was equal parts terror and homecoming. Ten years they had carried this sword as a corpse-weight, a relic of a murdered age. Ten years it had been silent. And now it woke, here, at the mouth of a road buried beneath a sea of glass, singing a note that harmonized with the heartbeat in the deep stone.

"My mother used to say the swords chose their speakers," Felix said. "I thought it was a story she told to make the chores bearable." They resheathed the blade, and the gold light dimmed but did not die, glowing faintly through the leather. "I think we're expected."

"Expected by *what*?" Wren demanded, but she was following them down the slope even as she asked, because Wren's fear had never once beaten her hunger—not for coin, not for answers, and lately, though she'd die before admitting it, not for belonging.

The tunnel swallowed the sky.

* * *

Inside, the world was amber.

The walls of the dragon-road were not cut stone—they were fused sand, like the plains above, but fused so completely, so long ago, that the glass had gone deep gold and translucent, thick as cathedral windows. Light came through it from somewhere, from everywhere, diffuse and warm, so that they walked inside a corridor of honey-colored dawn. And the walls were not empty. As Felix's eyes adjusted, they saw shapes moving in the depths of the glass—shadows, impressions, vast slow forms preserved like insects in resin.

Dragons.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. Flying through the memory of the glass, wings spread, necks arched, rendered not in detail but in essence—a sweep of wing, a coil of tail, the suggestion of a great head turning. And among them, smaller, upright, unmistakable: people. Walking beside the dragons. Riding them. Standing on the ground with arms raised, and the dragons bowing—or the people bowing, and the dragons lowering their heads to meet them halfway. It was impossible to tell, from the shadows alone, who honored whom.

And that, Felix realized, throat tightening, was the entire point. Neither. Both. Equals.

"This is a temple," Ilya whispered. His hand had drifted to the Warden sigil at his collar—an old habit, touching the badge for reassurance—and Felix watched him register what he was doing and drop the hand as if burned. "No. It's a treaty hall. A record. They built the covenant *into the land itself* so no chancellor could burn the documents."

"They built it into the land," Felix corrected softly, "because the land was part of the signing."

The sword was fully alight now. Felix had drawn it without remembering drawing it, and the gold radiance it cast reached far down the corridor, and where the light touched the walls, the shadow-dragons seemed to stir. Not animate—Felix was not fool enough to think the memories moved—but *responsive*, the way a face seems to change when you truly look at it. The deeper they walked, the more the impressions resolved, until Felix could see individual figures: a woman in traveling leathers with her hand flat against a dragon's snout; a man holding up a sword identical to the one in Felix's grip, its edge burning with the same honey-gold light; a circle of figures, human and draconic both, arranged around a central flame—

Around a beacon.

"The Emberhold," Felix breathed. "This is the forging. This is the day it happened."

The floor sloped gently downward, and the air grew warmer, and the heartbeat in the stone grew stronger, and Felix understood that they were not walking through a museum. They were walking through a living thing. The dragon-road was not a record *of* the covenant. It was a vessel *for* it, a channel dug through the world's flesh, running from the cities of men to the fire of the dragons, built so that the oath could flow between them forever.

Until someone dammed the channel.

Ten years ago. The night of the breaking. The night of the Prohibition. The night Felix's family died in a house fire that the official record called accidental and that Felix had always, in the secret cellar of their heart, known was neither.

"Felix." Wren's voice, very quiet. She had stopped walking. "Your face. You've gone white as the Steppe."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. You're doing the thing you do, the thing where you swallow whatever's killing you and call it discipline." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped further, gentled further, which for Wren was nearly unprecedented. "What do you see?"

Felix opened their mouth to deflect—to say *nothing*, to say *memories*, to say anything that wasn't the truth, which was that the visions were no longer in the walls. They were in Felix's head.

Because the moment Wren asked, the corridor vanished.

* * *

Felix stood on grass.

Real grass, green and knee-high, rippling under a wind that smelled of rain and wildflowers and woodsmoke. The Glass Steppe had been a steppe once—of course it had—and it had been *this*, rolling and alive, dotted with camps and crofts, under a sky where dragons wheeled like swifts at dusk.

Before Felix stood a dragon.

Not the vast mountain-sized monsters of frightened fireside tales. This one was large—a hill rather than a mountain—with scales the color of autumn oak leaves, bronze shot through with green, and eyes like molten copper, ancient and amused and unbearably kind. It lowered its head until its gaze was level with Felix's own, and its breath stirred Felix's hair, warm as an open oven.

*"You are late, little speaker,"* it said, and its voice was the hum in the glass, the pulse in the stone, the note the sword had been singing for three days. *"A hundred years late. A thousand. But blood keeps its appointments, given time."*

"I don't understand," Felix said, and heard their own voice as if from far away, young and unsteady. "I'm not—I was never sworn. The covenant broke before I was old enough to take any oath. Whatever you're looking for, I'm just—"

*"Just?"* The dragon's laugh rolled through the grass like distant thunder, shaking dew from the blades. *"Child. Do you know what an oath-speaker is? Not one who speaks oaths. One who *keeps* them. The magic was never in the words. The words are cheap—chancellors spend words like copper. The magic is in the keeping. In standing, year after year, in the place you swore to stand, doing the thing you swore to do, when every reason to stop has come calling."*

Images flooded Felix—not visions now but memories, someone else's memories pouring through them like water through an open gate. A woman kneeling in this same grass, pressing her lips to a dragon's brow. A man raising a burning sword while a hundred voices swore in chorus. A child—*Sera*, it was Sera, fourteen years old and fierce-eyed, standing on a chair to reach the table where the elders sat—Sera reciting the covenant-words from memory because she'd begged to be taught them, because she'd believed, even at fourteen, especially at fourteen.

*"Your sister kept the faith,"* the dragon said, softer now. *"She keeps it still, in a place where keeping it costs her everything. And you—you kept a smaller faith, in a smaller place, for ten dark years. You kept your father's charms alive. You kept your mother's craft from dying. You kept yourself hidden and whole and ready, though you told yourself it was only cowardice."*

"It *was* cowardice."

*"Was it? Then why are you here?"*

Felix had no answer. The dragon's copper eyes held them, and in their depths Felix saw reflections that were not reflections: the Emberhold, black against a storm-dark sky, its beacon guttering like a candle in a draft. A figure bound at the heart of the fire, light pouring out of her and none returning. Sera. Alive. Burning as fuel for a covenant her jailers had murdered and dared not let finish dying.

*"The road remembers what was sworn,"* the dragon said. *"The road can show you the words. But words are cheap, little speaker. What the fire needs is the keeping. Think on what you are willing to keep. Think on what you are willing to be kept *by*. And walk quickly. The flame gutters. Your sister gutters with it."*

"No," Felix said, and the word tore out of them with a force that scattered the vision like a kicked anthill. "No—wait—tell me where she is, tell me how to—"

Grass. Wind. Copper eyes, receding, folding into gold light, folding into amber walls, folding into the warm dark of the dragon-road.

* * *

Felix came back to themselves on their knees on the warm glass, the oath-sword blazing in their fist, tears cutting tracks through the dust on their face. Wren had both hands on their shoulders. Ilya stood over them, one hand on his knife, scanning the corridor as if the danger might be visible.

"—back, come on, come *back*," Wren was saying, her voice pitched high and tight. "Felix. Hey. Look at me. Look at me or I swear I'll—"

"I saw her." Felix's voice came out scraped raw. They gripped Wren's wrist, hard, anchoring themselves to the solid present-tense fact of her. "Wren. I saw Sera. She's alive. She's *at the Emberhold*—she's bound to the fire, they're using her, she's been feeding it with her own—"

"Slow down." But Wren's eyes had gone wet and furious, and her grip on Felix's shoulders tightened until it hurt. "She's alive. You're sure. You're actually sure."

"She spoke to me. Or—something spoke for her. A dragon. The road itself, I don't—" Felix pushed themselves upright, sheathed the still-glowing sword, and scrubbed a hand down their face. When they lowered it, the grief of ten years and the hope of six days had fused into something new and harder and clearer than either. Resolve, but load-bearing now. Resolve with weight on it. "The fire's failing because it was starved. Because the chancellors broke the covenant and lied about it, and the lie cut the road between the oaths and the flame. Sera's been holding the line alone for ten years with whatever she has left. And the dragon said—" Felix's voice caught. They steadied it. "The dragon said to walk quickly."

Ilya had gone very still. "Then we walk quickly."

"The Emberhold's four days east," Wren said, already counting in the way she counted—fast, practical, eyes flicking as if she could see the route drawn in the air. "Three if we push. Longer if Veyne's Wardens find our trail again. We need water, we need food, and we need to not die in the open when the next Tide comes through." She paused, and something almost shy crossed her sharp face. "Felix. What else did it say? The dragon. There was more. I know there was more."

Felix looked at her—at this pickpocket child of the Warden-camps who had appointed herself to a quest she barely understood, because being chosen, even transactionally, was more than she'd ever been offered. And at Ilya, the deserter, whose whole world had been a lie and who had walked out of it anyway. And at the sword, glowing patiently at their hip, waiting for an answer to a question the dragon had asked and Felix had not yet dared answer aloud.

*Think on what you are willing to keep.*

"There was more," Felix said. They started up the corridor, eastward, toward the light bleeding gold through the far end of the tunnel. "But it wasn't a warning. It was a bill. And I'll pay it when it comes due."

Behind them, deep in the amber dark, the heartbeat in the stone quickened—as if the road itself had heard, and approved, and begun to count the days.
