## 12. What the Chancellors Did

The dragon-road did not end. That was the first thing Ilya understood, walking deeper into it: the tunnel of warm stone went on the way a promise goes on—past the point where you expected it to be kept, past the point where you had any right to keep believing.

Behind them, the waystation and its Warden net were hours gone. Ahead of them, the glass above thinned in places to windows, and through those windows the Steppe poured light like something liquid. Felix walked at the front with the oath-sword drawn—not swinging, not threatening, just carried flat across both palms the way one carries a sleeping child—and the blade hummed against Ilya's teeth. He could feel it in his fillings. He could feel it in the old scar on his knuckle from his first year of training, when he'd gripped a practice sword wrong for six hours because Commander Ashe had told him grip was character.

Commander Ashe. The name had been sitting in Ilya's chest since the seal on that map, and now, as the visions thickened around them like heat-haze made solid, the name began to burn.

Because the road remembered. He'd thought at first it was only showing them the forging—the oaths, the fire, the two peoples swearing as equals beneath a sky full of wings. But the road remembered everything. And they were walking into the part of the memory no one was supposed to see.

* * *

It came first as cold.

The warmth of the stone fell away all at once, the way a hearth dies when someone rakes out the coals, and the light through the glass-windows turned the color of old bruises. Wren's hand found Ilya's sleeve without her seeming to notice she'd done it. Felix stopped walking.

Ahead, the road opened into a chamber that was not a chamber—or rather, it was the memory of one, rendered in the road's strange grammar: walls of standing glass, a long table of black wood, and seated around it twelve figures in the deep green and silver of the Chancellor's Conclave. Their faces were blurred, smoothed over like wax left near a flame—but their hands were sharp, terribly sharp, every ring and scar and tremor rendered with a cruelty of detail that made the facelessness worse, not better.

And above the table, held in chains of woven light, hung a dragon.

Not a great one. A young one, Ilya thought, its scales still bright along the spine, its wings folded tight against a body that had been starved down to architecture—bone and tendon and the terrible patience of a creature that had stopped struggling years ago. Its eyes were open. In the vision's merciless detail, Ilya could see that the eyes were the only part of it still alive.

"No," Felix said. It wasn't a word so much as a wound opening. "No, this isn't—it didn't happen like this. They told us the dragons broke it. They told us the dragons burned the Emberhold, they told us—"

"I know what they told us." Ilya heard his own voice come out flat and Warden-calm, and hated it. "I recited it. For six years I recited it to children in the camps. *The dragons broke faith. The dragons hoarded power. The Prohibition is mercy.*"

The vision moved.

One of the faceless chancellors rose—a woman, by the breadth of her hands, by the signet heavy on her right forefinger—and began to speak. No sound came from the memory, but the road did not need sound; it pressed meaning directly into the bone, the way pressure presses into a diver's ears. Ilya felt the words arrive whole:

*The covenant makes them our equals. Equals. Do you understand what has been signed away? Every charter, every border, every crown—we have sworn ourselves into a shared house, and we are the junior partners of it. They live a thousand years. We live seventy. Given time, given fire, given this* —the woman's hand rose toward the chained dragon— *given what they can do that we cannot, there will be no human realm worth the name in three generations.*

Another chancellor leaned forward, rings clicking against the black wood. *Then unmake the house. Quietly.*

*The dragons will not permit it.*

*The dragons are not here.* A third voice, older, and Ilya watched the hands fold together, watch the signet turn and turn. *We hold the Emberhold's fire-chamber. We hold the oath-vessels. Let the fire fail and let it be seen to fail. Let the last flame gutter before witnesses, and let the story be written before the smoke clears: the dragons broke faith. Grief is a country that believes whatever its rulers tell it.*

Ilya's knees nearly went. Not from horror—from recognition. Because he knew that cadence. He had heard that cadence in every briefing, every doctrine lesson, every solemn recitation of the Breaking that the Wardens had drilled into him since he was nine years old. *Grief is a country that believes whatever its rulers tell it.* Someone had built an order on that sentence. Someone had built *him* on it.

Felix had gone very still, the sword humming louder, and Ilya saw that their knuckles had gone white around the flat of the blade.

"The fire," Felix said. "The fire didn't fail. They *starved* it."

* * *

The vision turned, the way a page turns, and showed them how.

It showed the oath-vessels—great basins of fused glass at the heart of the Emberhold, the ones Felix had described from the forging-visions, the ones meant to be fed freely by speakers giving their oath-magic in trust. It showed the chancellors' hands diverting the flow, damming the vessels, siphoning the covenant's fire into reservoirs beneath the capital. Ten years of it. The barrier against the Hollow Tide thinning season by season while the chancellors' private stores grew fat and bright.

And it showed the cover, laid over all of it like a sheet over a corpse: the Proclamation of Prohibition, drafted in this same room, in this same green and silver. *Magic is the dragons' leash on mankind,* the document said, in language Ilya had memorized before he could write his own name properly. *To speak an oath of power is to surrender the human soul.* The Wardens were created in the same breath—an order of the grieving, armed with Tide-lures and truth-denying seals, charged with hunting the very craft that might have fed the fire back to life.

"They didn't outlaw magic because it was dangerous," Wren whispered. She had both arms wrapped around herself now, and her voice had the thin, scraped quality it got when she talked about the camps. "They outlawed it because it was *evidence*. Every charm, every knot, every speaker—that was proof the covenant could be rebuilt. Proof the lie had a seam." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I stole luck-knots to survive a famine their lie *made*, and they called me the criminal."

The vision was not finished with them.

The page turned again, and Ilya saw a corridor he knew. Knew the grey stone, knew the iron sconces, knew the specific way the light fell at the fourth turning outside the records vaults of the Warden citadel. His stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

A man stood at the vault door with a lantern in one hand and a sheaf of documents in the other. Broad shoulders going soft with age. Grey in the close-cropped hair. A habit of standing with his weight slightly favoring the left leg, from an old wound on the Steppe that he'd never once complained about.

Commander Ashe.

He was younger in the memory than Ilya had ever known him—this was years ago, before Ilya's apprenticeship, before—and he was reading. Ilya watched the commander's face do something Ilya had never seen it do in six years of service: break. Not crumble. *Break*, the way ice breaks, all at once and along clean lines, a man's entire understanding of his life fracturing in a single lamplit moment.

The documents in his hand bore the Conclave's seal. Ilya couldn't read them at this distance, but he didn't need to; the road poured their substance into him alongside the vision. Requisitions. Transfers of oath-fire to the capital's reservoirs. Maintenance schedules for the barrier that assumed, in dry bureaucratic prose, its deliberate failure. And a directive, countersigned by three successive Chancellors, authorizing the silencing of any speaker who learned the truth.

Ashe read until the lantern burned low. Then he copied what he could, hid the originals where only a dying man would think to look, and walked out of the citadel into the rain.

The page turned one more time.

There was a safehouse—Ilya recognized the district, the crooked chimney, the window with the blue shutters—and Ashe sat across a table from a young woman with Sera Kavanagh's face, sliding the copies toward her, saying words the road gave Ilya like a knife between the ribs: *Someone inside will carry it further, but it has to leave the city tonight. There's a runner. Ovid. He knows the Steppe better than the Wardens' own maps do. If anything happens to me, find the last swordspeaker. Find Felix Kavanagh. The truth needs a witness who can't be bought, and the fire needs an heir.*

"And then?" Felix asked the vision, though it could not answer. Their voice cracked straight down the middle. "And then what happened to you?"

The road answered anyway, because the road remembered everything.

It showed Ilya the report he had never been cleared to read. *Warden Commander D. Ashe, killed in the line of duty during the apprehension of a prohibited speaker. The speaker was destroyed. All materials recovered.* It showed him the gap of eleven days in the citadel's chronicle that every officer learned to step over without looking down. It showed him, finally, the thing that undid him entirely: the official account of the Breaking itself, the founding scripture of the Wardens, and in the margin of the master copy—in ink that matched the Third Chancellor's own hand—a single annotation, dry as dust:

*The people will need someone to blame. Give them the dragons. Grief forgives its authors faster than it forgives its victims.*

* * *

Ilya did not remember sitting down. He was on the warm stone with his back against the road's wall, and his hands were shaking, and somewhere far away Wren was saying his name, and none of it reached him through the roaring.

Six years. Six years of drills and doctrine and the solemn recitation. Six years of holding Tide-lures over frightened charm-sellers in the smugglers' market while telling himself it was mercy, it was order, it was the shield that kept the Hollow out. Six years of being *proud*. And underneath all of it, load-bearing, invisible, the lie: that the dragons broke faith, that magic was theft, that the Wardens were the wall between humanity and the dark.

They weren't the wall. They were the lock on the door of a room where the truth was starved to death—and he had polished that lock every morning of his adult life.

His commander had died trying to hand the truth to strangers. Had died, and been filed under *materials recovered*, and Ilya had lit the memorial candle for him every year. Every single year. He had spoken the Warden litany over Ashe's name—*let the faithful servant rest in the order's keeping*—while the order that killed him used his death as a warning label on the lie.

"I served them," he heard himself say. His voice sounded like someone else's, scraped raw. "I *recruited* for them. I stood in the camps and told hungry children that magic was the reason their parents were gone, and the whole time—" His fist hit the stone, and the road took the blow without complaint, warm and patient. "The whole time the people who broke the world were signing my pay."

Felix crouched beside him. Up close, Ilya could see that they were crying without any noise or drama about it, tears simply falling off their jaw onto the sword's flat, hissing faintly where they struck the waking steel.

"My family died for this," Felix said quietly. "Ten years ago. The night of the Breaking. Wardens came to our street with the new Proclamation and burned every speaker they could find, and they called it justice for what the dragons did." A breath. "The dragons did nothing. My parents died defending a covenant the chancellors murdered, and my sister has spent ten years carrying the proof alone."

"Veyne," Ilya said. The name came up like bile. "Chancellor-Hand Veyne signs the evacuation orders. Veyne commands the pursuit. He *knows*. He has to know—he's Third Conclave trained, he'd have access to the master copies, the reservoir schedules—"

"He doesn't just know," Wren said. She was standing over them both, and there was something new in her face, something with edges. "Think about it. Who diverts the oath-fire? Who keeps the reservoirs fat while the barrier rots? Whoever holds those stores holds enough power to rule the human realms for a century after the Tide comes. The Prohibition isn't fear anymore. It's a *harvest*."

The words settled over the tunnel like a shroud. Ilya looked up the dark stretch of road ahead, toward the distant, unseen Emberhold, and understood with perfect clarity what they were walking into: not a fortress but a vault, not a war but a theft in progress, and the thieves had an army, and Tide-lures, and ten years of practice at making truth disappear.

He should have been afraid. Some part of him noted, distantly, that a loyal Warden apprentice confronting this reality ought to be paralyzed by it. Instead, what filled him was something hotter and cleaner than fear. It was the feeling of a bone setting. Of a lie being pulled out of his chest by the roots, leaving a hole that hurt abominably and would, eventually, heal around something true.

"Ashe sent Ovid to find you," Ilya said to Felix. "Before he died, he set all of this in motion. Which means the Wardens' own founder-of-record, their own honored dead, chose your family to carry the truth." He pushed himself to his feet, and his legs held, and that decided something in him. "I'm done being their instrument. Whatever's at the end of this road, I walk it as yours."

Felix looked at him for a long moment, tears still drying on their face, and nodded once.

They walked on. And the road, as if it had been waiting for exactly this, began to warm again beneath their feet—and far ahead, through a window of thinning glass, Ilya saw the horizon flicker.

Not sunlight. Not dragon-fire.

A smear of grey, moving against the wind, vast and slow and eating the world as it came—the Hollow Tide, closer than any of them had guessed, rolling toward a scatter of rooftops and smoke at the Steppe's edge where a village lay sleeping, unaware, in its path.
