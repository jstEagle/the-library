## 13. Wren's Steppe

Wren smelled it before she saw it.

The Glass Steppe had its own smells—hot mineral breath off the glass plains at midday, the dry ghost of sage where the grass still held on, the faint ozone tang that Felix said was old dragon-fire baked into the land. Wren had learned all of them in three weeks of walking, had learned to read them like a pickpocket reads a crowd. But this smell was different. This one was older than the others and it came up out of her own chest before it came off the land: char and ash gone soft with rain, and underneath it something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a sealed room.

The Hollow Tide had passed through here once. It left a smell behind.

She stopped walking so suddenly that Ilya nearly trod on her heels. He'd been favoring his bandaged side since the pass, moving stiff as a man carrying a barrel of water he wasn't allowed to spill, but even wounded his reflexes were Warden-fast; he caught himself and looked at her with those careful gray eyes that always seemed to be asking permission to speak.

"What is it?" Felix asked, from the front. Felix always walked first now, though they never said so. It was just how their little procession had arranged itself—Felix leading, Wren ranging wide like a gull after a fishing boat, Ilya bringing up the rear because he said (once, quietly) that he liked to be the one facing what came after them.

Wren didn't answer. Her mouth had gone dry as seed husks.

She knew this place.

Not by sight—the horizon here meant nothing to her, glass and grass and sky in their endless gray-blue arithmetic—but by the ground itself. By the way her feet remembered the slope of it. She had learned to walk on this earth, back when it was earth, when it was dark loam and bentgrass and the roots of salt-plums you could dig out with your fingers. She had been four years old, maybe five, running barefoot along a track her mother's people had worn into the steppe over six generations.

The track was still there. That was the worst of it. The Tide hadn't erased the land—it had *emptied* it, scoured it down to a pale memory of itself, and then the rains had come through and fused the ash into glass, and now the old wheel-ruts ran beneath her feet like veins under skin, visible, permanent, going nowhere.

"Here," she said. Her voice came out small. "It's here."

Felix came back to her. They didn't ask *what* was here. They had known since the night around the fire two weeks ago when Wren had finally told them about the camp—told them in the flat, fast voice she used for everything, listing facts like goods she was fencing, and watched Felix's face do something complicated and gentle that she'd pretended not to see. They had known, and they had adjusted their route by half a day without saying a word about why, and Wren had let them believe she didn't notice.

She noticed everything. It was how she'd stayed alive.

"The maps put it two miles north," Ilya said softly, unrolling Ovid's leather case against his forearm. His hands were still clumsy on the buckles—another thing the wound had taken from him—and Wren found herself stepping in to work the clasp before she thought about it, the way you'd help a child or an elder. He let her. That was new too. There had been a time, not long ago, when letting a thief touch your gear would have been unthinkable to him.

"Your maps are wrong," she said. "Or the camp moved. Camps moved." Her mother had said it like a prayer: *the camp moves, so we cannot be caught; the camp moves, so we cannot be buried.* The irony of that sat in Wren's throat like a swallowed stone. "But this is the track. This is—" She crouched, laid her palm flat against the glass. It was warm. The Steppe's glass was always faintly warm, Felix said, remembering the dragons. Under her hand it felt less like memory and more like fever. "This is where I fell down and split my chin open. My mother carried me back to the fires and told everyone I'd fought a sand-wyrm."

"Did you?" Felix asked.

"It was a root," Wren said. "I tripped over a salt-plum root."

She heard Felix smile rather than saw it—they were behind her—but it was a small, careful smile, the kind you gave someone walking a ledge. And then Felix said nothing else, which was the kindest thing anyone could have done, and stood there in the wind while Wren knelt on the glass over the ghost of her own blood.

* * *

They made camp early that day, which Ilya protested on principle—he was still all watches and water-rations, the Warden drilled down into his bones—but Felix overruled him with a look, and Ilya subsided, and nobody explained anything to anybody because everybody already understood.

There was no wood to burn. There was no wood anywhere anymore; the Tide didn't leave standing things behind. They burned dung-chips and dried steppe-sage, and the smoke smelled almost home-like, which Wren decided was either mercy or cruelty and couldn't tell which.

She sat apart from the fire, the way she always did—not far, never far enough that they might think she was leaving, but apart, at the edge of the light where her hands could stay busy and her face could be her own business. She worked on her bootlaces. She worked on a snare she didn't need. She counted her charms for the fourth time that hour: two luck-knots, one fever-twist, half a warmth-knot that Felix had taught her to braid herself back on the dragon-road, when the tunnel stone hummed under their feet and Felix's sword sang in its sheath like a struck bell.

That night in the road had changed something. Wren kept turning it over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. Felix had taken her hands—her filthy, quick, thieving hands—and folded them around a length of cord, and shown her the knot, and said, *it's not magic yet, it's just a knot, the magic is only ever the last inch of it,* and Wren had braided it and braided it until her fingers ached, waiting for the fear to come, the old cold panic she felt whenever anything shimmered or sparked or whispered of the Prohibition and the Wardens and the camps where her cousin had been taken.

The fear had come anyway. But it had come smaller. And the knot had held a thread of real warmth, the first magic Wren had ever owned instead of stolen, and she had cried about it later, alone, furiously, and told no one, and was fairly sure Felix knew anyway.

"Wren." Felix's voice, low, from the fire. Not calling her over. Just saying her name, the way you'd set a cup down next to someone. An offer with no hand attached.

"I'm fine," she said automatically.

"I know," Felix said.

The silence that followed was enormous and full of stars. Out on the Steppe the sky went all the way down to the ground on every side, no roofs, no smoke-haze, no city walls to cut it up. Wren had hated that at first. Now, sitting in the ruins of her beginning, she thought maybe that was the whole trouble with the Steppe: there was nowhere to hide from any of it. Not from the sky. Not from the past.

She got up. Walked—not toward the fire, but north, following the wheel-ruts, and after a moment she heard footsteps follow her, unhurried, and did not tell them to stop.

* * *

The camp's bones were a quarter mile on, where the ruts widened into a trampled oval she recognized the way you recognize a face in a dream.

There was almost nothing left. That was the thing nobody warned you about ruin: how *little* there was. She had expected wreckage, char, monuments of grief. Instead there was a field of pale glass scored with faint darker lines—the foundations of the long-tents, the hearth-pits, the pen where they'd kept the goats. A scatter of fused lumps that might once have been iron pots. One corner post of the shrine-pole, burned to a black stub, still upright, absurdly stubborn, casting a shadow in the moonlight like a raised finger.

And the glass itself, everywhere, holding impressions the way wax holds seals. Here, a curve that had been a sleeping body curled under blankets. There, smaller prints—a child's bare feet, frozen mid-run. Wren stood at the edge of it and looked down at the footprint of some kid who had been sprinting through the camp on an ordinary evening ten years ago, chasing a dog or a brother or the last light, and who had run straight out of the world.

"That'll be Tam," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange, flattened, like sound over glass. "Tam Ashai. He was faster than me. Everybody said so." A breath. "He'd have been fourteen."

Felix stood at her shoulder. Ilya stood a little way off, hat in his hands, which he'd done since the pass whenever the dead were near—some old soldier's courtesy, baring his head. Neither of them spoke. Neither of them touched her. They just *stayed*, solid and breathing and present, two warm bodies between Wren and the whole indifferent dark, and that was somehow worse than comfort, because it left her nothing to push against.

"There were sixty of us," she said. "Sixty-one. Old Marette was dying anyway, she'd have gone that winter regardless, so maybe sixty." She was doing it again—the flat voice, the fence's inventory. She hated it. She tried to stop and couldn't; the list-voice was the only door she knew how to open. "My mother wove tents. Good ones. She traded them down at Corrow twice a year, that's how I know Corrow, that's how I—" Her throat closed. "That's how I got left there. We came in for the autumn trade and the Wardens closed the gate. Charm-bearers, they said. Someone had sold a charm to a Warden's own clerk, can you imagine, sold a *luck-knot*, and they sealed the camp outside the walls and took the names of everyone born under a woven roof and my mother told me to run, she gave me her luck-knot and told me to run and not look back, and I—"

"Ran," Felix said quietly. "You ran."

"I ran." The words tore coming out, like cloth ripped along a seam. "I ran and I lived in Corrow and I stole and I survived and I told myself it was fine, it was *fine*, because surviving was the point, right? Surviving is the win. You survive and that shows them." She laughed, and it came out broken, a terrible sound with nothing in it. "Shows them *what?* There's nobody to show. There's nobody *left*. I survived and it didn't mean anything, it was just—I was just the only piece left, rattling around in the box—"

Her legs went. She didn't fall so much as fold, knees hitting the glass, palms flat, and the sob that came out of her then was not the pretty kind, not the kind you could manage or shape. It was ugly and huge and it seemed to come up out of the ground itself, out of the warm glass, out of ten years of locked rooms inside her chest, and once it started it would not stop.

And this was the part Wren would remember for the rest of her life, the part she would carry like a coal in the pocket of every cold year after: neither of them looked away, and neither of them rushed her, and neither of them told her to hush.

Felix knelt beside her—not touching, just near, close enough that Wren could feel the warmth of them through the night air, and began, very softly, to speak the mourning-words of the Steppe camps. Wren froze. She knew those words. She hadn't heard them since she was seven years old. *The camp moves, so we cannot be caught; the camp moves, so we cannot be buried; the fire goes down into the earth and the earth keeps it.* Felix's accent was wrong, the cadence a little bookish, clearly learned from Sera long ago and carried like pressed flowers for ten years—and it didn't matter, it didn't matter at all, because the words rose over the ruined camp like smoke finding a chimney, and somewhere inside the storm of Wren's grief something answered them, some small buried thing turning over, listening.

Then Ilya came and knelt on her other side. He didn't know the words. He bowed his head over the glass and said, in his flat Warden cadence, "I don't have a rite for this. But I stood watch over the dead for eleven years, and I never once stood watch for someone's family. Let me do it properly, this time." And he began, simply, to name them—not their names, he didn't know them, but the watchman's count: *one soul, two souls, sixty souls, witnessed, witnessed, witnessed*—and Wren, sobbing, laughing, undone, understood that he was giving her the only thing he had, the only liturgy he'd ever been taught, and that it was enough. That it was more than enough.

They stayed there on their knees in the moonlight while the fire of her grief burned down from roaring to coals. Hours, maybe. The stars wheeled. The glass held its warmth under her hands like a living thing, patient, keeping its dead the way the earth was supposed to keep its fire.

When it was over—when the crying finally quit, wrung out of her like water from a rag—she was empty in the strangest, best way. Lighter. As if she'd been carrying the camp on her back for ten years and had finally, finally set it down, and discovered her spine still worked.

"My mother's name was Dessa," she said to the glass. To the stub of the shrine-pole. To the sixty. "Dessa of the Ashai tents. She wove the best luck-knots on the whole Steppe. Better than Felix's." A wet, cracked laugh. "Don't tell them I said so."

"We heard," Felix said gravely. "We will hold it over you forever."

Wren laughed again—really laughed this time, surprised by it, the sound startling birds out of the dark grass a hundred yards off—and wiped her face on her sleeve, and looked at the two people kneeling with her in the ruins of everything, these two impossible people, a fugitive swordspeaker and a traitor Warden, and felt the truth of it land in her like the knot's first spark:

*Mourning shared is mourning halved.* Her mother had said that too. Wren had always thought it was a lie grown-ups told. It wasn't. It was arithmetic, and she'd just watched it balance.

They walked back to the fire together, the three of them abreast, and Wren slept that night in the middle, between them both, and did not dream of running.

* * *

She woke before dawn to Felix shaking her shoulder, gently, and to a quality in the grey light that she had learned to dread: the color of weather that wasn't weather.

"Fog?" she mumbled.

"No," Felix said. Their face, above her, was tight as a drawn wire. "Look east. And then look at the ground."

Wren looked east. On the horizon, where dawn should have been gold, there hung a smear of absolute black, vast and slow, edged with a faint wrong shimmer, like heat-haze made of nothing. It was too far away to hear. It didn't need to be heard. Every hair on her body stood up anyway, the animal part of her screaming a warning older than language.

Then she looked down—at the ground, as Felix had said—and saw that the warm glass beneath their camp had gone cool. All across the plain, in a ring around them, the Steppe's ancient remembered heat was guttering, dimming, like embers breathed upon by something that drank warmth instead of air.

"The barrier's failing faster," Ilya said hoarsely, limping up with the map already open. "Ovid's notes said the Emberhold beacon had weeks. If the glass is cooling here—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

"How far to the Emberhold?" Wren asked. Her voice was steady. She was almost proud of it.

"Four days," Felix said. "If we push hard. If we don't sleep much."

Behind them, in the east, the darkness leaned a little closer to the light, patient as debt, and the shrine-pole's black stub cast its long thin shadow toward the west, pointing the way they had to go.
