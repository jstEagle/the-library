## 14. The Guttering

The ridge had a name once, Wren said—Kestrel's Spine, the drovers called it—but names didn't survive out here on the Steppe any better than fences did. It was a long black tooth of fused glass rearing up out of the grassland, and by the time Felix reached its crest their boots were smoking at the seams and their lungs felt full of ground light.

They stopped anyway. Because ahead of them, past sixty miles of shimmering plain and the slow silver scar of a river that remembered being wider, something was dying.

The Emberhold.

It stood on the horizon like a fist of basalt holding a coal. Even at this distance—even through the heat-haze that made the whole world swim like a fish seen through water—Felix could see the beacon in its crown: a pillar of gold-white fire that had burned, according to every story Sera had ever told her, since before there were stories. The covenant-fire. The thing that kept the barrier whole and the Hollow Tide on the far side of the world where it belonged.

It was flickering.

Not burning steady, not breathing the way fire should breathe, but stuttering—gold, then gray, then gold again—like a lantern running out of oil, like a heart missing beats. Each time it dimmed, the horizon seemed to lean closer, as if the dark beyond the world were taking a step in.

"By all the salt and stone," Wren breathed beside Felix. She had gone very still, which for Wren meant something; stillness was the one thing she'd never been built for. "I've heard about it my whole life. The Emberlight. They say you could see it from the camps on clear nights, before—" She stopped, because *before* was doing too much work in that sentence. Before the Prohibition. Before the camps. Before everything got taken away and called law.

Felix said nothing. Their hand had gone to the map without their deciding to touch it—to Ovid's scorched parchment, to Sera's cipher stitched through it in her quick, slanted hand—and they found themselves pressing it against their chest as if it were a wound that needed holding shut.

*Days,* Ovid's letters had said. *When the fire gutters low enough, the Tide comes through like water finding a crack. I have seen what it leaves. Get here before the guttering ends.*

That had been written, judging by the ink, better than two months ago.

"How long?" Ilya asked quietly. He stood a pace behind them both—he always seemed to stand exactly one pace behind now, as if he hadn't yet earned the right to stand beside—and his eyes, trained by ten years of Warden discipline to measure and estimate, were already doing arithmetic Felix didn't want done. "The flicker interval. When a signal-fire's fuel runs short, the gaps lengthen. That gap just now—I counted near four seconds of dark."

"And when we first saw it from the river valley?" Felix asked.

"Two. Maybe less." He said it flatly, the way he said everything now, but Felix heard the thing underneath it. Ilya had spent his life believing the Wardens held back the dark. Every hour he'd walked with them had peeled another layer off that belief, and this—this simple, terrible counting—peeled the last of it. "Three days. Four if the wind stays kind. Less if it doesn't."

Wren sat down abruptly on the hot glass, wincing, not caring. "Four days," she said. "We've been walking eleven. We're maybe half—" She did her own arithmetic, faster than Ilya's, the thief's arithmetic of distance and desperation. "No. We can't make it in four. Not on foot. Not unless we grow wings between here and the Emberhold."

Felix looked at the beacon. It flared—held—a moment of clean gold so bright it hurt—and then guttered again, and the darkness between its pulses felt like a hand closing around Felix's throat.

Then, distantly, behind them: horns.

* * *

They went to ground in the Spine's shadow, among tumbled slabs of glass that had flowed and frozen mid-collapse a century or more ago, and Felix crawled up through a crevice to look back the way they'd come.

What they saw made their mouth go dry as ash.

There were nine riders on the plain below, strung out along the old drove-road in a loose crescent, and even at three miles Felix knew what they were. Not because of the armor—the Warden gray would have told them that—or because of the banner, though the banner was wrong in a way that turned Felix's stomach: instead of the scales-and-sword of the Prohibition, it bore a single closed hand, gold on black. The mark of the Chancellery itself. No, Felix knew them because of what hung from each rider's saddle.

Cages.

Small ones, iron-banded, each one cradling a sphere of something that wasn't quite smoke and wasn't quite absence. From this distance they looked like lanterns carried wrong—dark lanterns, lanterns that ate the light around them rather than giving it. And the plain beneath the riders' path confirmed it. Behind the crescent, the grass lay gray. Not trampled, not burned—*grayed*, drained of color and then of texture, and then, in patches, of existence altogether, leaving bare scabs of nothing where the Steppe simply stopped.

Tide-lures. Felix had heard the word only twice, both times from Ovid's letters, both times in a handwriting that shook. *They have learned to bottle it,* Sera had written through him. *The chancellors' artificers. They carry the Hollow in iron cages and let it leak, and whatever leaks dies.*

"They're using it," Ilya said. He had come up beside Felix uninvited, and neither of them remarked on it anymore. His face was the color of old bone. "As a weapon. As a—gods, as a *herding dog*. They're driving the Hollow behind them like hounds on a leash."

"That's not possible," Wren said from below, but her voice said she already believed it. "You can't leash that. You can't put a bit in that. The Tide doesn't want things. It *unwants* them. That's what my gran said. It doesn't hate you, it just forgets you were ever—"

She stopped. Below, one of the riders had halted. Dismounted. Lifted a cage from its saddle hook, and set it down in the grass, and opened its door.

Even at three miles, even against the wind, Felix heard it. A sound like a held breath releasing—not loud, not dramatic, just vast, the sound of an enormous emptiness finally allowed to be empty. The gray spread from the open cage in a slow ring, and the rider watched it, hands on hips, casual as a farmer checking a field, until it had eaten perhaps thirty paces of the Steppe. Then he closed the cage, remounted, and rode on, leaving a circle of perfect nothing behind him like a hole punched in the world.

"Why?" Wren's voice cracked on the word. "Why would they *do* that? That's—that's their own country. Those are their own fields. People graze sheep there, there are waystations, there's—"

"It's a message," Ilya said. "Or a fence. Or both." He had gone very still, and Felix recognized the stillness—it was the same stillness Wren had shown at the ridge top, the stillness of someone watching their whole world rearrange itself into something monstrous. "A fence says: nothing crosses this line. A message says: this is what happens to what the Chancellor-Hand touches. Either way, everyone on the Steppe will see those circles and know Veyne is coming."

"Veyne." Felix said the name and tasted copper.

Chancellor-Hand Aldric Veyne. Second voice of the Chancellery, first sword of the Prohibition, the man whose signature was on the warrant that had burned Felix's family out of their home ten years ago—Felix had read it during the bad years, hunted down copies of every document connected to that night the way some people pick at scars. Veyne had signed the order with a small, precise, beautiful hand. *Executed per statute, assets forfeit.* Nine words for five lives.

And now he was coming himself. Not sending Wardens. Not delegating. Coming, with Tide-lures and a closed-hand banner, across the corpse-colored grass, toward the same beacon they were racing toward.

"He knows," Felix said. The understanding arrived cold and complete, the way the worst understandings do. "He's not chasing us. We're not worth the Chancellor-Hand's own banner. He's going to the Emberhold—and we happen to be in his road."

"Or both," Ilya said quietly. "He knows what you carry, Kavanagh. Ovid ran the courier-line for two years before anyone caught him. Two years of letters between the Emberhold and sympathizers inside the capital. Veyne has had time to learn everything Ovid knew." He paused. "Including your sister's name."

Felix closed their eyes. For a moment the wind was the wind off Corrow's rooftops, and they were fifteen years old again, and Sera was laughing at them over a badly tied luck-knot—*you pull too tight, little fox, luck needs room to move*—and then the wind was the Steppe wind, smelling of hot glass and that faint wrongness the lures left behind, like the smell of a room after a quarrel.

"Then we stop being in his road," Felix said, opening their eyes. "We go north. Around the drove-road, through the shardfields. It adds two days."

"Two days we don't have," Wren said.

"Two days he doesn't think we have," Felix corrected. "He'll expect us to race him straight up the drove-road, because that's what the map shows and he'll assume we're slaves to the map. So we aren't." They pulled the parchment out and looked at it properly for perhaps the hundredth time, and for the first time they looked not at the cipher but at the margins—at the faint annotations in Ovid's cramped runner's hand, the notes of a man who knew this country the way Wren knew Corrow's rooftops. *Shardfields passable in dry season. Avoid the Weeping Stones after rain. There is a second road.*

"There is a second road," Felix said aloud, and watched Wren's eyebrows climb.

"The dragon-road?" Ilya asked sharply.

"No. Older than that. A drover's track, pre-covenant, before the glass." Felix traced the faded line with one finger. "Ovid used it. Which means Veyne may know of it too—but Veyne thinks in roads and armies and timetables. He does not think in runners' shortcuts." They hoped that was true. They staked three lives and a sister on hoping it was true.

Below them, on the plain, the ninth rider in the crescent raised a horn to his lips, and the sound rolled across the Steppe—long, low, and patient. An answering horn sounded from the far side of the drove-road, miles off, where Felix had thought there were no more riders.

Nine had become eighteen while they watched.

"They're sweeping," Wren whispered. "They're sweeping the whole plain like—like beating a carpet."

*Like driving game toward the hunters,* Felix thought, and did not say it, because saying it made it real.

* * *

They came down off the Spine in the last light, moving north through broken glass country where the shards grew tall as wheat and sang faintly when the wind combed them—a thin, eerie ringing, the Steppe remembering the dragon-fire that had made it. Felix had hated the sound the first night they'd crossed such ground, back when it was only strange. Now it sounded like encouragement. Now everything that remembered the covenant sounded like encouragement, and Felix clung to that the way a drowning sailor clings to driftwood.

They made camp in a hollow ringed with standing shards, no fire, sharing dried meat and the last of the fever-tea. Wren took first watch because she claimed she couldn't sleep with the singing anyway, and Felix lay on their back looking up through the gap in the shards at a sky full of indifferent stars, and did the arithmetic they'd forbidden themselves at the ridge top.

Three days of beacon. Five days by the safe road. Eighteen riders with leashed emptiness, and a man with a small precise hand who had killed Felix's family once already and would not hesitate to do it again.

Sleep should have been impossible. But exhaustion is its own mercy, and Felix sank under despite everything, into dreams of the Emberhold's crown—except in the dream the fire wasn't gold, it was Sera's laugh, and it guttered, and guttered, and guttered—

"—up. Felix. *Up.*"

Wren's hand on their shoulder, shaking hard. Her face above them was gray in the starlight and absolutely stripped of everything except fear.

"What—"

"Look east." Her voice was barely breath. "Look at the sky."

Felix rolled over and looked.

Above the distant horizon where the Emberhold stood, where the beacon should have been painting the clouds gold, the sky was wrong. There was a patch of darkness up there that had nothing to do with night—a soft-edged blot, like ink dropped in water, spreading slowly against the stars. And within the blot, the beacon's glow showed through in ragged veins, gold light bleeding through a wound in the world, dimming even as Felix watched.

The barrier. Something had come through the barrier—or the barrier had thinned enough that the Hollow could press against it, show itself, begin the slow work of eating through. Sixty miles away, the oldest fire in the world was losing its fight, and the sky itself was showing the bruise.

"No," Ilya said behind them. Just that. Just *no*, said the way a man says it when the last wall of his life comes down.

Then, from the south—from the direction of the drove-road, of the eighteen riders, of the closed golden hand—a sound that Felix would hear in dreams for the rest of their life, however long that proved to be.

Horns again. But different now. Deeper. Arranged, somehow, almost musical, three notes rising in deliberate sequence, repeated and repeated, rolling out across the dark Steppe from a dozen throats at once.

"What is that?" Wren whispered. "What are they playing?"

Ilya answered, and his voice was dead calm, the calm of a man reading his own death warrant. "It's a Warden advance-call. Full muster. I've only ever heard it described—in the old manuals, from the founding wars." He swallowed. "It means the hunt is over. It means they've stopped searching."

He turned to look at them both, and the starlight caught the brand-new scar on his cheekbone, and the old uniform he still wore because he owned nothing else.

"It means they know exactly where we are."
