## 15. Veyne's Offer

The ridge camp was a bowl of black glass and wind, the kind of place the Steppe made when it wanted to remind travelers that it had once been ocean—fused sand rippling in frozen waves beneath their boots, sharp as broken crockery at every edge. They had made camp in the lee of a shattered waystone, one of the old covenant-markers whose carved dragons had been chiseled blind by Warden chisels a decade ago. Felix had chosen it because the stone still held warmth from something deeper than the sun. Wren had chosen to complain about it anyway.

"Your sister's people built roads under the world," she said, poking the fire with a stolen spoon, "and they couldn't be bothered to put a roof on any of them."

"The roads were for dragons," Ilya said from his place against the waystone. His wound had closed to an angry red seam along his ribs, but he still moved like a man carrying a full bucket of water he refused to spill. "Dragons don't mind weather."

"Lucky them." Wren pulled her coat tighter. She'd stopped shivering three days ago, which Felix knew from the camps was not a good sign. The cold got into steppe-born bones differently. "Felix. Tell me again what we're walking toward."

"A beacon that's dying," Felix said. "A fortress full of Wardens. And possibly my sister."

"And possibly a trap."

"And possibly a trap."

Wren nodded with the grim satisfaction of someone who preferred her bad news confirmed. "Good. As long as we're all agreed."

Felix almost smiled. That was the trouble with found family, they were learning—it gave the fear somewhere specific to live. Ten years ago Felix had feared for no one but themselves, a clean and selfish fear, easy to carry. Now the fear had hands and names. It sat across the fire eating their rations and complaining about the cold.

They were on watch when the lanterns came.

* * *

There should have been no lanterns on the Steppe. That was the first wrongness—a line of lights moving through the glass dark like a procession of small moons, steady, unhurried, utterly unafraid of being seen. Wardens traveled dark or died. Only one kind of traveler lit the night like a festival.

"Chancellor's column," Ilya breathed, and Felix watched ten years of discipline fail him all at once—the flinch, the half-rise, the hand going to a sword belt he no longer wore. "That's not a patrol. That's *him*."

Veyne. Chancellor-Hand Veyne, who had come out of the capital with Tide-lures dragging the Hollow behind his column like hounds on leashes. Felix had seen the aftermath of those lures at the village—the smooth absence where houses and goats and grandmothers had been—and had spent two days telling themselves the Hand would not catch them before the Emberhold.

The lanterns split. That was the second wrongness. Instead of sweeping the ridge, most of the column held position below while a single light climbed toward them, accompanied by perhaps six figures, walking openly, weapons sheathed.

"They know exactly where we are," Felix whispered.

"How?" Wren was already on her feet, knife out, eyes doing the thief's quick arithmetic of escape routes. There were none. Black glass in every direction, and behind them the pass, and beyond the pass—

Then Felix saw what walked at the front of the climbing party, between two Warden escorts, and understood everything.

Wren saw it too. Felix felt it happen to her—the way her whole body went still in a different register than caution. "No," she said. "No, no, that's—" She stopped herself saying the name, thief-instinct, never give them anything free. But her face said it plainly enough: *that's me.*

Except it wasn't. It was a girl in Wren's stolen coat wearing Wren's cocky slouch, and the illusion was good—good enough to fool firelight and distance—but Felix had spent weeks watching the real Wren eat, sleep, lie, and laugh, and the copy had none of her weight. None of her particular gravity, the way the actual girl occupied space like she was daring it to object.

"A glamour," Ilya said quietly. "Or worse. A lure-bait. They've done it before—dressed prisoners as fugitives to draw fire."

"It's not even trying to be her," Felix said. "It's trying to be who we'd expect her to be." Which meant they had studied Wren somehow, or studied reports of her, which meant—

Which meant the real Wren was already taken.

Felix turned. Their bedroll lay where it had lain. Wren's pack was gone. And there, pinned to the ground by a Warden dart through the sleeve of her second coat—the spare she kept rolled as a pillow, the one habit of hers Felix had teased her about only yesterday—was a scrap of paper.

Ilya read it aloud, voice flat as slate. "*Come down alone, speaker, or the next thing we send up won't be wearing her face.*"

"He doesn't need you alone," Felix said. "He wants you alone. Never give a negotiator what he asks for."

"You've negotiated before?"

"I sold charms to smugglers for ten years. Every sale is a negotiation where one side is lying about how much they need the luck-knot." Felix stood, sheathed the oath-sword—not as surrender, but so the blade wouldn't read as a threat across open ground—and looked at each of them in turn. "Ilya, you're the uniform. If this goes wrong, you walk into that column and claim you captured me. He'll want to parade a traitor apprentice more than he wants a dead fugitive. Wren—"

"Wren is coming with you," Wren said.

"No."

"Yes. Because if I stay here I'll do something stupid, and if I come down there I'll also do something stupid, but at least I'll be stupid *near* you." Her chin was up, her knuckles white around the knife. "Also, third option: I circle wide through the glass and cut his horses loose, and when everything goes sideways you both run east and don't look back."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly how I got out of the Salt Gate camps, and I'm still here, aren't I?" She was already moving, low against the glass, and Felix realized with a strange, sinking tenderness that arguing with Wren cost more time than trusting her. "Don't look back," she repeated, and was gone into the dark, a shadow among shadows, the best thing she knew how to be.

* * *

The Chancellor-Hand received Felix in the open, seated on a campaign chair before his tent as though the Steppe were his parlor. He was smaller than Felix had imagined—reports and rumor had built him into a colossus, the iron hand of the Prohibition, and here was a narrow, gray man with careful hands and the patient eyes of someone who had spent a career watching other people make mistakes. The false Wren stood beside his chair. Up close the glamour was worse, its seams showing, the borrowed face slightly too smooth, like a coin worn past its stamping.

"Speaker Kavanagh." Veyne did not rise. "You've been harder to find than your file suggested. Your file suggested a frightened market-charmer. Files are often wrong about the dangerous ones."

"I'm just a charm-seller."

"You were. You're carrying a dragon-road key now, and it's singing loud enough that my Tide-lures taste it from a mile off." He gestured, courteous, to a second chair that had been set facing his—an obvious theater, a merchant's table without the table. "Sit. Please. I promise you this conversation will be the most honest hour anyone has given you in ten years."

Felix did not sit. "Where is she?"

"Alive. Unharmed. Eating my sergeant's stew and insulting his cooking, which suggests she's recovering her strength." Something flickered at the corner of Veyne's mouth—not quite a smile; a ledger-entry. "She's a remarkable creature. Do you know she lifted my signet ring off my own hand while two men were carrying her? I nearly kept her for the audacity. But leverage is leverage, and sentiment is expensive."

"What do you want?"

"Honesty, as promised. So." He folded his careful hands. "You believe you're racing to save the world. The beacon gutters, the barrier fails, the Hollow Tide eats the Steppe and then the coast and then everything. You believe that if you reach the Emberhold and do—whatever the letters told you Ovid to do—you can reignite the fire and seal the breach. Yes?"

Felix said nothing. Silence was a charm too; their mother had taught them that before any knot-work.

"So I'll tell you what's actually true." Veyne leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register of a man discussing grain prices—deliberately mundane, deliberately sane. "The fire cannot be reignited by you. Not by Sera. Not by any speaker alive. The covenant was fed by freely-given oaths, and everyone qualified to give one is either dead, bound, or"—his eyes rested on Felix with terrible gentleness—"ten years out of practice and hunted. What remains at the Emberhold is a candle in a wind tunnel. Days, perhaps. A week if the weather is kind. The barrier fails regardless of what you do. The only variable left is *who is standing where* when it does."

"And the capital gets evacuated," Felix said. "We saw the village. We saw what your order decided was worth saving."

"The capital holds four hundred thousand people. The Steppe holds perhaps sixty thousand, scattered. Any chancellor in history would make the same arithmetic." He said it without shame, which was somehow worse than shame. "I am not here to defend the arithmetic, Speaker. I'm here because I'd rather not perform it. Bring me the map. Bring me whatever cipher your sister left, whatever the courier carried, everything Ovid gave you. Walk away east, take a pardon under a new name—I'll even throw in the girl, since she's clearly become a liability to you rather than an asset—and in exchange, the Emberhold falls quietly. No assault. No Tide-lures turned loose on the approach. No massacre of the loyalists inside, who are, I assure you, mostly starving idealists and one very tired prisoner."

The name landed like a dart through the sleeve.

"Sera," Felix said.

Veyne watched them with the patience of a fisherman who has already felt the line go taut. "Seraphine Kavanagh. Alive. Bound to the beacon-fire as fuel—her oath-magic, siphoned slowly, these ten years, to keep the candle burning. Your sister has been holding back the end of the world with her body, Speaker, and the chancellors who put her there let the world believe she died in the breaking." He spread his hands. "I didn't do it. My predecessors did. But I inherited the arrangement, and I have kept it, because the alternative was letting the fire die years ago. So you see, I am not your villain. I am merely the man keeping the books."

"You keep her caged and call it bookkeeping."

"I keep her *alive*. There is a difference, and you will understand it soon." For the first time something moved behind the gray eyes—not cruelty; exhaustion, deep as a well. "Here is the truth I will give you for free, because unlike my predecessors I prefer my ledgers balanced. If you storm the Emberhold, you will find her. You will embrace her. And then the fire will gutter out days later anyway, with her freed and useless and four hundred thousand people in the capital unevacuated, because you spent our remaining time on a rescue instead of a retreat. Or—" he tapped the arm of his chair, once, softly, "—you give me the map, and I add her rescue to the evacuation schedule myself. She comes west with the last column. I swear it on my office, which is the only thing I have left to swear on."

The wind moved over the glass plain, and far away, south and east, Felix thought they could almost see it—a smudge on the horizon where the sky didn't match itself, the Emberhold's beacon, guttering.

Every word Veyne said fit together. That was the horror of it. The arithmetic was sound. The offer was generous by any standard the last decade had taught them. A pardon. Wren freed. Sera rescued, scheduled, guaranteed. All it cost was the map, the cipher, the chance—the whole wild impossible hope that the letters might mean what they seemed to mean, that the fire could be fed, that the covenant could live again.

All it cost was believing that survival was the same thing as salvation.

"You're right about one thing," Felix said slowly. "This is the most honest hour anyone's given me in ten years. Because you actually believe it. You've run the numbers until the numbers ate the world, and now you think the numbers are the world." They took a step forward, and Veyne's guards shifted, and the false Wren's borrowed face watched with nothing behind it. "My mother sold charms in a market like yours, Chancellor-Hand. Fever knots, luck knots. Cheap things. And she used to say the worst bargain isn't the one where you pay too much. It's the one where you pay everything you have for something that was never for sale."

"The map, Speaker."

"My sister spent ten years as fuel for a lie," Felix said. "Ovid bled out at my stall carrying her words. A boy named Ilya tore his whole life open when he learned what your order buried. A girl who owns nothing risked the only thing she has—herself—to be standing near me tonight. You want me to price all of that in pardons?" They shook their head. "You can't afford it. Nobody can. That's what a covenant *is*, you gray little accountant. It's the debt you can't settle except by honoring it."

For a long moment Veyne simply looked at them, and Felix saw the moment the negotiation ended—not in anger, but in the quiet closing of a ledger. "So be it," he said, and there was no heat in it at all, which was the most frightening sound Felix had heard in ten years. "Then we do it the expensive way."

He snapped his fingers. The false Wren dissolved like breath off glass.

And the horizon answered.

South and east, the smudge that was the Emberhold's beacon flickered—once, twice—and then, impossibly, *flared*, a pillar of gold stabbing up into the night like a shout. Felix's heart lurched toward it before their mind caught up: that wasn't the beacon finding strength.

That was the beacon being *fed*. Fed fast. Fed desperately.

Fed by something—or someone—that had just begun to burn.

Veyne rose from his chair, and for the first time the careful gray man looked genuinely unsettled, staring south with his careful hands gone still. "That's not possible," he murmured. "She doesn't have that much left. She *can't*—"

Behind him, out of the dark, came a whistle—three notes, low and quick, a thief's signal, the one Wren had invented on the dragon-road to mean *run now, ask later.*

Felix ran.
