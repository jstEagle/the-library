## 16. The Bargain Refused

The rope around Wren's wrists was good work—Warden-issue, waxed hemp, the kind that didn't burn easy and didn't stretch. She'd know. She'd cut through a hundred lesser ropes in her time, and she'd spent three days in the Chancellor-Hand's camp learning exactly what this kind felt like against her skin.

Three days of Veyne's voice, smooth as river stone, explaining to her how leverage worked. *A thief is worth nothing dead,* he'd said, turning his wine cup in his long fingers while she sat bound at his feet like something he'd purchased. *But a thief who belongs to the last swordspeaker—that is an asset with interest accruing.* He'd smiled when he said it. Wren had spat at his boots, and one of his guards had broken two of her fingers for it, and Veyne had watched with the mild attention of a man observing weather.

So now her left hand was a swollen ruin and her wrists were raw where she'd been working them against the tent stake all night, and the rope was still holding, and somewhere out beyond the canvas walls Felix Kavanagh was deciding whether to trade a map for her life.

*Don't,* she thought, pressing her cheek against the cold ground, working the knot against the stake's rough iron collar. *Don't you dare trade it. Don't you dare make me the thing that ends this.*

She could hear the negotiation happening. Veyne had chosen the ground carefully—a shelf of rock above the pass, open on three sides, no cover for ambush, the Emberhold's guttering beacon visible far off across the glass like a dying star. She could hear Felix's voice carried up on the wind, low and level, the careful market-stall voice Wren knew so well, the one that never gave away what the hands were doing.

And she could hear Veyne answering, unhurried, certain, a man who had never once in his life been told *no* by someone who meant it.

The knot slipped a fraction. Wren bit down on the sound she wanted to make and kept working.

Here was the thing about being born beside the Steppe, in a camp of salt-gatherers and charm-runners: you learned early that everyone was expendable. The Tide took a village, the Wardens wrote a number, the caravans rolled on. Her mother had died in a ration line and been recorded as a clerical adjustment. Wren had survived by making herself small and quick and worth nothing to anyone—which meant, she understood now, lying in the dirt with broken fingers and rope burns, that she had spent her whole life agreeing with people like Veyne. Agreeing that a street rat was a rounding error. That love was just another currency, and she'd never held enough of it to be robbed.

Felix had ruined that for her. Felix and their stubborn, terrified, infuriating decency. Ilya too, with his cracked-open loyalist faith and his terrible cooking. Even Sera Kavanagh, whom Wren had never met, bound somewhere in the Emberhold's fire—the whole miserable Kavanagh family, alive and dead, teaching Wren that people could be worth dying for.

Which was exactly why she couldn't let herself be the price.

The knot gave. Her right hand came free, then the left, screaming pain through the broken fingers as blood rushed back into them. She lay still, counting breaths, listening.

"—and I will have her delivered to Corrow within the month," Veyne was saying. "Unharmed. You have my word."

"Your word," Felix repeated. Flat. Wren almost laughed. Ten years of hiding from these people, and Felix still couldn't keep the contempt entirely out of their voice.

"The word of the Chancellor's own Hand," Veyne said, "is the only law left between here and the capital. Consider what replaces it, Master Kavanagh. Consider what walks behind my columns even now." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something softer and infinitely worse: satisfaction. "You've seen the lures work. You know what I carry. Give me the map, give me your silence, and the girl goes free—and so do you. All of this—" a gesture Wren couldn't see but could hear in the wind of it, "—simply becomes someone else's problem. Isn't that what you're best at? Walking away?"

Silence. Long enough that Wren's heart climbed into her throat.

Then Felix spoke, and Wren heard it clearly, carried up the rock shelf on the steppe wind, and it was not the market voice at all.

"No."

Just that. No speech, no bargain, no cleverness. Wren closed her eyes.

"You misunderstand your position," Veyne said, and there was a new edge beneath the silk now, the first crack in the river stone. "I have soldiers. I have the Hollow itself leashed to my will. You have a map and three fugitives, one of whom is currently my guest."

"She's not your guest," Felix said. "She's your mistake."

Wren moved.

She'd spent three days watching the camp's rhythms through the tent canvas—guard rotations, cook fires, the sentry who leaned his spear against the tent pole every fourth hour because his shoulder ached. The spear was there now. She took it, silent, and slit the back wall of the tent in one long pull, and came out into the grey pre-dawn light with a stolen blade in her good hand and fury like a second pulse in her throat.

The camp was waking. She had perhaps thirty seconds before someone looked her way, and the rock shelf where they were talking was forty feet up a scree slope, and every guard between here and there would see her.

She didn't go for the slope.

She went for the lures.

They were stacked near the supply wagons—Veyne's terrible cargo, the Tide-lures, iron cages full of something that wasn't quite smoke and wasn't quite absence, each one humming with a wrongness that made her teeth ache. Three Wardens stood watch over them, bored, cold, their backs to her.

Wren had stolen from temples, from nobles, from a Warden quartermaster's strongbox once, which had gotten her exiled from two camps and taught her more about fear than any monster. But she had never stolen anything like this. She crept close in the wagon shadows, picked up a loose cobble, and threw it hard against the far wheel of the second wagon.

The guards turned. Wren opened the nearest cage.

She didn't touch what was inside. She just knocked the latch loose and ran, and behind her she heard the hum change pitch, heard a Warden shout, heard the particular quality of silence that meant men had stopped breathing. Then the shouting started in earnest—not alarm, but terror, the specific animal terror of soldiers whose weapon had turned in their hands.

The Hollow poured out of the cage like water finding a crack, and everything it touched simply stopped mattering. Grass went grey. Stone went soft. A supply wagon folded inward with a sound like a sigh, and the Wardens broke, all discipline gone, scattering away from their own camp while their sergeants screamed orders nobody obeyed.

Chaos was a language, and Wren was fluent.

She took the scree slope at a scramble, using her bad hand on the rocks and paying for it in white-hot sparks up her arm, and came over the lip of the shelf just as Veyne's guards turned toward the burning camp below—and found Felix standing alone at the shelf's center, map extended in one hand, face pale and set, and Wren understood with a sick lurch that Felix had actually been about to give it up. Had been about to hand ten years of truth to the man who'd broken the world, for her.

*No one is expendable,* Felix had told her once, on the glass plains, in the dark, when she'd asked why they'd come back for her in the Warden's Net instead of running. *Not anymore. Not ever again.*

Wren stepped out of the shadow of the rocks.

"Don't," she said.

Every blade on that shelf pointed at her. Veyne turned, slow, and his composure finally, fully cracked—not into rage but into something colder, recalculating, a merchant watching his ledger burn.

"Ah," he said. "The asset delivers itself."

"I'm not an asset." Wren walked forward, past the frozen guards, until she stood beside Felix, shoulder to shoulder, and put her ruined hand into Felix's free one. It hurt so much she saw white. She did it anyway. "I'm family. And family doesn't get traded."

"Wren—" Felix started, hoarse.

"Shut up," Wren said, not looking away from Veyne. "You were going to give him the map. I saw your face. We'll talk about it later, and by 'talk' I mean I'll yell at you across several counties." Her voice shook only a little. "But we don't trade. Not me, not Sera, not the world. He doesn't get to buy us, because we're not for sale. None of us. Not anymore."

Something moved above them—a flicker at the top of the pass road, a figure in a Warden cloak moving fast, and for one horrible heartbeat Wren thought the diversion had failed and the whole column was coming.

It was Ilya.

He came down the switchbacks at a dead run, cloak flying, and Wren saw immediately that something was wrong—he was alone, and he was carrying his commander's seal in his fist like a talisman, and behind him, far up the pass, she could see why he'd run: torchlight. Dozens of torches. The rest of Veyne's force, recalled from some other duty, closing on the pass from the north side.

"They're coming through the Narrows," Ilya gasped, reaching them. "Veyne split the column—I tried to delay them, I burned my name doing it, they know what I am now, they—" He steadied himself, and Wren watched him plant his feet the way he'd planted them in every fight since the waystation, the way a man plants them when he has decided something final. "There's a side path. Old pilgrim track, west of the scree. It reaches the Emberhold's lower gate. But someone has to hold the Narrows or the column reaches the gate first and you're fighting two hundred Wardens instead of twelve."

"No," Felix said.

"Felix—"

"*No.*"

"Somebody has to," Ilya said, and he was calm, terribly calm, and he pulled the seal from his neck and pressed it into Felix's hand. "My commander died trying to expose what Veyne serves. I deserted for this. If I fall holding a pass so the covenant can be reforged, then I'm not a traitor, I'm a Warden—the thing I was supposed to be before I learned what the Wardens became." He almost smiled. "Besides. Someone has to teach Sera Kavanagh that her brother's friends have manners."

"He's right," Wren said, and hated every word. "It's the play. It's the only play."

"It's not the only play," Felix said, and their voice cracked clean down the middle. "There's always another play. There has to be."

"There isn't," Ilya said gently. "You taught me that oaths are freely given or they're nothing. Well. This one's mine, and I'm giving it." He looked at Wren, and then at Felix, and something young and scared crossed his face before he mastered it. "Hold the line for me. Both of you. And if I don't come down the mountain—"

"You'll come down the mountain," Wren snapped, "because if you die I will find whatever afterlife takes ex-Wardens and steal your boots. Every pair. Forever."

That startled a laugh out of him, half-sob, and then the torches topped the ridge and there was no more time.

Ilya turned and went up the pass road to meet them, sword drawn, a single figure walking toward two hundred torches, and Wren grabbed Felix's sleeve and hauled them toward the pilgrim track before Felix could do something noble and fatal.

* * *

The pilgrim track was a knife-edge of old stone stitched along the mountain's flank, half-remembered by the rock itself, and they ran it while the dawn came up bloody behind them. Below and behind, Wren could hear the Narrows: the clash of steel, the shouts, and once—one moment, burned into her forever—Ilya's voice, lifted clear over the battle, bellowing the Warden's own charge-cry back at them like a curse.

"Hold the pass! In the Commander's name, HOLD!"

The column slowed. She could hear it slowing, the disciplined advance stuttering against one man and the narrowness of the stone, and she ran harder, Felix beside her, both of them crying without admitting it, breath tearing, the Emberhold growing ahead of them out of the morning haze—black towers, and above them the beacon flickering like a failing heart.

"He'll hold," Wren panted. "He's the most stubborn person I've ever met and I include myself."

"He learned it from us," Felix said, and their voice was wrecked, and they didn't slow down.

The lower gate of the Emberhold rose before them as the sun cleared the ridge—ancient dragon-wrought stone, warm to the touch even at fifty paces, carved with oaths in a script older than any kingdom. And standing before it, framed in the gateway's shadow, waiting as though she had known the exact minute of their arrival—

Sera.

Alive. Thin as wire, eyes like Felix's eyes, wearing ten lost years like a shroud she'd decided to stop mourning in. She looked at Felix, and at Wren, and past them down the mountain to where one man held two hundred soldiers at bay, and Wren saw her understand all of it in a single glance, the way family understands.

"The fire is nearly out," Sera said. "We have minutes, not hours. And the Hand is already inside—he keeps a back way, he always did." Her gaze went to the pass, to the distant glint of steel, and her voice gentled. "Your friend is buying us those minutes. Let's not waste them."

Wren looked back once. Far below, tiny as an ant, Ilya still stood in the mouth of the Narrows, and the torches had stopped advancing.

She turned away, took Felix's hand with her broken fingers, and walked with them through the gate, into the heat and the dark, toward the last fire in the world—and prayed to every god she'd ever pickpocketed that when the sun set, there would still be four of them to light it for.
