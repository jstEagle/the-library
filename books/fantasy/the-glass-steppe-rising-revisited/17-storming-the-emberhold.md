## 17. Storming the Emberhold

The storm came down off the ridge like something herded.

Felix had seen storms all their life—Corrow's gray drizzles, the salt squalls off the harbor—but never one that *moved with intent*. This one rolled across the Steppe in a slow, grinding wall, lightning crawling through its belly in veins of sickly green, and where it passed, the glass plains went dark and drank the light. The Tide-lures. Veyne's engineers had dragged three of them behind the Warden column like fishers trailing bait, great iron cages full of hollowed things that screamed without sound, and the Hollow answered. It poured out of the barrier's wounds in long black ribbons and braided itself into the weather.

"They're not using it to stop us," Ilya said. He stood at Felix's shoulder at the mouth of the dragon-road, his wound bound in Wren's stolen linen, his face gray but steady. "They're using it to *clear the way*. Veyne doesn't want the Emberhold defended. He wants it empty when he arrives."

"Then we let him clear it for us," Wren said. She was crouched over the map, Sera's cipher spread open on a flat stone, her fingers tracing the route they'd argued over for two days. "The road comes up inside the mountain. Here—" she tapped a mark shaped like a coiled flame "—the old servants' gate. Ovid's letters say it was sealed from within after the breaking, but seals are just locks, and locks are just things that haven't met me yet."

"And between us and the gate?" Felix asked.

Wren looked up. Her grin was thin, but it held. "Between us and the gate is everything he can throw at us. So we go now, while he's still feeding the storm."

* * *

The dragon-road ran warm beneath their boots even as the world above froze into green-lit dark. Felix felt it through the soles of their feet, that old heat, patient as a banked hearth, and felt too the sword at their hip answering it—a low hum that had become a pulse, then a heartbeat, then something that spoke in a language older than words. For ten years the oath-sword had been dead iron. Now it woke by degrees, and each degree frightened Felix more than the last, because waking meant remembering, and remembering meant the night of the breaking rising in them like floodwater.

They climbed. The tunnel narrowed, widened, opened into vaulted galleries where fused glass had run down the walls in frozen waterfalls of amber and violet—the dragon-fire of the forging, still beautiful after a decade of neglect. Wren walked with her head tipped back, drinking it in. Ilya kept his hand on his blade and his eyes forward. And Felix walked with the sword drawn at last, because it would no longer be sheathed; the moment they'd tried, back at the waystation, the scabbard had smoked and spat like a live coal dropped in water.

"It knows where it's going," Felix said quietly.

"Then follow it," Wren said. "It's got better manners than most guides I've robbed."

The first resistance came an hour in: a Warden advance squad, six of them, holding a chokepoint gallery with Tide-lanterns hung on hooks of black iron. The lanterns didn't light the room—they *unlit* it, carving cold absences out of the air, and the Wardens stood in those absences like men wading in a river of nothing.

The sword sang.

Felix hadn't decided to draw it fully—it had simply come free, blazing, and the light it gave was not firelight but *oath-light*, gold-white and absolute, and where it touched the Tide-lanterns' darkness the darkness broke apart like rotten cloth. One Warden raised a halberd; the blade turned it to drifting ash without slowing. Another shouted a warning that died in his throat as the light washed over him—not killing him, Felix realized dimly, but *unmaking the prohibition in him*, the enforced silence every Warden carried, and the man fell to his knees weeping, mouthing words he'd been forbidden to know he knew.

"Go!" Felix gasped, because the effort was enormous, like hauling a drowning world up a rope. "Around them—we're not here to fight Wardens!"

Ilya moved among the kneeling soldiers, pulling lanterns from their hooks and crushing them under his heel. "You were lied to," he told them, one after another, quick and fierce. "All of you. Go home and think about what your silence bought." Then he ran, and they ran after him, and behind them the gallery filled with the sound of grown men crying in a language they'd been taught to forget.

* * *

The servants' gate was exactly as Wren promised: locked, and then not locked. She worked the mechanism with two bent pins and a muttered insult, and the door swung inward onto heat.

Real heat. Not the road's banked warmth but a living, breathing furnace-glow, red-gold light pulsing in slow rhythm through a corridor of black stone, and Felix understood before they saw it that the rhythm was a heartbeat. The Emberhold's heart. The covenant-fire, guttering.

They followed the pulse downward, and the corridor opened into the forge-hall.

Felix had imagined it a hundred times since the cipher first yielded its secret: the great hearth of the Emberhold, where dragons and humans had once sworn as equals, a cavern of basalt and gold where the fire burned high and clean. What they found instead stopped their breath in their throat.

The fire was small. That was the first horror—that the legendary flame, the barrier against the Hollow Tide, the covenant made visible, was no bigger than a cookfire, crouched in the center of a vast cold hearth, flickering like a candle in a draft. And bound to it—

"Sera."

She hung in a lattice of chains and oath-script, suspended above the flame, and the fire fed on her. Felix could see it happening: each time the flame guttered, it drew, and she arched in the chains like a bow being strung, and light passed out of her skin and into the fire. Her hair had gone white at thirty. Her wrists, where the script-bit cuffs bit, were scarred in patterns Felix recognized from their mother's books—*fuel-marks*, the binding-signs of a living sacrifice.

Ten years. Ten years Felix had mourned a sister buried in a mass grave outside Corrow, had laid flowers on an empty plot, had built their whole careful invisible life on top of her grave. And here she was, alive, being burned slowly to keep a lie lit.

"Felix." Her voice was ash and rust, barely hers. Her eyes found them across the hall, and in them was recognition, disbelief, and something worse—fear. "*No.* You shouldn't have come. He'll use you—he's been *waiting* for—"

The doors blew in.

Not opened—blown, torn from their hinges by a fist of green-black wind, and Chancellor-Hand Veyne stepped through the wreckage with the storm at his back and the Hollow curling around his boots like obedient dogs. Behind him, Warden ranks poured into the hall, Tide-lanterns swinging, and the great doors of the far wall groaned as more took position.

Veyne looked at Felix, at the burning sword, at the guttering flame, and smiled the smile of a man whose trap has closed precisely on schedule.

"The last swordspeaker," he said, "delivers himself to the fire. How very convenient."

* * *

Everything happened fast after that, and slow, both at once—the way the worst moments do.

Wren moved first, because Wren always moved first, vanishing into the shadow of a buttress with her knives out. Ilya put himself between the Warden ranks and the hearth, uniform torn open to show his own fuel-mark scars from the pass, shouting something about lies and massacres that the ranks pretended not to hear. And Veyne raised one gloved hand, and the Hollow rose with it, a wave of un-being gathering at the edge of the hall, and Felix understood with terrible clarity that this had always been the plan. Not to capture them. To bring the last oath-bound speaker here, to the failing fire, and let the Hollow take them all in one place—one clean stroke, one final silencing, and the Prohibition complete forever.

"You should have stayed in your market," Veyne said. "Selling luck to fools."

"I did stay," Felix said. They lifted the sword, and it blazed until the whole hall turned gold. "For ten years. You people never left me any choice about the rest."

The Hollow came down.

Felix met it with the oath, the first true words of power they'd spoken since childhood, and the collision was like the sky cracking. Gold against void. The floor fused to new glass. Somewhere in the roar Felix heard Wren screaming their name, heard Ilya's blade ringing against Warden steel, heard—and this, above everything, cutting through it all—a voice cracked and white-haired and unmistakably, impossibly alive:

"*FELIX! The fire! Give it to the FIRE!*"

Sera. Bound, burning, spending herself drop by drop for a decade—and still fighting, still directing, still their sister. The flame leapt toward Felix's voice like a dog hearing its master, and Felix understood, in the half-second the collision bought them, what Sera had known since the courier fell bleeding into their stall:

The fire could be reforged. But only by an oath freely given, and only here, and only now—with Veyne's Hollow pressing in and no time left to be afraid.

Felix turned from the collapsing dark, from the battle, from everything, and ran for the hearth.

Behind them, Veyne began to laugh.
