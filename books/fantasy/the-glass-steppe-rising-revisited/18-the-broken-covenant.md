## 18. The Broken Covenant

Sera had counted ten years by the pulse of a dying fire.

That was the first thing Felix needed to understand, and the hardest to say. Not days, not seasons, not the turning of Warden patrols or the slow rot of the Prohibition—Sera had measured her imprisonment in heartbeats of light. The Emberhold's beacon swelled and shrank like a lung fighting for breath, and she had learned its rhythms the way a prisoner learns the footsteps of her jailer: intimately, hopelessly, with a tenderness that felt like madness.

She sat now on the cold lip of the fire-pit, her wrists still ringed with the pale scars of the binding-chains, watching her brother's face across the coals' dim glow.

Felix looked older than ten years should have made anyone. The careful invisibility they'd worn in Corrow had followed them up the mountain road—a habit so deep it had become flesh, shoulders drawn in, eyes always moving to exits that didn't exist here. But the oath-sword lay across their knees, humming faintly even at rest, and when Felix looked at Sera there was something in their face that no amount of hiding could smother: the raw, unguarded hunger of someone who had spent a decade mourning and refused, still, to believe it.

"You're thinner," Felix said finally. It was such an ordinary sentence. Such a small, human thing to say to a ghost.

"You're selling charms in smugglers' markets," Sera answered, and heard how her own voice had gone strange—rusty from disuse, roughened by smoke and cold air and years of speaking only to dragons who did not answer in words. "Mother would have skinned you."

"Mother's dead."

"Yes." The word fell between them like a stone into deep water. "I know. I've known for ten years, Felix. I've known everything for ten years, and I couldn't tell anyone, because the only people I could tell were the ones who already knew, and the only ones who didn't know were behind a barrier I couldn't cross."

* * *

She told it from the beginning, because there was no other way to tell it. The beginning was not the night of the breaking—that was what everyone believed, that history had a single terrible midnight, a dragon's treachery, a covenant torn in half. The beginning was three years earlier, in a council chamber in Corrow where Sera had stood as junior envoy, the youngest oath-speaker ever seated at the treaty table, taking notes in cipher while chancellors and dragons spoke across a span of polished glass.

"The chancellors weren't afraid of dragons," she said. "Everyone thinks fear was the reason. Fear of teeth, fear of fire, fear of things bigger than us. That was the story they sold afterward. But I was in those chambers, Felix. I watched them for three years. And what they couldn't stomach wasn't the dragons' power." She leaned forward, and the failing fire threw her shadow huge against the far wall. "It was their equality."

The covenant had never been a leash. That was the lie at the root of everything. When the first speakers had sworn it, generations ago, they had sworn as equals—human oath-magic freely given, dragon-fire freely given, each fueling the other in a circle that had no top and no bottom. The barrier against the Hollow Tide was not a wall dragons maintained for frightened humans. It was a shared act, sustained by both bloods, and every renewal required a speaker to stand before a dragon and swear again—not submission, not service, but *partnership*. Freely given, or it fed nothing.

"The fire isn't magic in itself," Sera said. "It's a vessel. Oath-magic is the fuel—the old speakers knew this. A promise made freely, kept faithfully, has weight. Real weight. The kind that can hold back an emptiness that eats the world. Dragons gave their fire as the vessel; we gave our oaths as the flame. For four hundred years it worked, because every generation raised speakers who understood what they were giving and gave it gladly."

"And then?"

"And then Chancellor Aldric Veyne's grandfather sat down with three other men in a room I wasn't supposed to know about, and decided that equality was a disease." Her voice flattened. "They couldn't break the covenant openly. An open breaking would starve the barrier in a season and let the Tide pour through while everyone watched them do it. So they did it slowly. They began restricting which oaths could be renewed. They invented doctrinal objections—said the dragons' demands had grown excessive, that renewal rites needed 'revision.' Each year the revisions cut deeper. Fewer oaths sworn. Smaller ones. Oaths hedged with conditions, because a conditional promise carries less weight, and they wanted less weight given."

"They were starving it on purpose."

"They were starving it *carefully*, on purpose, over three years, so that when it finally guttered they could point at the dragons and say: look, they have withdrawn their fire, they have betrayed us, we must bind all magic before it devours us too." Sera laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "And the night it broke—the night our family died—I was at the Emberhold. I'd come to beg the last renewal. I had the old forms memorized. I stood in this chamber with two dragons and swore the full, unconditioned oath, exactly as our ancestors had, and the fire took it, and flared—" her hands shook; she pressed them flat against her knees— "and flared brighter than it had in years. Because it *worked*, Felix. It worked right up until the moment it was made not to."

The chancellors had been ready. Warden companies moved on the Emberhold within hours—not to defend it but to seize it. The dragons who came to answer the flare were named traitors in proclamations written before they landed. And the speakers—the handful of humans who could still give true oaths, the only people alive who knew the fire could be fed—were killed in a single coordinated night. Our family. The Kavanaghs, who had spoken at every renewal for nine generations.

"They didn't kill me," Sera said quietly, "because I was more useful alive. Do you understand? They bound me to the fire itself. The old chains, the pre-covenant bindings—they dug them out of the archives, the things our ancestors had *replaced*. A bound speaker's oath-magic drains out slowly, drop by drop, keeping the beacon lit past its natural death. Not enough to sustain the barrier properly—the barrier's been failing for a decade, the Tide's been leaking through at the edges—but enough to keep the fire visible. Enough to keep the lie plausible. A burning Emberhold proves dragons betrayed us. A dark one proves nothing at all."

"So they've been bleeding you for ten years."

"Drop by drop." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I'm nearly empty, Felix. That's why the fire is guttering now, faster than their projections allowed. They've milked the last speaker dry, and when I'm gone there is nothing left in the vessel at all, and the Tide will come through like water through rotted cloth."

* * *

Wren had gone quiet somewhere in the middle of it. She sat near the tunnel mouth with her knees pulled up, the thief's stillness gone out of her entirely—she was just a girl now, maybe sixteen, listening to the machinery that had orphaned her described plainly for the first time. Ilya stood apart, his branded face turned toward the wall. He had been a Warden apprentice since he was twelve. He had learned his letters from men who wrote the proclamations Sera was describing.

Felix hadn't moved at all. They sat with the sword across their knees and their eyes fixed on Sera, and Sera recognized the expression because she'd seen it in a mirror for ten years: grief being forged, heat by heat, into something with an edge.

"Ovid," Felix said at last. "The courier. He died getting this map to me."

"He volunteered." Sera's voice cracked on it. "I sent him, Felix. I chose you. I've known for years that the fire would fail before another speaker could be trained—there are no other speakers, they made sure of that—and I spent years trying to find another way. There isn't one. So I sent my brother a map and a death-warrant and called it hope."

"What did you think I could do?"

"I thought you could do what I can't." She rose, unsteady, and walked to the edge of the pit where the beacon's light pulsed its slow, failing rhythm. "Look at it. The vessel is sound. Dragon-fire still pools at the bottom—what's left of it, what the loyalists have smuggled in over the years, feeding it drop by drop from their own reserves. But fire without oath is just fire. It burns and does not hold. What's missing is the fuel. And the fuel has to be given."

"Given by a speaker."

"Given by *the* speaker. The last one." She turned to face him. "A freely-given oath, whole and unconditional, spoken at the heart of the fire. That is the rite. That is the only rite. And here is what the archives say about what it costs, Felix, and I need you to hear all of it before you say anything brave."

She made him hear all of it.

An oath was not words. Words were only the shape of it. An oath was the speaker's whole capacity for binding-promise—the thing that lived in their blood, the gift that had made the Kavanagh name mean something for nine generations. To speak the reforging covenant was to pour all of it into the fire at once. Every drop. Not a tithe, not a renewal, not the slow drip the chancellors had wrung from Sera's chains. Everything, in a single giving, and the giving did not return.

"You wouldn't die," Sera said. "I want to be clear about that, because I spent two years hoping you were dead rather than facing this, and I won't soften it now. You would live. You would walk away from this mountain with your heartbeat and your breath and your stubborn Corrow face intact. And you would never touch magic again. No charms. No luck-knots that work. No cantrips for Wren to learn from. No oath-sword that hums—it would go quiet in your hand and stay quiet for the rest of your life. The gift doesn't come back. It doesn't rest and refill. It's spent, the way a candle is spent, and what remains afterward is an ordinary person holding an ordinary sword."

The silence after that went on so long that the fire's pulse filled it, swell and shrink, swell and shrink, a dying thing breathing.

"That's why they killed the speakers instead of using them," Felix said finally. Their voice was very level. Sera hated how well she knew that levelness; it was the voice Mother used before saying something unforgivable. "One freely-given oath and the whole lie collapses. One speaker at this pit, and the barrier holds for another age, and everyone learns the chancellors murdered a family to prevent it."

"One speaker who *knows the price*, yes. That's the other half of it. The rite fails if it's coerced. It fails if it's deceived. It fails if the speaker gives it hoping to be spared the cost. It has to be free, Felix—freely given, fully known. Which means whoever stands at this fire must choose it with their eyes open, knowing exactly what they're trading away, and choose it anyway." She stepped closer. "Which is why it can't be me. My gift is nearly drained. Ten years in those chains—there's barely enough left in me to light a fever-charm. If I spoke the rite, it would take everything I have and it wouldn't be enough, and the fire would flare for a day and die with both of us empty. It has to be someone whole. It has to be you."

"I haven't spoken a true oath in ten years either. I've been hiding. Selling luck-knots to people who don't know what they're buying."

"You've been *keeping* it. There's a difference. Hiding a gift and spending a gift are not the same wound." Sera crouched in front of her brother, close enough to see the exhaustion under their eyes, the ten years of careful small living written into the set of their mouth. "You are the last oath-bound speaker in the world. Do you understand what that means? Not the best one. Not the most willing one. The *last*. Whatever the Kavanaghs carried, whatever Mother carried and her mother before her—it's sitting inside you right now, unspent, whole, because you were too careful to use it. I used to think that was cowardice." Her voice gentled. "I know now it was preservation. Something in you kept it safe for this."

"Don't," Felix said. "Don't make it fate. Don't dress it up."

"I'm not dressing anything. I'm telling you the bill has come due, and I'm telling you the amount, because you deserve the number before you decide." She took their hands—scarred, charm-scarred, the hands of a market-huckster, and underneath, when she reached with what remained of her own spent sense, the vast quiet reservoir she had felt once before, the night the covenant broke, when she was fourteen and her little brother swore a child's oath to protect their mother and the whole house had rung with it like a struck bell. It was still there. All of it. Untouched.

"It's a terrible price," she said. "And it buys everything. The barrier sealed for another four centuries. The Tide pushed back beyond the Steppe. The dragons free to come home. The Prohibition dead in a single night, because a working covenant makes liars of the chancellors whether they confess or not. And Sera Kavanagh walks out of this mountain, and the family that was murdered gets to be mourned properly instead of erased." She squeezed their hands. "But I want you to hear the worst part too, Felix. The part I've turned over for ten years in the dark. After the rite, you'll be ordinary. And ordinary is what you spent ten years learning to be, and you were *miserable*, and you built a whole careful invisible life around never being it, and I am asking you to become it forever. On purpose. In exchange for a world that outlawed you."

* * *

Nobody else spoke for a long time.

Then Wren, from the tunnel mouth, in a voice smaller than any Sera had yet heard from her: "The cantrip. The one you taught me on the Steppe. Would that be gone too? From them?" She caught herself. "From—them teaching it. Anyone."

"Yes," Sera said gently.

Wren nodded slowly, processing, and said nothing more, but she looked at Felix with an expression that Sera couldn't read until suddenly she could: the look of someone tallying a debt they'd never expected to owe.

It was Ilya who asked the practical question, because soldiers always do. "How long do we have? Before the fire fails regardless."

"A week. Maybe five days." Sera looked back at the beacon. "Veyne knows the timetable better than we do. He's coming, Felix—he has to come. If the fire dies unrenewed, the Tide takes the Steppe and reaches Corrow within a season, and he loses everything he inherited. But if the fire is *renewed*—if a speaker stands at this pit and gives freely—then he loses the lie. He's spent his whole life defending his grandfather's crime. He will burn this mountain and everyone in it before he lets that happen."

"Then we hold the pass," Ilya said simply.

"We're four people and a sword," Wren said.

"We're four people and a sword and the truth," Sera said, "and the truth is going to burn hotter than any of us, if my brother chooses to spend it."

Felix looked down at the oath-sword. The hum in it had changed—deeper now, slower, like something vast turning in its sleep and beginning to wake. Sera watched her brother's hand tighten on the grip, watched them weigh ten years of careful nothing against one enormous everything, watched the exact moment the scales tipped, though they said nothing yet.

Instead Felix asked, very quietly: "When I give it—will I remember what it felt like? Having it?"

Sera thought of the chains. Of ten years of feeling her own gift drain away drop by drop, of knowing precisely what she was losing at every moment, of the strange phantom ache that remained, the way a lost limb aches.

"No," she lied softly. "You won't feel a thing."

Outside, far below the mountain, the horizon flickered with a light that was not dawn—and was not fire.
