## 19. The Last Oath

The Emberhold's heart was a wound in the world.

Felix felt it before they saw it—a cold that came up through the soles of their boots and settled behind the sternum like a swallowed stone. The great forge-hall had been built to hold a sun: vaulted ceilings of dragon-glass ribbed with veins of gold, walls carved with the names of every speaker who had ever sworn at this fire, floor tiles laid in the pattern of interlocked wings. Once it must have been blinding. Now the beacon at its center guttered in a basin of fused sand no larger than a cookfire, a fist of ember-light pulsing slower and slower, like a heart counting down to its last beat.

And around it, seeping through cracks in the ancient seals, the Hollow Tide breathed.

It did not look like smoke or shadow. It looked like *absence*—a place where the eye slid off and found nothing to hold. Where it touched the dragon-glass walls, the gold veins went gray and crumbled into powder that never landed, simply ceased. The silence it made was worse than sound. Felix's ears strained against a nothingness so complete it ached.

"Sera." The name tore out of them raw.

She knelt at the basin's edge, wrists bound in bands of black iron etched with Warden script—the same script Felix had spent ten years learning to fear—and chains of dull silver ran from those bands into the dying fire itself. She was thinner than memory, hair gone white at the temples though she could not have seen thirty winters, but her eyes—her eyes were Sera's, sharp and furious and unbearably alive.

"Felix." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "You stubborn, careful, *cowardly* little ghost. You actually came."

"You sent a dying man to find me with a scorched map."

"I sent the only person I trusted," she said, "because I'm out of time. Look at the fire, Felix. *Look at it.*"

Felix looked. And understood, in one sickening drop of the stomach, what the guttering meant. The beacon wasn't just failing. It was being *drained*—fed through Sera's chains, sipped at drop by drop by men who had discovered that a living speaker could keep a dead lie burning a little longer.

"They've been bleeding you for years."

"Since the breaking," Sera said simply. "I was fourteen. I swore my oath at this fire alongside our parents, and when the chancellors shattered the covenant, the fire began to starve. So they chained the last speaker to it and let her drip." A terrible smile. "Ten years of me. It's not enough anymore. Nothing is enough anymore."

Behind Felix, boots rang on glass. Wren came skidding around the pillar, knife out, eyes huge. "They're coming. All of them. Veyne's got Tide-lures lit and he's driving the Hollow *ahead* of him like hounds—he's not trying to capture anyone, he's going to bury us and the evidence together." She glanced at Sera, at the chains, and something flickered across her face that Felix had never seen there before: not calculation. Horror. "Is that—is she—"

"My sister," Felix said. The word tasted like a decade of ash finally lifting. "Sera. This is Wren. And Ilya—" They turned.

Ilya leaned against the doorway, one hand pressed hard to his side where Veyne's blade had opened him at the pass. His uniform coat was soaked dark, his face gray as steppe-dawn, but he stood, and in his hand he carried his commander's seal—the one that matched the map, the one that had started all of it.

"The archive under the hold," he rasped. "Veyne will come through the west gallery. We have minutes, maybe less. Whatever you're going to do—" he looked at Felix, and there was no doubt left in the young Warden's face, only a terrible clarity, "—do it now."

* * *

Felix drew the oath-sword.

It woke the moment it cleared the scabbard—not humming now but *singing*, a low golden note that rolled through the hall and made the dying flame lean toward it like a starving thing toward bread. Along the blade, script surfaced, letter by letter: the covenant-words, the ones every speaker learned as children and every speaker since the Prohibition had been forbidden even to whisper.

Felix had not spoken them aloud in ten years. Had barely allowed themselves to remember them. Magic had killed their family—or so the world said; so the Wardens said; so Felix had needed to believe, because believing anything else meant the whole shape of their grief had been built on a lie.

They walked toward the fire. Toward Sera. Each step echoed.

"Felix." Sera's voice dropped low, urgent, stripped of everything but truth. "Before you do this—you need to know what it costs. The fire was forged from freely-given oaths. Human and dragon, equal to equal, each pouring in a piece of their own soul's binding. When the chancellors broke it, they didn't extinguish it—they *unbalanced* it. It needs an oath again. A full one. Freely given, completely given." Her jaw tightened. "Not a cantrip. Not a charm. Everything. Every thread of magic in you, poured out until there's nothing left to pour. You'd be giving up the last of what Mother and Father gave us. You'd be ordinary. Forever."

Felix stopped at the basin's rim. The ember-light pulsed against their face, once, twice—slower now. Behind them, faint and growing, came the sound of boots and the wet wrongness of the Tide being driven forward like cattle.

"I sold luck-knots for copper," Felix said quietly, "for ten years. Little knots of nothing. Enough magic to warm a feverish child, to turn a thrown die. Do you know what I told myself? That it was survival. That staying small was how I honored them—by hiding, by surviving, by keeping my head down in a market stall while the world went gray." Their hand tightened on the hilt. "But it wasn't honor. It was fear wearing honor's clothes. And I am done being afraid."

"Felix—"

"You said *freely given*." They met their sister's eyes. "This is me, giving freely. For the first time in my life."

Sera made a sound that was half laugh and half sob. "We really are Mother's children."

* * *

Veyne came through the west gallery like winter coming through a broken door.

He did not run. Chancellors' Hands did not run. He walked, flanked by six Wardens whose faces were pale and set, and ahead of him—leashed on chains of script that smoked where they touched his gauntlets—the Hollow Tide poured into the hall like water finding its level. It spread across the dragon-glass floor in a slow tide of un-being, and where it passed, the names carved into the walls vanished one by one. Speakers erased. History erased.

"Felix Kavanagh." Veyne's voice was calm, almost kind, which was somehow the most obscene thing about him. "You've read the letters by now. You know what your sister knows. And still you crawl back to the fire that ate your family." He gestured, almost gently, at the guttering beacon. "Look what magic does. Look what it has always done. Give me the sword. Give me the map. Take your sister and go, and I will let the past stay buried—for all our sakes."

"You'd let the Tide take the Steppe," Felix said. "You'd let it take Corrow eventually. You'd evacuate the capital and call it governance."

"I would save *people*," Veyne said. "People are not magic. People are what remains when the magic is taken away. That is the whole of my creed, child, and it has kept the realms standing for ten years."

"No." Ilya pushed himself off the wall, swaying, hand red at his side. His voice cracked but held. "It hasn't kept anything standing. It's kept *you* standing. My commander found the archives, Chancellor-Hand. She found the chancellors' own signatures on the breaking. She died for a piece of paper, and you burned her name out of the rolls and called it justice." He raised the seal high. "I'm holding her name right here. And I'm not letting go of it ever again."

Something moved behind Veyne's composure—just a flicker, just a hairline crack. Then his gaze went cold and final. "Take them all. Burn the hall after."

The Wardens hesitated. Six of them, looking at a bleeding deserter, a thief with a knife, two speakers at a dying fire—and then looking at the Tide spreading at their own chancellor's feet, close enough now that its silence lapped at their boots. One of them took a single step backward. That was all it took. The line wavered like a candleflame.

Veyne snarled and *drove the lures down*.

The Tide surged.

* * *

What happened next, Felix would remember forever in fragments, because time itself seemed to tear along the seams.

Wren moving—not away from the Tide but *alongside* it, quick as a market-cutpurse, slashing the lure-lines from Veyne's gauntlets while he was focused on Felix. The Hollow, suddenly unleashed, curling upward in confusion, seeking the nearest source of binding-magic—

—which was the fire. Which was Sera. Which was Felix.

Ilya stepping into its path with his commander's seal raised like a shield, roaring words from the old Warden litany, the true litany, the one about guardians and equals, and the Tide recoiling from the seal as if struck—

Sera screaming, "NOW, FELIX!"

Felix drove the oath-sword into the basin.

Into the fire, through the fire, down to the fused sand at its heart, and spoke.

The words came up from somewhere deeper than memory—from the place where their mother's voice lived, where their father's hands had guided theirs over practice-swarms of light, where every charm they'd ever sold for copper had been a tiny, cowardly echo of this. The covenant-words. Not the mangled version in the Warden catechism, not the Prohibition's twisted parody, but the true forging, human to dragon to fire:

*"By freely given word and freely given self,
I bind my flame to yours and yours to mine.
Not master to servant. Not cage to captive.
Equal to equal. Breath to breath.
Let what I pour be poured without reserve,
and let the fire remember what we swore."*

Magic left them.

There was no other word for it. It rose out of Felix like heat off summer glass, like blood from a wound, every thread of it at once—the oath-bond in their bones, the small bright craft in their fingertips, the deep silent reservoir they hadn't known they carried until they felt it draining. It hurt. It hurt like being unmade politely. And underneath the hurt was something else, something that felt shockingly like relief, like setting down a weight carried so long the shoulders had forgotten its absence.

The fire *drank*.

The guttering fist of ember-light flared—flickered—took. Golden light raced up the sword-blade, along the veins of dragon-glass, across the ceiling ribs, and the carved names on the walls blazed back into being, letter by letter, speaker by speaker, the whole murdered history of the covenant igniting like dawn along a mountain range. The Tide shrieked—it had no mouth, and it shrieked anyway, a silence so total it burst eardrums—and recoiled, and recoiled, and the seals in the floor blazed and *sealed*, and the emptiness that had leaked into the world for ten years was pushed back, back, back through the cracks and closed out.

In the center of it all stood Veyne.

He had raised his hands against the light. But the light was not light only—it was *oath*, and oaths have judgments in them. The covenant-flood broke over him like a wave over a seawall, and it found every lie he had ever spoken in service of the Prohibition, every name erased, every speaker hunted, every village abandoned to the Tide, and it held them up to him, all at once, in the space of a single heartbeat.

Felix saw his face in that final moment. Saw understanding arrive—horrible, total, too late—and saw him open his mouth either to deny it or to confess, and never learn which, because the lie he had served consumed him from the inside out. He came apart like smoke in wind. Like a name carved from a wall. The Wardens who remained fell to their knees, and none of them, afterward, could say whether they had watched him die or watched him simply *stop having ever mattered*.

Then it was quiet.

Real quiet—the good kind, the kind with air in it. The beacon roared in its basin, tall and gold and steady, and the warmth of it reached the far walls for the first time in a decade. High above, through the shattered roof-glass, something vast passed between Felix and the stars: a wingbeat. Another. Dragons, answering a fire they had not heard in ten years, circling down out of the dark like the sky itself coming home.

* * *

Felix came back to themselves on the floor, on their knees, the oath-sword lying beside them—just steel now. Plain, cool, silent steel. They reached for the small craft reflexively, the way a tongue seeks a missing tooth, and found nothing. No hum. No warmth. No thread.

Ordinary. Forever.

It should have felt like loss. Instead, strangely, it felt like standing upright for the first time in years.

Hands caught them—two sets. Sera, free of the dead chains, iron bands fallen open, pulling Felix's head against her shoulder with a fierceness that bordered on violence. And Wren, kneeling in the glass dust, laughing and crying at once, saying something about "you absolute madman, you absolute *market-stall* madman," and beyond her Ilya sliding slowly down the pillar, pale and grinning, holding the seal against his chest like a talisman.

"You did it," Sera whispered into Felix's hair. Her voice broke entirely. "Ten years I kept the fire fed with drops of myself and told myself someone would come. Ten years, and I'd started to stop believing it." She pulled back and gripped Felix's face in both hands. "You gave everything."

"Not everything," Felix said. Their voice sounded strange to their own ears—lighter. They looked around at the three of them: a sister returned from the dead, a thief who had chosen to stay, a deserter bleeding out his loyalty at last in the right direction. "Turns out I had more than I knew. Turns out some of it wasn't magic at all."

Above them, the dragons wheeled, and their shadows crossed the blazing hall like blessings. At the far doors, the remaining Wardens still knelt, uncertain, unarmed—and at their head, hesitantly, the one who had stepped backward first rose to his feet and removed his helmet.

"What," he asked the burning hall, the returning fire, the four battered figures at its heart, "do we do now?"

Felix looked at Sera. Sera looked at Felix. And for the first time since a dying courier had bled out at a smugglers' stall, Felix Kavanagh smiled.

"Now," Felix said, rising on legs that trembled, reaching down to help Wren up, "we go home—and we tell everyone the truth."
