## 20. Ash and Ember

Wren woke to the smell of rain on hot stone, which was impossible, because it had not rained on the Steppe in ten years.

She lay still for a long moment, cataloguing her body the way she'd learned to in the Warden camps—counting what hurt, deciding what was broken. Ribs: bruised, both sides. Left arm: scraped raw from the pass, where she'd dragged Ilya's dead weight behind a fallen pillar while the Hollow screamed through the stones above them. Throat: hoarse from shouting Felix's name into fire that had seemed, for one endless moment, about to swallow them all.

Alive. All of them alive. That was the part she kept having to check, like a pickpocket counting the same purse twice because she couldn't believe her luck.

She sat up on the pallet they'd made for her in one of the Emberhold's lower chambers—a room that had once been a guard barracks, judging by the weapon racks still bolted to the walls, empty now except for dust and a single dragon-scale shield no human hand could have lifted. The stone under her palm was warm. Not fever-warm. Hearth-warm. The whole mountain was breathing again.

Felix was in the corridor.

Wren found them sitting on the floor with their back against the wall, legs stretched out, an ordinary sword lying across their knees. Not the oath-sword—that was gone, spent, its blade gone grey as old ash where it had once hummed with covenant-light. This was a plain soldier's sword they'd taken off a rack somewhere, the kind of thing you could buy in any Corrow market for six coppers and a bad attitude. Felix was turning it over slowly, looking at it the way a man looks at a stranger wearing his brother's coat.

"You're awake," Felix said, without looking up. Their voice was rough as gravel.

"Barely." Wren slid down the wall beside them. Up close she could see what the fire had taken. It wasn't wounds. It was something underneath the skin—Felix had always carried themselves like a drawn bowstring, even selling luck-knots in the smugglers' market, even running, even bleeding. Some tension lived in them that Wren had assumed was just how people were built. It was gone now. Felix sat loose and ordinary, like anyone, like nobody, like a person who had set down a weight so heavy they'd forgotten they were carrying it until their arms floated strange without it.

"Does it hurt?" Wren asked. "The—the empty part."

Felix considered the question honestly, which was more than most people would have done. "It's quiet," they said at last. "Ten years of hearing something at the edge of everything—like a word stuck behind your teeth. And now it's just..." They turned the plain sword in the light. "Quiet. I keep reaching for it and finding my own hands instead."

"That sounds terrible."

"It sounds," Felix said, "like being done." And then, after a moment, with a small crooked smile that Wren had never seen on them before and suspected was brand new: "I tried a cantrip this morning. The warming one. The one I taught you on the glass."

"And?"

"Nothing. Not a spark. I warmed my hands by blowing on them, like a farmer." Felix laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them, half wonder and half grief. "Do you know how long it's been since I blew on my own hands?"

Wren didn't know what to say to that, so she did what she'd learned to do on the Steppe, which was nothing. She leaned her shoulder against theirs and let the silence be warm instead of empty. Below them, deep in the mountain, the great fire breathed. She could feel it in the floor, in her teeth, slow and vast as a sleeping animal.

"I thought you were going to die," she said finally. Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. "When Veyne opened the Tide into the hold. When you started speaking the oath and your eyes went—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I've watched a lot of people die, Kavanagh. Camps are good teachers. I know what it looks like when someone spends the last of themselves. You looked like that."

"I know."

"And then you didn't."

"No," Felix agreed softly. "Then I didn't." They looked down at their hands—plain hands, scarred, charm-seller's hands, calloused from ten years of knot-work and coin-counting and hiding. "Sera says the oath doesn't take everything. It takes what you give freely. I gave it all—I meant to give it all—but apparently there was a piece of me holding back without asking my permission. The piece that wanted to see what happened next." They glanced sideways at Wren. "The piece that wanted to see who stole my charms out from under my nose and had the nerve to look proud about it."

"Someone had to keep you supplied," Wren said. "You were terrible at inventory."

They sat together while the mountain breathed.

* * *

By midday—or what passed for midday; the Emberhold's high windows showed a sky scrubbed clean by last night's storm—they climbed to the outer terraces, because Sera sent word that the dragons were coming.

Wren had seen a dragon exactly once before, from very far away, the year before the Prohibition ended for her family in every way that mattered: a shape against the sun, vast and brief, gone before the camp elders finished arguing about whether it was an omen or a bird. She had expected, standing on the terrace with the wind pulling at her hair, to feel fear. That was what the Wardens had taught everyone to feel. Dragons were the reason magic was outlawed. Dragons were the reason for the camps, the checkpoints, the burned villages, the whole architecture of fear she'd been born inside.

The first shadow crossed the plain below, and Wren felt no fear at all.

She felt awe, which was worse, in a way—awe went straight past all your defenses and took your breath hostage. There were seven of them, descending out of the high clouds in a long spiraling line, wings like sails catching light, scales throwing back the sun in colors Wren had no names for: ember-red, glass-green, one the deep bruised purple of a storm's underside. They came down onto the Steppe before the Emberhold's gates with a gentleness that undid her—creatures the size of cathedrals landing like herons in shallow water, folding their wings with a sound like wind through a canyon, lowering their great heads to the ground.

To the ground. To *her level*. To the level of the ragged, exhausted, bandaged humans standing on the terraces.

"They remember," Sera said.

She stood a little apart, and Wren studied her sidelong, the way she studied everyone—old habit, survival habit. Sera Kavanagh was gaunt and grey-skinned from ten years bound to the failing fire, and she moved like someone relearning her own body, but her eyes were clear, and when she looked at the dragons there was no fear in her either. Only recognition, worn smooth by time.

"The oldest ones held the far side of the covenant," Sera said. "The side the chancellors never wrote down. When the fire guttered, they couldn't cross the barrier—but they could wait. Ten years, they circled the Steppe's edge, waiting for the fire to rise again." Her voice caught, steadied. "The one in front—Vessith—she taught the first oath-speakers. She knew our grandmother's grandmother. She says—" Sera laughed, wet-eyed, disbelieving. "She says we've grown *small*, and she means it kindly."

The lead dragon raised her head. From the terraces she was a mountain range given opinions, and when she spoke, her voice arrived not through the ears but through the bones, through the warm stone, through the ground itself:

**LITTLE OATH-KEEPER. THE FIRE STANDS.**

And every soul on the terraces—survivors of the Emberhold garrison, steppe-folk who'd drifted in overnight from villages the Tide had spared, Wren herself, Felix, Ilya propped upright between two crutches with his bandages showing red at the edges—every one of them felt the words land like sunlight.

Sera stepped forward to the terrace's edge, alone, and Wren understood what was happening a heartbeat before it happened. Understood why Sera had asked Felix to stay back, why she wore the scorched courier's map-case slung across her chest like a badge of office.

"Sera," Felix said quietly. "You don't have to—"

"Yes I do." Sera glanced back at them, and for a moment the two siblings looked at each other across ten years and one sacrificed oath, and something passed between them that Wren wasn't meant to read and read anyway: *you gave the fire; let me give it a voice.* "Someone has to stand between the Steppe and the realms and say the true thing out loud. You've done enough true things for one lifetime, little sibling. Let me do this one."

She turned to face the dragons, and the wind, and the gathered survivors, and she spoke—not loudly, but the mountain carried it, the way it carries everything now, the way it will carry it forever, into the warm stone and out through the glass plains to every ear the Steppe has.

"My name is Sera Kavanagh," she said. "My family died in the breaking of the covenant, and I did not. For ten years I fed a fire I was told was a prison, and learned it was a promise. The chancellors lied. The dragons did not break the covenant—we broke it, we humans, out of fear of sharing the world—and the lie we told cost us the Tide, the Prohibition, and ten thousand lives." She drew a breath. "I am the Steppe's voice now, if the Steppe will have me. And the first thing this voice says is: the age of hiding is over."

The lead dragon bowed her head—an inch, no more, but from a creature that size an inch was a bow that would live in songs. And around the terraces, ragged and battered and weeping without shame, the survivors began to cheer.

Wren cheered too. Then she stopped cheering, because she caught sight of Felix's face, and Felix was crying—openly, unguardedly, the way Wren had never once seen them cry in all their careful hidden months—and Wren decided that some things were private even in public, and put her arm through theirs, and stood with them while their sister became a legend twenty feet away.

* * *

Evening found the four of them in the barracks chamber, sharing a meal that was mostly dried steppe-mutton and a hard cheese one of the survivors had produced from nowhere, because armies and refugees run on the same miracle. Ilya ate like a man making up for lost time, propped against the wall, his wounded leg stretched out, his Warden uniform gone—traded that afternoon for a plain steppe-cotton shirt two sizes too big, which made him look, Wren thought, approximately twelve years old.

"They'll court-martial me in absentia," he said contentedly. "Traitor to the order. Struck from the rolls. My mother will disown me in a very formal letter."

"Your mother," Wren said, "raised you to walk toward burning buildings. She'll get over the letter."

He smiled, then winced, then smiled anyway. Outside the narrow window, the sky was doing something Wren had never seen: dragons crossing the sunset in ones and twos, unhurried, their shapes black against the gold, and below on the plain the refugee fires were springing up in answer, dozens of them, a constellation come down to earth. Nobody flinched at the shadows overhead. She kept noticing that. Every time a wing-shape crossed the firelight, some part of her braced for screaming, and every time, the screaming didn't come. Just faces tilting up. Just people watching the sky like it belonged to them again.

Felix had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-conversation, the plain sword across their knees. Wren watched Sera notice, watch Felix sag, and move—slowly, carefully, a body still remembering itself—to ease them sideways onto a bedroll without waking them. Felix murmured something. Sera said, "Hush. I've got the watch." And Felix, ten years older than the last time those words would have applied, obeyed their big sister and slept.

Wren looked away, because her throat had gone tight, and found Ilya watching her watching them.

"What?" she said, defensive on principle.

"Nothing." He turned his cheese over in his fingers. "It's only—I spent three years learning to hunt people like you. Like all of you. And now I'm eating their cheese in a dragon's house, and I keep waiting to feel like I've made a mistake." He looked up. "I don't. Isn't that strange? I should feel like a traitor. I feel like I just got home."

Wren thought about the camps. About stealing luck-knots from a charm-seller's stall because a fever charm was the difference between her bunkmate living and dying, and the charm-seller catching her at it, and instead of calling the Wardens saying, *next time ask, and pay when you can.* About the way "family" had always been a word that meant *the people who die and leave you.*

"Yeah," she said. "Strange like that."

Later, when the others slept, she climbed alone to the highest terrace, because she wanted to see it once with nobody else's breathing in her ears. The Emberhold's beacon rose behind her, steady now, a pillar of gold fire climbing into the dark where for ten years it had flickered like a dying candle. Far out on the glass plains, the horizon glowed faintly where the barrier ran—a seam of light across the world, whole again, holding. Somewhere beyond it, the Hollow pressed and raged and got nowhere.

Wren sat down on the warm stone, pulled her knees up, and let herself feel it, all of it, the exhaustion and the terror and the unbearable tenderness of being alive when the arithmetic had said otherwise. She'd spent her whole life traveling light—one stolen bag, one false name, nothing you couldn't run with. But sitting here she took inventory, the honest kind, and found herself carrying things that weighed nothing and everything: a charm-seller who'd blown on their own hands like a farmer and laughed about it. A giant of a traitor who cried at cheese. A sister returned from the dead who'd just invented a new job for herself out of sheer nerve. A mountain that breathed.

A family that had chosen itself, out on hostile ground, one wary transaction at a time.

Down in the valley, a dragon's voice rolled across the plain—not words, just sound, a low thrumming note like a struck bell, and the refugee fires flickered in answer, and Wren laughed out loud at the sheer ridiculous size of the world.

She was still laughing when she heard footsteps on the stair behind her, and turned, expecting Felix—

—and saw instead a mud-spattered runner in courier's leathers, gasping, holding out a folded paper sealed with the Chancellor's own crest, saying the words that would send them all down the ambassador's road before the week was out:

"You're the ones who reignited the Emberhold? Then you'd better read this. Corrow's council is voting on whether to burn every charm-seller in the city—and they're using *your name* to do it."
