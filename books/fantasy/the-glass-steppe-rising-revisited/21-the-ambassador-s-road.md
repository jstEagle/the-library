## 21. The Ambassador's Road

The road back to Corrow was not the same road they had left by.

Felix had known this would be true in the way one knows a scar will ache before rain—an abstract certainty that turned out to be nothing like the thing itself. The salt gate still stood, its basalt teeth worn smooth by ten years of caravans, but the Wardens' checkpoint beside it was abandoned, its signal-lamp dark, its ledger desk overturned and half-buried in blown sand. A child's chalk drawing of a dragon adorned the wall where once the Proclamation had been nailed: *MAGIC IS FORBIDDEN. THE WARDENS WATCH.* Someone had crossed out the second sentence and written beneath it, in careful schoolroom letters, *the dragons watch too.*

"They're going to need better graffiti," Wren said, squinting at it from atop the cart. "Half of Corrow can't read."

"Then it's lucky," Felix said, "that I'm bringing them an ambassador who can."

The title still sat strangely on their shoulders, like a coat cut for someone taller. *First Ambassador of the Renewed Covenant*—Sera had pronounced it with her old ceremonial gravity, the gravity she'd learned as chancellor-daughter before the world broke, and then ruined it entirely by grinning. Wren had suggested alternatives, most unprintable. Ilya, his arm still bound and slung against his chest, had simply said, "They'll listen to you. They never listened to us." Coming from a Warden, it had landed with the weight of confession.

Felix drove the cart through the gate without stopping, because there was no one left to stop them, and felt the strangest sensation of walking through a door that had forgotten how to be locked.

* * *

Corrow had always been a city of thresholds—the smugglers' market under the aqueduct, the charm-sellers' row behind the tanneries, all the dim places where forbidden small magics changed hands for copper. Felix had spent a decade being invisible in those places, and they had expected, returning, to slip back into invisibility like a well-worn glove.

Instead the city saw them.

It began at the fish-quaes, where a woman selling smoked eel looked up from her stall and went white. "You're Kavanagh," she breathed. "You're the one who lit the beacon." By evening it was everywhere—that word, *beacon*, passed stall to stall, because no one in Corrow had a word for what had happened on the ridge above the Emberhold except the ones who'd seen the sky turn gold, and even those reports arrived garbled, magnified, mythologized already. Some said the fire had been visible from the capital. Some said the Hollow Tide had been thrown back across the Steppe like a blanket snapped clean. Some said a dragon the size of a cathedral had carried three humans out of the burning hold in its claws.

"Two humans and one very offended thief," Wren corrected anyone who would listen, which was everyone. She had discovered that fame, properly managed, could be monetized, and had begun charging children a button apiece to hear the story told correctly.

But it was not the stories that undid Felix. It was the small things. The luck-knots appearing on doorposts—not hidden now, not smuggled, but hung openly, braided red thread swinging in the sea wind off the quaes. An old man pressing a fever-charm into Felix's hand in the street, saying, "Made this myself. First one in ten years. Is it—does it work? Can you tell?" And Felix turning the clumsy knot over, feeling not a whisper of magic in it, magicless as a river stone, and saying honestly, "It works because you believed it would. That was always half of it."

That was the truth they were still learning to live inside: Felix Kavanagh had given everything to the fire, and what remained was ordinary. Their oath-magic was gone—the deep hum that had lived in their bones since childhood, the current that made a charm a charm instead of string. They had poured it into the covenant like wine into a wound, and the wound had closed, and the wine was gone. Some mornings Felix woke reaching for it the way an amputee reaches for a missing limb, and found only silence, and lay very still until the silence became bearable.

But here was the other truth, the one Sera kept repeating at dinner like a lesson neither of them had ever learned: *you were never only the magic.* The charms Felix had sold for ten years had been mostly craft anyway—knots tied in the old patterns, herbs bundled at the right hour, words spoken with the right weight. The Prohibition had outlawed the spark; it had never quite managed to outlaw the skill. And skill, unlike magic, could be taught, traded, argued before a council.

Which was why, eleven days after coming home, Felix stood in the Council Chamber of Corrow for the first time in their life, about to negotiate with frightened men.

* * *

The Council of Corrow met in the old customs house, beneath a ceiling painted with merchant ships that had gone gray with smoke and neglect. There were nine councilors. Seven looked like what they were: warehouse owners and guild-masters who had spent the war years hoarding grain and calling it prudence. The eighth was Magistrate Corven, who had administered the Prohibition in Corrow for a decade with the bland efficiency of a man stamping papers, and whose hands, Felix noted, did not quite stay still on the table.

The ninth was empty—a chair draped in black cloth—and when Felix asked, Corven said stiffly, "Councilor Ames. He spoke for opening the gates. He died last week. Fever."

"Fever," Wren muttered from her place against the wall, "or the same fever that killed the last four people who spoke for opening the gates," and Ilya, standing at parade rest in a uniform he had deliberately not burned, put one warning hand on her shoulder.

Felix had come unarmed, if one didn't count the luck-knots.

They had brought a full satchel of them—forty-one charms, each one tied by hand in the weeks since the Emberhold, each one dead of magic and alive of craft. They laid them out on the council table one at a time, narrating as they went, and the narration was the performance, though it was also, entirely, the truth.

"This is a fever-charm. Willow bark and bog-myrtle, bound in the Kellish pattern. It does not cure fever. It reminds the body what fighting fever feels like, and it gives the sick person something to hold while they do it. My mother sold these for twenty years. No one who bought one from her ever died alone."

"This is a storm-knot, for fishermen. It cannot calm a gale. It can be counted in the dark, so a man hauling nets knows how long between lightning and thunder, and whether the storm is coming or going."

"This is a grief-braid. It does nothing whatsoever. It is for holding."

Councilor Vask, a wool merchant with a face like a closed ledger, leaned forward. "These are trinkets. Pretty trinkets. What has this to do with—" he gestured upward, vaguely, at the sky, at the whole impossible changed world, "—with *dragons*?"

"Nothing," Felix said. "That is precisely my point. You are afraid that reopening the covenant means sorcerers burning down your warehouses and dragons eating your children and every bargain you've ever struck becoming worthless overnight. So I have brought you forty-one objects that contain no magic at all, and I am asking you to legalize them."

Silence. Somewhere above, a gull screamed against the painted ships.

"The Prohibition never stopped the charms," Felix went on. "I know, because I sold them. For ten years. In your market, under your aqueduct, while your Wardens looked the other way for a copper or a kindness. Every family in the low quarter owns at least one thing they shouldn't. The law did not protect you from magic. It only decided who had to be afraid of whom." They let that sit. Then, more gently: "The dragons are not asking for your fear. Believe me—I have met them. They are asking for the thing the covenant actually was, before the chancellors broke it. Not obedience. Not worship. *Partnership.* Shared roads. Shared weather-craft for your fishing fleets. Healings your physicians cannot manage. In exchange, safe passage through your skies, fair trade at your quaes, and the right of any dragon-blooded child born in your city to grow up unhidden."

"And the Tide?" This was Magistrate Corven, quiet, precise. "The reports say the barrier holds. Reports also said the barrier would hold ten years ago."

"The barrier holds because it was reforged," Felix said. "By an oath freely given, at a price I will not describe in this chamber, paid by someone you are currently deciding whether to arrest. It will hold for a generation, perhaps two, if the covenant is renewed widely enough that it does not depend on any single flame. That is what I have come to offer you: not my protection. Your own part in it."

Corven studied Felix for a long moment. His eyes went to the black-draped chair, and then to Ilya's Warden grey, and something moved behind his face—calculation, yes, but underneath it, exhaustion. Ten years of stamping papers. Ten years of the fever that took Councilor Ames taking others quietly, when no one was watching.

"You understand," Corven said at last, "that the capital will call this treason."

"The capital," Wren said from the wall, "is currently governed by whoever wasn't eaten by the lie they served. We checked."

"*Wren,*" Felix said, and then, because some things needed saying plainly, turned back to the council. "Yes. It may be called treason. But I watched the Hollow Tide eat a village in the time it takes to say grace, and I watched your Wardens' orders, and their orders were to evacuate the capital. Corrow was not on the list. If the fire had failed—if we had been three days later—you would not be sitting here debating trade terms. You would be ash on the Steppe. So yes: call it treason. Or call it the first honest bargain this city has made in a decade, and sign it."

The silence stretched. Old Vask picked up a fever-charm, turned it over in his soft merchant's hands, and did not put it down.

* * *

It took six hours. It took, in the end, less argument than tea—Felix noticed that councils were much like markets, and that no one signed anything hostile while drinking good bog-myrtle brew from a charm-seller's kettle. Wren, bored senseless by clause fourteen, fell asleep against Ilya's good shoulder and woke to find him carefully not moving.

What they signed, when the wax finally cooled, was modest, and that was its genius. Corrow would recognize the renewed covenant. Charm-craft was legalized—not the old wild magics, not yet, but the knots and braids and bundles, licensed and taxed like any other trade. A dragon-road waystation would be built beyond the salt gate, on ground the city owned and had never used. And a certain Felix Kavanagh, formerly fugitive, formerly charm-seller, was recognized as ambassador plenipotentiary of the Emberhold covenant, with the right to speak for dragonkind in all matters of trade and passage within Corrow's walls.

Vask shook Felix's hand at the end, gruffly, and pressed the fever-charm back into their palm. "For your trouble," he said. "It's probably worthless."

"It's worth exactly what someone believes it's worth," Felix said. "Welcome to the market."

Outside, dusk had come down over the harbor, and the lamps of the low quarter were lighting—and among them, unmistakable now, the small red glimmers of luck-knots hanging in doorways, dozens of them, dozens more than there had been that morning, as if the whole quarter had been waiting to see which way the wind would blow before daring to braid thread in the open.

Wren stood on the customs house steps and looked out at it with an expression Felix had never seen on her before—unguarded, young, almost frightened. "Ten years I stole those things," she said. "Risked my neck for them. And now they're just... *hanging there.* Like it was never even illegal."

"It was always legal," Felix said. "It just hadn't been admitted yet."

"That's the stupidest sentence I've ever heard, and I've heard you explain taxes."

Behind them, Ilya laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering oil. He was thinner than he had been at the pass, and his arm would never fully straighten, and he wore the Warden grey still, though he had taken the badge off and had not explained why. "Ambassador," he said, and there was dry irony in it, but underneath the irony, something else. Something that sounded almost like *thank you*. "There's a rider at the gate asking for you by name. Wouldn't give it to the guards. Says she'll wait till morning if she has to." He paused. "She's wearing Steppe-runner's colors. And she says—" he hesitated, clearly reluctant to repeat it, "—she says to tell you that Ovid sent more than one letter. That there's one you haven't read. And that Sera should hear it first."

The harbor wind came up the street, cold off the water, stirring the red threads in a hundred open doorways.

Felix stood very still, an ordinary sword at their hip and forty-one harmless knots in their satchel, and felt the past lean close again—but differently now. Not as dread. As a question, waiting patiently, like a guest at a table that had finally been set.

"Then we'd better go home," Felix said, "and set another place."
