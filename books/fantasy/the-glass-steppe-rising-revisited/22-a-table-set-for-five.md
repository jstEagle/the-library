## 22. A Table Set for Five

The morning the dragon's shadow crossed the market, Felix Kavanagh was arguing with a cabbage.

Not arguing, exactly. Negotiating. Old Maretta's cabbages had been priced at two coppers since before the Prohibition fell, and Felix had spent a year learning that the trick to Corrow's markets was not charm-magic—which they no longer had—but charm-craft, which turned out to be the older and more stubborn art. You leaned on the stall. You commented on the weather in a way that implied the weather owed you money. You picked up the worst cabbage in the pile and held it up to the light like a jeweler assessing a flawed stone, and you waited, because Maretta could not bear to have her goods insulted even by silence.

"Three coppers," she said finally, "and I'll throw in the ugly one for free."

"The ugly one *is* the one I'm holding."

"Then it's four."

Felix laughed and paid three, and that was when the shadow came.

It slid across the market square the way a cloud slides across a valley—except clouds did not have wings, and clouds did not make the awnings ripple in their wake-wind, and clouds did not cause every child in the square to stop mid-shriek and point upward with their mouths open. The dragon was high, a dark keel against the morning blue, riding the thermals off the salt flats toward the Steppe. Bronze-scaled, old-fashioned in its flight, unhurried. It did not circle. It did not land. It simply passed over Corrow the way a neighbor passes your window on the way to work, and beneath it the market did what markets now did, which was nothing at all.

A woman selling dried fish didn't look up from her ledger. Two porters kept arguing about a crate. A boy of perhaps six tracked the shadow across the cobblestones with his finger, said "big," with the profound indifference of the very young, and went back to chasing a hoop.

One year ago, that shadow would have emptied the square in ninety seconds flat. One year ago, mothers would have been hauling children into doorways and merchants would have been throwing tarps over their wares as if canvas could stop a creature that could carry a house in its jaws. One year ago, the Wardens' whistles would have shrilled from three corners and the Prohibition bells—the ones that had rung Felix's childhood into silence—would have begun their long bronze complaint.

Now there was just a shadow, and a boy saying *big*, and Maretta pressing an extra apple into Felix's bag because, she said, anyone who talked down a cabbage deserved feeding.

Felix stood in the middle of the square a moment longer than they needed to, letting the sun hit their face, letting the ordinary morning be extraordinary precisely by refusing to be anything but ordinary. The scar along their palm—the one left where oath-magic had poured out of them like water out of a broken jar—itched in the cold. It always itched on bright mornings. They had stopped minding.

They walked home through streets that still bore the war's scarring if you knew where to look: the Warden garrison on Tanner's Row converted, at last, into a granary; the Prohibition placards peeled from the walls leaving pale rectangles like scars after a healed burn; the salt gate standing open, actually open, its iron doors rusted back on their hinges because no one had ordered them closed in eight months. A city unclenching, muscle by muscle. It was slow. It was slower than Felix wanted and faster than they'd dared hope, and most days both of those things were true at once, which they were learning was simply what peace felt like from the inside.

The house on Bellwether Lane had been Sera's find—a narrow, crooked thing wedged between a chandler's shop and a derelict print-house, three floors of leaning stairs and windows that fit their frames the way a rumor fits the truth. The landlord had wanted an outrageous rent until he learned who Felix was, at which point he'd wanted no rent at all, and Sera—who had spent ten years as a prisoner and a symbol and had developed, accordingly, an allergy to being indebted—had negotiated him down to something fair and insulting to neither party. That was Sera all over. Ten years in a cage had not made her soft. It had made her precise.

Felix came in through the kitchen door to the smell of burning.

"Everything's fine," Wren called from somewhere near the stove, in the tone of someone announcing a verdict she expected no one to appeal.

"Nothing is fine. Something is on fire."

"It's *supposed* to be on fire. It's cooking." Wren emerged from the smoke looking triumphant and slightly sooty, a girl of maybe sixteen with sharp elbows, sharper eyes, and hair she cut herself with a belt-knife, which explained a great deal about her hair. She wore three of Felix's luck-knots braided into her sleeve—not because she believed in them anymore, she'd said, but because they were *hers* now, which was better than believing. "I'm making breakfast. For five. Like a person who lives in a house."

"You're making charcoal for five."

"I'm making *ambition* for five." She jabbed the spoon at Felix like a scepter. "Ilya says ambition is the first ingredient."

"Ilya also once told me he could hold a pass alone with a crossbow and a bad leg, and we both know how that ended."

"He *held* the pass."

"He held it until he fell off it."

Wren grinned—that quick, crooked grin that had once been a weapon and was now, mostly, just a gift—and went back to her smoking pan. Felix hung the market bag on its hook and stood a moment in the doorway, watching her. A year ago this child had blackmailed her way into their life with a stolen map and a thief's arithmetic: everyone is leverage, everything has a price, trust is a coin you spend before someone spends it for you. Now she cooked breakfast for five in a house with her name carved into the stair rail—she'd done it herself, badly, the first week, as if to make the claim legally binding—and the only things she stole anymore were apples from Felix's market bags, which Felix pretended not to count so she wouldn't stop.

The table was already set. Four places. Felix counted twice, out of a habit that had nothing to do with doubt and everything to do with savoring. Four bowls, four spoons, and the fifth place—the one at the corner nearest the window—set with the good cup, the glazed one with the chip in the rim, because Ilya liked that cup and refused on principle to use any other.

Speaking of whom: a thump from upstairs, then another, rhythmic and deliberate. Ilya's exercises. He did them every morning now, religiously, the way he'd once done drills—up and down the length of his room with a crutch and a wall and a jaw set like a man arguing with gravity and slowly, month by month, winning. The healers had said he might never walk without the crutch. Ilya had thanked them very politely, asked detailed questions about exactly which muscles were failing him, and then treated the answer as a list of enemies.

Felix climbed halfway up the stairs and leaned on the rail. Through Ilya's half-open door they could see him at the window, sweat-dark shirt, crutch under one arm, standing—*standing*, weight on the ruined leg—for a count that Felix knew ran to two hundred. He was watching the sky. Following the dragon, probably. He still watched dragons the way a man watches a doctrine he used to kill for: carefully, hungrily, with something being rewritten behind his eyes.

"You'll miss breakfast," Felix called.

"I will be punctual or dead," Ilya said, without turning around. "There is no third option."

"That's what you said about the pass."

"And I was *punctual*," he said, and there was the ghost of the joke in it now, where six months ago there had been only flint. His leg had cost him his career, his order, and nearly his life; Veyne's blade had opened him hip to knee on the mountain road above the Emberhold, and the healers said the Tide-cold that got into the wound would ache forever, a souvenir of the emptiness itself. But he'd chosen the fugitives over the uniform at a waystation in the middle of nowhere, and he'd paid the full price without blinking, and when Felix had tried once to tell him he didn't owe them anything, Ilya had looked at them with those gray Warden's eyes and said, "Owing isn't why. Staying is why. Learn the difference, ambassador."

He'd reached two hundred. Felix heard the crutch take his weight, heard him lower himself onto the bed with the controlled care of a man handling something explosive, and smiled, and went back down.

Sera was in the garden.

Of course she was. Their sister had taken the scrap of yard behind the print-house—six paces by nine, weed-choked, shadowed by the chandler's wall—and turned it into the strangest garden in Corrow: steppe-grass in the north corner, glasswort and salt-fern along the fence, and in the center, planted in a bed of imported sand, a single cutting of ember-thorn, the fire-flower that grew wild on the slopes below the Emberhold. It shouldn't have survived this far north. It survived anyway, stubborn and low and copper-leaved, and Sera tended it the way she did everything now—with the focused ferocity of a woman making up for lost decades.

She was kneeling in the dirt when Felix came out, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with the same strip of Ovid's courier-scarf she'd worn every day since the Emberhold. She didn't look up.

"You're hovering," she said.

"I'm observing. There's a difference."

"There is not, and there never was. You hovered over my cipher-work for eleven years and you hover over my garden now. Sit down before you fall down." She brushed soil from her hands and finally looked up, and there it was—the face Felix had buried twice, once with empty grief and once with impossible joy, older by ten hard years and lit from inside by a steadiness that no cage had managed to extinguish. Her wrists still carried the pale bands where the binding-chains had held her to the Emberhold's fire, fed to it drop by drop like a lamp fed oil. Some mornings she couldn't hold a knife. She'd learned to garden one-handed on those mornings rather than skip it. "Maretta's cabbages?"

"Three coppers."

"She likes you."

"She extorted me."

"That's how she likes people." Sera rose, joints cracking, and nodded at the ember-thorn. "Look at it."

Felix looked. The cutting had put out its first bud overnight—a tight knot of bronze at the stem's crown, furled like a fist deciding whether to open. In the old days, before the breaking, ember-thorn bloomed only where covenant-fire had passed, and folk had read its flowers like scripture: full bloom meant the pact strong, blight meant the pact strained. Nobody had needed to read it in ten years, because nobody had dared grow it.

"It's going to flower," Felix said.

"It's going to flower." Sera said it quietly, the way she said all the important things, as if loudness might frighten them off. "First bloom in Corrow in twelve years. I want Wren to see it open. She doesn't remember what the world was like before. Someone should show her what it's becoming."

From the kitchen came a crash, a curse in camp-cant, and Wren's voice announcing to no one in particular that the fire had been *excessive* and the situation was *contained*. Sera closed her eyes briefly, in the manner of a woman praying for patience and receiving siblings instead.

"We should rescue the kitchen," Felix said.

"We should let it burn a little longer. Builds character." But she was already moving toward the door, and Felix followed, and the last thing they saw of the garden was the ember-thorn bud catching the light like a coal remembering heat.

Breakfast, in the end, was edible, which Wren declared a triumph and no one disputed. They ate at the crowded table—Sera with her tea, Ilya installed in his chair with the good cup and his crutch hooked on the rail, Wren narrating her culinary journey like a war veteran, Felix mostly listening, doing the thing they had learned to do in the year since the Emberhold: being present. It sounded simple. It was the hardest skill they'd ever acquired. For ten years Felix had survived by being invisible—luck-knots sold from a shadowed stall, a name shortened, a past folded small enough to hide. Presence had been fatal. Now presence was the whole work: sitting in council chambers while frightened chancellors said frightened things about dragons; standing in the market while a shadow crossed the sun and no one flinched; being seen, deliberately, daily, until being seen stopped feeling like bleeding.

"Ambassador," Ilya said, over his cup—he still used the title ironically, except lately less and less ironically—"the delegation from the Salt Reach arrives Thursday. Have you prepared?"

"I have prepared three speeches and abandoned all of them."

"Good. Your fourth speech is always the one that works."

"My fourth speech is always me admitting I don't know what I'm doing."

"Yes," said Ilya, "and it works, because everyone else is also pretending, and honesty is the one coin nobody counterfeits." He drank. "Also the Salt Reach chancellor owes her seat to the grain trade, and the grain trade wants the dragon-roads reopened. You won't need to persuade her. You'll need to let her persuade you."

Felix stared at him. "You've been reading my correspondence."

"I've been *organizing* your correspondence. Someone has to. You alphabetize by mood."

"That's a system."

"It's a cry for help with folders." But he was smiling, and Wren was laughing into her porridge, and Sera was shaking her head with the particular resignation of the eldest sibling, and Felix sat in the middle of it and thought: *this*. Not the Emberhold, not the roaring fire, not the moment the Hollow Tide broke like a wave against reforged light and the world held its breath. This. Porridge and teasing and a chipped cup. The covenant had been remade in fire, but it lived here, in a crooked house on Bellwether Lane, renewed daily by people who chose each other over and over, at breakfast, without ceremony.

Afterward, Felix took the luck-knot out to the garden.

It was a small thing, hardly worth the name—a sprig of rowan shoot bound with red thread in the pattern their mother had taught them, the pattern that had once been illegal to tie, the pattern Felix had sold for coppers in the smugglers' market while their hands shook. They hadn't tied one since the Emberhold. Hadn't been able to. The knots had been the last of their magic, and magic was gone now, poured into the fire that burned again on the far horizon, visible some nights from the ridge above the salt gate as a faint gold smudge like a lamp left burning in an upstairs window. What remained in Felix's hands was just craft. Thread and wood and patience. Their mother's hands' knowledge, minus the wonder.

But Sera had tilled a corner of the garden by the south wall, and had said, in her quiet way, *plant something*, and Felix had understood that she wasn't talking about horticulture.

They knelt in the dirt. Dug with their fingers, properly, the way Wren had taught them on the Steppe—*roots want dark and loose, not deep and packed, same as people*. Set the rowan sprig in. Packed the earth around it. Tied the last loop of red thread at the base, not as a spell—they'd tested it, a hundred times, gently, hopefully, and felt nothing but thread—but as a promise. A marker. *Something was given here. Something grows here.*

"Is it magic?" Wren had asked once, watching from the doorway, genuinely curious, no mockery in it.

"No," Felix had said. "It's better. Magic gets spent. This stays."

The sprig stood in its bed, thin and green and ridiculous, three leaves against the whole weight of the sky. Felix stayed kneeling beside it longer than the task required, hand resting on the cool soil, feeling the morning move around them—the dragon's distant cry off toward the Steppe, Ilya's crutch tapping through the house above, Sera's voice teaching Wren the names of herbs, the city beyond the wall waking into its second year of the new age, clumsy and hopeful and alive.

Their scarred palm itched. Felix pressed it flat against the earth instead, and let it itch, and smiled.

Behind them, the kitchen door banged open. "Ambassador!" Wren shouted, breathless, alight with the news before she'd finished climbing down the steps. "The thorn—it's opening! Sera says come *now*, she says it won't wait—"

Felix rose, brushing the dirt from their knees, and turned toward the voice, toward the house, toward the family waiting in the gold-spilled morning—and followed.
