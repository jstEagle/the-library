## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Guild of the Glass Steppe kept its accounts in a room that smelled of cedar wax, cold iron, and — on certain evenings, when the wind came off the plain just right — faintly of ozone and old promises.

Beatrix Montrose had worked in that room for eleven years, and she had long ago stopped noticing the smell. What she had never stopped noticing was arithmetic.

"Three bottles of thunder," she murmured to herself, dipping her pen. "Sold at half price because one of them leaked."

The leak was, of course, the problem. Bottled thunder did not leak like water. It leaked like weather. The cellar beneath the counting house had been experiencing an unseasonable July for three days now, complete with distant rumbling and one truly spectacular hailstorm that had ruined Mrs. Abernathy's shipment of preserved moonlight. Beatrix had docked the seller's account accordingly, which he would protest, and which she would win, because the ledger always won. That was rather the point of it.

She wrote: *Adjustment — one (1) bottle thunder, compromised seal. Hail damage to adjacent inventory charged against seller. Seller may appeal by presenting himself in person, in writing, or in dream before quarter's end.*

In the Steppe, dreams were admissible. Everything was admissible, so long as it was written down first.

Beatrix sat back and flexed her fingers. Around her, the Counting Room settled into its evening noises — the tick of the great clock that ran slightly ahead of time out of professional ambition, the rustle of the paper files that were, technically, alive but very lazy, and the soft groan of the shelves bearing the ledgers themselves. There were forty-one of them. She had personally balanced thirty-nine.

The fortieth was her predecessor's, still open, because old Master Corvus had walked into the Steppe one morning to "check a measurement" and had never come back, and no one could close his books without him. The forty-first was hers, and it was nearly done for the night.

Nearly done meant one last pass through the day's entries, and one last pass meant reading them aloud under her breath, because her mother had taught her that numbers spoken softly were less likely to lie.

"Bottled thunder, three units, adjusted," she read. "One debt paid in years — four years, two months, eleven days, tendered by Mr. Hollis Fenwick against his late father's obligations, condition verified, interest waived as a courtesy." She paused there, as she always paused there. Four years of a man's life, sitting in a strongbox downstairs, waiting to be spent by whoever bought it. The guild had sold it within the hour to a widow who wanted to see her son grow up. Beatrix had processed the transaction without comment, because processing without comment was the job, and because if she let herself comment she would never stop.

"A memory of rain, one unit, gently used." That one had made her smile. Someone had traded away the memory of standing in a summer storm as a child, and someone else had bought it for their daughter, who had been born during a drought and would otherwise never know what rain felt like from the inside. Impossible things, yes. But some impossibilities were kinder than others.

"One door, secondhand, hinges intact."

That entry was not hers either, come to think of it. Doors were rare stock. A good door — one that opened where you needed instead of where it stood — could fetch more than a decade of somebody's life. This one had come in through the Salt Market with no provenance at all, which should have been flagged, and hadn't been, which meant she'd have to flag it herself tomorrow, and file the flag, and then file the form explaining the flag. Bureaucracy in the Steppe was not a human invention. It was load-bearing.

She dipped her pen again and turned to the final page of the day.

And stopped.

There was an entry there she had not written.

This was, in itself, not unprecedented. Other clerks sometimes left notes; the Mistress of Accounts occasionally appended instructions in her small, precise hand; once, memorably, a debt collector who had died forty years prior had kept filing paperwork out of sheer habit until they'd coaxed him into retirement. The Counting Room tolerated ghosts the way any large institution does — with a designated desk and a great deal of pretending.

But this entry was in *her* handwriting.

Not similar. Not close. Hers — the slight forward lean of her letters, the way she crossed her sevens twice when she was being careful, the little flourish on the terminal *s* that she'd picked up from Master Corvus and had never managed to shed. If Beatrix had been handed this page blind, she would have sworn on all forty-one ledgers that she had written it.

She had not written it.

*Received this night,* the entry read, *one (1) prophecy, century-class, sealed. Condition: active. Delivery pending.*

Below it, in the same hand, a date. Tonight's date.

Beatrix sat very still. The clock ticked its smug little ticks. Somewhere below, the bottled thunder grumbled about the humidity.

"All right," she said to the empty room, in the tone she used for misfiled invoices and for the paper files when they tried to nest. "Let's find out who's been practicing my penmanship."

Because that was the reasonable explanation. Someone in the guild — a junior clerk playing a joke, or worse, a forger testing a hand — had written in her ledger. Forgery in the Counting Room was not a prank. It was closer to counterfeiting a key to a city gate. Names had weight here; handwriting was a name made visible. Whoever had done this had committed something between trespass and blasphemy, and Beatrix intended to find them, fine them, and make them re-ink every mislabeled map in the archive as penance.

She turned back one page. Her own entries, hers, hers, hers — the memory of rain, the years, the thunder. All accounted for. Then the foreign line, sitting at the bottom of the page like a guest who had arrived after the doors were locked.

She held the page up to the lamp. The ink glistened.

Still wet.

Her stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn, the way it did when a map showed a river flowing uphill and the map insisted it wasn't wrong. Eleven years of competence sat up inside her and looked around nervously.

Wet ink meant minutes. Minutes meant whoever had written it was — what? Still in the building? The Counting Room locked itself at dusk; she had watched it do so, had heard the locks set themselves with the little satisfied click of a thing well made. No one had come in. No one had gone out. She had been alone with the ledgers for three hours, and the only sounds had been the clock, the files, and the weather in the cellar.

Unless, said a small voice in the back of her mind, the one she usually got to ignore, unless it didn't need a door.

"Don't be dramatic," she told the voice. "It's a ledger. Ledgers don't do anything. They just remember."

But even as she said it, she knew the sentence was only half true, and the false half was the comfortable half. Ledgers in the Steppe didn't just remember. They *preceded*. Every cartographer learned it in their first year: write a coastline, and somewhere a coast would consider forming. Write a trade, and somewhere two strangers would feel an odd compulsion to meet. The guild didn't record the impossible world. In a sense that nobody put in the orientation materials, the guild *ran* it, loosely, the way a dam runs a river — and dams, Beatrix knew, were judged entirely by what happened the day they failed.

A prophecy, century-class, sealed. Delivered tonight. Entered in her hand.

She looked at the date again. Tonight's date, written in ink that had dried a hundred years ago in a book she was absolutely not thinking about, because thinking about *that particular book* was against guild policy, guild tradition, and, after the incident with the previous cartographer who had thought about it too hard, basic self-preservation.

The Century Codex sat in the sealed vault two floors down, behind a door that required three keys, a blood signature, and a formal written request approved by the Mistress herself. Beatrix had filed exactly one request in eleven years, early on, out of curiosity, and had received back a single line in Sarelle Voss's elegant script: *Denied. Some accounts are closed for a reason. — S.V.*

She had accepted that. She was good at accepting things; it was most of what accounting was.

But the entry sat there, wet and patient, in her own handwriting, and the clock ticked, and the paper files rustled in a way that she chose to interpret as wind.

Beatrix Montrose was, by temperament and training, a woman who checked things. She checked totals twice and maps three times. She had once walked four days across the salt flats to verify that a lake marked on a rival guild's chart was, as suspected, a very confident mirage. Checking was not curiosity. Checking was *due diligence*, and due diligence was the difference between a keeper of accounts and a keeper of accidents.

So she did what she always did with an anomaly. She cross-referenced it.

She pulled the day-book, the intake register, the cellar manifest, and the courier log, and laid them open side by side, and went down each column with her finger like a woman searching a crowd for a familiar face. Nothing. No prophecy received. No courier logged. No delivery of anything larger than a hatbox all evening. The entry existed in exactly one place: her ledger, her hand, tonight's date.

An entry with no origin. A transaction with no counterparty. In the Steppe, that wasn't merely strange. That was *unpaid for*. Everything cost something here — that was the first law, older than the guild, older than the glass. A thing entered without a price was a thing that hadn't finished arriving.

Or hadn't finished being asked for.

Beatrix closed the day-book. She capped the ink. She tidied her pens into their row, squared the ledgers on their shelf, and performed the entire ritual of ending a working day with the thoroughness of a woman who very much intended to go home, eat soup, and sleep like a person with nothing on her mind.

Then she took off her spectacles, cleaned them, put them back on, and said, quietly, to the room:

"I'm going to regret this."

The clock, running ahead as always, seemed to agree.

She gathered her coat, her satchel, and — after a moment's argument with herself that she lost — the ledger itself, tucking it under her arm with the offending page marked by a ribbon. The Counting Room unlocked for her with a click of mild disapproval. The corridor beyond was lamplit and empty, and her footsteps sounded louder than usual, though she told herself that was the hour, and not the fact that she was listening for a second set.

Two floors down, past the archive of debts and the gallery of confiscated names, the vault door waited — black iron, unornamented, with the three keyholes arranged like a face that had decided long ago not to share its opinions.

Beatrix stood before it for a long moment, doing the arithmetic she was best at: the cost of walking away, weighed against the cost of not.

Under her arm, warm through its cover, the ledger waited too.

It had never been warm before.

* * *

She was halfway back up the stairs — she would swear this later, would swear she had turned around, had actually turned around, had gotten six steps toward home and soup — when the warmth against her ribs became unmistakable. Not lamp-warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a forehead is warm, the way a page is warm when something is being written on it *now*.

Beatrix set the ledger on the stair, opened it to the ribbon, and read the new line by the light of the wall-lamp, her heart keeping poor time against the clock upstairs.

Beneath the first entry, in her own handwriting, freshly wet, a second line had appeared:

*Buyer identified. Name to follow. Do not be afraid.*

"I am extremely afraid," she informed it, "and I would like that noted in the margin."

The ink shimmered, and began, unhurriedly, to write.
