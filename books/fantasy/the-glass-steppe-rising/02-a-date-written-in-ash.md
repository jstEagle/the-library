## 2. A Date Written in Ash

Beatrix Montrose had a rule about anomalies: never meet their eyes before you've counted them.

It was a cartographer's instinct. A single wrong contour on a map could be a drafting error, a printing smudge, a Tuesday. Two wrong contours meant the terrain had moved. Three meant *you* had moved, and that was when you sat down, made tea, and took inventory of your assumptions.

So she counted.

The ledger of impossible things — the guild's master volume, bound in leather the color of dried blood and chained to her desk by a lock older than the city it sat in — held forty-one entries for the day. She had written thirty-nine of them herself, in her own tidy hand, each one witnessed, countersigned, and filed according to the Guild's Articles of Trade. Entry forty she had already flagged: three vials of bottled thunder sold to a widow in the Salt Quarter who claimed she wanted "one good storm's worth of noise" to play at her husband's funeral, because he'd died quietly and deserved better. Beatrix had found this both morbid and touching, and had charged accordingly.

Entry forty-one was the problem.

*Received: one (1) memory of green rain, condition fair, provenance unknown.*

She had not written entry forty-one.

The handwriting was hers. That was the first thing she checked, because it was the easiest thing to check and the most frightening thing to confirm. The slant of the letters, the way the descender on the *g* curled like a fishhook, the little tick she put over her crossed sevens — all of it was Beatrix Montrose, rendered so faithfully it might have been pressed from a mold of her hand. But the ink was wrong. Her ink was iron-gall, brown-black, cheap, and slightly acidic; she liked it because it bit into the page and stayed. This ink was blue-black, glossy, and sat on the surface of the paper as if it had arrived from somewhere else entirely and hadn't decided whether to stay.

She turned the page back. Turned it forward again. The entry remained.

"Right," she told the empty counting room. "Counting continues."

She was, by nature and profession, a woman who believed that everything strange became merely difficult once properly catalogued. Her mother had called her stubborn. Her sister had called her worse, affectionately. The Guild called her *the Keeper*, which was a title, a job description, and a mild threat all at once, and Beatrix wore it the way she wore everything: competently, and with her sleeves rolled up.

The counting room at midnight smelled of tallow, old vellum, and the faint metallic tang of the Steppe bleeding through the walls. It always did after sundown. The Glass Steppe was not *in* the world exactly — it was threaded through it, accessible through doors drawn in chalk and contracts signed in blood-adjacent inks — but its edges leaked, and the leak smelled like hot sand struck by lightning. Beatrix had long since stopped noticing, except on nights when she needed reminding that the world was stranger than her ledgers, which tonight was shaping up to be.

She pulled the anomaly file. Not an official file — the Guild didn't have official files for anomalies, because officially the Guild had no anomalies, only *inventory*. Hers was a private ledger, kept in a drawer beneath a false bottom that contained nothing but a tin of peppermints, which was itself a kind of security measure. Nobody had ever looked under a false bottom expecting mints.

Her private ledger recorded the small wrongnesses of the past six years. A debt paid in years that aged the debtor eleven months instead of ten. A door in the Low Market that opened onto last Thursday. A client who bought his own shadow back twice, and both times swore he'd never sold it. Individually: curiosities. Collectively, arranged by date on her own graph paper in three colors of ink: a pattern.

The pattern said the impossible trades were clustering.

For years they'd been scattered evenly, one or two a season, like hailstones in July. In the last eight months they'd begun to bunch — and the bunching had a direction. Everything clustered around *now*. Around this week. Around, if she drew the trend line honestly, which she always did, because lying to yourself was just bad bookkeeping —

Around tomorrow night.

Beatrix sat very still. Somewhere above her, the building settled its bones, and a mouse in the wall dragged something small and valuable toward its nest with the workmanlike determination of a fellow professional.

"No," she informed the trend line.

The trend line did not care what she informed it.

* * *

There were three books in the Guild that were never supposed to be opened, and Beatrix had keys to two of them, which she considered a design flaw in the Guild's security philosophy and had said so, once, to Guildmistress Sarelle Voss herself.

Voss had smiled — Voss's smiles were beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, all pattern and no warmth — and said, "And why do you suppose that is, Keeper?"

"Because a lock you don't test is just a rumor," Beatrix had said.

"And yet," Voss had replied, "you've never tested mine."

That had been the end of that conversation, and Beatrix had thought about it approximately nine hundred times since.

The Century Codex was the third unopenable book, and she had no key to it, because it had no keyhole. It was kept in the sealed archive beneath the counting room, behind a door whose lock was a question — literally; the mechanism required you to answer aloud, truthfully, and the door judged you. What it judged *for*, nobody knew. The previous keeper had answered honestly every day of his tenure and been locked out for eleven years until he retired, at which point the door had let him in to say goodbye. Ivo Quill, who had trained Beatrix and retained the archive's memory better than anyone alive, maintained that the door was "temperamental, like all honest things."

Tonight, Beatrix stood in front of it with her lantern raised and her heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional.

"I'm here," she said, "because an entry appeared in my own hand that I didn't write."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"It's dated tomorrow."

More silence. Then, from inside the door, a sound like a breath drawn through stone, and the lock unfolded itself — not opening, exactly, but *reconsidering* — and the door swung inward onto air that tasted of dust and waiting.

"Well," said Beatrix, whose courage had always worked best when she was too annoyed to be frightened, "don't make it weird."

The Century Codex lay on its lectern in the center of the round archive room, surrounded by the first maps ever traded by the Guild: the Steppe as it had been a thousand years ago, before it fused; the Steppe as a living plain; the Steppe with borders that predated the idea of borders. And at the room's heart, on its stand of black glass, the Codex itself — a book the size of a paving slab, bound in ash-grey hide, closed with a clasp shaped like a closed eye.

Prophecy was illegal in the Guild. Not frowned upon — *illegal*, Article One, older than the Articles themselves. You couldn't trade in what would happen, because what would happen was the one commodity whose price destroyed the market. The Codex wasn't prophecy, technically. It was a *record* of prophecy, compiled a century ago, sealed on the grounds that knowing the future was bad for business and worse for sleep. Every keeper learned about it the way children learn about the sea: it exists, it is vast, and you do not go in.

Beatrix went in.

She told herself it was due diligence. Cross-referencing. The anomaly file pointed at tomorrow night; the Codex covered the next hundred years starting a century ago; therefore the Codex's final pages overlapped tomorrow night, and a competent keeper simply *checked the overlap*, the way a competent surgeon simply removes the arrow. She was aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking, and she addressed them directly: "You are holding a lantern. Comport yourselves."

The clasp-eye resisted her thumb for a moment — she felt it assessing her, the way the door had — and then softened and opened, and the Codex breathed out a century of stillness into her face.

The pages were heavy, grey-edged, dense with a script she half recognized: an early form of Ledger Law, the binding script the Guild used to make words enforceable. Prophecies, it turned out, looked like accounts. Columns of dates. Columns of events, described with the flat precision of inventory. *In this year: the salt remembers.* *In this year: a debt comes home wearing new shoes.* *In this year: the glass grows a throat.* She turned page after page, faster now, the dates marching forward decade by decade, and her dread — which had been crystallizing all evening, forming slowly, beautifully, terribly, the way frost forms on glass — began to take its final shape.

Because the dates stopped.

The last third of the Codex was blank. Page after page of nothing, except — no. Not nothing. As she reached the end, entries resumed, sparse and strange, written in that same blue-black ink as the forged entry upstairs. And then, four pages from the end, a date.

Tomorrow's date.

Not predicted. Not circled, not highlighted, not given any of the drama she'd have expected. Just set down in the left-hand column like any other account, in the same tidy hand as the entry upstairs, in ink that gleamed wet under her lantern.

Beside it, in the right-hand column where the Guild recorded *what was received*, were five words:

*Elias Vane. Full balance due.*

Beatrix read it four times. On the fourth reading she did the thing she always did when reality misbehaved: she touched it.

Her fingertip came away blue-black and glistening.

The ink was wet.

The ink was wet on a page written a hundred years ago by hands that had been dust for decades, and it was *wet*, and it had smeared slightly under her finger the way fresh ink does, and somewhere in her body a very old, very practical instinct said *close the book, walk away, draw a map of somewhere safe and go there* — and somewhere else in her body, the part that had drawn coastlines for countries that shouldn't exist since she was nineteen, said:

*Wet means recent. Recent means ongoing. Ongoing means someone is writing this now.*

"Who's Elias Vane?" she asked the archive.

The archive, being an archive, declined to answer. But the Codex — she could have sworn, and would later swear, and did swear to a man who'd have reason to believe her — the Codex turned its own next page. Gently, the way a dealer turns a card they know will win.

The following date was three days out. Beside it, in the same wet ink: *The vessel wakes.*

Beatrix stood in the sealed archive for a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat and the faint, far-off song the glass sang when the Steppe shifted in its sleep. Then she did three things in quick succession, in accordance with her character.

First, she took out her notebook and copied the entries exactly, including the smear her finger had made, because evidence was evidence even when it incriminated the universe.

Second, she ate a peppermint.

Third, she closed the Codex, apologized to it under her breath — manners cost nothing — and climbed the stairs to find out everything there was to know about a man named Elias Vane, who, according to a hundred-year-old book with wet ink, was due in full by tomorrow night.

* * *

The Guild's registry of persons occupied a shelf, a card catalogue, and — for the modern era — one drawer of a cabinet that hummed faintly and occasionally refused to acknowledge people Beatrix was looking directly at. She searched them all. She searched under Vane, under Elias, under anagrams of both, under common misspellings, because she had once spent three days locating a smuggler catalogued as "Elais Vnae" and had never forgiven the alphabet.

Nothing.

No Elias Vane. No record of birth, trade, debt, or death. In the Glass Steppe Guild's books — books that recorded everyone, that recorded *everything*, that had once charged Beatrix's grandmother a fee for dying without proper documentation — there was no such person.

Which was, of course, impossible. Everyone was in the books. That was rather the point of the books.

Beatrix sat back in her chair and regarded the humming cabinet with narrowed eyes. There were two explanations for a person absent from the ledgers. Either he had never been written in — which was impossible, everyone was written in, it was the first law of the Steppe, *that which is not written cannot be traded, taxed, tracked, or trusted* —

Or he had been written *out*.

She remembered, suddenly and unpleasantly, a case from her second year as keeper. A debtor named Marlowe Finch who had tried to escape his obligations by having himself erased — paid a renegade scribe to strike his name from every record. It had half-worked, catastrophically. For six weeks Marlowe Finch had existed as a hole in the shape of a man; doors forgot him, coins fell through his fingers as if he owed them money, and his own mother set a place at table for him while looking straight through his chair. They'd caught him eventually. Rewriting a person back in was expensive and painful, and Marlowe had wept with relief when the ink restored him, which Beatrix had found affecting at twenty-three and found considerably more ominous at her current age.

A hole in the ledgers, she thought. A man-shaped absence. And beside his name in a prophetic account: *full balance due.*

Due to whom?

She pulled the anomaly file again, laid it open beside the copied Codex entries, and did what she did best: cross-referenced. The cluster of impossible trades, bending toward tomorrow. The wet-ink entries. The absent man. And there — she almost missed it, and would have, if she hadn't been the sort of person who checked the backs of drawers — tucked into eight months of records, small transactions, trivial ones, the kind that never warranted a second look.

*Sold: one shadow, partial, male, purchased anonymously.*

*Sold: seven years of sleep, male donor, purchased anonymously.*

*Sold: the memory of a door, male donor, purchased anonymously.*

Male donor. Again and again and again, stretching back years, small sales that together added up to a person being *disassembled*. Someone had been buying pieces of a man in installments — and the pieces weren't in the vaults, she checked, she checked twice — and the man they'd come from wasn't in the books at all.

Beatrix looked at the window. Beyond it, the city went about its midnight: lamplighters, drunkards, a cat conducting important business on a fence. Somewhere out there was a surveyor's office — she'd seen the notice in the trade papers, some mapping contract in the eastern districts, night shifts, the name of the firm half-familiar. And filed among the Guild's routine correspondence, she now realized with a cold, slow, wonderful horror, was a requisition from that same firm, three years old, requesting standard guild registration for a new hire.

A new hire whose registration had never been processed.

Whose name, when she finally dug the crumpled requisition out of the pending tray where clerical errors went to be forgotten, read — in a tired clerk's hurried hand — *Elias Vane, night surveyor, references unavailable.*

Beatrix Montrose stood up, put on her coat, loaded her satchel with chalk, compasses, the anomaly file, and — after a brief internal argument with herself that she lost — a copy of the Codex entries folded against her ribs like a letter from an enemy.

The sensible course was to report to Voss. The sensible course was always to report to Voss. But Voss's smile had frost in it, and Article One made prophecy contraband, and Beatrix had just committed, by any reasonable reading, several counts of it. Besides — and this was the thought that settled it, arriving with the quiet certainty of a closing ledger — if the Guild knew about Elias Vane, then the Guild's books had known about him for a hundred years, and the Guild had done nothing, and institutions that did nothing while a man was sold apart in installments were not institutions you handed fresh information to.

She was a keeper of accounts. Someone's account was badly overdue. And nobody — not Voss, not the Choir, not whatever wet-handed ghost was writing tomorrow — collected debts in her territory without going through her first.

Beatrix blew out the lamps, locked the ledger with its ancient chain, and stepped out into the dark with her satchel, her chalk, and the address of a night-shift surveyor written on her palm.

The ink on her palm was dry.

She noticed that. Of course she noticed that. She was beginning to suspect she would never stop noticing things like that again, for as long as she lived — and, glancing back once at the dark windows of the counting room, she had the sudden, unwelcome certainty that *how long she lived* had just become a negotiable term.

The eastern district was forty minutes' walk. She made it in twenty-five.
