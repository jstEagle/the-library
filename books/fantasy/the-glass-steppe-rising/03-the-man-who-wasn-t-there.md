## 3. The Man Who Wasn't There

Elias Vane had three rules for night work, and he was proud of all of them.

One: never trust a level bubble after midnight. Two: if the dog on Halloway Street starts barking at nothing, the nothing is probably just weather. Three: no matter how strange the reading, write it down anyway, because a surveyor who fudges his numbers is a surveyor who eventually falls into somebody's cellar.

He was thinking about rule three as he stood in the middle of Cordwainer Lane at eleven forty, staring at a measurement that refused to be reasonable.

The lane was supposed to be forty-two meters from the bakery wall to the old iron post by the canal. It had been forty-two meters when he'd surveyed it last month. It had been forty-two meters, presumably, since before anyone alive had been born to care. Tonight his tape said forty-one and a half.

He measured it again. Forty-one and a half.

"Right," Elias told the lane, in the tone of a man negotiating with furniture. "Which one of us is wrong, then?"

The lane did not answer, which he took as an admission of nothing.

He knelt and checked the post. Solid iron, rusted at the base, bolted — or rather grown, the way old iron seemed to grow into old cities — into the cobbles. He checked the bakery wall. Brick, mortar, a drainpipe that dripped onto his shoulder with the cheerful regularity of a thing that had done so for a century. Both anchors were exactly where they should be. The distance between them simply wasn't.

He wrote it down. Rule three. Then he stood there a moment longer than he needed to, because the night felt like it was holding its breath, and Elias had learned — without ever being able to say where he'd learned it — that when a night holds its breath, you breathe for it.

* * *

The strangest part, he would think later, was how ordinary the rest of the shift stayed. He finished Cordwainer Lane. He walked the canal towpath and found every benchmark exactly where the city records said it should be, sitting smugly at their recorded elevations like well-behaved children. By two in the morning he had almost talked himself out of the forty-one and a half. Tape stretches. Cold shrinks things. Cities settle. There were, he was fairly sure, whole textbooks about this sort of thing, though for the life of him he couldn't remember having read one.

That was another odd thing about Elias Vane, though it was not the kind of oddity a man notices about himself. He knew things he couldn't account for knowing. He could read a contour map like other people read street signs. He understood triangulation the way some people understood breathing. Ask him where he'd studied and he'd say "around," and laugh, and change the subject, because the truth was a blank space shaped exactly like a memory and he'd learned not to press on it, the way you learn not to press on a bruise.

Three years ago he had walked into the Municipal Survey Office with a leather case full of instruments, references that checked out perfectly, and a name that opened every door it touched. Before that: nothing. No records. No school, no family, no doctor's visits, no debts. When he'd asked the clerk about it once, joking, the clerk had frowned at her screen and said, "You came up clean, Mr. Vane. Clean as a whistle." She'd said it the way people say *you have such an honest face*, which is to say kindly, and without any idea what she was actually saying.

Elias had decided, somewhere in those first weeks, that the past was a country he'd emigrated from and that asking too many questions about your own customs paperwork was a good way to get sent back. So he didn't ask. He worked nights, kept a small flat above a shuttered tailor's shop, fed the neighbor's cat when she traveled, and lived a life so unremarkable that it had achieved a kind of perfection, like a stone worn smooth by water.

It was nearly three when he saw the woman.

She was standing under the gas lamp at the corner of Weft Street, and she had been standing there, he realized with a slow crawl of unease, since Cordwainer Lane. Maybe longer. She wore a long grey coat and carried a satchel the way soldiers carry rifles — not casually, but with the settled weight of something that had become part of the body. She was writing in a notebook. She had been writing in a notebook for hours, apparently, and now she looked up at him across half a street of empty cobblestones with the expression of someone confirming a suspicion she'd been hoping was wrong.

Elias did what any sensible man does at three in the morning when a stranger stares at him: he pretended not to notice and kept walking, at a pace that was definitely normal and absolutely not faster than before.

She fell into step beside him four streets later.

"You're Elias Vane," she said.

"That's me," he said. "You're blocking my light."

She glanced at the lamp overhead, then at his surveyor's lantern, which was very much lit and swinging cheerfully from his hand. "Your light seems fine."

"It's the principle."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, maybe, or the ghost of a laugh — and was gone behind a professional seriousness that fit her the way the coat did. "My name is Beatrix Montrose," she said. "I'm with the Guild. I need to ask you some questions, and I need you to take them seriously, because I've spent tonight discovering that I don't have time to be polite about it."

"The Guild," Elias repeated. He knew the word the way you know a word from a dream — familiar and useless. "The tailors' guild? Because I have a coat with a torn lining, but it can wait until business hours."

"Not that guild."

"No," he said slowly, "I didn't think so." And the strange thing — one of the strange things, the night was collecting them like tolls — was that he believed her instantly, completely, the way you believe a door is locked when you run into it face-first.

* * *

They stopped, eventually, on the footbridge over the canal, because Beatrix Montrose stopped walking and Elias discovered he had no particular desire to walk away from her, which was its own small alarm bell. Below them the water moved black and patient. Somewhere a night bird made a sound like a hinge.

"I'm going to show you something," she said, "and I want you to understand that until ten o'clock tonight, I thought it was a clerical error."

"That's reassuring."

"It isn't. Clerical errors are how the world stays sane. This is worse." She opened her satchel and drew out a book.

Elias had seen old books. He'd seen the ledgers in the Survey Office, their spines cracked, their corners soft as bread. This was different. This book looked less old than *elsewhere* — bound in something dark that might have been leather and might have been bark, its pages, when she opened them, the color of cream left out overnight. The handwriting inside was dense and slanting, and even upside down and by lamplight, Elias felt it pull at his eyes like a current.

"What am I looking at?"

"A prophecy," Beatrix said, and had the grace to look embarrassed about it. "A century-old book of prophecies, sealed by the Guild, which I opened tonight against approximately nine kinds of regulation, because entries were appearing in my own ledgers in my own handwriting that I never wrote, and I wanted to know why. And I found this." She turned the book toward him.

There was a date inscribed there. Tonight's date. Written, by the look of the ink's age, a hundred years ago.

And beside it, in the same antique hand, two words:

**Elias Vane.**

The canal went quiet. Not still — quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone says your name in another room. Even the night bird stopped its hinge-creaking. Elias became aware of his own heartbeat, which was suddenly working much harder than the situation strictly required.

"That's my name," he said.

"Yes."

"Somebody wrote my name a hundred years ago."

"Yes."

"In a book of prophecies."

"I did warn you about taking this seriously."

Elias laughed. It came out wrong — thin, high, a balloon losing air. "This is a prank. This is an elaborate, expensive prank, and I want you to know that I respect the craftsmanship, but—"

The book grew warm.

He felt it through the air between them, a heat like an open oven door, and Beatrix felt it too; he watched her hands flinch on the cover. The pages began to glow faintly, the way coals glow when breathed on, and the ink of his name — his name, in a century-old book — shimmered, wet and fresh, as if it had been written seconds ago instead of generations.

"That," Beatrix said quietly, "does not usually happen."

"Wonderful. Wonderful. I'd hate to be predictable."

Then his shadow moved wrong.

He only saw it because the lamplight on the bridge threw both their shadows long across the planks, hers crisp and obedient, his — his lagging. That was the only word for it. He shifted his weight left and his shadow followed a half-second late, like a reflection in syrup, like a child imitating a parent slightly too slowly to be innocent. And when it caught up, it caught up *differently*: taller than it should have been, its edges fraying into something that was not quite fingers, its head tilted at an angle his head was not tilted at, listening to something below the waterline of the world.

"Elias," Beatrix said, very calmly, in the voice people use near spooked horses. "Don't move."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good. Your shadow is doing something shadows shouldn't do, and the book is hot, and I want you to know that none of this is an accusation. As far as I can tell, you haven't done anything. You may simply *be* something, which is worse, and not your fault, and also going to get you killed."

"That's — okay. That's a lot of information delivered very fast."

"I find speed helps."

His shadow raised its head. All the way up. Further than a neck should go, further than the lamp's geometry allowed, until it was looking at him — at *him*, not at the light that cast him — and although it had no eyes, no face, nothing but darkness shaped like attention, Elias felt seen in a way that made his skin try to leave his body. And underneath the fear, underneath the absurdity of a man on a bridge arguing with his own silhouette, something else stirred. Something vast and slow and *familiar*, turning over in the deep of him like a whale surfacing in dark water, and it pressed up against the inside of his ribs and it felt, horribly, like recognition. Like coming home to a house he'd never lived in.

The shadow spoke. Not aloud. It didn't have a mouth, or lungs, or any right whatsoever. But a voice arrived in him anyway, low and resonant, the way a bell arrives in your chest rather than your ears, and it said:

*Soon.*

Elias sat down. It wasn't a decision; his legs simply resigned. The planks were cold through his trousers and the cold was real and he held onto it like a rope.

"Beatrix," he said. His voice sounded far away, borrowed. "Beatrix Montrose. What's happening to me?"

She crouched in front of him, and here was the thing he noticed even through the roaring in his ears: she was frightened. Genuinely, thoroughly frightened — he could see it in the set of her mouth — and she was staying anyway, and doing her job anyway, and there was a stubbornness in that which he found, insanely, comforting.

"I don't know all of it," she said. "I know you appear in a book that predates you. I know you have no history before three years ago — yes, I looked, I'm sorry, that's the job. I know the Guild has a contract concerning you that's older than the paper it's written on, and that the people who enforce Guild contracts are already looking for you, and they do not knock politely. And I know—" she hesitated, and chose honesty over kindness, which he would come to learn was her most consistent flaw and virtue, "—I know that whatever is sealed under the Glass Steppe, the thing the prophecies circle around like scar tissue around a wound, it recognizes you. The book isn't predicting you, Elias. I think the book has been waiting for you."

The warmth of the Codex pulsed again, gentle now, almost tender, like a hand on a fevered forehead. On the page, beneath his name, new ink was blooming — letters surfacing through the old paper like fish rising in a pond, forming words neither of them had written:

*The vessel comes to the water. The water remembers its shape.*

"Right," said Elias Vane, last vessel of something, owner of a disobedient shadow, resident of a flat above a tailor's shop that suddenly felt very far away and very flammable. He put his head between his knees and breathed, and when he looked up, he found that terror had burned off enough to reveal the thing underneath it, which was — annoyingly, inconveniently — curiosity. Three years of blank space pressed against the inside of him, demanding answers.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Say I believe you. Say I believe all of it. What happens now?"

Beatrix closed the Codex and tucked it against her side, and looked out over the black canal toward the east, where the sky had begun, very faintly, to bruise toward morning.

"Now," she said, "we run. Because I heard them arrive in the city an hour ago, and guild enforcers keep business hours, and yours—" she glanced at the sky, then at him, with an apologetic wince that did not match the words at all, "—start immediately."

Down the towpath, three figures stepped out of the dark where no figures had been, moving with the unhurried confidence of things that had never once needed to hurry.

"*Immediately*," Elias repeated weakly, "is a strong word."

"It's the accurate one," said Beatrix Montrose, and pulled a stick of chalk from her coat.
