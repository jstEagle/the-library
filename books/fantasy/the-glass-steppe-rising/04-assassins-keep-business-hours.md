## 4. Assassins Keep Business Hours

Beatrix had exactly four minutes of warning, and she spent all of them wishing she'd brought better shoes.

The warning came as a sound: the particular creak of the surveyor's office stair that meant someone was climbing it who did not want to be heard. Ordinary visitors announced themselves with boots. Assassins — guild assassins, the ones on salary rather than contract — climbed like cats who had studied accounting. Each step placed at the exact center of the tread, where the wood was newest, where the boards had been replaced after a certain incident involving a debt collector, an axe, and what the incident report still called "enthusiasm."

She'd filed that report herself, eleven years ago. She remembered because she'd been the one to replace the boards.

Four minutes. She counted them against her pulse.

Elias Vane stood across the office from her in his shirtsleeves, holding a mug of tea he had entirely forgotten about, looking at her the way men look at women who have just told them their shadow is wrong. Which, to be fair, was what she had done. Ten minutes ago. In his own workplace. At three in the morning.

"Let me say this back to you," Elias said slowly, "and tell me if I have it right. You are a *cartographer*."

"A ledger-keeper, technically, but yes."

"For a *guild*."

"The Glass Steppe Guild, yes."

"That trades in—"

"Bottled thunder, deferred sunsets, debts paid in years, apologies so sincere they're legally binding in three kingdoms. Yes. All of it."

"And my name is in your book."

"In a hundred-year-old book, in ink that hasn't dried, next to tonight's date, which I checked twice, because the first time I assumed I'd been drinking." She glanced at the window. Three minutes. "Which I hadn't. Much."

He set the mug down with the exaggerated care of a man trying very hard not to throw it. "And you came here — to me — instead of going to the authorities, because..."

"Because the authorities are a department of the guild. Because your records begin three years ago out of nowhere, which means either you're a ghost, a fraud, or something worse. Because when I got close to you, the Codex got warm, and your shadow—" she gestured vaguely at the wall behind him, where the lamplight threw his silhouette, which had, for the space of a heartbeat, pointed the wrong direction — "did that."

They both looked at the wall. The shadow behaved itself, hanging off him like any honest shadow ought to.

"Huh," said Elias.

"'Huh,'" Beatrix repeated. "Yes. That's about the size of it. And now, if you'll excuse me, I need to do something criminally stupid, and I'd like you to be somewhere else when it happens."

"What kind of criminally stupid?"

The stairs answered for her. Two floors down, the outer door opened without its bell ringing — which should not have been possible; she'd oiled that bell into honesty herself — and then closed again just as quietly.

"They're here," she said. "Listen carefully. There's a writ for your collection. It predates anything I signed, which means it comes from the deep ledgers, which means whoever holds it has authority I can't outrank. They'll present it. If you go quietly, they take you somewhere you don't come back from — or worse, somewhere you *do*, changed. If you run, they're permitted to use enthusiasm." She winced at her own word choice. "Axe. Enthusiasm means axe."

"I understood the reference," Elias whispered. He had gone pale in a way that suggested his body understood before his mind did. "What do we do?"

"*We?*" She looked at him. He was already moving toward the back window, checking the drop, doing the arithmetic of escape with the same careful attention he probably gave to measuring drainage gradients. Something about that — the ordinary man doing ordinary math in an extraordinary disaster — made her chest hurt. "There's no *we*. There's a me, who walked into this with her eyes open, and a you, who was having a perfectly normal night until I showed up with a haunted book."

"You showed up with a haunted book that has my name in it," he said. "I think that makes it mine too."

Downstairs, unhurried footsteps crossed the office floor below them. Not hiding anymore. That was worse. Hiding meant they thought stealth was necessary.

Two minutes. Maybe less.

Beatrix did the thing she would later describe, in the few accounts that survived of that night, as *thinking*. What she actually did was feel around in her bag for the Codex, confirm it was still warm as a sleeping animal, and pull out her ledger-kit: pen, spare nibs, ink, chalk, and the small tin of ash that every senior guild officer carried and no junior officer was ever told why.

Chalk.

An idea arrived, whole and terrible, the way good ideas always do when there's no time to check whether they're bad ones.

"Elias," she said. "How attached are you to this building?"

"...As a concept or specifically?"

"Specifically. This room. This wall." She was already kneeling by the interior wall beside the filing cabinets, dragging her sleeve aside, pulling the chalk from her kit. "Because I'm about to draw a door through it, and doors drawn in chalk only work once, and the far side is a place I will explain to you *while we are falling through it*."

"Falling?"

"Mostly a figure of speech."

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three raps, evenly spaced, at the front door two floors down. Then a voice, pleasant as a bank manager's: "Guild business. Open the ledger, Keeper Montrose. We know you're up there. Your chalk smells like the Steppe."

*Your chalk smells like the Steppe.* Beatrix froze mid-line. Of course they could smell it. Of course. She'd been drawing doors since she was nineteen and had somehow never considered that chalk used on guild territory might carry guild territory's scent, like smoke in wool.

"Keeper," the voice said again, warmer now, the warmth of a hand on your shoulder guiding you somewhere you don't want to go. "The writ is old. Older than your employment. Older than Mistress Voss's patience, which is saying something. Come down, hand over the Vane asset, and go home to your accounts. No one needs to be enthusiastic."

She drew faster. A rectangle, tall enough to step through. A lintel. A threshold line — double-struck, because single-struck thresholds leaked. Around the frame, in the tight looping script of guild notation, she wrote the destination: not coordinates, coordinates were for places that agreed to be found, but a *name*: THE LONG SALT FLAT, AS SURVEYED BY IVO QUILL, YEAR OF THE FIRST LEDGER, CORRECTED FOR DRIFT.

The wall under the chalk went soft. Not visibly — the plaster stayed plaster, gray and dull — but when she pressed her palm flat against it, it gave, gently, like the flank of a large patient animal.

"Beatrix?" Elias's voice had gone thin. "The wall just breathed."

"Walls do that here. Don't touch anything else." She straightened, turned, and faced the door of the office, where the footsteps had stopped on the landing outside.

*

The enforcers came in without knocking, which told her everything about the quality of the writ they carried.

There were three of them, which was two more than a routine collection needed and one fewer than she'd feared. The lead was a broad woman in a gray coat with the guild's scales stitched at the collar in thread that moved slightly, even when the wearer didn't — balance-thread, spun from the Steppe itself. Behind her stood a thin man holding a leather folio the way priests hold scripture, and behind *him*, filling most of the doorway, a shape Beatrix's eyes kept sliding off. She hated that. She especially hated that she recognized the technique: the third one was written small. Deliberately, expensively small, the way you'd write a weapon.

"Keeper Montrose." The lead woman inclined her head with genuine courtesy. Her name, Beatrix recalled from the staffing rolls, was Dree Halloway, and she had once returned a lost wallet containing forty crowns, which Beatrix found more frightening than any amount of visible menace. "You're out of district."

"I'm auditing," Beatrix said. "Cross-referencing a personnel anomaly." She stepped left, casually, placing herself between Halloway and Elias. Out of the corner of her eye she saw him press back against the soft wall and immediately jerk his hand away from it, having forgotten, bless him, that it wasn't a wall anymore.

Halloway's gaze went past her shoulder, touched Elias, and came back. "That's him, then. The Vane asset." She held out her hand, and the thin man stepped forward and laid the folio in it, reverently. "Contract of sale. Dated sixty-one years before the subject's recorded birth, which we've all agreed not to dwell on. Signed by the seller. Sealed by the deep ledgers. It names him, it claims him, and it authorizes collection." She unrolled it across the thin man's back — using him as a table, which he bore with the serenity of long practice — and Beatrix saw the writing.

She had seen ten thousand contracts. She had copied them, corrected them, filed them, occasionally forged small clarifying amendments to them when the original drafters had been idiots. She knew guild script the way musicians know scales.

This one was wrong in a way that made her teeth ache. The seller's signature looped and flourished with terrible familiarity — she'd know that handwriting anywhere, because it was *her own*, aged sixty years, in a future that hadn't happened yet. And the seal at the bottom was not the guild's scales. It was a compass rose, drawn in a style she'd last seen on the first map of the Steppe, in Ivo Quill's archive, on the page where the center of the country should have been and wasn't.

Someone had sold Elias Vane in her handwriting, from a seal that belonged to the absence at the heart of the world.

"Well?" Halloway prompted. "You've read it. You see it's valid."

"It's *something*," Beatrix said. Her heart was going like a hammer, but her voice, thank every god she didn't believe in, came out dry and level. "It's valid the way a forged promissory note is valid — enthusiastically, briefly, and only until someone checks."

"Are you calling the deep ledgers fraudulent?"

"I'm calling this document interesting, which in my profession is a synonym." She took a slow step backward, toward the chalk. "And I'm noting, for the record, that the writ specifies collection of the Vane asset from its registered holder. Registered holder being the party listed in the current active ledger." She let that hang. Halloway's eyes narrowed a fraction. Good. Follow the logic. Follow it all the way.

"There's no registration," Halloway said slowly.

"No," Beatrix agreed. "There isn't. Yet." And she pulled the Codex from her bag, opened it one-handed to the page with tonight's date, and — while both enforcers flinched at whatever the book radiated when exposed to air, the thin man actually making a small wounded noise — she took out her pen and wrote, beneath *Elias Vane*, in her best formal hand, the six words that would either save them or end her career in the next thirty seconds:

*HELD IN TRUST BY THE GUILD.*

Property of the Glass Steppe Guild, entered in its oldest book, by its keeper of ledgers. Not a person to be collected. An *asset*. And guild assets could not be seized by guild agents — only transferred, audited, or settled, and settlement required the keeper's countersignature.

Which she was not going to give.

"You can't—" the thin man started.

"She absolutely can't," Halloway snapped, but she was already crossing the room, fast, coat flaring, hand dipping into her pocket for whatever lived there. "Burn it. Burn it now, Montrose, or I swear on the scales—"

Beatrix was already burning it. She'd had the match lit since *we know you're up there*, tucked against the tin of ash, waiting. The writ caught with a sound like a gasp, and the fire ran up it green — guild paper burned green, always had, though nobody liked to discuss why — and the seller's impossible signature curled and went to nothing, and the compass-rose seal screamed, actually screamed, a short high note like a wet finger on glass, and then there was ash drifting down onto Ivo Quill's floorboards and three enforcers staring at her with expressions ranging from fury to theological crisis.

"You just destroyed a deep-ledger instrument," Halloway said. Her voice had gone very quiet. Quiet was worse than loud. "Do you understand what happens to keepers who destroy instruments?"

"They get audited," Beatrix said. "I know. I've read the file on the last one. It's why I'm going to be very difficult to audit." She grabbed Elias's wrist — he yelped — and backed them both against the chalk door. "Because the asset is guild property, entered in trust, and its custodian is invoking transfer. Immediate transfer. To a secure location. Which requires my physical presence and my countersignature, neither of which you'll be getting, because the secure location doesn't appear on any map you're carrying, and the countersignature—" she rapped her knuckles on the soft wall, which hummed like a struck bell, "—is on the other side of a door that only opens once."

"Montrose." Halloway had stopped moving. She stood in the middle of the ruined office, green ash settling on her shoulders like terrible snow, and looked at Beatrix with something that was almost, horribly, respect. "You're spending yourself. You know that. A door like that costs. Trust-entry in the Codex costs. When Voss opens your books — and she *will* open your books — every mercy you've ever extended is going to come out of you with interest."

"Then it's fortunate," Beatrix said, "that I've always kept excellent records of my mercies, and none of them were cheap."

She stepped through.

*

Falling, it turned out, was not mostly a figure of speech.

The chalk door delivered them the way a wave delivers a swimmer — upside down, breathless, and with strong opinions about orientation. Beatrix landed on salt-hard ground, rolled, came up on one knee with her bag clutched to her chest like a child, and spent a moment taking inventory of her bones. All present. All complaining. Acceptable.

Beside her, Elias Vane lay flat on his back in the moonlight, breathing in great whooping gulps, staring straight up.

Above them hung the sky over the Glass Steppe, which was the sky over everywhere and also not quite: the stars here were arranged with a faint, unsettling neatness, as if someone had alphabetized them. The plain stretched to every horizon, fused glass gone smooth and dark under the moon, reflecting the sky so perfectly that Elias appeared to be lying on the edge of a second heaven.

"We fell through a wall," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Into a *sky*."

"You're lying on it, actually. Try not to scratch it. Scratches in the Steppe surface count as amendments, and amendments require paperwork." She sat up properly and allowed herself one long shaky exhale. Her hands, she noticed, were shaking too, now that shaking was permitted. "For what it's worth, I've never done that before. Any of it. The trust-entry, the door, the—" she gestured at her own face, meaning the entire performance of composure, "—all of it improvised."

"You threatened three trained killers with *accounting*."

"I find it's surprisingly effective if you commit."

He laughed. It came out ragged and half-hysterical, but it was a laugh, and hearing it loosened something in her chest that had been wound tight since the Century Codex first fell open under her lamp. He sat up slowly, wincing, and looked out across the endless dark glass, and she watched the awe move through his face like weather.

"It's beautiful," he said. "Why does it feel like it's watching me back?"

"Because it remembers you," Beatrix said, before she could stop herself. "Or part of it does. Or—" she shook her head. "No. That conversation requires sleep, and salt tea, and an old man named Ivo Quill who knows more about what you are than I do, and possibly a drink. In that order."

She got to her feet, brushed salt dust from her knees, and looked back the way they'd come, to where the chalk door had already faded to a smudge of white on nothing, already forgetting itself, as doors did.

Somewhere behind that closing gap, in an office in a city that suddenly felt very far away, Dree Halloway was sweeping up green ash and composing her report. By dawn it would reach Sarelle Voss. By midmorning, Beatrix's entire life would be spread open on the Guildmistress's desk like a dissected specimen, every mercy itemized, every kindness priced.

She had known that when she struck the match. She had struck it anyway.

"Come on," she said, and held out her hand to the man the world had sold before he was born. "We have a long walk, and I have a great deal to explain, and the Steppe gets *hungry* near the old survey lines if you stand still too long."

Elias took her hand. Under their feet, faint as a heartbeat, the glass began — very softly — to sing.
