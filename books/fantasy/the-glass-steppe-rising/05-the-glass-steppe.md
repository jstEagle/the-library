## 5. The Glass Steppe

The first thing Elias noticed was that the chalk door had no back to it.

He had gone through expecting — well, he wasn't sure what he'd expected. A broom cupboard. An alley. The kind of nothing you got when a magic trick went wrong and the magician had already left town with your wallet. Instead there was a moment of cold, like stepping through a curtain hung in front of an open freezer, and then light.

A great deal of light.

The sun here was low and white, the way it gets in late afternoon in places where nobody has anything better to do than watch it set. It came down on a plain that stretched to every horizon at once, flat as poured sugar and bright as a church window, and it took him three full seconds to understand what he was looking at.

Glass. All of it glass. Miles of it, fused smooth, faintly green at the edges where the light caught it, veined here and there with darker streaks like the fossilized ripples of water that had frozen mid-pour. Under his feet it made a sound when he shifted his weight — a low, ringing note, deep as a struck bell, that traveled outward in a circle and did not stop until he stopped listening for it.

"Careful," Beatrix said behind him. "The Steppe remembers footsteps. You'll want to be deliberate about which ones you leave."

"The Steppe *remembers*—"

"Everything," she said. She stepped past him onto the glass, her boots making crisp, confident little notes — *tip, tip, tip* — like someone who had long ago made peace with being overheard by the ground. "It's why we keep such good books. The territory keeps them too. We just write faster."

Elias turned around. Behind him, floating unsupported in the air about chest-height, was a rectangle of chalk lines. Through it he could see the loading bay of the survey office, the flickering fluorescent tube, the coffee ring on his desk shaped like Australia. His whole life, framed. It looked small from this side. It looked like a diorama of a man's life, built by someone who'd run out of budget halfway through.

"That door is going to close," Beatrix said. "In about four minutes, give or take. Chalk doors don't hold their line without a hand on the ledger, and my hand is currently needed elsewhere." She said it briskly, but he noticed she didn't look at the door while she said it. People look at exits when they're afraid of them.

"Elsewhere meaning?"

"Meaning walk, Mr. Vane."

He walked. There was, he reflected, not much else to do. His shadow — the one that moved wrong, the one he'd spent three years pretending moved fine — slid along beside him on the glass, and for the first time he could see exactly *how* wrong. Where Beatrix's shadow kept pace like a loyal dog, his lagged by half a step, then caught up in a hurry, like a man following directions he didn't trust. Occasionally it pointed the wrong way entirely, out across the plain toward something he couldn't see.

The horizon, he realized after they'd walked a while, was not behaving. He would walk toward what looked like a range of dark hills, and the hills would decline politely to get closer. A river of pale blue glass — actual flowing glass, moving slow as honey — crossed their path twice, once going east and once going west, and Beatrix stepped over it both times without comment, as if rivers were entitled to change their minds.

"All right," Elias said finally. "I'll bite. What is this place?"

Beatrix stopped. She seemed to consider how to begin, the way she'd considered the accounts last night — turning the question over, checking its edges.

"This is guild territory," she said. "All of it. Every mile of glass you can see, and quite a lot you can't. We call it the Glass Steppe. Once upon a time it was grassland, before the founding trade. Now—" she stamped one boot, and the bell-note rang out again, deeper this time, "—now it's a balance sheet."

"A what?"

"You heard me. Geography obeys ledgers here, Elias. Not physics. Not common sense. *Books.* If a thing is written into the guild's ledger, the land makes room for it. If a thing is struck out, the land forgets it ever stood here. There was a mountain called Harrowfell, two hundred years ago. A guildmaster sold its north face to pay off a war debt. Not the stone — the *face*. The view of it. Someone, somewhere, owns the fact of looking up at Harrowfell, and the mountain has been quietly embarrassed about it ever since. You can see the scar from the eastern road. It looks away when you do."

Elias laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, a short, disbelieving bark, and the glass carried it away in rings.

"You're joking."

"I am a guild accountant," Beatrix said. "Joking is the only thing I'm permitted to do for free." But she was smiling, just slightly, and he got the sense that she liked being disbelieved, that disbelief was a toll she collected happily. "Come on. I'll show you the market while the light holds. And I'll explain what all this has to do with you. Which, I'm sorry to say, is considerably more than either of us knew yesterday."

* * *

The market appeared the way weather appears — one moment empty glass, the next, tents.

They were pitched in rows along what Beatrix called the Ledgerline, a seam of darker glass running straight as a ruled margin to both horizons. The tents themselves were impossible in a way Elias was beginning to develop a tolerance for: some were canvas, some were paper, one enormous pavilion appeared to be made entirely of folded receipts, and above each stall hung a sign written in ink that rearranged itself slowly, like a school of fish deciding on a shape.

And the goods. Good God, the goods.

At the first stall, a merchant in a coat made of rainwater was decanting thunder. Actual bottled thunder — squat brown bottles, corked and waxed, each one grumbling faintly against the glass like a dog that wanted out. A customer held one to her ear the way you'd hold a seashell, and whatever she heard made her cry a little and buy six.

Next to that, a booth labeled **YEARS, FAIR TO GOOD CONDITION**. A bored clerk sat behind a counter of stacked hourglasses, each one holding not sand but a soft golden light that pulsed gently, like breathing. A handwritten card read: *No refunds on lived years. Buyer beware of nostalgia.*

"Is any of this real?" Elias whispered.

"'Real' is a word the guild doesn't trade in," Beatrix said. "Too volatile. Everything here is *true*, though, which is different and better. That thunder fell on a village in 1841 and never stopped falling; the guild bought the storm's remainder and sold the village the quiet instead. Those years belonged to a man who ran out of things to spend them on. He's very happy now. He plays the fiddle." She said all this the way another person might describe bus routes.

They walked the rows. Elias saw debts for sale, folded flat like linen. He saw a cage of imported dawns, still damp. He saw, in a locked case, a single jar labeled **THE SOUND OF YOUR OWN NAME, SAID CORRECTLY** — and felt, absurdly, a pang of want so sharp he had to look away.

Then Beatrix stopped walking, and he understood, by the sudden stillness of her, that they'd arrived at the reason for the tour.

It was an unremarkable stall near the end of the row, tucked between a dealer in secondhand shadows and a woman selling maps of places that hadn't happened yet. The proprietor was a thin man with spectacles and the patient air of someone who had waited out longer conversations than yours. On the counter before him, laid out on grey velvet like a jeweler's wares, were perhaps twenty objects.

Small ones. Humble ones. A milk tooth. A curl of hair tied with thread. A child's drawing of a house done in crayon, sun in the corner. A glass vial of something dark and slow-moving that might have been blood or might have been memory. A name tag from a school uniform, the name worn nearly illegible.

Each item had a tag. Each tag had a date. And the dates, Elias saw with a sensation like the floor tilting, went back nineteen years.

"What are these?" he asked, though the asking was a formality. His voice sounded far away. The glass under his feet had begun, very faintly, to sing — the same deep note as before, but sustained now, like a finger held on a wet rim.

"Inventory," Beatrix said quietly. "Elias. These are pieces of *you*."

* * *

He didn't remember sitting down. He was sitting on the glass, which was warm, which somehow made it worse — even the ground was complicit, even the ground was comfortable with this.

The proprietor watched him with mild professional sympathy, the way a doctor watches a patient absorb a diagnosis.

"Sold in lots," Beatrix was saying, kneeling beside him, her voice careful and level in the way voices go level when the speaker is holding something heavy with both hands. "Over the years. Small transactions, mostly — the kind that slip past audit. Your first word. The dream you had when you were seven, the one about the flood. Your grandmother's laugh, the specific one. Whoever the buyer was, they purchased slowly. Carefully. Like someone assembling a jigsaw and trying not to let the picture show."

"Who?" His own voice didn't sound like his. "Who bought them?"

"The ledger records the seller." She hesitated. "Your mother. The entries are in her hand."

The world went very quiet. Even the market noise — the haggling, the thunder muttering in its bottles, a vendor calling out that today only, memories of summer, half price — receded, as if the Steppe itself had leaned in.

"And the buyer?"

Beatrix looked at the proprietor. The proprietor looked at Elias, and for the first time the mildness in his face gave way to something older and more cautious.

"No name," the man said. "That's the trouble with the account, you see. Twenty-two purchases, all paid in full, all paid the same way — in glass. Raw Steppe glass, drawn up from the deep seams, which no one can counterfeit and no one else can afford. Paid always through intermediaries. And every receipt signed the same way." He turned one of the tags over. On the back, in ink that gleamed faintly like oil on water, was a mark: a circle, broken at the top, with a single line descending through the gap like a crack in a bell.

"You've seen it," Beatrix said softly. "Don't pretend you haven't. You went white as salt."

He had. He'd seen it a thousand times and never known it — scratched into the wet cement behind his childhood home, doodled in the margins of his schoolbooks in a handwriting that wasn't quite his, dreamed, dreamed endlessly, hanging in the dark behind his eyes like a streetlamp over an empty road.

"It's the seal," the proprietor said, almost gently. "Or what's left of it. And son — I've been in this trade forty years, and I'll tell you what forty years taught me. Nobody buys a thing unless they mean to use it. And nobody spends twenty years buying a man piece by piece—" he tapped the velvet, the tooth, the hair, the vial of slow dark blood, "—unless what they're building needs every piece to be *him*."

The singing under the glass grew louder. Around them, other stalls had gone quiet; merchants were looking their way, and several were looking not at Elias but at his shadow, which had stopped lagging now and stood perfectly, attentively still, pointing — unmistakably, insolently — at the broken-circle mark.

Elias looked at Beatrix. She looked, for the first time since he'd met her, like a woman whose books had stopped balancing.

"So who's buying me?" he asked.

Down the Ledgerline, far off, a tent flap lifted though there was no wind. And in the space where the horizon met the glass, the light bent — just slightly, just for a moment — the way it bends over something very large, standing very patiently, waiting to be acknowledged.

Beatrix stood up fast. "We need to leave," she said. "Now. Because I think it just heard you ask."
