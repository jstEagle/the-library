## 6. The Mistress of Accounts

The Guildhall did not so much stand in the Glass Steppe as preside over it, the way a headmistress presides over a schoolyard — with the quiet confidence that everything within sight belonged to her and knew it.

Beatrix had walked its marble approach a thousand times, but never with a fugitive at her elbow and a burned writ's ashes still ghosting her fingertips. Elias kept trying to walk like an ordinary man and failing; his shoulders had climbed somewhere near his ears, and his eyes kept snagging on things no sane eye should linger on — the chandelier made of frozen lightning, the receptionist whose desk was quite literally a tortoise the size of a rowboat (the tortoise did not appear to mind), the staircase that rearranged itself politely whenever someone important approached.

"Stop looking at the chandelier," Beatrix murmured.

"I'm not looking at the chandelier."

"You've looked at it four times. It notices. Everything here notices."

"That is somehow worse than not noticing," Elias said, "which I didn't think was possible, because I spent last night being dragged through a door drawn in chalk by a woman I met six hours ago, and that was *fine*, comparatively."

"It was not fine. You screamed."

"I made a sound of *startlement*."

They were shown up — or possibly sideways; the stairs were ambiguous about direction — to the Office of Accounts, which occupied the top floor and, rumor insisted, several floors that did not technically exist.

Beatrix smoothed her coat. Underneath, where no one could see, her hands were doing something unbecoming of a senior guildswoman, something closer to trembling. She had prepared a speech on the walk over: three justifications, two legal precedents, one apology she'd cut for weakness. She rehearsed it once more as the doors opened.

Sarelle Voss was feeding a fish.

This was, in itself, unremarkable — except that the fish swam in midair beside her desk, a foot of open water hanging in nothing, and except that the fish was gold, and except that when Voss tipped the little tin of flakes toward it, the flakes fell *upward* into the water, which meant either the fish lived in inverted gravity or Voss simply preferred it that way and physics had agreed without argument.

"Montrose." Voss did not turn around. Her voice was warm the way a banked fire is warm — pleasant from across the room, fatal if you put your hand in it. "You burned one of my writs tonight."

"The writ was invalid," Beatrix said. "It cited a contract I never countersigned."

"And yet you recognized the seal." Voss turned. She was small, silver-haired, dressed in dove-gray wool with the plainness of a woman who had decided long ago that ornamentation was for people who needed help being intimidating. A pair of spectacles sat low on her nose; through their lenses her eyes were ordinary brown. Everyone who met her learned, eventually, that the spectacles were not corrective. They were *limiting*. What lay behind them unfiltered was said to make strong men sit down without meaning to. "Which means you read it. Which means you knew what it was before you lit the match. Sit down, both of you. Would you like tea? I find tea helps in difficult audits."

"We're not here for an audit," Beatrix said, sitting. Elias sat too, half a beat late, like a man following someone into cold water.

"Everyone is always here for an audit, dear. Some simply haven't been told yet." Voss poured three cups from a pot that refilled itself with a soft, apologetic gurgle. She handed one to Elias directly, which Beatrix noted with a small internal alarm — the mistress of accounts did not serve people unless she was interested in them, and interest from Sarelle Voss was rather like interest from a spider. "Elias Vane. Surveyor. Night shift, municipal works, eleven years' service. And before that—" she smiled, and it reached all the way to her eyes, which was the frightening part, "—nothing at all. No birth record. No school marks. No debts, no leases, no library fines. Do you know how rare that is, Mr. Vane? In my line of work, a man with no paper trail isn't a mystery. He's an *opportunity*."

"I have records," Elias said. "I have a flat. I pay rent."

"You began existing approximately three years ago," Voss said pleasantly, "at roughly the same moment someone finished paying for you to exist. Before that you were— well. 'Held' is the accounting term. Held in escrow." She sipped her tea. "Like the thunder. Like the years. You'd be amazed what we hold, and for whom, and how long."

Beatrix set down her cup. It rang against the saucer louder than she intended. "Mistress Voss. With respect — why are you telling him this?"

"Because you were going to lie to him, Montrose, and lies between business partners rot faster than anything else I know." Voss removed her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve with unhurried care, and replaced them. Behind the glass, her eyes went briefly from brown to a color Beatrix had no name for and did not want one. "Besides. He should hear it from me. I keep immaculate books, and I dislike other people paraphrasing them badly."

She opened a drawer — the drawer opened onto darkness, and the darkness had a faint smell of salt — and withdrew a ledger. Not a guild ledger. Older. The binding was leather gone soft as cloth, and the clasps were bone.

"Contract of sale," Voss said. "Executed twenty-nine years ago this autumn. Seller: one Miriam Vane, laundress, of Harrowgate Stair. Buyer: the Glass Steppe Guild, per the standing instrument of acquisition. Item acquired—" she turned the book around and slid it across the desk to Elias, one finger marking the page, "—one infant son. Price: one life. Hers. Paid in full, in installments, over nineteen months. Consumption, the physicians called it. The ledgers call it what it was."

Elias did not touch the book. His hands stayed flat on his knees, and Beatrix watched him read it from a distance, upside-down, the way one reads a thing one cannot bear to look at straight-on.

"She sold me," he said. His voice came out level, which was worse than shaking.

"She sold you *to save herself*, and then died anyway, which is the tragedy of every bad contract — the terms always outlive the need that shaped them." Voss said it without cruelty, the way a physician says *the bone is broken*. "Your mother was dying, Mr. Vane. There was a remedy on offer. It required payment she didn't have, so she paid with the only asset remaining: you. The guild took custody of your — let's say your *substance* — and sealed it, and wrote you out of the world's records until such time as the holding matured. That time is now. Tonight's date, in fact, if you want precision, and I always want precision."

"Matured," Elias repeated. "I'm a bond."

"You're an *asset*," Voss corrected gently. "Bonds are boring. You, I suspect, are considerably more interesting than your paperwork suggests, though even I don't know all of what's inside you, and I want you to appreciate how much it costs me to admit that." She folded her hands. "Here are the terms, since Montrose has seen fit to complicate them. The writ stands. Delivery of the asset to the guild, by month's end. In exchange—"

"In exchange for nothing," Beatrix said. "He's a person."

"He's a person *and* an asset; the two categories overlap more often than romantics like to admit. I'm a person. So is the fish." The goldfish, hearing itself mentioned, did a slow, insolent lap of its pocket of air. "In exchange, Montrose, for delivery — your standing debt is forgiven."

The word landed in Beatrix's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall for a long time before it hit bottom.

"What debt?" Elias asked, looking between them.

"Ancient history," Beatrix said.

"Ninety-one thousand, four hundred crowns, plus accrued interest, plus one favor of unspecified magnitude owed under the Old Clause, which is the kind of favor that keeps mistresses of accounts awake at night, if we slept, which we don't." Voss recited it without looking at any book. She had never needed to. "Beatrix Montrose has been solvent, hardworking, and quietly drowning for eleven years. Every map she draws, every impossible thing she catalogs, a third of its value flows uphill to settle what her teacher left her. You didn't know? No. Of course you didn't. She'd have hated you knowing." Voss smiled at Beatrix, and there — buried somewhere in it, deep, like a coin at the bottom of a fountain — was something almost kind. "Deliver the man, wipe the slate. Walk away free. Retire to a coast somewhere and draw maps of places that actually exist. You've earned it. Heaven knows you've *paid* for it."

The office was very quiet. The fish hung in its water. Somewhere below them, the Guildhall creaked and settled like a large animal dreaming.

And Beatrix found, to her horror, that the temptation was real. That was the thing about Sarelle Voss — she never offered you a deal you could dismiss. Ninety-one thousand crowns. Eleven years of thirds. A coast. An ocean that behaved. A life where no one arrived with writs bearing seals she recognized, where the ink was never wet on century-old pages, where the worst thing in her day was a smudged contour line.

She thought of Elias's face when the chalk door opened. Not fear — wonder first, fear second. Wonder first. She thought: *he'll forgive me for telling him about the debt. He won't forgive me for selling him.*

"No," she said.

Voss raised one eyebrow, a small elegant arch, the gesture of a woman watching weather she had predicted.

"No," Beatrix said again, steadier. "The writ was invalid. The contract cites a guardian's consent that was never given — his mother's authority ended at her death, guild law, article twelve, and whatever she signed covered her lifetime only. You've known that for nineteen years, mistress. You've been waiting to see if anyone would notice. I noticed."

Something flickered behind the spectacles. It might have been annoyance. It might, horribly, have been approval.

"Article twelve," Voss mused. "Yes. Sloppy drafting on old Corvane's part; he was rushing to dinner. One does accumulate these little flaws over centuries." She rose, smoothing her gray skirt, and came around the desk — small, unhurried, stopping close enough that Beatrix caught the smell of her: ink and salt and, underneath, something mineral, like the air inside a cave. "So here is what will happen, since we're all being precise with one another. You have until month's end. Find a way to void the contract properly — lawfully, in writing, with all seals intact — or deliver him. If neither happens, I foreclose. On you, Montrose. Everything: your maps, your name in our rolls, your standing, your future earnings, your past ones. I will take the eleven years back, and I will do it *gently*, because I like you, and gentleness is the most expensive thing I own." She turned to Elias, and her voice softened into something genuinely warm, which from her was more terrifying than any threat. "And you, Mr. Vane — I'd advise you to learn what you're carrying before month's end. Because if you don't understand it, someone else will define it for you, and they will not be gentle, and they will not be wrong about the facts."

"Is that a warning?" Elias asked.

"It's customer service," said Sarelle Voss. "We're a guild. We pride ourselves on it."

* * *

The stairs let them down quickly, in the manner of stairs eager to be rid of guests. Neither spoke until they were through the marble hall, past the tortoise desk — the tortoise gave Elias a slow, sympathetic blink — and out onto the glass plain, where the evening light came up amber off the fused ground for miles in every direction, and the wind made a sound like a finger circling a wet rim.

"She sold me," Elias said finally. He was staring at his own hands as though checking them for price tags.

"Yes."

"And you owe this guild ninety-one thousand crowns."

"Four hundred," Beatrix said. "Ninety-one thousand four hundred. Let's be accurate; accuracy is the only dignity the poor get."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because it was irrelevant."

"It's *ninety-one thousand crowns* irrelevant!"

"It was irrelevant," Beatrix said, "to whether I'd burn the writ. I'd have burned it for a stranger. I've burned smaller writs for strangers." She started walking, boots clicking on glass, and after a moment he fell in beside her. "What matters is the deadline. Month's end. Twenty-six days."

"To do what, exactly? Void a contract older than both of us, held by a woman who owns the concept of paperwork?" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Beatrix. She said learn what I'm carrying. What *am* I carrying?"

Beatrix looked out at the Steppe — at the horizon where the glass gave way to something darker, and beyond that, if you knew which quarter of the sky to watch, the faint permanent shimmer that the old maps marked, in faded iron-gall ink, with a single word and a warning symbol.

Not yet. She couldn't tell him *not yet*; the word sat behind her teeth like a stone. But soon. Soon, and better from a friend than from a zealot or a ledger.

"There's an archive," she said. "Two days east, if geography cooperates, and it owes me a favor. Kept by an old cartographer named Quill, who was drawing maps before your mother was born and remembers everything, including the things everyone else agreed to forget." She glanced sideways at him. "If you want to know what's inside you, Elias — really know — we start there."

Elias was quiet for a dozen steps. Then: "Will it hurt? Knowing?"

"Yes," Beatrix said honestly. "But not knowing hurts slower, and charges interest."

Ahead, the light failed by degrees, and the first stars came out over the glass — and Beatrix, who knew the Steppe's skies the way she knew her own signature, saw that one of them was new. Small. White. Fixed in a constellation that had been complete for a thousand years.

Someone, somewhere, had just added a line to a page.

"Hurry," she said, and took his arm, and lengthened her stride.
