## 7. What Sleeps Under the Salt

The old cartographer's house smelled of cedar shavings and burnt coffee, which Elias would later learn was the universal scent of people who loved their work more than their sleep.

"Ivo Quill," the man said, shaking hands with a grip like a well-worn vise. He was small and round as a teapot, with a white beard that had clearly lost several arguments with his breakfast, and eyes the color of wet slate — sharp, patient, and entirely unfooled. "You must be the trouble."

"That's me," Elias said. "Elias Vane. The trouble."

"Vane." Ivo said it the way a jeweler says *flawed diamond* — with interest rather than dismissal. "And you're Beatrix Montrose's problem, or she's yours?"

"We're still negotiating ownership," Beatrix said from the doorway, where she stood with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular way Elias had learned meant she hadn't slept. Neither of them had slept, really, not since the Steppe, not since Voss, not since the words *sold at birth by his own mother* had rearranged everything Elias thought he knew about himself into a shape he didn't recognize.

"Well," Ivo said briskly, "come in before the street notices you. Trouble is like bread — best handled fresh, worst when left out in public."

The house was a museum pretending to be a home. Maps hung on every wall, some so old their ink had gone the brown of dried blood, others so new they still gleamed. Rolled maps stood in umbrella stands. Maps lay across the furniture like sleeping cats. There was, Elias noted, exactly one chair clear enough to sit in, and Ivo claimed it immediately.

"You'll want tea," Ivo said. "Or something stronger. People who come to see the First Map always want something stronger by the end."

"The First Map," Beatrix repeated. Her voice went carefully neutral, the way it did when she was trying very hard not to want something too much. "You actually have it."

"Have it? Girl, I've been keeping it for forty years." Ivo levered himself up and shuffled toward a back room, talking over his shoulder. "Guild thinks it's in the Vault under the Salt Hall. Guild has thought that for two hundred years. This is why I never corrected them. A secret kept by an institution lasts exactly as long as no one individual feels proud of it."

He disappeared through a door, and there came sounds of grinding stone, a muttered curse, and what Elias was fairly sure was a small avalanche.

"He's going to be all right back there?" Elias asked.

"Oh, absolutely not," Beatrix said. "But he'll be fine."

Ivo reappeared dusted white to the eyebrows, cradling a long flat case wrapped in oilcloth and bound with three different kinds of lock. He set it on the table with the reverence of a priest handling relics, then ruined the effect by blowing his nose thunderously.

"Now. Before we open this, you should understand what you're looking at." He fixed them both with the slate-colored stare. "Every map of the Glass Steppe ever drawn — every one, including the beautiful ones your Guildmistress keeps behind glass — is a copy. A tracing. A polite fiction. The First Map is the only one drawn *from life*, before the ledgers were written, before the trade began, back when the Steppe was still deciding what it wanted to be."

"And who drew it?" Elias asked.

"No one knows. That's rather the point of it." Ivo's hands hovered over the locks without touching them. "The mapmaker signed nothing. Left no notes. Just delivered this to the first keepers of the ledger and vanished from every record, which, if you know anything about how records work here—" he nodded at Beatrix, who nodded grimly, "—means either they never existed, or they were made not to."

Beatrix had gone very still. "Made not to."

"There are worse fates than being unwritten," Ivo said quietly. "Being unwritten *on purpose* is one of them."

He opened the locks — a key, a twist, and one that apparently required humming a particular note, which he did badly — and folded back the oilcloth.

Inside lay a sheet of glass.

Not paper. Glass, thin as windowpane and wide as a dining table, and etched into its surface in lines of silver-white was the Steppe itself — but wrong. Or rather, right, in a way that made every other map Elias had seen feel like a rumor. The borders breathed. The rivers weren't lines but suggestions, half-finished thoughts. The whole surface seemed to move if he didn't look at it directly, like something seen through water.

But none of that was what made his mouth go dry.

At the center of the map, where every modern chart showed the Salt Hall, the Vault, the heart of guild territory, there was nothing. Not blank space — nothing. A hole in the glass, smooth-edged and perfect, shaped like—

Like a person.

"It's called the Absence," Ivo said, watching him. "Has been since the beginning. The god-shaped hole, some call it, though the Guild prefers 'surveying anomaly.' Bureaucracies can make anything sound boring if they try hard enough, and the Guild tries very hard indeed."

Elias couldn't stop looking at it. The hollow was man-shaped — roughly — arms at its sides, head bowed. But it was more than a silhouette. It had a quality of *pressure*, like a footprint in snow, like something enormous had lain down against reality from the other side and left this impression.

"How old did you say this was?" His voice came out strange.

"A thousand years. Perhaps more." Ivo leaned over the glass, and the lamplight caught the etched lines and made them swim. "Here's what nobody outside this room knows, and only four living people inside it. When the First Map was drawn, the Steppe wasn't sealed. It was *occupied*. Something lived at the center of it — something the earliest accounts describe only in negatives. Not tall, not short. Not loud, not silent. The scribes ran out of language and started running out of courage around the same time."

"So they sealed it," Beatrix said. "Under the salt."

"They didn't seal it." Ivo shook his head slowly. "Sealing implies effort, girl, a battle. There was no battle. By every account, the thing simply... agreed. Lay down. Let them pour the salt and speak the bindings and write the contracts, and it *cooperated*. Do you understand how much worse that is?"

Elias did not understand, but something in his chest had begun to understand for him — a slow, cold pressure, like deep water rising.

"Why would it cooperate?" Beatrix asked.

"That," said Ivo, "is the question that has eaten forty years of my life, and I will tell you my answer, which may be worthless: because it was tired. Because it had been at war — the war the Choir sings about, the war that broke the world the first time — and it wanted rest. And so it made them a bargain. Sleep, in exchange for—"

He stopped. Both of them looked at him.

"In exchange for what?" Elias asked.

"A vessel," Ivo said gently. "A place to keep its dreams. You don't put a fire out, boy. You bank it. You give it somewhere to smolder where it can't spread. And every few generations, the embers need somewhere warm to live."

The pressure in Elias's chest rose another inch. His hands had gone numb. He thought absurdly, wildly, of the surveyor's office, of the night shifts, of the ordinary gray life he'd built out of three years of nothing — and how even that life had been a kind of container. Walls. Routine. Smallness.

"No," he said, and heard how unconvincing it sounded even to himself.

"Touch the map," Ivo said.

"I'd really rather not."

"Boy." The old man's voice was kind and immovable as bedrock. "It's been calling you since you walked in. I could hear the glass singing from the kitchen. Touch it, or spend the rest of your life wondering, and wondering is far crueler than knowing. I promise you that. Wondering is the interest; knowing is just the debt."

Beside him, Beatrix shifted. He felt her looking at him — felt it the way you feel sunlight through a window. She didn't say *don't*. He realized, distantly, that she probably wanted to, and was honoring him by keeping her mouth shut, and that this might be the most generous thing anyone had ever done for him.

Elias reached out and laid his palm flat against the glass.

The song hit him like weather.

It wasn't sound, exactly — it came up through his bones rather than his ears, a vast low chord like wind through a canyon the size of a country, like the hum of a power line carrying the current of a continent. The glass warmed under his hand. The etched rivers brightened. And the Absence — the god-shaped hollow at the center of everything — *filled in*, just for a heartbeat, just enough for him to see.

Salt plains under a sky like hammered tin. A figure standing at the center of them, vast past sense, made of the same stuff as the horizon, wearing stillness like a coat. And it turned toward him — it had been waiting a thousand years to turn toward him — and though it had no face, Elias felt it look at him with an attention so total and so gentle that his knees buckled.

And it *knew* him. Not his name. Deeper than his name. It knew the taste of the copper coins in his pocket. It knew the dream he'd had last Tuesday about a door in a field. It knew his mother's hands — no, it knew hands older than his mother's, hands that had held a squalling infant over a contract and wept while signing—

Elias snatched his hand back and sat down hard on the floor.

The chord faded slowly, reluctantly, the way a bell stops ringing. In the silence after, the only sounds were his own ragged breathing and, faintly, from somewhere under the floorboards, under the foundations, under the whole city — the glass of the world, still singing.

"Well," said Ivo, into the quiet. "Tea, I think. Strong. Very strong. Possibly with brandy in it, and possibly just brandy."

Elias stared at his own hand. It looked ordinary. It had always looked ordinary. It was attached to an ordinary man who paid his rent late and burned toast and had once cried at a dog food commercial. It was not attached to anything that made ancient glass sing.

"What did you see?" Beatrix crouched beside him. Her voice was careful, the way you talk near a frightened animal, and he hated that he was the frightened animal, and loved her for doing it anyway.

"Salt," he managed. "And something standing in it. It looked at me like—" He stopped, swallowed. "Like I was expected. Like I was *late*."

Neither of them laughed. He'd hoped someone would laugh. Nobody did.

"The vessel line passes down through blood," Ivo said from the kitchen doorway, mugs in hand, steam rising off them like incense. "Usually weakly. A great-grandmother who dreamed true. A cousin who could calm storms by sulking at them. Small change. But every so often—" he handed Elias a mug, and Elias's fingers were shaking too badly to hold it steady until Beatrix covered his hand with hers, "—every so often the blood comes down clean and full. Unmixed. Unspent. Someone sold at birth, perhaps, by parents who knew exactly what they were carrying and wanted it somewhere the Choir couldn't reach."

"My mother sold me," Elias said flatly. "To the Guild."

"Your mother sold you *to safety*, most likely, and the Guild bought you *as inventory*, and both things can be true at once." Ivo settled back into his chair with a groan. "People are rarely villains in their own ledgers. They're just accountants with bad information."

Elias drank the tea. It was terrible. It helped.

"So that's it, then," he said. "That's the big reveal. I'm a — what, a thermos? A flask for a god?"

"A vessel," Beatrix said softly.

"A vessel. Right. And if the prophecy finishes — the one with my name in century-old ink — the god wakes up, and the war starts again, and everyone gets to relive the apocalypse." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm a night-shift surveyor. I file reports about drainage easements. Three weeks ago the most exciting thing in my life was a vending machine that gave double change."

"And now?" Ivo asked.

Elias looked at his hand again. Then, before he could stop himself, he reached out and touched the map once more — lightly, the way you touch a stove to check if it's still hot.

The glass sang one soft note. Just one. Almost friendly. Almost *pleased*.

And beneath his feet, beneath the floorboards, beneath the streets and the salt and the thousand years of carefully written law, something immense rolled over in its sleep — and smiled.

From the kitchen, faintly, every cup in Ivo's cupboard rattled once, all together, like applause.

Ivo looked at the cupboard. Then at Elias. Then at Beatrix, with the expression of a man revising forty years of careful estimates upward by a significant margin.

"Girl," he said, "how soon can the Choir find us here?"

Beatrix was already moving to the window, already pulling back the curtain a finger's width, and the answer was in her shoulders before she said it.

"Tonight," she said. "Maybe sooner. We need to leave now, and Ivo — we need the map."

"The map?" Ivo clutched the oilcloth to his chest like a scandalized grandmother. "Absolutely not. This is the single most dangerous document in existence. It doesn't leave this house."

"It has to. If the Choir gets it, they get the rite's missing verses. If Voss gets it, she gets leverage over Elias forever. And if *we* get it—" she glanced at Elias, and her voice gentled into something almost apologetic, "—we might be able to teach him to close the door instead of opening it."

Ivo looked at her. Then at Elias, who was watching the cupboard with the expression of a man whose body had recently become a haunted house. Then, for a long moment, at the map itself, its silver rivers swimming in the lamplight, its god-shaped absence waiting patiently at the center of everything, the way it had waited for a thousand years.

"Oh, hang it all," Ivo said, and began wrapping the map with trembling, decisive hands. "Forty years I've kept this safe by keeping it still. Apparently stillness isn't on the menu anymore." He thrust the bundle into Elias's arms — and Elias felt the glass warm against his chest, felt the chord settle into his ribs like a cat curling up, like something coming home.

"One condition," Ivo said, shrugging into a coat approximately the size and shape of a small tent. "Wherever you're going — I'm coming. I've spent my whole life studying the Absence from the outside. I should like, before I die, to see what it looks like from within arm's reach of the man who fills it."

Outside, far across the dark city, a bell began to ring — not the hour, not the alarm. A third thing. A rhythm Elias somehow recognized the way you recognize a tune you've never heard, and his blood answered it before his mind could name it.

Choir practice, his mind supplied uselessly. Ash Choir. Evening service.

They were already singing.

"Back door," Beatrix said.
