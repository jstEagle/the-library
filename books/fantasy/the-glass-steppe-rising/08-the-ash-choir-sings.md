## 8. The Ash Choir Sings

Ivo Quill made tea the way he did everything else: slowly, ceremonially, and with the firm conviction that anyone who disagreed was simply hurrying toward the wrong life.

"Four minutes," he said, holding the sand-glass up to the lamplight as though it might confess to tampering. "Not three and a half. Not five. Four. The difference between tea and punishment is four minutes, Beatrix."

"You've told me this every visit since I was nineteen."

"And you've made bad tea every visit since you were nineteen. One of us is learning."

Elias sat at the archive's long reading table with the half-map spread before him — Ivo's half, torn down the middle in some ancient argument no one could reconstruct — and watched them bicker with the expression of a man who had discovered, three days into knowing her, that Beatrix Montrose had a family she hadn't mentioned: one irritable old cartographer in a cellar full of impossible geography.

"The god-shaped absence," Elias said, tapping the map's blank center. "You said the first surveyors drew everything *around* it. Why not just draw the absence itself?"

Ivo lowered himself into his chair with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. "Because, boy, a map is an agreement. You can only agree on what everyone can see. And nobody sees what sleeps there. They feel it. Like standing next to a door and knowing someone's behind it." He sipped his tea, closed his eyes, and pronounced judgment: "Perfect."

"You're showing off," Beatrix said.

"I'm eighty-one years old. Showing off is all I have left." But he smiled when he said it, and the lamplight caught the ink stains on his fingers — sixty years of stains, layered like tree rings — and Beatrix felt something she almost never let herself feel in the archives below the Steppe.

Safe.

She should have known better. She was, after all, a professional.

* * *

The singing started at midnight.

At first Beatrix thought it was the glass settling — the Steppe sang sometimes, faint harmonic complaints when the temperature dropped, like a cathedral clearing its throat. But this was voices. Many voices. Rising from the stairwell that connected Ivo's archive to the surface, and they were singing something old, something with the cadence of a lullaby sung by people who had never been children.

Ivo set down his cup. The porcelain didn't click against the saucer; he placed it so gently that the silence of it was worse than any noise.

"How many entrances does this place have?" Elias whispered.

"One," Ivo said. "That was always the point."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be accurate. Accuracy is its own comfort, if you're brave about it." He rose, crossed to the great iron door at the top of the short stair, and pressed his ear against it the way a physician listens for a heartbeat. His face went carefully blank, which from Ivo was a scream.

"They're early," he said. "The Choir wasn't due for another week. I had it in my diary."

"You have assassins in your *diary*?" Elias said.

"I have everything in my diary. Otherwise how would I know what to dread on which day?" He turned, and now Beatrix saw it — the thing underneath the dryness, the reason he'd spent six decades guarding a room full of paper in the middle of nowhere. "Beatrix. The map. Take the map and the boy and go through the salt gallery. There's a door in the west wall behind the third false coastline. It opens onto the border towns."

"You're coming with us."

"I am going to hold a door." He said it the way other men said *I am going to die*, but with better posture. "The Choir can't complete anything if the archive stays sealed, and it stays sealed if someone keeps the bar down. The bar weighs more than I do. Fortunately, so does my stubbornness."

"Ivo—"

"Beatrix Montrose." He used her whole name, and the archive seemed to lean in to listen. "I have spent my entire life keeping accounts of other people's courage. Let me make one entry of my own."

The singing stopped.

In the silence that followed, Beatrix heard something far worse than voices: the sound of the iron door beginning to glow. A dull orange seam appeared along its edge, thin as a hair, brightening.

"Ash Choir," Ivo said conversationally, as though reporting weather. "They burn what they cannot open. Rude of them. The door is older than their whole religion."

The seam widened. Through it came light, and heat, and the smell of something burning that was not wood or oil but *certainty* — a smell Beatrix had encountered once before, at a guild tribunal, and never forgotten.

"*Go,*" Ivo said.

* * *

What Beatrix remembered afterward, in the years when she permitted herself to remember anything, came in fragments:

Elias hauling the half-map off the table while she grabbed the Codex and Ivo's spare spectacles off their hook because some part of her brain insisted he'd want them later, and grief had already begun lying to her.

The salt gallery — a corridor walled with compressed centuries, each stratum a different era's mistakes, glittering like frozen weather.

Ivo's voice behind them, raised not in fear but in *address*, the way he spoke at guild functions: "Gentlemen. Ladies. You are standing on a surveyed floor. Every tile of it is accounted for. I wonder whether you've considered how thoroughly I can collapse it."

The Choir's answer was a single word, sung rather than spoken, and the salt walls wept condensation when they heard it.

And then — at the mouth of the escape passage, with the false coastline already rolled back and chalk-dust hanging in the air — Beatrix looked back.

She would tell herself forever after that she looked back to check his position. Cartographers check positions. It was practically a job requirement.

But she looked back, and she saw Ivo Quill standing before the great iron door with both hands flat against it, an old man holding back a furnace with his palms and his patience, and he turned his head just enough to find her in the dark, and he winked.

Then he spoke the collapsing words, and the archive came down between them like a held breath finally released, and the last thing the world gave her of Ivo Quill was the sound of him humming — four minutes exactly, perfect tea, perfect timing — under the roar of the fall.

* * *

They walked for a long time after that. Neither of them suggested stopping.

The passage debouched into a gully on the Steppe's eastern margin, where the glass gave way to honest dirt and scrub grass grew in stubborn defiance of every law of the ledger. Dawn was coming up the color of weak tea — three minutes' steeping, Beatrix thought absurdly, *he'd have hated it* — and Elias spread the half-map on a flat stone and stared at it without seeing it.

"He knew," Elias said finally. "He knew they were coming. It was in his diary."

"Yes."

"So why didn't he leave? Why didn't *we* leave yesterday?"

"Because leaving means the archive falls anyway, and the Choir gets everything in it, and Ivo dies somewhere inconvenient instead of somewhere that mattered." Beatrix heard her own voice and disliked it: level, tidy, the voice she used for closing accounts. She tried again. "He chose the version of the story where his death buys something. That's not bravery, exactly. It's bookkeeping. He was very good at it."

Elias looked at her. "Is that how you're going to survive this? Bookkeeping?"

"It's how I survive everything."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "It's how you *avoid* everything. There's a difference. I've had three days of you, and even I can see the difference."

She opened her mouth to explain — kindly, precisely, with citations — that he was a stranger carrying a god in his blood and she was the only person standing between him and every power on the Steppe, and that strangers did not get to audit her coping mechanisms.

What came out instead, to her considerable surprise, was: "He taught me to read maps. Before the Guild, before any of it. I was eleven and I couldn't stop crying over my sister's death and he put a compass in my hand and said, 'Girl, grief has no scale bar. So we'll give you one.' And I've been measuring things ever since, because measured things can't hurt you." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Except they can. They absolutely can. I just keep writing the entries wrong."

Elias said nothing. He was learning, she noted distantly, when not to fill silence. It was one of his better qualities, and she resented it.

The sun cleared the horizon. Light ran across the Steppe like fire across paper, and far to the west — impossibly far, miles of glass away — something glinted that was not sunrise. A column of smoke, thin and straight, rising from the direction of the fallen archive. Except the archive had fallen inward, buried, sealed. Nothing there should burn.

Unless the Choir dug.

Unless the Choir found the lower vaults, the ones even Ivo had only hinted at, the ones containing the *other* half of things.

"Give me the map," Beatrix said.

Elias handed it over. She smoothed it on the stone, weighted the corners with pebbles, and studied the torn edge — the ragged line where Ivo's half ended and the missing half began. For sixty years the two halves had been kept apart by design, because whoever first split the map had understood something the Guild had spent a century forgetting:

Some knowledge is a load-bearing wall. Remove it, and the whole structure comes down.

The tear ran straight through the god-shaped absence. Which meant the missing half contained the center. Which meant the Ash Choir — who had burned their way through an old man and a door older than their faith — would not stop until they held it.

"We need the other half," Elias said.

"Yes."

"Where is it?"

Beatrix traced the tear with one finger and felt the answer settle over her like a debt coming due. Because she knew. Of course she knew. She was the keeper of the ledgers; she had catalogued the acquisition herself, years ago, one line among thousands, filed under *instruments, cartographic, restricted*:

*Half-map, provenance disputed, held in trust, Vault Nine, Guildhouse.*

Held in trust by Sarelle Voss.

Which meant that to finish what Ivo died protecting, she would have to walk into the office of the woman hunting them, smile, and ask to see the collection.

"There's a town two days east," Beatrix said, rolling the map with hands that were almost steady. "Called Ledger's End. We'll need food, papers, and a plan. In that order."

"And the plan is?"

She slung her bag over her shoulder and started walking, because it was easier to say terrible things in motion.

"The plan," she said, "is that Sarelle Voss thinks I'm a rule-follower who got in over her head protecting a stray. And the best way to confirm a belief is to walk through the front door wearing it." She glanced back at Elias, and for the first time since the archive fell, she smiled — small, sharp, and entirely her sister's smile, though she no longer remembered whose it had been. "Besides. I owe the Choir a debt, and I owe Voss a debt, and I keep excellent books."

Behind them, the smoke over the archive stood straight in the windless morning, like a candle lit for the dead.

Beatrix did not look back again. She wrote him down instead — silently, in the ledger behind her ribs, in the hand that was hers alone: *Ivo Quill. Sixty years of service. Four minutes, exactly. Account settled in full.*

Then she squared her shoulders, pointed east, and began, with Elias Vane beside her, the long walk toward the one door on the Steppe more dangerous than any burning.

The door marked *Voss*.
