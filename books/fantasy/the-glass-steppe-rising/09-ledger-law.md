## 9. Ledger Law

The border town was called Meridian's Rest, which Elias thought was a strange name for a place that never seemed to rest at all. It sat on the edge of the Glass Steppe like a barnacle on the hull of a ship — a crooked sprawl of lantern-lit streets where the glass plain met ordinary dirt, and where everything from bottled weather to secondhand luck changed hands over scarred wooden counters.

They had been there three days, hiding in a rented room above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and old rope. Three days of Beatrix Montrose teaching him things no school had ever offered: how to walk through a market without being *noticed*, how to read the small chalk marks shopkeepers left on their doorframes like warnings scrawled for friends, and how to keep his shadow behaving.

That last one was harder than it sounded.

"Again," Beatrix said, sitting cross-legged on the floorboards across from him, a stub of candle between them. "Your shadow just tried to point east."

Elias looked down. His shadow lay flat against the boards, obedient as any shadow, but he could feel it now — that faint tug, like a dog straining at a leash. Toward the Steppe. Toward whatever slept under the salt.

"It's not doing it on purpose," he said.

"Nothing in the Steppe does anything on purpose. That's rather the problem." She tapped the floorboard beside her. "Sit properly. Feet flat. A shadow points where its owner is pointed, unless the owner has something in him that disagrees about direction. So we teach your feet to win the argument."

He shifted his feet. The tug eased, barely.

"You're enjoying this," he accused.

"A little," she admitted, and the corner of her mouth did the thing it did when she was trying not to smile — a quick, reluctant flicker, like a fish surfacing. "You have terrible posture and an uncooperative metaphysical appendage. It's the most fun I've had in years."

* * *

The lessons proper began the next morning, in the back room of a tea house called the Second Cup, which served only one cup per customer because, the proprietor explained cheerfully, "the first one belongs to whoever you used to be."

Beatrix ordered two first cups and paid for them with a coin that Elias noticed, when she handed it over, had her own profile stamped on it — younger, smiling, wearing a hat he'd never seen her wear.

"That's you," he said, once they'd settled into a corner booth.

"It was me. Or a version of me I sold to a mint in Vell, who needed a face for their currency and didn't care whose." She turned the teacup slowly. "She gets around. I see her on coins sometimes. She looks happy."

"Do you miss her?"

Beatrix considered this with the seriousness she gave everything — the same level attention she gave a map or a ledger line, as though the question were terrain to be surveyed honestly.

"I miss the hat," she said finally. "It was a good hat. Now. Lesson one."

She produced a small notebook — not one of the guild's great ledgers, but a pocket-sized thing with a cracked spine — and laid it on the table between them.

"The Steppe runs on three laws. Not ten. Not seven. Three, and every one of them is simpler and crueler than it sounds. First law: **debts are binding**. If you owe something in the Steppe — money, time, a favor, a memory — the debt exists as a real object until it's paid. It has weight. It can be carried, stolen, inherited, or enforced. Debts are the closest thing the Steppe has to gravity."

"Gravity," Elias repeated. "You mean people fall toward what they owe?"

"People get *pulled*. I've seen a man dragged three miles by an unpaid promise. He survived. The promise didn't." She flipped the notebook open. "Second law: **names are currency**. Your name isn't just what people call you. It's the record of you — every place you've been written down, every account you appear in. The more places your name is written, the more you're worth, and the more of you there is to take. People here spend names the way you'd spend silver. Carefully. Because—"

"Because if your name is written somewhere, someone can collect on it," Elias said slowly. "Like my contract. My mother wrote me into something before I could even talk, and that made me... collectible."

Something crossed Beatrix's face — that flinch she always had when his mother came up, guilt-shaped, quickly filed away. "Yes. Exactly. And third law, the one that governs the other two: **the law is whatever is written first**. Not what's true. Not what's fair. Not even what's written *better*. If two documents disagree, the older one wins. The Steppe is built on precedence the way a house is built on foundations. Which means—" she looked up at him, and her voice went quiet and precise, "—whoever writes first, rules. Everything else is negotiation."

Elias stared at the notebook. "And the prophecy. In the Century Codex. It was written a hundred years ago."

"Now you understand why we don't simply tear the page out," Beatrix said. "Tearing out pages doesn't erase what was written first. It just makes everyone very angry and slightly blind."

* * *

The afternoon lesson took place in the market itself, because Beatrix believed — with the fervor of the truly converted — that ledger law could only be learned by nearly dying of it.

"Watch my hands," she murmured, steering him through the crowd with a grip on his sleeve. "Not the goods. The goods are bait. Watch what people *write*."

And once he knew to look, Elias couldn't stop seeing it. A fruit-seller scratching a tally onto a slate after every sale — each mark a tiny contract. A woman pressing her thumbprint into a stranger's palm before handing over a wrapped bundle, leaving a faint ink ghost behind. Two men arguing heatedly over a scrap of paper while a third stood between them with a magnifying glass, reading dates.

"What's happening there?" Elias asked.

"Debt dispute. One of them bought the other's Tuesday." Beatrix said it the way she might say *bought the other's horse*. "The seller is claiming the Tuesday in question was already spent. The buyer says it wasn't. The reader is checking which claim was recorded first."

"Who wins?"

"The paper that's older. Always." She paused at a stall selling what appeared to be jars of fog, each labeled in careful script. "Which is why the first lesson of survival in the Steppe is not how to fight. It's how to write yourself small."

"How do you mean?"

She bought a jar of fog — haggling fiercely over whether it was morning fog or evening fog, since apparently the difference was worth four coppers — and led him to a quiet bench near the town's edge, where the glass plain shimmered beyond the last rooftops like a frozen sea.

"Every time your name goes into a record, you become easier to find, easier to price, and easier to collect," she said, setting the jar down between them. "So you learn to be *barely written*. Pay in anonymous coin. Rent rooms under descriptions instead of names — 'the tall man with the bad boots' owes nothing and can be owed nothing. Sign nothing. Say your name aloud as rarely as possible, because spoken names stick to walls here; the Steppe hears them." She glanced sideways at him. "You've been doing the opposite your whole life, haven't you? Signing things. Leases. Work orders. Tax forms."

"I'm a surveyor. Signing things is most of the job."

"Mm. You've been writing yourself *large*. Every signature a hook waiting for a fisherman." She opened the cracked notebook to a blank page and held out a pencil. "So. Write yourself small. Right now. One sentence describing Elias Vane that would let a stranger pick him out of a crowd, but wouldn't let a collector find him."

Elias took the pencil. He thought for a long moment.

*The tall man with the bad boots,* he wrote.

Beatrix read it and made a face. "The boots are genuinely terrible, so it's honest, but 'tall' is a category, not a description. Half the Steppe is tall. Try again. Smaller."

*A man who is tired.*

"Better. Depressingly better. Again."

*A man who is tired, who owes a cartographer his life and intends to pay it.*

Beatrix went still. She read the line twice, and when she looked up, the reluctant-fish smile had surfaced fully and stayed.

"That," she said, "is the smallest sentence anyone has ever written about me. Most people use entire paragraphs and get it wrong."

* * *

Over the following days, the lessons wove themselves into the shape of their hiding. He learned to haggle without giving his name, to buy bread by pointing, to sleep with his shoes facing the door and his shadow tucked under the bed like a misbehaving cat. He learned that Beatrix drank her tea bitter enough to strip varnish, that she talked to maps under her breath as though they were nervous horses, and that she kept a running mental account of every kindness done to her and grew visibly uncomfortable until she could repay it.

"She's going to notice us staring," Elias said one evening. They were watching her from the chandler's window as she argued with a lamplighter over a chalked door-mark, gesturing with the authority of a woman who had personally invented doors.

Ivo Quill — who had turned up in Meridian's Rest two days after the archive burned, singed and stubborn and refusing to discuss his own survival — snorted from his chair by the fire. "She notices everything. That's the trouble with her. Best cartographer the Guild ever broke, and they broke her because she kept noticing the wrong things." He puffed his pipe. "You trust her yet?"

Elias watched her throw up her hands at the lamplighter, who was clearly winning the argument through sheer refusal to understand.

"I trust her arithmetic," he said. "Everything she's spent on me, she's counted. Out loud, usually. While glaring."

"That's the highest compliment available in this economy," Ivo said. "In the Steppe, love is just a debt you never want settled."

* * *

On the sixth night, Beatrix spread the half-map across the floor of their room — the fragment they'd pulled from the burning archive, its edges charred, its center marked with the god-shaped absence that made Elias's teeth ache to look at.

"We can't stay here," she said. "Voss's auditors reached the border towns yesterday. They'll work outward from the Guild offices, and this town keeps Guild books. By week's end, every bed in Meridian's Rest will be a question with our names as the answer."

"So we run deeper into the Steppe."

"We cross it. There's a passage broker in Salt Hollow who moves people off the books entirely — no records, no names, clean as an unwritten page. But brokers like that don't take coin." She was quiet for a moment, studying the map, and Elias saw her hand drift to the pocket where she kept her notebook, the cracked-spine one, touching it the way other people touched locket photographs.

"What does a broker like that take?" he asked.

Beatrix looked up. For once, she didn't answer immediately, and the pause had weight — the specific, terrible weight of a number she had already calculated and didn't want to say aloud.

"Lesson eight," she said lightly, too lightly, rolling up the map. "Is that some prices aren't discussed until the buyer's ready. Get some sleep, Elias. We leave at dawn."

But he lay awake long after her breathing evened out, staring at the ceiling, turning over the laws she'd taught him. *Debts are binding. Names are currency.* And beneath them, unspoken but everywhere in the way she guarded that little notebook, a fourth law she hadn't written down:

*Everything is for sale, and the seller always knows the cost before the buyer does.*

Outside, far out on the dark glass, something sang — low and patient, a note felt in the floorboards more than heard. His shadow stirred under the bed.

And in the morning, when Beatrix packed her bag, Elias caught a glimpse of the notebook's latest page before she closed it. One line, freshly written, in her small exact hand:

*One memory of Wren. Fair value. No refunds.*

He didn't know who Wren was.

He was beginning to be afraid he was about to find out.
