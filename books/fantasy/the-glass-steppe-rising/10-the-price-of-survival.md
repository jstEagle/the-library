## 10. The Price of Survival

The border town of Lowmark smelled of rain on hot iron and frying onions, which Elias decided was the most honest smell he'd encountered since a door drawn in chalk had swallowed his entire life three days ago.

"Keep your hood up," Beatrix said, "and don't sign anything."

"I haven't signed anything in my life."

"You will. Everyone does eventually. That's how they get you — not with knives, with pens." She adjusted her satchel and steered him past a shopfront whose window displayed, neatly labeled, seven jars of bottled Tuesday afternoons. "Lowmark sits on the Steppe's eastern seam. Half the people here are guild, half are running from it, and the rest are selling both sides soup."

"It's that kind of town?"

"It's every kind of town. That's the point of borders." She said it lightly, but her eyes were doing the thing they did now — sweeping rooftops, counting exits, weighing strangers by the weight of their shadows. Three days on the run had taught Elias that look. He was starting to hate it, mostly because he knew what put it there: him.

They found lodging above a tavern called The Second Draft, whose sign depicted a beer and a document being revised. The landlady, a broad woman with ink-stained knuckles and a smile like an open ledger, looked at them exactly one second too long.

"Two rooms?" she asked.

"One," Beatrix said. "We're married."

Elias made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

The landlady grinned. "Married, are you. What's your name, love?"

"Ansel," Elias said, before his brain could veto it.

"And hers?"

"He calls me Ansel's wife," Beatrix said smoothly. "Very traditional."

The landlady laughed, took coin — real coin, Elias noticed; Beatrix had been paying in small brass tokens stamped with numbers instead of faces, and the landlady had counted them twice, then a third time with visible respect — and led them up narrow stairs to a room with one bed, one chair, and a window overlooking a courtyard where someone had chalked an enormous door onto a brick wall. It didn't open. It just sat there, being a drawing, radiating patience.

"Don't touch that," Beatrix said, catching him staring.

"What is it?"

"A joke. Or a trap. In this town those are usually the same thing." She set down her bag and finally, visibly, let her shoulders come down from around her ears. It was the first time he'd seen her do that. It made her look younger, and more tired, and more stubborn all at once.

"So," Elias said, sitting carefully on the bed as if it might bill him, "we're hiding."

"We're resting. Hiding is what we do when we stop moving. Resting is what we do while we plan the next move." She pulled off her boots and flexed her toes with genuine reverence. "Tomorrow we buy passage west, past the Salt Verge, to a place called Threnody. There's a cartographer there who owes me. Ivo Quill. He'll know what the Codex fragment means."

"The half-map we stole while zealots burned down an archive."

"We *salvaged*," Beatrix corrected. "Stolen things can be traced. Salvage is a legitimate trade activity. I have receipts."

"You have receipts for looting."

"I have receipts for everything. That's the whole point of me." She smiled at him then — quick, crooked, real — and Elias felt the guilt arrive right on schedule, the way it did every time she smiled at him these days. Because the arithmetic never changed. Everything this woman spent, she spent on him. And he was beginning, slowly, horribly, to understand the exchange rate.

* * *

Passage to Threnody, it turned out, could not be bought with coin.

The ferryman who worked the Salt Verge crossing kept a booth at the end of Lowmark's long pier, a lean structure built entirely out of stacked ledgers, spines outward, so that the whole building read like a wall of judgment. His name was Corvin Ashpole, and he had the face of a man who had heard every excuse ever invented and priced them all.

"No coin," he said, before Beatrix finished sitting down. He had a voice like wet gravel learning to be polite. "Coin's for tourists. Crossing the Verge costs memory. One year per passenger. Standard rate."

"Standard rate," Beatrix repeated. "Corvin, when did you last update your rates?"

"Never. Rates are sacred."

"Your rates were set by a man who died forty years ago because he tried to cross his own river during a flood."

"And a fine death it was." Corvin folded his hands. "One year each."

Beatrix reached into her coat and produced a slim black book. Elias recognized it with a lurch: her personal ledger, the one she guarded the way other people guarded their children. She laid it on the counter and opened it, and Elias caught a glimpse of pages dense with her small precise handwriting — entries, dates, names — before she angled it away.

"Let's talk about what you actually owe," she said.

"This isn't about what I owe—"

"Page twelve. You owe the Guild a crossing fee you've deferred eleven times, currently accruing interest at the Steppe's standard rate, which, Corvin, you know better than anyone compounds like regret. Page thirty-one: your booth's foundations are written as belonging to a pier that was decommissioned in the Year of the Quiet Tide. Technically, you're squatting inside a legal fiction. Lovely fiction. Very profitable. But fictions need maintenance, and maintenance requires friends." She turned another page. "Shall I go on, or shall we call it two crossings for the price of one memory?"

Corvin's gravel-voice came out smaller than gravel should. "One year. Total. And you keep the book shut."

"Done." She uncapped her pen.

Elias watched, fascinated despite himself, as the transaction happened. There was no ceremony. Beatrix simply wrote in her own ledger — *Paid: one year, to C. Ashpole, passage ×2, Verge crossing* — and then held the pen over a second line and paused.

Only a pause. A breath. Then she wrote something else, short and final, and closed the book, and it was done, and her face hadn't moved at all except to become very slightly, almost imperceptibly, lighter. Like a room where a picture had been taken off the wall. Nothing wrong with the room. Just a pale rectangle where something used to hang.

"That's it?" Elias asked, on the pier afterward. The wind off the salt flats pulled at their hoods. "You paid a *year*? Of what? Where does it go?"

"Of my life, presumably. Somewhere. Or of my sleep, or my summers — the ledger doesn't specify, and honestly it's better not to ask, because asking makes it itemized, and itemized debts collect faster." She squinted out at the white glare of the Verge. "It'll take a few days to feel. You wake up one morning and the year's gone. Not missing — gone. No gap, no ache. You just know less than you knew, and you can't tell what."

"That's monstrous."

"That's commerce." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "Everything here runs on it, Elias. Coin is a rumor. Names are currency. Memory is the hard money of the realm, because it's the only thing everyone carries and no one can counterfeit." She glanced at him sideways. "You paid nothing today. Notice that. Keep noticing it."

"Why would you pay a year for me?" The question came out blunter than he intended. "You've known me four days. Four days ago I was a man who surveyed drainage ditches."

"Yes," she said. "And now you're the vessel of a sealed god with a bounty on your soul and half a prophecy growing under your skin like ivy. The drainage ditch work was going nowhere anyway."

"That's not an answer."

She stopped walking. Out here, between the town and the salt, there was nothing to hide behind — just flat white country under a sky so wide it seemed embarrassed to contain them. She looked at him, and for once she didn't do the accounting look. She did the other one. The human one.

"When I was nine years old," she said, "my sister fell through the ice on the Merrow pond. I was closer to her than anyone. And I froze. I stood there doing sums — distance, weight, whether the ice near the bank would hold me, whether shouting would help or crack it worse — and I did the sums *right*, Elias, every figure correct, and she went under before I finished calculating." Her voice stayed level. That was the terrible part. It stayed perfectly level, like water holding its surface over something drowning. "I became a cartographer because maps let you measure everything before it happens. I joined the Guild because ledgers let you settle everything before it hurts. I have spent my whole life making sure I am never again the person standing on the bank doing math."

"And paying a year of your life for a stranger is—"

"Not math." She started walking again. "It's the opposite of math. Don't make me explain it further, I'll start invoicing myself."

He laughed despite everything — despite the fear, despite the god coiled somewhere beneath his ribs like a second heartbeat waiting for its turn, despite the fact that she'd just handed him a piece of herself wrapped in a joke. Especially because of it, maybe.

But the guilt stayed. It walked beside him the whole way back, matching his stride.

* * *

That evening, in the room with the chalked door, Beatrix spread the salvaged half-map across the floor and weighted its corners with candlesticks, a boot, and a jar of the landlady's pickled quail eggs ("Borrowed," she said. "We'll return it. Probably.").

The map was beautiful and wrong. Roads bent where roads shouldn't. Rivers flowed uphill toward mountains that cast shadows in the wrong direction. And along the western edge, where the parchment simply stopped, the ink continued anyway — faint lines trailing off the paper's edge and onto nothing, as if the map had been drawn on a larger world and someone had cut it carelessly.

"There," Beatrix said. "The Salt Verge. And beyond it—" she tapped the ghost-lines hanging in empty air "—Threnody. Ivo Quill's territory."

"How does a map draw itself off the page?"

"Because the Steppe doesn't end at its edges. It ends where the writing stops." She sat back on her heels, and the candlelight did something unkind to the shadows under her eyes. "Which is why the Choir wants you. Which is why Voss wants you. And which is why tomorrow we cross the Verge before either of them remembers Lowmark exists."

"You think they've forgotten?"

"I think Sarelle Voss has never forgotten anything in her life. I'm hoping she's busy." She rolled the map with the tenderness of someone bandaging a wound. "Sleep. We leave at first light."

He slept badly. He dreamed of salt singing, and of a voice under the world saying, patiently, kindly, *you can put it all down now* — and woke before dawn to find Beatrix already awake, already dressed, sitting by the window with her personal ledger open on her knees.

She wasn't reading it. She was looking at a page the way you look at a scar.

"What did you pay?" he asked quietly. "Yesterday. At the pier. What was the memory?"

Her pen stilled. For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Outside, the false chalk door on the courtyard wall caught the first grey light and refused, as always, to open.

"My sister's laugh," she said at last. "Not her. Not what happened. Just — the sound. I remember that she laughed. I remember that I loved hearing it. I remember everything *about* it." She closed the book gently, like tucking something in. "I just can't hear it anymore. The ledger took the sound and left the meaning. That's how they work. They're very fair, and they're very cruel, and it's the same thing."

"Beatrix—"

"If you apologize," she said, "I will charge you for it. Emotional labor. Current rates." But her voice cracked on the last word, one hairline fracture in all that level water, and she turned her face to the window fast, and pretended to check the street for assassins, and let him pretend to believe her.

Elias lay back down and stared at the ceiling and understood, finally, in his bones, what survival cost here. Not coin. Not blood. This: a laugh, traded away without flinching, so a stranger could live another day. And somewhere ahead — he could see it coming the way you see weather coming, a darkness gathering past the hills — there would be a price far larger than a laugh. She was already planning how to pay it. He could tell by the way she'd stopped doing math.

Below in the courtyard, someone new stood before the chalked door. A woman in travelling grays, unhurried, consulting a small black book. She raised her hand — not to knock, but to write — and began adding lines to the drawing that hadn't been there yesterday.

Lines that were, Elias realized with a cold drop in his stomach, addressed to them.
