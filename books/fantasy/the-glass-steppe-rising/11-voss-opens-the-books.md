## 11. Voss Opens the Books

The summons came by courier, which was how Sarelle Voss did everything: formally, punctually, and in a way that made you feel you'd already agreed to it.

*Guildmistress requests the presence of Keeper Montrose at her office, ninth bell tomorrow morning. The Guildmistress will be conducting a routine audit of accounts. Refreshments will be served.*

Beatrix read the note twice, then a third time, because *routine audit* from Sarelle Voss was like *routine surgery* from a butcher. She looked across the border-town inn's common room, where Elias sat hunched over a bowl of stew, learning to eat like a man who had spent three years believing his life was real.

"Good news?" he asked.

"Sarelle wants to see me."

"Is that bad?"

"She's bringing refreshments."

He considered this. "Bad," he concluded, and went back to his stew.

---

Sarelle Voss kept her office on the Steppe itself, in a tower of smoked glass that stood precisely where no tower had any business standing — which was to say, she had written it there, and the glass had obeyed. Beatrix arrived at the ninth bell exactly, because arriving late to Sarelle gave her leverage, and arriving early gave her more.

The office smelled of cedar, ink, and old paper — the smell Beatrix privately thought of as *home*, which annoyed her, because Sarelle had cultivated that smell deliberately. Everything about the woman was deliberate. Her silver-streaked hair pinned with a quill-shaped pin. Her spectacles worn low on her nose so that she could look over them at people, which made everyone feel audited even when she was only passing the salt.

"Beatrix." Sarelle gestured to the chair opposite her desk. "Sit. Tea?"

"You hate tea."

"I keep it for people I'm being kind to." She poured anyway. "Consider it an omen."

On the desk between them lay a ledger. Not just any ledger — Beatrix recognized the binding instantly. It was her own career, bound in green calfskin, every entry she'd ever made for the Guild copied out in Sarelle's neat, merciless hand. Eleven years of work. Eleven years of entries.

"You've been busy," Beatrix said.

"It's what I do." Sarelle opened the book. "Now. Let's talk about your mercies."

Here it was, then. Beatrix wrapped her hands around the teacup — it really was terrible tea, Sarelle bought it specifically so guests would have nothing to enjoy — and braced.

"The Guild trades in impossible things," Sarelle began, in the tone of a lecturer addressing a promising but wayward student. "Bottled thunder. Debts paid in years. Doors to places that don't exist yet. And do you know why the trade functions, Beatrix? Why the whole mad machine doesn't collapse into a shrieking pile of paradox?"

"Because you terrify everyone."

"Because everything is *written down*." Sarelle tapped the ledger. "A debt exists because someone recorded it. A name has value because someone entered it. The moment we begin erasing things out of sympathy — out of *mercy* — the whole edifice wobbles. And you, my dear, have been wobbling us for years."

She turned the ledger around and slid it across the desk.

"Page forty-one. The widow Anselm, whose husband traded away their daughter's laughter and couldn't pay. You wrote the debt down as 'settled in full' and covered the difference out of your own account. Three years of your life, Beatrix. You paid a stranger's debt with three years of your own life."

Beatrix said nothing. The tea was terrible. She drank it anyway.

"Page sixty-eight. The surveyor Pell, who falsified a map and should have been struck from the rolls — name erased, history erased, unmapped. Unmapping is a death sentence, you know that better than anyone. Instead you rewrote his map yourself, corrected, and logged the error under your own seal. He still walks around thinking he got lucky."

"He was young. It was an honest mistake."

"There are no honest mistakes in accounting. That's rather the point of accounting." Sarelle turned another page, and another. "Page ninety. The child who wandered through a chalk door and got stranded on the far Steppe for six days. Protocol said log him as lost, claim the territory he'd touched. You carried him home yourself and burned the record. Page one hundred and two — shall I go on?"

"You clearly intend to."

"There are forty-seven instances." Sarelle closed the ledger gently, almost tenderly. "Forty-seven times you reached into the machinery of the Guild and quietly unjammed it with your own hands, your own name, your own blood. Do you know what I find remarkable? Not that you did it. Anyone can be soft. What I find remarkable is that you were *good* at it. Every one of those mercies was clean. No loose threads. No witnesses. Beautifully done. I found them all anyway, but beautifully done."

There was a silence in which Beatrix became aware that her heart was doing something complicated.

"Why?" she asked finally. "Why dig through eleven years of my work looking for kindness?"

"Because I needed to know what you're worth." Sarelle removed her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve — a small domestic gesture that Beatrix knew was calculated to make her feel like they were two friends talking, and hated herself for half-believing. "You see, I have a problem. His name is Elias Vane, he is currently hiding in a border town eating stew, and he is worth more than every entry in this room combined. The contract on him predates both of us. His mother sold him before he could walk. The Choir wants him. Half the Steppe's creditors want him. And the one person I trusted to bring him in has instead burned a writ, declared him guild property, and vanished into my territory with him."

"You know about the writ."

"I know about everything. It's my job, and unlike yours, I don't do it with mercies." She folded her hands. "So here is what happens now, Beatrix. Here is the shape of your morning."

She opened a second ledger — a thin one, black, and Beatrix's stomach dropped, because black bindings were reserved for forfeitures.

"By guild law, harboring contracted property makes you liable for the contract's value plus penalties. Elias Vane's contract value is—" she glanced down, though Beatrix suspected she knew it by heart— "considerable. Considerable beyond anything you could pay in years or memories or names. By law, I should take your maps. Your seals. Your standing. Your name, eventually. You'd be unmapped within the season, and honestly, given what the Choir would pay me for your location, I'd be a fool not to."

"And yet," Beatrix said, "the tea."

"And yet the tea." Sarelle smiled, and it transformed her face from something carved into something almost warm, which was the most frightening thing she could do. "Because I read your ledger, Beatrix. All of it. And I found something I didn't expect. Underneath all those mercies, underneath the sentiment and the rule-breaking and the three years you gave to a widow you barely knew — I found the finest keeper this Guild has produced in two centuries. Precise. Creative. Loyal in the ways that matter. You broke rules the way a surgeon breaks skin: never for pleasure, always with a steady hand, and you closed what you opened." She leaned forward. "I don't want to unmake you. I want to *promote* you."

The word hung in the cedar-scented air.

"Deliver Elias Vane by month's end," Sarelle said. "Hand him to me intact, unharmed, and willing if you can manage it — and every one of those forty-seven mercies becomes retroactively sanctioned. Sanctioned and *paid*. The Guild reimburses your account. The widow's three years come back to you. Pell's error leaves your record. Forty-seven debts forgiven, forty-seven weights lifted. Plus the promotion. Senior Keeper. Your own territory. Authority to draw doors without countersignature." She paused delicately. "And immunity, permanent and total, for everything you've done these past weeks. The writ-burning. The harboring. All of it. A clean page, Beatrix. Do you understand what a clean page is worth, to a woman who keeps books?"

Beatrix understood exactly what it was worth. That was the horror of it. Sarelle hadn't built a trap; traps could be seen. She had built an *invoice* — itemized, fair-seeming, each line reasonable, the total ruinous.

"You'd forgive all of it," Beatrix said slowly. "For one man."

"For the *right* man. You've seen the Codex. You know what sleeps in his blood. You know what wakes if the prophecy runs its course. I'm not asking you to hurt him — I give you my word he'll be treated gently. Better than gently. There are ways to keep a vessel dormant, comfortable, dreaming. He'd live out his life in comfort, and the world would keep its shape." Sarelle spread her hands. "You've spent eleven years keeping accounts balanced so that ordinary people can live ordinary lives. This is the largest balance you'll ever be asked to keep. One uncomfortable man against a war that would burn every ledger, every map, every chalk door, every *widow* you ever saved. Weigh it, cartographer. You're good at weighing."

And the truly awful thing — the thing Beatrix would carry into the night like a stone — was that the scales didn't slam down on one side. They trembled. She thought of Elias's face when the glass sang under his feet, the wonder and the terror of it. She thought of the Ash Choir, whose song she'd heard once from a distance and never forgotten. She thought of Ivo Quill, dead holding a door, and of the widow Anselm getting three years of her own life back, laughing in a kitchen somewhere, never knowing why the light felt lighter.

Sarelle watched her weigh it. That was the worst part. She watched with patience and something horribly like respect.

"One night," Beatrix said.

Sarelle raised an eyebrow.

"You said month's end. I'm asking for one night to consider. Not because I'm undecided—" although she was, appallingly, "—but because a decision this size deserves a proper audit, and I learned auditing from the best."

Something flickered behind Sarelle's eyes — amusement, maybe, or the memory of having been young once, or possibly just indigestion from her own terrible tea.

"One night," Sarelle agreed. "Return by ninth bell tomorrow with your answer. And Beatrix—" she held up the green calfskin ledger, her copy, the record of forty-seven kindnesses— "whatever you decide, know that I meant what I said about your work. You are very good. It's why I'm offering. If you were mediocre, I'd simply have had you collected."

"How comforting."

"I'm told I have that effect." Sarelle rose, ending the audience the way she ended everything: formally, punctually. At the door, Beatrix paused.

"The mother," she said. "Elias's mother. Who bought the contract? Who was she selling to?"

Sarelle's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a door closing, softly, in a house of many doors.

"That information wasn't in the deal I offered," she said. "Ninth bell, Keeper."

---

The chalk door let Beatrix out into the border town's alley, where the evening smelled of rain and frying onions — ordinary smells, blessedly unaccounted-for. She found Elias on the inn's back step with the half-map spread across his knees and Ivo's compass beside him, and she must have looked the way she felt, because he took one look at her and said:

"What did she offer?"

"Everything."

"That bad, huh." He scooted over to make room on the step. "Want to tell me what 'everything' means, or should I guess?"

She told him. All of it — the forty-seven mercies, the reimbursement, the promotion, the word *dormant* delivered like a kindness. He listened without interrupting, which was new for him; three weeks ago he'd have interrupted four times and knocked the compass off the step twice.

"So if you give me up," he said at last, "you get your life back. All the pieces you gave away. And I get to sleep forever, comfortably, in a tower somewhere."

"Dreaming," Beatrix said. "Comfortably."

"Yeah. My mother said something similar once, I think, right before she sold me." He laughed, but it came out crooked. "Sorry. That was — I'm not trying to make this harder. Honestly? Part of me gets it. Part of me thinks you'd be stupid not to take it."

"Part of you is very generous with my stupidity."

They sat while the rain started, fine and cold, beading on the half-map until Ivo's careful ink began to blur at the edges. Beatrix watched the lines soften and found her chest tightening for reasons she couldn't file anywhere.

"Elias," she said. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You've asked me several strange things already. I've stopped keeping count."

"When you look at the Codex — the prophecy pages. What do you actually see?"

He frowned. "Words. Old words, mostly nonsense. Why?"

"No reason."

But it wasn't no reason, and she sat with it long after he went inside, turning the thought over the way she'd turn an unfamiliar coin. Because when *she* looked at the Codex, she saw prophecy — fixed, finished, inevitable, ink dry as bone after a hundred years. But the ink hadn't been dry. She'd checked. It had been wet. Wet, after a century.

Prophecies were records. Records were written after events, and dried with time.

Wet ink meant something else entirely. Wet ink meant the page was still being written.

Beatrix pulled her coat tighter against the rain and stared at the dark window where Elias moved behind the glass, lighting a lamp, unaware. One night, Sarelle had given her. But it occurred to her, with the slow cold certainty of a tide coming in, that the question of what to do with Elias Vane might not be hers to answer at all.

It might belong to whatever hand was still writing him.
