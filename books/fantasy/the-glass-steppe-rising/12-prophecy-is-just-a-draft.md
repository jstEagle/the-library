## 12. Prophecy Is Just a Draft

Elias had learned three things about border towns in the two weeks since he'd learned they existed at all.

First: nobody looked at you twice if you carried a ledger. A ledger said you belonged to something — a guild, a business, a debt — and belonging to something was safer than belonging to nothing.

Second: the stew at the Saltcellar Inn was ninety percent barley and ten percent ambition.

Third: Beatrix Montrose talked in her sleep, and what she talked about was margins.

"Quarter inch," she muttered into her crossed arms, three nights running now, her cheek pressed to the table beside a half-finished map of the borderlands. "Leave a quarter inch for the river. Rivers always lie about where they're going."

He'd covered her with his coat the first night. She'd woken with it folded neatly beside her and thanked him so briskly and formally that he'd felt like a clerk who'd handed over the wrong pen. The second night she'd fallen asleep mid-sentence, correcting him on his lettering — "your *e*s are drowning, Vane, give them air" — and woken an hour later without apology or acknowledgment, which, he was learning, was its own kind of trust. You didn't fall asleep across from someone unless some part of you had decided they were safe.

He was trying very hard to deserve that decision. He was also trying very hard not to think about the fact that he was, according to a book older than any nation, the body that a sleeping god intended to wake up in.

The Codex sat between them on the table, chained shut with a lock of braided iron that Ivo Quill had pressed into Beatrix's hands along with half a map and a dying instruction: *Don't let them finish it.*

They were hiding in plain sight, in the corner of a common room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, because Beatrix's theory was that the best place to hide a stolen prophecy was under a candle stub and a plate of bread. "Nobody robs a table," she'd said. "People rob vaults. Tables are beneath suspicion." She'd then fallen asleep on it, which Elias felt somewhat undermined the strategy but undeniably proved her commitment.

He hadn't touched the Codex since the archive. Since the glass had sung under his palm like a struck bell and Ivo had looked at him with an expression Elias kept finding again in the back of his mind — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man seeing a face he'd only ever known as an absence on a map.

But tonight the inn was quiet, the rain was working hard against the windows, and the candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and Elias found himself doing the thing he did when he couldn't sleep, which was work. Surveyor's habit. He took out his field notebook and began transcribing, from memory, the passage Ivo had shown him — the one with tonight's date, the one with his name, the one that had started everything.

*When the vessel walks unclaimed through the door of chalk,* he wrote, *the god shall rise through him as water rises through salt, and the war that was ended shall be begun, and the Steppe shall be His, and all that is mapped shall be unmapped—*

His pen stopped.

Not because he'd forgotten the words. Because he'd noticed something.

He read the line again. Then he flipped back through his notes — weeks of them, every scrap of prophecy anyone had quoted in his hearing, copied down in his neat, drowning-*e*d hand because writing things down was how he kept the world from sliding. He laid them side by side on the table and stared at them the way he'd stare at a survey that wouldn't close — two measurements of the same boundary that refused to agree.

The passages didn't match.

Not in wording. In *tense*. In emphasis. In the little details. The version Ivo had shown him said *through the door of chalk.* The version the Ash Choir's screed had shouted in the market square said *by the door of chalk.* The fragment Sarelle Voss had recited in her office, courteous as a knife being sharpened, hadn't mentioned the door at all — it went straight from *vessel* to *god*, as though the door were optional furniture.

Prophecies were supposed to be fixed. That was the whole horror of them. That was why he'd spent two weeks flinching at his own reflection. A prophecy was a sentence already written; he was just the ink drying.

Unless.

Elias sat very still, the way you sit when the ground shifts under a building and you want to know if it's going to keep shifting.

"Beatrix," he said.

She made a sound like a hinge that wanted oil.

"Beatrix. The Codex. When did the Century Codex get its name?"

She surfaced by degrees, one eye opening, hair stuck to her cheek at an angle that suggested she'd been dreaming about geometry. "Century because it covers a century of entries," she said, in the tone of a woman answering a question she considered beneath both of them. "It's resealed and rebound every hundred years. Why are we discussing binding history at — " she squinted at the clock on the mantel, which had been broken since before either of them arrived and showed a time that could generously be called *opinion* — "at whatever this is."

"When it's rebound. Who writes it?"

"Scribes. Successive scribes. It's a copying tradition, it goes back—" She stopped. He watched her stop, watched the sleep drain out of her face the way water drains out of a lock, watched her mind arrive where his had arrived by a different road, faster, because she was Beatrix and roads were her whole profession.

"It goes back a thousand years," she said quietly. "Through eleven bindings. Through dozens of hands." She sat up fully now, pulling the coat around her shoulders without seeming to notice she was doing it, and reached for the Codex. "Every copyist introduces errors. Every copyist makes *corrections*. We know this. It's the first thing they teach you about old texts — no text survives its readers intact."

"So which version is true?"

"That's the wrong question." Her fingers found the iron braid, worked the lock with the small key Ivo had given her, and her voice dropped into the register she used for things that frightened her, which was unfortunately indistinguishable from the register she used for inventory. "The right question is: was there ever a true version? Or has a thousand years of careful hands been *drafting*?"

The chain fell open. She lifted the cover.

Up close, in bad light, with tired eyes, Elias saw what he should have seen in the archive: the Codex wasn't one voice. It was many. The early pages were written in a hand like iron nails driven in rows. Later pages leaned and loosened. Margins filled with annotations in three colors of ink, some arguing with the main text, some striking words through, one — in a cramped, apologetic script — adding a note beside a line about the god's rising that read, in full, *I have checked this against the Salt Annals and believe it to be an interpolation. Left in for completeness.*

"Left in for completeness," Beatrix breathed. "Someone thought the end of the world was a footnote dispute."

She turned pages with increasing speed, and Elias watched her do the thing she did — the thing that had made her a celebrated cartographer before she was thirty, according to people who said it like it explained everything about her. She was reading the way she read maps: not for the territory but for the mapmaker. Where had the hand hesitated? Where had the pressure changed? Where had someone pressed too hard because they were afraid?

"Here," she said. "Look here. The date of your birth — the entry that flagged you to the Guild's contract in the first place. The ink is different from the surrounding entries. Newer. Someone *added* it decades after the page was written." She tilted the page toward the candle. "And here — the clause about delivery by month's end. That phrasing isn't liturgical. It's contractual. It's *Guild* phrasing. Someone translated the god's will into invoice language."

"Voss's predecessors."

"Or Voss herself. Or whoever's been buying pieces of you for twenty years." She looked up at him, and in the candlelight her eyes had the particular brightness they got when she'd found an error in something everyone else believed. "Elias. This isn't a prediction. Predictions don't have revisions. This is a *document*. It has authors. It has editors. It has a style guide, apparently, and the style guide is terrifying, but it exists."

"A draft," Elias said. The word came out of him strange, half wonder and half vertigo. All this time he'd been living inside a finished sentence, and it had never been finished. It had been *in revision*. For a thousand years, clerks and zealots and accountants had been redlining the apocalypse, and somewhere in the last century somebody had gotten ambitious enough to add his mother's signature and a delivery date.

"Drafts can be edited," Beatrix said.

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; the innkeeper's dog sighed at whatever dogs sigh at.

"That's the most hopeful thing anyone's said to me since this started," Elias said, "and I notice you haven't told me the cost yet. There's always a cost. Everything in this world costs. Bottled thunder costs. A memory of my neighbor's face apparently costs. So." He braced his elbows on the table. "What does editing a god's biography cost?"

Beatrix didn't answer immediately, which from Beatrix was itself an answer. She turned to a page near the end — the current draft, the live one, the page where his name sat in ink that had still been wet a hundred years after it was written — and held it so the candlelight came through the paper.

"Do you see this?" she said. "Hold it up."

He did. And he saw. The ink of his name didn't sit *on* the page the way ordinary ink did. It sat *in* it, threaded through the fibers like root through soil, and when he tilted the sheet, the letters moved — not smearing, not running. *Settling*. The way sediment settles in water. As though the name were heavy. As though gravity applied to it differently than to the rest of the sentence.

"The ink isn't ink," Beatrix said softly. "Or it's not only ink. Look at the color."

He looked. Under the candlelight the letters weren't black. They were a deep, arterial brown-red, the color of blood dried into cloth, and once he saw it he couldn't unsee it, and once he couldn't unsee it he understood why his fingertips had prickled every time he'd touched these pages, why the Codex grew warm in his bag, why the glass had sung.

"It's mine," he said. "Somebody's been writing this with me."

"With vessels' blood, I think. Down the centuries. One drop per entry — enough to bind the text to the body, not enough to kill. The Choir would call it communion." Beatrix's jaw tightened. "An accountant would call it a standing authorization. You can't edit a document signed in blood with ordinary ink, Elias. The signature outranks the pen. Any amendment would be rejected the way a forged writ gets rejected — the ledger would simply refuse to recognize it."

"So we need a better pen."

"We need the *same* pen." She met his eyes, and he watched her decide to say the next part plainly, because Beatrix believed — he'd learned this the hard way, over burnt stew and bad maps — that people deserved the truth even when, *especially* when, they would hate it. "The god sealed itself into your line. Your blood is its blood, thinned by generations but recognizable — the way a bank recognizes a seal. If you amend the prophecy, it has to be in blood. Yours. Written in your own hand, because the god will trust nothing it hasn't touched. And not a drop, Elias. A correction substantial enough to void a thousand years of drafting — that's not a signature. That's a *chapter*."

He looked at his hands. Ordinary hands. Callused from carrying survey poles. A healing nick on the left thumb where he'd slipped with a knife cutting bread, because apparently even the apocalypse couldn't make him competent in a kitchen.

"How much blood is a chapter?" he asked.

"I don't know. No one knows. No vessel has ever tried. They've all either run until the Choir caught them, or surrendered, or — " she hesitated — "or been managed. Bought young, like you were. Kept ignorant. The system works because nobody edits."

"Then we'd be the first."

"We'd be the first." She said it without any of the comfort he might have wanted and all of the steadiness he actually needed. That was Beatrix's gift, he was beginning to understand. She never lied to soften a number. She just stood next to you while you read it.

Elias turned back to the page with his name in it. Up close, he could see other things now that he knew what he was looking at — fainter lines beneath the visible ones, ghost-text where earlier drafts had been struck through but not erased, the palimpsest skull of the document showing through. *The war that was ended shall be begun* had been, two drafts ago, *the war that was ended shall be remembered*. Somebody had changed *remembered* to *begun*. Somebody had decided, at some point in the last few centuries, that remembering wasn't enough.

"You could bleed a lot and write nothing worse than that," he said slowly. "You could correct *begun* back to *remembered*. Make it a memorial instead of a resurrection."

"Yes."

"And the god would accept the correction?"

"The god would have no choice. Ledger law. First written stands — unless amended by an authorized hand." Beatrix almost smiled, and it was a strange, fierce, tired thing. "It turns out the apocalypse has terms of service. We just have to survive reading them."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised them both. Then he picked up his pen, his ordinary goose-quill pen, and set it down again, and put his hand flat on the page instead, over his own settling name, and felt the warmth rise to meet his palm like a hand reaching up from underwater.

There was one more thing, and he made himself say it, because she'd taught him that too — say the cost out loud or it grows in the dark.

"If I write in blood," he said, "a chapter of it, maybe more — the god feels it. Right? The seal is already thin. Ivo said the glass sang because I'm leaking. If I open myself that far, deliberately, with a thousand-year-old hunger on the other side of the wound..." He swallowed. "I might not stop at the corrections. I might start agreeing with it. Halfway through the amendment, I might decide the original draft was fine."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. The candle guttered, recovered.

"Then we don't do it alone," she said. "I'll hold the page. I'll read the true lines aloud while you write — anchor you to the text we *want*. And if you start drifting..." She looked at the candle, then at him, and her voice went dry and level, which was how he'd learned to recognize her kindness. "If you start drifting, I'll strike your hand off the page. I've done crueler things to preserve a document. Ask anyone I've edited."

"That's supposed to reassure me?"

"That's supposed to inform you," she said. "Reassurance is above my pay grade."

He grinned despite everything — despite the blood-ink, the Choir, the month ticking toward its end, the god coiled in his marrow like floodwater behind a dam. Because this was the other thing he'd learned in the border towns, the thing under the three things: that terror shared across a sticky table at an indecent hour, with a woman who corrected your lettering in her sleep, weighed less than terror carried alone.

"Tomorrow," he said. "We rest tonight. Sharpen everything. And tomorrow I start drafting."

Beatrix nodded, snuffed the candle with two fingers, and in the sudden dark her voice came soft and certain:

"For the record, Vane — your *e*s are getting better."

* * *

He woke to her shaking his shoulder, and it was wrong, everything was wrong, the window gray with a dawn that shouldn't be dawn yet and her face lit by a light that wasn't daylight at all.

Down the street, at the far end of the lane, the salt-crusted chapel bell was ringing — not the measured toll of morning service but a fast, joyous, terrible pealing, and beneath it, threading up through the floorboards and the rain and his own bones, Elias could hear singing. Many voices. Harmonized. Certain.

The Ash Choir had found them.

And in his bag, warm as a living thing, the Codex had opened itself to the final page, where his own name was glowing softly in the dark like a coal deciding whether to become a fire — and beneath it, in fresh wet ink that had not been there last night, a single new line had appeared:

*Amendment noted,* it read. *Counter-amendment pending.*
