## 13. The Choir's Cathedral of Salt

They did not bind his hands.

That was the first thing Elias noticed, and the noticing itself frightened him more than ropes would have. Ropes meant they thought he might run. The Ash Choir walked him through their cathedral with his arms loose at his sides and a cup of something warm in his palms — spiced wine, or what passed for it out here on the salt flats — as though he were a guest arriving late to a wedding rather than a prisoner taken off a burning archive road three days ago. The warmth of the cup was the only warm thing in the whole white world.

The cathedral had no roof.

It didn't need one. It rose from the salt plain in tiers of translucent stone that someone had grown, not built — Elias could see the seams where each layer had been *encouraged* into shape, the way frost encourages itself up a windowpane — and above it all the sky sat directly on the open crown of the place like a lid of hammered pewter. Snow fell through the crown and never seemed to land. It hung at a certain height inside the dome of air, suspended, glittering, so that walking beneath it felt like walking under a ceiling of held breath. The Choir called the effect *the Patience*. He'd heard two of them mention it by name, reverently, the way other people said *grace*.

"Brother Vane." A young woman fell into step beside him, her gray robes dusted white at the shoulders. Her voice was soft and she used his name the way you'd set a full cup down carefully on an unsteady table. "You've been given the eastern gallery. I hope it suits. The light is best there in the afternoons."

"The light," Elias said. "For what?"

She smiled. She had kind eyes. That was the worst part; every single one of them had kind eyes. "For seeing."

He wanted to throw the cup. He wanted to scream at her that Beatrix Montrose was somewhere behind them — alive, he had to believe, alive because the alternative was a weight he couldn't carry while walking — that Ivo Quill had died holding a door for people like this, that kindness was just what certainty looked like when it dressed itself up for company. But the god under his skin stirred at his anger, slow and vast and pleased, and Elias had learned in three days of captivity that his rage fed it, and that feeding it was exactly what the Choir wanted, and so he swallowed all of it and said, "Thank you. That's very hospitable."

The young woman beamed as though he'd recited scripture.

---

They kept him well. That was the horror underneath the horror: they kept him *well*. His room in the eastern gallery had a real bed, a washbasin, a window of glass so clear it barely existed. Meals came twice a day, always warm, always enough, served by acolytes who thanked him for eating. When he'd coughed on the second night — a rawness left over from breathing archive smoke — a healer had come within the hour and pressed cool hands to his chest and hummed something low until the rawness went. No one had asked him a single question. Not one. In three days.

"They don't interrogate you," Elias said that evening to the old brother who brought his supper, a round-faced man named Aldous with flour still on his sleeves from the bakery ovens. "Aren't you even curious why I'm here? Don't you want to know anything about me?"

Aldous set down the tray and considered him with genuine, unhurried puzzlement.

"Why would we ask?" he said. "We already know everything about you, Brother Vane. We've known since before your mother knew she was carrying you. You're not a mystery to us." He gathered the empty dishes, paused at the door, and added — kindly, gently, the way you'd tell a child the stove is hot — "You're the answer. Answers aren't asked things. They're *received*."

The door closed. Elias sat on the edge of the bed with his heart going hard, and under his sternum something enormous rolled over in its sleep and settled again, and he understood with terrible clarity that Aldous had not been speaking in metaphors.

---

On the fifth day they showed him the vision.

Not a dream, not a hallucination — a *presentation*, formal as a guild audit. He was woken before dawn, washed, robed in white, and led with ceremony up through the tiers of the cathedral to a chamber at its crown, directly beneath the hanging snow of the Patience. The chamber was circular and empty except for a raised basin of dark water at its center, and around the basin stood seven figures in gray, and at the far side, alone, a tall man with silver hair and a face like a folded letter.

"This is Father Meridian Sole," whispered the young woman — Sister Anthe, she was called, and had not left his side in five days. "He has waited his whole life to meet you."

"I know," Elias said, and was surprised to find it true. He'd felt the man's attention for days now, a pressure like weather. Somewhere below his ribs, the god had begun purring.

Father Meridian Sole raised both hands, palms out, and the seven around the basin bowed their heads.

"Elias Vane," he said. His voice was quiet. It carried anyway, the way certain voices carry — not by force but by being impossible to interrupt. "I want to begin by asking your forgiveness."

Elias blinked. "Forgiveness. For what?"

"For the war." Sole's folded-letter face unfolded, slightly, into something like grief. "For what is coming. For what we intend to do with you. None of it will be painless, and much of it will be terrible, and I have spent forty years making my peace with that so that you won't have to make yours in a single night." He gestured to the basin. "But you deserve to see what we see. Not to be persuaded — persuasion is coercion's polite cousin, and I despise it. You deserve simply to *see*, and then to choose, if choosing is still available to any of us. Will you look?"

Every instinct Elias owned said *don't*. Under his sternum, the god said nothing at all, which was worse than saying yes, which was worse than saying no, which meant the god already knew how this ended.

"Show me," Elias said.

Sole touched the water.

It did not ripple. It *opened* — the surface peeling back like a page turning, and then Elias was falling forward without moving, and the vision took him.

---

He stood on the Glass Steppe, but not the Steppe as it was. The glass underfoot was molten-bright and growing, spreading in flowering sheets toward horizons that kept retreating to make room for it, and the sky was full of light that had no sun in it. There were people everywhere — millions, tens of millions, more than the real world could hold — and they were building. Cities rose in hours. Rivers ran uphill, cheerfully, to water terraced gardens on mountaintops that had not existed last week. And at the center of everything, vast past sense, a figure of fused glass and salt and light stood with its arms open, and every person who looked at it wept with joy, and Elias knew it the way you know your own reflection in a dark window.

It wore his posture.

"It's beautiful," Elias heard himself say, inside the vision, and hated that it was true.

"It is," said Sole's voice, beside him though Sole wasn't there — the vision spoke with his voice anyway, gentle as ever. "And it costs nothing but what we have already lost. Look closer."

The vision obliged. The cities resolved, and Elias saw what joy had blurred: every person in them bore a small mark on the throat, a line of script, and when he looked harder the script resolved into words, and every word was the same word, written in every throat in the world:

*Settled.*

"No one owes anyone anything anymore," Sole said softly. "No debts. No contracts. No ledgers. No guild deciding who may own what impossibility. The god settles all accounts. Everyone belongs to everyone, and no one belongs to anyone, and the books close forever." A pause, tender as a hand on a shoulder. "Your cartographer friend would hate it. I'm sorry. But look — truly look — at what replaces her ledgers."

Elias looked. And here was the trap of it, the thing he would lie awake chewing for nights afterward: it *worked*. The vision-world was not a hellscape. It was not ash and screaming. It was a garden, vast and warm and lit from below, and the people in it were happy — genuinely happy, in the way of people whose burdens had been lifted by something strong enough to carry them. He searched the edges of the vision for suffering and found none. He searched for resistance and found only a few figures standing apart from the cities, unmoved, unwritten, and the vision showed them no cruelty — just space around them, and the choice, forever renewed, to come in or stay out.

Even the refusal was honored. Even the dissent had a ledger-line.

"That's the part you can't forgive us for, isn't it," Sole said, and it wasn't a question. "That we're right. Or that we might be. That the world we're offering isn't a cage painted to look like freedom, but the actual thing — peace, real peace, the kind that lasts." His voice dropped lower. "I was a debt-collector once, Elias. Forty years ago. Before I heard the god sing under the salt. I broke a baker's hands over four crowns of arrears. I remember the sound. Every day, I remember the sound. And I swore that if I could ever build a world where that sound was never made again, I would pay any price, commit any sin, bear any name history wants to give me. Tyrant. Zealot. Monster." A breath. "I'll wear them all gladly if no child ever again learns what interest is by watching her father kneel."

The vision dimmed. Elias came back to himself standing at the basin, gripping its rim, shaking, tears on his face that he had no memory of starting — and he could not tell, and this was the deepest cut of all, whether they were tears of horror or of longing.

Under his sternum, the god turned over, stretched like a cat in sunlight, and purred.

*See?* it said, in the wordless way it said things. *See how easy?*

"You want me to surrender," Elias managed. "That's the rite. That's what the prophecy says. I let you — let *it* — finish waking, and the world gets rewritten into a garden."

"We want you to *choose* it," Sole corrected, gently, terribly. "Prophecy can compel a body. Only consent can compel a god. The Choir has known this for a hundred years — it's why we've waited, why we keep you comfortable instead of chained. The god sealed itself away rather than rule the unwilling. It can only rise through an open hand." He stepped closer, and his silver hair caught the light of the hanging snow. "So we will wait, Elias. We are very good at waiting. We will show you the vision every day if you wish. We will feed you and heal you and speak kindly to you until the day you open your hand yourself — because you will. Everyone does, eventually. Suffering is patient too." He smiled, and it was the kindest smile Elias had ever been on the wrong end of. "Until then, you are our guest. Nothing more. Nothing less."

He turned to go, then paused.

"Oh — one more thing. Your cartographer. Beatrix Montrose." Sole said the name carefully, tasting it, like a man checking a coin for clipping. "The archive burned. She escaped, we believe. We haven't found her." He looked back over his shoulder, and his folded face unfolded fully, into compassion. "I tell you this not as a threat but as a mercy, because you deserve truth: she is looking for you. And the guild is looking for her. And neither of them can win against what's coming — not her maps, not Voss's contracts, not anything written in any book anywhere. Except one book." He nodded toward the basin, where the dark water had gone perfectly still. "And that book, Elias, is currently being held by a man who hasn't yet decided what he believes."

---

That night, alone in the eastern gallery, Elias did not sleep.

He lay on the soft bed under the hanging snow-light and turned the vision over and over like a stone in his hands, looking for the flaw, the seam, the hidden cost — because there had to be one, because Beatrix would have found it in an hour, Beatrix who read margins and footnotes and the fine print under the fine print. And somewhere past midnight, exhausted and hollow, he finally found it — not in the vision at all, but in the vision's *frame*.

Everyone in the garden-world wore the word *Settled* in their throats.

Written. Inscribed. Entered.

It was a ledger. The whole radiant garden was a ledger — just one with a single owner, and a single column, and no margin anywhere for a name that didn't fit. Sole hadn't shown him heaven. He'd shown him the most beautiful accounting document ever drafted, with every living soul entered as an asset, and the god as sole proprietor, and Elias Vane as the pen.

*She'd have seen it immediately,* Elias thought, and the thought hurt so much he pressed his heel against his eyes. *She'd have read the whole shining thing and said, 'Who keeps the books?'*

And under his sternum, faint and patient and horribly fond, the god began to hum.

---

Far below the cathedral, in a chamber of salt the acolytes were not permitted to enter, Father Meridian Sole knelt before a wall of ancient ice and watched the thing frozen inside it breathe.

Once a century, roughly.

He had watched it for thirty years, waiting for this breath, and tonight — tonight, with the vessel sleeping overhead and the vision planted like a seed in the man's heart — it breathed, and the ice flowered with new cracks, and through the cracks came a smell like hot glass and old wars.

Sole pressed his forehead to the ice.

"Soon," he whispered, with all the gentleness in his folded face. "Soon. He's beginning to listen."

Behind him, unannounced, a door of air opened in the salt wall, and a woman stepped through it covered in ash and half-dead and absolutely furious, and the last thing Father Meridian Sole ever heard clearly was the sound of chalk snapping in half.
