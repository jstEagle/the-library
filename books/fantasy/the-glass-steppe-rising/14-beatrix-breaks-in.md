## 14. Beatrix Breaks In

The Salt Cathedral kept its books in a chapel of its own.

Beatrix learned this from a smuggler named Fenwick, whom she paid with the name of her childhood dog — a small thing, but names were names, and Biscuit had been a good dog. Fenwick wept a little when he said it back to her and couldn't remember why it mattered. She'd felt that particular grief before, by then. It was becoming familiar. That was perhaps the worst part: she was getting good at losing things.

"The ledger's kept in the Vestry of Names," Fenwick said, wiping his eyes with a sleeve already damp from previous transactions. "Everything the Choir is, everything they own, everyone they've ever baptized or bought — written there. You can't get into the cathedral proper unless you're in that book. The doors read it. The guards read it. The salt itself reads it." He sniffed. "I knew a man once who tried to walk in unlisted."

"What happened to him?"

"Nothing," Fenwick said. "That was the horror of it. He walked in and the cathedral simply declined to acknowledge him. He starved to death in the nave over eleven days while four hundred worshippers stepped around him on their way to morning prayer. They couldn't see him. He wasn't written."

Beatrix wrote this down in her field notebook, because she was who she was, and underlined *declined to acknowledge* twice.

"Thank you, Fenwick."

"Don't thank me. I don't know your face anymore." He said it without bitterness, more as an observation of weather. "Whoever you are."

"Nobody," Beatrix agreed, and meant it more than she liked.

* * *

The problem, as she explained it to herself that night in a rented room above a border-town tavern, was one of accounting.

She could not forge her way into the Vestry of Names. The Choir's ledger was a living document, maintained by scribes who checked every entry against three witnesses and a drop of blood. Forgery would be caught before her second footstep crossed the threshold. No — if she wanted into that book, she had to be entered honestly. And entries required payment.

She spread her assets across the bed like a merchant laying out wares, which was almost funny, given what the wares were.

Her name. Beatrix Montrose. That was the anchor, the thing that held all the rest together, and she couldn't spend it — not yet, not entirely — or she wouldn't have enough self left to walk through the door. But pieces of it. Her middle name, Rosalind, after a great-aunt nobody remembered anyway. That was worth maybe an hour inside the walls.

Her memories. These were the deep coffers. She catalogued them with a cartographer's precision: the summer she was nine and taught herself to whistle through a blade of grass (minor, expendable). Her first kiss behind the guildhall, which had been disappointing and therefore oddly valuable — the Choir paid well for regrets. The smell of her mother's kitchen. The entire text of a lullaby whose tune she could no longer recall, only the words, which was somehow worse than losing both.

And years. Years were the hardest currency. Every year she traded came off her life at the far end, quietly, without ceremony. She'd already spent two getting half a map out of Ivo Quill's burning archive, though she hadn't told Elias that. He had enough guilt to carry. He carried it beautifully, actually, like a coat two sizes too big; she'd have found it endearing if she weren't so busy being terrified for him.

She sat on the edge of the bed and did the arithmetic.

To be entered in the Vestry as staff — a scullery clerk, someone too lowly to notice, the accounting equivalent of furniture — she needed roughly what she calculated at forty percent of herself. Name fragments, memories, years. Enough to pass the doors. Not enough to come home whole.

"Well," she said aloud to the empty room, in the brisk tone she used for closing difficult accounts. "It's only most of me."

The room, having no opinion, let her sleep.

* * *

The transaction took place at dawn in a border-market stall run by a woman called Mother Ninepence, who dealt in identity the way other merchants dealt in fish — quickly, cleanly, wrapped in paper.

"You want to sell yourself down to staff," Mother Ninepence said, not looking up from her scales. "At the Salt Cathedral."

"That's right."

"That's suicide with extra steps."

"That's also right," Beatrix said. "What's your rate on a lullaby?"

Mother Ninepence looked up then, and something in her craggy face softened — not kindness exactly, but recognition. She'd seen this kind of purchase before. Everyone in her trade had. It was always for somebody. Nobody emptied themselves out for the sheer pleasure of it.

"Lullabies go cheap," she said. "Too common. Everyone's got one and nobody can sing them anymore." She weighed an invisible nothing in her palm. "Your whistling summer, now. First joys fetch better than first kisses. People hold onto those."

They worked through the inventory together, item by item, and it was terrible and it was administrative, and Beatrix discovered that the two things were not so different as she'd once believed. Each memory went onto the scale, was appraised, was wrapped in grey paper and set aside. With each one, something went quiet in her. Not gone — she could still see the shape of where it had been, the way you can feel a tooth's absence with your tongue. The summer of grass-whistling left first. Then the disappointing kiss, which stung her pride even as it left. Then larger things, negotiated hard: her grandmother's laugh, spent at a premium because it was a good laugh. The color of the sea off the eastern coast, which she'd mapped at nineteen and loved at twenty, traded for three years of life she might need more than sunsets.

By noon she was lighter. By late afternoon she was a woman with a hole in her where a childhood used to be, standing in front of a cracked mirror in Mother Ninepence's back room, practicing being nobody.

"Name?" Mother Ninepence prompted.

Beatrix reached for it and found the edges frayed. *Beatrix* still held. *Montrose* held, barely, like a coat button hanging by a thread. *Rosalind* was gone — sold an hour ago, and she felt its absence as a small draft through her chest.

"Bee," she said. "Just Bee. Scullery clerk, third shift."

"Good. Small names slip through small doors." Mother Ninepence pressed a folded chit into her hand — a receipt, written in ink that shimmered like oil on water. "This goes in the Vestry tonight. By tomorrow's dawn bell, you're staff. And Bee—" the old woman caught her wrist, grip surprisingly strong, "—when you've done whatever you're doing, come back. Some of these can be bought home again. Not all. But some."

"Some," Beatrix agreed, and put the word away carefully, the way you pocket the last coin you own.

* * *

The Salt Cathedral rose out of the plain like a wound that had decided to be beautiful. It was built of compressed centuries — salt laid down in strata, white and grey and bone-pale, veined with veins of glass where old prayers had melted into the stone. The Choir sang somewhere inside, and the sound came out through the walls the way heat comes out of an oven: constant, patient, inescapable.

Bee joined the dawn queue of actual staff — laundresses, ash-sweepers, a boy whose job appeared to be carrying water from one place to another for reasons no one had ever questioned. She kept her eyes down and her ledger-mind open. The guards at the door didn't check faces. They checked the book. A robed acolyte stood at the threshold with the day's staff manifest open on a stand, running one finger down the column of names, and each person in the queue stepped forward, was touched briefly on the forehead, and passed through.

*Bee, scullery clerk, third shift.*

The finger stopped on her line. The acolyte looked up — through her, nearly, his gaze catching on her the way light catches on dust — and touched her forehead, and the salt beneath her feet hummed once, tasting her, finding her written, and let her in.

Inside, the cathedral was worse than outside. It was vast and bright and full of people singing, and every surface was carved with text — the Choir's scripture, their prophecy, their certainty, floor to ceiling, mile upon mile of it. Bee carried a mop she didn't need and scrubbed floors she cleaned better than anyone had a right to expect, because if you were going to be furniture you may as well be excellent furniture, and all the while she listened.

She learned the rhythms of the place. Learned that the rite was scheduled for month's end but the Choir's leader — a gentle-voiced woman the others called the Cantor — had begun moving the rehearsals earlier. Learned which corridors led to the Vestry of Names and which led to the holding cells below, where the salt was stained faintly gold, and where, if she pressed her ear to certain drains, she could hear a young man breathing.

Elias. Alive. Three floors down and forty feet of consecrated rock between them, and she allowed herself, crouched over her mop in a corridor where no one saw her because no one could see her, one minute of pure, undignified relief. Then she stood, wrung out the mop, and got back to work.

Love, she reflected, scrubbing at a stain of ancient hymn, was mostly logistics. All the ballads lied about that. Love was knowing which door opened at which hour, and how much of yourself the toll cost, and paying it anyway, and keeping the receipts.

* * *

On the fourth night she reached the Vestry of Names.

It was smaller than she'd imagined — a round room with a single desk, a single lamp, and shelves rising into darkness where the Choir's whole history slept in bound volumes. A scribe sat at the desk, an elderly man with ink-stained fingers and the serene expression of someone who has never once doubted a sentence he's written.

Bee had planned for this. She'd spent another memory — her father teaching her to sail, a good one, a painful one — to buy a forged work order authorizing shelf maintenance in the Vestry. The forgery was flawless. She'd made it herself, and she was, whatever else remained true of her, still the finest forger of documents in three realms.

The scribe examined the work order for a long moment.

"This is in order," he said.

"Thank you," said Bee.

"I don't recognize you."

"Scullery clerk. Third shift." She smiled the smile of a person with no interesting qualities whatsoever. "Nobody recognizes me. It's rather my whole thing."

He nodded, satisfied by this, and returned to his writing, and Bee moved among the shelves with her duster and her heart going like a trapped bird, reading spines. *Baptisms. Contracts. Tithes.* And there, on the central lectern, chained open under the lamp: the Rite itself. The liturgy they would speak over Elias. The prophecy, transcribed fair and final, awaiting only the blood.

She read it in the time it took to dust one shelf.

It was well-written. That was the awful thing. Whoever had drafted it a century ago had understood cadence, had built the lines to climb toward a single word at the end — *risen* — and the whole cathedral was tuned to that word like a bell to its strike. She memorized it. All of it. Cartographer's memory, the one asset she hadn't sold, because she hadn't known its price until now and thank every unmapped god for stubbornness.

Then, because she was who she was, she did one more thing. She turned to the staff manifest, found her own line — *Bee, scullery clerk, third shift* — and beside it, in pencil, in a hand disguised as her own disguised hand, she added a small notation:

*Authorized to amend.*

It shouldn't have worked. It was absurd. It was the bureaucratic equivalent of writing *please* on a bank robbery note. But the Vestry's magic ran on the principle that anything written in the book was true, and the scribe was dozing over his desk, and when the night clerk came through on his rounds and glanced at the manifest, he saw the notation, and frowned mildly, and decided it must have been authorized by someone above his station, because that was what notations in ledgers meant.

Bee finished her shift. She mopped the corridor above the cells one last time and, passing the drain, murmured downward, very softly, in a voice pitched to carry through salt and stone and despair:

"Hold on. I've read the ending. It needs editing."

And far below, faint as a heartbeat, she heard him laugh — wet-eyed, exhausted, but laughing — and she straightened her back, shouldered her bucket, and walked unseen through the singing halls of the enemy, a woman made mostly of receipts, planning the largest amendment in the history of the written world.

The dawn bell rang. The rite had been moved up. It would begin at sunset — today.

Bee looked at the light coming gold through the salt-glass windows, and began, calmly, to count what she had left.
