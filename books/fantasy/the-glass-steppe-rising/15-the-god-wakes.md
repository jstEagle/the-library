## 15. The God Wakes

They came for him at dawn, which Elias thought was rude. Even the guild's assassins had kept business hours; the Ash Choir apparently did not believe in them.

He woke to the sound of singing.

It was not a terrible sound, at first. That was the trap of it. The Choir's voices rose through the salt walls of his cell like water through cloth — low, patient, harmonized — and for a long, stupid moment he lay there thinking it was beautiful. Then he remembered where he was, and whose cathedral this was, and what they intended to do with him, and the beauty curdled into something he could feel in his teeth.

The cell was not a cell. The Choir had been very clear about that. It was a *guest chamber*, hewn from packed white salt, lit by lamps that burned without oil. They had given him clean clothes, good food, a window that looked out on a plain of salt so vast and flat it made the Glass Steppe look cluttered by comparison. They had answered every question he asked with gentle sincerity. Their leader, a woman called Mother Cassia who had eyes like warm bread, had held his hands and told him, "You were made for glory, Elias Vane. We are only here to help you remember."

The door was salt too, and it was open.

That was how he knew the rite had begun early. They didn't need to lock anything anymore. Whatever was rising in him, they trusted it to keep him.

Elias stood, and his knees buckled, and the floor did not catch him so much as the *something else* did — a pressure under his skin like floodwater against a door, patient, enormous, almost kind.

*Oh*, he thought. *Oh, you're awake.*

---

He made it eleven steps down the corridor before the first wave hit.

It came up through him like water finding its level. Not pain — that was the horrifying part, there was no pain at all — but *fullness*. Every empty space in him filled. Every hunger he'd ever carried, every loneliness, every night-shift hour spent surveying roads for people who would never know his name: all of it flooded, and then overflowed, and then simply *stopped mattering*, because something vast and warm had moved into the vacancy and was rearranging the furniture.

*You are tired*, said the presence, and it did not speak in words so much as in certainty. *You have been tired your whole life. Put it down.*

"I'm not tired," Elias said out loud, to an empty salt corridor, and heard how unconvincing he sounded even to himself.

A Choir acolyte rounded the corner ahead of him — a young man with salt dust in his eyebrows and an expression of radiant calm. He did not raise an alarm. He bowed.

"Vessel," the young man said. "You're walking the wrong way. The cathedral is that way." He pointed back toward Elias's cell with the tenderness of a man giving directions to a lost child.

"Right," said Elias. "Terrible sense of direction. Always had it. You'd think a surveyor would—" The floodwater rose again, and he gripped the wall, and the wall *sang* under his palm, a single pure note that went through the whole building like a bell through a body. The acolyte wept openly with joy.

"See?" the young man whispered. "See how it loves you already?"

"It's not love," Elias managed. "It's a landlord. And I haven't signed anything."

But even as he said it, he could feel the truth of what the Choir believed, and that was the worst discovery of the morning: they weren't lying. The god — whatever slept coiled in his blood, whatever Ivo Quill had died showing him, whatever absence sat god-shaped at the center of the oldest map — it *did* offer peace. Real peace. He could taste it, the way you can taste rain before it arrives. All he had to do was stop being Elias Vane. Stop being the man with no records before three years ago. Stop being the man Beatrix Montrose had dragged through a chalk door while assassins closed in, the man she'd taught to write himself small, the man who had watched her trade her sister's memory for their passage and pay without flinching.

All he had to do was dissolve, gently, into something enormous and eternal and utterly without opinions about breakfast.

*You will not suffer*, the presence promised. *You will not grieve. You will not lose anyone again, because you will no longer be anyone to lose.*

"That," Elias said, pushing off the wall, "is the worst sales pitch I've ever heard, and I once bought bottled thunder."

---

He ran.

The cathedral of salt was built like a spiral — everything curved inward, every corridor bent toward the center, toward the nave where the Choir sang. He ran outward instead, against the grain of the architecture, and the architecture noticed. Doors he passed began, very quietly, to be somewhere else. Corridors lengthened with the mild, unhurried confidence of a thing that had all morning. The salt under his feet hummed the liturgy's bass line.

And the floodwater kept rising.

By the time he reached what he hoped was an outer gallery, it was up to his ribs. He could feel it pressing behind his eyes. When he looked at his hands, they seemed slightly translucent, as though light were passing through him on its way to somewhere more important. His own name felt far away — a word from a language he used to speak.

*Beatrix*, he thought, deliberately, like striking a match. *Beatrix Montrose. She has ink on her fingers right now. She always has ink on her fingers. She once told me the difference between a map and a territory is that a map can apologize.*

The floodwater paused. Considered. Rose higher.

*She is not here*, it said, with terrible gentleness. *No one is coming. No one has ever come. That is why surrender is mercy.*

"She came into a cathedral full of zealots," Elias panted, running, "by writing herself into their ledger as staff, which is the most Beatrix sentence I have ever heard, and if you think she isn't currently marching down your corridors crossing things out—" He hit a junction, chose left, immediately understood it was wrong, doubled back. "—then you don't know her, and honestly, neither do I, but I'm *planning* to, and you're not going to take that plan away from me."

Somewhere below, the singing changed key.

He felt it happen in his sternum. The liturgy had moved into its final movement — he didn't know how he knew, but he knew, the way you know a storm has turned toward you. The Choir's voices climbed, sweet and certain, and each line they sang landed in him like a nail going home:

*Blessed is the vessel that opens.*

*Holy is the hand that pours.*

*Peace, peace, to the drowned and the drowning—*

"Nope," Elias said. "Absolutely not. I am not a vessel. I am a *surveyor*. I measure things. It's a humble profession and I love it, and the first thing I ever measured properly was a drainage ditch outside Marrowfen, and it was boring, and it was *mine*—"

The gallery ended in a window. Beyond it: the salt plain, blinding white, and the sun coming up over nothing. For one absurd second he considered the window, and the drop, and whether a god could drown in a body that fell fast enough.

Then he heard footsteps behind him, and turned, ready to fight or flee or negotiate badly.

It was Mother Cassia.

She was alone. She carried no weapon. Her hands were folded, her face was kind, and behind her, down the spiral of the corridor, the entire Choir knelt in rows, still singing, their voices threading up through the salt like roots through soil.

"You don't have to run," she said. "I know you're frightened. Everyone is frightened, the first time. But Elias — may I call you Elias? — look at your hands."

He looked. The translucence had spread past his wrists. Through the skin of his palms he could see light moving, slow and tidal, like something breathing under ice.

"The rite does not need to finish," Cassia said softly. "Do you understand? The words are only courtesy. A door held open. You could walk through on your own. You could end your fear *right now*. No more running. No more debts. No more being sold, and hunted, and written about in books by strangers." Her voice caught with genuine tenderness. "You were sold before you drew breath, child. Let us give you back to something worthy of you."

And God help him — and he meant that literally now, God being the thing currently flooding his lungs — it sounded good. It sounded like sleep after a long shift. It sounded like putting down a pack he'd forgotten he was carrying. The floodwater reached his shoulders, and it was warm, and it was *kind*, and it asked for so little. Just the small matter of Elias Vane.

His knees touched the salt floor. He hadn't decided to kneel. That was the thing he would remember afterward, in the fragmentary way one remembers a fever: he never decided anything at all. Deciding required someone to do the deciding, and the someone was going, going—

*Peace*, said the god, gathering itself like a tide drawing back before the big wave. *Peace, little vessel. Let go.*

Down in the nave, the Choir reached the antepenultimate line of the liturgy. He felt every syllable land.

*What was sealed, let it rise.*

*What was broken, let it wake.*

Cassia's hand settled on his head, light as a blessing. "Sing with us," she whispered. "Just the last line. Just one word. Say *yes*."

The floodwater gathered. The whole cathedral leaned in. Ten thousand tons of salt held its breath, and beneath Elias's dissolving skin the god rose like dawn under the horizon of him, vast and patient and almost loving, and the last line of the liturgy opened before him like a road —

*What sleeps, let it—*

"—*stop*," said a voice from the doorway, breathless, furious, and unmistakable, "taking dictation from a *building*."

Elias's heart, which had been politely resigning its post, slammed back into service.

Beatrix Montrose stood in the entrance to the gallery, salt-white to the elbows, hair escaping its braid in gray-flagrant disarray, a stolen Choir robe worn inside-out over her cartographer's coat, and ink on her fingers — fresh ink, wet ink, ink that was *still drying*, because she had clearly come directly from doing something illegal to the cathedral's ledger. In her left hand: the Century Codex, its pages fluttering like a startled bird. In her right: a pen.

Not a weapon. A pen.

Of course, thought Elias, from the bottom of the floodwater. Of course she brought a pen.

"Beatrix," he said, and his voice came out in two registers at once, his own and something older underneath, and both of them were relieved.

"Hi," she said, slightly winded. "Sorry I'm late. Your cathedral has *terrible* signage, and also I had to forge myself a promotion. I'm apparently the Assistant Undersecretary of Salt now. Don't ask. There wasn't time to make it convincing, only real." Her eyes found his translucent hands, the light moving under his skin, and something crossed her face that she filed away for later, the way she filed everything away for later. "Elias. Listen to me. Whatever it's offering you —"

"Peace," he said. The god said it with him, in harmony, and Beatrix flinched.

"Yeah, well." She stepped forward, past Mother Cassia, past the kneeling Choir, past ten thousand tons of held breath, and planted herself between him and the last line of the liturgy like a clerk defending a filing cabinet. "It offered my sister's memory to a broker last week. It offers everyone peace. It's a *creditor*, Elias. And I have read enough contracts to know—" she raised the pen, and the Codex, and grinned the grin of a woman about to commit fraud against heaven itself, "—that nothing is binding until it's signed."

Mother Cassia turned, serene and sorrowful. "You cannot stop what is already waking, keeper of accounts."

"Mm," said Beatrix. "Funny thing about that."

She opened the Codex to tonight's date — his name beside it, the ink a century old and somehow still wet — and set the nib against the page.

"Watch me try."

The last line of the liturgy hung unfinished in the air, and the god rose in Elias like a wave that had waited a thousand years for shore, and Beatrix Montrose began, very calmly, to cross things out.
