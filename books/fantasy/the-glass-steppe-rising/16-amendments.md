## 16. Amendments

The liturgy had a rhythm to it, Beatrix noticed, the way all terrible things did.

She crouched behind a pillar of fused salt — one of hundreds that held up the Choir's cathedral like the ribs of some enormous, patient whale — and listened to the Cantor's voice roll through the vaulted dark. It was a beautiful voice. That was the worst part. Zealots were supposed to shriek; this woman *sang*, low and warm, and two hundred white-robed faithful sang back, and the sound climbed the salt columns and pooled under the dome like light.

*And on the last night of the waning year,* they sang, *the vessel opens, and the god pours through—*

Beatrix checked her pockets for the fourth time. Chalk stub. The half-map. The Century Codex, warm as a sleeping cat against her ribs. And the pen — Ivo Quill's pen, salvaged from the archive rubble, its nib still stained with his ink. She had not let herself think about Ivo since she'd crawled out of the burning archive. She was not going to think about him now. Grief was a debt, and debts could be settled later, when there was time. There was never time. That was practically the guild motto.

*The god pours through,* sang the Choir, *and the world is made new.*

"Made new," Beatrix muttered. "You'd think after a thousand years they'd have learned to proofread."

Above the altar, bound in a cradle of salt and singing, Elias Vane opened his eyes.

She felt it before she saw it — a pressure change, like the moment before a storm breaks over the Steppe, when the glass plain goes quiet and every compass needle forgets which way is north. The Codex warmed against her ribs. And Elias looked down at the congregation with an expression she recognized from their weeks on the run: the particular horror of a man discovering, again, that he contained something enormous.

He wasn't gone yet. That was the thing the Choir didn't understand, because certainty had made them lazy readers. The prophecy said the vessel *opens*. It did not say the vessel stopped being a person first. Somewhere in that floodwater of god, Elias was still holding on — she could see it in his face, the way he'd once held on to his surveyor's kit when the Steppe tried to swallow it, stubbornly, absurdly, like a man refusing to drop his lunch pail during an earthquake.

*Hold on, you ridiculous man,* she thought. *I'm coming.*

She pulled out the Codex and opened it to tonight's page.

---

Here is what Beatrix Montrose knew about prophecy, and it was more than anyone in this cathedral suspected: prophecy was just a draft.

She'd spent twenty years keeping ledgers, and ledgers taught you one truth above all others — nothing written is finished until someone signs it. Contracts had clauses. Deeds had easements. Wills had codicils scrawled in dying hands. Every document in the Glass Steppe Guild's archive, every impossible trade ever recorded, bore the marks of revision: lines struck, margins annotated, whole pages pasted over by clerks who'd realized too late what they'd agreed to.

The Century Codex was no different. It only *believed* it was different. It believed so hard, and everyone around it believed so hard, that its words came true — but belief was just pressure, and pressure was just another kind of ink.

The problem was the hand. A prophecy edited carelessly would simply rewrite itself, the way water fills a footprint. To make a correction stick, you needed authority. You needed a hand the text trusted.

Elias's blood, he'd told her, three nights ago in a border-town attic, holding up his bandaged palm with the sheepish expression of a man presenting a parking ticket. *It has to be mine. I'm the collateral. The whole contract's written against me.*

*Then we amend the contract,* she'd said.

*With my blood.*

*Ideally with as little of it as possible,* she'd added, because she was a professional, and professionals managed costs. He'd laughed. She'd memorized the laugh. She was doing a great deal of memorizing lately, and telling herself it was habit rather than anything else.

Now she flattened the Codex on her knee and read the night's appointed verses, and began, quietly, to plan the edit.

---

The rite reached its second movement. The Cantor descended from the pulpit, trailing white silk, and the congregation parted for her like a ledger opening to a fresh page. She carried a basin. Beatrix didn't want to know what was in the basin. Actually, she did want to know, professionally, but knowing would require getting closer, and getting closer required the third movement, when the Choir turned to face the four corners of the world and sang the god awake.

*Third movement,* she told herself. *That's your window. They'll be facing away. Their eyes will be shut. Certainty always closes its eyes at the important part.*

She used the waiting time well. She found the verse — the hinge of the whole prophecy, the line everything else balanced on:

*And the vessel shall open, willingly or no, and the god shall pour through, and the sealed war shall wake, and the Steppe shall drink its fill.*

Willingly or no. There it was. A thousand years of terror built on a subordinate clause. Whoever drafted this had been in a hurry — she could tell, the way she could always tell, by the cramped letterforms and the missing flourish. A hurried drafter left openings. A hurried drafter assumed no one would ever dare pick up the pen.

Beatrix dared constantly. It was practically her defining trait, according to three separate performance reviews.

She wet Ivo's nib on her tongue — an old cartographer's trick, and also, she suspected, exactly the sort of sentimental gesture Ivo would have approved of — and waited.

---

The third movement began, and two hundred voices turned to the corners of the world, and Beatrix walked out from behind her pillar into the open floor of the cathedral.

No one saw her.

This was the miracle she'd bought with nearly everything she had: she was written into this building's ledger as *staff*. Not guest, not intruder, not sacrifice — staff. And no one in the Choir's domain could see what was not written, and what was written as staff was beneath notice the way a broom is beneath notice, the way a clerk is beneath notice. She had spent names and memories and eleven years of her own life to become furniture, and furniture, thank the founders, could walk.

She crossed the nave. The singing swelled. Salt dust drifted down from the dome like snow that had given up on being cold. At the altar, Elias strained against his bindings, and his eyes found hers across fifty feet of holy floor, and widened.

*Don't,* he mouthed.

*Yes,* she mouthed back.

*I mean it—*

*I know you do.* She reached the altar steps. Up close she could see what the god was doing to him — frost forming on his eyelashes in a cathedral warm as a kiln, veins standing dark at his throat like ink rising through blotting paper. His shadow moved wrong on the salt behind him, moving when he didn't, reaching when he couldn't.

She climbed the steps. The Cantor stood ten feet away, back turned, arms raised, pouring her whole gentle certain heart into the fourth verse. Beatrix stood beside the altar, invisible, unremarkable, staff, and drew the Codex from her coat, and spoke — not loudly, not dramatically, just clearly, the way she read entries aloud when closing the guild's accounts at night, alone, to hear that the numbers were true.

"Amendment one," she said. "Strike: *willingly or no.*"

She dragged Ivo's nib through the line.

The cathedral flinched.

There was no other word for it. The singing stumbled — two hundred voices catching on the same missing syllable, like a choir walking downstairs in the dark and finding one step gone. The Cantor's arms faltered. Somewhere overhead, a crack ran thirty feet along the dome with a sound like ice on a pond.

"*What—*" the Cantor began, and then, because faith is resilient and reorganizes fast, she raised her voice to carry the verse anyway — and the verse wouldn't come. The words weren't there anymore. Her mouth shaped them and found nothing, because there was nothing left to shape. Beatrix watched certainty meet its first blank page, and it was, she would later admit, deeply satisfying.

"Amendment two," Beatrix said, louder now, because subtlety had officially left the building. "Strike: *and the sealed war shall wake.*"

Nib through ink. The crack in the dome widened. Salt pillars groaned. In the congregation, a woman screamed — not in pain, in *betrayal*, the raw sound of someone whose text had lied to her, or rather whose text had been lied *about*, which was worse, because it meant the lie had authors, and authors could be found.

Elias was staring at her. "Beatrix," he rasped, "the blood — you can't strike without—"

"I know." She already had the little knife out, the one she used for trimming quills. "Give me your hand."

"You're enjoying this."

"Immensely. Hand."

He gave her his hand. She pricked his palm along the old cut — efficiently, apologetically, the way she did everything — and pressed his bleeding fingers to the page.

"Now write," she said. "Small. Neat. Margins are for amateurs."

His handwriting, she'd learned during their weeks of hiding, was a surveyor's handwriting: precise, slightly cramped, obsessed with measurement. He wrote with a shaking, bleeding hand, and the letters came out perfect anyway, because the god in him wanted the words true, and Elias wanted them *otherwise*, and between those two wants the page itself seemed to hold its breath.

*Amended,* he wrote beneath the struck lines, in blood that gleamed black on the century-old vellum: *The vessel opens only by consent freely given, renewed each dawn, revocable at will. The sealed war stays sealed. The Steppe drinks rain.*

The Codex shuddered like a living thing.

And then — this was the part Beatrix had not fully planned for, though she should have, because she of all people should have known — the rest of the book began to argue.

Prophecies are drafts, yes, but they are drafts with *cross-references*. Strike a clause here and seventeen other clauses woke up, offended, citing themselves. She felt it happen — a vibration through the vellum, a chorus of marginalia demanding attention, footnotes squirming like worms under a lifted stone. The Codex flipped its own pages, hunting for a version of events that still worked, and where it found none, it began to smolder.

"It's contesting the edit," she said. "Of course it is. Everything in this damned Steppe contests the edit."

"What do we do?"

"We out-stubborn it." She took his bleeding hand again and set it flat on the open page. "You're the collateral. Tell it what you consent to."

Elias closed his eyes. And Beatrix, watching his face, saw the floodwater rise in him — saw the god come up close to the surface, close enough to speak, and heard — she would swear forever after that she heard it, a voice like a glacier deciding — say through his mouth, in a voice that was both his and vastly not:

***I consent to sleep.***

The Codex went still.

Then, slowly, grudgingly, the way a great institution accepts anything, the ink settled. The struck lines stayed struck. The amendment dried black and permanent. Across the nave, the Choir's song collapsed entirely — not into screaming, at first, but into a confused murmur, two hundred people turning to their neighbors asking *what do we sing now, what do we sing now*, because their whole lives had been lyrics and the lyrics were gone.

The Cantor turned around.

She looked at Beatrix — actually *looked*, which meant the staff-writ was failing, burned off her by the amended page, and Beatrix felt herself snap back into visibility like a lamp being lit. The Cantor's face was a study. Grief, fury, and underneath both, horribly, relief.

"The text," the Cantor whispered. "You changed the text."

"Somebody had to," Beatrix said. "The punctuation alone was criminal."

For one heartbeat, Beatrix thought it was over. Then the Cantor's eyes went to Elias — to the god looking out through him, drowsy now, sinking, consenting — and her grief curdled into something older and simpler.

"If we cannot wake him," she said, lifting the basin, "we can at least refuse to let him be *hidden* again. Come, children! If the book is lost, we are the book! We will remember the verses ourselves! We will *be* the prophecy!"

And two hundred zealots, robbed of their scripture, began — with terrifying sincerity — to recite it from memory.

"Oh, you cannot be serious," Beatrix said.

Salt cracked. The dome shed a sheet of white. And through the shattered rose window at the cathedral's western end, silhouetted against the moon, came figures in grey — dozens of them, descending on glass wires with the unhurried precision of an audit arriving on schedule — and at their head, unmistakable even at distance, a woman consulting a small black book as calmly as if she were checking the time.

Sarelle Voss had come to collect.

Beatrix grabbed Elias's wrist. "Amendment three," she said. "*Run.*"
