## 17. Sarelle Collects

The silence after the Choir's certainty shattered was not a comfortable one. It was the silence of a cathedral holding its breath, salt dust still drifting down from the vaulted ceiling like the world's slowest snowfall, and somewhere beneath their feet — beneath everything, Beatrix thought, beneath every map she had ever drawn — something vast turning over in its sleep and deciding, grudgingly, to keep dreaming.

Beatrix stood at the center of it all with ink on her hands that was not entirely her own blood, which by Steppe standards made this an unusually good day.

Elias sat slumped against the altar steps, pale as unmarked paper, his right hand wrapped in strips of the Choir's own vestments. He had bled corrections onto prophecy for the better part of an hour, and it showed. But he was alive. He was himself. When he looked up at her and managed a crooked, exhausted half-smile, she felt something in her chest loosen that had been clenched since the night she'd found wet ink on century-old paper.

"You know," he rasped, "when you told me guild work involved long nights with ledgers, I feel like I was misled."

"I never once claimed to be honest," Beatrix said. "I'm an accountant of impossible things. Dishonesty is practically a fiduciary duty."

She was reaching down to help him up when the doors of the Cathedral of Salt opened.

Not broke open. Not forced. They simply *opened*, the way a ledger opens — deliberately, from the other side, by someone who had every right.

The light that came through was wrong for a cathedral built by zealots in a salt waste. It was lamplight. Warm, domestic, the color of the Guild's reading room at closing time, and it spilled across the salt floor in a long, patient rectangle, as if someone had set out a welcome mat made of accounting hours.

Sarelle Voss walked through it.

Behind her came the guild's full weight, and Beatrix use to seeing that phrase mean enforcers — but this was different. This was clerks. Dozens of them, in grey coats, carrying portable desks that unfolded themselves with soft mechanical sighs. Carrying scales. Carrying *books*. They fanned out along the cathedral's flanks with the unhurried efficiency of people setting up a market stall, and within two minutes they had, impossibly, established a functioning branch office in the nave of a fallen god's prison.

One of them hung a small brass plaque on a pillar. Beatrix read it upside down: *GUILD OF THE GLASS STEPPE — TEMPORARY PREMISES. PLEASE HAVE YOUR ACCOUNTS IN ORDER.*

"They're joking," Elias whispered. "Tell me they're joking."

"The Guild doesn't joke," Beatrix said quietly. "It *documents*."

"Beatrix Montrose." Sarelle's voice carried without effort, the way a signature carries. She crossed the salt floor in sensible boots, her grey coat immaculate despite the ash and wreckage, her silver hair pinned back as though the apocalypse were a mildly inconvenient weather event. She stopped six paces away — exactly the distance required for a formal audit, Beatrix noted with professional despair — and folded her hands.

"Mistress Voss," Beatrix said. "You missed the rite. There was singing. Terrible singing, admittedly, but there was a great deal of it."

"I read the minutes." Sarelle's gaze moved past her, to Elias, to the desecrated Codex on the altar, to the Choir's leader sitting dazed amid her shattered certainty with salt dust in her hair like a bride at a funeral. Something flickered in Sarelle's expression — not quite disappointment. Recalculation. "An interesting outcome. Not the one the contract anticipated."

"The contract," Beatrix said, "is voided. The prophecy has been amended. In blood. By the vessel himself. Legally speaking—"

"Legally speaking." Sarelle smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had read every book in the building, including the ones written about her. "Yes. Let's speak legally. It's my favorite language. Sit down, Beatrix. Both of you. This will take some time."

"We've just fought off a religious death cult—"

"And admirably. Truly. I've already drafted commendations." Sarelle gestured, and a clerk materialized at her elbow holding a folder that looked thick enough to stop arrows. "But the Guild does not recognize outcomes. The Guild recognizes *entries*. And your entries, Keeper Montrose, are in a state I can only describe as creative."

* * *

They sat at a portable desk while clerks read aloud.

That was how Sarelle Voss conducted war — as a recitation. A clerk with a reedy voice and the unshakeable calm of a man who had audited worse than apocalypse worked through Beatrix's file entry by entry, and each entry landed like a small, precise stone.

"*Entry four thousand and twelve. One mercy, extended to a debtor named Corwin Hale, whose debt of three years' labor was forgiven without documentation.*"

"*Entry four thousand and thirty-one. One door drawn in chalk, granting passage to an unmapped individual, Elias Vane, without proper writ.*"

"*Entry four thousand and forty-four. One writ, burned. Guild property declared retroactively and without authority.*"

On and on. Every kindness Beatrix had ever extended, every corner cut, every rule bent for a frightened person or a foolish one or simply because it was Tuesday and the alternative was being cruel — all of it laid out in the warm lamplight, itemized, totaled.

Elias listened with growing horror. Halfway through, he leaned toward her and whispered, "Is this... are these all things you did? For people?"

"Some of them," Beatrix murmured back.

"Why would she keep records of your *kindnesses*?"

"Because in the Steppe, kindness isn't free. It's a liability." She kept her eyes on Sarelle. "Every mercy I extended created an imbalance. An account in deficit. She's been collecting evidence of my solvency problems for twenty years."

"Not evidence," Sarelle corrected pleasantly, without looking up from the summary page before her. "A *pattern*. You see, Beatrix, I could forgive any single one of these. Even a dozen. That is what a mistress of accounts does; she manages irregularities. But this—" she turned the page around so they could see it, columns of figures marching down in Beatrix's own handwriting, cross-referenced against decades, "—this is a career of generosity. Do you know what generosity is, in a system built on binding debts?"

"A flaw," Beatrix said.

"A *leak*." Sarelle closed the folder. "And tonight you capped the leak with something far worse than mercy. You helped rewrite a sealed prophecy. You have committed, by my count, eleven capital offenses, four of which didn't exist until you invented them tonight, which the legal department assures me makes them worse, not better."

One of the clerks raised a hand timidly. "Technically, Mistress, inventing new crimes requires prior registration under the Act of—"

"Note it down, Fenwick."

Fenwick noted it down.

Beatrix felt the old familiar exhaustion settling into her bones, the weariness of a woman who had spent her whole life keeping books balanced and had finally, irreversibly, knocked one over on purpose. She looked at Elias. He was watching her with that expression she'd come to dread and treasure in equal measure — the one that said he was about to do something noble and stupid, and that he'd learned it from watching her.

"Don't," she said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're thinking of offering. Don't. Your blood is worth more than both our accounts combined, and she knows it."

"That's rather the point," Sarelle said. "I'm glad we agree on valuation, at least. Yes, Master Vane — you are, at present, the single most valuable asset in the known territories. The last vessel of a sealed god. Even asleep, even amended, even *edited*, what sleeps in you is collateral beyond reckoning. Which brings me to my offer."

"There's an offer?" Elias asked.

"There is always an offer. The Guild is nothing if not generous — in strictly transactional terms." Sarelle laced her fingers together on the desk. "Deliver yourself to Guild custody. Voluntarily. Peacefully. In exchange, every debt, every offense, every liability accrued by Beatrix Montrose over the course of her entire career — forgiven. All of it. She walks out of here solvent, clean, restored. Her name unstained in every ledger in the Steppe."

The lamplight seemed to hold very still.

"She keeps her name," Sarelle said softly. "Her history. Her place in the records. Everything she has spent her life building. All you have to do is give up yours."

* * *

"No," Beatrix said.

"You haven't heard the terms fully—"

"Yes, I have, and no. Absolutely not. Next offer."

"There is no next offer, Beatrix. That's the offer." Sarelle's composure didn't crack, but something behind it shifted — a weight redistributing, like sand finding its level. "And before you refuse again with that admirable stubbornness, consider: I don't actually need your consent. The Guild's full weight is in this room. We could take him. We tried courtesy first because courtesy is cheaper than force, and because force, applied to a divine vessel, has historically produced *complications*."

"The Choir tried force tonight," Elias said. His voice was hoarse but steady. "How'd that go?"

"The Choir," Sarelle said, "were amateurs with a liturgy. I am a professional with a contract. But I would prefer not to test the difference on holy ground. Hence: the offer stands. Deliver yourself, and she goes free."

Elias looked at Beatrix. And Beatrix watched him do the arithmetic she knew he was doing — the same terrible, generous arithmetic she'd caught in his eyes a dozen times since the chalk door, the counting of his life against hers as though the two sums were comparable.

"Elias," she said warningly.

"It's a good deal," he said.

"It's an *awful* deal. You'd be handing a sleeping god to the most patient predator in creation."

"I'd be saving you."

"You'd be trading a person for a reputation. That's not salvation, that's a *bad exchange rate*, and if you've learned nothing else from me in these terrible weeks, you will learn that." Beatrix stood up. Her legs were shaking and she made them stop. "Mistress Voss. I have a counteroffer."

Sarelle raised one eyebrow. "You are not in a negotiating position, Keeper."

"I am always in a negotiating position. It's the one perk of knowing where every body is buried, metaphorically, and several literally." Beatrix walked to the edge of the desk, put both ink-stained hands flat on it, and met the Guildmistress's eyes. "You want the vessel. You want the god secured, the asset contained, the account closed. Fine. But you can't take him by force without risking waking what's in him — you just admitted it. And he won't go willingly while I'm held over him. So the question becomes: what leverage exists that gets you everything you want, cleanly, permanently, without a single complication?"

"Enlighten me."

"There's a clause." Beatrix's heart was pounding so hard she could hear it in her ears, but her voice came out steady, the way it did when she closed the big trades, the way it had the night she burned the writ. "Older than the Charter. Older than your office. It's in the founding codex — the one nobody reads because it's bound in glass and kept in the deep archive, and because it is *boring*, which is the best security there is. Article One. Paragraph Nine. Read it when you get home. I'll wait."

Something changed in Sarelle's face. It was very small — a fractional narrowing, the look of a woman reaching for a shelf in memory and finding it exactly where feared. "That clause hasn't been invoked in nine hundred years."

"No. It hasn't."

"Because invoking it is madness."

"Because invoking it is *expensive*, which in this Guild is the same word." Beatrix straightened. Somewhere in the lamplight, the clerks had gone quiet. Even Fenwick's pen had stopped. "Say it anyway, Sarelle. Say it aloud, so everyone hears, so it's entered properly. What may the keeper of the ledger settle?"

The silence stretched. Salt dust drifted. Under the floor, faintly, impossibly, the god turned in its sleep.

"The keeper of the ledger," Sarelle said at last, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "may settle any account... with herself."

"Any account," Beatrix confirmed. "Any debt. Any contract. Any prophecy, if it comes to that. Paid in full, by the keeper's own hand, from the keeper's own holdings." She smiled, and it wasn't a brave smile; it was the smile of an accountant who has finally found the number that balances. "Your contract on Elias Vane is an account, Mistress Voss. And I am the keeper."

"You would void it by paying it yourself." Sarelle studied her the way she might study a forged bill of sale — looking for the flaw, the trick, the escape hatch. "Do you understand what 'yourself' means, in the old law? Not your money. Not your years. Not your memories, though those would be accepted. *Yourself.* Name, history, record. Every entry you appear in, struck. Every person who remembers you, unwritten. As far as the ledgers of the world are concerned, Beatrix Montrose would never have existed at all. No one would mourn you. No one could. Mourning requires a name."

"I know what it means," Beatrix said. "I've known since the Century Codex. Why do you think I checked the anomaly protocols twice?" She glanced at Elias, and her chest did something painful and complicated, and she filed the feeling under *later*. "I've spent my whole life writing other people into and out of the world. I know precisely how it works."

"No." Elias was on his feet now, swaying, his bandaged hand pressed against the desk. "No, no — that's not — Beatrix, that's not a settlement, that's *erasure*—"

"That's *accounting*," she said gently. "There's a difference, and it matters."

"I won't allow it."

"You don't have a vote. You're not even technically a person under Guild law; you're an asset. Assets don't get opinions." She softened. "Besides. You taught me something, these past weeks. You showed me the prophecy was just a draft. Well — so am I. And drafts can be edited."

"This is not funny."

"I'm not laughing." And she wasn't. Her eyes were burning and her hands were perfectly steady and that combination, she suspected, was the truest thing about her. "Look at me doing the math, Elias. One name against a god's freedom. One history against a world that stays mapped, and lawful, and boring, and safe. It's not even a close trade. Any competent accountant takes it."

"Any competent accountant," Elias said, and his voice cracked clean through, "would take *me*."

"Yes," Beatrix agreed quietly. "Which is why it's fortunate neither of us is competent."

Sarelle had not moved. She was watching Beatrix with an expression Beatrix had never seen on her before, and it took a long moment to identify it, because it belonged on no ledger: it was something adjacent to respect, and adjacent to grief, worn by a woman who had probably believed herself incapable of either.

"If I accept this," Sarelle said slowly, "it must be done properly. Entered, witnessed, executed under the old forms. Tonight, before anything else moves — the god, the Choir, the law. The clause permits no amendment once invoked." She paused. "Are you certain, Keeper Montrose?"

Beatrix looked at Elias.

He was staring at her as though she were a page tearing, and she wanted to say a hundred things — that he had been the best mistake she ever tracked down, that his shadow moved wrong in ways she'd grown fond of, that somewhere in a border town he'd written himself small in her notebook margins and she'd never erased it. She wanted to tell him to check the orphaned entries, someday, the ones that lived outside the ledgers. She wanted—

Later, she told herself firmly. File it under later.

"I am certain," she said. "Enter it, Mistress. Witness it. Close the account."

Sarelle nodded once and turned to Fenwick. "Bring the founding codex. Article One, Paragraph Nine. And Fenwick—" her voice dropped to something almost gentle, almost human, "—use the good pen."

As the clerk hurried away into the lamplight, Elias grabbed Beatrix's arm, and his grip was desperate and shaking, and beneath the cathedral's broken ceiling the god stirred in its sleep, and Beatrix Montrose — keeper of ledgers, mapper of impossible realms, the most solvent insolvent woman in creation — smiled at him one more time, and began, in her head, to draft the terms of her own deletion.

There was only one line she refused to strike.

She wrote it nowhere, told no one, kept it in the one ledger no auditor would ever find: *somewhere, a compass rose drawn in chalk, pointing nowhere.*

She only hoped he'd think to look.
