## 19. The Unmapped Woman

The silence afterward was the worst part.

Elias had expected — no, he had *braced* for — noise. The Choir's cathedral of salt collapsing in a roar of white dust. Voss arriving with an army of clerks and knives. Thunder, at minimum. Some grand punctuation to match the way Beatrix had screamed his name while the ink took her, while her hands came apart into letters, while she smiled at him — *smiled*, the absolute maddening woman — as if being unwritten were a filing error she could forgive.

Instead there was only a sound like a page turning, very slowly, somewhere beneath the world.

Then nothing.

He lay where she had held him, in the ruins of the liturgy circle, and waited for the silence to become something else. It didn't. It just deepened, the way snow deepens, until it stopped being an absence of sound and became a presence of it.

"Beatrix," he said.

The glass did not answer. The glass never answered; that had been one of the first things she'd taught him, back when everything was chalk doors and borrowed names and her exasperated voice explaining ledger law for the fourth time because he kept asking *but why*. *The Steppe doesn't listen, Elias. It records. There's a difference, and it will save your life to know it.*

He said her name again anyway. He was allowed to be bad at lessons today.

* * *

Getting out of the cathedral was its own small humiliation. His legs worked, technically, but they worked the way a debt works — reluctantly, and with interest. He crawled through a corridor of collapsed salt, cut his palms on what remained of the Choir's altar rail, and emerged blinking into daylight that looked exactly like every other day's daylight, which felt personally insulting.

The Ash Choir was scattering. He watched them from the ridge above the salt flats: hundreds of figures in ash-grey robes walking away from their own cathedral in loose, dazed knots, like ants whose hill had been kicked over by a god who turned out not to want them. Some wept. Some sang, quietly, the old liturgy with its final lines now missing — struck out, amended, corrected in blood that was still drying on his knuckles. A few simply stood facing the ruins, waiting for instructions from a certainty that no longer existed.

One of them, an old woman with kind eyes and a voice like warm bread, passed close enough to touch him. She looked at his bleeding hands, then at his face, and whatever she saw there made her bow — actually bow, low and solemn — before shuffling on after the others.

"I'm sorry," Elias called after her, and didn't know if he meant for the broken prophecy or the broken people or the fact that he couldn't manage to feel more triumphant about any of it.

She didn't turn around. But she raised one hand, briefly, the way you wave to someone across a station platform when the train is already moving.

* * *

Voss came at dusk.

Of course she did. Assassins kept business hours, and so, apparently, did guildmistresses. Elias heard the footsteps before he saw them — dozens of boots moving in perfect unison across glass, which should have been impossible and was merely expensive. Sarelle Voss crested the ridge with perhaps forty clerks behind her, all of them carrying ledgers, all of them carrying knives, none of them pretending the knives weren't also for accounting purposes.

Elias stood up. His knees filed a formal complaint. He ignored them.

"You're late," he said. "The rite was this morning."

"I know precisely when the rite was." Voss's voice was as courteous as ever, which from her was worse than shouting. "I also know what happened at it. I read the aftermath, Mr. Vane. All of it." She lifted the ledger under her arm — a thick black thing with a clasp shaped like a closed eye — and set it down on the glass between them, open, like a peace offering or a trap. Both, probably. With Voss it was always both.

"The account is settled," she said. "You understand what that means?"

"I understand you can't touch me."

"Oh, I *could* touch you. Touching is free." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, haunting a house it used to live in. "What I cannot do is collect. Not from you. Not from anyone, regarding you. The keeper invoked the old clause. She settled the entire contract against herself." Voss looked down at the open ledger, and for one unguarded moment her face did something complicated. "Do you know how long I've been in this trade, Mr. Vane? Two hundred years. I have seen people pay with memories, with years, with children, with their own reflections. I have never once seen anyone pay with *everything*."

"She didn't ask me first," Elias said. His voice cracked on it, which embarrassed him, which made it crack worse.

"No," Voss agreed. "They never do. That's rather the point of the clause." She closed the ledger with a soft, final sound. "The Choir is scattered. Their leader woke this morning unable to remember why she'd ever been certain of anything, which I'm told is the most merciful thing that's ever happened to her. The god sleeps, sealed deeper than before — the amendment was well drafted, incidentally. Sloppy penmanship, but excellent structure. Your cartographer would be proud."

"Don't," Elias said.

Voss regarded him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she inclined her head — a small bow, precise as a signature.

"The Guild withdraws from the matter of Elias Vane. Permanently. Consider yourself..." she paused, and chose the word with visible care, "...unmapped." She gathered her clerks with two fingers, and the column of them turned and marched away across the glass, forty pairs of boots striking in time, growing smaller and smaller until the horizon folded them away like a letter being put back in its envelope.

At the last moment, one of the younger clerks — a round-faced boy who couldn't have been older than twenty — glanced back and mouthed something at Elias. He couldn't make it out. He chose to believe it was *good luck*.

It helped about as much as you'd expect.

* * *

The Steppe stabilized over the following days, the way a shaken snow globe settles.

Elias stayed. He told himself it was to make sure the seal held, which was true, and also a lie, and he suspected Beatrix would have appreciated the bookkeeping of it. He walked the glass plains alone, mapping them badly in a battered notebook, because someone had once told him that a place unmapped was a place unprotected, and he had decided to take up her methods even though he ran them like a man doing arithmetic with his elbows.

The changes came slowly, then all at once. Rivers that had run in straight ruled lines began to wander. Hills that had held their elevations for a thousand years, obedient to some entry in some ledger, shrugged and grew a few feet taller, just to see. In the eastern reaches, where the glass had always been flat as a table, something green was pushing up through the seams — actual living things, weeds first, then grasses, then a stubborn sapling that Elias visited every morning like a proud uncle.

The Steppe was growing wild. Unwritten. For the first time since the god had been sealed beneath it, the land was making itself up as it went along.

"It's lovely," Elias told the sapling on the ninth morning. "I hate it. She should be here to see it."

The sapling, being a sapling, declined to comment. This was, he was learning, the default response of the universe lately.

* * *

On the twelfth day, he went back to the place where she had died.

There wasn't a body. Of course there wasn't. She hadn't died, exactly — she had been *transferred*, paid out, entered into the great ledger of things-that-are-no-longer-entries. He had held her as the ink took her, and the ink had taken all of her: her name, her history, her place in every record the Guild kept, every memory anyone held of her face. He knew this because he could feel the hole where she had been. Not in the world — in himself. There was a shape in his mind, warm and sharp-tongued and smelling faintly of chalk, and when he reached for it, he found only smooth blankness, like a page torn cleanly out.

He remembered loving her. He could not remember her.

He sat down on the glass at the center of the ruined circle and pressed his palms flat against it, the way Ivo Quill had once shown him, the way you listened for the god underneath.

"Come on," he whispered. "You owe me. Well — you don't, actually, the accounts are settled, that's the whole point, but consider it a gesture of goodwill. A rounding error in my favor. Anything."

The glass sang, faintly, the way it always did now when he touched it — the god dreaming below, stirred but sleeping, sealed behind amendments written in Elias's own blood. It sang a note that might have been sympathy and might have been tectonics.

And then he saw it.

A few paces away, half-scuffed by windblown salt, drawn directly onto the glass in white chalk: a compass rose.

Not large. Perhaps the size of a dinner plate. Four points, a ring, the little fleur marks at north and south — drawn with the quick, confident strokes of someone who had drawn ten thousand of them and got bored of perfection around number nine thousand. It was not elegant. It was not precise. One of the cardinal points wobbled slightly, as if the hand had been shaking, or hurrying, or crying.

Elias crawled to it on his hands and knees.

Chalk. There was no chalk left anywhere in his pockets. He checked. He checked twice, turning them inside out like a street magician, finding nothing but lint and a button he didn't recognize. And yet the rose was there, drawn in chalk that had no source, in a hand that no longer officially existed, at the exact spot where a woman had traded herself out of the world.

His heart performed something acrobatic and unwise.

"Okay," he breathed. "Okay. Okay okay okay."

He touched the rose. The chalk was real under his fingers — gritty, slightly damp, unmistakably chalk. He laughed, a single cracked syllable that startled a bird off a rock thirty feet away, because there were birds now too, birds that hadn't existed last month, the whole world going feral and wonderful without anyone's permission.

Then he noticed the direction.

Compass roses pointed. That was their entire personality. They pointed north, or they pointed toward safety, or water, or home. This one had four points like any other rose, but the primary needle — the long, deliberate stroke that meant *this way* — did not point north, or south, or toward the distant smudge of the border towns, or anywhere else on any map Elias had ever seen or drawn.

It pointed off the edge of everything. Out past the last surveyed line, past the wild-growing east, past the margins of the world, into country that appeared on no chart, in no ledger, in no memory.

It pointed *nowhere*.

Or — and here Elias's heart did its second acrobatic feat of the minute — it pointed somewhere that had never yet been mapped. Somewhere that couldn't be found on any record, because the woman who drew it had made very sure she existed on no record at all. Somewhere only reachable by someone willing to walk off the edge of every known thing, following a doodle in chalk, on faith and arithmetic and a love he could no longer remember the details of but could still feel in his chest like a banked fire.

"That's cheating," he told the rose, and his voice wobbled exactly the way the northern point did. "That is *textbook* cheating. You'd dock me points for this. You'd write it up in red ink. 'Mr. Vane relies on sentiment rather than proper surveying technique.'"

The rose said nothing. Roses are famously tight-lipped.

But the needle seemed, in the slanting afternoon light, to gleam just slightly fresher than the rest of the chalk. As if it had been redrawn recently. As if it might be redrawn again tomorrow, and the day after, by a hand with no name, patiently, in a language of lines and directions, saying the only thing she had left to say:

*This way.*

Elias Vane sat on the glass beside the impossible compass rose until the sun went down, and then he opened his battered notebook to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in careful letters:

*Expedition One. Destination: unknown. Purpose: unknown. Equipment: insufficient.*

Underneath, after a moment, he added:

*Party of one. Hopeful.*

Then he looked up at the darkening edge of the world, where the needle pointed, and began, at last, to plan.
