## 20. What the Ledgers Forgot

The trouble with running a guild of impossible things, Elias discovered in his third week as acting Keeper of Accounts, was that the accounts refused to behave like accounts.

He sat at Beatrix's old desk — his desk now, though he still couldn't say the word *mine* without flinching — and stared at the morning's ledger. The entry read:

*Received: one jar of dawn, slightly used. Paid: two favors, one apology, and the promise of rain over the Vetch farm every Tuesday for a year.*

The problem was that the Vetch farm had burned down eleven years ago. The farmer had sold the land to a man who turned out to be a very committed flock of crows wearing a coat. And yet the ledger insisted the debt stood, and when Elias had tried to strike it out, the ink had simply waited until he looked away and rewritten itself.

"You have to negotiate," said Pip, the guild's youngest clerk, who was fourteen and had been born into the trade and therefore treated miracles the way other children treated chores. She was balancing a bottle of bottled thunder on her palm while she talked, which she claimed helped her think. "You can't just cross things out. The Steppe doesn't obey ink. It obeys *intent*. You crossed it out like you were angry at it."

"I *was* angry at it."

"Well, there's your problem. Now it thinks you're declaring war on a farm."

Elias put his head down on the desk, next to the jar of dawn, which glowed faintly and smelled, inexplicably, of his grandmother's kitchen. He missed her. He'd never met her. That was the kind of sentence that had become normal in his life.

Three weeks since the glass had gone quiet. Three weeks since Sarelle Voss had looked down at the settled account — at the place where Beatrix Montrose should have been standing and wasn't, where no one remembered her standing, where even the floor seemed faintly puzzled by the shape of the absence — and withdrawn, bound by law older than her office. Three weeks since the Ash Choir had scattered like ash does, which is to say everywhere and then nowhere.

And three weeks since Elias had woken up every morning reaching for a name that wouldn't come, knowing only that something was missing, the way you know a tooth is gone by the strange smoothness of your own smile.

He didn't remember her. That was the whole point. She had traded herself out of existence so thoroughly that grief itself had been voided from the record. He knew facts — Ivo Quill had died holding a door; half a map had been saved; a woman had walked into a cathedral of salt and forged herself into its ledgers as staff — but the facts came without a face attached. He knew someone had struck lines from the prophecy aloud while he bled corrections onto the page. He could not, no matter how he tried, remember the voice.

Except sometimes, at night, he dreamed in italics.

* * *

"Entry forty-one," Pip announced, reading from the day's intake. "A widow in Saltrow traded her late husband's last words for a winter that forgives. The words were 'tell her the blue one was always my favorite.' We don't know what the blue one is. Neither did she. That's why she sold them."

"That's heartbreaking," Elias said.

"It's *inventory*," Pip corrected, but gently, the way you correct someone you've decided not to give up on. "You're getting better, though. Last month you would've cried."

"I did cry."

"Yes, and Master Fenwick had to explain to the widow that the guild's tears were not part of the payment, and then the tears became an asset somehow, and now we owe a mermaid." She flipped the page. "Also the thunder jar is humming again. It does that when it's happy. You should stop apologizing to it."

"I'm not apologizing to a jar."

"You apologized to the *door* yesterday. I heard you."

"The door was stuck!"

"And now," Pip said, with the weary air of a much older woman, "the door feels guilty about it and sticks worse. This is what she meant about intent."

*What she meant.* The phrase slipped out of the girl so naturally that Elias went still. Pip had been hired two years ago. Beatrix had trained her. But Pip didn't remember Beatrix either — nobody did — and yet the methods survived, passed hand to hand like a recipe whose author had been eaten by the book.

"What did who mean?" Elias asked, carefully.

Pip frowned, chasing something. Her brow furrowed, unspooled, furrowed again. "I don't — there was a way of doing things. Write everything twice. Once for the guild and once for yourself, because the guild's version belongs to the guild but yours belongs to you. Only—" she patted her pockets, checked under the blotter, looked genuinely troubled— "I don't know where my second copies went. I know I kept them. I know I'd never throw them away. It's like they've been..." She trailed off, unable to find the word, because the word was *unwritten*, and the language itself had agreed not to contain it.

"Erased?" Elias offered.

"No," Pip said slowly. "It's more like they were never written. But I remember writing them. Which means I'm remembering wrong, or the world is." She shrugged, fourteen years old and already fluent in impossibility, and went back to her inventory. "Anyway. Thunder jar. Stop being nice to it."

* * *

That night, alone in the counting room, Elias did what he had taken to doing after hours: he audited Beatrix's methods instead of the books.

He had reconstructed her habits from their residue — the way a cartographer reads a landscape that's been burned off, finding the roads by what refuses to grow across them. She wrote everything twice. She negotiated with debts rather than striking them. She left margins wide, "for the truth that arrives late," though he didn't know how he knew she'd said that, only that the phrase lived in him like a splinter working toward the surface.

He honored all of it, badly. His columns drifted. His sums wandered. Twice he had accidentally purchased a bridge. But he was honest, and in the Glass Steppe, honesty was rarer than thunder and worth roughly the same.

It was while reconciling October's impossible-interest accruals that he found the first orphaned entry.

It was in the wrong book entirely — tucked into the back of the *Inventory of Weather Owed*, which no one had opened since the flood of some year that had happened differently than anyone remembered. The handwriting was small and precise, slanted slightly rightward, with a cartographer's habit of crossing sevens.

*Oct. 9 — Traded my memory of the harbor at Vell to the ferryman for safe passage. Worth it. The gulls were loud anyway.*

Elias's hands stopped moving.

It wasn't a guild entry. There was no counterweight, no witness mark, no seal. It existed in no account. It was written in the margin of a book it had nothing to do with, in ink that didn't shimmer the way guild ink shimmered, in a hand that belonged to no one, because its owner had been subtracted from every registry that might have claimed it.

But it hadn't been erased. That was the thing. It had been *outside* the erasure. You cannot unwrite a woman from records she was never written into. Every official ledger had forgotten her — but these weren't official. These were the private second copies, the ones kept *for yourself, because yours belongs to you*. Scattered through the guild's archives like seeds after a fire, in margins, in flyleaves, in the backs of books no auditor would ever open.

She had known. Somewhere between the cathedral and the end, she had known exactly what the trade would cost and exactly what it couldn't touch, and she had spent her final weeks hiding pieces of herself in the one place the ledger's reach failed: the unofficial, the marginal, the true.

Elias sat down on the floor. It seemed important to be closer to the ground for this.

* * *

He searched for six nights.

Pip thought he'd lost his mind, which was fair, because he spent them crawling along the archive shelves with a lamp, reading flyleaves like a man reading tea leaves. Master Fenwick, the guild's ancient treasurer, assumed he was embezzling, which was also fair, and watched him with the satisfied expression of a man who has finally caught the new management doing *something*.

The entries surfaced slowly, each one a small detonation.

In the back of *Treatise on Doors, Drawn*: *Bought a chalk stick from a blind vendor. He charged me a secret. I gave him the one about my sister and the well. Fair price. I'll miss it.*

In the flyleaf of a water-damaged atlas: *Note to self: the surveyor with the crooked shadow asks good questions. Do not tell him about the Codex yet. Tell him tomorrow. Tomorrow is a lie I am telling myself and I know it.*

Elias read that one four times. He didn't know who the surveyor was. He understood, distantly, that it was him — that he had once been a stranger with a shadow that moved wrong, and that someone had watched him ask questions and decided, against all sense, to tell him the truth tomorrow. He felt the shape of a feeling with no name attached to it, like pressing your tongue into the gap where a tooth was.

Some entries made him laugh out loud, alone in the dark, which frightened the thunder jar downstairs:

*Traded three hours of sleep for a map of a country that doesn't exist yet. The broker said it was speculative. Everything worth having is speculative.*

*Owed Ivo Quill one argument about north. Settled tonight. He was wrong. Miss him already, and he isn't even dead yet.*

And once, pressed so faintly into the back of the *Register of Forgiven Debts* that he nearly missed it:

*If anyone ever finds these: I'm sorry about the mess. I was going to be tidier, but I ran out of existence.*

He laughed at that one too, and then he didn't laugh at all, and he sat in the lamplight with the little book open on his knees while the candles burned down around him, grieving a person he could not picture, pierced clean through by the impossible fact of her handwriting — proof that she had been real, that she had been *funny*, that she had chosen this, that somewhere in the arithmetic of her sacrifice she had left him a trail on purpose.

Because it was on purpose. He understood that by the fifth night. The entries weren't scattered randomly. They were ordered. Oldest first, deepest in the archive, each one pointing — in the way she had pointed at everything, sideways, patiently, with margins left wide for the truth that arrives late — toward the newest.

The last entry he found was dated the night before the cathedral. It was written inside the cover of the guild's oldest blank ledger, the one nobody used because its pages rejected ink, and hers had held anyway, the way she had held anyway:

*I don't know if anything survives the trade. But if I were going to survive anywhere, it would be here — outside the books, in the white space, where the map hasn't been drawn yet. A cartographer's instinct: always leave yourself an unmapped coast to walk toward.*

*If you're reading this, Elias: check the compass roses. I drew one extra on every chart I ever made. Count them.*

*Then follow the one that points nowhere.*

His heart was going hard against his ribs. He took the stairs two at a time, woke the entire archive, and pulled the charts — her charts, the ones that hung in the map room like saints' icons, the Steppe rendered in her precise, patient lines.

He counted compass roses. One chart had nine. Another, twelve. The great master chart of the Steppe, the one Ivo Quill had called her masterpiece, had thirteen.

Every other chart had twelve.

The thirteenth rose sat in the lower corner, small and perfect, drawn in the same confident hand as everything else — except its needle did not point north, or south, or any direction a needle had ever been permitted to point. It pointed off the edge of the parchment, past the border of the mapped world, into the blank paper beyond.

And when Elias leaned close, close enough that his breath fogged the old varnish, he saw that the blank space beyond the rose was not blank anymore.

Faintly, in pencil — tentative, recent, growing — a new coastline had begun to draw itself.
