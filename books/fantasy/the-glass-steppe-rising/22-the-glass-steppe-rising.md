## 22. The Glass Steppe Rising

For a thousand years, the Glass Steppe had been a well-behaved landscape.

It sat where its ledgers said it sat. It stretched exactly as far as the last surveyor had measured, to the inch, and not one inch further. Its weather was filed quarterly. Its seasons arrived on schedule, were catalogued by the Guild's Office of Temporal Affairs, and departed with receipts. When rain fell on the glass plain, it fell in amounts previously agreed upon, in droplets of approved size, and it evaporated according to contract.

This is what Beatrix Montrose's sacrifice had purchased: not merely Elias Vane's life, not merely the god's continued sleep beneath the salt, but something no one alive had thought to ask for.

She had bought the Steppe its freedom.

And the Steppe, being an old thing that had spent a millennium holding its breath, did what any freed thing does first.

It grew.

* * *

The botanists noticed before anyone else, mostly because they were the only people who bothered looking down.

Old Marta Fenwick, who had spent forty years documenting the eleven species of lichen legally permitted to exist on the glass, came out one gray morning to find a twelfth. It was small and stubborn and green, and it was growing in a crack near the eastern boundary where no crack should have been, because cracks required geological events and geological events required paperwork.

Marta knelt. She touched it. She laughed until she cried, which her colleagues found alarming, since Marta had not been known to laugh since the incident with the bottled thunder in '09.

"It's not in the ledgers," she kept saying. "It's not in *any* ledger."

By midsummer there were hundreds of such things. Grass, impossible grass, threading up through seams in the fused glass like green handwriting across a blank page. A flower nobody could name, purple and shameless, blooming in drifts along the old trade roads. A tree — an actual tree, with bark and ambitions — pushing up through the floor of the Salt Market, cracking three flagstones and one very expensive statue of Guildmistress Sarelle Voss, who looked upon this development, when she passed it on her way to the counting house, with an expression that some swore was almost approval.

The ledgers, for their part, did not know what to do.

The great accounting books of the Glass Steppe Guild had always been complete. That was their nature and their pride. Every stone accounted for, every wind itemized, every shadow assigned a value. Now the clerks opened their books each morning to find the world quietly ahead of them, adding new entries faster than pens could follow, and the books — and here the clerks would lower their voices when they said it — the books seemed *pleased*.

There was a new column appearing in the margins. No clerk had written it. No clerk could erase it. In a hand that several of the older employees found hauntingly familiar, though none could say why, the column read simply:

*Unaccounted.*

Beneath it, the numbers climbed daily.

* * *

Elias Vane ran the guild badly, and everyone agreed this was an improvement.

He was honest in a way the institution had never learned to be. He paid debts in full and on time, which unsettled the old traders so deeply that two of them renegotiated their contracts just to feel normal again. He refused to sell bottled thunder to men who wanted to weaponize it, citing a policy he invented on the spot and then, because this was the Steppe and writing made things true, entered into the guild charter retroactively. He gave refunds. The word alone caused fainting in the accounts department.

But he ran it honestly, and he ran it in her methods — triple-checking every entry, reading the margins twice, trusting the ink but never entirely trusting the hand that held the pen. And once a week, without fail, he walked the length of the counting house to the shelf where the Century Codex rested under glass, and he looked at the last page.

The prophecy was still there, but it was finished now. Amended. Struck through in a hundred places, corrected in blood that had long since turned brown, and at the bottom, in a final line that no scholar had ever managed to parse:

*Account settled. Keeper's discretion. Let it grow.*

Below that, a compass rose drawn in chalk — chalk, on vellum, which should have been impossible, which smudged if you breathed on it, which pointed nowhere and had never once pointed anywhere else.

Elias touched the glass over it every week. Some weeks he talked to it. He told it about the tree in the Salt Market. About the twelfth lichen. About how Marta Fenwick had named the new flower *montroseii* and gotten away with it because nobody remembered who Montrose had been, and the name simply sounded right, the way certain names sound right, like they've always been waiting in the language for someone to say them aloud.

The compass rose did not answer. But some weeks, when he left the archive, he could have sworn the chalk smelled fresh.

* * *

The Ash Choir scattered after the cathedral fell, as certain things do when their certainty shatters. Most of them went home. Some of them wept. Their leader, the gentle one with the terrible sincerity, was last seen walking east into the new growth, unarmed, singing — and the strange thing, reported by the surveyors who watched her go, was that the grass seemed to lean toward her as she passed, the way grass leans toward sun, or forgiveness.

Sarelle Voss stayed. Bound by the settled account, she could not pursue what had been paid for, and what had been paid for was everything, so she pursued nothing. She ran the guild's remaining territories with her old precision and her new silence, and once — only once — she stood at the edge of the wild growth for a long time, watching grass move in a wind that appeared on no schedule.

"Reckless," she said finally, to no one.

Then, quieter, in a voice she would have denied owning:

"Good."

* * *

Under the salt, the god slept.

It slept more deeply than it had ever slept, dreamless and unburdened, because a vessel had bled corrections onto its prophecy until the prophecy said only what a free thing says: *maybe*. The war it had carried in its ribs for a thousand years was struck from the text. The glory, the remaking, the righteous flood — amended, revised, redlined by a cartographer's voice and a surveyor's hand until nothing remained but rest.

Sometimes, deep in the glass, there was a sound like a sigh. The geologists called it settling. The poets called it dreaming. The clerks, checking their impossible new column, called it *unaccounted*, and let it be.

* * *

In autumn of that first wild year, a surveyor named Pell was mapping the northern reaches — properly mapping them, with instruments and permission slips, because even freedom appreciates good technique — when he found the footprints.

Two sets. Side by side.

They emerged from the old chalk door site, which had long since crumbled into ordinary wall, and they walked north together into country no ledger described, no map claimed, and no law reached. One set of prints was deep and deliberate, the stride of someone used to measuring ground before trusting it. The other wandered — pausing here, doubling back there, once veering sharply sideways for no reason Pell could determine except, perhaps, delight.

Pell followed them for half a day. They never faded. Footprints in the new grass should have faded; everything in the Steppe faded, eroded, returned to the account. These didn't. They went on and on, fresh as the hour they were pressed, heading toward the horizon where the maps stopped pretending and the world began.

He reported it, of course. He was a professional.

Elias came out himself to look. He crouched at the edge of the prints for a long time, and Pell, watching, saw the guildmaster smile in a way that had nothing of administration in it.

"They're not going anywhere," Elias said at last, standing. "Look. They just keep going. Whatever's making them isn't in a hurry."

"Should we map them, sir?"

Elias considered this. Wind moved through the wild grass, bending it in waves toward the unmapped north, and somewhere far off the glass sang — softly, the way it sang now, more lullaby than alarm.

"No," he said. "Some things you leave room for."

* * *

Far north, past the last surveyed stone, past the last honest guess, a woman with no name walked beside a man who introduced himself too often, and the world grew around them.

She didn't remember him. She had traded that away with everything else, and the trade was final, and the price had been fair, and she would have paid it again — though she couldn't have said why, only that sometimes when he laughed she felt a warmth like a page turning, like an entry being written in a book she'd lost.

But she was drawing coastlines. That much remained in her hands, whatever had been taken from her head. She drew them in chalk on the air itself, and the air obliged, and seas assembled themselves along her lines with the enthusiasm of things doing what they were made for. He carried her bag. He had opinions about where the mountains should go, all of them wrong, all of them offered anyway, and she kept them out of spite and then, gradually, kept two or three because they were good.

"You know," he said one evening, as she sketched a harbor into the dusk, "where I come from, there's a flower named after somebody nobody remembers. Purple. Shameless. Grows everywhere now."

"Sounds invasive."

"Sounds *free*," he said, and she paused, chalk hovering, feeling that page-turning warmth again, and drew the harbor one curve wider than she'd planned. Room for boats. Room for more.

Behind them, to the south, the Glass Steppe rose — not in war, not in prophecy, not in any shape the old books had drafted for it, but upward and outward in green and glass and impossible bloom, a thousand years of held breath let out all at once. The ledgers tried to keep up. The ledgers failed cheerfully. In the margins of every book in the counting house, the same patient hand added one more entry to the column no one had written:

*Unaccounted: nearly everything.*

*Growing.*

And in the new growth, side by side, two sets of footprints went on toward the edge of every map ever made — unhurried, unfading, walking into country that was only just learning its own name, leaving behind them a trail that no ledger would ever hold, pointing like a compass rose drawn in chalk:

nowhere,

and therefore

everywhere.
