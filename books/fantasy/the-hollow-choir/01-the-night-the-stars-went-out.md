## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The last mile of the Kettle Road was always the worst, and Silas Voss knew it by heart: a switchback spine of shale that clung to Thornfell's eastern shoulder like a scar that hadn't decided whether to heal. She climbed it in the dark without a lantern, because lanterns on the Kettle Road got people killed—by rockfall, by border patrol, by the kind of neighbors who remembered your face at the wrong moment. Her boots found the stones the way other people's hands found their own doorframes. Twenty years of running this road had worn it into her body somewhere below memory.

On her back, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped beneath a false layer of dried fish, rode forty pounds of unlicensed dreamsalt out of the lowland marshes. On her hip, wrapped in nothing at all because it needed no hiding, rode the thing she actually valued: her map case.

She paused at the bend where the trail crested the shoulder of Crag's Tooth and did what she always did there, what she'd done on this exact stone since she was nineteen years old and too green to know better. She looked up.

Thornfell's sky was the finest mapmaker's sky in the world. Every smuggler said so, though none of them would say why it mattered. But Silas navigated by stars the way priests navigated by scripture—the Wagon, the Sisters Three, the pale smear of the Veil arcing north like spilled milk—and she checked them the way a merchant checks his ledger. If the sky was right, the world was right, and if the world was right, then Silas Voss could go on being nobody, which was the only thing she had ever wanted to be.

The sky was wrong.

Not clouded. Not storming. Clouds had texture, weight, a smell before them and a hush after. This was different. She stood on the crest with the wind pulling at her collar and stared into a blackness so complete it felt less like night than like absence—a ceiling of nothing, smooth as the inside of a closed eye. No Wagon. No Sisters. No Veil. The mountains around her were still visible, faintly, in the gray wash of the risen moon—

She turned west. The moon was gone too.

Silas stood very still for a long time. Below her, the switchbacks fell away toward the valley and the waystation lights of Harrowgate, small and warm and ordinary. Above her, nothing. Her breath came out white. Somewhere down the slope a stone clattered loose, and the sound went on much longer than it should have, falling through a darkness that seemed to have gotten deeper since she'd started listening.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *You've been three weeks on bad sleep and worse food. The fog rolled up from the valley and you walked under it.*

But fog didn't sit above the peaks. Fog didn't erase the moon while leaving the mountain grass silvered with its light. And Silas Voss had survived twenty years in the margins of Thornfell precisely because she never lied to herself about what she saw. She wrote down what was true. That was the whole discipline of her life. Everything else—her silence, her solitude, the careful dullness of her reputation—was built on top of that one foundation.

She took out her field book, braced it against her knee, and by touch made a note in the dark: *No stars. No moon. Light source unknown.* Her hand was steady. She noticed that her hand was steady, and was grateful for it, and hated how much she needed to be grateful for it.

Then she shouldered her pack and started down toward Harrowgate, walking faster than caution strictly allowed.

* * *

Harrowgate Waystation sat in the throat of the pass like a swallowed stone: a squat customs house, a stable, an inn with a leaning chimney, all of it built by some long-dead surveyor who had understood that roads are rivers and waystations are the fords. Silas had stopped there perhaps two hundred times. She knew the innkeeper, Marta, well enough to trade salt for stew and not one question further. That was the arrangement. That was the beauty of the arrangement.

Tonight the yard was full.

Wagons crowded the stable yard in attitudes of abandonment—one with its harness half-slipped, another backed against the customs house as if it had arrived in a hurry and meant to leave the same way. People stood in knots in the cold, talking low and fast, or sat on wagon benches staring upward with the slack faces of people watching something that refused to happen. A child was crying somewhere, not loudly, in the exhausted rhythm of a child who has been crying a long time.

Silas slipped her pack straps loose and let the fish-and-salt bundle slump against the stable wall where any honest carrier's pack might rest, and went inside.

Marta's common room smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath both, a wrongness Silas couldn't name until she'd crossed half the room: no one was drinking. Mugs sat full and forgotten. In twenty years, Silas had never seen an untouched drink in Marta's common room.

"Marta." She set a coin on the bar. "Stew. And whatever's wrong."

The innkeeper was a broad woman with forearms like a farrier's, and Silas had watched her laugh off blizzards, tax collectors, and a knife fight over a card debt without spilling the stew. Now she wiped the same clean spot on the bar over and over.

"Sky's gone," Marta said. "That's what's wrong. Sky's gone and half these people can't say where they come from."

"Can't say—" Silas frowned. "Drunk?"

"Not drunk. Look at them. Go on, look."

Silas looked. Near the fire, a drover she vaguely recognized from the southern runs—gray beard, knuckles like walnuts—was being questioned gently by a woman in a customs officer's coat. Silas caught fragments.

"—left on the fourth day of the month, I know that, I know my wife packed me smoked eel—"

"And where did you leave from, Master Corben? What town?"

A pause. The drover's face worked. Silas had seen that face before, on men trying to remember a dream.

"There's a... it's on the river. There's a bridge with a shrine on it. My mother put a ribbon on the shrine when I took the road." His voice rose slightly. "I can see the bridge. I can see the whole street. But I can't tell you the name of it. It's—it's like the name's a word in a language I used to speak."

The customs woman wrote something down. Her hand, Silas noticed, was shaking.

"They're all like that," Marta said quietly. "Every soul that came through tonight from the east side. Can't name their towns. Can't name their counties. One man couldn't remember his own mother's face, just that he had one. And they keep saying the same thing—" she lowered her voice, "—they keep saying it feels like the name isn't gone. Like it's *behind* something. Like someone shut a door on it."

Silas's skin prickled. She thought of her note in the field book. *Light source unknown.* She thought about doors.

"And the ones from the west?" she asked. "From our side?"

"Loud and frightened but whole. They remember everything." Marta finally set a bowl in front of her, and the stew was the usual good brown stew, and Silas found she had no appetite at all. "It's the east side, past the old ward-stones. Anything that crossed the high passes today came in hollowed."

Silas ate three spoonfuls anyway, because a body is a tool and tools get maintained. Around her the room murmured like a hive in winter. She catalogued what she heard the way she catalogued everything: the Provisional Council's riders had passed through at dusk, heading east, fast; the customs house had sealed its strongbox; someone claimed the ward-tower at Grey Saddle had gone dark, and someone else said that was nonsense, wards didn't go dark, wards were older than the country itself—

"Wards," Silas said. "What wards?"

Marta gave her a look. "The border wards. The ones every child learns the rhyme for. *Stone to stone and star to star, the ring holds fast wherever you are.*" She sang it flatly, absently, the way you sing a thing you've sung ten thousand times. "They run the whole circumference of Thornfell. Old beyond old. Ward-keepers used to tend them, before the order fell apart. You must have walked past a hundred of the standing stones."

"I've mapped them," Silas said. "I've mapped every one." And she had—drew their positions, noted their inscriptions, filed them away as landmarks and nothing more, the way everyone did, the way she'd been taught by the world to think of them: old, holy, decorative. "What about them?"

"They're broken." Marta's voice dropped further. "Customs man came through an hour ago from Grey Saddle. Says the ward-stone there is shattered. Not cracked—shattered, like it burst from the inside. And he says the shards are still warm, and he says—" she hesitated, and Silas realized the innkeeper was afraid of the sentence itself, "—he says there was writing in the air above it. Hanging there. Letters nobody could read, burning slow, like the stone had been holding words back for a hundred years and finally let them out."

The fire popped. The crying child in the yard had gone quiet, which was somehow worse.

Silas sat with her cooling stew and felt the strange sensation, familiar to anyone who has spent a life being invisible, of invisibility failing. All her arrangements—all the dullness, the distance, the decades of being a woman who carried fish and asked no questions—rested on the world staying predictable. Predictable was safe. Predictable was the whole architecture of her survival. And tonight the sky had gone out, the roads were filling with people whose hometowns had been stolen out of their mouths, and the oldest stones in the country were bursting open with unread letters.

Someone would come asking questions now. Someone always came asking questions when the world broke, and they'd ask them of everyone, including carriers of fish and salt, and Silas Voss's carefully blank life was going to be turned over and examined like a smuggler's pack at a checkpoint.

But that wasn't the worst of it, and she knew it even as she catalogued the practical dangers. The worst of it was smaller and colder: the drover by the fire, weeping softly over a bridge he could see perfectly and name never. Because Silas had spent her whole life keeping her own name quiet, keeping her history folded up small, and she recognized the shape of what had happened to him. Someone had done to those towns what she did to herself every day—only from the outside, only all at once, only forever.

And the stars. She kept coming back to the stars. She had navigated by them for twenty years; she knew their positions the way she knew her own scars. Which meant she knew, with a certainty that sat in her stomach like swallowed ice, that the pattern was wrong even in memory. She tried to place the Wagon relative to the Sisters Three and found the geometry slipping, sliding, redrawing itself behind her eyes like ink bleeding through paper.

Her maps. Her beautiful, obsessive, twenty-year archive of every road, ridge, ford, and standing stone in Thornfell. If the sky could lie, what else had been lying?

"Room for the night?" she asked Marta, and paid for two, because she intended to be awake in one of them doing something she hadn't done in years: checking her work.

* * *

By lamplight, in a narrow room that smelled of tallow and old pine, Silas unrolled her master map of the eastern passes across the bed and weighted the corners with her boots.

She had drawn this map eleven times over twenty years, refining it, correcting it, loving it in the wordless way she loved almost nothing else. She knew it was accurate. Accuracy was the one clean thing about her.

She checked it against her field notes from the spring run. The village of Lowmere should sit where the Aldwater bent south, a day's walk below the shrine bridge. She looked at the map.

Lowmere was not where she had drawn it.

The river was where she had drawn it—but the village sat two miles east, on ground she had personally surveyed as bare hillside, in a fold of terrain that did not exist. And it wasn't smudged or altered in any way she could detect. The ink simply read differently, as if it had always read that way, as if she had always drawn it there, and only her memory insisted otherwise.

Silas sat down slowly on the edge of the bed.

Below Lowmere, the town of Ashford Ferry had moved north. The pass at Grey Saddle had shifted a quarter-mile deeper into the range. And along the entire eastern margin of the map, following the line of the old ward-stones, the ink had begun to fade—not uniformly, not like water damage, but letter by letter, name by name, as if something were eating the words in a specific order.

She picked up her pen with a hand that was no longer entirely steady, and wrote in the margin, in her smallest, hardest script:

*The country is lying to me. Or I have been lying to myself for twenty years, and tonight it stopped working.*

Outside, in the starless dark, something began to hum.

It was very faint, very far away, threading up through the floorboards and the frozen ground like water finding a crack in a wall. It was not a machine. It was not wind. It was voices—many voices, layered, rising and falling in a pattern that was almost a song, almost a lament, almost a list. Silas pressed her palm flat against the floorboards and listened, and the hair rose on her arms, because underneath the strangeness of it she heard something unmistakable:

It sounded like names. Thousands of names, being spoken by no one, in the dark, over and over, as if someone had finally remembered them and could not stop.

Silas Voss blew out her lamp, sat in the black with her back against the wall and her map case hugged to her chest like a shield, and did not sleep at all.
