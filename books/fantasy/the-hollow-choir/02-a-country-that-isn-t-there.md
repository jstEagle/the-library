## 2. A Country That Isn't There

Silas slept four hours and woke to the sound of her maps dying.

Not a sound, exactly—she'd been a cartographer long enough to know that maps made no sound when they were right. It was the silence that was wrong. The waystation at Grey Ford had a particular creak at dawn, timbers settling as the frost let go of them, and beneath that creak there should have been the small industrious noises of her trade: the whisper of her brush against vellum, the tick of her dividers, the scratch of ink. Instead there was nothing but her own breathing and a wrongness so complete it sat on her chest like a stone.

She lit the lamp. She looked at the table where she'd left six finished sheets of Thornfell, each one the product of eleven years of walking, measuring, lying to border officials, and bribing shepherds for their grandfathers' memories.

The town of Harrowgate had moved.

Not drifted. Not smudged. *Moved*—the way a chess piece moves, decisively, from one square to another. On every sheet, in her own careful hand, Harrowgate now sat forty miles east of where it had stood the night before, nestled into a valley Silas had personally surveyed and found empty. The river Vane had shifted with it, obediently rerouting itself through country that had never held water. And the roads—the roads she knew like the veins of her own wrist—had rearranged themselves to serve the new position, as if they had always done so.

Silas stood very still. She checked the date on her field notes. She checked the wax seal on the map case, unbroken since Grey Ford. She took out her oldest sheet, the first map she'd ever drawn, aged and foxed and soft as cloth, the one she kept less for use than for proof that she had once been someone who finished things.

Harrowgate had moved on that one too.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, because the room needed to hear a human voice say it.

The room did not agree with her. The room simply went on being wrong.

* * *

By midmorning she had done what she always did with a problem: she walked toward it. She went down into Grey Ford proper, past the shrine with its starless dome of painted sky, and asked the ferryman how far it was to Harrowgate.

"Two days' walk," he said. "East, along the Vane."

"West," said Silas. "It's west."

The ferryman looked at her with the flat patience of a man who has answered fools before. "I've lived here fifty years, miss. My father poached eels out of the Vane below Harrowgate's walls." He turned back to his ropes. "You want the east road."

She wanted to grab him by the collar and shake the truth out of him, and that frightened her more than the maps did, because she could see—he believed it. Completely. There was no lie in his face, no flicker of the effort it takes to hold a falsehood. Somewhere in the night, the world had changed its mind about where Harrowgate was, and every soul in it had changed its mind along with the world. Everyone except her.

Everyone except her, and whoever else was still awake inside the forgery.

She thought of the travelers at the waystation who couldn't say where they'd come from. She thought of the shattered wards, the black sky, the way the dark above Thornfell hadn't felt like night but like a ceiling, close and load-bearing. She thought of Ede Marrow.

Every smuggler on the mountain roads knew the name, though none of them said it above a whisper. Ede Marrow, once Warden-First of the Thornfell Line, the youngest ever raised to the rank, the man who had maintained the border wards for twenty years—and then, twelve years ago, had burned his own commission, broken his own oaths, and walked into the high country to live alone in a tower that appeared on no map. Silas had found that tower years ago, the way she found everything: by noticing what the land tried to hide. A road that bent around a hill for no reason. Birds that refused to cross a certain ridgeline. She had marked it in her private atlas under a name that meant nothing—"the crooked mile"—and she had never gone in, because whatever a warden kept in exile, he kept it alone on purpose.

That was yesterday's thinking. Yesterday, Silas Voss had wanted only to be invisible. Today the world had proven that invisibility was a privilege granted by something, and that something had withdrawn it.

She packed light: instruments, ink, three days of food, and all her maps, even the wrong ones. Especially the wrong ones. Then she climbed east—or what the world now insisted was east—toward the crooked mile.

* * *

The land argued with her the whole way.

It was subtle at first. A shepherd's hut she remembered standing alone in a saddle now had neighbors, three of them, with fences and a well and a dog that barked at her as though she were the stranger. A stand of birches she'd used as a landmark for a decade had become beeches, smooth-barked and wrong. Twice she passed milestones whose carved distances matched nothing—not her memory, not her maps, not each other.

And once—only once—she saw the seam.

She had stopped to fix a bootlace on a ridge above the Vane, and when she looked up, the valley below was in two places at once. For the space of perhaps ten heartbeats, the river ran both its old course and its new one, braided together like two threads in a single stitch, and on the far bank she saw a village that was also not there: chimneys smoking, laundry on lines, a church tower—and then the image folded shut like a book closing, and there was only grass and the single new channel of the Vane, running obediently east.

Silas sat down hard on a rock. Her hands were shaking. Not from cold.

Because she had recognized the ghost-village. She had grown up two valleys over from it. Its name was Lowmere, and Lowmere had burned down when she was nine, and everyone had said so, and she had seen the ruins herself a hundred times—

Except that she hadn't. She realized it now, turning the memory over like a coin she suspected was clipped. She had no memory of the fire itself. No memory of smoke, of refugees, of the bell tolling. Only the fact of it, sitting in her mind like a receipt for goods never delivered. And the ruins she'd visited—she tried to picture them and got grass, just grass, a green hollow where a ruin should have been, and herself standing in it wondering why she'd come.

"How much else," she whispered. The valley didn't answer. The valley was complicit.

She walked faster after that. She did not look at any more villages.

* * *

The crooked mile announced itself the way it always had: a bend in the track that served no purpose, a hill the road declined to climb for no reason a surveyor could defend. But today the wrongness had teeth. As she approached, the air thickened like the air before a storm, and her skin prickled with the old static feeling of standing near a ward-line—except the wards were dead, all of them, she'd seen them dead with her own eyes, and yet here was this one small stubborn knot of them, holding.

The tower rose out of the hollow beyond the ridge: squat, grey, older than the style of any era she could name, which was itself an answer of sorts. Smoke curled from its chimney. Someone was home.

She hammered on the door until her knuckles ached. When it finally opened, the man in the gap was not what rumor promised. Rumor said Ede Marrow had gone mad, or monstrous, or both. The man before her was gaunt and grey-bearded, with ink stains to rival her own and eyes red-rimmed from sleeplessness or weeping or both, and he looked at her the way a drowning man looks at driftwood.

"You can see it," he said. Not hello. Not who goes there. "You can see the tower. You came through the fold in the road." His voice cracked. "How? The wards are down. Everything is down. How are you still *seeing*?"

"My maps moved," Silas said. It sounded insane spoken aloud, and she pressed on anyway, because insanity shared was half carried. "Overnight. Harrowgate jumped forty miles east. The river followed it. Every sheet, including ones I drew ten years ago. And the people—" She swallowed. "The people remember the new version. All of them. Like they were rewritten with the paper."

Ede Marrow closed his eyes. He leaned against the doorframe as if the question had weight enough to shove him.

"Eleven days," he said. "I've been watching it happen for eleven days, and you're the first person through my door who noticed anything at all." He stepped back and pulled the door wide. "Come in, mapper. Before the land notices you noticing."

* * *

Inside, the tower was one tall room stacked floor to ceiling with ward-stone, brass, and paper. Ede cleared a space on a workbench by sweeping aside a model of the border line—a beautiful thing, a miniature Thornfell in painted wood, with tiny copper studs marking the old ward anchors—and Silas saw, with a lurch, that the model's mountains were wrong. Or rather, that they were *doubled*: beneath the familiar peaks of Thornfell, visible where the paint had worn thin, lay the faint ghost of another range, another shape, drawn first and painted over.

"Tell me what you know," Ede said. "All of it. Start with the stars."

She told him. The run over the pass, the stars going out between one glance and the next, the waystation full of travelers who couldn't name their origins, the wards shattered, the maps rewriting themselves, the ferryman, the doubled valley, the village that had never burned. He listened without interrupting, and the longer she spoke the stiller he became, until by the end he was motionless as the ward-stone around him.

"The stars," he repeated softly. "You understand that's the part that matters most. The rest—the towns, the roads—that's the ledger being cooked. The stars are the vault door coming off." He turned to the painted model and laid his palm flat across its peaks. "What do you know about the founding of Thornfell, Silas Voss?"

"It's old," she said. "Older than the Crown. The First Charter says the wards were raised to keep out the Unmapped—raiders from beyond the line, savages, the usual."

"The First Charter." He smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, grief wearing courtesy as a mask. "Yes. I wrote the commentary on it, once. I was proud of that commentary." He lifted his hand from the model and began, gently, to scrape at the paint of one peak with his thumbnail. Beneath it, the ghost-range showed clearer: different valleys, different rivers, a different spine to the land entirely. "Look at the strata. Look at what's underneath your country. The wards were never built to keep the Unmapped *out*, Silas. There are no Unmapped. There never were."

"Then what were they for?"

"To keep something *in*." He said it quietly, the way you'd speak in a sickroom. "A hundred and eight years ago, the founders of Thornfell committed the largest theft in the history of the world. They didn't conquer a nation. Conquest leaves ruins, refugees, songs. Songs get sung. No—they *unmade* one. They took the kingdom of Vael—every town, every road, every grave—and they folded it up beneath their new mountains, and they wrote a false history over it, and they bound every stolen soul into a single instrument to hold the lie together." His hand trembled against the model. "They called it the Choir. We called it the border wards. The voices of everyone who was erased, singing the forgery into place, forever. Keeping the mountains where we put them. Keeping the rivers in their channels. Keeping the *names* suppressed."

Silas heard her own heartbeat. "The Hollow Choir," she said. "The smugglers' story. Singers in the passes at night."

"A story with a source," Ede said. "For a hundred years the Choir sang and the lie held, and the stars shone, because the founders stole those too—there was a true sky over Vael, and they folded that up with everything else and hung a copy in its place. Eleven days ago, the Choir stopped obeying. The voices rose. The wards broke. And now the land is trying to remember what it actually is, and the forgery is fighting back, and every person in Thornfell is caught between the two versions like paper in a press." He met her eyes. "Including you. Except you're not caught. You're awake. Do you understand how impossible that is? The rewriting takes everyone. It took me for six hours last week—I woke up on my own floor with no idea why I lived here. Something is keeping you *you*."

Silas thought of her mother's lullaby, the one with the melody that fit no scale she'd ever learned. She thought of the green hollow where Lowmere's ruins weren't. She thought of the way she had always, always needed to know exactly where she was, as if being lost might dissolve her.

"I don't want to be special," she said. "I want my maps to be true."

"No," Ede said, and now the mask slipped entirely, and what was underneath was a hundred and eight years of inherited guilt. "You want the *world* to be true. So do I. That's why I'm still alive in this tower instead of dead by my own hand—I have to see how it ends." He swept the little copper studs into a leather roll and thrust it into a satchel, already moving, already deciding. "There's an archive under the old Warden's College at Candlemere. Records the order swore never existed. If we're going to survive what's coming—if anyone is—we need the original survey of Vael. The true map. The one they buried."

"And if we find it?"

He paused at the door, silhouetted against the grey light, and outside, faint and far away down the folded valleys, Silas heard it for the first time with waking ears: singing. Hundreds of voices, wordless, rising off the mountains like weather.

"Then we'll finally know," Ede Marrow said, "what we're standing on. And God help us, mapper—I think it's starting to stand up."

Behind them, on the workbench, the painted model of Thornfell shivered on its shelf. And slowly, like a bruise surfacing, the ghost-range beneath began to show through the paint.
