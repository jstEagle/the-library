## 3. The Child Who Hums

The village had no name by the time they reached it.

Silas knew it had one—her oldest map said *Brackenholt* in her own careful hand, ink gone brown at the edges—but when she asked the first woman she saw, a broad-shouldered farmer stacking firewood in the grey morning light, the woman only frowned and touched her own mouth as if checking whether anything lived there.

"Name of what?" the woman said. "The village? Villages don't have names. They just... are."

Behind her, the well. Behind that, a crooked row of houses with slate roofs and smoke rising from chimneys, all of it ordinary, all of it wrong. A sign hung over the tavern door where a name should have been painted, and the wood beneath was pale and clean, like a scar.

Ede Marrow stopped in the lane beside her and went very still, the way he did when something confirmed a fear he'd already priced out. "It's spreading," he said quietly. "The erasure. It started at the borders and now it's working inward, eating the small things first." He looked at the blank sign the way other men looked at corpses. "Names go before people do. It's how it always worked. You can't unmake a place while its name is still being spoken."

"Then someone should start speaking," Silas said, and raised her voice to the empty lane. "This is Brackenholt. Brackenholt. Say it back to me."

A door opened down the street. Then another. Faces appeared in windows—not hostile, just lost, the way sheep look when the flock scatters. An old man came out onto his step and mouthed the word once, twice, tasting it, and Silas watched something kindle behind his eyes, some small coal of selfhood catching.

Then the horses came.

She heard them before she saw them: shod hooves on packed earth, too many, moving too fast for a village this size. Ede's hand closed on her arm. "Council outriders," he breathed. "Off the road. Now."

They went over a fence into a turnip field and lay flat among the wet green tops as six riders drew up in the lane. Their armor was new, unblemished, the Provisional Council's white-and-gold still bright enough to hurt the eye. The officer dismounted in front of the old man on his step.

"You spoke," the officer said. His voice carried easily; he'd done this before. "We heard you speaking a word. What word?"

The old man's mouth worked. Whatever courage Silas had lit in him guttered under the weight of those white pauldrons. "I don't—I don't recall, sir. Just an old sound."

"There are no old sounds anymore," the officer said. "There is the Council's tongue and there is silence. Anyone found using unauthorized nomenclature will be detained for assessment." He turned to the firewood woman. "You. Have travelers passed through? A woman with maps. A man who talks about wards."

Silas pressed herself flatter against the cold earth. Beside her, Ede had gone the color of ash, but his eyes were moving, calculating, and she realized with a strange lurch that he was not afraid for himself. He was afraid they'd find what he carried—the archive pages sewn into his coat lining, a century of crime written in his own order's hand.

The riders moved on through the village, kicking in doors with a courtesy that was somehow worse than violence, and Silas lay in the turnips and thought: *they're hunting memory itself.* Not smugglers, not spies. Anyone who remembered. The whole country was becoming a house where the owners burned every photograph and called the emptiness peace.

When the hoofbeats faded eastward, Ede pulled her up. "Not through the village," he said. "Around it. There's a drover's track north—we can pick up the high road past the tor."

"I know the track," Silas said. "Knew," she corrected, and hated the word.

* * *

Her maps were lying to her again.

She'd stopped to check their position at midday, unfolded the Thornfell sheet across a flat stone, and felt the vertigo rise like floodwater. The drover's track was drawn a full mile west of where it actually ran. The tor itself had drifted south. Rivers she'd forded a hundred times now bent toward mountains they'd never touched. And in the blank corner of the map where nothing should be—where she'd always noted *unproductive scrubland, no water*—faint lines were surfacing through the paper like bruises coming up through skin. Roads. A road network, dense and deliberate, converging on a point in the deep fells that her ink had never marked because her ink had never been allowed to see it.

"Ede," she said. "Come look at this."

He studied it for a long moment. "That's Vael," he said finally. "Or the ghost of it. The roads underneath. Your maps aren't redrawing themselves, Silas—they're remembering. Paper holds memory better than people do. Nobody ever thought to ward the paper."

"So the country I've spent my life mapping—"

"Was drawn on top of another country," Ede said, "the way you paint over a wall to hide the damp. And the paint is peeling." He rolled the map up with hands that weren't quite steady. "Which means the roads underneath are waking too. Old paths. Paths that exist again whether anyone remembers them or not."

They heard the humming that evening.

It came drifting down from the high crags as the light failed—a thin, wavering thread of melody with no words in it, or words worn away, the way a coin wears smooth. It rose and fell and rose again, patient as water working on stone. Silas stood in the bracken with her hand on her knife and felt the hair lift on her arms, because the tune was familiar in a way she could not account for. Not familiar like a song she knew. Familiar like a room she'd dreamed.

"It's a child," Ede said. "Listen—that's a child's voice."

"A child alone on the fells at dusk is dead by morning."

"Or it's bait," Ede said grimly. "The Council uses whatever works. Remembered things draw us; maybe forgotten things draw them."

But even as he said it, Silas was already moving uphill, and she told herself it was reconnaissance, due diligence, the caution of a professional—and knew she was lying. There was something in that humming that pulled at her like a hook set under the ribs. She climbed through the bracken and gorse with the last light dying violet behind the peaks, following the melody through folds of rock, until she rounded a shelf of granite and found her.

The child sat cross-legged on a flat stone like a small dark bird, rag-wrapped, barefoot despite the frost beginning to silver the grass, maybe eight years old, maybe ten—it was impossible to tell, because her face had the odd unfinished quality of a face that hadn't decided yet whom it belonged to. Her eyes were enormous and dark and entirely unafraid. She watched Silas come the way a fox watches: cataloguing, weighing, unhurried.

And she kept humming.

"That song," Silas said, keeping her voice low and level, the way you'd speak to something wild. "Where did you learn it?"

The child tilted her head. Her lips moved around the next phrase without sound, and then she said, in a voice like dry leaves: "Everyone knows it. They just forgot they know it." She pointed one thin finger at Silas's chest. "You know it. It's in you. Underneath."

"I don't sing."

"You hum when you think no one's listening," the child said. "You did it crossing the ford yesterday. Wrong notes, though. You got it wrong." Something flickered across her face that might have been pity, which was a horrifying thing to receive from an eight-year-old. "You'll get it right later."

"How—" Silas crouched slowly, keeping her hands visible. "What's your name?"

The child went still. Utterly still, in a way children never were, in a way stones were. When she answered, her voice had changed—flattened, hollowed, like wind through an empty house.

"They took it. Before I could keep it safe." She touched her own throat. "I'm saving up for a new one."

Every instinct Silas owned said *run*. But she'd spent her whole life reading terrain, reading weather, reading the small signs that told her which way trouble would come from, and everything in her read this child and found no trap in it—only hunger, and cold, and a loneliness so vast it had its own gravity.

"Pip," Ede said softly, behind her.

Silas hadn't heard him climb up. He stood at the edge of the granite shelf with his coat collar up, staring at the child as if looking at a ghost—which, Silas was beginning to understand, might be exactly what he was doing.

"What did you say?"

"Pip," Ede repeated. "In the old ward-records. The Choir's registers listed the taken by number, but the first ones—the very first, the founding theft—there's one entry with a note in the margin. *The little one who piped.* They used her voice to tune the wards, Silas. To teach the walls what silence sounded like." He knelt, slow and careful, meeting the child's enormous eyes. "Is that you? Are you the piper?"

The child considered him for a long moment. Then she began to hum again—and this time the melody *changed*, opened, deepened, and the air around the flat stone seemed to lean in and listen. The frost on the grass melted in a neat circle around them. Somewhere below, Silas felt rather than heard the ground shift, the way a sleeper shifts, turning toward a familiar voice.

"The paths are awake," the child said, stopping abruptly. She pointed north, into the black fells, where Silas's map showed nothing at all. "The old road. It's open tonight. It won't be open tomorrow." She stood, quick as a darting bird, and grabbed the hem of Silas's coat with fingers like ice. "Soldiers are coming up the valley. Six of them. They'll be here before the moon clears the ridge. We have to walk the road that isn't there."

"There is no road there," Silas said automatically. "I've mapped these fells my entire life. There is nothing north of here but—"

"Nothing," Pip agreed solemnly. "That's why we can walk it. Nothing can't be followed."

Silas looked at Ede. Ede looked at Silas. Below them, faint but unmistakable, came the ring of a horseshoe striking stone, and then another, and the child's grip on her coat tightened with terrible certainty.

"They're coming," Pip said. "Hold my hand. Both of you. And whatever you hear behind us—don't look back at the place where you used to be."

Silas took the small cold hand. It was, she would think later, the first truly free choice she ever made.
