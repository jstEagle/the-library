## 4. What the Wards Were For

The archive was behind the waterfall.

That was the joke of it, Ede thought as he hauled aside the curtain of falling water and let the cold soak through his coat for the hundredth time in eleven years. The Wardens' Order had built its greatest vault beneath a cataract in the Thornfells because no one trusted a place that was always washing itself clean. Water forgot nothing, his teachers used to say. It only carried things away. He had believed them once. He had been young, and the Order had been kind to him in the way that institutions are kind to useful instruments.

He turned and looked at his companions on the wet stone shelf. Silas Voss stood with her arms crossed against the spray, her maps wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to her back like a wounded comrade she refused to abandon. Behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of her coat, was the child—Pip—who had not stopped humming since they'd pulled her out of the nameless village two days ago. The humming wasn't a tune, exactly. It was more like weather. It moved through the gorge and made the water sound different, made the rock sound different, made Ede's own pulse feel like it was being counted by something patient.

"The wards will have sealed," he said. "They do that when—" He stopped. *When their keeper is disgraced,* he'd been about to say. *When their keeper runs.* "When they're left alone too long."

"Then open them," Silas said. No accusation in it, which was worse. She simply stated it the way she stated distances and river crossings, as though the world were a thing that could be surveyed. "You brought us here."

"I brought us here," he agreed, and turned to the rock face.

His hands remembered the sequence even after eleven years: left palm flat over the seam where the waterfall's mist silvered the stone, three words in the old cadence—not spoken so much as *shaped*, breathed into the grain of the mountain—and then the slow grinding surrender of granite parting along a line no mason had ever cut. The door didn't open so much as agree to be elsewhere.

Warm air came out. Dry air, smelling of vellum dust and lamp oil and, underneath both, something faintly sweet that Ede had never been able to name and had privately begun to think of as *the smell of lies kept well*.

"You first," Silas said.

*Of course me first.* He went down into the dark.

* * *

The archive was a cathedral turned inside out—a hollowed chamber tall enough to hold its own weather, its walls shelved floor to ceiling with ledgers, folios, ward-plates, and instruments whose purposes Ede had learned and then spent a decade trying to unlearn. He lit the standing lamps one by one, and each flame caught with a soft intake of breath, as if the room had been holding it since he left.

Pip slipped past him immediately. She moved through the aisles like something returning to water, trailing her fingers along the shelf edges, and her humming changed—deepened, gathered harmonics it shouldn't have been able to hold in such a small body. Ede watched her and felt the old dread rearrange itself into a new shape.

"She knows this place," Silas said quietly, at his shoulder.

"No." He made himself say it plainly. "This place knows *her*. Or what she carries."

Silas looked at him. In the lamplight her eyes were the grey of a map before the ink goes down—all possibility, all patience. "Then stop circling, Marrow. Tell me why the stars went out."

So he did what he had sworn never to do. He walked to the center of the archive, to the reading table beneath the great dome, and he took down the First Ledger.

It was heavier than memory. Bound in grey hide that no one would ever confirm was hide at all, its clasps worked in the shape of interlocking thorns—the sigil of the Wardens' Order, the seal he still sometimes caught himself tracing absently on tabletops, the way a man touches a scar. He laid it on the table. Pip's humming stopped. The silence it left behind was enormous.

"The Order teaches that the border wards protect us," Ede said. His voice sounded strange in the vaulted quiet, stripped of the echo it should have had, as if the room were drinking his words. "That beyond Thornfell there are enemies, wastelands, the unwarded dark. That every Warden who dies at his post dies holding the line. I believed it. I *taught* it, in my last years. I taught it to children."

"And?" Silas said.

"And it's backwards." He opened the ledger.

The pages were star-charts—but wrong, wrong in a way that took the eye a moment to catch. Constellations he knew were there, but mirrored, displaced, annotated in a hand he recognized as First Warden Corvane's: precise, beautiful, and utterly without mercy. Around each chart ran columns of figures, and the figures were not measurements of stars.

"They're counts," Silas said. She had come around the table without him noticing; she read upside-down fluently, the way smugglers and cartographers learn to. "Population counts."

"Census rolls," Ede said. "Every soul in the kingdom of Vael. Recorded, weighed, and—" he turned the page, and the sweet smell of the archive thickened, "—allocated."

The next folio was a map. Not of Vael. Of the space *where Vael had been*—a blank rectangle drawn with loving care, its borders ruled in red, and across the emptiness, in Corvane's hand, the founding sentence of everything Ede had ever served:

*Here we will build a country that cannot be accused of stealing anything, because there will be nothing left to accuse us.*

Below it, smaller, almost tender:

*Unmap them. Fold them into the Choir. Let the sky forget them last.*

Ede heard Silas set something down—her hand braced on the table edge—and when he glanced up, her face had gone the color of the blank map.

"The Choir," she said. "The thing the child hums."

"The Hollow Choir." He said the words he'd only ever said in whispers, in confession-booths of his own construction, alone on mountainsides. "A century ago, the Order needed material. A country isn't just land—it's *agreement*. Thousands of minds agreeing on where the rivers run, where the roads go, what the stars are called. You can't forge that from nothing. So Corvane's circle found a kingdom that fit the shape they needed—Vael, rich in exactly the kind of settled, believing population a forgery requires—and they unmapped it. Not destroyed. *Erased.* Every person folded out of the world and into a reservoir of pure agreement. A choir with no bodies. And the false country—Thornfell, the lowlands, all of it—was written on top, using the stolen voices as its ink."

Pip had drifted close. She was looking at the ledger's final page, where the last entry stood alone:

*Sky allocation complete. The heavens themselves are warded construct. On completion, extinguish reference-stars. Nothing above may remain that remembers below.*

"The stars," Ede said. "There never were any stars. Not real ones. The whole sky is the lid on the jar. When the wards began failing, the sky failed first—that's why they went out. We aren't seeing darkness up there, Silas. We're seeing the truth. There's nothing between us and whatever is actually overhead, and the actual overhead has been waiting a hundred years to be noticed."

The lamps flickered. Or perhaps the room flinched.

* * *

Silas sat down slowly on a reading stool, the way people sit when the ground has become theoretical. Pip climbed up beside her without asking, the way children do, and leaned against her side. Silas let her. Ede watched that small surrender—the smuggler who trusted no one letting a feral, humming child lean on her—and felt something in his chest pull tight and painful.

"Why you?" Silas asked at last. "Why does a disgraced Warden know all this?"

"Because I found the ledger." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Eleven years ago. I was a rising man. They gave me archive duty as an honor, cataloguing the deep collection, and I found a misfiled folio—a census page with names on it. Real names. *Vaelish* names. And beside each name, a notation I'd been taught meant 'harmonized.' I asked my superior what it meant. She smiled at me the way you smile at a child asking where babies come from, and told me the deep collection was above my grade." He touched the ledger's clasp, the interlocking thorns. "I should have walked away. Instead I kept reading. And when they realized what I'd read, they didn't kill me. Killing creates questions. They *disgraced* me instead—forged evidence, staged a trial, stripped my rank and cast me out. A disgraced Warden's warnings sound like bitterness. Who believes a fallen man?"

"I did," Silas said. "Eventually."

"Yes. Well. You're a smuggler. You know forged goods when you see them." He closed the ledger, and his hands were shaking now, and he let them shake. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? My entire life—my vows, my discipline, every cold night I stood watch believing I was guarding something—I was a lock on a prison. Worse. I was a stitch in a theft. Every soul in that Choir has a name in these pages, and my order wrote those names down *so they could take them away properly*. Erasure requires excellent record-keeping. That's the joke at the bottom of everything. We were meticulous."

Pip reached out and put her small hand flat on the ledger's cover. Her humming started again—soft, and this time Ede could hear, threaded through it, fragments of melody that matched the census entries' spacing, as if the child were reading the roll call aloud in the only language the dead had left.

And then, horribly, wonderfully, the archive answered.

From the shelves—from the ledgers themselves, hundreds of them, thousands—a sound rose like wind through a canyon. Not voices. Not yet. But the pressure of voices, the way a held note presses against a door. Dust lifted off the shelves in fine bright spirals. The lamps bent. Somewhere in the walls, ward-plates that hadn't sung in a century began to resonate, each one a struck bell with no bell-ringer.

"They're here," Ede whispered. "The records are keyed to them. Every ledger is a cage, and the cages are—" He swallowed. "*Listening.*"

Silas was on her feet, one arm around Pip. "Can they hear us?"

"They've always been able to hear. That's how the forgery works—we speak, and the stolen voices hold our words in place. Roads stay where we remember them. Towns stand where we agree they stand." He looked up at the resonating shelves, at a century of meticulous crime singing faintly in the dark, and felt the guilt in him turn, at last, into something with an edge. Resolve, maybe. Or just the end of cowardice. "But the wards are broken now. The sky is off. And they're not holding our world up anymore, Silas—they're pushing. Asking. Demandering their names back."

"Demanding," Silas corrected softly.

"Demanding," he agreed.

Pip tugged his sleeve. When he knelt to her level, she pointed one grimy finger at the far wall—at a section of shelving he had never been permitted to approach, the Restricted Vault, sealed behind a door of black iron with no visible keyhole. Above it, carved into the stone in letters meant to be read only by men who already knew what they'd done:

*THE CHOIR'S HEART. DO NOT WAKE.*

The iron door was glowing. Faintly, from within, like a banked fire seen through a stove's seams. And beneath the glow, in the space of a single breath, every lamp in the archive went out—

and in the dark, very clearly, a voice that was not Pip's and not Ede's and not Silas's spoke the first word anyone in that room had heard from the erased kingdom in a hundred years.

"*Marrow,*" it said. "We know your name. Bring us ours."
