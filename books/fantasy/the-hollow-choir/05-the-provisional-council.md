## 5. The Provisional Council

They took her boots first.

Silas noticed that before anything else—the small indignity of it, standing barefoot on cold flagstones while a soldier with careful hands set her boots aside like evidence. No laces to hang herself with, no heel-iron to pry a window latch. Someone in the Provisional Council had thought about smugglers before. Someone had been a smuggler.

The cell they'd held her in for two days had no door she could find, only a seam in the wall that opened when the guards willed it. Ward-work. She'd pressed her palm against that seam until her skin went numb and felt nothing—not the faint hum of Thornfell's old wards that every mountain child learned to read through their teeth, but something newer, thinner, a sound like a struck glass still ringing.

Now they were walking her down a corridor that was too long for the building it sat inside. She could feel that too. The corridor's proportions were wrong the way her maps had gone wrong—true on paper, false underfoot—and she kept her eyes on the back of the guard's neck and counted her steps so the wrongness couldn't settle into her bones.

*Forty-one steps. A corridor this long should have turned twice.*

It didn't turn at all. It opened into light.

* * *

The Council chamber had been a granary once. Silas knew granaries the way priests knew psalters; she'd slept in a dozen of them along the Thornfell roads, and she recognized the iron tie-rods overhead, the grain-chutes bricked shut, the smell of dust and old wheat soaked so deep into timber that no amount of scrubbing would lift it. They had hung banners over the worst of it—grey silk with the new seal, a mountain pierced by a road—and set a long table beneath them where five people sat behind candle-glass lamps.

Four of them were strangers. The fifth made her stomach drop.

"Master Quillon," she said.

"Miss Voss." Aldous Quillon folded his hands on the table. He had been the factor at Harrowgate who paid best and asked least, the man whose ledgers had laundered half her runs into honest commerce. He looked exactly as he always had: soft-jawed, precise, a man who wore grief like a coat he might need later. "You'll forgive the theater. We needed to be sure of you."

"Two days in a hole to be sure of me?"

"To be sure you weren't *changed*." He said it gently, which was worse. "People come out of the mountains different now. Some of them hum. Some of them remember things that never happened. You came out of your cell asking for your boots by name, complaining about the draft, and trying to pick my junior officer's pocket." A thin smile. "You're entirely yourself. It's a relief."

One of the strangers—a woman with a provost's chain and a face like a closed ledger—spoke without looking up from her papers. "Sit her down, Quillon. I have a schedule."

"There," Quillon said, gesturing to a chair placed facing the table, alone, like a witness box. "Please."

Silas sat. She did not look at the chair legs to see if they'd been nailed to the floor. She already knew they hadn't; they wanted her mobile, cooperative, *willing*. That told her more than the last two days had.

"You've seen what's happening," Quillon said. "Of course you have. You were in the high passes when the stars went out—we know because your route crossed the Cindermark on the third night, and there is no one else it could have been. Don't trouble to deny it. Your routes are the only ones still coherent. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Every surveyor's map in the country has gone soft. Every military chart redraws itself by morning. But yours—" he tapped the table, and a guard laid her own satchel upon it, her oilskin rolls spilling out, "—yours hold."

She looked at her maps spread under lamplight, and something in her chest went tight and strange. She had drawn those lines over eleven years, in rain, in fever, in the blue hour before dawn when the mountains showed their true bones. She had drawn them to be invisible—to be a woman no one needed to remember—and now five strangers leaned over them like pilgrims over relics.

"The towns are sliding," the provost said flatly. "Greywater was forty miles east of here three weeks ago. Yesterday it was twelve. My garrison reports roads that end in fields that weren't there. The passes close and open like mouths. And underneath all of it—" she paused, and for the first time looked up, and Silas saw fear in her that no battlefield had put there, "—there is singing. My soldiers hear it in their sleep. Two of them walked into the fells last week following it. We found their coats. Nothing else."

"What do you want from me?" Silas said.

Quillon slid a document across the table. Heavy paper, wax the color of dried blood, the new seal stamped deep. "A road," he said. "There is one route left that doesn't shift—one thread of stable ground running west through the Thornfells to the coast. Our surveyors can't find its beginning. But you can. You've walked it. You've walked everything." His voice softened into the register he'd used across her ledger-tables for years, the voice that said *this is simply business*. "Map it for us. Fully. Waystations, water, chokepoints, timings. In exchange—"

He turned the document so she could read it.

Her own name. *Silas Voss.* Not the false names, not the trade-names stitched through her smuggling years like beads on a wire—her actual name, written in a clerk's fine hand, followed by words she had trained herself never to want. *Full pardon. Commission of Roads and Passes, Provisional Council of Thornfell. Estate grant, Harrowgate district. Protection extended to known associates.*

Protection extended to known associates. Her mind went, traitorously, to Ede Marrow in his exile hollow with his forbidden archive, and to Pip—Pip, whom the Council's soldiers shot on sight if they caught her humming.

"You'd be legitimate," Quillon said quietly. "No more looking over your shoulder. No more sleeping in granaries. Your name in the record, Miss Voss. A life anyone could see." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and that was how she knew he believed it. "Everything you pretended not to want."

* * *

They gave her an hour alone with the document, which was either courtesy or a trap, and she spent it pacing the granary floor in her stockinged feet, learning the trap's shape.

Because it *was* shaped like everything she'd ever wanted. That was the horror of it. Eleven years she had lived as a smudge—a woman whose name appeared on no roll, whose face no factor could describe twice the same way—and she had told herself it was craft, discipline, freedom. But freedom, she was discovering, smelled like a cold cell and felt like bare feet on flagstones, and the document on the table smelled like warm wax and promised her a door with her name carved above it.

*Duty,* she thought, sitting down hard. *They've dressed it up as duty.*

And duty had always been the thing she was weakest to. Not gold—she'd left gold on tables when the job smelled wrong. Not threats—threats she catalogued and answered in kind. But the moment someone said *we need you, only you, no one else can do this*, something in her chest stood up straight like a soldier. It was why she'd carried messages through blizzards for people who wouldn't remember her name. It was why she'd mapped a country that never intended to see her.

They had studied her. Whoever built this offer had studied her the way she studied passes.

She picked up the document. The wax was still faintly tacky. Fresh. Which meant the offer was fresh, which meant—

Which meant they were leaving soon. Councils did not commission escape roads unless they intended to use them.

She unrolled her maps and looked at them properly for the first time since capture, and the cold went through her like meltwater. Because she knew the western route. Of course she knew it—she'd run it six times, the deep road through the Cindermark, the one that stayed true while everything around it shifted. And she knew what lay at its heart now, because Ede had shown her on his terrible charts, and because Pip's humming grew thick and sweet whenever they'd come within a day's walk of it.

The last stable road ran straight through the sunken capital of Vael. Through the place where the erased kingdom lay folded under the mountains like a letter never delivered. Through the Choir's heart.

They weren't asking her to map an escape. They were asking her to lead five hundred officials and their soldiers, wagons and gold and guns, directly into the throat of everything rising beneath the country—and either the Choir would take them, or the collision would tear what remained of Thornfell apart like wet paper. Perhaps both. Perhaps both was the plan; perhaps some cold mind at this table had decided that if the country was dying anyway, it should die useful.

Or perhaps they genuinely didn't know. Perhaps they were only frightened people buying a road from the only mapmaker left, blind to what waited at the end of it.

She honestly couldn't decide which was worse.

* * *

When they came back for her answer, the chamber had changed. More guards along the walls, and among them—her breath caught—two soldiers dragging a boy of maybe fifteen between them, his wrists bound, his lips moving silently, endlessly, shaping sounds no one could hear. One of the hummers. They'd brought him to show her what happened to people who remembered.

Quillon watched her see the boy. Watched her hands close.

"Miss Voss," he said. "Time presses. The road, or the cell. There is no third door."

And Silas Voss—who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being unreadable, who had lied to wardens and factors and border-magistrates with a face like still water—looked at the document with her name on it, and let something warm and hungry rise into her expression, and heard herself say:

"I'll need my boots."

The provost exhaled. Quillon's smile bloomed. Somewhere behind the candle-glass lamps, pens began to move, writing her into the record, making her real, making her *theirs*.

She flexed her ink-stained fingers under the table and thought about a child's humming, steady as a heartbeat, somewhere out in the folding hills.

*I'm coming,* she thought. *I just have to lie beautifully first.*

They brought her boots. They even laced them for her.
