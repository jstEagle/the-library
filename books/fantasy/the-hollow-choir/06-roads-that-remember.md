## 6. Roads That Remember

The Council's courier left at dawn with the sealed timetable, and Silas watched him go from the window of a room she'd been given—*given*, not rented, as though she were a person who deserved rooms—and felt the trap close around her heart like a well-oiled snare.

They had been generous. That was the worst of it. A commission with her name inked in gold leaf, a stipend, a promise of protection for "the child, once she is recovered." They had spoken of Pip like a parcel to be reclaimed, and Silas had smiled the smile she'd spent thirty years perfecting, the one that said *I am no trouble to anyone*, and she had said yes.

And then she had gone upstairs and packed in the dark, because she knew what the last stable road was. She'd mapped it herself, years ago, back when she thought maps were innocent things. It ran through the Vale of Ashen Bells, straight through the place where the ground now hummed at night, straight through the Choir's gathering heart. The Council didn't need a road out. They needed someone expendable walking ahead of them into the dark, holding a lantern, so they could see what ate her.

She left the commission on the table, weighted under the water pitcher where it would be found too late.

Ede was waiting in the stable yard with two mules and an expression like a man chewing on his own guilt. Pip sat on the wall above him, cross-legged, humming something that made the frost on the cobblestones rearrange itself into spirals.

"You're certain," Ede said. Not a question, really. A last chance for both of them to lie.

"The roads east of here aren't roads anymore," Silas said, tightening a girth strap. "They're memories. I know which ones still hold weight." She swung up onto the mule. "I've smuggled enough lies across Thornfell. Time I carried something true."

Pip stopped humming long enough to say, "You smell like leaving," and grinned, gap-toothed, and Silas's chest did something complicated and unwelcome that she chose not to examine.

They rode north before the sun cleared the ridge.

* * *

The old smuggler roads were not drawn on any map Silas had ever sold or stolen. They lived in the body instead—in the count of switchbacks, in the way a certain scree slope rang hollow underfoot, in the smell of juniper that meant water within the hour. For three days they traveled by Silas's memory alone, and the land seemed to lean in and listen.

On the fourth day, the road began to answer back.

It happened at a ford she'd crossed perhaps forty times. The river was wrong—too wide, running silver and slow as poured mercury—but when she spoke aloud, *Ford of the Nine Stones, knee-deep in autumn, ice at the edges by first frost*, the water shivered and drew back, and stones rose beneath the surface in a line, nine of them, exactly as remembered.

"Careful," Ede said quietly. He had gone pale. "That's ward-craft. Old craft. The land isn't obeying you, Silas. It's *spending* you."

"Spending me how?"

He didn't answer, but Pip looked at her with those too-old eyes and hummed a low, warning note, and Silas understood anyway. Every mile bought with memory was memory paid. The road was honest commerce. It simply never posted its prices until after.

She decided not to tell them that. There were things she could afford to lose. Everyone had gravel in them—old grievances, dead-end years, faces of people who'd cheated her fair and square. She would pay in gravel. She would keep the gold.

For six more miles, she managed it. She paid with the name of the man who'd taught her to pick locks (she could no longer remember why she'd hated him, only that hating him had mattered once). She paid with the taste of the first meal she'd ever eaten alone, free and unafraid, in a tavern in Lowmark. She paid with a whole gray year she couldn't account for anyway, somewhere in her twenties, and felt lighter afterward, and hated herself a little for feeling lighter.

Pip walked beside her mule and hummed, always humming, fragments of melody that knitted the air together behind them. Where the child sang, the road held its shape longer. Where she fell silent—to eat, to sleep—the world went soft at the edges, hills breathing like sleeping animals, distances lying about themselves.

"Where did you learn those songs?" Silas asked her on the seventh evening, watching the girl turn a beetle over with one finger, gentle as a surgeon.

"They're not learned," Pip said. "They're owed." And she went back to humming, and the fire burned steadier for it.

* * *

The pass called the Shepherd's Tooth was where the price came due in earnest.

Silas knew it as a day's climb through a notch in the ridge, narrow enough that you could touch both walls, with a shrine to some forgotten shepherd-saint halfway up where travelers left buttons and ribbon. She began the climb reciting it: the notch, the walls, the shrine, the wind that funneled through singing a low minor note—

Halfway up, she realized she couldn't remember her mother's face.

Not the name—*Maren Voss*, that held, carved deep. But the face above the name was gone, scooped clean, and in its place was only a warm shape, a presence at a stove, hands kneading bread, and even the hands were fading now, going soft-focus, becoming *someone*, then *anyone*, then—

Silas sat down on the mountain path and put her head in her hands.

She hadn't chosen to pay with that. That was the thing no one told you about debts: the collector decides. She had spent a lockpick teacher's grudge and a gray year and a stranger's taste of freedom, small coins, and the road had reached into her purse and taken the one thing she'd have fought armies to keep.

"She had flour on her," Silas said aloud, to no one, testing. "She always had flour on her hands. She'd wipe them on her apron and then push my hair back and leave white streaks in it, and I'd pretend to be annoyed."

The words were true. She knew they were true the way you know a story is true. But there was no face attached anymore. There was flour, and warmth, and a voice saying something kind that she could no longer hear, and a shape of love so large that everything else about the woman had been worn away paying for nine stones in a river.

Ede crouched beside her. He didn't touch her; he'd learned that much about her by now. "We can go around," he said. "Add four days. Risk the lowlands, the patrols."

"And spend what else?" Her voice came out flat. "No. We keep the schedule I stole from the Council. Every hour we don't use, they gain." She stood. Her knees ached. Grief, she discovered, lived in the knees. "Keep walking."

Pip took her hand.

The child's palm was small and rough and warm, and she didn't say anything, just laced her fingers through Silas's and started humming—a new fragment this time, slower, in a mode that made the wind in the notch soften to match it. And for a moment—not a replacement, nothing could replace it—Silas felt something settle over the absence like snow filling a footprint. Not her mother. But *held*. As if somewhere, in whatever vast throat the Hollow Choir had become, a woman named Maren Voss was being sung, carefully, note by note, kept safe until her daughter could come and collect her.

"She's in there," Pip said quietly, without stopping the song. "The ones you lose. They don't go nowhere. They go *in*."

Silas walked on, weeping without sound, and let the road take the next mile out of her, and the next, because Pip was right, because some things were worth forgetting if the forgetting meant they were kept somewhere truer than a skull.

* * *

They came down off the Shepherd's Tooth at dusk on the ninth day, and the world below had stopped pretending.

The valley of the Ashen Bells opened before them, and it was full of light—not sunlight, not lamplight, but a pale luminous rising out of the ground itself, as if the earth had become a lamp with the flame turned very low. Thousands of faint figures stood in the fields and along the ruined road, motionless, faceless, arranged in rows that followed no geometry Silas recognized. And the humming Pip carried all this time—it was coming from them too, from everywhere, a chord sustained so long it had become part of the air.

At the valley's center, sunk into the earth like a coin pressed halfway into wax, stood a city.

Towers. Spires. Walls of white stone veined with silver. A capital, drowned to the rooftops, glowing from within.

Ede made a sound beside her like a man taking a wound. "Vael," he whispered. "That's Vael. That's the sunken city. It's *real*, it was real the whole time, under us, under everything—"

Pip let go of Silas's hand and walked forward, down toward the fields of waiting voices, and the Choir's hum shifted, gathered, resolved into something almost like words, almost like a welcome.

And far behind them, back along the road they'd paid for mile by mile, Silas heard the thing she'd been dreading since the stable yard: hoofbeats. Many. The Council had found the empty table, the abandoned timetable, the trail of a road that remembered being ridden.

They were coming. And the only path left ran straight down into the Choir's open mouth.
