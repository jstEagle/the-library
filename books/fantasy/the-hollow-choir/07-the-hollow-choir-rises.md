## 7. The Hollow Choir Rises

Ede Marrow had spent forty years learning the grammar of wards—the way a line of chalk could hold back hunger, the way a spoken syllable could stitch a wound in the world—and he had believed, until this morning, that he understood what power looked like. Power was precise. Power was small and careful and paid for in increments. He had built his whole life on that belief, and then he had walked down into the sunken capital of Vael and watched precision die.

The road down had been Silas's doing. Of course it had. She had led them along a smuggler's track that no honest map admitted to, a switchbacking descent through pine and fog where the trees grew wrong—too tall, too quiet, their needles pointing not up but *inward*, toward something below. Pip rode on Ede's shoulders for the last mile, humming her broken fragments of melody, and with every bar she hummed the air grew heavier, as though they were wading into deep water that had learned to grieve.

Then the trees ended, and there was Vael.

It lay in a bowl of land like a drowned city lying at the bottom of a dry sea. Towers rose from the mist that pooled in the basin, but the towers were translucent, the way a thing is translucent when it is only half-remembered. Streets spiraled outward from a central spire—a cathedral, or what had been a cathedral, its bell tower snapped off clean as a finger. And everywhere, everywhere, the light moved strangely, sliding across surfaces without ever quite touching them, because these were not surfaces. They were absences wearing the shape of surfaces.

"Stars went out," Ede said quietly, "because the sky was part of the theft." He had said it before, in the archive, reading his order's own confession in a hand that shook. Saying it again did not make it easier. "This is what they took."

Silas stood beside him with her map case clutched to her chest like a shield. Her maps had been redrawing themselves for days; now, Ede saw, she wasn't even looking at them. She was looking down at the city with an expression he recognized from his own mirror: the face of someone watching the floor of their life open.

"It's beautiful," Pip said.

And it was. That was the horror of it.

* * *

They descended into the outskirts by noon—or by what passed for noon under a starless sky that gave no light of its own, only a gray diffusion like light seen through closed eyelids. The streets of Vael remembered being streets. Ede felt them remember him, felt the cobbles shift faintly beneath his boots, testing his weight, asking him wordless questions: *Are you one who was taken? Are you one who took?*

He could not answer. That was the worst part. He had kept the archive's secrets for thirty years, had burned the pages he couldn't bear to keep, and now the city wanted a confession and he had nothing to offer but the truth: *I didn't do it, but I ate from its table every day of my life.*

Pip stopped humming.

Ede noticed the silence the way you notice a missing heartbeat. The child slid off his shoulders and stood very still in the middle of a boulevard lined with half-real linden trees, her head tilted, listening.

"Pip?" Silas said.

"They're waking up," Pip said. Her voice was different—older, layered, as if two throats spoke at once. "All of them. All at once. They can feel us walking on them."

The ground shivered.

Not like an earthquake. Earthquakes are blind. This shiver had *attention* in it. It passed under their feet and out through the city in rings, and wherever it went, the mist began to rise—not drift, *rise*, climbing the walls of the hollow like water reversing, and inside the mist there were lights, and inside the lights—

Faces. No. Not faces. The *idea* of faces, worn like borrowed coats by things that had given theirs away a century ago. They came up out of the mist in their hundreds, in their thousands, drifting between the translucent towers, and none of them had eyes, and all of them were looking.

Ede fell to his knees. He would be ashamed of that later, if there was a later; in the moment it seemed the only rational response to standing inside a sound.

Because that was what they were, finally. Not ghosts, not figures. A sound. A chord so vast it had grown bodies out of the air to hold itself, thousands of voices braided into one sustained note that was not sung so much as *insisted*, a note that said, in a language older than words:

*We were here.*

*We had names.*

*Give them back.*

Silas had her knife out, which was absurd and human and Ede loved her a little for it. Pip walked forward into the choir with her arms spread wide like a child greeting rain, and the voices parted around her, tender as water around a stone.

"Don't—" Ede started, and then the note changed key, and every thought he had was blown away like chaff.

* * *

Here is what Ede Marrow knew about ward-craft, and here is why he reached for it anyway, kneeling in the street of a murdered city with the Hollow Choir rising around him like floodwater:

Wards are bargains. Every ward is a contract written in force: *you may not pass, and in exchange I will hold this line until my strength fails.* His order had taught him that the first law of warding was symmetry—that nothing could be held which was not also offered. You want a door sealed? Give the door something to seal against. You want a mind protected? Offer it a shape worth protecting.

And the Choir, he realized, with the terrible clarity of a man watching his own mistake approach in slow motion, was the greatest ward ever raised. Not a wall around a country. A wall *made of* a country. A hundred thousand people folded into a single binding, their names taken as the offering, their voices as the lock. The founders hadn't killed Vael. They had *used* it. Had turned an entire nation into the mechanism of its own imprisonment.

Which meant the Choir was still, at its heart, a ward. And wards could be negotiated with.

"Ede," Silas said, hauling at his arm. "Ede, get up, we have to move—"

"No." He heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him. "No, wait. Listen to me. It's asking for names. It's not attacking—it's *asking*. It's been asking for a hundred years and nobody answered, because answering means admitting what we did." He got his feet under him. His hands were shaking. He let them shake. "I'm a ward-keeper. Broken, disgraced, exiled—but I know how to talk to a binding. Let me try."

"You tried bargaining with the Council," Silas said. "Look how that went."

"The Council was men." He stepped forward, toward the wall of light and voice. "This is a wound. Wounds want to heal. They just need someone to stop pretending they're not wounds."

He opened the satchel at his hip and took out the tools of his disgrace: chalk of grave-dust and ash, a stylus of black iron, the little book of counter-signs that had gotten him cast out of the Order of the Veil twenty years ago. Forbidden craft. Craft that worked *with* bindings instead of against them. He had sworn never to use it again, and he had kept that oath right up until keeping it became another way of helping the theft continue.

He knelt in the street and began to draw.

The circle came first, then the spokes, then the interlocking names of the four cardinal bindings—except he left the names blank, drew empty brackets where names should go, because that was the whole point, that was the confession made visible: *here is where your names should be, and here is where we wrote our own instead.*

The Choir noticed.

The great note bent toward him like a head inclining. The mist-thronged figures drifted closer, close enough that Ede could see they weren't mist at all but memory given texture—hands mid-gesture, mouths mid-word, a woman holding a child who was also a woman holding a child, generations folded into each other like paper. He felt their attention settle on him the way snow settles on a roof: gently, and with crushing weight.

"I can't give you your names," he said aloud. His voice sounded tiny, obscene, a mouse squeaking in a cathedral. "They're not mine to give. They were taken wrongly, and I don't know where they were put. But I know how the taking was done. I know the shape of the lock." He lifted the chalk. "Let me show you the shape of the lock, and you tell me if I'm lying."

He wrote the counter-sign of the Veil—the true one, the one from the burned pages, the founding signature that his order had spent a century denying existed. It blazed up from the cobblestones in cold white fire, and the Choir *screamed*.

Not with anger. With recognition. A hundred thousand voices hitting one note of recognition, and the note cracked windows in towers that weren't fully real, and split the gray sky overhead like old plaster, and behind the crack—for one impossible second—Ede saw stars.

Then the Choir turned its whole vast attention on him, and began to unmake him.

* * *

Later, he would struggle to describe it. The nearest he ever came was this: imagine every fact about yourself being read aloud by a stranger, quickly, in a bored voice, and struck from the record as it's read. Your name—gone. Your mother's kitchen—gone. The smell of chalk dust, the weight of the stylus, the reason your left knee ached in rain—all of it read and erased, read and erased, and underneath the erasure a question, patient and enormous:

*What are you, if we take everything? What's left? Is anything left?*

He felt himself thinning. He felt the street beginning to show through him, translucent as the towers. He thought, distantly, with a scholar's last flicker of interest: *so that's what it's like. That's what they did to a whole country, one person at a time, and called it peace.*

Something caught his hand.

Small fingers. Ink-stained, always ink-stained, the child never stopped drawing. Pip stood in the failing circle of his own chalk with her other hand pressed flat against the largest of the faceless figures, and she was singing—not the broken fragments anymore but a whole line, a long unbroken line of melody, and the Choir faltered around her like a wave meeting a breakwater.

"He's telling the truth," Pip said, in the doubled voice, in the old voice, in whatever voice she was when she wasn't only herself. "He's the first one in a hundred years who's told the truth. Don't eat him. We need him. We need all three."

The erasure stopped. Ede collapsed forward onto his hands, gasping, feeling himself re-condense—name returning first, slamming back into him like a door in a storm, then the knee, then the chalk-smell, then everything, everything, and he knelt there weeping over his own continued existence like a man pulled from drowning.

Above them, the crack in the sky widened. Through it, faint and impossibly far away, stars.

And the Choir spoke—not in the vast chord now, but in a single voice, one voice lifted out of the many, an old woman's voice, tired and clear, addressing them directly for the first time:

*"You've seen the lock. Now find us the key. My child has been walking among you, and she remembers more than she knows. Ask her what she is. Ask her what she was made for. And hurry, mapmaker—"*

The land above them groaned. Far up the valley, Ede saw the ridge line fold—actually fold, like cloth taken up in a hand—as Thornfell began, at last, to collapse into the hollow of what it had buried.

*"—because we are done waiting, and the sky is coming down."*
