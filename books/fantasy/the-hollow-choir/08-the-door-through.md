## 8. The Door Through

Pip had never told anyone the whole of it, because the whole of it was too big for her mouth. Words came out of her small, like pebbles, and the truth was a mountain. But tonight the mountain was coming down anyway, folding into the dark under Thornfell, and so she sat on the broken step of the sunken gatehouse with her knees pulled up and decided to let it out all at once.

Ede was pacing. Ede was always pacing when he was frightened, his ward-keeper's coat flapping around him like a crow trying to decide whether to land. Silas stood at the edge of the terrace where the stone dropped away into nothing—into the singing nothing—and her hand was pressed flat against her chest as if she could hold her own heart still by force.

They were both talking over each other.

"There has to be a way out," Ede said. "Every ward has an egress. Every seal has a relief valve. I built half my career on that principle, there is always—"

"Out isn't a direction anymore." Silas's voice was flat and careful, the voice she used on bad roads. "I've been watching the edges. The country isn't shrinking, it's *folding*. There's no edge to run to. The map is eating itself from the outside in."

"And the Choir won't stop," Ede said. "It can't. It doesn't hate us, that's the terrible part—it just wants what it's owed, and we are standing on the debt."

Pip hummed three notes.

They stopped. They always stopped when she hummed, because they'd learned that her humming meant something, though they thought it meant less than it did. They thought she hummed fragments. She didn't hum fragments. She hummed the parts she could bear.

"I know where the door is," Pip said.

Silas turned. In the starless dark her face was all shadow and one pale line of jaw, but Pip could feel her looking, the way you feel sunlight through a wall.

"You've said that before," Silas said gently. "The humming paths. The roads that aren't there."

"Not those doors." Pip picked at a loose thread on her sleeve, the red thread she'd carried since the village with no name. "Those were little doors. Mouse doors. I mean the door. The real one. The first one."

Ede crouched down in front of her, which he did when he wanted her to talk, making himself small so she wouldn't have to look up. His eyes were wet-rimmed and steady. "Tell us, little bird. All of it this time."

Pip took a breath that went all the way down to her boots.

* * *

She told them about the beginning, or the part of the beginning she could carry.

"I was the first one they took." She said it plainly, because plainness was the only way it fit through her teeth. "Before the wards went up, before the stars were stolen, before any of it. The ward-makers needed a keyhole. You can't fold a kingdom out of the world without somewhere to put the fold, and the fold has to be held by something living, or it springs open. So they took a child. A girl who sang. They cut her name out first—that's how you make a key, you hollow it—and then they folded her into the middle of the working and left her there."

She watched their faces do what faces did when she said true things: Silas went very still, stiller than stone; Ede's hands curled up like dying leaves.

"A hundred years," Ede whispered. "You've been—"

"Asleep. Singing. Both." Pip shrugged. "The Choir grew out of me. Every person they erased got folded into the song, and the song got folded into me, and I got folded into the ground under Vael. That's why I can walk roads that don't exist. They exist *in me*. That's why I hum. I'm not remembering the Choir." She looked down at her own small hands, ink-stained now from copying Ede's charts, dirt-stained from every mile. "The Choir is remembering itself through me. I'm the part of it that got legs."

Silas knelt beside Ede then, two grown people crouched low in front of a child on a broken step above a falling country, and her voice came out rough. "Then when the Choir rises—if it takes everything back—what happens to you?"

"That's the door part." Pip hugged her knees tighter. "There's no door out. I've looked. I've walked every mouse-door and every smuggler road and every crack in the world, and there's nowhere *out*, because out was never part of the design. The ward-makers only built one opening. One door. And it doesn't lead away." She lifted her chin and met Silas's eyes, because this was the important part and important parts deserved eye contact, Ede had taught her that. "It leads *through*. Back to before. The door opens onto Vael the way it was supposed to be—the true country, un-folded, restored. Everything the theft swallowed comes back through it. The people. The names. The stars."

"The sky," Silas breathed. "The sky was part of the theft."

"The sky was the lid." Pip nodded. "And here's the thing nobody knows except me, because I'm the keyhole and the key both: the door is already unlocked. It's been unlocked for a hundred years. It's just too heavy for the Choir to open alone, because a door needs an anchor on this side. Something of the false world, freely given, to hold the frame while everything passes through. A name. Given on purpose. Not taken, not stolen, not lost—*given*." She spread her hands. "Nobody in a hundred years ever gave anything freely. Everyone clung. Even the ones who loved this fake country, even the ones who hated it—they all held on. Holding shuts the door."

The wind off the singing dark moved through the ruined gatehouse, and far below them the Hollow Choir rose and fell like breathing, thousands of voices with no faces, patient as water.

"So it waits," Ede said hoarsely. "It's been waiting for someone to give—"

"It doesn't wait mean," Pip said. "Waiting is just what doors do. But yes. It's waiting."

* * *

Afterward they made a fire in the gatehouse hearth, though the wood burned strange and slow, giving its light grudgingly, as if even fire wasn't sure it existed anymore. Pip sat close to the flames and watched Silas sharpen her knife without needing to, stroke after stroke on a blade already keen, the way she did when thinking hurt.

"You knew," Silas said finally, not looking up. "When you found me at the waystation. Or soon after. You knew what I was for."

Pip considered lying, which she was bad at, and decided against it. "I knew you remembered things," she said. "That's rare now. Most people's memories got soft when the wards broke, but yours got *harder*. Like your whole life was practice for being given." She tilted her head. "I didn't know it would be you specifically. But I hoped."

"Hoped." Silas set the knife down. Her laugh had no floor under it. "A child hoped I'd sacrifice myself. That's—" She stopped. Started again. "Pip, I've spent my whole life making sure I mattered to no one. No debts, no ties, no name on any list. And now you're telling me the only thing I'm good for is the one thing I can't take back."

"You're good for lots of things," Pip said seriously. "You're good at maps and being quiet and sharing food even when you're hungry. But yes. Mostly that."

Ede made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. He sat down heavily across the fire, an old man suddenly, all his ward-keeper's certainty burned down to ash. "There has to be another anchor. A name is a name—mine would serve as well as hers. Mine should serve better. I'm the one whose order built the cage. Let the jailer's name pay for the jail."

"No," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"It has to be *freely* given, and it has to be *whole*, and yours isn't." She said it gently, because she liked Ede, but truth was truth. "Your name is full of guilt and orders and the Archive and every wrong thing the ward-keepers did. If you give it, the door opens crooked. I felt it when you tried to bargain with the voices—you remember. It nearly unmade you because the Choir couldn't tell where your sorry ended and your self began." She hugged her knees. "Silas's name is clean. Not good—clean. She kept it locked away her whole life. Nobody used it, nobody spent it, nobody bent it. It's the last whole name in the false country. That's not an accident. I think maybe she kept it her whole life *for* this, even though she didn't know."

Silas stared into the fire. When she spoke, her voice was the road-voice again, flat and careful, and Pip hated the road-voice because it meant Silas was carrying something too heavy to show. "And if I don't? If I refuse?"

"Then the Choir keeps rising and the country keeps folding until there's nothing left to fold, and everyone inside goes into the song without their names, forever, and Vael stays buried, and the sky stays empty." Pip shrugged. "Or somebody else gives a name eventually. Maybe in another hundred years. Maybe a worse name. Doors are patient."

"And if I do?" Silas asked. "What happens to—to the part of me that gives?"

Pip looked at her for a long time. Under her skin, faint as veins in marble, she could feel the Choir listening—not demanding, just listening, the way it always listened through her, and she let it listen, because Silas deserved an answer heard by everyone she belonged to.

"You go into the song," Pip said. "Like everyone they took. Your name goes to the door and the rest of you goes to the music." She paused, and then gave her the other half, the half that mattered, the half that was hers alone to give: "But songs aren't prisons. Songs are *carried*. If someone loves the song enough—if someone learns your melody and sings it back, note for note, meaning it—then the song gives back. That's the secret even the ward-makers never learned, because none of them ever loved anybody they stole. Nothing taken can be sung back. Only things *given*. Only things loved."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, the Choir swelled, and for the first time Pip heard a new thread in it—not demand, not grief. Something almost like hope, thin and high, a melody with room in it for one more voice.

Silas looked at her across the flames, and her face did something complicated, fear and wonder fighting over the same ground, and Pip understood that the grown-up was finally seeing it clearly: not a trap, not a duty, not a debt. A door. Just a door, and love on the other side of it.

"When?" Silas asked quietly.

Pip stood up. Below the broken terrace, in the deep of the folding dark, the sunken capital of Vael was glowing now along its buried streets, and at its heart she could feel the door the way you feel a tooth—present, aching, ready.

"Now," Pip said. "While the country's still soft enough to open. Come on. I'll walk you there." She held out her hand, small and certain, and after a moment Silas took it, and Ede rose too, and the three of them started down together into the singing dark—

—and from the ridge behind them came the clash of iron and torchlight, the Council's soldiers pouring over the hills, come too late to stop them and determined to try.
