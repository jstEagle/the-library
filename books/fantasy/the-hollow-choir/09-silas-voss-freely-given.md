## 9. Silas Voss, Freely Given

*Silas*

The land was coming apart the way old plaster comes off a wall—in sheets, in curls, revealing nothing underneath but dark.

Silas stood at the edge of the sunken capital with Pip's hand locked in hers and watched the horizon fold. The mountains of Thornfell, which she had spent thirty years mapping ridge by ridge, were lifting at their roots like tapestries being taken down. Behind them, where the sky should have been, there was only the pale absence where stars had been, and now even that absence was thinning, going translucent, showing the seams.

"They're coming," Ede said. He had his back to her, arms raised, ward-craft guttering blue around his fingers like a fire drowning in its own smoke. "Council outriders. Two hundred, maybe more. And behind them—" he flinched as something vast moved through the ground beneath them, a tremor that had no earthquake in it, only intention— "behind them is everything they're running from."

"Then we're out of time." Silas turned to Pip. The girl was humming, low and constant, the melody she had carried since the village with no name—the melody that was not a song but a list, a roll call of ten thousand stolen voices. Her eyes were fixed on the door.

It stood in the middle of the flooded plaza of Vael, rising out of black water like a tooth: an archway of white stone that did not match any stone Silas had ever mapped, carved with names in a script that rearranged itself when she wasn't looking directly at it. Through it there was no other side. There was only light—not sunlight, not lamplight, but the particular gold of a thing remembered perfectly.

"The door needs a name," Pip said. She said it simply, the way children say terrible things, because they have not yet learned to be afraid of words. "Freely given. That's what the Choir told me. That's why I couldn't open it myself." She looked up at Silas, and her face did something complicated—hope and guilt fighting for the same ground. "I tried to give mine. But I don't *have* one. That's the whole point of me."

The soldiers crested the far ridge. Even from here Silas could hear them—boots on stone that was forgetting how to be stone, the creak of leather, someone shouting orders into wind that ate sound. She thought about the Council chamber, the warm lamplight, the commission scroll with her name written on it in a clerk's careful hand. *Legitimacy. Safety. Belonging.* All of it offered by people who lived inside a lie so large they had mistaken it for the world.

She had spent her whole life being invisible. Careful. Unnamed in every way that mattered, because a smuggler with a reputation was a smuggler with a grave. And here, at the end of everything, she understood that she had been practicing for this her entire life: how to give herself away without leaving anything behind.

"Ede," she said.

He didn't turn. "Whatever you're about to say, don't."

"Hold the threshold. When I go through, the land will try to take you both with it. The Council will try to take you first. You hold the door until the last name is sung."

"*Silas.*"

"That's the idea," she said, and smiled, and found that it didn't hurt. "Say it like you mean it. It'll be the last time anyone does."

* * *

*Ede*

He had failed his order, his craft, and his country, and he had made his peace with all three failures years ago in a cold house above the tree line. This failure was different. This one had ink-stained fingers and a way of looking at him as if maps were just another kind of promise, and he could not make his peace with it at all.

The first arrows came out of the smoke on the ridge, and he caught them—or rather, he caught the space around them, folding the threshold's old ward-craft into a shield that cost him something with every impact. Blood ran freely from his nose. He let it.

"Pip!" he shouted. "When she steps through—sing her *through*. Not just her name. Everything she is. Can you do that?"

Pip looked up at him with those too-old eyes. "I can sing anybody anywhere," she said. "That's what they made me for. Before they made me for nothing."

The soldiers came down the slope in a wave, and behind them came the unmaking—a rolling front of silence where hills used to be, advancing at the speed of forgetting. Ede planted his feet on the white stone of the arch and understood, finally, what the founders must have felt when they built the original wards: this is what it costs. This is what it has always cost. Someone stands at the door and pays.

Silas kissed Pip's forehead. Then she turned to Ede, and took his face in both hands, and he saw that she was crying without any grief in it at all.

"You were the first person who ever told me the truth," she said. "Even when it was ugly. Especially then."

"Silas Voss," he said, and his voice broke on it, gave it to her whole, the way you'd give water to someone dying of thirst. "Freely given."

"I know," she said. "I heard you the first time, thirty years ago, in a tavern in Lowmark. You just never asked me to stay."

Then she stepped backward off the edge of the world, into the arch, into the light, and Pip opened her mouth and began to sing.

* * *

*Silas*

Dissolving, it turned out, felt like being read aloud.

Her name went first—she felt it leave her the way a word leaves the tongue, still warm—and then everything the name had held came loose and rose: her mother's kitchen, the smell of tallow and wet wool; the first map she'd ever drawn, of the creek behind the house, wrong in six places; the taste of stolen apples; Ede's voice saying *you just never asked me to stay*, which she had carried for thirty years without knowing its weight until now, when she could finally set it down.

Around her, the Choir received her. Ten thousand voices, and none of them faceless anymore—they were faces now, thousands of them, turning toward her like flowers toward sun. They did not speak. They *sang*, and what they sang was her: every road she'd walked, every border she'd crossed, every night she'd slept alone under stars that were stolen goods. They sang her better than she had ever known herself.

And through it all, small and clear and impossibly steady, Pip's voice, singing her *through*.

On the far side of the arch, something was waiting. Not a place. A beginning.

* * *

*Ede*

The false Thornfell peeled away like old paint.

Ede watched it happen while holding the threshold with the last of his strength, blood sheeting from his nose, knees buckling, refusing to fall. The mountains lifted off. The sky peeled back. Beneath it all, revealed at last after a hundred years of theft, lay the true country: green valleys that had never been mapped because they had never needed to be, rivers running silver under returned stars, and the city of Vael rising out of its own foundations, whole, washed, breathing.

The soldiers stopped at the ridgeline. Some of them fell to their knees. One man tore the Council badge from his coat and threw it into the water, and the water accepted it without comment, the way it accepts everything eventually.

In the arch, the light dimmed to something gentler. Pip walked out of it alone, and she was changed—taller somehow, or standing taller, a girl with a history instead of a hollow. She had tears on her face and a melody in her throat that she was still humming, softly, like a lullaby she refused to finish.

"She's in the Choir," Pip said. "But Ede—Ede, listen—it's not the end. It's never been the end. The Choir isn't a grave. It's a *chorus*. And choruses—"

She stopped. Her head tilted, listening to something over the hills, something on the wind.

And Ede heard it too.

Coming across the restored valleys of Vael, threading through the dawn chorus of ten thousand waking voices, was a single melody he knew. A tavern tune from Lowmark, thirty years old, slightly out of tune in the same places it had always been out of tune, sung by a woman who had once told him that maps were just another kind of promise.

The stars were back. The door stood open. And somewhere in the new morning, a voice that the whole world had just learned to sing again was working its way toward a house above the tree line, toward a disgraced keeper and a child with a history, singing a song that meant, in the oldest language there was:

*I'm coming home.*

By nightfall, there would be ink-stained fingers knocking at his door.
