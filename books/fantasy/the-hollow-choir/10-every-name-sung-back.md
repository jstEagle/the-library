## 10. Every Name Sung Back

Ede Marrow had spent forty years learning how to hold a ward, and in the end it was the simplest thing he'd ever done: he stood at the threshold of the door through, hands open, and held nothing but the space where Silas Voss had been.

The false country peeled away like old paint. He felt it go—felt Thornfell's borrowed mountains slough off the true bones beneath, felt a century of stolen sky lift like a lid. And under all of it, rising, the sound.

Not a sound, really. A remembering.

He knelt on the stones of the sunken capital with Pip pressed against his side, her small hand fisted in his coat, and watched Vael come back the way dawn comes back—not all at once, but in bands of light climbing the walls. Towers he had only seen as drowned silhouettes in Ede's forbidden maps now stood whole and wet with morning. Streets uncoiled from the silt. Windows filled with glass that had been waiting a hundred years to be needed again.

And everywhere, names.

They came up out of the ground like wells filling. Ede heard them as words first—*Maren Oduun, keeper of the eastern locks; Tam Broadwater, who mended nets and grudges alike*—and then as voices, and then as people, standing blinking in doorways that had not existed an hour before, wearing faces the Choir had carried folded and hidden for a century until someone finally asked for them back.

Pip tugged his sleeve. "That one," she said, pointing at a broad woman by the fountain who was weeping into her own hands like they were new. "She used to sing me to sleep. Before I was just humming."

"Do you remember her name?"

"Brava." Pip said it carefully, tasting it. "Her name is Brava." Then she was running across the square, and the woman caught her up, and Ede turned away to give them the privacy of it, because some reunions are doors too, and you don't watch through those unless you're invited.

He looked up instead.

The stars were returning.

They came back the way the land did—in bands, in tides, though it was morning and no stars should have shown at all. They burned pale through the blue like coins at the bottom of clear water, and Ede understood that this sky was not the sky he'd been born under. This was Vael's sky, the one that had been rolled up and locked away when the founders built their forgery over it, and every star in it was a name too—a fixed point, a thing that refused to be lost.

One of them, low near the horizon, pulsed a slow green-white. He knew that rhythm. He had watched it flicker over a smuggler's campfire while she pretended not to be looking at him.

"Go on," he told it quietly. "Show off."

* * *

They gave him a room in the restored archive—which was to say, they gave him a room in the ruins of his order's greatest crime, because Vael's archivists had died holding their records and the records had waited a hundred years underground, patient as seeds. Ede spent the weeks after the restoration doing penance in the only language he spoke: cataloguing. Every stolen map of Thornfell went into a fire-box marked *Evidence*. Every surviving chart of Vael was copied, corrected, cross-referenced against the memories of the returned.

It was slow work. It was holy work. He suspected those were the same thing.

Pip came and went like weather. Brava had taken her in, and there were other children now—other choir-children, the ones who had been folded youngest, waking in houses that remembered them even if they didn't yet remember themselves. Pip still hummed sometimes, old fragments with nowhere to live, but more and more she sang instead, whole songs with beginnings and ends, and she had opinions about everything: the food (too soft), the lessons (too many), Ede's handwriting (a scandal).

"You write like a dying spider," she informed him one evening, watching him annotate a boundary charter.

"Ward-craft notation is precise."

"It's *ugly*, which is worse." She hopped onto the table, heels drumming the wood. "Brava says you used to be important. In the old country. The false one."

"I used to be a ward-keeper. Important is a strong word."

"She says you helped build the cage."

Ede set down his pen. Outside the window, the green-white star was rising early, stubborn, bright.

"Yes," he said. "I did. Not the founding theft—I wasn't born yet—but I maintained it. Forty years of maintenance. I checked the locks every season and called it duty."

"And now?"

"Now I check the doors every season and call it penance. It's remarkable how similar the work looks from the inside."

Pip considered this with the terrible seriousness of nine-year-olds everywhere. Then she said, "Silas would've liked that answer," and left the way she always left, mid-air between one sentence and the next, and Ede sat alone with the charter and the star and the ache that had stopped being sharp and become something he could carry, the way you carry a stone in your pocket—proof of weight, proof of ground.

* * *

Spring came to Vael the way everything came to Vael now: suddenly, and as if it had never left.

On the anniversary of the night the stars went out—the night they came back, depending on which ledger you read—the city held its first Dawn Chorus. It wasn't a ceremony so much as an instinct. The returned gathered in the squares and rooftops and hillsides before sunrise, and when the light came, they sang. Not hymns. Names. Their own and each other's, passed around like bread, because the Choir had learned something in its hundred years of darkness that the living were only beginning to understand: a name spoken aloud by another mouth is worth ten kept silent in your own chest.

Ede stood on the archive steps with a cup of tea going cold in his hands and listened to a nation sing itself awake.

Then, above it all, threading through the massed voices like a silver wire through grey cloth, he heard a melody he knew.

It wasn't a Vaelish song. It wasn't a Thornfell smuggler's tune either, not exactly—it was both, the way its singer had been both, a border-woman's melody that mapped the passes between two countries that had turned out to be one. She used to hum it on the road when she thought he was asleep. She'd claimed it was to keep the paths honest. He'd known it was to keep *him* company, and neither of them had ever said so, because some things are truer unsaid.

The voice singing it now came from everywhere and nowhere—from the dawn itself, from the green-white star fading into morning, from the space between the notes of the chorus below.

His tea trembled. He set it down very carefully on the step.

"Pip," he said, without turning around, because he could feel her at his shoulder the way you feel weather change. "Am I hearing—"

"She sings it every year," Pip said. "At dawn. Brava says the Choir keeps what's given freely, and Silas gave everything freely, so she's—" Pip's voice did something complicated, the way children's voices do when they're carrying adult-sized grief. "She's not gone, Ede. She's *in everything*. That's what freely given means. You can't lose a gift like that. You can only wait for it to come back around."

The melody rose, crested, and—this was new, this had never happened before—resolved. Into words. Into a voice with a smile buried somewhere in it, the way hers always had:

*"You write like a dying spider, Marrow. Come to the door."*

* * *

He ran. Forty years of dignity, abandoned on the archive steps; he ran through streets full of startled singers, past the fountain where Brava laughed, down the hill road with his heart slamming like a fist on a gate, and the whole time the melody walked ahead of him, unhurried, amused, leading him home.

To his door. The small house behind the archive, the one with the crooked lintel he'd never fixed because fixing things had never once made them truer.

The knocking started before he reached it. Three knocks. Patient. Ink-stained, if knocks could be such a thing.

He opened the door.

She stood on his step in the full gold of morning, and she was *whole*—not the half-lit echo he'd braced himself for, not a voice or a memory or a kindness the universe was doing him. Solid. Present. Her hair was longer than he remembered and her eyes were exactly the same, and there was ink on her fingers, fresh ink, black as the first line of a new map.

"The Choir finished singing me back last night," Silas Voss said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "A hundred years of names, and apparently mine took the longest. Something about being given rather than taken. Brava says the freely-given ones can walk out again, if someone holds the threshold long enough." She looked at him, and the smile got loose. "You held it, Ede. All winter. Don't think I didn't notice."

"I didn't do anything," he managed. "I drank cold tea and wrote ugly."

"You held the door." She stepped closer. Behind her, at the corner of the lane, Pip was pretending very badly not to watch, and further up the hill the whole city was still singing, and overhead—faint, persistent, showing off—the green-white star refused to fully fade from the morning sky. "That's the whole job, isn't it? That's what you told me at the threshold. Somebody has to stand in the doorway and believe the person on the other side is real."

"I also told you the wards were unforgivable."

"They were. So am I, probably. We'll match." She raised her ink-stained hand and touched his cheek, and her fingers were cold from the dawn and warm from everything else. "I brought something. From the deep archive, where the Choir keeps what it can't sing yet. Maps, Ede. Charts of places that were never stolen—lands beyond Vael, beyond Thornfell, beyond anything either of us ever drew. Roads nobody has walked because nobody knew they were there." Her eyes were bright and terrible and full of tomorrow. "The borders are open now. All of them. And I'm the best cartographer either country ever produced, and I need a ward-keeper who checks doors instead of locking them, and there's a child up that hill who hums paths into existence and will absolutely follow us whether we invite her or not."

Ede Marrow stood in his doorway, on the threshold, where he had always lived, and found that for the first time in his life he wanted to step through.

"Where would we even go?" he asked.

Silas smiled, and behind her the dawn chorus swelled, thousands of names sung back at last, and somewhere in it a child's voice rang out clear, already packing, already coming.

"Anywhere," she said. "Everywhere. I hear the roads out there remember *everything*."

She held out her ink-stained hand, and he took it, and together they stepped over the threshold into the wide, unwritten world—while behind them, in the house with the crooked lintel, the kettle began, all on its own, to sing.
