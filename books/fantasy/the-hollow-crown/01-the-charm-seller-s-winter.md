## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

The first snow of the year always came to Greyhollow the way grief came—slowly at first, so fine it seemed like nothing, and then all at once, too much to bear.

Isandro Yarrow stood in the doorway of her shop and watched it begin: a grey sift of flakes drifting down past the eaves of Tanner's Row, settling on the cobbles like ash that had forgotten which fire it belonged to. The harbor below was already darkening, the fishing boats hauled up for the season, their hulls looking like the ribs of patient animals waiting out the cold. Somewhere a gull cried once and thought better of it.

Behind her, the shop smelled of hemp twine and dried rowan berries and the tallow candle she allowed herself one hour before closing. Ordinary smells. Honest smells. She had spent seven years teaching herself to love them.

"Yarrow." The voice came from down the street, roughened by wind and something else. Maren Coalharness came hurrying along the Row with her shawl pulled tight and her eyes red-rimmed, and Isandro's stomach did the small familiar drop it always did when a customer arrived after dark with fear written on them plain as a brand. "Yarrow, you're still open? Tell me you're still open."

"For you, Maren, I'm open till the snow buries the door." She stepped back to let the fishwife in, and the bell above the lintel gave its cracked little note—a real bell, bought from a real bell-maker, rung by nothing but wind and hands. That had been one of her mentor's rules, back when rules were things she'd learned instead of things she bled by: *Let everything in your shop be exactly what it seems. It makes the lies easier to carry.*

Maren stamped slush off her boots and stood wringing her cap in both fists. She was a broad woman, strong as a mooring post, who had gutted ten thousand fish without flinching, and she could not meet Isandro's eyes.

"It's Tam," she said. "My boy. He's going out on the *Kestrel* tomorrow. Last run before ice. And his father—" Her jaw worked. "His father went out on the last run before ice, eleven years back."

"I remember," Isandro said quietly. Everyone on the Row remembered. Half the widows of Greyhollow had been made in the same week of the same winter.

"They're saying the water's wrong this year. Wrong cold. Old Pell says he's never seen the fog lie on the harbor like that in forty years of fishing, and old Pell is a liar about everything except the sea." Maren finally looked up, and there it was—the thing Isandro had seen a hundred times in this shop, in this city, in this long slow decade of bad harvests and colder winters and whispers at the well. Not superstition. Worse than superstition. *Hope.* "I know what you sell. I know it's knots and string and nonsense. But I'll pay silver for nonsense tonight, Yarrow. I'll pay anything."

The candle guttered. Outside, the snow thickened, and somewhere far beneath the floorboards—farther than floorboards went, farther than any honest cellar—something Isandro did not let herself think about shifted in its sleep, the way it always seemed to shift when someone in Greyhollow spoke of the water being wrong.

"Sit," Isandro said. "Warm your hands. Nonsense takes time if it's going to be good nonsense."

She moved through the shop lighting the lamps she kept for just such customers, and while Maren thawed herself by the little iron stove, Isandro took down her working tray: the spools of cord, red and grey and undyed white; the bone needle worn smooth as sea glass; the little dish of iron filings she used for nothing at all, because iron filings in a charm were worth exactly as much as anyone believed they were, which was sometimes quite a lot.

Seven years ago she had been able to do it properly. Seven years ago she could have taken a length of cord and spoken to it in the language under kingdoms, and the cord would have *listened*, would have knotted itself around Tam Coalharness's luck the way a mother knots a scarf around a child's throat, snug and sure against the weather. She could have tied a wind into a sail-cloth. She could have set a ward on a door that would have turned away drowning itself.

Now she had string, and knots, and the oldest magic left in the world, which is the magic of doing a thing carefully because someone you love might depend on it.

She chose three cords—red for blood and home, grey for the grey water, white for the thing she didn't name—and began the knot her mentor had called the fisherman's mercy, though it predated fishermen, though it predated mercy, though it had probably been old when the kingdom of Greyhollow was young. Over and under and through. Nine crossings. She counted them aloud under her breath, not because the counting mattered but because Maren needed to hear it, needed the sound of work being done on her son's behalf, needed the nine crossings the way she needed the prayer the priests no longer dared to say aloud in a city where the king's men burned people for less.

"What do I do with it?" Maren whispered, when Isandro pressed the finished knot into her palm. It was a small ugly beautiful thing, tight as a fist.

"Tie it to the prow. Left side, below the waterline if you can reach. And then—" Isandro hesitated. This was the part that cost her, every time, the part where she sold the only true thing she had left. "Then you tell him you tied it yourself. You tell him his mother made it for him with her own hands, and that she counted nine crossings, and that not one of them was wasted. Don't tell him where you bought it. Mothers' knots hold better than shopkeepers' knots. Everyone knows that."

"Do they?" Maren asked, and her voice broke on it.

"No," Isandro said honestly. "But they will, if you say it like you mean it. That's how all the old magics got started."

Maren laughed—a wet, startled sound—and pressed silver into Isandro's hand, too much silver, and Isandro took only half and lied about why. Then the fishwife was gone into the falling snow, clutching a knot of string like it was her son's whole life, and maybe it was, and Isandro stood alone in her warm yellow shop and felt the familiar weight settle onto her shoulders: the weight of being the only person in Greyhollow who knew that the luck-knots were nothing, and the wards were failing, and the two facts were connected by a door.

* * *

When the lamp oil was spent and the street was silent, Isandro knelt on the floor of her back room and pried up the third board from the wall.

Beneath it lay the real inventory of Yarrow's Charms & Knots, the stock she would burn before she let it be found: a bundle of true rowan wands cut from the old wood above the cliffs, still faintly warm to the touch after seven years in the dark. A jar of grave-ice that never melted, traded to her grandmother's grandmother by someone who had come down from the north with frost in his veins and sorrow in his mouth. A book bound in grey leather with no title, whose pages showed different words to different readers, and which had shown Isandro, on the single occasion she'd dared open it, a list of names ending with her own.

And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth, the gatekeeper's tools: the tally-stick of black oak that hummed when the seal weakened, and the ring of nine keys that opened nothing anywhere in the world except one place, and the thin bone stylus with which she renewed, night after night, the marks that held the world's oldest lock.

She took out the tally-stick first. She always took it out first, the way a sailor checks the sky.

It was quiet. It was always quiet now—that was the trouble. In her first year as keeper it had murmured constantly, a low insect drone of strain, and she had learned to read its moods the way Maren read the harbor fog. Three years ago the murmur had faded to a whisper. Two years ago, to a pulse. Now it lay in her palm like any dead branch, and she knew—I have known since the first hard frost, she admitted to herself, in the privacy of the dark under her own floor—she knew that silence was not peace. Silence was the sound of a thing that had stopped resisting and started waiting.

She put it back. She put everything back, board over boards, rug over boards, and sat for a moment in the dark with her hands flat on the floor, feeling through the wood and the stone and the sixty feet of older stone beneath it the faint, patient cold of the Hollow Door.

*Not yet,* she told it, the way she told it every night. *Not yet. Not tonight.*

It never answered. That, at least, she could rely on.

* * *

The way down began behind her own hearthstone, because her mentor had believed—correctly, as it turned out—that the safest hiding place for a forbidden door was inside the life of an unremarkable woman selling string to frightened mothers.

Isandro banked the fire, lifted the hearthstone with the iron hook, and descended the narrow stair that spiraled down through rock that grew older with every turn. She carried no torch. The passage lit itself for her, reluctantly, in seams of pale blue light that woke in the walls as she passed—not magic she commanded, merely magic that remembered her, the way an old dog remembers the hand that feeds it even after the hand has nothing left to give.

Seventy-two steps. She had counted them every night for seven years, and every night for seven years she had hoped to find the count changed, the stair collapsed, the whole arrangement dissolved into ordinary geology. Seventy-two steps, and then the antechamber.

It was a small round room, and it would have been nothing at all—the kind of chamber a besieged city digs for grain or bones—if not for the far wall. Or rather, if not for what the far wall was not. The Hollow Door was a doorway with no door: a tall arch of black stone standing free in the living rock, and where a door should have been there was only air, and beyond the air there was—

There was nothing. Not darkness, which is something. Nothing, which is the absence the dark is afraid of. Looking at it too long made her eyes water and her teeth ache, and every keeper before her had recorded the same sensation in the same careful hand, generation upon generation, a lineage of women and men wincing at a hole in the world.

The arch was carved. Of course it was carved. The Old Blood had carved everything they built, and they had built to outlast meaning. Above the arch ran a line of script in the pre-kingdom tongue, letters like bare branches, like cracks in ice, like the tracks of birds walking on a frozen lake:

*WHEN THE CROWN IS WHOLE AGAIN, KNOCK.*

Below it, smaller, almost tender, another line that the kingdom's scholars had never been permitted to translate, because the kingdom had decided there was nothing below the Door worth translating:

*WE GAVE OUR CHILD. KEEP YOUR WORD.*

And around the arch, renewed in fresh-cut lines that glowed faintly silver in the blue light, ran the seal—her seal, her work, seven years of nightly strokes laid down over the strokes of the keeper before her, and hers over his, and his over hers, back and back, a palimpsest of vigilance older than the kingdom, older than the burning of witches, older than the word *witch*.

Isandro took out the bone stylus and the little pot of ink she ground herself from ash and salt and one drop of her own blood, drawn from the pad of her thumb, because the treaty had specified blood freely given and the treaty, whatever else it was, was specific.

She began at the lower left of the arch, as her mentor had taught her, as his mentor had taught him.

"Keeper's stroke," she said aloud, because saying it aloud was part of it, because the words in the old tongue were the only words of that language she permitted herself, and they tasted like cold iron and like someone else's childhood. "Keeper's stroke, freely given. Hold."

The stylus moved. The line followed. Where the new stroke crossed the old ones, the silver brightened, and for one breath—one breath, every night, the same breath—the whole seal sang, a harmonic so low she felt it in her sternum rather than heard it, and beneath the singing she felt the other thing answer. The presence behind the nothing. The tenant of the hollow crown.

It was not evil. She had learned that early, and wished she hadn't. Evil would have been simpler. It was *patient*, which is worse, and it was *sad*, which is worst of all, and on nights like this one—snow coming down on Greyhollow, a mother's silver in her pocket, a boy sailing out on the *Kestrel* with a knot of string tied to his prow—it pressed against the seal the way a mourner presses against a closed door, gently, endlessly, without hope and without surrender.

*One honest generation,* her mentor had whispered, dying, pressing the stylus into her hand. *That's all we bought them. One generation where no child is born knowing the old words. Spend it wisely, girl. It's nearly spent.*

He had been right. It was nearly spent. She could feel it the way she could feel weather in her knee: the kingdom's memory softening, the old prohibitions becoming stories, stories becoming jokes. Children had begun drawing spirals in the frost again. Someone had chalked a counter-sigil on the granary wall last month, and the constable had laughed it off as boys' mischief, and Isandro had walked home shaking.

Nine strokes across the top. Nine down each side. Nine across the bottom. Thirty-six, and the thirty-sixth always the hardest, because the thirty-sixth was the one that sealed the seal, the stroke that said *not yet* and meant it with her whole blood.

She drew it. The silver flared and settled. The presence behind the Door receded—not gone, never gone, but folded back into its patience like a letter folded back into its envelope.

And then Isandro stood alone in the antechamber, in the blue waking light, and did the thing she did every night and told no one: she listened.

For footsteps above. For the tally-stick humming. For the voice beneath the ice that her mentor had sworn never spoke to keepers, only through them.

Snow whispered against the mountain overhead. The harbor fog lay down on the water where old Pell said it shouldn't. In a house on Tanner's Row, a boy named Tam slept with his boots by the door, ready for the last run before ice.

And in the silence after the thirty-sixth stroke, Isandro Yarrow heard—faint, far off, impossible, unmistakable—a knock.

Three knocks. Patient. Almost polite.

Coming, not from beyond the Door, but from the top of the stairs. From her shop. From the street door of Yarrow's Charms & Knots, where no one knocked at this hour, where no one ever knocked at all.
