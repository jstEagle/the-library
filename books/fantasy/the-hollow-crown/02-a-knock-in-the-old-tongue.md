## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The first snow of the season came at dusk, fat and slow, like ash falling from a fire no one could see.

Isandro watched it from behind her shop window, watching it settle on the crooked shingles across the lane, on the empty market stalls, on the shoulders of the last few stragglers hurrying home before dark. Greyhollow in winter was a city holding its breath. The chimneys smoked, the gutters froze, and every soul who passed beneath her window walked with their head down and their collar up, as if the sky itself might be listening.

She had learned, over seven years, to do the same.

The luck-knot had taken her most of the afternoon. It was good work—red thread over grey, three turns and a binding loop, each pass whispered with just enough intent to make the fibers remember. Not enough to light a candle. Not enough to wake a ward. Just enough that a fishwife's husband might come home from the ice-fishing grounds with all his fingers still attached. Isandro held it up to the lamplight and turned it slowly, checking the tension, the lie of the threads. It looked like nothing. That was the whole art of it. In Greyhollow, magic that looked like magic got you burned; magic that looked like string kept people alive.

A knock at the door. Three raps, quick and nervous.

Maren Codline stood on the step with snow in her hair and terror in her face, and Isandro felt the old familiar weight settle onto her chest—the weight of being the only person in a frightened city who could actually help.

"Isandro." Maren slipped inside before she was invited, bringing a gust of cold and the smell of brine and woodsmoke. "They're saying the ice is wrong this year. Out past the shoals. Old Toma swears he heard something under it. Singing."

"Singing," Isandro repeated, keeping her voice flat and bored. She set the luck-knot on the counter between them. "Toma drinks."

"Toma sees things too." Maren's hands were chapped raw, knuckles split from hauling nets. She stared at the knot without touching it. "My Bram goes out tomorrow. First proper freeze, and he's out on the black ice with half the harbor men because there's no coin coming in any other way. I need—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, Isandro could see her hating it. "I need you to do the thing you do."

"The thing I do is knots," Isandro said. "Knots and string. Two coppers."

Maren paid without haggling, which told Isandro everything about how frightened she truly was. She took the luck-knot in both hands as though it were an egg, murmured her thanks, and paused at the door with snow melting down her collar.

"You ever think about leaving?" she asked suddenly. "Before the real cold comes. South, maybe. Where they don't burn people for talking to their own shadows."

Isandro smiled the smile she'd practiced for seven years—warm, tired, slightly stupid. "And leave my shop? Who'd buy your husband's fish then?"

Maren laughed, but it was thin, and her eyes stayed worried, and then she was gone into the falling snow, and Isandro stood alone in the lamplight with her heart going hard against her ribs.

*Singing under the ice.*

She thought of the floorboard beneath the third shelf, and what slept beneath it, and how many nights she'd lain awake counting the sounds of the city and telling herself: *still sealed, still sealed, still sealed.* Seven years of small lies. Seven years of selling knots and charms to frightened people while the truth sat beneath her floorboards like a corpse she couldn't bury.

She banked the fire, turned the sign to *closed*, and was reaching for the lamp to carry downstairs when the wards hummed.

Not rang. Not chimed. *Hummed*—a low, resonant note she felt in her back teeth, in the bones of her feet through the floorboards, in the hollow behind her sternum where the oath lived. In seven years the wards had hummed exactly twice: once when a drunk had tried to pry the lead flashing off her roof, and once on the night she'd first spoken the words of the seal aloud and understood, finally and completely, what she had inherited.

Isandro went very still.

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Outside, the snow had thickened until the world beyond her window was a soft grey blur, and somewhere in that blur, footsteps.

Small ones.

* * *

The girl came around the corner of the tannery wall like something the storm had coughed up. Barefoot. Barefoot, in the first snow of winter, and not stumbling either—walking with a strange deliberate care, placing each foot as though the ground were a page she was reading. Her dress was fine once, Isandro saw even at a distance: deep blue wool gone grey with road-dirt, hem torn to ribbons, sleeves too long so that only her fingertips showed. Dark hair cut raggedly short, as if someone had hacked at it with a knife rather than scissors. Maybe eight years old. Maybe younger. Hard to tell with children who have been hungry.

No one else was on the street. Of course no one else was on the street. The city had emptied itself the way cities do when the weather turns honest, and Isandro stood in her doorway with the cold biting through her sweater and thought: *go away, go away, whatever you are, whoever sent you, go away.*

The girl stopped at the bottom of her steps and looked up.

Her eyes were the problem. Even from ten feet away, even in failing light and falling snow, Isandro could see that they were wrong—not the color, which was ordinary brown, but the depth, the stillness. Eyes like wells. Eyes like the space beneath ice. A child should have been shivering. This child stood in the snow as though the snow were a rumor she hadn't decided whether to believe.

Then she spoke.

"*Vaela sorren, vaela dun.*"

The words hit the wards like a struck bell.

Isandro felt it happen—felt the hidden lattice woven through her walls and doorstep and window-glass wake up and sing, a rising harmonic that made the frost on her windows crawl into new patterns, that made the lamp flame stand up straight and tall, that made the hair on her arms lift. The sound was old. Older than the kingdom. Older than the king's grandfather's grandfather. It was the language carved into the lintel above a door seven levels below her cellar, the language her mentor had made her memorize syllable by syllable with a switch in his hand and grief in his eyes, saying: *you will never speak this to anyone, and someday you will know why.*

The girl said it again, patient, as though Isandro were simply slow. "*Vaela sorren, vaela dun.*"

*I am looking for the keeper. I was told to find the door.*

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. Terror came first—that was honest, that was animal, the body knowing before the mind that everything had just changed—and behind the terror, arriving like dawn behind a mountain, recognition. Because those weren't random syllables. Those were the opening words of the inscription. The greeting. The *knock*. Carved in stone by hands two thousand years dead, and now standing barefoot in her snow-covered street in the mouth of a child who could not possibly be more than nine.

"No," Isandro said aloud, to herself, to the girl, to the evening. "No, no—you've got the wrong—this is a charm shop. Knots. String. Nothing here for you."

The girl tilted her head. Snow gathered in her lashes and did not melt.

Then Isandro saw what she clutched against her chest with both hands, fingers white-knuckled with the grip of someone who has been told, in whatever words convince a child, that this thing must never be dropped, never be shown, never be lost.

Iron. Crown-shaped. Small enough to fit in a palm, black with age except where the child's hands had polished it bright, and even at a distance—even without touching it—Isandro's skin recognized it the way a scar recognizes weather. The token pulled at the air. Pulled at the wards. Pulled at something far, far below them both, seven floors of stone and silence down, where a seal older than the kingdom lay pressed against a darkness that had waited a very long time to be knocked for.

Every story has a moment when the floor drops out. For Isandro Yarrow, it was watching a barefoot child hold out a piece of iron older than empires and say, in the tongue of the Old Blood, in a voice pitched high and clear through the falling snow:

"*Keeper. Vaela sorren. Dun.*"

*Find the keeper. Open the door.*

* * *

She got the girl inside. She would never afterward remember deciding to do it—only the cold of the doorstep through her stockings, the shocking warmth of the child's hand (warm, impossibly warm, warm as a hearthstone in a freezing street), the door closing behind them both with a sound like a held breath released.

Up close, the girl smelled of woodsmoke and pine and something underneath both, something mineral and cold, like water that has never seen sun. There were cracks in her lips from thirst and dirt ground so deep into her bare feet it looked like shadow. But she wasn't frostbitten. Isandro checked—turned the small blue-tinged hands over, pressed the toenails, did everything Maren's mother had taught every fishwife's daughter about reading cold in flesh—and found nothing. The girl's feet were pink and healthy as a summer child's. As though the snow had simply declined to touch her.

"What's your name?" Isandro asked.

The girl watched her mouth move with careful attention, the way one watches a foreign animal. Then she shook her head. Not refusal—something sadder. She pointed at her own throat and made a small rasping sound, and Isandro understood: the voice worked for that language and perhaps not well for others. Or perhaps the girl had simply decided, with a child's iron practicality, that there was no point wasting words on someone who couldn't answer.

"Right," Isandro said faintly. "Right."

She fed her first—stew from the pot, bread, watered wine cut further with water because the child's hands trembled around the cup, though whether from weakness or some other cause Isandro couldn't guess. The girl ate with terrible control, small measured bites, as though she had learned somewhere that eating fast got food taken away. Watching her, Isandro had to turn toward the fire and busy her hands for a moment.

Above the hearth, on the shelf where she kept the charms that were only charms, the luck-knots and the fever-threads and the little braided circles that meant *safe travels* to people who needed to believe in something—the red thread of the newest knot had begun, very gently, to sway. No wind. No draft. It swayed in time with something Isandro could feel through the soles of her feet, a rhythm rising from far below, slow and vast, like breathing.

Like something waking up.

The girl noticed too. She looked at the swaying thread, then at Isandro, and her expression shifted—for the first time, something moved behind those well-deep eyes that was not patience and was not hunger. It was fear. A child's fear, sharp and sudden, quickly mastered, and it broke Isandro's heart even as it terrified her, because children who have mastered their own fear are children who have been taught why they must.

The girl set down her cup. She reached into her dress and drew out the iron token again, and this time she did not clutch it. She held it out flat on both palms, an offering, and said—softly now, almost apologetically, the way one delivers news one knows is bad:

"*Vaela sorren, vaela dun. Senn avra, senn kol.*"

*I bring the key. The crown remembers.*

And the words rose off her tongue and hung in the warm air of the little shop, and above the Door—seven floors down, behind stone and seal and seven years of Isandro's careful, desperate, unwitnessed vigil—the Hollow Door answered.

Not with sound. With absence. Every fire in the shop guttered at once. The lamp died. The coals went grey. And in the sudden dark, Isandro heard—or felt, or remembered; the distinction had stopped mattering—a single knock from below. Patient. Enormous. The kind of knock that does not need to be repeated.

When the flames struggled back to life, the girl was watching her with those still brown eyes, and the iron token lay gleaming on her palms like a small black moon, and Isandro Yarrow understood, with the perfect clarity that arrives only at the worst moments of a life, that the seven years of held breath were over.

Something had knocked. And the Door knew its name.

She looked at the child. The child looked back. Outside, the snow fell thicker, erasing the street, the lane, the city, the world—and somewhere out in that whited-out dark, Isandro realized with a fresh spike of dread, there might be others following the same small footprints. Others who had spent two thousand years learning to listen for a language that made wards hum.

She blew out the lamp, bolted the door, and turned to the girl with a question she already dreaded the answer to:

"Who taught you to say that?"
