## 5. Seven Years of Small Lies

The tannery smell reached the shop before Odrik did.

It came up Rope Lane on the morning wind like a rumor—smoke and lime and wet hide, the scent I had spent seven years learning to call *home* rather than *endure*. Most folk in Greyhollow wrinkled their noses at it. I had come to find it honest. Everything in this city lied a little: the fishmongers about the freshness of their catch, the priests about the mercy of their god, the king's criers about the prosperity of his reign. But a tannery smelled exactly like what it was. There was comfort in that, these days more than ever.

I was restringing a shelf of luck-knots when the bell above my door gave its small brass cough, and Odrik ducked under the lintel with the careful stoop of a very large man in a very small room.

"Yarrow." He said my name the way he said everything—like a stone set down gently on a table. "You've got frost on your shutters already. First snow's coming early."

"It always comes early on Rope Lane. The chimneys breathe wrong."

He grunted, which from Odrik was agreement, disagreement, and philosophical reflection all at once. He filled the doorway the way a barrel fills a cellar, forearms scarred white where the lime had bitten him over thirty years of work, beard going to iron at the chin. His hands were the hands of a man who had held dying things gently and living things firmly, and never confused the two.

"I brought you something." He set a bundle on my counter, wrapped in oilcloth. Inside: a strip of leather worked soft as cloth, dyed the deep brown of river mud, edged in a braid he'd clearly practiced until his fingers bled. "For your belt. Yours is cracked through. You'll lose a charm down a drain one day and then where will Greyhollow's luck be?"

I ran my thumb along the braid. It was beautiful. It was also, I realized with a small cold turn in my chest, an excuse. Odrik did not bring gifts without reasons. He brought gifts the way other men brought questions—wrapped so they could be set down without anyone having to look at them directly.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, and hated that I immediately began searching it for the question inside.

* * *

I should explain what Odrik was to me, though I have never been able to explain it even to myself.

Seven years ago, when I came to Greyhollow with a dead man's oath in my mouth and a dead man's key hidden against my ribs, I knew no one. I rented the shop on Rope Lane because it was cheap and because the cellar beneath it connected—by ways I will not write here—to the Hollow Door, and because the previous tenant had died of a fever that made the rooms easy to claim. For the first winter I spoke to no one but customers, and my customers spoke only to buy.

Odrik's yard backed onto mine. In the second spring, I found him standing at the fence at dawn, holding one of his hides out flat in the frost-light, and he said—not to me, just to the morning—"You can tell a lot about a hide by how it takes the cold. The ones that stiffen fast were sick before they were ever cut."

And I, who had not spoken an unnecessary word in eight months, heard myself say, "Like people."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed—a sound like a cart going over cobblestones—and said, "Aye. Like people," and went back to his work, and something in me unclenched that has never fully clenched again.

We did not become friends quickly. We became friends the way roots grow, invisibly and all at once. He fixed my roof without being asked. I mended the charm-bag he wore against the tanning fevers, and told him it was only knotted thread and belief, which was true in the way that all my best lies are true. When the fever took his wife, Marta, six years past, I sat with him through three nights of it, and on the third night he put his head down on the table beside her hand and wept the way big men weep, silently, as if ashamed of the noise, and I kept vigil and did not let myself think about how easily death moved through this city, or what waited beneath it, patient as water.

He never asked what I was. Not once in seven years. He watched me sell charms to frightened fishwives and lovesick apprentices and once, memorably, to a king's tax collector who wanted protection from his own conscience, and he never asked whether any of it was real. I think he understood, in the wordless way of practical men, that some trades require a person to keep certain rooms of themselves locked, and that knocking on those doors is a discourtesy between neighbors.

Which is why, when he finally asked, it undid me.

* * *

It happened on the fourth day after Vess came to my door.

I had kept her in the back room, behind the curtain of dried herbs, telling the street she was a niece from upcountry come to help with the winter stock. A thin story. Stories about children are always thin; children have a way of being seen. But Greyhollow is a city of averted eyes—it survives on them—and for three days the averted eyes held.

On the fourth day, Vess got restless.

She was seven years old, maybe eight, small for it, with the pale watchfulness of a creature raised in hiding. She did not cry. She did not ask for anything. That was the worst of it—I had known children who wailed and children who sulked, and I knew the grammar of both, but this child simply *waited*, sitting by my window with the iron token clutched in her fist, watching the street below with the patience of someone who had learned that wanting things aloud gets people killed.

That afternoon she discovered the cats.

Greyhollow's dockside cats are half-wild, gray as the fog, and one of them—a torn-eared queen we all called the Widow—had taken to sleeping in my window box among the dead marjoram. Vess crouched at the glass and made a sound. Not a word. A sound, low and rising, in that language that made my wards hum in their sleep, and the Widow turned her head and came to the window like a soldier answering a whistle, and pressed her face to the pane, and stayed there while Vess breathed on the glass.

I stood in the doorway with a basket of knotwork in my hands and felt the floor tilt under seven years of careful footing.

Because the Old Blood had been friend to beasts before they were anything else. My mentor told me that once, in the last lucid hour of his life, when the fever had burned everything else away: *They spoke first to wolves and rivers, girl. To kings only afterward, and reluctantly.* And here was a child calling cats to windows in a tongue older than the kingdom, and outside my window, on the street, in plain view—

The door opened.

Not the shop door. The yard door, the one that stuck and that only two people in Greyhollow could open without cursing. Odrik came through it carrying a sack of charcoal for my stove, and he stopped halfway across the yard, and he looked up.

At the window. At the child. At the cat pressed flat to the glass like a gray hand.

Vess looked back at him. She did not flinch and she did not hide, and I understood in that instant that she had been seen too many times to believe anymore that being unseen was possible. Whatever world had made her, it had taught her that lesson the hard way.

"Well now," Odrik said.

* * *

He came into the kitchen the way he came into everything, filling it, and set the charcoal down, and looked at me over the child's head with an expression I had never seen on him before. Not suspicion. Something worse. *Concern.*

"Yarrow. Who is this?"

"My niece," I said. "From upcountry. Her mother's sent her to help me through the winter."

Three sentences. I had rehearsed them for three days, and they came out smooth as oiled leather, and they were the first true lies I had ever told him, and he knew it. I saw him know it. That is the thing no one warns you about lying to someone who loves you—they don't catch you in the lie. They catch you in the *telling*. The words were fine. The space around the words was wrong, the way a hide is wrong when it's been stretched over a frame too fast, and Odrik had spent thirty years reading hides.

He crouched down. Slowly, the way you approach a hurt animal, knees cracking like green wood. He got himself to Vess's level, and he studied her—the bare feet I hadn't been able to fill with shoes yet, the iron token she'd tucked into her collar but not hidden, the eyes that watched him with neither fear nor trust but some older thing than either.

"What's your name, little one?" he asked, gentle as a man talking to a spooked mare.

Vess looked at me.

That was the moment. I want to be precise about it, because I have turned it over every night since, the way you turn a stone looking for what's underneath. She looked at *me*. Not for permission—for instruction. She had been given to me, or left with me, or sent to me, and she did not know the rules of this new country, and she was asking me to give them to her. And the rule I chose—the rule I opened my mouth and gave her, with Odrik kneeling in front of us and the frost coming down outside and seven years of quiet loyalty hanging in the air like smoke—was:

*Hide.*

"Vess," I said. "Her name is Vess. She doesn't talk much. She's been ill."

The lie about the illness was almost kind. It explained the silence, the watchfulness, the bare feet, the way she held herself like something braced for a blow. Odrik nodded slowly, and I watched him accept it—not because he believed it, but because he loved me, and love will carry a lie a long way before it sets it down and asks what's in it.

"She's welcome to warm by my kilns if the cold bites," he said. "Marta's old cloak is still in the loft. Child-sized near enough." He rose, joints protesting. "Upcountry, you said."

"That's right."

"Hm." He gathered his empty sack, folded it with the deliberation of a man buying time. "Funny thing. I was upcountry in the autumn, buying bark for the pits. Walked most of the high valleys." He met my eyes. "Didn't see many houses burning. Didn't see any orphans walking the roads either. Roads were empty as a church on market day."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the shop, a luck-knot I'd strung too tight gave a small creak, like a held breath.

"People move," I said.

"They do." He slung the sack over his shoulder. At the door he stopped, and did not turn around, and spoke to the frame instead of to me, which was worse. "You know I've never asked you what you really sell in this shop. Seven years, Yarrow. Never asked. Because whatever it is, you've done right by this street, and a man doesn't need to open his neighbor's letters to know his neighbor's good."

"Odrik—"

"But you're lying to me now." Still to the doorframe. Quietly. "First time. I'd know the taste of it anywhere, same as you'd know bad lime in a batch. And I'm not angry. I want you to hear that. I'm not angry. I'm *afraid*." Now he turned, and his face was the face of a man looking at a river in flood season, measuring it. "Whatever walked into your life four days ago, it's big enough that you'd lie to me over it. Nothing small makes a person do that. So I'll ask you one question, and you don't have to answer it today, or this week, or ever. But answer it honestly when you do."

He looked at Vess. She looked back, unblinking, the Widow cat's reflection ghosted on the glass behind her.

"Is she in danger?"

And I said, "No."

And it was the worst lie yet, because it wasn't even skillful, and because we both stood there in the smell of charcoal and tanned leather and drying herbs and knew it for what it was: the sound of a woman choosing, for the first time in seven years, to protect a secret instead of a friend.

He nodded once. "Right," he said. "Charcoal's by the stove. Mind the frost on the shutters—first snow's coming early."

Then he was gone, and the yard door stuck behind him the way it always did, and I stood in my kitchen with a child who was a key and a lie that was a door closing, and I could not tell you which loss weighed heavier.

* * *

That night I performed the ritual at the Hollow Door, as I do every night, and the seal hummed under my palms, and I listened for the deep slow breathing of the thing below, and it was there, patient as water, patient as winter.

But I could not make my mind sit still on the work. It kept returning to the yard door, sticking shut. To the word *afraid*, said quietly, to a doorframe, by the only person in Greyhollow whose good opinion I had never had to purchase with a charm.

Here is what I did not understand yet, that night: that Odrik had not closed a door at all. That a man who tells you he is afraid, and stays anyway, and leaves charcoal by your stove, has not withdrawn—he has enlisted. That love does not stop at the first lie. It stops at the last one, and it marks the spot.

Three days later, when the witch-hunters' black-liveried riders came up the coast road into Greyhollow, asking after a child, it would be Odrik who stood in the tannery yard with lime-stained arms crossed and told Captain Rellane's scouts, truthfully, that he'd seen nothing—because he had decided, somewhere between the doorframe and the stove, that whatever I was protecting mattered more than whatever I was hiding.

But that night I didn't know it. That night I banked the fire, and checked the wards, and lay awake listening to Vess breathe in the back room, and thought: *seven years of small lies, and the largest one yet cost me nothing but the voice of my oldest friend.*

Outside, the first snow began to fall—early, even for Rope Lane.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, down where the Door stood in its long dark, something that wore my mentor's voice began, very softly, to laugh.
