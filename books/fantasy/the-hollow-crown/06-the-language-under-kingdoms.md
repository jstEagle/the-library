## 6. The Language Under Kingdoms

The world is made of doors.

Vess knows this the way she knows her own name, which is to say: not at all, and completely. Her name is a sound that lives in other people's mouths. *Vess*, they called her, in the tower, before the towers burned. *Vess*, said the woman with river-grey hair who pressed the iron into her palm. *Vess*, says the voice beneath the ice, patient as winter, saying it over and over like a bell rung in an empty room.

She is walking. She has been walking for—she counts on her fingers, but the numbers slide away from her like fish. Days. Many days. The road behind her is a grey ribbon unspooling through country she does not remember crossing, and ahead of her the city rises out of the marsh fog like a jaw of broken teeth. Greyhollow. She does not know how she knows its name. The name simply arrived inside her one morning, whole and cold, the way snow arrives on a windowsill.

*Find the keeper,* the woman had said, kneeling in the burning doorway so their eyes were level. There was ash in her hair then too, the way there is ash in everything now. *Find the keeper of the Hollow Door. Say what I taught you. She will be frightened. Frightened things bite, but they also shelter.* And then the woman had smiled, and her smile was the last warm thing Vess saw for a very long time.

*

In dreams, the towers are always burning.

Not falling—burning. Standing in their places, tall and white-veined with frost, wrapped in orange silk of flame, and no one screams. That is the part Vess cannot make anyone understand, because there is no one to tell. In the burning towers nobody screams. They sing.

She walks through the dream-towers barefoot—the floor is warm, pleasantly warm, like sun-baked stone—and the singers turn to look at her as she passes. Their faces are wrong in the way of dreams, smooth as eggs, and their mouths open and close and pour out the language. Not the kingdom tongue, the flat clattering tongue of the roads and the markets where men shout about grain prices. The other language. The deep one. The one that lives under her own ribs and hums when she is afraid.

The language has no word for *goodbye*. She learned this from the woman, who laughed once, bitterly, and said: *We never needed one. We were never meant to leave.*

But the language has eleven words for *door*. Vess lies awake in ditches and barns and once, memorably, the hollow of a dead tree, reciting them to herself like beads on a string. Door-that-opens. Door-that-closes. Door-that-was-never-built. Door-of-teeth. Door-of-mourning. Door-the-world-forgot. She falls asleep counting doors instead of sheep, and in her sleep she walks through all eleven, and on the far side of the eleventh there is always ice, and beneath the ice there is always the voice.

*Vess.*

It does not shout. It has never shouted. It says her name the way you'd say the name of someone sleeping in the next room, someone you are waiting to wake up at exactly the right moment.

*Vess. Come down. It's warmer here than you think.*

*

She wakes in Greyhollow because a cart nearly rolls over her.

The wheel passes close enough to stir her hair. A man leans down from the seat and swears at her in the kingdom tongue, calls her a gutter-rat, tells her to get under a roof before the snow takes her fingers. Vess nods because nodding is safe. She has learned that nodding is almost always safe. Then she runs, because running is safer still.

The city smells of fish and smoke and tar and something else underneath, something old, something that makes the humming under her ribs change pitch. She stands in the mouth of an alley with her arms wrapped around herself and lets the feeling move through her like a current through water. It pulls left. She goes left. It is not a thought. It is older than thoughts, older than the kingdom, older maybe than the towers, and it tugs her through the waking streets the way the voice tugs her through the dreaming ones.

Past shuttered stalls. Past a shrine where a fat clay god grins with a chipped nose. Past children throwing stones at a scarecrow dressed in a woman's coat, shouting words Vess doesn't understand but whose shape she recognizes anyway—*witch*, the stones-throwers are shouting, *witch, witch*—and she presses herself against cold brick until they pass, because whatever a witch is, it is a thing people throw stones at, and Vess has decided she would prefer not to be thrown at.

The pulling stops.

She looks up. A shop. Narrow, crooked, leaning against its neighbor like a tired old man against a wall. A painted sign above the door shows a knot of thread and a sprig of dried lavender, the paint peeling, the lavender more grey than purple now. Charms, the sign says. Luck-knots. Wards against ill weather. Nothing here but knots and string.

But the door—

Vess's breath comes out in a small white cloud, and the cloud seems to *lean* toward the door, and the humming under her ribs rises up into her throat and sits there, singing, and she understands suddenly, with the terrible clarity of children and animals, that this is the place. This crooked little shop with its peeling sign is the hinge of the world.

Above the door, cut into the stone lintel where no one passing ever bothers to look, there are letters. Old letters. Deep-cut, worn soft by centuries of rain, but she can read them the way she can read her own hand.

*Here ends the road. Here begins the keeping.*

Her eyes fill with tears and she does not know why. She wipes them with the back of her hand, leaving a smear of road-dirt across her cheek, and lifts her fist to knock.

*

That night, in the room above the shop, in a bed that is too soft after weeks of ditches, Vess dreams of the ice again.

She is standing on a frozen lake that stretches to every horizon, white and seamless, and far below her feet—so far down that she should not be able to see it, but dreams do not care about distance—there is a hall. A great hall lit by greenish light, full of tall figures sitting motionless in high chairs, crowned, robed, still as furniture. A court. A court asleep, or pretending to sleep, or waiting so long that waiting has become a kind of sleep.

And moving among the frozen court, between the thrones, pacing the length of the hall like a caged thing, there is a figure made of shadow and longing, and when it sees her through all that fathomless ice it stops, and lifts its face, and its face is kind. That is the worst part. Its face is the kindest face she has ever seen.

*You came back,* it says, and its voice is the woman's voice, the river-grey woman's voice, except the woman is dead now, Vess knows this the way she knows the name of the city, whole and cold and without explanation. *You came back to us. We waited. We kept your chair. Come down, little key. Come down and see what we kept for you.*

*I'm not a key,* Vess tells the ice, in the deep language, and her voice sounds small even inside her own head. *I'm a girl. My name is Vess.*

The kind face smiles wider. Too wide. Wide as a doorway.

*Yes,* it agrees gently. *That's what a key is.*

She wakes gasping, heart slamming, and for a moment she doesn't know where she is, and then she remembers: the shop, the bed, the keeper downstairs who looked at her iron token like it was a snake and a crown at the same time. The keeper with the tired eyes who spoke the deep language haltingly, like someone remembering a song from childhood, and who went pale as milk when Vess answered her.

Below the floorboards, faintly, Vess can feel it: a slow pulse, like a heartbeat under a blanket. The door. The Hollow Door. It beats once every few breaths, steady and patient, and each beat makes the iron token warm slightly against her chest where she has hidden it inside her shirt.

She takes it out and holds it up to the moonlight coming through the shutters. Half a crown, the keeper called it. Iron, black with age, shaped like a circlet snapped clean in two, its broken edge jagged as a bite mark. On its inner curve there is writing, tiny, dense, in the deep language, and Vess reads it aloud in a whisper just to hear the syllables in the dark:

*What was sundered in grief shall be joined in truth, or never joined at all.*

She doesn't know what it means. Words are just words. But the token grows warmer when she speaks, and somewhere far below the floorboards, far below the cellar, far below the roots of the city itself, something turns over in its sleep and murmurs her name like a prayer, like a promise, like a trap closing by inches.

*Vess. Vess. Come down.*

She puts the token away. She pulls the blanket up to her chin. She thinks about the woman with river-grey hair, and the burning towers where nobody screamed, and the eleven words for door, and she decides—firmly, the way children decide things, with her whole small stubborn self—that tomorrow she will ask the keeper what the words mean. All of them. Even the ones carved over the door. Even the ones written inside the iron.

Especially those.

Because tonight, listening to the heartbeat under the floor, Vess has understood one more thing, and it sits in her stomach like a swallowed stone:

The voice beneath the ice has been calling her name since before she had a name.

And it is getting closer to getting it right.
