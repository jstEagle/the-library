## 8. The Traitor's Mark

The snow came to Greyhollow the way guilt came—slowly at first, then all at once, until it lay over everything and made even the honest streets look like they were hiding something.

Captain Sera Rellane stood in the doorway of the Saltrow chandlery and watched it fall through the grey light, and she did not let herself think of her grandmother's hands, which had also been steady in cold weather, which had also known how to hold a blade. She thought instead of ash. Ash was safer. Ash was what she had been raised on.

"Again," she said, without turning. "From the beginning."

Behind her, the chandler—a soft man named Pellwick with flour-white hands and a face built for apologizing—started his story for the fourth time that morning. A girl. Barefoot. Strange speech. Seen near the charm-seller's shop on Copperlane, three nights past, before the first snow sealed the gutters.

"She didn't buy anything," Pellwick said. His voice had the wet, careful quality of a man walking on river ice. "She just stood there. Looking at the shop. And then she—" He swallowed. "She spoke. Not to me. To the door, maybe. Or to the air. Words I never heard before, Captain, and I've heard sailors from every coast."

"And you reported this when?"

"That same night. To the garrison."

"You reported it," Rellane said, "to the garrison. Who filed it. Who passed it up the chain. Who sat on it for two days while my order's writ gathered dust under someone's inkpot." She turned from the doorway at last and let him see her face, which was not unkind but was not anything else either. "Do you understand what a delay of two days means, Master Pellwick?"

"No, Captain."

"It means she could be anywhere. It means whoever brought her here has had two days to move her, hide her, or teach her to stop looking strange." Rellane crossed the chandlery's cluttered floor, stepping over coils of rope, and paused at the counter where a luck-knot hung from a nail—cheap string, dyed red, the kind sold by the dozen in this city to people who no longer believed in anything but wanted to keep the habit of hoping. She touched it briefly. It was just string. It was always just string. That was what she told herself, and mostly, these days, it worked.

"Describe the words," she said.

Pellwick blinked. "I—I couldn't repeat them, Captain. They weren't words so much as—"

"Sounds, then. Describe the sounds."

He tried. He was bad at it. But he got one thing right, one thing that mattered: he said the girl's voice had gone *down* when she spoke, not out. Like something spoken into a well. Like something meant to be heard from beneath.

Rellane wrote that down in her ledger, in the small precise hand her instructors at the Ashen Order had beaten into her along with everything else, and thanked the chandler, and told him the Crown was grateful for his vigilance, and stepped back out into the falling snow.

* * *

Copperlane smelled of tallow and fish-scale and, underneath it all, of the particular sourness that clung to districts where magic had been burned out of the walls a generation ago and had never quite stopped smoldering in memory. The Order called such places *scorched ground*. The people who lived there called them home, and resented the word *scorched*, and were right to.

Rellane walked the lane slowly, her two remaining knights—Vosk and Imry, young men who still believed the Order's histories were histories rather than selections—fanning out behind her. She stopped at each shopfront. She asked her questions. She collected her small lies, each one wrapped in politeness like a bitter pill in honey.

No, they hadn't seen any child. No, nothing strange. Yes, the charm-seller Yarrow kept regular hours, very respectable, very quiet. No, they'd noticed nothing unusual about her shop.

It was the last answer, repeated four times in four different mouths with four slightly different hesitations, that told Rellane the most. People lied badly about things they didn't care about. These people lied carefully. Careful lying meant investment. Investment meant affection. Somewhere on this street, someone loved Isandro Yarrow enough to lie for her, and love, in Rellane's experience, was the single most reliable indicator that there was something worth finding.

She stopped outside the charm-shop itself and looked at it the way she'd been trained to look at everything: first at the whole, then at the parts, then at the spaces between the parts.

A narrow building, timber and plaster, older than the kingdom's current dynasty if the lean of its roofline meant anything. Shutters painted a blue that had faded toward the color of old bruises. A sign with a knotted cord carved into it. Window display: charms, knots, dried herbs, small glass bottles of colored sand. Harmless. All of it harmless. The law required it to be harmless, and the law in Greyhollow was enforced by fire, so it generally was.

But Rellane had learned, in nineteen years of service, to read buildings the way scholars read manuscripts—and what she read in the spaces between the parts of this one troubled her.

The threshold stone was worn wrong. Doors wore down in the middle, where feet crossed; this one was worn deepest at the edges, as though people entered and left pressed close against the frame, avoiding the center. Avoiding the center of a threshold was an old habit. An *old* old habit. Her order's manuals called it *courtesy to the sill* and dated it to the pre-kingdom period, and it appeared in exactly none of the folk-practices of modern Greyhollow.

And above the door, set into the lintel, was a carving she had dismissed on her first walk-through as decorative scrollwork.

She looked at it now for a long time, snow gathering on her shoulders, and let her memory open the forbidden drawer where she kept the things she was not supposed to know.

* * *

The Ashen Order's archive occupied the lowest level of their chapterhouse in the capital, behind three locks and a vow, and Rellane had sworn that vow at twenty-two years old with her hand on iron and her grief still raw enough to taste. Only senior interrogators were permitted below. Only senior interrogators were permitted to see what the kingdom had actually been, before the Cleansing, before the treaties, before the long honest silence.

Most of them came up from the archive changed. Some came up and asked to leave the Order. One had come up and walked into the sea. Rellane had come up and written in her private journal, in cipher, a single sentence: *We are not the wall. We are the lid.*

She had hated herself for the thought. She had never managed to disprove it.

Because the archive held things like the folio marked **HOLLOW DOOR — SITE RECORDS**, and the site records held things like rubbings taken from the Door's outer stones two hundred years ago, before the Order decided that even accurate drawings of the sigils constituted a danger, and burned the plates, and kept only the rubbings in a locked case because destroying knowledge entirely felt too much like admitting fear.

Rellane had spent one night of her promotion year alone with those rubbings, by lamp-light, copying selected fragments into her own hand because some part of her had understood, even then, that a day would come when she would need to compare what was written against what stood.

She had assumed that day would find her standing before the Hollow Door itself, far to the north, in the ruined province where the old war had ended—or paused, or whatever the sanctioned histories called it this century.

She had not expected a lintel in Copperlane.

* * *

"Captain?" Vosk, at her shoulder, breath steaming. "You've been standing here a quarter hour."

"Look at the lintel," Rellane said. "Tell me what you see."

"Scrollwork, Captain. Old style."

"Look again. Slowly. What does scrollwork do?"

He frowned. Snow ticked against the shutters. "It... flows. Curves. Decorative lines."

"And this?"

He looked longer. She watched the moment arrive in his face—the small settling, like a house shifting on its foundations, when a man realizes the pattern he is looking at is not decoration but *writing*, and not writing in any alphabet he owns.

"I don't know it," he said quietly.

"No. You wouldn't. Neither do I, not properly. But I have seen its shape." She pulled off one glove and traced the air an inch from the carving, following the strokes without touching the stone. "Here. This cluster—it repeats. Three times across the lintel. In the archive rubbings, the same cluster appears on the Door's western face, above what the old surveyors called the listening-stone." She lowered her hand. "This is a fragment of the Door-script, Vosk. On a charm-shop. In a fishing city, six hundred miles from the Door."

"Could it be coincidence? Merchants copy pretty patterns. Half the tilework in the capital is—"

"The tilework in the capital isn't keyed." She said it flatly, and watched him go still. "Put your hand on the stone."

"Captain, protocol forbids—"

"Protocol forbids touching unknown script ungloved during an active investigation, yes. Put your hand on the stone anyway. Lightly. Tell me what it feels like."

He glanced at Imry, down the lane, pretending to question a fishwife who was giving him nothing. Then he reached out, quick, guilty, and laid his palm flat against the lintel.

Rellane saw it happen. Saw it in his forearm first—the fine muscles jumping—then in his shoulders, then in his face, which went the color of tallow candle-fat.

"Captain, it's—there's something *in* it. It's like putting your hand on a struck bell. It hasn't stopped ringing. It's been ringing for—" He snatched his hand back and cradled it against his chest like a wounded bird. "How long has it been ringing?"

"Longer than the kingdom," Rellane said. "That's the part the histories leave out. Come away from the door. Both of us. We're done looking at it today."

They walked ten paces down the lane before she trusted her voice again.

"Here is what we know," she said. "Three nights ago, a barefoot child speaking pre-kingdom script was seen at this shop. This morning, four separate neighbors lied to me about it with unusual care. Above the shop hangs a fragment of living Door-script, still resonant, still *awake*. And yesterday, at the harbor warehouse where the girl was first sighted, we found this."

She drew from her coat a folded paper and opened it between them, sheltering it from the snow. On it, in her own careful hand, was a drawing: the counter-sigil scratched into the Door's outer stone, discovered by the advance team two days before her arrival—the mark that should not exist, cut by a human hand, from the *outside*, into stone that had stood untouched for two centuries.

"I copied it from the field report," she said. "Now watch."

From an inner pocket she took a second paper, older, softer at the folds, and unfolded it beside the first. Two drawings side by side in the falling snow.

They matched. Stroke for stroke, proportion for proportion, down to the slight asymmetry in the third character that a forger would have corrected and a copier would have preserved.

Vosk stared at them for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register men used in chapels. "That second drawing, Captain. Where is it from?"

"The Order's forbidden archive," Rellane said. "Case nine. Rubbing taken from the inner face of the Hollow Door itself, from the side that faces *down*." She folded both papers away. "Someone has copied the Door's own countersignature and carved it onto the Door from outside. Which means someone knew what it looked like. Which means someone has access to knowledge that exists in exactly one place in the world, behind three locks and a vow."

The snow thickened around them. Down the lane, Imry had given up on the fishwife and was stamping warmth into his boots, young and cold and certain that witches were the whole of the world's evil.

"An archivist," Vosk said. "Someone in the Order."

"Or someone who was in the Order." Rellane watched the smoke rise from the charm-shop's chimney—thin, domestic, ordinary smoke, and somehow the most alarming thing she had seen all day. "Think it through, Knight. Why does a countersignature exist? A signature seals. A countersignature *unseals*. Someone carved the opening-word of the Hollow Door onto the Hollow Door. Not to destroy it. Destruction doesn't need script. To *open* it. Quietly. From the outside. While the whole kingdom sleeps behind its honest little laws believing the war is a story we tell children."

"But the Door's been guarded—"

"By one keeper. One aging charm-seller in a dying district, holding a post the Order stopped staffing properly forty years ago because we convinced ourselves the problem was solved." The bitterness in her own voice surprised her, and she mastered it. "So ask yourself the real question, Vosk. Ask it the way I taught you to ask everything."

He thought. She could see him thinking, and she respected him for how long he resisted the obvious answer.

"Who benefits?" he said finally. "If the Door opens... the Old Blood returns. The war resumes. Nobody benefits. Unless—" and there his face changed, because he arrived where she had arrived two sleepless nights ago. "Unless someone believes they can control what comes through. Unless someone inside the Order wants the Door open *for reasons of their own*."

"Or," Rellane said, "someone believes the Door must open to prevent something worse. There is always a faction that thinks the seal is the disease. There always has been. The archive calls them the Unbinding. We burned them out twice. We assumed the second time took."

"And now?"

"Now I want the chapterhouse records for this province pulled. Every knight posted here in the last decade. Every correspondent. Every name attached to the maintenance stipend for the Door—which, note this, Vosk, was paid faithfully every year to a keeper the Order never once inspected, because inspection would have meant admitting the post still mattered." She pulled her glove back on, flexing her fingers against the cold. "And I want the harbormaster's passenger lists for the last month. Our barefoot child did not walk here from the north. Someone *brought* her. Someone delivered a key to a lock, and then started cutting the opening-word into the stone."

"You think the child and the traitor work together?"

"I think," Rellane said, "that a key and a carved lock arriving in the same city within the same season is either the worst coincidence in two hundred years, or a plan. And I stopped believing in coincidences the year I buried my family." She turned back toward the charm-shop, and its thin thread of ordinary smoke, and its worn threshold, and its singing lintel. "Start with the lists. Quietly. If our traitor sits inside the Order's machinery, I want him learning we're looking from the same moment we learn it ourselves."

Vosk saluted and went. Rellane remained in the middle of Copperlane a moment longer, snow settling on her shoulders like a hand.

She should knock on the charm-seller's door. Protocol demanded it; instinct endorsed it; the worn threshold and the lying neighbors and the singing lintel all pointed at Isandro Yarrow like compass needles finding north. One interview. One afternoon of questions. The woman would lie, the lies would crack, and the investigation would advance.

Instead Rellane found herself looking at the blue-faded shutters and thinking about lids, and walls, and the difference between them, and about her grandmother, who had signed something once, in a hand much steadier than hers, whose name Rellane had seen only once—in the treaty folio, among the signatures of those who surrendered the city's magic forever—and had never been able to ask her about, because by the time Rellane read it, her grandmother had been eleven years in the ground.

*Tomorrow,* she told herself. *Tomorrow I'll knock.*

She gave the shop's chimney one last look, and turned up the lane toward the garrison, and did not see—no one saw—the curtain in the charm-shop's upstairs window fall back into place, or the face that had watched her from it, or the way the girl's lips moved silently, shaping words in the old tongue that the lintel, faintly, patiently, answered.
