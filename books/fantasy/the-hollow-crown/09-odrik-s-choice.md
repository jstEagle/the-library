## 9. Odrik's Choice

The tannery stank of lime and blood and salt, and Odrik Vane had loved it for thirty years the way a man loves a scar—because it was his, because it had shaped him, because no one else would want it.

He was working a hide that morning when he heard the girl.

Not her voice. She didn't speak, or if she did, it was in that strange liquid tongue that made the hair on Odrik's arms rise like hackles on a dog. He heard her instead in the small sounds she made moving through Isandro Yarrow's shop next door: the whisper of bare feet on floorboards, the clink of a jar set down too carefully, the particular silence of someone trying very hard to be quiet, which is its own kind of noise to a man who has spent three decades listening to the wet music of his trade.

Odrik set down his fleshing knife. He wiped his hands on his apron out of habit, though the apron was beyond saving, and went to the door between the buildings—the old one, the low one, that Isandro kept bolted from her side and he never used.

It stood open an inch.

That alone should have told him everything. In seven years of sharing a wall with the charm-seller, that door had never once been open. Not in summer when the heat made both buildings groan. Not the night the lamp oil caught in the alley and they'd passed buckets hand to hand through the street. Seven years, and the door had been a dead thing in the wall, and now it breathed.

Through the gap he could see the back of Isandro's shop: the shelves of luck-knots and hag-stones, the counter worn pale where a thousand frightened hands had gripped it, and beneath the counter—where the floorboard sat crooked, where it had always sat crooked, and he had always pretended not to notice—a dark square of lifted wood.

Isandro knelt there with her back to him, laying something into the space below. Even from this angle he knew what it was. You didn't spend your life around hides and dyes without learning to know a thing by its shape in shadow. It was a bundle. And bundles hidden under floors in Greyhollow, in the year of our king's great cleansing, were not bundles of string.

* * *

He should have gone back to his hides. That was the first thought, clean and cold as well-water. Go back. Pick up the knife. Let the woman bury whatever she's burying and let the earth keep it.

But Odrik Vane had buried a wife in the last plague winter and a son in the king's first purging, and he had learned something at both gravesides that he'd spent seven years trying to forget: that the ground does not care what you let it take. It takes it all the same. The only thing you get to choose is what you put there.

He knocked on the doorframe. Gently. The way you knock on a sickroom door.

Isandro went rigid. He watched it happen—watched her whole body become a held breath—and then she turned, and her face did something complicated and quick, grief and fury and fear all fighting for the same few inches of skin, before it settled into the shopkeeper's smile he'd seen her wear for strangers a thousand times.

"Odrik." Her voice was easy. Too easy. "Door sticks. I was oiling the hinges."

"In winter," he said. "Before the snow."

"The cold swells the wood."

"It does," he agreed. "Funny thing, though." He stepped through the doorway, and the smell of her shop washed over him—dried herbs and beeswax and underneath it something older, mineral, like the air off deep water. "Cold swells wood shut. It's heat that opens it. Any tanner knows it. We stretch hides against the grain, not with it."

The smile stayed on her face but the light behind it went out like a pinched wick.

"Isandro," he said quietly. "I've known you seven years. I bought your charms for my workers' boots and my own rheumatism, and half of them worked, which always seemed generous for knots and string. And I have watched you carry that crooked floorboard like a man carries a wound—careful, and always, always aware of it." He nodded at the open space in the floor. "I'm not asking what it is. I'm asking if the girl is yours."

Behind him, from the shadows by the herb shelf, came a small sound. A breath. And then the child stepped out—barefoot still, though the frost had come to the gutters now, thin as a winter branch, with eyes the color of the sea before weather. She looked at Odrik the way animals look at a new hand: weighing, deciding, remembering.

"She's nobody's," Isandro said. "She's hers."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Isandro agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They sat in the back room among the drying bundles of rue and mugwort, and Odrik learned more than he wanted to know and less than he needed to.

He learned that the child's name was Vess. That she had come out of the north with nothing but an iron token shaped like a broken crown and words in her mouth that made wards hum. That witch-hunters of the Ashen Order had come to Greyhollow asking after living script, and their captain had a face like a drawn blade and a ledger full of names.

He learned, without being told, the rest. You didn't need telling. Everyone in Greyhollow knew the story they told children: that magic was the wound the world had suffered, that the king's grandfather had burned it out of the kingdoms like rot cut from timber, and that any soul who practiced it was either a fool or a traitor. What everyone also knew, in the wordless way people know the things they agree not to say, was that the story had holes in it. That the burning had never quite finished. That some doors had been locked rather than destroyed.

And that Isandro Yarrow, who sold luck-knots to fishwives and salves to tanners, had been standing guard in front of one of them for seven years.

"You could have told me," Odrik said. It came out harder than he meant. "All this time. You could have—"

"Told you what? That I'm a dead woman walking? That every knot I sell you is a small treason?" Isandro laughed, and there was no humor in it, only exhaustion worn smooth as river stone. "Odrik. Every person you tell is a person Rellane can peel. And she peels gently—I've seen her work in the south. She'll sit across from you with kind eyes and tea, and she'll ask about your wife, your boy, your debts, and she'll be so reasonable, so understanding, until you've handed her everything you love and thanked her for taking it." She looked at him, and her eyes were the gray of the sky over the harbor. "I left you ignorant because ignorance is armor. Don't you dare be angry at me for keeping you alive."

"I'm angry because it wasn't your choice to make."

"No," she said softly. "It wasn't. That's rather the point of oaths."

The child, Vess, sat cross-legged on the floor between them, turning the iron token over and over in her fingers. Half a crown, Odrik saw now. Broken clean down the middle, the break old and blackened, as if it had been sundered in fire long ago. She wasn't looking at them. She was looking at the floor—at the place, Odrik realized with a slow cold turn of his stomach, where the boards crossed above whatever lay beneath the Hollow Door.

"She understands us," he said.

"Some. More each day. She's learning fast." Isandro's voice gentled when she spoke of the girl; he heard it happen, the way a stream changes note over different stones. "Faster than is comfortable."

"And the hunters are here for her."

"They're here for what she is. Which may be worse."

Odrik looked at his hands. They were ruined hands, tanner's hands, cracked and stained to the second knuckle with dye that would never come out, oak-bark brown soaked forty years deep. His father had had the same hands. His son would have had them too, if the Order's fire had left anything of the boy to bury.

He thought about that. He thought about it the way you press a bruise to check whether it still hurts, knowing it will.

"I lost a son to them," he said. "You know that."

"I know."

"They said he was a warlock. He was fourteen. He could make flowers open out of season—that was all, that was everything he could do, and they built a pyre for it in the market square and made us watch." The words came out flat and even, the way he pulled hides, grain side up, working steadily so the knife wouldn't catch. "I stood there and I watched, and I did nothing, because doing nothing was survivable. I've been doing nothing for eleven years, Isandro. I'm very good at it. It's the only craft I have besides leather."

"Odrik—"

"Let me finish. I've earned a finish." He drew a breath that hurt going in. "Yesterday, Captain Rellane came to the tannery. Sat in my workshop among the stinking vats with her grey coat and her courteous voice, and asked me about my neighbor. Asked if I'd noticed anything unusual. Strange visitors. Unusual smells. Words I didn't understand." He met Isandro's eyes. "And I told her no. I lied to an Ashen captain with her hand on my shoulder, and she smiled at me and thanked me for my honesty, and I went home and was sick behind my own house like a boy."

Silence. Somewhere out front, the wind worried the shutter of the shop, a small persistent knocking, like a visitor who would not leave.

"So understand me," Odrik said. "When I say what I say next, know that it costs me nothing to say it and everything to mean it." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, the way he sat over a difficult hide. "I'm done surviving. It's a poor trade. Poor margins. I want in."

* * *

Isandro refused him twice. He counted. Once with anger, once with fear, and both times he watched her refuse him and understood that she was refusing herself more than him—that every ally she accepted was another throat she'd have to answer for, another name on the list she kept somewhere behind her eyes.

The third time, she didn't refuse. She just looked at him for a long moment, and then at the child, and something in her face gave way—not broke, but gave way, the way ice gives way in spring, reluctantly and all at once.

"There's a ledger," she said. "In your counting room. Debts. You showed me once, years ago—every favor owed you in Greyhollow, every coin, every obligation. You said a tanner's real wealth was paper."

"Aye."

"Burn it."

He stared at her. "Why?"

"Because if Rellane comes for you—and she will, eventually, because you're mine now and mine is what she collects—she'll read it. Every name in it becomes a lever. Every debt becomes a confession waiting to be sold." Isandro's voice was quiet and terribly certain. "You want to help me? Then become a man with nothing anyone can take. No debts owed to you means no debts anyone can call. Burn the ledger, Odrik. Tonight. Before you change your mind, because you will change your mind, everyone does."

* * *

He went home through the early dark with the snow beginning—not the real snow yet, but the trial snow, the scattered flakes that fall like a warning shot. The lamps of Greyhollow were being lit along the high street, and beyond the harbor wall the sea was the color of iron filings, and somewhere in the city a bell was ringing the hour in a voice gone hoarse with age.

His counting room smelled of ink and tallow. The ledger sat where it always sat, in the drawer beneath the scale, bound in calfskin he had tanned himself, young and supple, from a beast he remembered.

He opened it. He hadn't meant to read it, but a man doesn't burn a book unread; that's a different sin entirely.

Eleven years of names looked up at him. Master Corwin, who owed him four crowns and had paid in herring through two bad winters. Widow Sela, whose debt he'd forgiven the day her husband drowned but had never struck from the book, because striking it felt like closing a grave. The cooper Aldous. The ferryman's boys. Page after page of the small gravity that holds a neighborhood together—owings and holdings, favors banked like cordwood against a hard season. Thirty years of being a man others could lean on.

And there, in his own younger handwriting, dated the week after the pyre in the market square, a single line he'd forgotten writing:

*For Tam. Everything owed to me is owed to him. Collect it from the ones who took him.*

He sat with that a long while. The candle guttered. Outside, the trial snow thickened, finding its courage.

Then Odrik Vane rose, took the ledger to the hearth, and fed it to the fire page by page, not fast—slowly, letting each name go up in its own light, a small funeral for thirty years of careful accounting. The calfskin cover caught last and burned blue-green, and the smell was the smell of his trade, and he found he was weeping without any sound at all, the way men weep in rooms where no one taught them how.

When it was done he raked the ashes fine and mixed them with the cold cinders, and there was nothing left to find. No levers. No confessions. Just a man with empty drawers and clean hands, or as near to clean as a tanner's ever come.

He went back next door through the falling snow and knocked on Isandro's door, properly this time, the way friends knock.

She opened it with the child behind her, and he could see she'd been crying too, or close to it, and he loved her for pretending otherwise.

"It's done," he said. "Ashes. Now—" and here his voice did something unexpected, steadied itself on thirty years of hauling and scraping and standing at gravesides, "—now tell me what a keeper does, and what the Door needs, and where a man with strong arms and no obligations can be useful. Because I saw the way that girl looks at your floorboards, Isandro. Same way you do. Like something down there is calling her home."

Isandro's face went white as the snow gathering on his shoulders.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because I've lived beside the Hollow Door for seven years," Odrik said, "and I'm not deaf. Last night, during the storm, something under this street was singing. And this morning, Captain Rellane posted men at both ends of the lane—with shovels."
