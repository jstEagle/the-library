## 10. Beneath the Ice, a Voice

The storm came down off the Grey Teeth three days early, the way bad news does.

Isandro felt it before she heard it—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like old coins on the back of her tongue. The seal had been singing to her all evening, a thin high note beneath the floorboards that only she could hear, and by the time the first sleet rattled against the shop's shutters she already knew it was going to be a long night.

"Vess." She looked up from the luck-knot she was retying for the fourth time, her fingers too cold and too unsteady to get the pattern right. "Come away from the window."

The girl didn't move. She stood with both palms flat against the frost-ferned glass, watching the storm swallow the street lamp by lamp, and she was humming.

It was the same tune she always hummed—the one Isandro had spent two months refusing to name. It was not a child's song. It was the cadence of the wards, the slow descending phrase carved into the lintel of the Hollow Door in letters older than the kingdom, older than the road, older than the stone they were cut into. Vess hummed it the way other children breathed: without knowing, without effort, without mercy.

"Vess." Softer this time. "Please."

The girl turned. In the lamplight her eyes were the grey of deep water, and for a moment—just a moment—Isandro saw something look out of them that was not seven years old. Then the child blinked, and it was only Vess again, small and chapped-lipped and shivering in Odrik's borrowed sweater, and Isandro's heart broke a little more quietly than usual.

"Storm's coming," Vess said. She still spoke little of the common tongue, but she had learned the important words. *Storm. Hide. Quiet.* "Under the ice. It's angry tonight."

"I know," Isandro said. "That's why we're going downstairs."

* * *

She had prepared the antechamber years ago, back when preparation still felt like prudence instead of paranoia. Blankets. A candle stub. A week's worth of bread and hard cheese wrapped in oilcloth, because there were nights when the Door did not let you leave until it was done with you. She carried Vess down the hidden stair behind the hearth with the girl's arms locked around her neck and the girl's breath hot and frightened against her throat, and all the while the note beneath the world climbed higher, thinner, closer to breaking.

The antechamber was a low vault of black granite, older than any mason's art Isandro knew, its walls furred with frost that never melted even in high summer. At its far end stood the Hollow Door itself.

People in Greyhollow thought the name was a joke, or a superstition, or a keeper's flourish. They imagined some grand portal, iron-banded, dragon-carved. What Isandro tended was worse than that. The Door was a slab of grey stone perhaps nine feet tall, featureless except for the ring of script around its edge—and the script was hollow. Not engraved. *Hollowed*, each letter a channel cut clean through the stone, so that when you held a candle behind it—and no one had held a candle behind it in seven years, because Isandro did not dare—you would see the light come through the letters like eyes opening.

Tonight, without any candle at all, the letters were glowing.

A faint blue-white seeped up from the channels, pulsing in time with the high note, and the frost on the walls had begun to crawl, creeping inward from the corners in slow feathered patterns that Isandro recognized with a lurch as *writing*. Old Blood script, forming and dissolving, forming and dissolving, like a man talking in his sleep.

"No," she whispered. "No, no—we're not due. We're not due for another—" She stopped herself. The calendar meant nothing. It had never meant anything; it was just a comfort she'd built out of her mentor's notes, a way to make the unbearable feel scheduled. The seal cracked when it cracked. All her arithmetic had ever bought her was the illusion of control.

She settled Vess in the corner farthest from the Door, wrapped her in both blankets, and pressed the iron token into the girl's hands. "Hold this. Don't let go of it, whatever you hear. Whatever you *see*. Do you understand me?"

Vess nodded, solemn as a small priestess. Then, in the old tongue, soft enough that Isandro almost missed it: "*Keeper, don't listen with your heart.*"

Isandro went very still. "What did you say?"

But the girl only shook her head, the way she did when the words came from somewhere she couldn't point to, and pulled the blanket up under her chin.

*Don't listen with your heart.* That was Maren's phrasing. That was Maren's exact cadence, the way she'd said it a hundred times over seven years of training: *The thing below will learn your voice, Isandro, and then it will learn your loves, and then it will speak to you in the shape of everything you've ever wanted. Don't listen with your heart. Listen with your feet. If your feet want to walk toward the Door, that's how you know.*

Maren had been dead four years. Isandro had buried her under the rowan tree behind the shop, in a grave with no marker, because markers invited questions, and had recited the oath alone in the snow with her hands bleeding from the passing-on rite. Four years. And still, some nights, when the seal sang, she could have sworn—

The note broke.

It was not loud. That was the horror of it. Seven years of dread, and the crack came as gently as an icicle dropping from an eave—a single bright *tick* somewhere inside the stone, and then silence, and then the frost-writing on the walls stopped crawling and began, very slowly, to arrange itself into lines. Neat lines. Patient lines. Reading lines.

And the voice came up through the floor, through the granite, through the soles of Isandro's boots and the bones of her ankles, warm as banked coals.

"There you are, little wren. I wondered when you'd come down."

Isandro's whole body turned to ice that had nothing to do with the room.

She knew that voice. Of course she knew that voice. It was roughened with age and smoke, dry-humored, fond—it was the voice that had taught her the difference between a ward and a wall, the voice that had laughed at her the first time she'd vomited after the passing-on rite and said, *good, means it took*. It was Maren's voice, every rasp and warmth of it, rising through the stone as if the dead woman were sitting just on the other side of the Door with her back against it, the way she used to sit against the kitchen stove.

"You're not her," Isandro said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Whatever you are. You're not her."

"Mm." A pause, and in the pause the frost-script flickered, and Isandro understood with sick clarity that the thing below was *tasting* her words, learning from them, adjusting. "Aren't I? Who taught you the warming charm for chilblains? Who told you the fishwives' knots should always be tied left-handed, because the harbor folk distrust anything that comes easy? Who held your hair back your first winter, when the seal poured itself through you and you thought you'd die of it? I remember all of it, wren. Every year of you. I kept them safe for you, down here, where nothing rots."

"Stop it." The candle flame leaned hard sideways though there was no draft. "Stop it, or I'll—"

"You'll what?" Gentle. So gentle. "Rebuke me? Renew the binding? Oh, love. Come and put your hand on the stone. Feel how thin it's gotten. You've been holding a door shut with paper and prayers for seven years, and you know it, and you're so tired. You're so tired, Isandro. You were tired the day I gave you the oath and you've been tired every day since, and for what? For a city that would burn you alive if it knew what you were? For charms made of string?"

In the corner, Vess began to cry—not loudly, just a thin continuous sound, the way children cry when they are trying not to be heard—and something in the voice below shifted, softened, went tender in a way that was infinitely worse.

"And now you have *her*," it said. "Oh, wren. Oh, you poor brave fool. You think you're keeping her safe. You're keeping her in a cage with a starving thing, and the thing is patient, and the thing has waited four hundred years, and it is so, so hungry. But it doesn't have to be. Here is what I offer, and I offer it once, and I offer it true, by the root and the crown and the cold: give me the key. Open the Door, set her on my side of it, and I will seal it again myself—from this side—and your city will never feel so much as a draft. No witch-hunters. No Rellane with her ledgers and her ash. No mobs. Your Odrik keeps his tannery. Your fishwives keep their luck. And you—" the voice caught, and the catch was perfect, it was flawless, it was Maren's real grief worn like a borrowed coat, "—you finally get to sleep."

Isandro realized she had taken three steps toward the Door.

Her feet had decided without her. That was the thing Maren had warned her about—that was exactly the thing—and her feet were on the frost-slick stone and her hand was rising and the blue light through the hollow letters was warm on her palm like skin, and somewhere very far away a child was crying, and the voice was saying *yes, yes, that's it, just a little further, I know how heavy it's been—*

"*Don't listen with your heart,*" said Vess.

Not crying anymore. Standing, blankets fallen, one small bare foot planted on the granite, the iron token raised in both fists like a lantern. And her voice when she spoke the old tongue was not a child's voice at all—it was layered, it was many voices, it was a choir compressed into one small throat—and the frost-writing on the walls flared white and *shrieked*, every line of it curling away from the girl as if burned.

Isandro's hand stopped an inch from the stone.

An inch. She could see her own reflection in the Door's surface, warped and blue-lit, and behind her reflection another shape, vast and dim and wrong, wearing Maren's outline the way a puppet wears strings.

She snatched her hand back and stumbled away, and her legs nearly gave, and she caught herself against the wall with the frost biting through her sleeve, and she said, aloud, in the common tongue, in the plainest words she owned:

"No. Whatever you are—no. You don't get her. You don't get my grief. You don't get to wear her face in the dark where I'm alone, because I am *not alone*, and you have just shown me exactly what you are: something that can only imitate love, never survive it."

Silence. Long silence. The blue glow guttered, steadied, dimmed.

Then the voice came one last time, and it was not Maren's anymore. It was not anyone's. It was the sound of a great depth of cold water speaking, and it said:

"The offer stands, keeper. Winter is long. And you will need to know what was promised, before you choose. Ask your Order's ashes. Ask the burned names. We kept our half."

The light died. The frost-script slid apart into meaningless feathers. The high note resumed beneath the floorboards—but lower now, quieter, like something settling back to wait.

*We kept our half.*

Isandro slid down the wall until she was sitting, and Vess crossed the room and climbed into her lap without asking, the way children do, and Isandro held her and shook and did not let either of them see it.

Because the thing below had made a mistake. Grief had made her careless, but anger made her precise, and underneath the terror a single sentence was repeating itself with the clean terrible clarity of a bell:

*Ask the burned names.*

There was an archive beneath the Ashen Order's chapterhouse. Everyone in Greyhollow knew it existed; no one living admitted to knowing what was in it. Maren had mentioned it exactly once, drunk on winter mead, with a bitterness Isandro had been too young to read: *They burned the names, girl, but they kept the papers. Kings always do.*

The seal had cracked tonight. It would crack again, sooner, and harder, and next time Isandro might not have a choir-voiced child standing behind her.

Which meant that before the next storm, she needed the original terms of the seal—and the only place left in the world that might hold them was the one building in Greyhollow where discovery meant fire.

She kissed the top of Vess's head, tasting salt and woodsmoke, and above them the storm hammered on, and somewhere across the sleeping city a witch-hunter was writing in a ledger by candlelight, and Isandro Yarrow began, silently, to plan a burglary.
