## 11. The Archive of Burned Names

**Isandro**

The Ashen Order kept its archive in the old salt-cathedral on Tallow Row, a building the city had outgrown and then forgotten. I had walked past it a thousand times without looking—seven years of not-looking, which is its own kind of discipline—and now I stood across the street in the blue hour before dawn, watching frost crawl up its black windows, and wondered how I had ever believed myself a careful woman.

Careful women did not break into witch-hunters' houses.

"You're doing the thing," Odrik said beside me. He was a mountain of a man even hunched into his borrowed coat, and he had wrapped a scarf over half his face so that only his eyes showed, which did nothing to disguise him and everything to make him look like a burglar. "The thing where you stare at a wall like it's going to apologize."

"It's not a wall. It's a ward." I could feel it from here, faint as a headache behind the eyes—a lattice of ash-sigils laid into the mortar, keyed to the Order's own blood. "And it isn't going to apologize. It's going to scream."

"Then we'd best be quiet about it." He shifted the satchel on his shoulder. Inside it were my tools: chalk that wasn't chalk, a knife that had never cut bread, three luck-knots I had unknotted and reworked until they held something older than luck. Everything I was supposed to have burned seven years ago. Everything I had hidden beneath my floorboards like a coward with a conscience.

The storm had come down off the mountains two nights ago, and the seal had cracked, and the thing below had spoken to me in Maren's voice, and I had refused it—barely, by a thread, by the width of a prayer. But refusal was not an answer. Refusal was just holding your breath longer than the other fellow. I needed to know what I was actually holding shut. I needed the original terms.

Every gatekeeper learned the litany: *The war is ended. The Door is sealed. The kingdoms won.* Maren had taught it to me with her hand pressed flat against the Hollow Door, her voice steady as bedrock, and I had repeated it for seven years because the alternative was madness. But the entity below wore Maren's face now, and it had said things—*ask them what we traded, little keeper; ask them what they took*—and a lie that can be whispered back at you through a crack in the world stops being comfort and starts being a question.

So: the archive. The Order burned what it condemned, but it never burned its own records. Rellane would know that. Rellane, who had turned Greyhollow inside out for a week hunting living script, who had stood in my shop with snow melting on her shoulders and asked me questions with kind, terrible eyes. She and her hunters slept at the cathedral now. Which meant we were not breaking into an empty house.

"We go in through the undercroft," I said. "The old salt cellars run under the whole row. There's a stair from the cellar into the nave cloister, and the archive sits behind the cloister."

"And the wards?"

"Are keyed to Order blood." I pulled off my glove. My breath hung white between us. "Which is why you're going to do the cutting, and I'm going to do the lying."

Odrik looked at my bare hand, then at my face, and whatever he saw there made him go still. "Isandro. What does it cost?"

"Less than the alternative." I smiled, and felt it sit wrong on my face, like a charm knotted by someone else's fingers. "Give me your knife."

* * *

**Odrik**

I had known Isandro Yarrow for six years, and in six years she had lied to me exactly once, and I had let her, because some lies are kindnesses. This was not one of those nights. This was the night after the lie, when the truth comes due with interest.

She took my skinning knife—the good one, bone-handled, the one I'd used since I was fourteen—and opened a line across her own palm without flinching, which told me she'd done worse to herself before and would again. Then she painted the blood along the mortar seam beneath the salt-cellar stair, quick and precise, muttering words I didn't catch and didn't want to. The air changed. It went thick, the way it does before summer lightning, and somewhere above us a bell that hadn't rung in thirty years gave a single low note, soft as a finger laid on a string.

"That's it?" I whispered.

"That's it pretending we belong here." She wrapped her hand in linen, clumsy with her teeth. "It'll hold an hour. Maybe less if there's a reader awake upstairs."

"And if there is?"

"Then we're two citizens who got lost looking for the privy." Her eyes said we both knew how that story ended. Witch-fires in Tanner's Square. I had watched one burn when I was a boy. You don't forget the smell.

The cloister stair came up behind a screen of rotted cedar, and beyond it the archive waited like a throat.

I had imagined shelves. Books, ledgers, dust. What I found instead stopped me on the threshold with my heart slamming. The room was round, and every inch of its walls was covered in names—thousands of them, tens of thousands, inked directly onto stone in a hundred different hands, some fine as spider silk, some gouged deep enough to bleed shadow. They spiraled upward toward a ceiling lost in dark. Burned names, I thought. Every witch the Order had ever taken. Not a record. A ledger of debts, written where no debtor could ever settle them.

"They write them down first," Isandro said quietly. "Before the fire. So the name survives the burning. So God—or whatever's listening—knows exactly who they killed." She touched one name near her shoulder, gentle as touching a wound. "Maren's is here. Third spiral, low. I've never been able to read it without—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Terms of the seal. Pre-kingdom documents. They'd be in the iron chests. Help me look."

We worked fast, hands cold, lantern hooded. Chests of treaty-letters, of land grants, of correspondence between dead chancellors. And then my fingers found a box that was wrong—heavy, seamless, no lock but a sigil burned into the lid, and cold. Cold like river-bottom cold, like the cold that had come early this winter and wouldn't leave.

"Isandro."

She came to me, and when she saw the box her breath went ragged. "That's it. That has to be it." She set her wounded palm against the sigil, and I watched her jaw tighten as the ward drank from her, and the lid rose with a sound like a long-held sigh.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay a scroll. And beneath the scroll, a strip of iron, broken clean across, its edges worn smooth as if by centuries of holding.

Isandro went very still.

"I've seen the other half of that," she said. Her voice had gone strange, flat, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant. "It's hanging around a child's neck."

* * *

**Isandro**

I unrolled the scroll with hands that would not stop shaking, and I read, and the floor of my life tilted beneath me.

It was not a victory record. There was no triumph in it anywhere, none of the brass-and-banners language the temples used, none of *the Old Powers cast down, the kingdoms delivered*. It was a contract. Dry. Careful. Terrified. Two parties named in the old script: the Thrones of Men on one side, and on the other, simply, *the Court Below the Ice*. And between them, terms.

*The war ends not in conquest but in exchange.*

*The Court Below surrenders the field and yields its claim upon the sunlit world, forever.*

*The Thrones of Men surrender magic entire—every craft, every art, every opening of the eye—forever, in themselves and in their heirs, unto the last generation.*

*As pledge and bond between them: the Court gives a child of the Old Blood, to live among men, that each side may hold hostage to the other's faith.*

*A key, given in trust, to be neither used nor harmed.*

*When both sides keep faith, the Crown may be made whole. When either breaks it, the Door answers.*

Below that, signatures. Dozens of them. Kings I knew from schoolroom histories. Chancellors. And at the bottom, in a hand I recognized because I had seen it carved above the Hollow Door a thousand mornings—names in the pre-kingdom tongue, the Old Blood's own, ending in a single word I suddenly understood for the first time: *Vess*. Not a name. A title. *Key.*

"Oh," I said. Just that. Just a small sound, like something breaking in a far room.

"What?" Odrik's hand landed heavy on my shoulder. "What does it say? Tell me plain, Isandro. Plain words."

"The war never ended," I heard myself say. "It was *settled*. Like a debt. Like a marriage." I looked up at the walls of names, thousands of names, all the people the Order had burned for practicing the craft their own grandfathers had signed away. "We didn't win. We traded. We gave up magic—all of it, everyone, forever—and they gave us a child to hold. That's the seal. That's what I've been tending. Not a lock. A *hostage arrangement*."

"Vess." Odrik said her name softly, the way you'd handle something hot. "They sent her back."

"Or she was stolen. Or she ran. Or—" I pressed the heel of my bandaged hand against my eyes. Seven years. Seven years of believing I guarded the city against the dark, and all this time I had been a jailer's daughter keeping watch over a treaty nobody remembered signing, holding a door shut on a bargain whose terms I hadn't even known. Maren had known. Maren must have known, and she had taught me the litany anyway—*the kingdoms won*—because the truth was unbearable, or because she thought I couldn't carry it, or because a lie kindly meant is still a chain.

And the iron token. Half a crown. Vess wore half of it around her neck, and the other half sat in this chest, cold as the bottom of a well, and the entity below had said: *ask them what we traded.*

It hadn't wanted the door open. It had wanted the *ledger balanced*.

"Someone broke faith," I said slowly. Working it aloud, because saying it made it real and real things could be fought. "The treaty says the Door answers when either side breaks. The seal started failing years ago—Maren's last letter called it 'a settling,' nothing more. But it cracked the night the storms came. Something changed. Either the Court Below did something—" 

"Or we did," Odrik said.

I looked at him.

"Witch-hunters," he said. "Burnings. An order that writes ten thousand names on a wall and lights the fires underneath. Your Order, Isandro. Their grandfathers promised to give up magic forever, and instead they built an industry out of taking it away from everyone else." His voice was low and steady, tanner-steady, the voice of a man who had spent his life deciding what hides were worth saving. "Maybe the Door doesn't count signatures. Maybe it counts *faithfulness*. And maybe a kingdom that burns children in squares stopped keeping faith a long time ago."

The lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft. In the silence I could hear it: faint, far beneath the salt-cellars, beneath the foundations, beneath the frozen ground—a sound like breathing. Or like waiting.

Above us, in the nave, a floorboard creaked.

* * *

**Odrik**

I put my hand over Isandro's mouth before she could gasp, and pulled us both down behind the iron chests, into the dark between the wall of names and the shelving. My heart was going like a hammer on hide, but my hands were steady. Thirty years of working carcasses teaches you that panic is just noise, and noise gets you hurt.

Boots on stone. More than one pair. Lantern-light swung thin and gold through the cloister doorway, laying bars across the floor.

"—swear the bell moved, Captain. One note. Low."

"Bells settle in cold," said a woman's voice, and I knew it, and beside me Isandro went rigid as a stretched hide. Rellane. Close enough that I could hear the wool of her coat whisper. "But check the archive. And if the ward-seal on the undercroft stair shows blood, wake the garrison before you wake anyone else."

"Yes, Captain."

Footsteps crossed the nave toward us. Isandro's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it—not for comfort. For permission. I could feel her gathering whatever was left in her, the last of the craft she'd sworn was dead, getting ready to spend it buying us seconds.

And then Rellane stopped.

She had reached the wall of names. We could see her now through the gap between chests: a tall shape in grey, lantern raised, her face tipped up toward the spiraling dark. For a long moment she didn't move. When she spoke, she wasn't speaking to us. She wasn't even really speaking to her soldier.

"My grandmother's name should be on this wall," she said. "By rights. She had the sight—small things, finding lost sheep, knowing when the ice would take a boat. The Order gave her a choice her whole village watched her make." Her voice was level, almost gentle, and that was the worst part, because it wasn't cruelty. It was grief wearing armor. "She chose the fire so her daughters wouldn't inherit it. That's what I tell myself the burning is. Mercy, forward through time."

The soldier said nothing. Neither did we.

"So whoever you are," Rellane said, still to the wall, still to the dead, "whoever came down here tonight and bled on my wards—you'd better be looking for the same thing I'm starting to look for. Because I've read the counter-sigils on the Hollow Door now. I've matched them against the forbidden shelf. And I know someone in this city wants that Door open, and it isn't the witches we've been burning."

Her lantern turned. Light swept the shelving, the chests, the gap where two people crouched holding their breath—

—and stopped on the open iron box, on the broken crown-half gleaming inside it.

For three heartbeats, nobody in the archive of burned names breathed at all.

Then Rellane said, very quietly, in a voice that had stepped off the edge of everything she believed: "Where is the child?"
