## 12. Half a Crown

The token did not cool in my hand.

I had set it on the table while I lit the lamp, and when I came back to it the iron was warm as a held coin, though the shop had gone cold enough that my breath showed. Seven years I had kept charms that were only knots and string and small honest lies, and never once had anything in this room carried heat of its own. Heat was a thing magic spent. This thing *held* it, the way a stone holds the day's sun long into dark.

Vess sat across from me with her hands folded in her lap like a child at lessons, watching the token with an expression I could not read—too old for wonder, too young for grief. The firelight from the little stove caught the crown-shape of it: five points, but broken, sheared clean at the right side as if some smith had struck it off mid-forging. The cut face was smooth. Too smooth. Iron does not cut that way unless it wants to.

"Where did you get this?" I asked, in the old tongue, because we had fallen into speaking it together the way two people who have found one island fall into sharing it.

She answered the way she always answered now, in that voice like water over stones, the pre-kingdom syllables rising out of her without effort or accent. *"It was given. It was mine to carry."*

"Given by whom?"

Her brow furrowed. She reached for words the way a child reaches for fruit on a high branch, and what she brought down was: *"The ones who wait. The ones under. They said—carry it up. Carry it to the keeper. They said the keeper would know what it opens."*

My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own telling: my hands did not shake. Everything else in me shook, but the hands held, because seven years of tending a failing seal teaches you that shaking is a luxury paid for later.

"And do you know what it opens?"

She looked at me then with those grey eyes, eyes the color of the sea before weather, and said, *"No. Only that it is half of something. Half of a crown. And that they are very sad about it."*

*They are very sad about it.* A child's phrasing, and yet no adult I had ever met—not my mentor Maren on her deathbed, not the archivists whose burned names I had read by candle-smoke in the Order's stolen ledger—had ever described the thing beneath the Hollow Door so precisely. Sadness given power. Grief wearing the shape of a court. I had learned that much in the archive, and I had told myself I understood it.

I understood nothing. Understanding is a door you think you've opened, and then you find another door behind it, and behind that one, a child holding half a crown.

* * *

I sent her to bed in the back room—the room that had been Maren's, that had been mine, that was now hers by the simple arithmetic of need—and I sat alone with the token and the lamp and the truth working through me like cold water through a hull.

Here is what the archive had taught me, the night Odrik and I broke into the Order's burned-names vault with our breath smoking and our hearts hammering like apprentices' fists on a door: the war did not end. Wars end with victors, with treaties signed in ink that dries, with bells rung from towers. What ended beneath Greyhollow was not a war but a *bargain*, and bargains are a different animal entirely. A bargain has teeth on both sides of it.

The Old Blood gave a hostage. A child of their line, blooded, keyed to the Door, carrying half the Hollow Crown—the crown that had been the seal's first anchor, before the kingdoms melted down their oaths and called it peace. In exchange, the kingdoms gave up magic. Not renounced it. *Gave it up*, surrendered it into the seal, starved the world above of the very stuff that had burned towers and salted fields and made the last century a howling place. No more witches. No more wonders. One honest generation, the treaty said, and then the terms could be renegotiated by whoever still stood.

One generation. It had been eleven generations. The kingdoms had simply—stopped counting. Stopped remembering. Built a religion of ash and rope around the surrender and called the burning of witches justice rather than the keeping of a promise so old its signatories were dust. Rellane's order was not hunting evil. It was enforcing, blindly, savagely, a clause in a contract no living soul had read.

And the other side? The Old Blood had waited below, patient as roots, for the term to come due. Eleven generations is nothing to things that live in ice and stone. They had kept their half. They had kept the child.

Except—

Except the child was here. Above. Barefoot in my shop, sleeping in Maren's bed, clutching half a crown and saying *they told me to find the keeper.*

Hostages are not returned early. Hostages are not sent up with tokens and instructions and a child's blessing of sadness. Hostages are kept until the exchange, and the exchange happens at the Door, and both parties stand present to make it. That was the shape of every bargain ever struck beneath crowns, hollow or otherwise.

So either the Old Blood had broken faith—or they claimed the kingdoms had.

I turned the token over. On its underside, so faint I had to tilt it against the lamplight, ran a line of script in the old tongue. Not carved. *Grown*, if iron can be said to grow anything—the letters raised like frost-ferns, delicate as the veins in a leaf. I read them three times before the meaning settled into me like a stone settling into mud.

*RETURNED FOR FAITH BROKEN. THE KEEPER WILL KNOW THE DEBT.*

The keeper will know the debt.

I laughed. I am not proud of it; it came out of me like something escaping a trap, short and ugly in the empty shop. *Know the debt.* Of course. Of course they would say that. Because I didn't know it. Because Maren had died with the truth locked behind her teeth, passing me an oath instead of an explanation—a lamp instead of a map. *Tend the Door, Isandro. Feed it your small magics. Never let it hunger. Never ask it questions.* Seven years of feeding a fire I was forbidden to look into, and now the fire had sent up a child to tell me the hearth belonged to someone else.

Faith broken. Whose faith? Broken how? Broken when?

There was no one left to ask. That was the architecture of the lie, I realized—that was its genius, if a lie can be said to have genius. Everyone who knew the original terms was dead. The kingdoms had burned their own memory along with their witches; the Order's archive survived only because someone inside it had committed treason to preserve truth. And below, the ones who remembered had waited so long that waiting itself had become their nature. Eleven generations of silence on both sides of a door, each side certain the other had cheated.

And in the middle, me. The last link. The hinge. The keeper who had inherited a duty built on a sentence she'd never been allowed to finish reading.

* * *

Sleep did not come. Around the third hour past midnight I heard Vess crying in the back room—not loudly, not the way children cry for comfort, but the low, worn crying of someone who has cried herself out of tears many nights running and is doing it now mostly from habit. I went to her.

She was curled facing the wall, and when I touched her shoulder she flinched, then stilled, then turned. In the dark her eyes caught what little light there was and gave it back wrong, the way ice gives light back.

*"Did I do something bad?"* she asked. *"Coming here. Did I break something?"*

The question went through me like a hook through soft flesh. A child, asking if her rescue—if her *flight*—was a sin. What had they told her below, in that frozen court where grief wore faces? What stories had been poured into her to make her carry an iron crown-half across a kingdom that burns people like her?

"No," I said, in her tongue, and my voice cracked on the word, which I suppose was honest at least. "You haven't broken anything. You came to the right place. You found the keeper."

*"Then why are you sad?"*

Children see everything. It is their one great magic, and no treaty has ever managed to burn it out of the world.

"I'm sad," I said carefully, "because I just learned that the story I was told my whole life isn't the whole story. And learning that hurts, even when the rest of the story is good."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then: *"That happened to me too. When I was small. Before I could remember properly. Someone told me the sky was a lid, and then they took me up once, to the edge of the ice, and I saw it wasn't."* She paused. *"I cried for a season. But I liked the sky better after. Even though it was bigger and scarier."*

I gathered her against me, this strange, grave, impossible child, and felt her heartbeat—quick, mortal, ordinary—and thought: *they gave you up, or you were stolen, or you were sent, and whichever it is, you are the only innocent party in an eleven-generation quarrel, and everyone with power intends to spend you like coin.*

Not while I breathed.

* * *

Dawn came grey and late, the way dawn comes now, the winter pressing down on the city like a thumb on a wound. I opened the shop because opening the shop is what I do, because routine is the armor I have worn for seven years, and because if Rellane's hunters were watching—and they were; I had seen the ash-marked man across the lane twice yesterday—then the charm-seller must be seen selling charms.

Marta the fishwife came for her second luck-knot of the season, haggled me down two coppers, and left wrapped in wool and small false hope. A carter bought a knot for his axle. An old man I did not know bought nothing and looked too long at the back-room curtain, and I smiled at him until he left, and hated how easily the smile came now, how practiced the lying had become.

When the street emptied I took the token to the floorboards.

Beneath them, in the space where I had hidden my real work for seven years—the true charms, the ward-threads, the offerings that fed the seal—lay the ledger Maren had left me. Her hand, cramped and fading. I had read it a hundred times. I read it again now, looking for what I had never known to look for: a mention of the Crown. A mention of halves. A mention of debt.

Near the end, in an entry dated the winter before her death, I found this:

*The Door dreams of a crown tonight. It has not dreamed of the crown in forty years. I do not know what waking means for a thing like that, but I know what dreaming means. It means the other side remembers. God forgive us all, the other side remembers, and we have forgotten so thoroughly that our children burn the proof.*

We have forgotten so thoroughly.

I sat back on my heels among the hidden charms and understood, finally, the full shape of my inheritance. Maren had known. Not everything—maybe not even much—but enough to be afraid, and she had passed the fear to me unlabeled, the way you pass a hot coal wrapped in cloth, saying only *carry it, don't open it.* She had chosen ignorance for me. Perhaps kindly. Perhaps because knowledge would have made me refuse the oath, and someone had to hold the Door, and the dying do not have the luxury of honesty.

Or perhaps—darker thought, and I let it sit in me like a swallowed stone—perhaps she had known exactly what the debt was, and had decided I should pay it without being asked. That the keeper's life was always meant to be the final installment. That *reseal the door with your own life* was not my invention, born of desperation, but the original term, written in a hand I had never seen, in a treaty I had never read, agreed to by keepers dead eleven generations before my birth.

Grief and fury arrived together, the way they do, indistinguishable, a single hot weight behind the breastbone. Grief for Maren, who may have loved me and used me in the same breath. Fury at the dead of both sides, who had built a machine out of a child and a city and a lie, and then died conveniently, leaving the maintenance to fools.

And underneath the fury, colder and quieter, a third thing. Recognition.

Because the token was warm. Because the script had grown like frost. Because the Door had dreamed. Whatever had broken faith—whenever, however—the other side was done waiting. The hostage was returned. The key was delivered. The message was on my table in letters of raised iron:

*THE KEEPER WILL KNOW THE DEBT.*

And I did know it, now. Or I knew its shape. Somewhere below the Hollow Door lay the other half of the Hollow Crown, and the two halves wanted each other the way rivers want the sea, and when they met, either the seal would be remade or the world would learn what eleven generations of stored grief did when it finally stood up and stretched.

There was a third option—I could feel it out there, unrecorded, unwritten, the option no keeper had ever been brave or foolish enough to seek. But finding it would mean going down. Through the Door. Into the ice. With a bleeding seal above me, witch-hunters in my street, a child-key asleep in my back room, and a neighbor whose loyalty I had already spent one lie too many against.

I closed the ledger. I put the token in the iron box that had held Maren's strongest wards, and even the box seemed warmer for holding it.

Above the shop, snow began—the first true snow, fat and silent, falling on Greyhollow like ash falling upward. Somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour, and beneath the ringing, so faint I might have imagined it, I heard the Door hum.

It was humming the same phrase, over and over, in the old tongue.

*Half and half. Half and half. Come and make it whole.*
