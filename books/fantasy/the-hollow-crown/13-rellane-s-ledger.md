## 13. Rellane's Ledger

The Ashen Order kept its archive beneath the garrison chapel, down a stair that smelled of cold iron and old smoke, and in eleven years of service Captain Maren Rellane had never once been given the key.

She had never asked for it either. That was the arrangement: hunters hunted, archivists remembered, and neither looked too long at what the other carried. Her warrant gave her authority over every witch, warlock, and worker of forbidden script in Greyhollow and its hinterlands. It did not give her authority over paper. Paper belonged to the Order's past, and the Order preferred its past buried deeper than any witch she'd ever burned.

But the sigils on the Hollow Door's outer stone had changed all that.

She stood now before the archive door at the hour when even the chapel above was dark, holding the lantern high so its light caught the lock. The door was oak banded with bronze, older than the kingdom, older perhaps than the city it served. And this morning, when she had come to report her findings to Archivist Corvane, the hinges had been freshly oiled—and the dust along the threshold showed the print of boots. Two sets. One large, one smaller, both recent, both going *in*.

Corvane had gone white as tallow and sworn no one had entered. He had sworn it twice, which was once more than an honest man needed.

So here she was, past midnight, with a stolen key ring taken from the sleeping sexton's belt and a lantern turned low to nothing but a coin of light. She told herself it was procedure. A breach of the archive was a breach of the Order itself, and breaches were her trade. She told herself that, and the telling sat poorly in her chest, like a stone swallowed whole.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

Inside, the air was dry as a mummy's mouth. Shelves rose into darkness on every side, stacked with ledgers bound in grey leather, each spine stamped with a year going back further than she could read by lantern-light. The Order had burned books for three centuries—burned them in squares, in courtyards, in the snow, while crowds sang hymns to keep their courage up—but it had never burned *these*. These were the accounts. What was taken. From whom. In whose name.

Rellane walked the central aisle slowly, letting her boots whisper on the flagstones, and found the disturbance within ten minutes.

A shelf near the far wall, in the section marked with the oldest sigil—the pre-kingdom glyph that looked like a crown seen through water—had been pulled out and pushed back crooked. Dust lay thick everywhere except there. Three ledgers had been moved. One lay open on the reading stand at the aisle's end, as if whoever had come had wanted it found—or had simply run out of night.

She set her lantern beside it and read.

*Terms of Cessation, being the Accord concluded between the Crowns of Men and the Court Below, in the year the ice came early.*

Her breath fogged despite herself. The chapel above was cold, but this was another kind of cold, one that seemed to rise out of the page.

She had expected theft. She had expected heresy—some grimoire of bindings, some witch's recipe for unmaking wards. Instead she found a treaty. An *accord*. Words like *concluded*, like *terms*, like *both parties agreeing*, and every one of them wrong, because there had been no agreement. There had been a war, and the war had ended in victory, and the victory was why Greyhollow existed at all. That was the first lesson every child in the city learned before they learned their letters: *the witches lost.*

She read on.

The language was formal, legal, worn smooth by centuries of clerical hands adding marginal notes. But the shape of it came through clearly enough to stop her heart.

The Old Blood had surrendered something. Not territory—they had none left to surrender. Not weapons. They had given up *a child*. A living key, born under the Door, blood-bound to the seal, to be held in trust by the kingdoms for as long as the truce endured. In exchange—

In exchange, the kingdoms had given up magic. All of it. Everywhere. Forever.

Rellane read the clause three times. *And the Crowns of Men shall suffer no working within their borders, nor teach, nor suffer teaching, upon pain of the same forfeiture, that the balance be kept and neither side hold advantage over the other.* It wasn't a punishment. It had never been a punishment. The witch-laws—the pyres, the hunts, the Order itself, her whole life's architecture—were not the spoils of victory.

They were the *payment*.

She gripped the edge of the reading stand until her knuckles ached. Somewhere above her, the chapel bell ticked against the hour and did not ring; the sexton slept, and the key ring in her pocket weighed like a sin.

*Both sides disarmed,* she thought. *That was the peace. Not conquest. Balance.* And if that was true, then every witch the Order had burned for three hundred years had not died protecting the peace.

They had died *breaking* it.

* * *

She did not know how long she stood there. Long enough for the lantern oil to gutter and need trimming. Long enough for her legs to remember they were hers. When she finally moved, it was to turn the pages backward, toward the signatures, because treaties lived or died in their names.

There were nine. Nine marks at the foot of the accord, some signed in fine clerk's script, some pressed as thumbprints in wax gone the color of dried blood. Six belonged to men whose titles she recognized from history lessons: kings, regents, a prince-bishop. Two bore names in the Old Tongue, rendered phonetically by some careful hand, followed by the notation *of the Court Below, speaking for those who remain.*

The ninth signature stopped her breathing.

It was a woman's name. Written small, in a hand that slanted leftward, hurried but precise, the kind of hand that had learned to write quickly because there was never enough candle. Beneath it, in the same ink, a title: *Warden of Greyhollow, consenting for the city and its people.*

And beneath the title, where the other signatories had written only their names, this woman had added four words, cramped into the margin as though she'd fought the clerk for the space:

*I am sorry. Forgive us.*

Rellane knew that handwriting.

She had known it her whole life without knowing she knew it—known it the way you know the smell of bread baking, the way you know a lullaby sung before memory begins. It hung framed behind glass in her mother's parlor. It filled the frontispiece of the family psalter. It was scratched, faded, half-legible, into the headstone in the garrison cemetery where she had laid flowers every Frostfall since she was six years old, standing on tiptoe while her mother pointed and said, *that was your grandmother, little wren. She kept the city safe.*

Edda Rellane. Warden of Greyhollow. Hero of the Cessation, the histories called her—the woman who had held the gates during the last winter of the war, who had starved beside her people rather than treat with the enemy below, who had earned the family its coat of arms and its place in the Order's honors.

*Consenting for the city and its people.*

Maren Rellane put out her hand and touched the ink, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child, and felt the floor tilt under her.

Her grandmother had signed away the magic. Had signed away *everything*—every hedge-charm, every birthing-song, every charm against fever that grandmothers had whispered over cradles since before there were cradles. Had agreed that her own descendants would hunt and burn anyone who remembered otherwise. Had written *forgive us* in a margin and then gone home to raise children who would never know.

And Rellane understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, why the Order kept this archive locked. Why no hunter was ever given the key. Why the past stayed buried deeper than any witch.

Because the treaty didn't tell the story the pyres were built on. The treaty told the story the pyres were built to *hide*.

* * *

She thought of her mother then, absurdly—her mother in the parlor, polishing the frame around Edda Rellane's letter, saying, *she did what had to be done, wren. Someone always has to do what has to be done.* And Rellane had built her whole self inside that sentence. Had joined the Order at sixteen because someone had to do what had to be done. Had stood at pyres with her jaw set and her eyes dry because feeling was a luxury the peace could not afford. Had crossed half the kingdom chasing rumors of living script because somewhere, someone was trying to undo what her grandmother's generation had paid for in fire.

She had believed she was carrying on her grandmother's work.

She had been carrying on her grandmother's *crime*.

No—not crime. That word was too easy, and everything in her recoiled from easy. Her grandmother had been starving. They had all been starving; the histories said the last winter of the war had killed one soul in three. A woman backed against annihilation signs what she must and prays her grandchildren never have to read it. Rellane could imagine it too well: the candle burning low, the clerk waiting, the pen shaking, the margin words squeezed in like a confession smuggled past a guard.

*I am sorry. Forgive us.*

But then—then the other thought arrived, the one she could not put down.

If the kingdoms had promised to give up magic forever, and the Old Blood had given up a child forever, then the peace was a scale with two pans. And for seven years—for exactly seven years, the length of time the Door's keeper had tended her shop of harmless charms—someone had been claiming the scale was tipped. Someone had been hunting the Old Blood's remnant, breaking faith in secret while wearing the Order's grey coat. And if the counter-sigils scratched into the Hollow Door's stone matched records in *this* archive—if, as her own field report stated, the marks were of Order provenance—then the betrayal wore a face she might have saluted.

The hunters were being hunted from inside their own house.

Or worse: the house itself had decided the treaty was inconvenient, and had never told the hounds.

Rellane closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had thought they would shake. Eleven years of discipline does not dissolve in a single night, whatever the ballads say about revelation. But beneath the steadiness, something had cracked along a fault line she hadn't known was there, and she could hear it settling, grain by grain, like ice on a river before the thaw.

She took the ledger.

Not to destroy it—she was not yet so far fallen as that, and besides, destroying evidence was the Order's trick, not hers. She took it because a treaty was a living thing, and living things deserved better than a locked room, and because if she was going to commit treason she intended to do it thoroughly, with documents.

She wrapped it in her cloak, tucked it under her arm, and paused at the shelf. Three ledgers had been disturbed. She checked the others quickly: one was a census of the Old Blood households at the war's end—names, mostly, row upon row of names, most annotated in a later hand with a single word: *gone.* The third was thinner, a day-book, and its last entry made her go very still.

*The key is returned. The child walks. May whatever gods remain forgive what we have sent her to do.*

Dated seven years ago. The winter Isandro Yarrow had opened her charm shop.

Rellane stood in the dark among the dead paper and let the two facts meet inside her, and their meeting was quiet and total, like two river currents joining. The child-key had been sent out seven years ago. The keeper had been waiting seven years. And now a barefoot girl spoke the pre-kingdom tongue in the shop district, and counter-sigils bloomed on the Door like frost, and the Order's own captain had been dispatched to stamp out the very proof that the Order's founding story was a lie.

Someone had broken faith. She no longer believed it was the child.

* * *

She climbed the stair slowly, ledger hidden beneath her cloak, and let herself out through the chapel's side door into the street. Snow had begun again—the long unnatural winter's snow, dry and fine as ash, falling without wind, straight down, as if the sky itself had grown too tired to drift. The district beyond the chapel wall lay silent under it. Somewhere out there was a tanner named Odrik whom her informants had placed near the archive's district gate at the wrong hour, a big man with kind eyes and debts, the sort of man who breaks into places only for love of someone else. Somewhere out there was a charm-seller who had lied beautifully for seven years. Somewhere out there was a child.

Eleven years ago, Rellane would have woken her sergeants. Would have had Odrik in irons by dawn and the charm-shop surrounded by noon, and by evening there would have been a fire in the square, and she would have slept well after, because the peace required it.

She stood instead in the falling ash-snow, in the doorway of the chapel that housed the lie, and did not move for a long time.

Then she pulled her collar up, settled the ledger against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and walked—not toward the garrison barracks, but toward the tanneries—while behind her, in the locked dark beneath the chapel, the empty space on the shelf waited to be found, and the whole grey machinery of the Order began, without knowing it yet, to turn toward her.

At the corner she stopped once, just once, and looked back at the cemetery wall where her grandmother slept under stone, under the words *SHE KEPT THE CITY SAFE.*

"Grandmother," she said aloud, to no one, to the snow. "What did you keep us safe *from*?"

The snow fell straight down and answered nothing, and Maren Rellane went on into the dark, a traitor now in her own eyes and a truth-teller in no one's, carrying the treaty that unmade her life against her heart like a blade she had not yet decided which way to point.
