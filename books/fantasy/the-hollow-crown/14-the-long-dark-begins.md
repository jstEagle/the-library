## 14. The Long Dark Begins

The snow came in the ninth week of autumn, and it did not stop.

Isandro Yarrow stood at her shop window and watched it fall on Greyhollow like ash from a fire no one had lit. It fell straight down, without wind, without swirl, as though the sky itself had grown too tired to move the air. The rooftops of the Tanner's Row went white one by one, then the weathervanes, then the crooked chimney of Odrik's yard across the lane, and by dusk the whole city looked like something buried—like a thing that had been dead a long while and only now been covered.

Seven years she had kept this shop, and seven winters she had watched the first snow come when it should: late, grudging, melting by morning off the cobbles. This was not that. This was snow with intent behind it. Snow that fell as if summoned.

Behind her, Vess sat cross-legged on the counter, turning the iron half-crown over in her small hands. The girl had not spoken—not in words Isandro could understand—since the night of the storm, since the voice beneath the ice had worn her mentor's face and asked so sweetly for what it wanted. But she watched everything now. She watched the snow with the same unblinking attention she gave the Door, and Isandro had learned to read the set of her shoulders the way sailors read cloud.

The child was afraid. Not of the snow. Of what the snow meant to the people above it.

"Put it away," Isandro said gently, nodding at the token. "Not today."

Vess slid it into the lining of her coat without protest. She was learning caution faster than any child should have to.

Outside, a fishwife hurried past with her shawl drawn tight, and Isandro recognized her—the same woman who had bought the luck-knot all those weeks ago, back when the cold was merely weather. The woman did not look at the shop. She crossed to the far side of the street to avoid passing its door, and her eyes, when they flicked up once, held something Isandro had never seen directed at her before in seven years of selling small mercies.

Fear. And beneath the fear, the beginning of blame.

* * *

Captain Rellane of the Ashen Order stood in the granary yard and counted what remained.

She did it the way she did everything: precisely, silently, with her hands clasped behind her back and her breath steaming through the grey wool of her coat. The quartermaster walked beside her, reciting figures she had already read twice on the manifests, because he needed to say them aloud and she needed someone to hear them said.

"Barley's gone soft in the third and fourth vaults. Rot, or frost-rot—we've never seen frost take it through stone like this. The river ice is three hands thick and it's barely mid-autumn, Captain. Three hands. The ferry's done until spring, and spring—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Spring is a long way off, the way this is going."

"How long," Rellane said, "will the stores last?"

"At quarter-rations? Until thaw. If thaw comes when it should." He hesitated, and then, because men always told her things they shouldn't: "If it comes at all. My grandmother saw a winter like this once. Said the sun came up grey for four months running and the priests rang the bells backward every dawn." He laughed, a thin sound with nothing in it. "Old stories. Superstition."

"Everything is superstition," Rellane said, "until it isn't."

She thought of the archive. Of the treaty pages she had copied by candlelight and then burned the copies of, one sheet at a time, watching her own grandmother's name curl and blacken—the name signed at the bottom of a surrender that had bought Greyhollow its peace with magic's death. *The kingdoms gave up magic forever.* That was the sentence she kept returning to, turning it over like a coin worn smooth. Her whole order existed to enforce that surrender. Every pyre, every interrogation, every name in her ledger—it was all of it just the treaty's long signature, renewed year after year in blood.

And the winter was breaking the terms of it. Or revealing that there had never been terms at all—only a pause, and pauses end.

"Captain?" the quartermaster said. "There's talk in the lower quarters. About witches."

"I know."

"They're saying the cold follows certain houses. Saying old Mother Casselin on Rope Walk hasn't bought coal since the frost came and her windows still glow warm. Saying the charm-sellers are—"

"I know," Rellane said again, more quietly.

She knew because she had written some of the rumors herself, months ago, seeded through the district to flush out the living script. Rumors were tools; she had never pretended otherwise. But tools slipped their handles. The rumor she had planted as a fishhook had come back as a net, and now it was dragging through the whole city, catching everyone, catching things that had nothing to do with sigils or seals or the war her order believed it had won.

That evening she stood at her window above the garrison and watched a crowd gather around a chandler's shop on Candle Street—a shop whose owner had once sold a love-charm to a soldier's widow, nothing more, a knot of red thread—and she watched the crowd grow, and darken, and begin to carry torches in weather when torchlight was the only warmth left to anyone.

She did not go down. She told herself it was because the watch would handle it. She told herself many things that night, standing in the cold, listening to glass break.

* * *

Odrik found the mob on his way home from the tannery.

He had worked late—hides wouldn't cure right in the cold, everything took longer, everything cost more—and he came up out of the tannery reek into streets gone strange and white and silent, silent except for a sound ahead of him that he couldn't place at first. A low murmur. Many voices braided into one, the way a river braids before it goes over a fall.

They had cornered a woman outside the baker's on Mill Lane. Old Sefa, the herbwife, who had pulled teeth and brewed febrifuges for thirty years and charged poor folk nothing. They had her against the wall, and a man Odrik knew—a man who drank at the Drowned Gull, who had carried Odrik's daughter on his shoulders at the harvest fair two summers past—was shouting into her face.

"*Where do you keep it?* Where's your familiar, witch? We'll find it. We'll find it and burn it in front of you—"

"She's a healer," Odrik heard himself say, and his voice came out flat and loud and stranger to him than any spell. "She set my arm when I broke it falling off the kiln roof. She's a healer, Tam, and you know it."

The crowd turned. Forty faces, maybe fifty, lit orange from below by the torches, and every one of them was the face of a hungry man or a hungry man's wife. Odrik had seen hunger do this before, in the famine year, but never this fast, never this early, never with this particular gleam—the gleam of people who have decided that their suffering must be someone's *fault*, because if it is no one's fault then it is simply weather, simply fate, simply the world grinding on indifferent, and that is unbearable.

"The cold followed her house," someone said. "Frost on her shutters and none on mine."

"Frost on everyone's shutters," Odrik said. "Look up. Look at the sky. There's no wind. You think an old woman did that?"

"You'd say that," another voice called, sly and rising. "Wouldn't you, tanner? Don't you keep company with the charm-woman on Tanner's Row? Yarrow, they call her. Little knots and strings. Harmless, eh? Then why's her shop the only one on the row with no frost on the door?"

It wasn't true. Isandro swept her step every morning like everyone else. But truth had stopped mattering somewhere in the last week, the way a lamp gutters and you don't notice the exact moment the light goes.

Odrik opened his mouth to deny it and found, to his horror, that he could not—not cleanly, not with fifty faces waiting, not when the denial itself would be a kind of confession. *I lie to him for the first time*, he thought wildly, remembering how Isandro had lied to *him*, gentle lies about charms and luck, and how he had chosen to burn his debts rather than betray her. He had thought that was the hard part. He had thought choosing loyalty was a single fire you walked through once.

But loyalty wasn't a fire. Loyalty was a winter. You didn't cross it; you lived in it, night after night, and every night it asked you again.

"Sefa goes home," he said instead, stepping sideways, putting his broad body between the crowd and the old woman. "You want to burn someone, Tam, you come through me first, and then explain to the Drowned Gull why you killed a man who bought your drinks all summer."

For a moment it balanced—horribly, delicately, like a scale with a knife on one pan. Then a bell began ringing from the citadel, the watch bell, three strokes and a pause, the signal for dispersal, and the crowd's spine went out of it all at once. Torches guttered. Faces turned away. Within minutes Odrik stood alone in the trampled snow with old Sefa shaking against the wall, and he put his coat around her shoulders and walked her home and did not let himself think about what he had just promised the whole street with his body.

By morning, everyone on Mill Lane knew the tanner was protecting witches.

* * *

Vess dreamed of the ice again, but the ice had changed.

In the first dreams, back in the burning towers, the voice beneath had called her name in the old tongue, sweet as water. Now the voice was quieter. Now it sounded tired. In the dream she knelt on the frozen lake inside the Hollow Door's antechamber—that impossible room that was bigger than the shop above it—and beneath her feet, far down through the black glass of the ice, she could see lights. Not torchlight. A court's light, cold and gold, and shapes moving among it, slow as weeds in deep water.

They were not angry. That was the thing no one had told her, the thing she had no words for even in her own tongue. They were not angry at all. They were *waiting*, the way the dead wait, which is to say without hope and without choice, and their waiting pressed up against the underside of the world like a hand pressed against a window from the other side.

She woke in the antechamber's dark—Iandro had moved her bedding there after the mob trouble started, saying the wards were strongest at the threshold—and lay listening to the shop above. Footsteps. Two sets. Isandro's quick ones, and heavier ones that came and went at odd hours now, the tanner-man, the one who smelled of oak bark and smoke and worry.

The heavy footsteps stopped overhead. A knock, soft, on the floorboards. Their signal.

Vess climbed the hidden stair and came up through the trapdoor into the shop, where lamplight and two anxious faces waited, and where Isandro immediately crouched to her level and searched her eyes.

"Bad dream?"

Vess nodded. Then, because she was tired of being only a key, only a charge, only a responsibility shaped like a child, she reached out and took Isandro's hand and pressed it flat against the floorboards, over the place where the seal ran beneath, and let her feel it.

The tremor. Faint, rhythmic, wrong. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall—except the heart was beating too fast, and had been for days, and was getting faster.

Isandro went very still. Above them, beyond the shuttered window, the snow fell without wind, without mercy, without end, and somewhere across the dark city a second bell began to ring—not the watch bell this time but the great bell of the citadel, rung backward, dawn-bell at midnight, the way they had rung it in the old stories when the seasons themselves were believed to have been bewitched.

Odrik swore under his breath. "They'll blame her for that. They'll blame the bells, the crops, the ice—they need someone to burn, Isandro, and there's a mob on Mill Lane already calling your shop the cold-house—"

"I know."

"And Rellane's men were at the tannery today asking after me. Asking after *us*. She knows, Isandro. Maybe not everything, but she knows enough, and when the city gets hungry enough she won't be able to protect us even if she wants to—"

"I *know*." Isandro closed her eyes. Under her palm, the seal trembled like a trapped bird. When she opened them again, something had settled in her face that Odrik had never seen there in all their years of neighborship—not fear, and not courage exactly, but the terrible calm of a person who has finished counting the cost and found the sum unavoidable.

"The seal can't hold through a winter like this," she said quietly. "Whatever's pressing on it from below is pressing harder because the cold feeds it—or it feeds the cold, I don't know anymore which. And I can't hold it alone, and I can't explain it to a city that's lighting torches, and I can't run, because the Door doesn't close behind you. It never has."

"So what do we do?" Odrik asked.

Isandro looked at Vess—at the iron half-crown's faint outline beneath the child's coat lining, at the old blood sleeping in her veins, at everything the archives had taught them about hostage exchanges and broken faith and a crown that was hollow because it held nothing but mourning.

"We stop trying to hold it," Isandro said. "We go down, and we end it properly. Before the mobs find us, before Rellane's orders find us, before that heartbeat under the floor gives out entirely." She drew a breath that shook only a little. "Tomorrow night. All three of us."

Above them, the backward bell tolled on, and the snow fell, and beneath the floorboards something ancient turned over in its sleep and dreamed, for the first time in seven years, of waking.
