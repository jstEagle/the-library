## 16. The Descent

The Hollow Door had never opened for Isandro before.

Seven years she had knelt before it, oiling the hinges that never turned, tracing the sigils carved into its iron face, whispering the maintenance-words her mentor had taught her in a voice gone thin with dying. Seven years of tending a lock as if it were a shrine, and in all that time the Door had kept its silence, patient as stone, indifferent as winter.

Now it breathed.

She felt it before she saw it—a slow exhalation from the seam beneath the door, carrying up through the floorboards a smell like deep water and old copper, like the inside of a bell that has not been rung in a hundred years. The wards along the walls, which had hummed their low warning note since Vess first arrived, shifted pitch. They did not warn now. They *welcomed*, and that frightened Isandro more than any alarm could have.

"Keeper." Vess stood at the foot of the stairs, barefoot as always—she would not wear the boots Odrik bought her, said they made the floor go quiet, and Isandro had learned not to press. The girl's dark eyes were fixed on the Door with an expression too old for her face. "It's time. It's been asking all night."

"I know."

"It's scared," Vess added, and her small hands twisted together. "Underneath. It's been scared for so long it forgot it was scared. It just sounds angry now."

Isandro closed her eyes. In seven years she had catalogued every sound the seal made—the groan of settling stone, the tick of contracting iron in hard frost, the almost-inaudible pulse beneath it all, like a heart beating under a blanket of earth. She had told herself it was machinery. Old craft, running down. She had told herself many things.

Behind her, Odrik came down the last stair with a lantern in one hand and a coil of rope over his shoulder, moving carefully in the cramped space, a big man trying to be small. He had shaved for this. She didn't know why that detail undid her—it was such an ordinary thing, a tanner shaving before a funeral—but it did.

"Cellar's stocked," he said quietly. "Firewood, salt meat, water. If we're not back in three days, Marta knows to send word to the harbor priest. Not the Order." His jaw worked. "Not the Order. Whatever happens."

"Nothing is going to happen to us."

"You're a terrible liar, Yarrow. Always have been." He set the lantern on the step and looked at the Door, and Isandro watched him take in the sigils glowing faintly along its frame—not with fear, but with the careful attention of a man reading a bill of sale, checking for the trick hidden in the terms. "That mark there. Third from the bottom left. That's the same one that was scratched on your outer stone, isn't it? The counter-sigil."

"Yes."

"So whoever wanted this open has already been standing where we're standing." He said it without drama, the way he'd say *the hides are curing slow this month*. "Good. Then at least we know the way's been walked."

Isandro knelt before the Door. Vess came to stand beside her, close enough that the child's shoulder pressed against her arm, warm and solid and impossibly light. The iron token hung at Vess's throat—half a crown, cold even against skin, humming now in harmony with the wards.

"The last keeper who opened this door," Isandro said, mostly to herself, "was Maren. My teacher. She sealed it behind her when she came back up, and she never spoke of what was below except once, at the end, when the fever had burned everything else out of her. She said—" Isandro stopped. Swallowed. "She said, *it isn't a prison, Isandro. Prisons are for the guilty.*"

"What did she mean?" Odrik asked.

"I don't know yet. But I think we're about to find out." She laid her palm flat against the iron. It was warm. In seven years it had never once been warm. "Vess. When I say run, you run back up those stairs and you don't stop until Odrik's cellar door is barred behind you. Do you understand me?"

The girl looked up at her with that terrible gravity children sometimes wore, the gravity of having seen things no one should see. "You'll say run," Vess said. "But you won't mean it. And I won't go. You need me to open things. And I need—" Her voice caught, steadied. "I was told to find the keeper. I found her. I'm not leaving."

* * *

The Door opened without force.

Isandro pressed her palm to the center sigil, spoke the opening words—words she had only ever spoken in reverse, sealing—and the iron sighed inward like a chest unclenching. Beyond it lay not the passage she had imagined, not a stair descending into rock, but a short corridor of fitted black stone, dry and utterly silent, lit by veins of pale luminescence running through the walls like frost in wood grain. The air that came out was cold but breathable, tasting of minerals and something older, something like the memory of incense.

They went down.

The corridor descended in long shallow ramps rather than stairs, turning back on itself in wide switchbacks, and Isandro counted the turns because counting kept the fear in its box. Four turns. Six. Eight. The walls changed as they went—the lower courses of stone bore carvings, at first geometric, then figurative: processions of robed figures, crowned figures, figures whose faces had been deliberately effaced by later chisels. Someone had come down here after the carving and tried to erase the people from their own history.

At the ninth turn, Vess stopped walking.

"Vess?" Odrik's hand went to her shoulder. "Child, what is it?"

She was staring at the wall, at a section of carving where the effaced faces gave way to something else—a single figure, small, uncrowned, standing apart from the procession. The chisel had spared this one. Around its feet, worn nearly smooth by age or touch, ran a line of script in the pre-kingdom tongue.

Vess read it aloud. Her voice in that language was different—lower, resonant, each syllable landing like a stone dropped into still water. Then she translated, in her flat, careful Common: *"The key walks home on its own feet."*

Silence took the corridor. Even the lantern flame seemed to lean away from them.

"That's you," Odrik said softly. Not a question.

"It was always going to be someone," Vess said, and started walking again, and Isandro's heart broke a little at how matter-of-fact she was, how a child learned to carry prophecy the way porters carry loads—shifted to the strong hip, never set down.

They passed a niche where a skeleton sat cross-legged, robed in cloth that had gone the color of ash but had not rotted. Its hands rested palm-up on its knees. On the floor before it, arranged with evident care, lay a rusted sword point-down, and beside the sword, a clay tablet bearing a single line of script. Vess glanced at it as they passed and did not translate, and Isandro did not ask. Some things a child should be allowed to keep.

* * *

The traps began at the twelfth turn.

There was no warning. One moment the corridor stretched empty ahead; the next, Odrik's lantern light caught a shimmer across the passage like heat-haze in high summer, though the air here was cold enough to fog breath. Vess made a sound—a small, bitten-off cry—and stopped so suddenly that Isandro, walking behind, nearly collided with her.

"What is it? What do you see?"

"Threads." The girl's voice had gone thin. "Silver threads. All across. Like a spider made them, but they're singing." She pressed her hands over her ears. "They sing my name. They know my name. If I walk through them they'll—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Isandro looked at the shimmering barrier and understood with cold clarity: wards keyed to Old Blood. The court below had not wanted just anyone climbing up to disturb them—or perhaps, some colder part of her mind noted, they had not wanted their hostage leaving without permission. Either way, the threads sang Vess's true name, and anything that knew a true name could take hold of it.

"We go around," Odrik said. "There's always another way. There's a side passage there—" He pointed to a gap in the eastern wall, half-hidden behind a fallen slab.

"There isn't." Vess's voice cracked. "I can feel it. The threads go everywhere. They've had years to grow. We can't—I brought you here and now we can't—"

"Hey." Odrik crouched to her level, and his voice gentled into the register he used with spooked animals and frightened apprentices. "Look at me. Nobody's trapped. Not while I'm standing here. We'll figure it out."

But Isandro was already looking at the threads, really looking, letting her own magic rise to meet them the way she had let it rise a thousand times before the Door above. And she saw what Vess could not: the threads were keyed to the blood of the Old Blood, yes—but they were also old. Old and starving. Wards fed on power, and whatever power had woven these had been drawn down to filaments by centuries of neglect. They would not strike like a blade. They would strike like a snare—gripping, draining, holding.

And they were keyed to blood. To *her* blood, too. Because Maren had bled her into the gatekeeper's oath, all those years ago, in a room that smelled of tallow and iron, saying *this is the price and the honor both, child, and I am sorry for the price.*

The oath-blood of a keeper was, by the oldest logic, the blood of the Door. And the Door belonged to both sides of the treaty. Both sides' signatures were on it. Which meant—

"I can pass," Isandro heard herself say.

Odrik turned. "What?"

"The threads are keyed to Old Blood. Mine carries the oath. It's diluted, it's keeper's blood, not court blood, but it's *in* the weave—they'll recognize it the way a dog recognizes its master's family." She was unbuckling her coat as she talked, folding it, setting it down with the methodical calm she used for closing the shop at night. Underneath, her hands were shaking. She made them stop. "I walk through first. I show them what I am. Then I hold the threads apart, and Vess comes through touching nothing but me."

"No." Odrik rose to his full height, and for a moment the tanner vanished and something larger stood in his place. "No. Absolutely not. We find another way. We cut them. We wait them out. You don't walk into a thing that sings a child's death-song and just—"

"Odrik." She touched his arm. Through the linen of his shirt she could feel his heartbeat, fast and stubborn. "If we wait, the storm above gets worse, Rellane finds the entrance, and everything we did in the archive was for nothing. If we cut them, they scream, and everything below hears exactly where we are. This is the quiet way. This is the *only* way." She managed a smile. It felt like wearing someone else's face. "Besides. I've spent seven years keeping things out of Greyhollow. Let me spend five minutes keeping one child safe. It's the best trade I'll ever make."

"You come back," he said. It wasn't a plea. It was a term of contract, spoken in the voice of a man who had burned his own ledger once already and knew the weight of debts. "You hear me, Isandro Yarrow? You walk through, you hold the door, the girl comes through, and then you walk back out, and we finish this together, and afterward I'm buying you a drink at the Salt House and you're going to drink it. Those are the terms."

"Those are the terms," she agreed.

She stepped toward the shimmering veil.

Up close, the threads resolved into beauty, which was somehow worse. They hung in the air like the strings of some vast instrument, silver-white, thrumming faintly, and each thread carried a thread of song, and the songs braided together into a single sustained chord that Isandro realized, with a lurch, was a name. Vess's name. Sung continuously, patiently, for however many years these wards had waited. Somewhere below, something had been singing a little girl's name in the dark, over and over, like a lighthouse calling a ship it meant to wreck.

*I'm coming,* Isandro thought at the threads, at whatever listened beyond them. *But you'll have to go through me.*

She raised her hand, let her magic gather in her palm—her small, hoarded, seven-years-rationed magic, the candle-end she had guarded so carefully—and stepped forward.

The threads took her.

It was not pain, at first. It was recognition—a shiver running through the whole silver web as her blood answered theirs, kin calling to kin. For one heartbeat she thought it might actually work, that the web would part for her like curtains.

Then the chord changed.

The singing bent downward, darkened, and Isandro felt the web's attention snap onto her like a fisherman feeling a bite. Not kin, then. Or kin, and therefore *trespasser*—because the oath-blood in her veins was the blood of the ones who had signed the treaty, the ones who had taken the child and given up magic, the ones the court below had every reason to hate. The threads tightened. Cold flooded up her arm, her shoulder, her chest—an icy drawing-out, the web drinking her magic straight from the source, and beneath the cold she felt the deeper current, the hook setting itself: the web wasn't just feeding. It was *reeling*. Pulling her forward and down, toward whatever anchor lay at the far end of the silver lines.

Through the shimmering veil she saw Vess lunging forward, screaming her name, and Odrik catching the girl around the waist, holding her back.

"Don't—" Isandro gasped. The word cost her. Everything cost her now. "Don't touch the—"

The web hauled. Her feet left the stone.

And in that suspended moment, weightless in a web of singing silver, Isandro Yarrow discovered what her mentor had never had time to teach her: that a ward is a question asked in power, and questions can be *answered*. The web demanded her identity—demanded to know what blood dared traverse it. So she gave it the truth, all of it, in a single burning pulse: *I am the keeper. I keep. I have kept. Seven years I have kept, alone, unthanked, unwitnessed, and I will keep one moment longer.*

The web hesitated. A ward built by grieving dead had no protocol for duty freely offered.

In that hesitation, Isandro tore herself free—not cleanly, never cleanly. The threads ripped out of her the way barbs rip out of flesh, taking what they had gripped: a ribbon of her magic, torn loose at the root, and a line of pain across her ribs where the nearest strand had scored her through shirt and skin. She fell through the far edge of the veil and hit the black stone rolling, came up on her knees, and turned immediately, because the terms of the contract mattered, because Odrik was watching, because she had given her word.

"Hold it open," she rasped. Blood ran warm down her side. "I can hold it—from this side. The web's confused. It thinks I belong to it now. Come on, Vess. Take my hands and don't let go, no matter what it sings at you."

Vess came. Of course she came—she had said she wouldn't leave, and children keep their promises with a ferocity adults forget. She stepped into the veil with her chin up and her eyes shut and her small hands locked around Isandro's bleeding fingers, and the web sang her name in voices like breaking glass, and Isandro poured every remaining scrap of herself into the space between them, a wall of keeper's-oath and stubbornness and something that was not duty at all anymore and had not been for a long time.

The threads recoiled from it. From *that*, specifically. The web could parse blood and oath and office. It had no answer for love.

They stumbled through together. Odrik followed, hunched low, passing through the space they'd opened as if wading through surf, and behind them the silver web shivered, re-formed, and went on singing its endless chord into the empty corridor—as if nothing had happened, as if it had all the time in the world, which, Isandro thought grimly, pressing her hand to her bleeding ribs, it probably did.

* * *

They rested in a chamber off the fourteenth turn, a round room where the luminous veins in the walls gathered into something like a hearth-glow. Odrik bound Isandro's wound with strips of her own shirt hem and a paste of crushed herbs from his pack, his big hands unexpectedly deft, his face carefully neutral in the way of men who are frightened and refuse to show it.

"Deep," he reported. "But clean. You'll scar."

"I've got others."

"Not like this one." He tied off the bandage and sat back on his heels. "That web took something out of you. Don't tell me it didn't. Your eyes have gone—there's less light in them. Like a lamp turned down."

Isandro flexed her fingers. He was right. She could feel the absence the way an amputee feels weather: her magic, always a low warmth in her sternum, now guttered like a fire missing half its coals. Whatever the web had torn free, it hadn't given it back.

"Then I'd better spend what's left well," she said.

Vess sat against the curved wall, knees drawn up, watching Isandro with an expression Isandro couldn't read—until the girl spoke.

"You bled for me." Flat. Wondering. As if cataloguing a phenomenon with no precedent in her experience. "The threads asked for me and you put yourself in between. Why? You didn't know me. Seven days ago you didn't know I existed."

Isandro considered the question seriously, because the child deserved seriousness. "Because someone did it for me once," she said finally. "Maren. She took an oath that was killing her and passed it to me so I'd have a life instead of a death. She called it duty. It took me seven years to understand it wasn't duty at all." She reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind Vess's ear, the gesture simple and human in that ancient place. "Duty is what you owe. Love is what you pay anyway. Come on. We're close. Can't you feel it?"

Vess could. They all could, now. Beneath the stone, beneath the silence, a vibration had begun—rhythmic, vast, patient. Not the seal's failing pulse from above. Something deliberate. Something that had noticed them, the way the sea notices a single swimmer.

The corridor ended at a final archway, taller than any of the others, its frame carved with two intertwined crowns—one whole, one broken—and between them, in the pre-kingdom script, words that Vess read aloud in her resonant other-voice:

*"Here ends the kingdom of the living. Enter with truth or not at all."*

Beyond the arch, stairs descended into blue-black darkness, and far below—so far it might have been imagination—light flickered. Torchlight. Lamplight. The light of a hall where someone, or something, had been waiting a very long time.

And rising up the stairs, faint and unmistakable, carried on air that had not moved in a century, came the sound of a voice Isandro knew better than her own.

Maren's voice, warm and dry and fond, saying: *"You're late, child. But you brought her. Come down, then. Come down and see what we kept for you."*

Odrik's hand found hers in the dark. Vess's small fingers closed around the other.

Together, they began to descend.
