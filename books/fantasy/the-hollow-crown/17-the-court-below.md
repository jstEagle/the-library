## 17. The Court Below

The dark down here is not empty. Vess knows this the way she knows the taste of snow before it falls—by something older than knowing.

She walks between them. The keeper-woman on her left, limping now, her blood leaving small dark stars on the stone. The tanner-man on her right, carrying a lantern that seems ashamed of its own small light. And ahead of all of them, the passage opening like a throat.

Vess does not tell them that she has dreamed this place. She does not tell them that she has dreamed it every night since the ice, since the towers burned, since the voice beneath everything began to say her name in a language with no word for *alone*.

Some things you keep in your mouth like a stone. It keeps your words company.

The walls are carved. She runs her fingers along them as they go—she cannot help it—and the carvings answer. Not with sound. With *recognition*. The Old Blood script rises under her touch like gooseflesh, like a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep, and each letter hums a note so low she feels it in her teeth, in the soft places behind her eyes, in the hollow of her throat where her voice used to live before she gave it away.

*You were told to find the keeper,* the voice had said, back when there was still a voice and not only silence. *You were told to find the keeper, and you will, and when you do—*

But the rest of the instruction had been lost in the burning. That was the cruelty of it. That was the joke the world played on small people: it gave them half a task and called it a destiny.

The passage widens.

The cold changes. It stops being weather and becomes *attention*.

* * *

They come out into a hall so vast that the lantern gives up trying to fill it, and the darkness beyond their little circle of light is not the absence of seeing but the presence of something watching.

Pillars rise around them like the trunks of drowned trees. Between the pillars stand the court.

Vess sees them first as statues. That is how they present themselves to strangers, she thinks—or perhaps that is how they appear to people who have forgotten them, because to Vess they are not statues at all. They are *paused*. A woman mid-turn, her robes flowing backward from a movement begun four hundred years ago. A man with his hand raised in greeting or warning or blessing, fingers spread, face tilted toward a door that is no longer there. Children. There are children here too, frozen in the attitudes of play, one of them caught forever in the act of looking over her shoulder, and Vess's chest hurts when she sees her because the girl looks like everyone and no one, the way all the Old Blood look, the way Vess looks in the water when she forgets to make her face ordinary.

Their skin is pale as the underside of ice. Their clothes are rich and ruined. Frost grows on them in patterns that are almost beautiful, almost deliberate, and Vess understands suddenly that it *is* deliberate—that the frost is writing, that the cold itself has been keeping their record, telling and retelling their story in a language of crystals for as long as they have stood here waiting.

Waiting for what?

For her.

The thought arrives without permission and settles in her stomach like a swallowed coin.

Beside her, the tanner-man makes a sound low in his chest, the sound a man makes when he wants to run but his legs have not yet agreed. "Saints' teeth," he whispers. "Isandro. Are those—"

"Yes." The keeper-woman's voice is flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on a frozen river, listening for the crack. "Don't touch anything. Don't speak names. Vess—" Her hand finds Vess's shoulder, warm through the cloth, and Vess leans into it the way a candle leans toward a window. "Stay behind me."

But Vess cannot stay behind her. Because at the far end of the hall, where the pillars give way to a dais of black stone, something is moving that was not moving before.

Something is coming down the steps.

It wears a grey robe. It walks with a slight hitch in its left knee, the hitch Vess has seen in the keeper-woman's walk every day since they met. Its hands are folded the way a teacher folds her hands. Its face—

Its face is kind. That is the worst thing. In a hall full of frozen nobility and ancient grief, the thing approaching them wears the kindest face Vess has ever seen, lined and patient and faintly smiling, silver hair braided back from a brow creased by decades of worry for other people.

The keeper-woman makes a sound Vess has never heard a grown person make. Small. Wounded. The sound of a child finding a dead bird.

"Maera," she says.

*Maera.* Vess holds the name carefully, the way you hold an egg. She has heard it before—in the shop, late at night, murmured over the floorboards; in the antechamber, spoken to the Door like a password and a prayer at once. The mentor. The dead mentor. The one whose grave the keeper-woman visits when she thinks no one follows.

"That isn't her," the keeper-woman says, but her voice shakes on the *isn't*, and Vess hears what lives underneath the shaking: *please let it be her, please, even if it kills me.*

"It could be," says the thing with Maera's face, gently. "That is the question, isn't it, little keeper? Whether anything down here is real. You've wondered since the day you took the oath. You wonder it now."

Its voice is Maera's voice. Vess has never heard Maera's voice, and still she knows it, the way she knows the script on the walls, the way she knew the shape of the Door before she ever saw it. Some knowledge doesn't travel through ears. It travels through blood, and blood remembers everything.

"You're dead," the keeper-woman says. "I buried you. I sat with you for three days. I *buried* you."

"And grief," says the thing, spreading Maera's hands, "is a kind of door. We have had a very long time to learn about doors."

* * *

The court wakes as the entity passes among them—not all at once but in ripples, like wind crossing a field. Heads turn. Eyes open, frost-crusted lids peeling back to reveal irises the color of deep water, of storm glass, of the green-black under a frozen lake. They do not step out of their poses. They simply *continue* them, slowly, the way a clock continues after someone winds it, and Vess realizes with a lurch that they have been moving all along—so slowly that stone-speed and stillness are the same thing—and that she is the reason they are moving faster now.

They turn toward her the way flowers turn toward light.

A noblewoman in a rotted gown of sea-green silk drifts near, close enough that Vess can see the frost-script crawling on her cheekbone, spelling and respelling a single word. Vess reads it without wanting to. The word is *stolen*. The word is written on all of them, everywhere, in every fold of every garment: *stolen, stolen, stolen.*

"She's frightened," the noblewoman says, and her voice is beautiful and terrible, like bells rung underwater. "Look how she shrinks. Four hundred years we waited, and they send us a child who flinches from her own people."

"She is not your people," the tanner-man says, stepping forward, and Vess's heart clenches—big foolish brave Odrik, with his burn-scarred hands and his ledger of debts, putting himself between her and a dead queen. "She's a child of Greyhollow. She's—"

"She is *ours*," says a man at the queen's shoulder, and his voice cracks the air like lake ice. "Stolen from the cradle by the treaty-mongers. Given away like a goat to settle a debt. You wrote it in your histories as mercy. *The kingdoms gave up magic forever*, your scholars say, so pious, so proud—as if surrender were a gift and not a theft. What you truly surrendered was *her*. Our last daughter. Our key. Our future, handed to strangers who locked her blood away in a box and called the box a peace."

"That's not—" the keeper-woman begins.

"It is exactly what happened," says the queen.

And Vess feels the words go into her like cold water into a crack, because part of her—some deep, unformed, child-shaped part—has always known she did not simply *come from* somewhere. She was *sent*. Sent with half a crown of iron in her fist and instructions that burned before they finished. Sent by people who loved her, or said they loved her, or loved what she was more than who she was—the difference matters, the voice beneath the ice used to say, the difference is the whole of everything—and sent to find a keeper in a city that killed her kind.

"Then take me home," Vess says.

Her voice comes out cracked and rusted, the first full sentence she has spoken aloud in longer than she can count, and it costs her—it costs her like pulling a tooth—but she says it again, louder, because some things must be said twice to be true. "Take me home. If I'm yours. Take me home."

The court goes still. Even the frost seems to stop growing.

The entity wearing Maera's face turns to look at her, and in that moment Vess sees past the borrowed kindness, sees what is underneath: something vast and tired and starving, something that has been holding a shape for so long it has forgotten its own, something made entirely of mourning and calling the mourning a kingdom.

"Oh, child," it says. "We intend to."

"No." The keeper-woman moves, puts herself between Vess and the dais, and her limp is worse now, her leg dark with blood below the knee, but her voice is iron. "No. You don't get to say *home* to her. You don't get to wear my mentor's face and talk about theft. I've read the terms. The exchange was made freely—both sides, freely, or the seal would never have held. Your people *chose*—"

"*Chose?*" The laugh that comes out of the entity is Maera's laugh, warm and rueful, and hearing it come out wrong is worse than any scream. "Little keeper. Come. Walk with me. Look at what choosing left us."

It gestures, and the hall answers.

Light rises—not flame, nothing so alive—pale luminescence seeping up from the black stone floor, and the shadows retreat just enough to show Vess the truth of the court, the truth the darkness had been mercifully hiding. The frozen figures are not whole. Limbs are missing, dissolved into the frost. Faces are worn smooth as river stones in places, eroded by centuries of standing. One of the children—frozen mid-play, forever reaching for a ball that has long since crumbled—is translucent at the edges. Vess can see the pillar behind her through the girl's shoulder.

"We chose," the entity says quietly, "to give our last living child to the enemy rather than watch the war eat her as it ate everything else. And your kingdoms chose to take her, and lock the Door, and bind magic so thoroughly that within three generations your own people called it crime and heresy and hunted it into gutters and charm-shops. Both sides kept faith with the letter of the treaty." Maera's face tilts, sorrowful. "Neither side remembered what the treaty cost. So we stood here. And waited. And froze. And wrote our grievance in frost, and taught the frost to hope, and hoped for a key."

"The seal is failing," the keeper-woman says. "I know. I've spent seven years—"

"You've spent seven years *holding shut a door that was never meant to be yours*." For the first time, the gentle voice sharpens, and the whole court sharpens with it, a hundred dead faces turning in unison, frost cracking like knuckles. "Your oath was a janitor's oath. A lock-tender's vow. You were never asked to save the world, Isandro Yarrow. Only to delay it. And now the delay is done, and the debt has walked to your door on bare feet, and you would deny her her own people because—what? Because you love her? You've known her a season. We mourned her four hundred years."

"I know she's a *child*," the keeper-woman says, "and not a *key*, and the difference is the whole of everything."

Vess's breath catches.

Because that phrase—that exact phrase—is the one the voice beneath the ice used to say to her, in the dark, in the years of walking. *The difference is the whole of everything.* She had thought it was hers. She had thought it came from inside her own head, her own small wisdom. But it belongs to both of them. To everything old. As if the same sentence has been waiting on both sides of the Door, and she is the hinge it was balanced on.

Both sides claim her. Both sides speak in her own voice. And both histories cannot be true at once—*we gave her freely* and *she was stolen*—and yet Vess stands in the middle of them, small and mute-made-voiceful, and understands with terrible clarity that both sides believe their history completely, and that belief, given four hundred years and enough grief, becomes indistinguishable from truth.

Which one is she? The daughter sent in mercy, or the hostage taken in war?

Maybe the answer is: whichever side tells it better. Maybe the answer is: whoever she chooses. Maybe the answer is the one thing no one down here, above, or beneath has ever considered.

Maybe the answer is hers.

She opens her mouth to say so—to say *I choose*, the two most dangerous words in any language—and that is when the tanner-man's lantern gutters, and the keeper-woman goes rigid, and Vess feels it too: footsteps on the stair behind them. Many footsteps. The ring of steel. The particular cold, disciplined silence of soldiers who have been taught that silence is a weapon.

Light blooms at the mouth of the passage. Torchlight, hard and orange, nothing like the pale ghost-glow of the court.

And a voice carries down the stair, clipped and clear, a voice that has read ledgers and burned names and does not frighten easily:

"Hold there. In the king's name—hold."

The entity wearing Maera's face smiles, slow and satisfied, like a door finally opening.

"Ah," it says. "Right on time. All the pieces of the story, arriving together."
