## 19. The Witch-Hunter's Blade

The stair beneath the Hollow Door had not been built for climbing down. It had been built for keeping things below.

Rellane understood this the way she understood most true things—too late, and by the ache in her hands. The steps were cut from stone that predated every kingdom she had sworn to, and they descended at an angle that made no concession to human knees or human courage. Her lantern threw a circle of greasy light that could not reach the walls on either side; she heard them more than saw them, a whisper of wet stone breathing past her shoulders as she went down.

Behind her, far above, the antechamber door stood open. She had left it open deliberately. A witch-hunter who meant to come back did not seal her own retreat.

A witch-hunter who meant to come back, she thought, and almost laughed, and the laugh came out wrong in the dark, a small cracked sound that the stair swallowed whole.

She had burned the order's dispatch before descending. That was the first treason, though she had not named it so. *Confirmed: keeper alive. Child located. Descending to cleanse.* Cleanse. The word sat in her mouth like ash. Twenty years in the Ashen Order and she had never once asked what happened to the ash after a cleansing. It went into the ground. It went into the river. It went into the mouths of children downwind of the pyres, and they grew up breathing it, and no one ever called it anything but necessary.

Her boots found each step by faith alone. Somewhere above, Greyhollow was starving under a winter that had come three months early and refused to explain itself. Somewhere above, mobs were gathering in the fish-market with torches and rope, calling it justice. Somewhere above, her grandmother's signature lay in a forbidden archive on a treaty Rellane had read four times now by candlelight, each time hoping the ink would rearrange itself into something she could still obey.

*We, the undersigned, do surrender forever the working of magic within our borders, in exchange for the return of that which was taken.*

Her grandmother had signed with a steady hand. Rellane knew the hand because she had inherited it—the same long strokes, the same impatient crossbar on the T. She had spent her childhood practicing that signature on slate, proud of it, proud of the woman whose name it was. Grandmother Sera, who kept bees. Grandmother Sera, who had lost two sons to the last war and signed away magic so that no other mother would lose what she had lost.

And Rellane had built her whole life on the story that came after the signing: that the surrender was mercy, that the Old Blood were monsters, that the Order existed to hold the line against their return.

The line, it turned out, was a lie drawn around a hostage.

She descended another hundred steps. Another hundred after that. The cold changed character somewhere past the third landing—it stopped being weather and became intention, a cold that pressed against her skin like something reading her. Her wards, the small ones stitched into her coat hem, guttered one by one, and she let them go without mourning. They had been made for hunting women like Isandro Yarrow. Whatever lived down here was older than the word *witch*, and would find her wards about as frightening as a child's drawing of a knife.

The blade at her hip was not a child's drawing. It was Coldmark, forged in the founding year of the Order, quenched—so the archive claimed—in water that had touched the Door itself. It had killed forty-one practitioners in her service. She had sharpened it that morning out of habit, and the habit shamed her now, because she had sharpened it knowing, already knowing, what she would find below.

No. Not knowing. Refusing to know. There was a difference, and the difference was the last scrap of herself she was trying to save.

* * *

The stair ended without announcement. One moment stone underfoot, the next a floor of black ice, smooth as poured glass, and Rellane's lantern light ran away from her across it in all directions at once, revealing a hall so vast that the ceiling was a rumor.

Pillars. Hundreds of them, receding into dark, each carved with script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it. Between the pillars stood figures—she flinched, hand to Coldmark—and then saw that they were statues, or had been statues once, courtiers of the Old Blood frozen mid-gesture, mid-bow, mid-reach toward one another. Frost had thickened over their faces until they were less than faces. They looked like a court caught waiting for a king who never arrived.

They had been waiting a long time. She could feel it in the hall the way you feel grief in a house where someone died—a pressure in the ears, a hush that was not empty but *attending*.

"Yarrow," she said, and her voice came back at her from too many directions, thin and doubled. "Keeper Yarrow. I know you're here."

"I'm here."

The voice came from ahead and slightly right, near a dais half-swallowed by ice, and it was steadier than Rellane expected, which annoyed her, because she had wanted the woman afraid. Fear would have been simpler. Fear would have fit the ledger she'd carried for twenty years.

She walked toward it. The statues watched her pass—not moving, nothing so merciful as moving—but she felt their attention turn like the attention of owls.

Isandro Yarrow knelt at the base of the dais, and she was bleeding.

Not dying—that was Rellane's first professional assessment, and she hated how quickly it came, how the training held even now—but bleeding freely from a wound along her ribs where her coat had been cut through, cloth and flesh together, by something that had left edges too clean for any blade Rellane knew. The keeper had one hand pressed to her side and the other wrapped around a child.

The child.

Rellane had hunted many things in twenty years. She had taken practitioners from their beds, from their altars, from their children's arms. She had learned to look at the accused and see only the crime, because looking at anything else was how hunters went soft and got people killed. She had been very good at not-looking.

She looked now, and there was nothing to un-see.

The girl was small. Seven years old, perhaps eight. Barefoot on the ice, barefoot in a winter that had killed three beggars in Greyhollow's lower streets before the solstice. Her hair was matted. Her wrists showed the marks of bindings, old ones, healed badly. And she was starving—not thin the way the poor of the city were thin, which was a slow arithmetic of hunger, but starved the way siege cities starved, hollowed, the body eating its own story to keep the heart going. She clutched an iron token against her chest with both hands, the way other children clutched dolls, and she was looking up at Rellane with an expression Rellane recognized from mirrors, from her own childhood, from the night the soldiers came through her village burning the last of the hedge-witches:

The expression of someone deciding whether the person holding the blade was going to be the kind who used it.

"She took a wound for me." Isandro's voice was rough. "There are traps down here keyed to Old Blood. One of them opened, and she—" The keeper's breath hitched, and she mastered it. "She pushed me aside. Eight years old, and she pushed me aside."

"The wound is hers to bear, then." Rellane heard herself say it. Heard how it sounded. Her grandmother's voice coming out of her mouth, or worse, the Order's voice wearing her grandmother's cadence. "The Old Blood gave a key. Keys don't get pity."

"The Old Blood didn't give anything." Isandro rose slowly, one hand still at her ribs, and there was blood on her lips where she'd bitten through something holding a cry. "That's what your own archive says, Captain. If you've read it. They gave a *child*. And your kingdoms promised to give up magic forever, and then your kings' grandsons started quietly buying charms off women like me because power doesn't stay surrendered, it just goes underground and gets ugly. And when the treaty broke, nobody came to tell the child. Nobody came to tell the dead court down here that the bargain died. So they've been waiting. All of them." She gestured, small and terrible, at the frozen pillars, the frosted faces. "Waiting for a war that ended before most of them drew breath."

"You're lying."

"I'm the last gatekeeper of the Hollow Door," Isandro said. "Lying is the one thing I've done for seven years, and I am so tired of it that I would pay money to stop."

The child spoke then.

It was not the kingdom's tongue. It was the other one—the old one, the living script, the language that made wards hum and made Rellane's order burn people. But she had spent three nights with the archive's phonetic keys, cross-referencing the treaty's original terms, and she understood it anyway, the way you understand a song you heard in infancy.

The girl looked at Rellane and said, in a voice worn down to a thread: *"Are you the one who comes to end the waiting?"*

Rellane's hand was on Coldmark's hilt. She could feel the blade's balance, the wear of the grip, the forty-one deaths folded into the steel. She could feel the dispatch she'd burned, the order she'd been given, the whole architecture of her life standing behind her shoulder saying *cleanse, cleanse, cleanse*.

And the child asked if she was the one who would end the waiting.

"Yes," Rellane heard herself answer, in the old tongue, badly pronounced, and the word meant both things at once and she could not take either meaning back.

* * *

What happened next, she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe properly.

The hall answered her. Not the statues—the hall. The ice beneath her feet lit from below with a slow blue pulse, and the moving script on the pillars stilled, all of it at once, like birds going quiet before a storm. From the dais behind Isandro, from the throne that Rellane had not let herself fully see, something rose. It wore the face of a dead man—she knew the face from Isandro's file, from the portrait of the previous keeper, Aldous Wren, deceased seven years—and it wore the face the way a coat fits a scarecrow, correctly and horribly, with nothing inside it but the shape of wanting.

It spoke with Aldous Wren's voice, and it spoke to Rellane directly, in perfect Ashen-court diction, the accent of the capital, the accent of her instructors.

"Captain Rellane. Of the Order of Ash. Daughter of Greyhollow. You have brought the blade that was quenched at our door. Good. We remember its making. We remember everything." The thing smiled with a dead man's patience. "You have been told we are monsters. Look at us. Look at us and tell me what you see."

She looked. Statues. Frost. A starving child. A bleeding woman kneeling in her own blood rather than step away from the child. A court that had waited past the end of its own species for a promise to be kept.

"I see," Rellane said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her, "a debt."

"We were owed a war," the thing said gently. "Or an ending. Either would have been a kind of payment. Instead we were owed nothing, forgotten, sealed under a door with a lock made of a little girl, while the world above grew fat on our surrender. Kill the key, Captain. End the ritual. Free us to be angry honestly. It is the only mercy anyone has offered us in three hundred years, and you would be doing the child a kindness besides. She is so tired. Look how tired she is."

And Vess—the archive had given her a name, Vess, and Rellane held onto it like a handhold on a cliff—Vess looked up at Rellane and did not run. Did not hide behind Isandro. She simply turned the iron token in her hands, half a crown, worn smooth by centuries of desperate thumbs, and waited.

Everyone in the hall was waiting. That was the whole horror of it. Three hundred years of waiting, gathered into one room, and Rellane standing in the middle of it with a drawn blade and a choice.

She thought of her grandmother's bees. A strange thing to think of, at the bottom of the world. But Sera had kept bees after the war, after losing her sons, and little Rellane had once asked her why, why tend creatures that sting, and her grandmother had said: *Because something has to keep the hive when everyone else has given up on it. That's not weakness, lamb. That's the whole of the law.*

The Order's law was written in ash. Her grandmother's law was written in wax and honey and the stubborn tending of things that could hurt you.

Rellane drew Coldmark.

Isandro shifted, putting her wounded body between the blade and the child—an instinct, Rellane saw, that cost the keeper real pain, and was offered anyway, without calculation. Even now. Even bleeding. Even facing the woman sent to kill her.

"No," Rellane said. "Hold still. Both of you. This isn't for you."

She reversed the blade.

With Coldmark's edge—the edge that had killed forty-one practitioners, the edge consecrated in the Order's founding fire—she cut the sigil from her own coat. The Ashen brand, the wheel-and-flame, stitched in silver thread over her heart, the mark she had earned at nineteen in a courtyard full of cheering strangers. She cut it free in four strokes, careful, precise, the way she'd been taught to cut sigils out of captured coats as trophies, and the silver thread parted, and the wheel-and-flame fell to the black ice and lay there, small and bright and meaningless, a badge with no chest left to warm.

The hall exhaled. Every statue seemed to lean. The thing on the throne tilted its borrowed head.

"Treason," it observed, with something that might have been approval. "How novel. How very human. And what will you do instead, Captain? You cannot kill us. You cannot save us. You are one mortal with one blade in a hall of the dead."

"I'm not here for you," Rellane said. She sheathed Coldmark—Coldmark, which had never been sheathed in the presence of the enemy in the entire history of the Order—and knelt, grimacing at the pain in her knees on the ice, to be level with the child. "I'm here for her. And for the truth. And I'm going to carry both of them back up those stairs if it kills me, and then I'm going to put the truth in front of every soul in Greyhollow, starting with my own order, and let it do what truth does."

"It will burn," the thing said. "Truth always burns first."

"Yes," Rellane agreed. "I've made a career of burning things. I know exactly how it's done."

Vess reached out—one small, chapped hand—and touched Rellane's cheek, where the frost had bitten red. She said something in the old tongue, soft, and Rellane didn't have the words for it, but Isandro translated from beside them, her voice cracking on the second word:

"She says: *the hunter has laid down her winter.*"

"Tell her," Rellane managed, "that spring owes her an apology. From all of us."

Above them, muffled by three hundred steps and a sealed door and a city's worth of lies, a bell began to ring. Not the temple bell. Not the watch bell. The Order's bell—the one rung only when a hunter failed to report, when a name was struck from the rolls, when the hunt turned inward.

They were coming. Her sisters in silver thread were coming down the stair behind her, blades drawn, carrying orders she had written herself.

Rellane stood, and put her body between the stair and the child, and rested her hand on Coldmark's hilt with a new understanding of what the blade was for.

"Well," she said to Isandro Yarrow, as the light of torches began to bleed down the ancient steps. "It seems we're on the same side of the door now, Keeper. I suggest you tell me everything you know about reforging a crown—quickly."
