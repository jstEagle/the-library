## 22. Spring, Eventually

The ice on the Hollow Door wept first.

Vess noticed it on a morning in the third spring after the Long Dark, when she came down the crooked stair from her room above the shop with a broom over her shoulder and found the frost that had clung to the iron for three years sliding down its face in long silver threads, pooling at the threshold like something finally allowed to cry. She stood there a while, barefoot on cold stone, watching it drip.

*It's melting,* she thought, and then, because she was eleven now and had learned to be careful with words, she said it aloud in the old tongue, softly, the way you'd tell a sleeping person good news: *The winter is letting go.*

The Door did not answer. It never had, not since the night Isandro joined the two halves of the Crown and gave the mourning below somewhere else to go. But the wards hummed — a low, warm note, like a kettle far off in another room — and Vess had learned to read that hum the way other children read weather. It said: *all is well enough. All is well enough for today.*

She swept the step. That was the first law of keeping, the one Isandro had taught her before any other: *sweep the step every morning, whether or not anyone is coming, because the Door should never look forgotten.* Behind her, Greyhollow was waking. Carts rattled over cobbles still dark with meltwater. Somewhere a fishwife — Marta, probably, who had bought a luck-knot seven years ago on the eve of the first snow and still swore by it — was shouting cheerfully at her son about herring. The smell of bread came down Tanner's Row from the ovens, and under it, fainter, the tanneries themselves, the sharp animal tang that Vess would always associate with Odrik humming off-key as he worked.

Three years since the winter that came too early and stayed too long. Three years since the city had starved and raged and burned its witches' marks into doorposts out of fear, and then — slowly, grudgingly, the way ice lets go of a riverbank — begun to believe otherwise.

Vess finished sweeping, hung the broom on its peg, and went to open the shutters.

* * *

The shop looked different these days. Not larger — it was still the same narrow room with its crooked shelves and its counter worn smooth by forty years of Isandro's elbows — but lighter. The new law had come in the second spring, drafted by a council that included a former witch-hunter with a scar where her order's sigil used to sit, and one of its first honest sentences had been this: *small magics may be sold openly, so long as they are small, and so long as the seller can say truly what they do.*

*Say truly what they do.* Rellane had insisted on that phrase. Vess had heard her say it once, standing in this very shop with her arms crossed, glaring at a shelf of charms as though daring them to lie. "Half the horror of the old years," she'd said, "was that everything was sold as more than it was. Luck-knots that claimed to hold back death. Love-charms that were just knots. If magic comes back to Greyhollow, it comes back *honest*, or not at all."

So the shelf labels were written plainly now, in Vess's careful hand:

*KNOT FOR LUCK AT SEA. Works sometimes. Costs nothing but string.*

*SALVE FOR BURNS. Real. Made from comfrey. The magic in it is only patience.*

*WARMING STONE. Holds heat through one night. Do not sleep holding it; you will burn your fingers and it will be your own fault.*

And at the end of the shelf, in smaller letters, because Isandro had made her write it and then had stood looking at it for a long time without saying anything:

*ALL CHARMS SOLD HERE ARE ONLY WHAT THEY SAY THEY ARE. THE KEEPER'S WORD ON IT.*

Isandro kept the shop now the way she did everything — quietly, competently, with her sleeves rolled up. She was ordinary, after the reforging. Truly ordinary. Her charms were knots and string and herbs and patience, exactly what the labels promised, because whatever had lived in her hands for thirty years had gone into the Crown, and the Crown had gone into the grief, and the grief had finally been allowed to end. Some mornings Vess caught her standing at the counter turning her own hands over, palms and backs, palms and backs, like a woman checking whether a tool still worked. Other mornings she laughed more easily than Vess had ever seen anyone laugh, as if ordinariness were a weight she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten it could be set down.

"The melt's come early," Isandro said that morning, coming up from the back room with a tray of salve pots. "Rellane says the passes will open a month ahead of schedule. There'll be traders."

"There'll be questions," Vess said.

"There are always questions." Isandro set the tray down and looked at her over the rim of her spectacles — spectacles! plain glass, bought from an ordinary peddler, worn because ordinary eyes needed them now. "That's why we keep the Door, little wren. Not to answer them. To make sure they're asked in the right place."

Vess was the apprentice keeper now. Not in name — there was no guild anymore, no oath carved in stone, no archive of burned names to inherit — but in the older sense, the sense that mattered. Isandro had taught her the wards, the rituals, the hundred small attentions the Hollow Door required. And she had taught her the histories, all of them, both sides of them, the way the court below had told it and the way the kingdoms had told it and the truth that sat between like a coin worn smooth: *there was never a war to resume. There was only mourning, given power, and a child made into a key because grieving things cannot open their own doors.*

Vess remembered the frozen court. She tried not to, most days. But some nights she dreamed of the hall beneath the ice, of faces like winter stars, of the voice that had worn Isandro's dead mentor like a coat — and she woke and put her hand flat against her floorboards and felt the ward-hum rise to meet her, and remembered why the shop opened at dawn and the step got swept and the labels told the truth.

Some doors must stay open. Tended by those who remember why.

* * *

Odrik came by at midmorning, as he did most days, ostensibly to deliver leather for the charm-straps and actually to eat half of Isandro's lunch and complain about the weather in the satisfied tone of a man who has earned the right to complain about anything at all.

"River's up," he announced, dropping onto his usual stool. "High as I've seen it since before the Dark. Old Ferro swears the fish are running thick as thieves up past the weir." He glanced at Vess, and his craggy face did the thing it always did when he looked at her — a softening around the eyes, quickly covered with gruffness. "You'll want your boots waxed if you're going down to watch the meltwater. Don't give me that look, girl, I can see you planning it."

"I wasn't planning it."

"You were born planning it." He turned to Isandro. "Council meeting Thursday. They're talking about letting the charm-sellers form an actual register. Names on paper, honest goods, the whole rigmarole. Rellane's pushing for it."

"Is she still on the council?" Vess asked. "I thought she'd retire to the coast."

"She threatened to, twice a year, every year." Odrik grinned. "But she says somebody has to keep the honest law honest, and she doesn't trust anyone else to know what dishonesty looks like from the inside." His grin faded a little. "She came by the tannery last month. Just to talk. We talked about the archive, a bit. About names." He didn't say whose name — his grandmother's signature was not among those in the treaty, but his ledger of debts, burned in a hearth seven years ago rather than be used to betray a friend, had become the kind of story people told quietly in Greyhollow, the way you tell stories about saints who were only ever ordinary people who chose well once. "She's all right, Rellane. For a woman who spent twenty years being wrong in the right direction."

"That describes half this city," Isandro said.

"Aye, and the other half was right in the wrong direction, which is worse." Odrik heaved himself up, took his strap-leather, and paused at the door. For a moment his hand rested on the frame, on the place where, years ago, a witch's mark had been scratched and later sanded away. "Thursday," he said. "Both of you come. City ought to see its keepers when it makes laws about them. Keeps everyone honest."

He went out into the bright noise of the street. Vess watched him go — big, slow, unhurried Odrik, who had found hidden charms under floorboards once and answered them by burning his own debts — and felt the familiar warmth in her chest that she had learned, in this city, to call by its plain name: *home.*

* * *

Afternoon brought customers.

This was still strange, and probably always would be. In the old years, the shop had served fear: people came furtive, hooded, quick, buying luck-knots the way drowning people buy driftwood, and leaving without meeting your eyes. Now they came in daylight, chatting, bringing their children, arguing about prices. A farmer's wife bought a warming stone for a lambing shed and stayed twenty minutes to gossip. Two boys bought nothing but pressed their noses against the shelf of knotwork until Isandro shooed them out with a wooden spoon. An old man asked, seriously, whether she had anything "for remembering" — and Isandro, instead of selling him a charm, sat him down and made him tea and listened to him talk about his brother lost in the Long Dark, and sent him home with nothing but the tea and her attention, and charged him nothing, and Vess understood that this too was keeping. Perhaps most of it was.

Toward evening, when the light through the front window had gone gold and long, Vess was restocking the salve shelf when she felt it.

Not heard. *Felt* — the way she had learned to feel the wards, a change in the air's texture, a note struck somewhere below hearing. The hum of the Hollow Door shifted key. Not alarm. Not the terrible grinding dissonance of the night the seal cracked in the storm. Something smaller. Older. Like a word in the pre-kingdom tongue spoken very far away, by someone very small.

She went still. Across the shop, Isandro looked up from her ledger. Their eyes met. Neither spoke, because neither needed to; seven years of shared silence had built a language plainer than either tongue.

Then came the knock.

Two soft taps. Hesitant. The knock of someone who has knocked at many doors and been turned away from most of them.

Vess crossed the shop and opened the door.

A child stood on the step. Small — younger than Vess had been, maybe six, maybe seven — barefoot despite the season, in clothes that had been fine once and were ruined now: cloth of a weave Vess recognized with a lurch in her stomach, because she had worn cloth like it in another life, in a tower that burned. The child's hair was pale as winter starlight. Her eyes were the grey of deep ice. And she was clutching something to her chest with both hands, knuckles white, the way you cling to the one thing you were told never to let go.

In her fist, catching the last of the light, was a token of black iron. Shaped like a crown — but broken, snapped clean across the middle, half of a thing that had once been whole.

Vess's heart stopped and started again, somewhere lower, somewhere older.

The child looked up at her. And she spoke — one word, two, in the language under kingdoms, the language that made wards hum, the language Vess had carried across a burning country in her mouth when she was small enough to be a weapon without knowing it.

*I was told to find the keeper.*

Behind her, Vess heard Isandro set down her pen. Heard the chair creak. Heard the long breath of a woman who had spent her whole life holding doors and had known, in her bones, that the world does not let you finish holding them — that keeping is not a task you complete but a torch you pass, and that someday, on an ordinary golden evening, with the meltwater singing in the gutters, the knock would come again.

Vess did not feel afraid. That surprised her most of all. She felt the way the river must feel when the ice lets go — not broken, but *moving*, at last, toward a spring it had almost stopped believing in.

She stepped back from the doorway, and held it open, and said in the old tongue, gently, the words that had once been said to her on this same step by a tired woman with ordinary hands:

"You found her. Come in out of the cold."

Behind her, the Hollow Door hummed — low and warm and awake — and the frost on its iron face ran silver in the lamplight, weeping, perhaps, or laughing, or simply glad, after so long a winter, to be a door again: open, tended, and remembered.
