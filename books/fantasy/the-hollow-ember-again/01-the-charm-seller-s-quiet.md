## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet

The market of Vell woke the way it always did: slowly, grudgingly, like an old man rising from a bed he no longer trusted. By the time the sun cleared the slate roofs of the Tanner's Quarter, Ada Galloway had already been at her stall for an hour, arranging her wares with the particular care of a woman who had learned that invisibility was a craft, and that she was very good at it.

Dried lavender bundles for sleep. Iron nails wrapped in red thread against bad luck. Little cloth sachets of salt and rosemary that did absolutely nothing but smell pleasant and cost two coppers. She sold them under a hand-painted sign that read *GALLOWAY'S GENTLE REMEDIES — CHARMS & COMFORTS*, because *charms* alone sounded too much like magic, and magic in Vell was a word people said the way they said *plague*—quietly, and only when necessary.

Twenty years since the Edict. Twenty years since the mages sealed the Ember and the city forgot whatever it had once been, and the Wardens began their patient, polite patrols, asking questions of anyone whose remedies worked *too* well. Ada had been six years old then. She remembered almost nothing of it—or told herself she didn't—and she had built her whole life on the foundation of being forgettable. Plain brown dress. Plain brown hair pinned back without ornament. A smile calibrated to be warm enough to sell, cool enough to discourage conversation.

It was a narrow life. But it was hers, and it was safe, and she had learned to call safety peace.

"Morning, Mistress Galloway." Old Marta from the fishmonger's row leaned over the stall, squinting at the lavender. "My hip's been singing all week. Storm coming?"

"Rain by evening," Ada said, and pressed a sachet into the woman's hands. "For the ache."

Marta paid her three coppers and shuffled off, satisfied, and Ada felt the small familiar satisfaction of a harmless transaction. Salt and rosemary. Nothing in it. Nothing in any of it, if you were a Warden looking closely, and there was always a Warden looking closely.

She was reaching for the next bundle when she heard it.

*Please.*

Not aloud. Never aloud. The voice came the way it always came—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like copper and fur at the back of her throat, words that arrived already understood. She kept her hands moving through the lavender, steady, unhurried, while every nerve in her body went taut as wire.

*Please. Small mother. Please.*

She found it without letting her eyes move: a rat, wedged beneath the wheelbarrow two stalls down, its hind leg caught in a snap trap some grain merchant had set. It lay half in shadow, gray-brown, trembling so finely it looked like something vibrating rather than living. Its black eyes were fixed on her with a terrible, naked hope.

*I smell you,* it said. *You hear. You HEAR. Help me. Please help me.*

Ada's hands stilled on the lavender.

This was the part no one warned you about, having a gift in a city that had outlawed wonder. Not the fear of being caught—the fear was old, worn smooth as river stone, hardly felt anymore. It was this. The moment when something small and dying looked at you and believed, with its whole animal heart, that you were good.

"I can't," she breathed, barely a shape of sound, lips barely moving. Around her, the market roared on—haggling, cart wheels, a fishwife's laugh—as though the world itself were conspiring to drown out her cowardice.

*Small mother—*

"I can't." Louder than she meant. A woman browsing the iron charms glanced up; Ada smiled at her, bland and apologetic, and the woman moved on. Ada's heart slammed against her ribs. *Fool. Fool.* Twenty years of careful quiet, and she nearly spoke to empty air in the middle of Tanner's Market.

But the rat was still watching her. Still hoping. That was the cruelty of it—they never stopped hoping, not until the very end. Dogs left chained in alleys. Cats drowned in sacks. The cart-horse last winter, lamed and led away to the knacker's yard, who had sent her one final image as it passed: green grass, open field, the memory of running, offered to her like a gift, like a prayer.

She had stood in the snow and done nothing then, too.

*It hurts,* the rat said simply. No accusation in it. Just the fact, laid out between them like a coin on a counter. *It hurts, and I am afraid, and you hear me, and no one has ever heard me before.*

Ada closed her eyes.

Here is what she could have done: crossed the four steps to the wheelbarrow, made some small noise about checking her neighbor's goods, sprung the trap with a stick, let the creature vanish into the drains. Thirty seconds. The merchant would curse his lost trap and think nothing more of it. Rats died in Vell by the thousand every day; no one counted them, no one mourned them, no one noticed.

No one but her.

And here is what she actually did: nothing. She arranged her lavender. She served a nervous young clerk a sleep-charm and took his coppers. She watched the light shift across the cobbles as morning wore toward noon, and all the while the small voice thinned and frayed beside her—*please, please, small mother*—until somewhere past midday it simply stopped, the way a candle stops, and she knew without looking that the trembling had ceased, and the hope with it.

The silence where the voice had been was the loudest thing in the market.

* * *

She closed the stall early.

Her hands shook as she folded the canvas, and she hated them for shaking, hated herself with a slow, grinding loathing that had become as familiar as her own heartbeat. This was the bargain she had made, wasn't it? This was the price of survival in Vell. You kept your head down. You sold salt and rosemary. You let the small things die so that you would not be seen, because to be seen was to be taken, and to be taken was—

What? She didn't even know anymore. She'd been six. Whatever the Wardens did to the gifted, she knew it only as a shape of dread inherited from her mother's face, from the one clear memory she had left: her mother's hands pressing something into her own small palms, her mother saying *hide it, Ada, hide it deep, promise me*, and then—nothing. Smoke. An absence shaped like a person.

She had promised. She had kept the promise for twenty years, so faithfully that sometimes she wondered if she was still hiding her gift or if the gift had simply rotted in its hiding place, gone strange and sour, until all it could do was make her watch small creatures die and hate herself in perfect safety.

"You're closing early."

Ada startled. Bramble sat on the awning post above her stall—a crow, big and ragged-feathered, with one pale eye and one dark, watching her with what she had long ago decided was insolence. He had shadowed her for years now. She'd never fed him, never called to him, never once acknowledged him, and he had never once left.

"Birds don't get an opinion on my hours," she said, and immediately wished she hadn't spoken, because speaking to a crow in the street was the sort of thing that got quiet women noticed.

Bramble tilted his head. The pale eye caught the light.

*You heard it die,* something in his posture seemed to say—not words, not quite, but she was tired and raw and her guard was down, and meaning bled through anyway. *You hear everything. And you sit in your little stall selling nothing, and you let it die, and tomorrow you will do it again.*

"Shut up," Ada whispered.

He ruffled his feathers, unbothered, and she snatched up her satchel and walked, fast, head down, through the narrowing lanes toward home. The crow followed above her along the rooftops, a ragged shadow against the graying sky, and she pretended not to see him, the way she had pretended for years, the way she pretended about everything.

Rain arrived with the dusk, just as she'd promised Marta. It fell soft and gray on Vell, on the slate roofs and the crooked chimneys, on the city that had forgotten its own name and called itself nothing but Vell, which was not a name at all but a shrug. Ada climbed the stairs to her rooms above the shop, lit one lamp, and sat at her table with her cold hands wrapped around a cup of tea she didn't drink.

In the corner of the window, sheltering under the eave, Bramble settled his wings and watched her through the wet glass.

"I did the right thing," she told the empty room. Her voice sounded thin and unconvinced, a charm made of nothing, salt and rosemary. "There was no other right thing. There isn't any other kind of life."

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the walls, a bell rang—one stroke, twice, then a third, the pattern the Wardens used for a city alert. Not fire. Not flood. Something else, something rare. Ada frowned at the window, at the distant flicker of lanterns moving along the wall road, and felt—for the first time in twenty years of practiced quiet—a cold thread of attention pull tight in her chest, as if something far away had turned its eyes toward her little shop, her little life, and begun, patiently, to walk.

Above the eave, Bramble spread his wings against the rain and made a low sound in his throat that was almost, almost a word.
