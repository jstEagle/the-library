## 2. A Stranger's Last Breath

The bell above my door had not rung all morning, and I had begun to hope it wouldn't.

Hope, in Vell, was a dangerous thing to keep in stock. It sat on the shelf beside the luck-charms and the fever-knots, and like everything else in my shop it had to be small enough to hide. A woman who sold charms for coin could be tolerated. A woman who *believed* in them—that was the kind of woman the Wardens came for.

So I sat behind my counter with my hands busy and my mind carefully empty, braiding horsehair into a knot that was supposed to calm nervous animals. The irony of it kept me company. Twenty years in this city, and the only creature I'd ever soothed with my gift was a merchant's spooked mare, once, when no one was looking—my palms flat against its sweat-dark neck, whispering nothing aloud, letting the words pass between us the way heat passes through a wall.

The mare had looked at me afterward with an eye like a brown moon, and I had never felt so seen or so terrified in my life.

Outside, the market went about its business. Fishwives arguing over the morning catch. Children running messages between stalls for coppers. The crier at the fountain reading the day's notices in his flat, official voice: curfew at dusk, Warden inspections in the Tanners' Quarter, no assembly of more than six persons. The ordinary liturgy of a city that had spent two decades teaching itself not to want anything.

I had gotten good at not wanting. Mostly.

* * *

He came in just past midday, when the light through my front window had gone thin and gray.

I heard him before I saw him—the wrongness of his footsteps, uneven, dragging, each one arriving a half-beat late as if his body were arguing with itself about whether to continue. Then the bell chimed, once, weakly, and a man fell through my doorway.

Not walked. Fell, caught himself on my shelf of dried lavender, and brought half of it down with him. Bundles scattered across the floorboards, filling the room with a sweetness that was immediately, horribly wrong—a funeral smell, a shroud smell, laid over something else beneath it. Burnt meat. Ozone.

"Shop's closed," I said, because I am a coward, because twenty years of survival lives in the body deeper than any kindness does.

He looked up at me, and I stopped being a coward whether I wanted to or not.

He was young—younger than me, maybe five-and-twenty—and he was dying. There was no ambiguity in it, no hopeful maybe. His left side was soaked black from ribs to hip, and the wound beneath was not a knife wound. It was too wide, too clean-edged, and around its borders the skin had gone the color of old ash, as if the hurt itself were burning outward from the center. I knew what made wounds like that. Every child in Vell knew, from the stories we weren't supposed to tell anymore.

Magic did that. Wild magic, the kind that wasn't supposed to exist anymore.

"You're—" He tried to stand straight against my counter and failed. "Galloway. Ada Galloway."

My name in a stranger's mouth. My whole name, not the *Ada from the charm shop* that neighbors used, not the careful half-name I'd built my life inside. Something cold walked down my spine.

"You have the wrong shop," I said.

"No." He laughed, and the laugh turned into wet coughing, and he gripped my counter hard enough that his knuckles went white. "No, I don't. She said you would be here. She said—" Another cough. "Twenty years she said. *If the seal fails, find Galloway's daughter.*"

The world went very quiet. The market noise outside seemed to happen behind glass.

"My mother," I heard myself say, "has been dead for twenty years."

"I know." His eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "I know exactly how long. That's rather the point."

He fumbled at his coat—at the scorched ruin of a courier's coat, I saw now, the livery of the Gray March posts half burned away—and pulled free a leather tube sealed with wax the color of dried blood. And then something else, something that made my breath stop in my throat: a stone, no bigger than a quail's egg, but it *glowed*. Faintly, sullenly, like a coal buried under ash, but it glowed, here, in Vell, where nothing had glowed for twenty years except the Wardens' lanterns.

An ember-stone. I'd only ever seen one in a woodcut illustration, in a book my mother shouldn't have owned, hidden under her floorboards. The book had been gone by morning, and so had she, three days later, and then so had everything else.

"Take it." He pressed both into my hands—tube and stone together—and his fingers were shaking so badly I had to close mine over his to keep them from dropping. His skin was cold. Cold as a riverstone. "The covenant is failing. Do you understand? Not weakened. *Failing.* The Hollow is waking, and when it wakes fully, it won't stop at the March. It'll come through every crack the mages left, and there are cracks everywhere, they were sloppy, they were *afraid*—"

"Stop." My voice sounded far away. "Whatever you think I am—"

"You're the keeper's daughter." He said it simply, the way you'd state the weather. "That's all that matters now. Not what you do. What you *are*."

Somewhere out in the market, a whistle blew. Short, sharp, doubled. The sound every shopkeeper in Vell knew the way they knew their own heartbeat, the sound that meant close your shutters and look busy and be nobody.

Wardens.

The courier's eyes went past me to the window, and I watched something settle in his face—not fear, exactly. Arithmetic. He was calculating how much time he'd bought me with his blood, and finding the sum short.

"Back room," I said. "Now. Can you walk?"

"For about four steps."

It took eleven. I got my shoulder under his arm and hauled him through the curtain into the little room where I slept and ate and kept the few things I owned, and I laid him down behind the loom, and I dragged the dye-chests in front of him, and none of it was going to be enough. I knew it the way you know rain is coming—in your teeth, in your joints, before the first drop falls.

"Listen to me." I knelt beside him. His breath was coming in shallow pulls now, each one whistling. "Who sent you? Who is *she*?"

"She?" He almost smiled. Blood had gotten into the creases of his lips. "You'll meet her. Or you won't. Depends if you run or if you—" His hand found my wrist, grip surprisingly strong. "The map shows the way to the Ember Shrine. Solstice. You have until solstice, Galloway's daughter, and after that there's no shrine to reach, only a door standing open with nothing on the other side of it but hunger."

"The map is scorched. Half of it's burned away."

"It's not burned." His voice dropped to a whisper, and his eyes held mine with terrible gentleness, the way you speak to someone at a graveside. "It's *hiding*. It shows itself to whoever's meant to carry it. Look again. When I'm done talking. Promise me."

"I'm not promising you anything—"

"Promise me, or I die having failed, and I've died twice already today and I'd very much like the third time to count."

There was no time to argue. The whistling had come closer—I could hear boots now, several pairs, the particular cadence of Warden patrol, unhurried because they were never hurried, because the city belonged to them and they knew it.

"Ada," the courier whispered. My name again, but softer this time, almost kind. "The Hollow is waking. Whatever your mother did—whatever you think she did—it isn't finished. Nothing is finished. That's the curse of covenants. They outlive everyone who makes them."

The knock on my front door was polite. Three measured raps. Somehow that was worse than kicking.

"Warden inspection," said a voice I knew, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the earth, into some cold place below the world. "Open in the name of the city."

Elias Thorn. Of course it was Elias Thorn. The universe had a sense of humor like a knife has an edge.

I stood up. My legs felt borrowed. In the space of ten heartbeats I ran through every option I had, and the list was short: I could open the door and pray he searched quickly; I could refuse and be searched anyway; I could—

The courier caught my sleeve. "Go," he mouthed. "Let them find me. I'm dead either way. You don't have to be."

And that—that was the moment. I've turned it over a thousand times since, looking for the seam, the place where the woman I'd been tore away from the woman I became. It was there, kneeling behind my own loom with a stranger's blood soaking into my floor, hearing Elias Thorn's patient knock for the second time.

I didn't go. I couldn't make myself go. Instead I pressed the ember-stone into the courier's palm, folded his fingers over it, and said, "Hold onto this. If they take me, someone should still—"

The door splintered.

They didn't wait for a third knock. The frame cracked and gave and three Wardens came through in a wash of gray cloaks and lantern-light, and Elias Thorn was not among them—he was still outside, I could see him through the broken door, standing in the street with his back half-turned, studying the sky as though it required his attention. Studying anything but my shop.

The lead Warden was a woman named Corvane, and she had the flat, patient eyes of a person who has taken apart many rooms and found many guilty things.

"Charm seller." Her gaze swept the main room, the spilled lavender, the dark smear the courier had left across my floorboards. Her nostrils flared. "You have a smell in here."

"Lavender," I said. "For sleep charms. It's popular."

"It is." She stepped over the stain without looking down at it, which told me she had already seen it and filed it away. Two of her people moved toward the back curtain, and I watched them come the way you watch water rising, knowing exactly what they'll find, unable to move a single thing in the world to stop it.

The curtain rang on its rod. A pause. Then:

"Warden. Back here."

Corvane went through. I stayed where I was, because my legs had finally and completely stopped taking orders, and I listened to the sounds a man makes when he is found dying in a charm seller's back room, and then the sounds he doesn't make at all.

When Corvane came back out, her expression hadn't changed, but her voice had cooled by several degrees, the way metal cools—still holding shape, but no longer warm.

"He's alive. Barely. He'll be questioned at the Bastion, if he lasts the night." She stopped in front of me, close enough that I could see the fine scarring on her jaw, old burn marks, the kind the Wardens' oath-lanterns leave when you swear yourself to the city too young. "Strange thing, though. Dying man, bleeding out on your floor—and no ember-stone on him. No map. Couriers carry tokens. It's how we track them." She let the silence sit between us like a blade laid flat on a table. "Where are his effects, charm seller?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." She looked at my hands. Both of them, slowly, the way a butcher looks at a carcass. My right fist was clenched tight against my skirt, and inside it, small and warm and beating faintly like a second heart, the ember-stone pulsed against my palm. Warm. Getting warmer. As if it knew it had been found, and was glad.

"Inspection's concluded," Corvane said at last, turning away. "We'll take the courier. You'll keep your shutters closed for the rest of the day. And Galloway—" she paused at the ruined doorframe, not looking back, "—the Bastion will want to speak with you. Tomorrow. Don't be late."

They carried him out between two Wardens, his head hanging, his ash-gray wound catching the afternoon light. As they crossed my threshold his eyes opened—found mine—and he smiled. Small. Almost satisfied. As if everything had gone precisely according to some plan I hadn't been shown.

Then the door was empty, the street was empty, and I was alone with the lavender smell and the blood and the thing in my fist.

I opened my hand.

The ember-stone sat in my palm, glowing up at me like a patient eye. And beside it, unrolled across my bloodstained floor where it had slipped from my sleeve without my noticing—without my *remembering* taking it—the scorched map lay open, its burnt edges crawling slowly inward like frost spreading in reverse, lines redrawing themselves, roads appearing where moments before there had been only char.

It showed a route out of Vell. Through the Gray March. To a mark shaped like a flame, drawn in ink the color of embers, and beneath it, in handwriting I knew better than my own—because I'd seen it on every note she ever left me, every label on every jar, the last handwriting I ever watched write my name—

*For my daughter. Finish what I started.*

The map would not leave me now. I understood that the way you understand a door has locked behind you. Somewhere across the city, in a cell in the Bastion, a dying man was buying me time with whatever hours he had left, and somewhere in my chest, beneath twenty years of practiced silence, something that had been sleeping since I was nine years old opened one eye.

Outside my broken door, a crow landed on the sill. It looked at the map. Then it looked at me.

"Well," it said.
