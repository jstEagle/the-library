## 3. What the Crow Remembers

I have watched her for seven years, and in all that time she has never once spoken to me.

Not aloud. Not with the voice she keeps folded up inside her like a stolen letter. She talks to the rats and the pigeons of Vell in that other way—the way beneath words, the way that runs along the nerves like a current under ice—but never to me, and never out loud where a window might be open or a Warden's ear might be sharp. She thinks I don't know what she is. She thinks I follow her for the crumbs.

Crows are not loved for their honesty, so let me be honest: I stayed for the crumbs at first. A crow must eat. But I stayed after for other reasons, reasons with feathers of their own, and one of those reasons has a name, and the name is not Ada Galloway.

Her mother's name was Maren.

* * *

The night the courier died, I sat on the gutter above the charm seller's shop and listened to a man's breath go ragged as a torn wing. I heard the map rustle. I heard the ember-stone click against her palm—heard it, mind you, the way you hear a struck bell through a closed door—and something old turned over in my chest, an ache I had carried so long it had grown a shell around itself, the way a pearl grows around grit.

She hid him too late. I saw the Wardens' lanterns swing down Gilders' Row, and I saw her do the arithmetic of survival that twenty years in Vell teaches every hidden thing: herself or him. She chose herself. She will hate herself for it, which is how I knew she was Maren's daughter before I ever confirmed it. Maren would have done the same, and hated it the same, and gone on anyway, because going on is what keepers do.

Yes. Keepers. There is a word the city forgot, along with its own true name, along with the color of the river before the blight grayed it, along with the reason the walls stand at all. The city forgot because forgetting was bought and paid for, sealed into stone and spell on a night I remember better than I remember my own first flight.

I was there. Remember that. Whatever else I am—a thief, a cynic, a scavenger of shiny things and bitter truths—I was there.

* * *

The morning after the courier's death, Ada opened her shutters late. I dropped from the eaves to her windowsill, as I have done a thousand mornings, and did my thousandth performance: sidestep, head-tilt, the beggar's shuffle that makes humans think crows are simple. She tossed me a heel of bread. I caught it, and I did not eat it. I set it down between us on the sill, deliberate as a signature.

She frowned. "You're not hungry?"

I said nothing, because I could say nothing—not in her tongue, not without her gift opening the door between us. That is the cruelty of it, the joke the world played on both our kinds: I can understand her kind's speech plain as rain, but she cannot hear mine unless she *speaks*, unless she opens that folded-up letter inside her and lets the current run. Her gift works both directions, but only if she pays the toll of using it. And she has not paid that toll toward me in seven years. Toward rats in traps, yes—small, guilty payments, made in whispers when no one watched. Never toward me. I used to wonder if she suspected what I was. Now I know better. She suspected what I *reminded her of*. Something from childhood. Someone.

So I did the only thing left. I picked the bread back up, hopped three steps along the sill to the gap where the shutter met the frame, and pushed it through into her shop. Then I looked at her. Crows cannot raise eyebrows, but we can hold a gaze, and I held hers until her hands went still on the shutter latch.

"That's—" she started, and stopped. Her eyes did the thing human eyes do when they are deciding whether to be afraid. "That wasn't an accident."

No, I thought. It was not.

"You've been following me for years." Her voice dropped low, market-habit, though the shop was empty and the door bolted. "Since before the wall repairs. Since—" She pressed her lips together. "Why?"

I tilted my head. Waited.

And here is the thing about a locked gift: the lock is on her side, not mine. All she had to do was speak—to me, aloud, with intent, the way her mother used to stand in the orchard at dusk and talk to the starlings like neighbors leaning over a fence. All she had to do was stop pretending, for one moment, in one room, that she was nothing but a charm seller with clever fingers.

"I'm losing my mind," she whispered. "Talking to a bird."

Then she laughed—short, cracked, nothing like a laugh at all—and she looked at me properly, and I watched her decide. I have seen many creatures decide things at the edge of a cliff. It looks the same in every species: the pupils widen, the weight shifts back, and then forward.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. If you can understand me—if any of this is real—then understand this." She leaned close, and her voice came out in the shape of her gift, the current rising under the words like heat off summer stone: "*Who are you? What do you want?*"

Seven years. Seven years of crumbs and silences, and the door finally swung open, and what came through it was her mother's cadence, her mother's exact intonation, the rising lilt Maren used when she asked the wind for news. For a moment I could not speak at all. Grief is heavier than any stone a crow has ever carried.

"*Bramble,*" I said, and my voice came out like a hinge long unoiled, because I had not used the speech of the gifted in twenty years either. "*My name is Bramble. And I served your mother.*"

* * *

She did not scream. I'll grant her that. She sat down hard on her stool among the hanging charms—little brass bells, knotted threads, harmless trinkets sold to a city that no longer remembers why it once needed protection—and she stared at me, and her hands shook, and she said, very quietly:

"My mother was a herb-seller. My mother died of fever. My mother was *nobody*."

"Your mother," I said, "was the last Keeper of the Ember, and the city you live in erased her, and it erased her *on purpose*, and I watched it happen."

The bell over her door gave a small silver shiver, though there was no draft. The charms on their hooks stirred like sleeping birds. Even now, even hollowed and half-dead, the ember-stone in her pocket had begun to warm—it had been warming since the courier pressed it into her hands, warming like a coal remembering fire—and I could feel it from the sill, a faint sweetness in the air, the smell of the Hollow before the breaking. Pine and smoke and green growing things. The city smells of ash and tallow. It had forgotten even its own stink.

"Start at the beginning," she said.

"The beginning is longer than your courage," I told her. "So I'll start in the middle. Twenty years ago, nine people walked into the Ember Shrine on the longest night of the year. Eight came out. Your mother was the ninth. The city was told she betrayed the covenant—that she broke the pact between your kind and mine, that the wild lands went silent and gray because of her. They burned her name into the Warden rolls as a traitor. They burned the *true* records too, all but the ones kept by fools and birds. And then the mages worked the great forgetting, and Vell slept easy, and no one asked why the beasts stopped speaking, or why the borderlands died, or why the walls—which were raised by covenant-magic, not mason's mortar—began, year by year, to crumble."

Ada's face had gone white. "You're lying."

"I am a crow," I said. "Lying is our mother tongue. So don't believe me. Believe the stone in your pocket. Ask it why it chose you. Ask it why a dying courier ran through a city of forty thousand doors and collapsed against *yours*."

She pulled the ember-stone out. It sat in her palm like a live coal wrapped in gray felt, and it pulsed—once, twice—slow as a heartbeat, slow as something waking. I saw her feel it. I saw twenty years of careful invisibility crack straight down the middle.

"They'll come for me," she said. "The Wardens. The courier died in my shop."

"Yes."

"And you're offering to help me. Why? What does a crow care about covenants?"

I hopped closer. Close enough that she could see, in the black of my eye, the reflection of the ember-stone's glow. "Because I made a promise to a woman in an orchard at dusk, twenty years ago, and I have not yet kept it. Because your mother spent her last free hour asking me to watch over her daughter, and I have watched, and watching is not keeping. And because"—and here I gave her the truth whole, unfeathered—"because the Hollow is waking whether you go to it or not, Ada Galloway. The beasts are going silent because they are being called home. The gray is spreading because the Ember is dying. When it dies, the walls die, and everything your mother traded her life to buy—every year of peace this city squandered and forgot—dies with it. Solstice is nineteen days away."

"Nineteen days," she repeated. She looked at the stone. She looked at me. Outside, somewhere down Gilders' Row, a Warden's whistle sounded, thin and routine, and we both flinched—we, the traitor's daughter and the traitor's witness, flinching at a whistle in a city that had already forgotten what it should fear.

"If I do this," she said slowly, "if I cross into the Hollow—you'll guide me? You know the way?"

"I flew it when it was green," I said. "I'll fly it gray. The way remembers me. Whether it forgives me is another matter."

"And the Wardens?"

"One of them has kind eyes and bad judgment," I said, "but that is a story for later, and one I suspect you already know by heart."

Color rose in her cheeks. Good. Anger is fuel; shame is ash. I'd rather she burned.

"Last question," she said, and her voice steadied into something I recognized—the keeper's voice, Maren's voice, the voice that had once quieted a stampede of panicked hart with two sentences. "Why now? You've had seven years to tell me who you were. Why wait until a man bled out on my floor?"

"Because telling you sooner would have killed you," I said. "Knowledge is a scent, and Wardens hunt by scent. As long as you were only a charm seller, you were invisible. Now the stone has chosen you, and invisibility is over. So I waited for the world to break its own silence first." I ruffled my feathers, and let the last truth out gently, the way you draw a splinter. "Besides. Your mother made me promise not to tell you until you spoke to me first. She said—her words—'*she'll need to choose it. Gifts chosen are armor. Gifts given are chains.*'"

Ada was silent a long moment. Through the window, Vell went about its morning: carts rattling, vendors crying their wares, the great gray wall standing over it all like a headstone nobody could read. Somewhere beyond that wall, past the blighted March, the Hollow waited, and things older than the city were stirring in their long sleep, calling their children home.

"Nineteen days," she said again. Then she reached up and unlatched the shutters wide, letting the cold light pour across her counter, across the stone, across me. "Then we leave tonight. Before the Wardens finish their paperwork and come knocking." She fixed me with a look that was almost a smile and entirely her mother's. "Can you fly ahead and find us a road the maps won't show?"

I picked up the heel of bread at last, and ate it, and it tasted like the beginning of keeping a promise.

"For you," I said, "I'll find the road the world itself forgot."

But even as I lifted from the sill, even as her shop fell away beneath me and the gray wall rose up to meet the morning, I did not tell her the last thing I remembered of her mother's face—not the orchard, not the dusk, but the shrine, the final hour, the way Maren had looked at the eight who walked out and said, calm as weather: "*One of them will come for my daughter someday. Make sure the girl knows the difference between a guide and a trap.*"

Eight walked out of that shrine. One of them wears a Warden's coat now, and his boots are pointed toward Gilders' Row.

I flew east, into the wind, and hoped I would not have to teach Ada the difference the hard way.
