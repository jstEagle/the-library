## 4. The Warden's Oath

The summons came at the hour when Vell's lamps guttered low and the Wardens' Hall smelled of tallow and old ink—Elias Thorn's least favorite hour, because it was the hour when orders stopped being suggestions.

He stood before Magistrate Corven's desk while rain ticked against the tall window behind him, and he listened to a man who had never run anywhere in his life describe a chase.

"The courier died in the Galloway woman's shop," Corven said. He did not look up from the ledger open beneath his fingers. His voice had the dry patience of a man reading a list of debts. "Two witnesses saw him stagger through her door. She was seen speaking to him. And then—" Corven turned a page with one deliberate finger, "—he was gone."

"Gone," Elias repeated.

"Body removed. Blood mopped. Shutters closed an hour past curfew." Corven finally raised his eyes, pale as river ice. "You know her, Thorn. The charm seller. You've known her since you were both children stealing apples off the temple wall. So you will understand why I am not sending someone who *doesn't* know her. Familiarity is an asset. You'll find what she's hiding faster than a stranger would."

"And if she isn't hiding anything?"

"Then you'll clear her name in writing, and we'll all sleep soundly." Corven closed the ledger. From its pages he drew a folded sheet of paper and slid it across the desk. "One more thing. The courier carried this. Or rather, half of it—the other half burned. Our scribes can't make sense of it. Perhaps you'll have better luck."

Elias unfolded the paper on the desk, and the world went very quiet.

It was a map—or the scorched remnant of one. Ink had been seared away along its western edge in a long black tongue, but what remained was enough: the winding line of the Gray March, the blighted borderlands no honest map of Vell bothered to draw anymore. And pressed into the lower corner, half-eaten by fire but unmistakable, a seal.

A circle of interlocking leaves around a single flame.

He knew that seal. He had known it before he could write his own name. It had been stamped into the wax of his father's letters, branded onto the beam above the door of the house where he'd grown up, carved—though almost no one alive remembered why—into the old waystation stones out past the walls. His father had called it the Ember mark, and had spoken of it only once, drunk, on a night Elias was supposed to be asleep: *We used to be something, boy. Before they took the name away.*

His father had died believing the city had forgotten something worth remembering. Elias had spent twenty years being politely embarrassed about it.

Now the seal sat on a magistrate's desk, torn from a dead man's pocket, pointing west toward the Hollow.

"Where did the courier come from?" Elias asked, keeping his voice level.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if others are coming after him. If he was running from something, I should know what."

Corven studied him for a moment too long. "He came from the March. That's all the gate logs say. Find the woman, Thorn. Find out what she knows. You have three days." A thin smile. "And do try not to let old affection cloud your judgment. I'd hate to think I chose the wrong man."

*So would I,* Elias thought, and saluted, and left.

* * *

Ada Galloway's shop sat crooked at the end of Tanner's Row like a tooth that hadn't quite fallen out. Elias had walked past it a thousand times in twenty years—sometimes slowly, sometimes not letting himself slow at all—and every time he felt the same small ache behind his ribs, the ache of a door he'd chosen not to open.

He opened it now.

The bell above the frame gave its cracked little chime, and there she was, behind the counter, arranging dried lavender into paper twists with hands that stilled the instant the bell sounded. Twenty years had put lines around her eyes and silver threads through her dark hair, but she still had the same face she'd had at fourteen, when she'd pulled him out of the flooded culvert behind the tannery and sworn him to secrecy about how she'd known he was drowning—because a rat had told her.

She looked up. Their eyes met. Something passed between them that had no words in either of their vocabularies, and then she smiled the shopkeeper's smile, bright and empty as a coin.

"Warden. Looking for a charm? I've a fine one against rheumatism. Very popular with the Watch."

"Against Warden visits?"

"I'm afraid that one doesn't exist. Believe me, I've tried."

He crossed to the counter and set the scorched map down between them, unfolding it so the seal faced upward. It was a crude move and he knew it—he watched it land in her like a stone dropped in still water. Her breath caught. Her fingers curled once against the wood. But her face held, gods bless her, it held.

"You recognize it," he said quietly.

"I sell charms, Warden. Charms and lavender. I don't read maps."

"Ada." Her name came out softer than he intended, and he hated himself for it, and kept going anyway. "A man died in this room two nights ago. Bled out on your floor, by the witnesses' account. And when the Watch arrived, there was no body, no blood, nothing. Someone made a dying man disappear, and that someone is you, or you know who it is. Now there's a seal on this map that I have seen exactly one other place in my entire life, and that place is gone, and the magistrate has given me three days to bring him answers." He leaned closer. "So don't give me lavender. Give me the truth, or I can't protect you from what comes next."

For a long moment she said nothing. Rain drummed on the shutters. Somewhere down the row a dog barked twice and fell silent, and Elias noticed—filed away without understanding—that every animal on the street had gone quiet at once, as if listening.

Then Ada reached across the counter and pushed the map back toward him with one finger.

"I don't know what that is," she said. "I'm sorry about your courier. I never saw him." Her eyes, when they lifted to his, were steady and full of grief, and it was the grief that undid him—not the lie itself, but the fact that she was lying *well*, lying like a woman who had practiced invisibility for twenty years, and he suddenly understood that everything he didn't know about Ada Galloway was a country he had never been permitted to enter.

"Three days," he said. "After that, it's out of my hands."

"Thank you, Warden," she said, and the shopkeeper's smile came back up like a shutter closing.

He walked out into the rain without buying anything, and stood under the eaves across the lane, and watched her silhouette move behind the frosted glass, lighting a lamp, then another, then sitting down at her workbench with her head in her hands for a long, long time.

* * *

The report was due at dawn. Elias sat at his desk in the barracks with a blank sheet before him and a candle burning down between his knuckles, and he wrote four different openings and burned each one in turn.

*Investigated the premises of one A. Galloway, charm seller...*

True, so far as it went. The trouble was everything that came after.

Deputy Sera Voss had offered to accompany him; he'd refused, and now he understood that refusal had been the first false step, the first brick laid across a road he hadn't meant to build. A lone investigation was a hole in the record. Holes invited questions. Questions invited Corven.

He thought about his oath. He'd spoken it at nineteen, hand on the gray stone of the Hall, words older than anyone living: *I am the wall between the city and the dark. What the city forgets, I remember. What the city fears, I face.* He had believed every word. He still believed most of them. But standing in that shop, watching Ada lie to save something she wouldn't name, he had felt the oath split quietly down its middle—because the city he'd sworn to protect had sent him to hunt a woman whose only crime, so far as he could prove, was knowing too much, and the dark he'd sworn to face might be wearing a magistrate's ring.

His father's voice, drunk and low: *Before they took the name away.*

He dipped the pen. And he lied.

*Investigated the premises of A. Galloway, charm seller, Tanner's Row, in connection with the death of an unidentified courier. Found no evidence linking the subject to the courier or his effects. Subject cooperative. Premises consistent with lawful trade. Recommend no further action.*

He read it back twice. It was the first report in eleven years of service that he had falsified, and it went down easier than he expected, which frightened him more than the act itself. Lies were like that, his father might have said. The first one opens the door and the rest just walk through.

He signed it. Sealed it. Set it on the corner of the desk for morning delivery.

Then he sat very still and asked himself the question he'd been avoiding since the shop: *What are you actually doing, Thorn?* Because clearing her name bought her three days, maybe five. Corven wanted answers about that seal, and magistrates were like dogs with old bones—they did not lose interest, they buried things to dig up later. If the courier had come out of the Gray March carrying the Ember mark, then whatever was happening was already moving, and Ada Galloway was standing directly in its path, lying to the only man positioned to stand in front of her.

There was one more thing he could do. One thing that wasn't a lie and wasn't yet a betrayal.

He took a fresh sheet and wrote, in a hand disguised as a merchant's, a short note:

*They're watching your shop. Three days, then worse. If you have somewhere to be, be there soon. — E.*

He sealed it plain, no crest, and considered how to deliver it. Under her door was too obvious. Through the market post, too slow. In the end he did the simplest thing: on his rounds at dusk, he passed Tanner's Row, found her shuttered window latched but not locked—as it had been since they were children, a detail he had never let himself wonder about until tonight—and slipped the note inside among the drying herbs.

As he withdrew his hand, something moved in the shadows of the eaves above him. A crow, black as wet slate, regarding him with one eye that held far too much attention in it. It didn't startle. It didn't caw. It tilted its head, looked from Elias to the window and back again, and made a single low sound—not a crow's cry at all, but something almost like a word.

Then it lifted off the eaves and flew west, over the walls, toward the Gray March, and Elias stood in the darkening street with rain beginning again on his shoulders, understanding that he had just become part of something much larger than a falsified report.

Three days, Corven had said.

Elias touched the hilt of his Warden blade and started walking home the long way, past the old waystation stones, where the Ember mark waited in the granite like a question no one had answered in twenty years.

He had a feeling he was going to have to answer it soon.
