## 5. Crossing the Gray March

The wall of Vell had a hundred gates in its long history, and now it had one that mattered: the Sewer's Mouth, where the Graywater left the city stinking and slow. Ada had spent three nights learning its rhythm—the changing of the Warden watch, the barge that came at moonrise to dredge the outflow, the quarter hour when the grate hung open like a missing tooth.

She went through it at the bottom of the third night, wading hip-deep in water that smelled of tanneries and old lies, with a satchel of charms she no longer believed in and a crow riding her shoulder like a judgment.

"You're breathing too loud," Bramble said. His voice was dry leaves and old gravel, and hearing it aloud—actually answering her, actually *talking*—still made the hairs on her arms rise. Twenty years of silence between them, broken in a single night, and now he wouldn't stop commenting.

"I'm wading through sewage. I'll breathe however I like."

"You'll breathe quietly if you want to keep your head. The dredge crew has dogs."

"The dogs don't matter." She said it before she could stop herself, and felt the truth of it settle cold in her chest. "That's the problem, isn't it? Nothing on this side of the wall matters anymore. Not to them."

Bramble said nothing for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice was almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "No. It isn't."

* * *

They came up out of the Graywater two miles beyond the walls, where the channel widened into marshland and the reeds grew tall enough to hide a kneeling woman. Ada wrung out her skirts and looked back once at Vell: a smudge of lamplight against the stars, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, the Warden towers black and patient as headstones.

Twenty years she had lived there. Twenty years of small charms and smaller kindnesses, of keeping her eyes down and her gift buried so deep she'd half convinced herself it wasn't real. She had told herself it was survival. Standing here, outside it, watching the city glow like an ember in ash, she felt less like a survivor and more like something that had been buried alive and had only just noticed.

"Move," Bramble said. "The March doesn't get kinder with daylight."

The Gray March began where the marsh ended, and it began the way grief begins: gradually, then all at once. First the grass simply stopped being green—a gray so pale it was nearly white, growing in sparse tufts from soil the color of old bone. Then the trees came, or what passed for trees: silver-barked things with leaves like hammered tin, standing in ranks too regular to be natural, as if some enormous hand had planted them and then forgotten why.

And it was silent.

Ada had grown up in Vell, where silence meant danger and noise meant life, but she had never heard anything like this. No birdsong. No insects. No wind moving through the tin leaves. Even their own footsteps seemed muffled, swallowed by ground that didn't want to remember sound.

"It's been dying," she said. "For how long?"

"Since the Ember went dark." Bramble hopped from her shoulder to a fallen branch, walking beside her now in that strange half-loping way crows have when they choose not to fly. "The covenant was a door, girl. Doors swing both ways. Your mother's people held it open, and everything on both sides breathed through it. Magic into the city, wildness into the wild. Now—" He gestured with his beak at the dead land around them. "Now the door is shut, and everything on this side is suffocating slowly."

"And my mother helped shut it."

"She was there." Bramble's voice was carefully flat. "I was there. What she did, what she meant to do—that's a longer story, and this isn't the place for it."

"You keep saying that."

"Because you keep asking in places where the ground has ears."

Ada walked on. The scorched map rode against her ribs inside her coat, and the ember-stone rode against her heart, warm as a sleeping bird. She had studied the map by candlelight until her eyes burned: the city, the March, the river, and past all of it a hand-drawn boundary marked in faded ink—*the Hollow*—with the Ember Shrine drawn at its center like a wound.

A courier had died pressing it into her hands. A man she'd never met had trusted her with the last thing he carried, because someone, somewhere, had decided she was the one who could carry it further. She still didn't know if that was faith or cruelty.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a ruined waystation—a low stone building whose roof had fallen in, its walls furred with gray moss. Ada built no fire. Fire drew Wardens, and Wardens were the least of what might be listening.

It was full dark when she heard the hart.

Not heard, exactly. *Felt.* A wrongness in the silence, a shape moving through the tin trees with a gait that stumbled and caught, stumbled and caught. Bramble went rigid on her knee, feathers flat.

"Don't," he said.

"It's hurt."

"It's *dying*. There's a difference, and the difference is that dying things in the March aren't always only dying things."

But Ada was already moving, because twenty years of burying her gift hadn't killed it, and in this silent country it rose in her like water finding a crack. She found the hart in a hollow between the roots of the largest tree she'd ever seen—a stag the size of a horse, coat gone gray as ash, one foreleg bent at an angle legs are not meant to bend. Great antlers spread above him like the branches of a drowned forest, and his eyes—

His eyes were the worst part. They were clear, and they were afraid, and when they fixed on her they were *knowing*.

"*Keeper's daughter,*" he said.

The voice didn't come through her ears. It came the way her gift had always worked: meaning arriving whole in her mind, wearing whatever shape the beast's soul gave it. But no beast in Vell had ever spoken to her like this. This was language the way rivers are water—vast, old, barely contained by the vessel.

"I'm not—" she started. "My mother's dead. Twenty years dead."

"*Dead,*" the hart agreed, and the word carried an ache that made her knees weaken. "*All the keepers are dead, or worse. And the Ember starves. And the door stays shut.*" He struggled, trying to rise, and failed, and lay back with a shudder that ran through his whole vast body. "*You carry the stone. I can feel it burning. Little warmth in all this cold.*"

Ada knelt in the bone-gray grass. Up close she could smell him: not decay, exactly, but the absence of life, like snow that hasn't fallen yet. "Can I help you? Is there—my charms are small, but—"

"*Nothing small will mend me. I broke myself carrying a message, same as your courier. Same as everyone the Hollow sends out into the world, hoping one of them reaches a keeper before the last light goes.*" His breath fogged, though the night wasn't cold enough for it. "*So I will spend what's left differently. I will show you.*"

"Show me what?"

"*What was broken. Who broke it. What your mother—*"

"No." The word came out sharp, louder than she'd meant. Bramble shifted behind her but said nothing. "No. Whatever you think you know about my mother, I've spent my whole life not knowing, and I've made my peace with—"

"*Have you?*" The hart's gaze held hers, gentle and merciless. "*Then why did you cross the wall? Why do you carry a dead man's map toward a shrine you've never seen, if you have made your peace with not knowing?*"

Ada had no answer. The hart exhaled, long and slow, and closed his eyes.

"*Look,*" he said. "*And forgive us. We were so tired.*"

* * *

The images came broken, the way a shattered window still shows you the sky.

*A hall of white stone, pillars carved with running beasts. Her mother—younger than Ada had ever known her, dark-haired, laughing, a circlet of antler-bone on her brow—standing at the center of a circle of robed figures, her hands raised over a basin of fire.*

*Fire. Real fire, living fire, the Ember itself, and Ada understood with a jolt that she was seeing the shrine, seeing the covenant renewed as it had been renewed for centuries, her mother's voice rising in words that were older than the city—*

*Then other faces. Men in Warden gray, but older uniforms, finer. Mages with hollow eyes. And among them, close enough to touch her mother, a man whose face made the vision shudder—*

*The image fractured. Reformed. Night now. Her mother running through these same tin woods, the ember-stone clutched to her chest, blood on her sleeve. Behind her, the sky over the shrine glowing orange, wrong-orange, burning-orange. Voices shouting words Ada couldn't hold onto: *seal it, seal it, we have no choice, it's calling something, it's calling something UP—*

*Her mother turning to face pursuers she couldn't see, pressing the stone into the roots of a tree, whispering: *keep it, keep it, let them think me traitor, let them forget me entirely, only let the stone wait—*

*And then hands. Gray hands. And darkness.*

The vision snapped off like a snuffed candle.

Ada was on her hands and knees in the grass, shaking, her breath coming in ragged pieces. The hart's great head had lowered until his brow nearly touched hers.

"*You saw,*" he said. It wasn't a question.

"She was running *from* them." Ada's voice cracked on the second word. "She was—she hid the stone. She told them to make her a traitor. She *chose* it, so the stone would survive, so someone would—" She couldn't finish. Twenty years of believing her mother had helped break the covenant, twenty years of a shame she'd never once spoken aloud, and it had been a lie. A lie her mother had *built*, deliberately, with her own destruction as the mortar.

"*The city forgot its name,*" the hart murmured. "*The mages saw to that. And the keepers became traitors in every story that survived. It was cleaner that way. Safer. If no one remembers the door, no one looks for the key.*" His breath rattled. "*Your mother bought twenty years with her name. The question, keeper's daughter, is what you will buy with yours.*"

"I don't understand what happened at the shrine. The images—they broke. There was a man, and fire, and they said it was *calling* something—"

"*Some doors are shut from outside,*" the hart said. "*Some are shut from within. Ask the crow. He stood closer to the fire than any of us.*"

Behind her, wings beat once. Ada turned. Bramble stood on a root, and in the starlight she could see something in the old bird's posture she had never seen in twenty years of his company: he looked *afraid*.

"Bramble," she said. "What did my mother do?"

The crow was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was lower than she'd ever heard it.

"Not here," he said. "There's a waystation ahead—old, from before the forgetting. Walls thick enough that the March can't listen. I'll tell you there." He paused, and his head cocked, one bright eye catching the light. "And you'd best sleep first, girl. Because after I tell you what happened the night the Ember burned, you're going to wish you'd never learned to talk to birds at all."

The hart sighed once, deep and long, like a wind going out. By the time Ada turned back to him, his clear eyes had gone dim, and the great antlers lay still against the roots, and the Gray March had taken another voice into its silence.

She closed them gently with her palm, and stood, and followed the crow into the dark.
