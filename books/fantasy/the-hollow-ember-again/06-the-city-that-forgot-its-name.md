## 6. The City That Forgot Its Name

Crows do not forget. That is the first thing you should know about us, and the last thing anyone believes.

Men remember what they are told. Crows remember what we saw.

I had carried the truth of that night for twenty years, folded up inside me like a stolen coin, and I had never once spent it—because there was no one left alive to spend it on. Or so I believed until a charm seller with her mother's stubborn jaw finally opened her beak and *spoke* to me, two days past the Gray March, in a voice cracked from disuse, asking what I knew about the Ember.

Everything, little keeper's daughter. I knew everything.

But the telling of it needed walls around it and a roof, however broken, because some truths are like birds: let them loose in open country and they'll scatter before you can catch their meaning again.

The waystation rose out of the blight like a rib cage picked clean. It had been a grand thing once—I remembered it when its lanterns burned and its stables smelled of hay and horse sweat instead of ash and rot. The Hollow Road had run through it, the artery between Vell and the wild lands, thick with couriers and pilgrims and mages in their gray traveling cloaks. Now its roof gaped to the sky, its beams were black as burnt bone, and the only travelers were the wind and me and the woman who crouched at its threshold with her hand resting on a scorched map as if it might bolt.

"Inside," I said, hopping along the sill of a shattered window. "And mind the third floorboard. It lies."

Ada Galloway stepped over the lying floorboard without question, which told me she was learning. Twenty years of silence had taught her to read the world sideways—the way beasts do—and now that she'd finally let herself use the skill, she was hungry for it. She built a small fire in the old hearth, using wood I chose for her because everything in this place was mine to choose; I had known these beams when they were trees.

She sat. She waited. She did not fill the silence with human chatter, and I will confess something no crow admits: that pleased me.

"You said you served my mother," she said at last. "Before. You said you were there."

"I was there," I said. "I was more than there. I was hers."

* * *

Let me tell it properly, the way it happened, not the way the city remembers—which is to say, not at all.

Twenty years ago, Vell had another name. Say it soft, even now: **Vellamaris**, the City Beneath the Tide of Light. It stood at the hinge of two worlds, the human realm on one side and the Hollow on the other, and between them burned the Ember—a fire older than either realm, older than the first king or the last wolf. The Ember was not a flame the way your hearth is a flame. It was an agreement given a body. Every covenant needs a heart, and the Ember was ours: it held the pact between humankind and the wild lands, the promise that each would leave the other room to live.

Your mother kept it.

Maren Galloway was the last Keeper of the Ember, though no crown ever named her so and no history ever wrote her down. Keepers were never written down—that was the point of them. They lived plain lives in plain houses and tended a fire most people walked past without seeing, because the Ember hid itself from eyes that weren't meant to find it. Maren sold charms in the market, just as you do, Ada. Just as you do. Did you never wonder why a woman with so little magic in her hands could sell charms that actually worked?

I met her when I was young and full of thorns. She found me with a wing broken by a boy's sling-stone, and instead of eating me—as any sensible person would have—she set the wing and talked to me the whole time, really *talked*, the way no one had since I'd learned that humans mostly make noise at each other rather than to anything. She never once asked me to serve her. So I did.

For eleven years I flew the circuit of her keeping. I watched her feed the Ember with small things—memories, mostly, little ones, a summer afternoon here, a stranger's kindness there—because that is what the covenant ate. Not blood. Not gold. Memory. A living pact wants a living price, and nothing is more alive than what a person carries in their head. Small payments, freely given, and the Ember stayed warm, and the wild lands stayed wild but gentle, and Vellamaris stayed bright.

Then came the night the mages came.

* * *

Ada had gone very still by the fire. In the light her face looked carved, all the softness gone out of it, and I understood that she was already ahead of me—already seeing where the story bent. Children of liars always know the shape of the lie before they know its contents. It's why they flinch at certain tones of voice.

"Keep going," she said.

"There were nine of them," I said. "Gray cloaks. I counted from the rafters of the shrine, because counting is what crows do when men gather in numbers—they're deciding something, and decisions made by nine men in secret are never good. They came to the Ember at moonset, and they did not kneel to it. That was the first wrong thing. Everyone knelt to the Ember. Kings knelt. Your mother knelt every dawn of her life, not because it demanded it but because reverence keeps a door oiled."

"They didn't come to worship," Ada said quietly.

"No. They came to close the door." I hopped down from the beam to the hearthstone, closer to her, because this part deserved proximity. "They called it mercy. I want you to hold onto that word, girl, because it's the one they used to choke the truth with. *Mercy.* The wild lands had grown restless—this was true. Beasts crossing borders, old things waking in the deep woods, a hart seen on the outskirts of the city in broad daylight. The mages said the covenant was failing anyway, that the price was rising, that soon it would eat whole minds instead of afternoons. And maybe they even believed it. Belief is cheap. It lets men do terrible work and call it necessity."

"But my mother—"

"Your mother stood in front of the Ember," I said, "with nothing but her body and her voice, and she told them no."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, something moved through the dead grass, and I marked it and dismissed it—small, harmless, afraid. Everything out here was afraid. That was what the breaking had done.

"She told them the covenant wasn't failing," I went on. "She told them it was being *starved*—that the city had stopped paying, stopped believing, that the mages themselves had been skimming the Ember's warmth for their own workings for years and calling the resulting hunger a natural decay. She told them that if they sealed it, the wild lands would not die. They would simply stop being bound. And she was right, Ada. Gods, she was right, and they knew she was right, and that is the only reason what happened next happened the way it did."

"Because they couldn't refute her," Ada whispered.

"Because they couldn't afford to let her speak anywhere else." My feathers lifted along my neck, the way they still do, twenty years on, when I fly back over that night. "There were nine of them and one of her, and she was the last Keeper, and her word carried weight in places their authority had never reached. So they did not argue with her. They erased her. All of it—the rite of unbinding they worked that night took the memory of the pact out of every mind in Vellamaris, mage and merchant and child, and it took the city's name with it, because the name was part of the pact, woven into it. Vellamaris forgot itself and woke up as Vell, a city that had always been magic-free, always closed, always safe. Safe. There's that word again. They built twenty years of peace on it like a house on a grave."

"And her?" Ada's voice had gone thin. "What did the rite take from her?"

I looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried the ruined roof, and the stars through the gap wheeled slow and indifferent, and I thought: *twenty years I've carried this, and it has not gotten lighter, and it will not get lighter for her either.*

"The rite needed an anchor," I said. "A memory large enough to hold all the others. Something with roots deep enough in the pact to draw the whole of it out. They used the Keeper. They fed her to her own fire—not her body. Her *self*. Every memory she had of the Ember, of the covenant, of her office, of the wild lands, of the people she'd protected—gone into the sealing. What walked away from that shrine was a woman who sold charms in a market and could not have told you why. Who woke the next morning in a house she didn't quite recognize, beside a daughter she loved with her whole heart and could not entirely explain."

Ada made a sound. Small. I have heard that sound from dying animals and I have heard it from grieving women and it is the same sound, and there is no comfort in the world shaped right to fit against it.

"She didn't betray the covenant," Ada said. "Everyone—the stories, the whispers, the things people say about the woman who broke the pact—"

"Who says that?" I asked sharply. "Who in Vell says your mother broke anything? No one remembers the pact exists. No one remembers your mother at all."

"No one human," Ada said. "But the beasts remember. Out on the March, the hart showed me her face among the betrayers. Bramble—it *showed* me her standing with them. I've spent two days hating her."

"Then hate the image, not the woman," I said, "because the image is theirs. Nine mages worked a great erasing, and erasings leak. Memory doesn't vanish clean, girl—it smears. What leaked into the wild lands was the shape of that night without its meaning: a woman at the Ember, mages around her, fire going dark. The beasts read it the only way it could be read. They don't know about anchors. They don't know she went into that fire willingly, at the end, when she saw what the rite would do unanchored—when she saw it would burn the city's children's minds blank instead of just their pasts. She climbed onto the hearthstone herself. I watched her do it. I screamed at her from the rafters and she looked up at me and she smiled, Ada, she *smiled*, and she said—"

My voice caught. Crows' voices don't catch; we are not built for it. But something in my chest did, some old scar tissue pulling tight.

"She said, 'Look after the little one. She'll have the gift, and the world won't be kind to her for it. Look after her until she can look after herself.'"

The fire had burned low. Ada sat with her arms wrapped around her knees, and tears ran down her face without her permission or her movement, the silent kind, the kind that fall like the first rain after drought.

"Twenty years," she said finally. "You watched me for twenty years."

"I watched you for eleven years before that too, from the rafters of your mother's kitchen. You used to throw crusts at me. Terrible aim."

A wet, broken almost-laugh. Good. Grief needs somewhere to go that isn't straight down.

"Why didn't you tell me sooner?" she asked. "All those years. Why let me hide? Why let me think—"

"Because telling you would have killed you," I said flatly. "A Warden catches a charm seller talking to a crow, she's fined. A Warden catches the daughter of the last Keeper of the Ember carrying the gift and knowing what it's *for*—she disappears. The mages who worked that rite are not all dead, Ada. Some of them sit high in Vell's councils under other names, keeping the seal patched, keeping the silence swept. They erased a city once. Erasing one woman is an afternoon's work. I kept you ignorant because ignorance was armor. And then a courier died in your shop with a map in his hand, and the armor stopped mattering, because the Hollow is waking whether you know the truth or not."

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—something burned out of it, something else forged in its place.

"The map. The ember-stone. The courier said the shrine would take a price." She looked up at me, and her eyes in the firelight were her mother's eyes exactly, and I had to look away. "Bramble. If the Ember eats memories—if that's its food—then whatever it asks of me at that shrine—"

"Will be real," I said. "Yes. I won't lie to you about that. I've done enough lying by omission for one lifetime, and the Ember does not accept counterfeit coin. It will ask you for something you cannot bear to give, and you will give it anyway, because the alternative is watching both realms come apart at the seam."

She nodded slowly, and fed the last of the wood to the fire, and I settled on the hearthstone beside her, closer than I had ever let myself perch in twenty years of shadowing her.

"One more thing," I said, as she banked the coals. "The waystation. I brought us here for a reason. This was the last courier post before the Hollow Road, and couriers kept logs. If any record of the pact survived the erasing, it'll be in the strongroom below—paper doesn't live in heads, and paper can't be eaten by a rite."

Ada turned toward the dark mouth of the cellar stairs, where cold air breathed up out of the ground smelling of stone and old ink.

And from the darkness below, before either of us took a single step, came the sound of pages turning.
