## 7. Thorn at the Ford

The rain had followed Elias Thorn out of Vell like a debt he couldn't shake, and by the time he reached the Gray March it had settled into a fine, needling drizzle that turned the world the color of old pewter. He rode with his hood down. Let the water find him. It was a small penance, and he suspected he would owe far larger ones before this was done.

Behind him, Deputy Corren Hale kept pace on his dun mare, silent in the way that had always unsettled Elias more than talk. Hale was twenty-six and ambitious the way knives are sharp—useful, until you forgot which end you were holding. He had been quiet for two days now. Too quiet.

*He knows,* Elias thought, for perhaps the hundredth time since they'd left the city gates. *Or he suspects. And suspicion is worse.*

The courier's death had set the Wardens humming like a kicked hive. Three days after Ada's shop had been sealed with wax and official ribbon, the order had come down from Warden-Commander Vess herself: *find the charm seller Galloway. Question her. If she flees, treat her as an accomplice.* Elias had read the order twice, his thumb pressed hard against the wax seal as if he could melt it back into blankness.

Ada Galloway. The woman who mended broken teacups with charms that lasted exactly as long as grief did. The woman who had once laughed so hard at his terrible cooking that she'd cried, and who had looked at him across her little shop counter three years ago with something in her eyes he'd been too much of a coward to name. The woman whose map-seal—the same seal burned into the courier's dying hand—had hung above his childhood bed, because his father had served under her mother in the last days of the old covenant, and had never stopped mourning what was lost.

*Question her,* the order said. As if there were questions gentle enough for what was coming.

They found the ford at midmorning, where the river Sere cut through the blighted borderlands in a long gray ribbon. The water ran slow and strange here—too clear, too still, moving without sound, as though the land itself had forgotten how rivers were meant to behave. On the far bank, the March stretched away in bruised purples and dead grasses, and somewhere beyond it, invisible but unmistakable to anything with blood in its veins, lay the Hollow.

And at the water's edge, small and mud-spattered and utterly unrepentant about it, stood Ada Galloway.

She was crouched, filling a waterskin, a black crow perched on her pack like a gargoyle with opinions. She hadn't seen them yet. The rain blurred everything; the land swallowed hoofbeats. Elias reined up beneath a leaning dead elm and sat there, watching her, feeling the moment stretch like wire about to snap.

"Commander." Hale's voice, low. "That's her."

"I see it's her, Deputy."

"The order says—"

"I know what the order says." His own voice came out flatter than he intended, and he heard Hale register the flatness, file it away. Twenty years of service, and it took one falsified report to make his deputy start listening to him properly.

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it. He'd already lied once—written in his neat Warden's hand that the charm seller's shop had shown no sign of flight, no evidence of contact with the courier, recommending surveillance rather than arrest. A lie bought time. Time was a currency, and he was spending all of it on her.

But orders could be countermanded. Other hunters could be sent. Vess didn't trust anyone fully these days, least of all him—he'd seen it in her eyes when she gave the order, that long calculating look. Which meant someone else might already be on the road behind them. Which meant every hour he spent riding *toward* Ada instead of reporting her position was an hour stolen from whatever mercy the city still had left to offer her.

Elias dismounted.

"Sir?" Hale's voice sharpened. "What are you doing?"

"What I should have done three years ago," Elias said, and walked down toward the river.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him—of course she did, she'd always heard him, some part of her tuned to his footsteps the way a tuning fork is tuned to its note. She came up off her heels and turned, and for one suspended heartbeat they simply looked at each other across forty feet of dead grass and twenty years of silence neither of them had chosen.

"You're a long way from your jurisdiction, Warden Thorn." Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; he saw them flex at her sides, saw her weight shift backward, ready to run. The crow lifted off her pack and circled once, screaming a raw note of warning.

"Ada." Just her name. It came out rougher than he meant it to, scraped raw somewhere between the city and the river.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you say my name like that and I forget you're wearing that badge. I've seen what your Wardens did to the tinker's family in Lowmarket. I know how this ends."

"It doesn't have to end that way."

"Doesn't it?" Something cracked open in her face then—not fear, worse, *grief*, the kind that comes from hoping against evidence. "Elias, there's a courier dead in my shop's doorway. There's a map with my mother's seal on it telling me the sky is falling. There are Wardens hunting me, and you—" her voice caught, steadied, "—you rode three days through the rain to catch me. So tell me how this doesn't end the way these things always end."

He closed the distance while she talked. Ten feet. Close enough to see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the scratch healing along her jaw, the ember-stone hanging at her throat, dull as a coal gone cold.

Except it wasn't cold. Even at ten feet, even through the rain, he could feel it—a faint warmth against his own skin, like standing near a hearth in another room. His father had described that warmth once, drunk and weeping, the night before he died: *the Ember remembers us, boy. That's what they took from us. Not magic. Memory.*

"Vess sent me," Elias said quietly. "But she'll send others. Kestrel Markham left the city yesterday with six blades. He won't stop at a ford to talk."

Ada went very still. "Then why are you here?"

"Because I'm not going to stop you." He said it fast, before courage failed him, the way you pull a splinter. "I'm going to help you. Or at least—I'm going to fail to stop you as loudly as I can manage, and give you the time the difference buys."

For a long moment she just stared at him. Rain dripped off her chin. The crow landed on a stone between them and cocked its head with an air of profound skepticism, as if it had heard this speech before from better men.

"Elias," Ada said slowly, "do you understand what you're saying? They'll hang you. Or worse—they'll *unmake* you. The Wardens have ways. Old ways. You'd be erased from the rolls. Your name struck. Your mother would be told you died of fever."

"My mother's been telling people I died of fever since I joined the Wardens. She'll hardly notice the difference."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed. "It's really not."

* * *

He drew the blade.

Ada flinched—then stilled as she understood what he was actually doing. Not drawing to threaten. Drawing to *give*. He held it out across both palms, the way a squire presents a sword to a knight, the way his father had presented his own blade to Ada's mother twenty-two years ago, on the last morning either of them had believed in anything.

It was a good blade. Warden steel, forged in the old style, unadorned except for the seal stamped into the ricasso: a circle broken by a single vertical line. The mark of the covenant wardens, older than the city, older than the lie. His father had carried it. His father's father before him. It had never been drawn against anything that bled.

"They'll know it anywhere," Ada whispered. "Any Warden who sees this will know—"

"—that whoever holds it speaks with Thorn's authority," Elias finished. "Markham's men won't love it, but they'll hesitate. Hesitation is hours. Hours is distance. Take it."

"I can't take your sword, Elias."

"You can, and you will, because I can't go with you." The words cost him more than he showed. He'd rehearsed this on the road, all the noble versions, all the clean ones. None of them survived contact with her actual face. "If I cross that river with you, they don't hunt a fugitive anymore. They hunt a traitor with a name and a rank and a garrison full of people who answer to him. But if I stay—if I report that I found you, that you overpowered me, that I'm pursuing alone—" he shrugged, rain sliding off his shoulders, "—then I'm still useful. Still inside. Still able to misdirect, delay, lose dispatches. I can buy you weeks from the inside. Out there I'm just another mouth to feed and a slower horse."

"And if they figure out you let me go?"

"Then I'll be hanged as a traitor," he said simply, "and you'll be at the shrine, and the covenant will hold, and everyone I've ever failed will be slightly less damned. Ada. I've spent twenty years being a good Warden in a city built on a bad lie. Let me spend the rest of it being something else."

The crow made a sound then—not a caw, something lower, almost a word—and hopped closer to the blade, studying the seal with one bright eye.

"He remembers it," Ada said softly. "Bramble. He knew my mother. He's been trying to tell me for years, and I—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Elias, my mother helped break the covenant. The man who died in my shop said so. Everything I thought I knew about her—"

"Your mother," Elias said, "was the last Keeper of the Ember, and my father died defending her retreat, and if you repeat what the dying man told you to anyone else I will deny ever having this conversation." He watched her reel. "I don't know how I know that. I know it the way I know my own name. It's in the seal. It's in the blade. It's in whatever's left of the oath my family swore and was made to forget. Take the sword, Ada. Please."

She took it.

Her fingers brushed his around the scabbard, and neither of them moved for a moment, and the rain fell, and the river made no sound at all, and Elias thought: *three years I could have had this. Three years of standing in her shop buying charms I didn't need, being a coward with excellent manners.*

"Thank you," she said. And then, fiercer, stepping close enough that he could see the ember-stone flicker against her throat like a heartbeat: "Come find me after. Whatever happens at the shrine—after. Do you understand? Don't you dare die holding a pass or a bridge or any other stupid heroic piece of ground. Come *find me*."

"I've never successfully disobeyed you in my life," Elias said. "Why start now?"

* * *

He was turning back toward the horses when Hale's voice cut across the ford like a whipcrack.

"Commander!"

Hale stood by the dead elm, mounted, and he wasn't looking at Elias. He was looking at Ada—at the Warden blade in her hands, at the seal catching what little light the day allowed. His face had gone carefully, terribly blank.

"How long have you been standing there?" Elias asked.

"Long enough, sir." Hale's hand rested on his own hilt, not drawing, not sheathing. Calculating. "Long enough to know that when we get back to Vell, one of us is going to be lying in our report. And I'd very much like to know which one of us it's going to be before I decide what I write."

Across the river, Ada had frozen halfway to the water's edge. The crow screamed once, a sound like tearing metal.

Elias stepped forward, placing himself squarely between his deputy and the woman he loved, and felt—with strange, unexpected calm—the last bridge behind him burn.

"Hale," he said. "Let me tell you a story about a circle with a line through it."

Far to the south, faint as a memory, thunder rolled over a city that had forgotten its own name—and on the road behind them, dust rose where six riders were coming fast.
