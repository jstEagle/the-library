## 8. The Hollow Opens

The trees ended all at once, the way a sentence ends—not fading, but cut.

Ada stood at the last rowan on the ridge and looked down into a country that should not have existed so close to Vell. Below her, the land fell away into a valley of green so deep it seemed lit from beneath, as if the roots of the world had caught fire and were glowing up through the soil. Mist moved through it in slow rivers. And everywhere—everywhere—she could feel them. Not hear them. *Feel* them: a pressure of attention like standing in a room where every portrait's eyes have opened.

"They're watching," she said quietly.

"Of course they're watching." Bramble shifted on her shoulder, his talons kneading her cloak with a restlessness he'd shown for three days straight. "You've come to their door wearing your mother's face and carrying a dead woman's stone. What did you expect? A welcome feast?"

"I expected you to stop mentioning my mother."

"And I expected you to sleep more than two hours a night since the ford. We are both disappointed creatures." He ruffled his feathers, but there was no bite in it. Since Thorn had pressed his blade into her hands at the water's edge—since the deputy's shout had chased them into the hills—Bramble had stopped pretending to be only a bird. He perched closer now. He spoke more gently, when he spoke at all.

Ada touched the ember-stone at her throat. It had been warm since the hart died under her palms three days ago, warm since the broken images of her mother had poured through her—the betrayer's face, the sealed gate, the lie that had built Ada's whole life like a wall around a hollow house. She still didn't know which memory was true. The hart's dying vision or Bramble's telling of it. Perhaps both. Perhaps neither.

There was one way to find out. It was waiting below her, and it was not patient.

She started down.

* * *

The grass of the valley was wrong in a way she couldn't name until she knelt and pressed her palm flat against it. Then she understood: it grew toward her. Blades curved inward like cupped hands, like an audience leaning forward. The mist tasted of iron and honey. Somewhere ahead, water ran uphill with a sound like slow applause.

And the beasts came out to meet her.

They came without sound, which frightened her more than roaring would have. A stag with antlers like winter branches. Three wolves whose fur held shadows that didn't match their bodies. A fox with too many tails, each one moving independently, tasting the air. A bear the size of a haywain, old and gray-muzzled, walking with the deliberate dignity of something that remembered when the world was younger and had not been impressed by anything since.

They formed a crescent around her, twenty paces off, and stopped.

Ada stopped too. Her heart hammered against the ember-stone. Every instinct honed by twenty years of hiding screamed at her to lower her eyes, to make herself small, to be nothing. In Vell, being seen was death. In Vell, she had made an art of being overlooked.

But this was not Vell.

"You know why I've come," she said aloud—and then, before she could stop herself, before the habit of a lifetime could clamp down, she let the gift rise through her voice like light through water. "You know why I've come," she said again, and this time the words went out carrying everything underneath them: the rat in the trap she'd failed to free, the dying courier's blood on her floorboards, the hart's last images burning behind her eyes, twenty years of silence cracking open all at once.

The valley heard her.

She felt it the way she felt weather—a change in pressure, a gathering. The grass stilled. The mist paused mid-drift. Every beast in the crescent turned its head in the same small motion, like a key turning in a lock.

Then the bear spoke.

Not in words, exactly. In meaning that arrived whole inside her skull, heavy and slow as a glacier shifting: **YOU SPEAK WITH THE OLD TONGUE. WHO TAUGHT YOU?**

"No one taught me. It was born in me." She swallowed. "From my mother."

**YES.** The word landed like a dropped stone. The wolves' ears flattened. The many-tailed fox went rigid. **WE KNOW HER NAME. WE HAVE NOT SPOKEN IT IN TWENTY YEARS, AND NOW IT WALKS BACK INTO OUR VALLEY ON HER DAUGHTER'S LEGS.**

"I'm not her."

**NO,** the bear agreed, terrible and calm. **YOU ARE NOT HER YET. THAT IS WHAT YOU MUST PROVE.**

* * *

They circled her then—all of them, the whole crescent breaking apart and flowing around her like water around a stone, close enough now that she could smell the musk and frost of them, could feel the heat radiating off the bear's shoulders. Bramble lifted from her perch and wheeled above, screaming warnings she couldn't parse, and the beasts ignored him entirely.

**THE LAST KEEPER STOOD WHERE YOU STAND,** the bear said, from behind her now, from everywhere. **THE NIGHT THE EMBER WAS SEALED. SHE CAME TO US BLEEDING AND BEGGING, AND SHE TOLD US THE HUMANS HAD TURNED ON HER, THAT THE COVENANT MUST BE BROKEN TO BE SAVED. SHE ASKED US TO TRUST HER.**

"She lied?" Ada's voice came out thin.

**SHE DID NOT LIE. THAT IS THE TRAGEDY. SHE BELIEVED EVERY WORD.** The bear's meaning darkened. **AND THEN SHE SEALED THE EMBER ANYWAY, AND THE SEALING TOOK HER MEMORIES OF WHY, AND SHE WENT HOME TO YOUR CITY AND FORGOT SHE HAD EVER BEEN OUR KEEPER AT ALL. WE WATCHED HER FORGET. WE COULD HAVE STOPPED IT. WE DID NOT.**

The ground seemed to tilt. Ada gripped the ember-stone through her shirt, its warmth pulsing against her palm like a second heartbeat. "That's not what the hart showed me. The hart showed me betrayal—her face among the ones who broke it."

**THE HART SHOWED YOU WHAT ITS DYING EYES SAW: A KEEPER AT THE SHRINE ON THE NIGHT THE LIGHTS WENT OUT. IT DID NOT SHOW YOU WHICH SIDE OF THE KNIFE SHE STOOD ON. MEMORY IS A BLADE, DAUGHTER-OF-MAERWEN. IT CUTS ACCORDING TO THE HAND THAT HOLDS IT.**

Her mother's name. Maerwen. She'd never known it—had grown up with only *your mother*, a closed door, a face in no portraits. Now it sat inside her chest like a coal.

"Why tell me this now?"

**BECAUSE THE SHRINE WILL OFFER YOU THE SAME CHOICE SHE MADE.** The beasts had stopped circling. They faced her now, all of them, a wall of eyes and breath. **THE EMBER DOES NOT BURN WITHOUT FUEL. TO REFORGE THE COVENANT, IT WILL DEMAND A PRICE FROM THE ONE WHO KINDLES IT. MAERWEN PAID WITH HER OWN PAST. YOU WILL PAY WITH SOMETHING OF EQUAL WEIGHT. AND WE CANNOT TELL YOU WHAT IT WILL TAKE, BECAUSE WE DO NOT KNOW. THE EMBER KNOWS ONLY WHAT YOU LOVE.**

The mist had gone very still. Somewhere far behind her, north and east, toward the pass she and Bramble had crossed, a bird alarm-called once and fell silent. Wardens, maybe. Or worse. Solstice eve was tomorrow.

"And if I refuse to pay?"

**THEN THE HOLLOW DIES, AND THE WILD LANDS GO SILENT FOREVER, AND YOUR CITY'S WALLS HOLD BACK NOTHING BUT THE MEMORY OF WHAT THEY ONCE KEPT OUT.** The bear lowered its great head until its breath stirred her hair—warm, grass-sweet, unbearably sad. **WE DO NOT DEMAND YOU PAY. UNDERSTAND THIS. WE ONLY DEMAND THAT YOU CHOOSE WITH OPEN EYES, AS SHE DID NOT.**

* * *

They led her to the threshold as the sun went down.

It was not a door. It was a place where the green simply gave up—where the valley's impossible lushness met a ring of ash maybe fifty feet wide, and beyond the ash rose the shrine: a hill of black glass crowned with a single point of dull red light, like a coal buried under snowfall, breathing. In. Out. In. Out. Twenty years of almost-dead, and still breathing.

The beasts would go no further than the ash line. They stood at its edge in a silent rank as Ada walked forward alone, Bramble riding her shoulder with his feathers slicked flat.

At the center of the burned ring, she turned back. The first stars were out. Behind the beasts, the valley glowed faintly, root-fire green. Ahead, the Ember pulsed its slow dying rhythm.

**DAUGHTER-OF-MAERWEN.** The bear's voice followed her across the ash. **ONE MORE THING. WHEN THE EMBER ASKS ITS PRICE, IT WILL NOT ASK FOR WHAT YOU VALUE MOST. IT WILL ASK FOR WHAT YOU CANNOT BEAR TO LOSE. THESE ARE NOT ALWAYS THE SAME THING. REMEMBER THE DIFFERENCE.**

Ada's hand found the Warden blade at her hip—Thorn's blade, given at the ford with a look she still hadn't finished understanding. She thought of him holding the pass behind her. She thought of his report, falsified; his career, spent; his voice saying *go* like it cost him a rib to say it.

She thought of the shop. The quiet mornings. The life she'd built out of smallness and safety, and the man who had almost made her believe she deserved a bigger one.

"What do I love?" she whispered—to the Ember, to the beasts, to no one.

The ember-stone flared hot against her chest, and every beast on the ash line bowed its head at once, and Bramble said, very softly, in the voice of an old servant who has watched this exact grief before:

"Now you understand why your mother never came home whole."

The Ember breathed in. The Ember breathed out. And somewhere behind her, in the dark of the pass, steel rang against steel.
