## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards had a sound, if you'd stood watch long enough to hear it.

Edmund Vasquez had stood eleven years of watches at the Ashen Valley's border, and she could hear it the way old sailors heard weather in their knees: a low, continuous hum beneath the world, like a struck bell that never finished ringing. The recruits never caught it. They heard wind in the gray pines and the creak of the watchtower timbers, and they thought that was all there was. But Edmund had learned to listen past the wind, down to that buried tone, and for eleven years it had been the most constant thing in her life. More constant than the company. More constant than Tomas, who wrote twice a year from the mill town and once sent a letter so late she'd nearly ridden down to check he hadn't drowned in his own flour.

More constant, even, than Vael's silence.

That silence was the oldest thing of all. Everyone in the valley grew up on the same story: the god Vael had walked among their great-grandparents' great-grandparents, and then one day he simply stopped speaking. No wrath, no departure, no burning bush or shattered idol. He just went quiet, the way a house goes quiet when everyone you love has left it. The priests called it the Long Rest. The mercenaries called it Tuesday. Edmund had been raised on both versions and believed neither entirely, because she had been born without any feeling of him at all—no warmth in the shrines, no weight in prayer—and she'd decided young that a god who wouldn't answer wasn't worth the ache of waiting for.

So she listened to the wards instead. They were honest. They hummed.

On the night the stars went out, the humming stopped between one breath and the next.

She was halfway through the third hour of her watch, boots up on the parapet rail, working a whetstone along her belt knife because the company's quartermaster charged for sharpening and Edmund Vasquez did not pay for what she could do herself. Below her, the border stones ran off into the dark like a row of pulled teeth, each one carved with the same spiral sigil that every child in the valley could draw before they could write their own name. Beyond them, the outer world lay flat and black under a sky salted with stars—the way it always was, the way it had been every night of her life, because the valley's sky was as reliable as its silence.

The knife slipped. Not because her hand moved. Because the *sound* went out of the world.

It was like standing in a room your whole life and only noticing the ticking of the clock when it dies. The absence hit her in the sternum. She sat up so fast her spine cracked against the parapet stone, and for three full seconds she didn't move, didn't breathe, just listened with her whole body to a silence that was suddenly, horribly complete.

Then the first star went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. It was simply *gone*, snuffed like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the darkness where it had been didn't fill back in. Edmund's eyes went to it instinctively, the way eyes go to a missing tooth, and while she stared, a second star died, then a third, then a handful, then a swath, as though something vast were moving across the sky eating light, or as though someone above were closing shutters one by one over windows that had burned for longer than the valley had kept records.

"Corvo," she said. Nothing. "Corvo." Louder now, and the word came out wrong, scraped thin.

Corvo Ives was the senior watch that night, a broad, gray-bearded man dozing sitting up against the tower door with his crossbow across his knees like a sleeping hound. He woke the way veterans wake—all at once, hand already on the stock—and followed her pointing finger up into the sky. His face did something complicated. She watched him try out explanations the way a man tries keys in a stubborn lock.

"Cloud," he said finally.

"It's not cloud."

"Fog off the high snows, then. Comes down fast some nights."

"Corvo." She made herself speak slowly, the way you speak to a man walking in his sleep near a cliff edge. "There is no fog. There is no cloud. Look at the horizon line. You can see the mountains. The stars are going *out*."

He looked. And this time she saw him actually see it—a star dying while he watched, another after it, the black spreading across the sky like ink dropped in water. His jaw worked. Eleven years she'd known Corvo, and she had seen him face a hillside charge of raiders with an expression of mild professional interest. Now his knuckles had gone white around the crossbow.

"Weather," he said, but the word had no bones in it.

Below them, the camp stirred. The company—Brass Company, forty-odd spears and bows under Captain Ondine Marrow, hired by the valley council to guard a border that hadn't seen true trouble in living memory—was waking in the ugly, muttering way of soldiers denied sleep for no reason they respected. Edmund heard Ondine's voice cutting through it, sharp and level: lanterns, kit, nobody panics. Good officer habits. Edmund had learned most of hers from watching Ondine do exactly this.

But Edmund couldn't make her feet carry her down the ladder. Because there was something else happening inside her, something that had no place in any soldier's report, and she needed a moment before she had to stand in lantern light and lie about it.

For the first time in her life, Edmund Vasquez had felt her god.

It hadn't been a voice, exactly. It had been older than voice—a pressure behind her ribs, a heat in the mark on her forearm that she'd carried since birth and never once thought about, the small gray spiral every child of the valley bore somewhere on their skin, faded to nothing, decorative as a birthmark. Except it wasn't faded anymore. In the starless dark she pushed back her sleeve and the spiral was *lit*, faintly, sullenly, like coals raked over and still holding fire. And through it, straight into the marrow of her, had come a single word—not heard so much as *known*, the way you know you're falling before you feel the ground:

*Run.*

One word. From a god who had not spoken in six generations. One word, and it wasn't comfort, wasn't warning, wasn't explanation. It was the word you shout to someone standing on ice that's already cracking.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of detached irritation, the way she'd watch a recruit fumble a simple drill. Eleven years of discipline, and it took a dead sky and a whispering birthmark to undo it.

She got down the ladder. She put her hands in her pockets and made them stop.

The camp had gathered in the open ground between the tents, faces tipped up, and the sight of forty hardened mercenaries staring at the heavens like frightened children would have been funny on any other night. Ondine stood at the center with a lantern held high, as if light could argue with the dark.

"Report," she said when she saw Edmund.

"Wards are down," Edmund said. "I felt them fail. All of them, along the whole border, at once."

Murmurs. Someone laughed—one of the new men, Dree, a wiry slinger with more mouth than sense. "Felt them? Nobody feels the wards. That's the whole point of wards."

"I felt them," Edmund said, and did not explain further, because explaining meant talking about the mark, and talking about the mark meant saying words like *god* in front of forty people who had all grown up on the same comfortable story of a god who took his rest and left the valley alone.

Ondine studied her for a long moment. "Sky?"

"Gone dark. Stars failing, west to east. No cloud cover, no fog, no smoke. It isn't weather." She paused. "Captain, I don't know what it is. That's my full report."

Ondine turned to the company, and Edmund watched her do the thing officers do—build a wall of calm out of whatever materials lay at hand. "High mountain weather phenomenon. Light refraction. We've all heard of stranger things coming off the peaks. The wards have stood since before anyone's grandmother drew breath; they aren't going anywhere. First watch doubles, second watch stands down, and anyone who spreads panic talk does laps until sunrise." A few grim chuckles. The wall held. Walls usually held, right up until they didn't.

But Corvo came and stood beside Edmund at the fire afterward, and said nothing for a long time, and then said, quietly, "You felt them fail."

"Yes."

"And something else."

Edmund fed a stick to the fire. Around them the sky continued its slow murder, star by star, until whole constellations she'd navigated by for a decade were simply gone, and the darkness overhead had a texture to it now, a pressure, like deep water. "There's a story we tell children," she said. "About how Vael went silent."

"There's a story about everything. Doesn't mean—"

"He spoke tonight." She kept her voice low enough that only Corvo could hear it over the fire's crackle. "First time in my life. First time in anyone's lifetime. One word."

Corvo's face had gone carefully blank, which was how he looked when he was most afraid. "What word?"

"*Run.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark beyond the lanterns, the border stones sat in their neat row, sigils dark for the first time in centuries, guarding nothing, promising nothing. Edmund looked at them and felt the shape of her whole life shift under her—the eleven years of watches, the certainty that the valley was safe because it had always been safe, that silence was peace because peace was all anyone remembered. It had all been built on that hum, and the hum was gone, and underneath it she could suddenly hear everything the hum had been covering.

Whatever bargain had bought the valley its quiet, the bill had come due tonight.

"You should sleep," Corvo said at last.

"So should you."

"One of us has to be awake when the sky finishes falling." He settled his crossbow across his knees again, facing the dead wards, and did not look at her. "Go on, Vasquez. Whatever word your god gave you—you don't have to run yet. But start packing."

Edmund walked back toward her tent through a camp of men pretending the night was ordinary, and above her the last stars in the west guttered and went out, one by one, like a door being closed very gently from the other side.
