## 2. What the Valley Owes

The Shrine of Vael smelled like dust and old candle smoke and, faintly, of the tallow the archivist used to keep the leather bindings from cracking. Edmund had always found the smell comforting. Tonight it felt like a lie told by a room.

She had not slept. After her watch ended—after the sky went black and Sergeant Ivo called it "ash weather, happens every decade or so, stop gawping, Vasquez"—she had walked home past her brother's window, seen the candle burning late as he mended a fishing net, and kept walking. To the shrine. Because she had nowhere else to put the word that was still ringing in her skull.

*Run.*

Vael hadn't spoken in three generations. The priests said so every festival, with the particular pride of people describing an heirloom that no longer worked. *Our god is quiet, but he is here.* And then last night, on the border stones, with the wards failing under her hands like wet paper, Edmund had felt something vast turn over in its sleep and say one word into the marrow of her bones.

You didn't run from a word like that. You went looking for what it was running *from*.

"You're not supposed to be down here," said Sefa, without looking up from her desk.

Sefa was ninety if she was a day, small as a folded letter, and had been the shrine's archivist since before Edmund's mother was born. She wore four shawls in summer and six in winter and had once fined a magistrate's wife for returning a hymnal with a wine ring on page forty. Edmund liked her enormously.

"I need the archives," Edmund said. "The deep ones."

"The deep ones are for priests."

"I'm not asking for a blessing. I'm asking for paper."

Sefa finally looked up. Her eyes were milky at the edges but sharp at the center, and they went over Edmund's face the way she'd go over a damaged folio—cataloguing the damage first, deciding whether it could be repaired later.

"Something happened on your watch," she said.

"The stars went out."

"Ash weather."

"Sefa. I've stood that border for eleven years. Ash weather makes the stars look *tired*. Last night they were *gone*. All of them. One by one, like someone pinching out candles." Edmund leaned on the desk. "And Vael spoke to me."

The quill stopped moving. That was how Edmund knew it was serious. Sefa's quill had continued moving through the death of her husband, two floods, and the Great Ferret Incident of '61, which had destroyed an entire shelf of birth records and one archivist's dignity.

"Sit down," Sefa said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway. You'll want to be sitting." She rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and led Edmund past the reading room, past the reliquary where Vael's supposed sandal-strap lay under glass (Edmund had always privately suspected it was a belt), and to a door she had never noticed despite walking this corridor a hundred times. It wasn't hidden. It was just so thoroughly *unremarkable* that the eye slid off it like rain off oilcloth.

"Is that a charm?" Edmund asked.

"That's furniture placement," said Sefa. "The oldest kind. Nobody guards a door nobody sees." She unlocked it with a key from a chain around her neck, and inside, lit a lamp that burned a color Edmund had no name for—a color that made her god-mark itch beneath her sleeve.

The room beyond was long and low and full of shelves, and the shelves were full of ledgers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not hymnals, not histories. Ledgers.

"What is all this?"

"Accounts," said Sefa. "The valley keeps very good accounts. That's rather the whole problem."

* * *

It took Sefa an hour to find the right ledger, mostly because she insisted on explaining things properly, and Edmund loved her for it even while her skin crawled with impatience.

"Every settlement has a founding story," Sefa said, climbing a wheeled ladder with the agility of a woman a third her age. "Ours is: Vael led our ancestors through the pass, set wards against the wild magic outside, and rested beneath the mountain. Peace in exchange for devotion. Lovely story. Very popular at festivals." She pulled a ledger bound in grey leather and blew dust off it in a cloud that made Edmund sneeze. "Also incomplete. Here. Read the flyleaf."

Edmund read. The handwriting was old, formal, cramped:

*Let it be recorded that the silence of Vael was purchased, not decreed. Let it be recorded that the wards were not his gift but his ransom-price. Let it be recorded, so that no generation may claim ignorance, what we agreed to pay.*

"Pay for what?" Edmund asked. Her voice came out flat.

"Turn to the third section. The Tithe Ledger proper."

Edmund turned pages. Census entries. Grain tallies. And then, recurring every forty years like a fever, entries in a different hand—older, stranger, the letters shaped wrong, as though written by someone who had learned script from watching it underwater:

*Year 212. Given: the soul of Maren Ostrow, weaver. Received: forty years' quiet.*

*Year 252. Given: the soul of Dannel Ostrow, her son. Received: forty years' quiet.*

*Year 292. Given:*—

Edmund stopped. Turned back. Read the names again. Mother, then son. Then, forty years later, another name from the same family line. She flipped forward, faster now, her finger running down the column, and the pattern held like a scar holds its shape. Every forty years. One soul. And the family names repeated, generation after generation, because—of course—the court beneath the mountain did not want strangers. It wanted *lines*. It wanted blood it had already tasted.

"Who chose them?" Edmund asked quietly.

"The council. With the priests. There was always a ceremony—what do they tell you? A 'consecration.' The chosen one goes to serve Vael directly. A great honor." Sefa's voice was dry as the dust on the shelves. "My grandmother wove Maren Ostrow's burial shroud, you know. Empty coffin. Very beautiful work, she said. A shame about the weight of it."

Edmund did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Two hundred twelve, plus forty, plus forty, plus forty. The generations stacked up neatly, obediently, like soldiers on a roster. The last entry was dated eighty years ago. Which meant the next entry was due.

Now.

Her finger found the newest page. The ink was fresh—not fresh like yesterday, fresh like *this week*, the black still holding a faint shine. The hand was the strange underwater hand. The entry read:

*Year 372. Given: the soul of Tomas Vasquez, fletcher. Date of conveyance: three days hence. Received: forty years' quiet.*

The room did not spin. Edmund would remember that later, with a strange clinical pride—that the room did not spin, that her knees held, that she did not vomit on a two-hundred-year-old ledger. What happened instead was smaller and worse. Everything got very clear and very quiet, the way the valley got on frost mornings, and she heard herself say, in a voice that sounded borrowed:

"Tomas doesn't fish. He's a fletcher. They got his trade wrong."

"Yes," said Sefa gently. "That's usually the first thing people notice. Isn't it remarkable? The soul of your brother, sold, and what wounds you is the clerical error."

"It's not a clerical error. It means whoever wrote it never met him. It means he's a *line item*." Edmund's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the ledger, as if she could hold the page down, hold the whole arrangement down. "Three days. My birthday is in three days. He made me—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He made me arrows. Every year since I joined the company. He says a mercenary should never have to buy her own."

"And now you know why he never spoke of being chosen."

"He doesn't *know*. Does he? Tell me he doesn't know."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. In the lamplight her face was all soft folds and hard eyes. "The chosen are told seven days before. Told it's an honor. Given a farewell feast they're told is a festival." She tapped the ledger. "Your brother will have been told yesterday or today. And he will smile, because he was raised in a valley that taught him smiling is the price of peace, and he will pack his good coat, and in three days he will walk up to the mountain gate and everyone he loves will wave."

Edmund thought of the candle in her brother's window. The net in his hands—he'd been mending a net, and Tomas hated mending nets, he always made her do it, which meant he'd been keeping his hands busy so his mind wouldn't chew through the walls. He'd known. He'd been sitting there knowing, and he'd let her walk past, because what else did you do? What else had anyone ever done?

"We bought peace," Edmund said. "That's what I inherited. That's what my mother's mother's mother handed down. Not safety. A *debt*. And the interest is paid in people."

"Now you understand the word."

"What word?"

"*Run,*" said Sefa. "You said Vael spoke to you. One word." She closed the ledger with terrible tenderness, the way she handled anything true and breakable. "Child, I have spent sixty years in these archives, and I will tell you what the old marginalia says, the parts the council never read aloud. Vael did not fall silent. Vael was *made* silent. His voice was part of the original payment—the court took it to forge the bargain, and everything since has been signed in his stolen name." She looked up. "If your god managed one word through all that silence, after a hundred and sixty years, I should think it was not advice about weather."

Edmund stood very still. Beneath her sleeve, the god-mark—the faint silver whorl every child of the valley received at their naming, the mark everyone said was decorative, vestigial, a fossil—was burning. Not painfully. Burning the way a banked coal burns when you finally give it air.

Three days. She had three days before her brother walked up the mountain smiling, and a lifetime of peace proved to be a receipt, and the only god she'd ever sworn to whispered to her across a hundred miles of stolen silence.

"Sefa," she said. "I'm going to need the rest of these ledgers. And then I'm going to need something stronger than ledgers."

"Company?" the old archivist asked, already reaching for her sixth shawl.

"Company," said Edmund. "Because I am not letting this valley wave him goodbye."
