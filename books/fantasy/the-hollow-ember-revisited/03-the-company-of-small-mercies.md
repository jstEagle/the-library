## 3. The Company of Small Mercies

The Company of the Small Mercies had a rule about bad news: you shared it over food, because nobody throws a punch at someone holding bread. Edmund had learned that in her first year, when she'd told Dagny her cooking was a war crime and survived only by offering a second loaf.

So she bought bread. Good bread, from Marta's stall, still warm enough to fog the paper. She carried it to the company's usual table at the Salted Boar and set it down in the middle like an offering at a very small, very nervous altar.

Dagny eyed it. "Who died?"

"Nobody yet."

"That's never been the start of good conversation."

Edmund sat. Around the table: Dagny, who could break a door with her shoulder and a heart with a sentence; Rell, who counted everything twice; Osk, who was technically asleep with his eyes open; and Petra, who sharpened knives during conversations she didn't like, which meant she was sharpening one now on principle.

"I went back to the border last night," Edmund said. "After the sky went dark."

"We know," Rell said. "You came back smelling like panic and wet wool."

"The wards are gone. Not damaged. Gone. And I think—" She stopped. Saying it aloud made it real, and real things had consequences. "I think it wasn't weather. I think something took our stars."

Petra's knife paused mid-stroke. That was as close as Petra got to gasping.

"And here's the worse part." Edmund turned the bread plate slowly, like a wheel of fortune offering nothing but crust. "I've been reading. In the shrine archives. There's a ledger. Every forty years, the valley gives up a soul. To whatever's under the mountain. That's the price of the wards. That's what peace costs."

Silence. Then Osk said, without opening his eyes, "Is that why the bread? You thought we'd need fortifying for this?"

"Yes."

"Fair," he said, and took a roll.

"It's Tomas," Edmund said. "His name is in it. Three days from now."

Rell set down his cup with exaggerated care, the way he did when he wanted everyone to know his hands were steady. "Ledgers can be forged."

"This one's written in a hand older than the shrine. It has every tithe going back two hundred years. Names, dates. I checked against the memorial wall. Every forty years, the valley loses someone. A fever. A fall. A disappearance. We buried them or we forgot them, and we called it luck."

Dagny pushed her chair back. The legs shrieked against the floor, and half the tavern looked over. "You're asking us to believe the valley's been selling people. Our people. For generations. To keep magic out."

"To keep *peace* in," Edmund said. "That's how they'd say it."

"Whose peace?" Dagny stood. "My grandmother died in a fever year. Forty-one years ago." Her jaw worked. "You're telling me that wasn't fever."

"I'm telling you the ledger says Tithe, 41st year, and your grandmother's name is on the same line."

Dagny looked at her for a long moment. Then she picked up her coat. "I'm going home. I'd suggest you burn that bread and that book and go to sleep, Edmund, because what you're describing is either madness or the end of everything we have, and I don't want to find out which."

She left. Petra followed, knife sheathed, which somehow felt more final than if she'd drawn it. Rell lingered, counting something—his coins, maybe, or his loyalties—and then said, quietly, "I'm sorry. Truly. But if there's even a chance you're wrong, and you drag the company into it—" He shook his head and didn't finish, which was its own answer.

Osk opened his eyes all the way. He looked at Edmund for a long time, and then at the bread, and then he took one more roll for the road.

"Sorry, kid," he said. "I'm too old to be brave on purpose. It has to sneak up on me."

And then Edmund sat alone at the table with a plate of rolls and the knowledge that she had just spent her family, such as it was, on the truth.

* * *

The magistrate's office smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of the dried flowers he kept in a jar because his late wife had liked them. Magistrate Corvin Hale was seventy, mostly deaf in one ear, and had signed off on Edmund's mercenary permits since she was seventeen. He poured her tea before she said a word, which was the first sign this was not a normal visit.

"You've been in the archives," he said.

It wasn't a question, so she didn't treat it as one. "How long have you known?"

He sat down across from her, and the lamplight caught all the creases in his face at once, so that he seemed suddenly much older than seventy, old as the valley itself. "Known?" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the ledger pages. "Child, my grandfather held this office when the last tithe was paid. I was raised on it the way you're raised on prayers. Quietly, and without explanation."

"Then you knew Tomas's name would come up. You let him walk around. You let *me*—"

"What did you want me to do?" He spread his hands. "Post a notice? 'Attention: the Vasquez boy is due beneath the mountain Tuesday'? Do you know what happens then? Nothing. Nothing changes. The court takes him anyway, because the bargain doesn't care whether we consent, only that we don't interfere. All we'd get is three days of a boy knowing exactly when he dies. Is that mercy? Is that honesty?" His voice cracked on the last word, and he covered it with his teacup.

Edmund leaned forward. "Then help me change it. You have authority. Guards. Records. If the whole valley stood together—"

"The whole valley stood together forty years ago," Hale said, "and eighty years ago, and every time, and every time the choice was the same: one soul, or all of them. Because that's the other thing the ledger makes clear, Edmund, if you read past the names. Break the bargain, and the wards don't just fail. Everything the silence has been holding back comes through at once. Magic isn't weather out there. It's a flood. And we built our houses in the dry years and forgot the river exists." He set down the cup. "I am begging you. As the man who signed your first permit, as an old man who has carried this longer than you've been alive: stop. Grieve your brother. Live. That's what the valley does. That's what it has always done."

"That's what the valley was *made* to do," Edmund said. "There's a difference."

She left the tea untouched. It seemed important, somehow, not to accept anything from him today.

* * *

She found Sefa where she always found Sefa: in the archive's back room, up a ladder, dusted gray to the elbows, arguing with a shelf.

"The catalog says the third census volume is here," Sefa was saying to the shelf, "and yet the shelf disagrees, and frankly the shelf has been unreliable all month."

"Sefa."

Sefa peered down. She was small, sixty-ish, with spectacles mended twice with wire and hair escaping its bun like it was also trying to escape the valley. "Edmund Vasquez. You're the reason the magistrate sent a runner this morning asking who accessed the restricted stacks. I told him I couldn't remember. Which was a lie, and I want you to appreciate that. I haven't lied since 811, and it gave me hives."

"They're going to stop me," Edmund said. "Hale knows everything. Half the company reported me. By tomorrow there'll be guards on my door, if Hale still bothers with doors."

"Mm." Sefa climbed down with the unhurried precision of someone whose knees had filed formal complaints decades ago. "And you came here because...?"

"Because you're the only person I know who's read more than I have. And because when I pulled the Tithe Ledger, someone had already loosened the seal. Before me. Recently. The wax crumbled like it had been handled."

Sefa took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, put them back on. All of it very slow. Buying time, or performing innocence badly.

"It was you," Edmund said.

"I re-cataloged that section eleven years ago," Sefa said. "I read the ledger then. I've read it every year since, on the anniversary, like a vigil. Do you know what I did the first time? I brought it to the magistrate. I was younger than you. I believed institutions were just people agreeing to be decent." She smiled, and it was a bruised kind of smile. "Magistrate Hale thanked me for my discretion and locked the ledger away for another decade. So yes. I loosened the wax. I've been waiting eleven years for someone else to find it, because I apparently lack the courage of my own curiosity."

"You're coming with me," Edmund said. It came out as an order, and she winced. "I mean—I could use help. With the research. The route. Whatever's under the mountain, someone wrote about it once, somewhere, and you're the person who'd know where."

"Absolutely not," Sefa said. "I'm an archivist. My skills are dust, indexing, and mildew. Out there—" she gestured vaguely at the world beyond the walls, beyond the wards, beyond everything— "out there I will be eaten by something magical within a day, and it won't even be a significant magical creature. It'll be some minor enchanted shrubbery. My obituary will be embarrassing."

"Sefa."

"Don't 'Sefa' me. I've spent my whole life among dead people's papers because living ones kept disappointing me. Why would I leave now, at my age, for a suicide errand?"

"Because you've read that ledger every year like a vigil," Edmund said. "Vigils aren't for the dead. They're for the living who couldn't do anything. This time you can."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, the archive clock ticked with the smugness of clocks everywhere.

Sefa exhaled through her nose. Then she walked to her desk, unlocked a drawer Edmund had never seen open, and lifted out a satchel already packed—maps, chalk, a knife sized for a woman who had clearly never intended to use it but had bought one anyway, years ago, just in case.

"How long has that been packed?" Edmund asked.

"Since the night the stars went out," Sefa said, shouldering it. "I finished the lie to the magistrate and thought: well. Either the girl reads the ledger or she doesn't. If she doesn't, I die of old age surrounded by mildew. If she does—" She patted the satchel. "If she does, I refuse to be the reason the rescue party can't read the map."

Edmund laughed—a short, startled sound, the first one in three days—and it hurt her ribs, and she found she didn't mind.

"One condition," Sefa said, holding up a finger. "We stop at my cousin's in Lowmark for proper boots. I am not walking into hell in archival shoes."

Outside, the sun was setting behind the mountain—the mountain, the one with the court beneath it—and for the first time Edmund looked at it not as scenery but as a destination, a door, a throat. Two of them, then. An army of two: a mercenary marked by a silent god and an archivist with packed bags and a knife she'd never use.

It would have to be enough. It was, at least, a beginning.

Behind them, in the magistrate's office, a runner was already saddling a horse.
