## 4. Crossing Where Magic Cannot Follow

Sefa had spent thirty-one years believing the world ended at the wards, and she had expected that belief to die dramatically—thunder, perhaps, or a wall of light. Instead it died in a sheep pasture at dawn, with Edmund Vasquez crouched beside a fence post saying, "Huh."

"Huh," Sefa repeated, without conviction.

The ward-stones were supposed to hum. Every child in the valley learned the sound before they learned their letters: a low, patient thrumming like a hive asleep, audible if you pressed your ear to the stones and believed hard enough. Sefa had pressed her ear to this particular stone every spring of her childhood, on the festival walk her mother insisted upon, and she had always heard something—or told herself she had.

Now there was nothing. The stone was just stone, cool and lichen-spotted, sitting in the grass like any other rock that had never been asked to hold back the world.

"It's not broken," Edmund said. She ran her thumb along the post's carved sigil, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather. "Broken would be a crack. A shatter. This is—" She searched for the word, frowning the way she frowned at bad ledgers, which was to say the way most people frowned at corpses. "Empty. Like someone took the coin out and left the purse."

"Is that better or worse?"

"Worse." Edmund stood, rolling her shoulders the way she did before a fight, though there was nothing here to fight but morning fog. "A broken lock means someone broke it. An empty one means someone *took* what was inside. There's a difference between burglary and theft, Sefa. One's desperate. The other's planned."

Sefa hugged her satchel to her chest. It contained, in descending order of importance: three days of dried mutton, the Tithe Ledger copied out in her own careful hand, a change of stockings, and a book on outer-world botany she had taken from the shrine shelves mostly because it had a beautiful illustration of a plant called a foxglove and she wanted very much to see whether reality matched the engraving. Thirty-one years an archivist, and when the world ended, she had packed for it like a picnic.

"You're stalling," Edmund said, not unkindly.

"I'm *appreciating*," Sefa said. "There's a difference. One's desperate. The other's planned."

Edmund laughed—a short, surprised bark—and Sefa felt absurdly proud of herself, as though she'd coaxed a flower out of season. Then Edmund turned to face the fence line, and the fog beyond it, and everything past the fog, and stopped laughing.

"The moment we cross," Edmund said, "we're outside the valley. Outside Vael's silence. Whatever's out there that can smell magic—" she touched her own forearm, where the mark sat under her sleeve like a coal wrapped in cloth, "—it'll smell me first."

"And you're certain you want to do this anyway."

"No," Edmund said honestly. "But my brother's down there being filed like currency, and I've read enough ledgers to know entries don't correct themselves. So." She stepped over the fence line, one boot, then the other, and stood in the grass on the far side, arms slightly out, like a woman testing ice.

Nothing happened. The fog drifted. A bird somewhere made an ordinary bird noise.

"Well?" Sefa said.

"Well what?"

"Aren't you going to be struck blind or transformed or something? Isn't that what happens to valley-folk who leave? My grandmother said your shadow stays behind and walks around causing trouble."

"My grandmother said the same thing about *her* grandmother, and half those women are buried in the valley churchyard, shadows presumably still attending services." Edmund held out her hand. "Come on, archivist. History's waiting to be misfiled."

Sefa climbed over the fence. Her heart hammered; her knees complained; nothing magical occurred. But the moment both her boots touched foreign grass, she understood, with a lurch, that the world was not smaller than the valley. The valley had been small *inside* the world. The fog ahead went on farther than fog had any right to go, and above it, where the stars should have been, there was only a smooth black absence, like ink spilled across the whole dome of the sky.

She sat down abruptly in the wet grass.

"Sefa?"

"I'm fine," Sefa said, from the ground. "I'm having a thought. It's large. Give me a moment."

Edmund waited, hands on hips, while Sefa stared up at the starless dark and let the size of it settle into her bones. All her life, *up* had meant a ceiling of familiar constellations—the Shepherd, the Loom, the Ember itself, burning red over the mountain. Now up meant nothing. Up meant a question with no answer written anywhere.

"They took the stars too," Sefa said quietly. "The court. That wasn't weather. That was *theft*."

"Yeah." Edmund's voice had gone flat and hard, the mercenary voice. "Add it to the ledger."

* * *

They walked all day through country that should not have existed.

That was the thing no archive had prepared Sefa for—not the danger, not the cold, but the sheer *inventory* of the outer world. The valley had eleven kinds of tree; Sefa knew them all by name and use. By midday she had counted forty kinds she could not name, including one with silver bark that chimed faintly in the wind, and she had to stop twice simply to write descriptions before the words escaped her. Edmund, meanwhile, moved through it all like a woman walking through a house she'd once lived in and tried to forget—checking sightlines, noting game trails, reading the land the way Sefa read marginalia.

"You've been out here before," Sefa said, catching up on a ridge.

"Border work. Raids come from outside sometimes; someone has to meet them." Edmund didn't slow. "Never more than a day's walk. Never past the old road markers. The company has rules."

"And now?"

"Now I'm breaking all of them in order of how much trouble they'll cause."

It was near dusk, camped in the hollow of a deadfall, that things went wrong.

Sefa noticed it first as a wrongness in her teeth—a pressure, like a storm coming, except the sky was clear and black and empty. Edmund noticed it second, and Edmund noticed it *hard*: she went rigid, hand flying to her forearm, and yanked her sleeve back.

The mark was glowing.

Not metaphorically. Not the way pious folk claimed relics glowed, which meant imagination. It was a thin seam of pale gold light tracing the lines of the sigil on her skin, bright enough to cast shadows, and it was getting brighter, pulsing gently, steadily, like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

"Make it stop," Sefa whispered.

"I'm trying. It doesn't take requests." Edmund scrubbed at it with her palm, spat on it, wrapped her sleeve over it—which helped exactly as much as wrapping a lantern in gauze. "This is what I was afraid of. Out past the wards, everything magical can see me. And apparently I'm *loud*."

"How loud?"

The answer came out of the dark behind them: a sound like a bell being unmade. Sefa spun. Three figures stood at the edge of the hollow, and they were wrong in a way her eyes kept sliding off of—too tall, too symmetrical, faces like masks left out in the rain until the expressions wore away. Collectors. She knew it the way you know a word in a language you don't speak: by its shape.

They carried no weapons. They didn't seem to need any. The nearest one raised a hand, and the air around Edmund *bent*, folding toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone, and Edmund gasped and dropped to one knee as though something had hooked her spine.

"*Run*," Edmund gritted.

"I'm not leaving you!"

"That's touching and stupid—*go*—"

But Sefa had never run from anything in her life except a fire in the west stacks, and even then she'd gone back for the index. She grabbed the nearest object—her beautiful botany book, heavy as a brick—and threw it at the collector's head.

Here is what Sefa learned in the next six seconds, and would spend the rest of her life turning over:

The book passed *through* the collector like a stone through smoke. But the collector flinched. Not at the impact—at the *passage*. Its blank face turned, tracking the tumbling book, then tracking Sefa, then tracking Edmund, and its head tilted with something that looked horribly like confusion.

And Edmund, still kneeling, still caught in whatever invisible grip had folded around her, started to laugh.

"It can't find me," she breathed. "Sefa—it's looking *right at me* and it can't *find* me."

"What?"

"The mark—it's not mine. It's *his*. It's a piece of Vael, and Vael is silent, and silence doesn't glow, it *swallows*—" She was scrambling backward now, dragging herself out of the fold in the air, and the collectors' heads were turning in slow, wrong arcs, sweeping the hollow like owls listening for a mouse that had learned to hold its breath. "Everything in me that's *me* is shouting. But the god-part is quiet. Quietest thing in the valley. They're hunting a beacon and all they can hear is a hole."

One collector lunged—not at Edmund, at Sefa, fast as a falling door—and Edmund moved. Later Sefa could not reconstruct the sequence; there was a knife, a shout, Edmund's shoulder hitting the thing's midsection, and the collector came apart like smoke kicked by wind, reassembling ten feet away, furious in some register below fury.

"Up!" Edmund hauled Sefa to her feet. "*Now.*"

They ran. Through the deadfall, up the slope, through brush that clawed at Sefa's satchel and her dignity in equal measure, Edmund's arm blazing gold beneath her torn sleeve like a comet dragged low. Behind them the unmade-bell sound rose again, closer, and then—

Then Edmund skidded to a halt at the lip of a ravine, looked down at the black water, looked at her own glowing arm, and said, "Oh, you have got to be joking."

"What?"

"Water kills the light. River-spirit logic—I don't know, it's *his* mark, ask *him*." She was already jumping.

Sefa jumped too, because the alternative was staying with the collectors, and because thirty-one years of shelving books had taught her that when someone braver than you jumps, you jump, and apologize to your knees later.

The water was so cold it erased thought. It was also, miraculously, shallow—waist-deep, running fast, and the moment it closed over Edmund's arm the gold light guttered and died, swallowed whole. Above them, on the ravine's edge, the collectors' shapes gathered, heads tilting, sweeping, searching a darkness that now contained, as far as anything magical could tell, two absolutely ordinary drowning women.

They stood in the river for what felt like a year, teeth chattering in unison, while the wrongness in the air slowly, slowly drained away like a fever breaking.

"They'll be back," Edmund said finally, through chattering teeth. "They know the light went into the water. They'll watch the banks."

"Then we walk downstream," Sefa said. "In the water. All night."

"All night."

"In freezing water."

"You packed stockings, didn't you?" Edmund said. "Dry ones. For after."

Sefa stared at her. "You are a terrible person and I am following you into a river."

* * *

Dawn found them huddled on a gravel bar two miles downstream, steaming gently by a fire Edmund built with the competence of long habit, wearing every dry garment between them, which meant Sefa wore Edmund's spare coat and Edmund wore Sefa's spare stockings and neither of them mentioned how ridiculous the arrangement was, which was its own kind of friendship.

Sefa's fingers had just stopped shaking enough to write when Edmund spoke.

"It talked to me. In the water."

Sefa looked up. "Vael?"

"One word. Same word as the night the wards fell." Edmund was staring into the fire, and the firelight caught the mark on her arm—dark now, dormant, just a scar-shaped shadow. "Run."

"That's not much of a conversation."

"He's been silent for four hundred years, Sefa. Four hundred years of being used as a signature on other people's bargains. You don't come back from that eloquent." Edmund flexed her hand, watching the mark as though willing it to speak again. "But he said it the night everything broke, and he said it tonight when the collectors came, and both times he was *right*. He's not giving orders. He's giving warnings. There's a difference." She glanced sideways, and for a moment the mercenary mask slipped and Sefa saw underneath it something young and frightened and ferociously hopeful. "One's desperate. The other's planned."

Sefa smiled despite everything—despite the cold in her bones and the collectors surely watching the banks and the starless sky pressing down like a lid. "You've been saving that one."

"Borrowed it. Good structure deserves reuse." Edmund fed a branch to the fire. Then she went still.

"Sefa. Look east."

Sefa looked. Beyond the black hills, far off across the plain, a mountain rose against the starless dark—and around its peak, faint but unmistakable, burned a ring of pale gold light. The same gold as the mark. The same gold, Sefa realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the river, as the ember that had hung over the valley in every painting of the old festivals.

Except the paintings showed it *red*. Warm. Alive.

This light was cold, and steady, and it pulsed like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

"That's where they keep him," Edmund said quietly. "That's where they keep everything they've taken." She stood, pulling the coat tight, and Sefa saw that her hands had stopped shaking entirely. "Two hundred miles. We should get moving."

"Edmund," Sefa said. "The light. It's the same color as your mark. If the collectors hunt that color—"

"Then we'd best learn to walk quietly," Edmund said, and started down the gravel bar toward the distant, patient gold, "carrying the loudest thing in the world."
