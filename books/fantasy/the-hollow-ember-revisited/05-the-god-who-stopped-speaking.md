## 5. The God Who Stopped Speaking

The drowned temple announced itself the way old grief does—quietly, and then all at once.

They had walked three days through country Edmund had no map for, following Sefa's notes and a rumor bought from a one-eyed toll-keeper: *a woman who hears what gods can't say, down where the river swallowed the church.* The river in question turned out to be a wide, slow thing the color of pewter, and the temple stood hip-deep in it, its bell tower leaning like a drunk uncle at a wedding, its doors long since rotted to lace. Water lapped at steps carved with words worn smooth by a century of patient drowning.

"Charming," Sefa said, wringing out her sleeve for the fourth time that morning. "I've seen nicer ruins. I've also seen ones that didn't have eels."

"There are eels?"

"I'm choosing not to find out. You go first."

Edmund went first. It was, she was learning, simply what she did now—the valley's habits dying hard even as everything else she'd believed died around them. She waded up the flooded nave, boots finding the submerged floor by feel, past pillars furred green as old bronze. Light came down through a collapsed section of roof in pale columns, and fish scattered from her shins like startled thoughts.

At the far end, where an altar should have been, sat a woman on a half-sunken bench, feet bare in the water, hands folded in her lap. Her eyes were closed. Her eyes, Edmund realized as they drew closer, had been closed for a very long time—the lids sunken, the skin beneath them smooth as river stone. Blind, and not recently.

"You're loud," the woman said. "Both of you. The water carries voices beautifully. Come sit. The dry spots are limited but I've saved you the best one."

Her voice was warm and unhurried, the voice of someone who had made peace with every silence available to her.

"We're looking for an oracle," Edmund said carefully.

"You found a retired one. But oracles are like soldiers—we never really stop." The woman patted the bench beside her. "I'm Maren. And you're the one who's been burning across three counties like a signal fire. Do you know what you look like, from the outside? Like a candle in a cellar full of moths. Sit down before something with wings finds you."

Edmund sat. The stone was cold through her trousers. Up close, Maren smelled of river weed and lamp oil, and there was a small brass brazier beside her with a stub of incense burned down to nothing.

"You can see me," Edmund said. "Without eyes."

"I see heat. I see the shape of souls. Yours is—" Maren paused, and her head tilted, listening to something. "Yours is strange. There's a hole in you where a god used to be, and something of the god still lodged in the hole, like a splinter. Or a seed. Which one depends entirely on what you do next."

Sefa settled onto the bench on Maren's other side, notebook already out despite the damp. "The mark," she said. "On Edmund's skin. We thought it was Vael's brand—a claim. A leash."

"Mm. And what did your archives say about Vael?"

"That he went silent," Edmund said. "Generations ago. That he abandoned us. That the wards went up because—" She stopped. Because she'd never actually asked why the wards went up. Nobody had. The wards were weather. The wards were the sky. You did not interrogate the sky.

Maren smiled, and it was a kind smile, which somehow made it worse.

"Abandoned you," she repeated. "That's a lovely story. Very tidy. The kind of story people tell so they don't have to be angry on a dead god's behalf." She turned her face toward Edmund, and though there were no eyes to fix her with, Edmund felt seen more thoroughly than she ever had in her life. "Let me tell you what actually happened, soldier. And then you can decide whether to weep or burn. In my experience it's usually both."

* * *

"The court beneath the mountain is old," Maren said. "Older than your valley. Older than most of the names we give things. They deal in souls the way merchants deal in grain—by weight, by ledger, by contract. And a soul freely given is worth ten times a soul taken. So they don't take. They bargain. They always bargain."

"The tithe," Edmund said. Her voice came out flat. "One soul every forty years."

"One soul every forty years, yes. But a tithe is rent. Rent keeps the landlord away. Your valley wanted more than that—they wanted magic itself kept out, forever, warded borders, a world with the dangerous parts amputated. And that kind of bargain needs collateral of a different order." Maren lifted one hand from the water and let it fall back with a small splash. "It needs a god's voice."

Edmund's breath caught. "Vael."

"Vael. Your sworn god, patron of the Ashen Valley, keeper of its hearths and its oaths. When the founders came to him begging for protection, he refused them—gently, I'm told; he was always gentle—but he refused. Magic cannot be locked out of the world, he said, only out of hearts, and hearts must choose. So the court went to work on the choice instead." Maren's voice dropped, and the water seemed to hush to hear it. "They took his voice, Edmund. Not killed him—a dead god is free, and martyrs make terrible collateral. They captured him and cut the speaking out of him, and they used that stolen voice to forge the original bargain. Every ward along your border is built from a sentence he never got to finish. Every forty years, when the tithe comes due, it's his stolen voice that speaks the terms. Your valley has been living inside your god's mouth this whole time, and calling the silence abandonment."

The nave was very quiet. Somewhere above, water dripped from the broken tower with the patience of a thing counting centuries.

Edmund found she could not move. She had spent her whole life with a specific grief shaped like a specific absence: *our god left us.* Mercenaries swore by Vael anyway, the way you keep lighting lamps in a house whose owner moved out—it was habit, heritage, a small stubborn warmth. She had prayed to him on watch the night the stars went out, and he had answered with one word, *run*, and she had thought: *so now he remembers us, when it's too late.*

He hadn't remembered. He had never forgotten. He had been screaming the whole time, and the wards had eaten the sound.

"He said *run*," she managed. "Three nights ago. First word I ever heard from him. I thought—I don't know what I thought. That it was pity."

"It was a man hammering on the inside of a door," Maren said. "You happened to be standing near the hinge." She reached out and found Edmund's wrist with surprising accuracy, her fingers cool and sure. "Now. Your arm. Show me."

Edmund pushed back her sleeve. The mark sprawled from wrist to elbow—a branching silver-white scrawl like frost or lightning frozen mid-fall, which had appeared the night the wards fell and had burned like a beacon ever since. Every magical thing in the outer world had felt it. Collectors had hunted them by it. She had come to think of it as the wound that would get her killed.

Maren's fingers hovered over it without touching. "Ah," she breathed. "Oh, you poor magnificent fool. This isn't a brand. This isn't a claim." She looked up—or turned her face up—with something close to wonder. "When the wards fell, the bargain cracked, and a piece of the stolen voice escaped. It went looking for a vessel. It went looking for *you*. This is Vael's own self, Edmund. A fragment of your god, lodged in your arm like a coal carried from a house fire. He didn't mark you as prey. He gave you the only thing he had left to give."

"The collectors," Edmund whispered. "Everything that hunts us—"

"Hunts the ember. Yes. The court wants its property back. If they reclaim the fragment, they can reforge the wards, seal the bargain, and your brother's soul stays filed in their cathedral forever, and the valley goes back to sleep for another forty years." Maren released her wrist. "If you keep it—if you learn to carry it instead of merely being carried by it—then you are holding the one weapon in the world that can unmake their ledgers. Their whole architecture is built from his voice. His voice can refuse to hold it up."

Sefa had stopped writing. "She's not a vessel," Sefa said slowly. "She's an heir."

"She's an inheritance," Maren agreed, "that everyone told her was a curse. Which, if you'll forgive an old woman's editorializing, is rather the family tradition, isn't it? Safety purchased with lives. Debt dressed as blessing. Your valley has been handing that down for generations, Edmund. The question is whether you hand it down too—or whether you spend it."

* * *

They stayed the night in the temple, on the dry stretch of bench and the one intact alcove above the waterline, because Maren insisted the eels were worse after dark and refused to elaborate.

Edmund couldn't sleep. She sat with her feet in the shallows, sleeve rolled up, watching the mark. In the dark it gave off the faintest light, like a banked fire, and for the first time she didn't look at it as a target painted on her skin. She looked at it as what it was: a piece of home that had finally, after all these generations, come home to someone who'd carry it.

"I used to talk to him," she said quietly, when Maren drifted over. "Every year, at the shrine. Told him about contracts, about Tomas. Never an answer. I thought I was talking into a well."

"You were talking to a prisoner," Maren said. "Through the wall of his cell. I promise you he heard every word. Prisoners memorize their visitors' voices. It's how they stay sane." She lowered herself onto the wet stone with a grunt, entirely unbothered by the soaking. "May I tell you something else? Free of charge, since you look like a woman recalculating her entire life."

"Please."

"When the fragment chose you, it wasn't random. The stolen voice has been straining toward the valley for two hundred years, looking for a crack, a door, a person. It chose a mercenary who swears by the god everyone thinks abandoned her. Someone loyal enough to walk out of the only world she knows, and stubborn enough to survive it." Maren's ruined eyes crinkled. "Gods don't pick vessels, child. They pick heirs. There's a difference. Vessels get poured full and emptied out. Heirs inherit—and decide what to do with the estate."

Edmund laughed, and it came out rougher than she intended, halfway to something else. "The estate is a debt. One soul per generation. My brother is the current payment."

"Then you know what an heir does with a bad will." Maren patted her knee, found it, patted it again with satisfaction. "She contests it."

They sat together in the dripping dark, the blind oracle and the marked soldier, while fish nudged curiously at Edmund's ankles and the temple held its breath around them like an old friend keeping a secret.

"Tomorrow," Edmund said at last, "we go to the mountain. And I need to get inside the court. Past its collectors, past its gates, into whatever cathedral they keep their ledgers in. Can you—"

"I can tell you how brokers dress," Maren said. "And how they speak. And what the Queen-Below values, which is paperwork above all things—she is a creature of contract, and contracts have manners, and manners have gaps." She smiled. "But more usefully, child, I can tell you this: walk into that place knowing what you carry. Not a curse. Not a beacon. An ember from a house that was never allowed to burn out. They will look at your arm and see stolen property." Her grip tightened on Edmund's knee. "Make them see a torch instead."

Near dawn, half-dreaming against Sefa's shoulder, Edmund heard it—not in her ears, but along her arm, in the silver branches of the mark, faint as a voice through stone:

*Not run,* the ember seemed to say, warming against her skin like a hand closing around hers. *This time, forward.*

She woke Sefa before the light did.

"Get up," Edmund said, rolling down her sleeve over the small bright fire of her inheritance. "We're going to buy a soul-broker's coat. And then we're going to rob a god-thief."
