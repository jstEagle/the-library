## 6. Road of Borrowed Names

Sefa had spent thirty years among paper, and she had believed—right up until the night the wards fell—that paper was the safest thing in the world. Ink did not ambush you. Margins did not lie in wait. A well-ordered archive was a promise that the universe, whatever else it got up to, could at least be filed.

Three weeks on the road outside the valley had cured her of this.

"Stop scratching," Edmund said.

"I'm not scratching. I'm adjusting." Sefa tugged at the collar of her borrowed coat, which was dyed a green so aggressive it seemed to be shouting. "This coat is lying about me. It says I am someone who *chose* this color."

"You are a spice merchant's widow from Lowmarsh with poor eyesight and a limp."

"My eyesight is excellent and my limp is an invention."

"Then you're a widow with a flair for drama. Keep up." Edmund walked ahead with her hood down and her hands loose at her sides, the picture of a woman with nowhere to be and nothing to hide—which Sefa had learned was precisely how Edmund looked when she expected to be murdered by nightfall. The mark of Vael sat under her sleeve like a coal banked in ash. Out here, past the wards, it burned whenever anything magical came near, which out here meant *often*, which meant Edmund had taken to wearing it like a secret she carried in her skin.

They were looking for the caravan. That was what the drowned temple's oracle had told them to do—*follow the smell of other people's lives*—and for two days Sefa had privately despaired, because everything smelled like horse. But on the third morning they crested a ridge above a dry riverbed and there it was: a dozen wagons painted every color of disrepute, hung with lanterns even though the sun was up, and around them perhaps forty people who moved with the particular looseness of those who owned nothing worth weighing.

A woman met them at the edge of camp. She was small, silver-haired, and wore a vest covered in keys, most of which clearly opened nothing, because Sefa counted three that were made of wood.

"Travelers," the woman said, in the tone one uses to identify a species of bird.

"Pilgrims," Edmund said smoothly. "Bound for the coast shrines."

"Mm." The woman's eyes went to Edmund's sleeve, then away, deliberately. "We're the Vesper Company. We deal in names. If you've brought your own, keep it. If you need one, we'll find you something secondhand but honest." She paused. "Secondhand but honest is our whole creed. I'm Mother Quill. Yes, I chose it myself. No, I won't apologize."

Sefa decided immediately that she would have followed this woman into a fire.

* * *

The Vesper Company, it emerged over supper—a stew that tasted mostly of pepper and optimism—traded in identities the court beneath the mountain had discarded. When the court took a soul, Sefa learned, it took the whole person: name, history, debts, loves, the way they laughed. What remained was filed, catalogued, and eventually, when the ledger pages filled or the entries grew too faded to be useful, *discarded*. Burned, most places. The Vespers had made a business of fishing the pages out of the fire.

"We don't sell souls," Mother Quill said, catching the look on Sefa's face. "Souls are gone where they're gone; we can't touch that. We sell the *shells*. A name-entry is a shape. Put yourself in the shape, walk through any checkpoint in the world." She ladled more stew into Sefa's bowl without asking. "You two want shapes. Don't argue, dear, your friend has been walking like a woman carrying a lit lamp in a dry field since you arrived. Whatever's under that sleeve, the road will notice it before we do."

Edmund's jaw did the thing it did when she was swallowing an argument. Sefa had begun keeping a mental catalogue of Edmund's jaws. It was a surprisingly rich text.

They bought their names that evening. Sefa became "Maren Ostler, bookbinder," which was so close to the truth it felt like cheating, and Edmund became "Wren, no family name, courier," which suited her so perfectly that Sefa suspected the ledger page had been waiting for her specifically.

It was while browsing the discarded entries—Mother Quill kept them in a wagon lined with cedar, because "names mold, dear"—that Sefa saw Edmund go still.

Not the stillness of danger. Sefa knew that one now; it was all edges. This was different. This was the stillness of a woman reading something written in a hand she recognized.

"Sefa." Edmund's voice came out strange. Flat, and underneath the flatness, cracked. "Come look at this."

The entry was old, its ink browned, its corners soft with handling. A child's name-entry, sold cheap, the sort of shell nobody wanted because children's names fit almost no adult. The name written across the top was not Tomas Vasquez.

It was **Tam**.

Tam. Short for Tammas, because six-year-old Tomas had refused to answer to anything else for one entire summer, and their mother had finally surrendered and let him be Tam until school started and shame reclaimed him. Tam who had declared he'd fight the river if the river touched his boat again. Tam who used to sleep with a wooden sword under his pillow and once, memorably, tried to knight the family goat.

"Tomas never left the valley," Sefa said carefully. "He told you—"

"He told me he'd never seen the stars beyond the wards. He told me he was afraid of the outer dark. He—" Edmund stopped. Her thumb moved over the entry, over the little stamped seal of the court, over the date. Sixteen years old. Sixteen years ago. "There's a travel record attached. Surface visit. Three days. Three days, Sefa. He stood under the real sky and came home and never said a word."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, a Vesper was tuning a fiddle badly, which was to say, at all.

"He was protecting you," Sefa offered, because it was true, and because it was also not enough, and she watched Edmund hear both halves of it.

"From what? Knowing him?" Edmund laughed, and it was the worst sound Sefa had heard from her yet, worse than the screaming in the ambush, worse than the ward-night. "I spent my whole life thinking I was the strong one. Watching out for him. And he walks out here alone at sixteen, deals with *these people*, stands in front of the court's brokers, and comes home and lets me keep believing he needed me." She set the entry down as though it might bite. "What else didn't I know?"

Sefa had spent thirty years among archives, and she knew the answer to that question, and it was *everything, always, about everyone*. Every life was a book with half its pages glued together. She had read ten thousand files and never once known a single soul entire. But she understood that saying so now would be like offering a man a map while he was drowning.

So instead she said: "He named himself Tam again. Look—the entry's signed in his own hand. Not Tammas. Tam." She tapped the bottom of the page. "Sixteen years old, alone, dealing with smugglers, and he chose the name his mother called him when he was brave enough to fight rivers."

Edmund stared at the signature for a long time.

Then she sat down on the cedar chest, quite suddenly, and put her face in her hands, and cried—not loudly, not dramatically, just the quiet relentless crying of someone who has been holding a door shut for weeks and has finally let it swing. Sefa sat beside her and did not say anything at all, because some pages you simply hold open and wait.

After a while Edmund wiped her face with her sleeve and said, thickly, "If he went to the surface at sixteen, he knew how the tithe worked before I did. He knew what was coming for him. He had years to run."

"And he stayed."

"And he *stayed*." Edmund looked at the lantern flame. "Why does that make it worse?"

"Because love usually does," said Mother Quill from the wagon door, having apparently materialized with the silent competence of a woman who had eavesdropped professionally for decades. She held out two cups of tea. "Drink. Grief dries the throat." She waited until they'd each taken a sip, then added, briskly, "Now. You're not here for shells, are you. Wren. Maren. You're here because you're going below, and you wanted to see how names work before you try to wear one into the Queen-Below's own hall."

Edmund didn't bother denying it. Sefa admired that about her, in the way one admires a cliff.

"Then hear an old smuggler's counsel," Mother Quill said. "The court doesn't check names. It checks *weight*. A false name carries light unless you feed it. Feed it grief, feed it want, feed it a brother's childhood nickname folded in your pocket—and it'll bear you anywhere." She looked at Edmund steadily. "But mind: the court reads what you carry. Go in carrying Tam, and the Queen-Below will know exactly what you love, and exactly how much."

* * *

They left the Vesper camp two mornings later, at grey dawn, with fiddle music chasing them up the ridge and Mother Quill pressing a bundle of spare entries into Sefa's hands—"for barter, dear, everyone below respects paperwork"—and a wooden key into Edmund's.

"It doesn't open anything," Mother Quill said. "That's rather the point. Carry a key that opens nothing, and the court will spend forever wondering what you know that it doesn't."

Sefa liked her enormously.

They walked east, toward the mountain that ate souls, and the sky did its strange starless thing overhead, and Edmund was quiet in the way of someone reorganizing everything she believed. Near midday she spoke without turning around.

"When we get him back," she said, "the first thing I'm going to do is yell at him. For Tam. For all of it."

"Naturally," Sefa said.

"And then I'm going to ask him why."

"Yes."

"And Sefa—" Edmund's voice dropped, and she still didn't turn around, and the mark on her arm had begun, very faintly, to glow through her sleeve like a lamp behind cloth. "What do we do if the answer is that he *wanted* to stay?"

Ahead of them, the mountain rose against the blind sky, and along its lower slopes, where no road should have been, torches were lighting one by one—descending, in a neat and welcoming line, toward the place where the ground opened like a ledger waiting to be signed.
