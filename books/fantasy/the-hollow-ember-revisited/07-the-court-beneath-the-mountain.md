## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The road down was not a road. That was the first thing Edmund had to accept about the court beneath the mountain: it did not admit of roads, or stairs, or any honest means of descent that a body could trust. Instead there was a door in the cliff face, older than the wards, older than the valley's founding, and it opened for Sefa's forged papers with a sigh like a man settling into a chair he'd been waiting on all day.

"Cheerful place," Sefa murmured as the dark swallowed them.

"You have a strange relationship with cheerful."

"I find it helps to name things wrongly at first. It keeps the fear off balance." She patted the satchel of broker's seals against her hip. "Besides, you look very convincing as a monster."

Edmund touched the collar at her throat—the soul-broker's badge, a sliver of black iron stamped with a sigil she'd spent two days learning to draw without her hand shaking. Brokers were the court's middlemen, the ones who carried contracts up into the world and brought souls back down. They were tolerated, feared, and above all *ignored*, which was exactly what Edmund needed to be.

The tunnel went down in long spirals, and the air changed by degrees. First it lost its smell. Then it lost its temperature—neither warm nor cold, just *present*, like water too still to be felt. By the time light appeared ahead of them, Edmund had stopped being able to hear her own footsteps, and Sefa had stopped humming, which was how Edmund knew her friend was frightened. Sefa hummed when she was happy, when she was nervous, and once, memorably, when she'd been concussed. Silence from Sefa meant something had gone past nervous entirely.

The tunnel opened into the cathedral.

Edmund had grown up in a valley that worshipped a silent god, so she knew what empty sanctuaries looked like. This was not that. This was a sanctuary so full it had burst its seams and kept going—a vaulted cavern vast enough to hold weather, ribbed with pillars of pale stone, and every surface covered in ledgers.

Not shelves of ledgers. Not cabinets. The ledgers grew out of the stone itself, spines rising like coral, pages fanning open in slow, breathing motions. Some hung from the ceiling on chains, turning gently in a wind Edmund couldn't feel. Others lay stacked in drifts along the floor, deep as snowfall, and between the drifts moved figures—clerks, she supposed, though they were grey and slightly translucent at the edges, like people remembered rather than people seen. They walked the aisles and turned pages and wrote with pens that scratched without sound.

"There," Sefa breathed. "That's the exchange floor. And—" she squinted at a plaque of green flame beside the entrance, "—visiting brokers register at the Receiving Desk. Try to look bored. Boredom is camouflage down here."

Edmund summoned every hour she'd ever spent standing watch in the rain, every shift where the most exciting event was a goat crossing the border post, and put it on her face like armor. At the desk, a clerk with spectacles and no eyes behind them examined her seal, made an entry in a ledger that licked its own page when he finished, and waved her through with the weary courtesy of a customs officer who has seen ten thousand smugglers and cares about none of them.

*You belong here,* Edmund told herself. *You are the most tedious person in this entire mountain.*

It almost worked.

* * *

Finding Tomas's entry took four hours, and it was the strangest mercy of the day that the search itself was simple. The ledgers were organized by valley, then by generation, then by name—and the Ashen Valley's shelf was small, because the Ashen Valley paid promptly and asked no questions. Forty years, one entry. Forty years, one entry. Edmund walked down the row of her homeland's history and read the names of the dead who weren't dead, the neighbors and great-aunts and one child whose entry was annotated, in neat clerical script, *paid under protest; mother required escort from the premises.*

Her hands were steady until she found the current volume.

*TOMAS VASQUEZ.* Her brother's name, written in ink that hadn't dried in three weeks and never would. Below it, the terms of his sale, the signatures of the magistrate and three elders she'd drunk with, dined with, bled beside. Below that, a line that stopped her breath:

*Status: In service. Position: Seneschal to Her Majesty Below. Disposition: Content.*

"Content," Edmund whispered.

She'd imagined cells. Chains, maybe, or some gray half-existence, a brother wasting in the dark waiting for her. She had prepared herself for rescue, for carrying him out over her shoulder if she had to, for the long work of teaching him to be alive again.

She had not prepared for him to be *working.*

"Excuse me." A grey clerk had drifted up beside her, pen hovering. "Are you auditing that entry?"

Edmund's mind, mercenary-trained, produced a lie before her heart could object. "Verifying disposition. The valley requests confirmation of... satisfaction."

"Satisfaction is noted quarterly." The clerk peered at the page. "He's one of our better placements, actually. Her Majesty favors him. You may observe, if you observe quietly. Seneschals keep afternoon hours in the Counting Hall."

"Observe," Edmund repeated.

"That is what brokers do." The clerk said it without malice, and somehow that was worse than malice—that the watching of stolen souls was simply a service provided, like checking on livestock. He drifted away, already turning pages elsewhere.

Sefa found her a few minutes later, materializing out of the stacks with the expression of someone who had seen too much and catalogued all of it. "I found the identity wing. Edmund, they *file people*. There's a drawer of names that were worn out from use. There's a whole section for souls that were traded twice." She caught sight of Edmund's face. "What happened?"

"He's not imprisoned. He's employed."

* * *

The Counting Hall was smaller than the cathedral but grander, floored in stone polished to mirror-brightness, lit by chandeliers of green flame that burned without heat. And at the far end, at a desk of black wood, sat a figure bent over documents with a quill moving in quick, competent strokes.

Tomas.

He was thinner. That was the first wrongness, though it took her a moment to place it, because everything else about him was *right*—the set of his shoulders, the way he tucked the quill behind his ear when he reached for a fresh page, the little frown of concentration he'd worn since childhood whenever sums were involved. He looked like Tomas. He looked more like Tomas than Tomas had in years, unlined, unweathered, preserved like a flower pressed in a book.

And his eyes, when he glanced up to receive a document from a passing clerk, were the color of a window with nothing behind it.

Edmund stood in the shadow of a pillar and watched her brother sign away an hour, a day, a decade, with a small contented smile, and understood for the first time why the valley's bargain had held for so many generations. It wasn't only fear. It was this. It was the fact that the stolen didn't struggle, because they were given something in exchange—purpose, order, a desk and a title and the soft anesthesia of usefulness. The Queen-Below didn't keep souls in cages. She gave them jobs.

A clerk approached the desk. "Seneschal, the western accounts require—"

"After the recess," Tomas said. His voice was his voice. That was the cruelest part. "Her Majesty prefers the recess observed properly."

"Of course. Forgive me."

He rose, stretched, and crossed the hall toward a side alcove—and toward Edmund, though not toward her, never toward her, passing within six feet of the pillar where she stood pressed flat. Up close she could see the details the distance had hidden: no stubble, though he'd needed a shave daily since he was sixteen. No calluses, though he'd swung a scythe every autumn of his life. His skin was smooth as paper, and somewhere beneath it, she would have bet her sword arm, there wasn't a single scar left. The court had filed those away too.

Then he paused.

Just for a moment. Just a hitch in his stride, a fractional turn of his head, the way a man pauses when a word from a dream follows him into waking. His brow creased—that same childhood frown—and he looked directly at the pillar, at the space beside her head, and something moved far back in those hollow eyes. Something small and drowning.

"Tomas," Edmund said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out as a thread of sound, less than a whisper.

His frown deepened. "Do I..." He blinked, and the flicker guttered, and the smooth contentment flowed back over his face like water closing over a dropped stone. "Forgive me. I thought—I've forgotten my errand." He smiled at the air near her shoulder, polite and vacant. "If you'll excuse me, madam broker. Her Majesty dislikes waiting."

He walked on. Edmund stayed against the pillar long after he'd gone, her nails biting her palms, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

Because here was the horror she'd carried down the mountain without naming, and now it had a name: if she dragged him out of here—if she broke his contract and hauled him blinking into the daylight—he might fight her. Not because he loved this place, but because he didn't know anything else anymore. The court had taken his scars, his aging, his restlessness, his grief for their parents, and what remained might thank her captor for the rescue. She could steal him back from a life he'd been hollowed into accepting, and from the inside, from his side of it, theft was exactly what it would look like.

Rescue and robbery wore the same gloves down here.

"Edmund." Sefa's hand found her elbow. "Your face is doing something alarming. We should not be in this hall."

"He said 'madam broker.'" Edmund's voice came out scraped. "He looked right through me, Sefa. But for a second—for one second, when I said his name—"

"Then we get him out and we give him time to remember what he was."

"And if there's nothing left to remember?" She heard the bitterness in her own voice and hated it. "What if we pull him out of here and all we've done is take away the only self he has left? What if saving him feels to him exactly like what they did?"

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then, gently: "Then we teach him a new self. That's what families are for. It's slow work. I'm told it involves a lot of meals."

Despite everything, something in Edmund's chest loosened a fraction. "You're impossible."

"I'm an archivist. We're trained to believe everything can be recovered given enough patience and proper storage conditions." She tugged Edmund's sleeve. "Come. We've observed enough. We should—"

The green flames in the chandeliers dimmed, all at once, the way candles bow before a draft.

Across the mirrored floor, the clerks stopped mid-stride and turned—not toward Edmund, but toward the far doors, and bowed, in perfect unison, like wheat before wind.

The doors opened.

And the cold that came through them was not the court's still, neutral chill. It was old, and attentive, and it knew, somehow, precisely where Edmund Vasquez was standing.

"Ah," said a voice like silk drawn slowly over a knife. "The valley sends a visitor. How *novel.*"

Sefa's grip tightened on her arm. "Edmund," she whispered. "That's her. That's the Queen-Below."

"And she's looking at us."

The Queen-Below smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Looking? Child. I've been reading your file since you crossed my threshold. Do come in. We have so much to discuss—your brother, your god, and the little matter of what you intend to burn."
