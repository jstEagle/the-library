## 8. A Bargain Refused

The Queen-Below received her in a chamber that had no right to exist beneath a mountain.

Edmund had expected stone. She got a garden instead—a vast, starless conservatory where pale flowers bloomed without sun and the air tasted of cold honey. The Queen sat at the center of it on a throne grown, not built: white roots braided upward into something like a seat, something like a cage. She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say she made Edmund's teeth ache.

"You are the mercenary," the Queen said. Her voice was soft and carried anyway, the way a whisper carries across frozen water. "The one who walks with a god's splinter under her skin. Do you know how loud you are, Edmund Vasquez? You come into my court ringing like a struck bell."

"I've been told I have a memorable presence."

Something moved at the corner of the Queen's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been hunger wearing amusement's coat.

"You want your brother."

"I want what was stolen from my valley. He happens to be part of it."

"Semantics." The Queen rose, and the garden leaned toward her, every pale flower turning like faces. "Let us not waste the night fencing. I know why you came. I let you come. Do you understand that? Your little disguise, your forged broker's seal—charming. But nothing enters this court that I do not permit, and nothing leaves it either." She drifted closer, roots creaking beneath her. "And yet. I find myself inclined to offer you a bargain."

Edmund kept her face still. Twenty years of mercenary work taught her that the moment before an offer is when you learn the most about the offerer. The Queen's eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone, and they were fixed on Edmund's chest—on the place where Vael's fragment slept under her skin like a coal banked for morning.

"Name it," Edmund said.

"Your brother's freedom. His entry torn from my ledger, his soul returned whole, his passage home guaranteed past every ward and watcher I command." The Queen paused, savoring it. "In exchange: yours. And the voice you carry."

The words dropped into the silence like stones into a well. Edmund heard them all the way down.

"The voice," she said carefully, "isn't mine to give."

"It is Vael's, or the piece of him he hid inside a mortal child rather than let me take it whole. Sentimental of him. Foolish, but sentimental things usually are." The Queen circled her now, slow as a season changing. "He bargained with me once, your god. Did you know that? Before your valley's wards went up, before your grandmothers' grandmothers were born. He came down here proud as sunrise and tried to buy his people's safety with himself. I took his voice for the forging of the pact—the tithe needed a divine signature—and he hid what remained of himself in the only vessel I couldn't claim." Her gaze flicked to Edmund again. "Until now."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Tomas stays. Content. Unaging. Serving me as faithfully as he has served for eleven years." The Queen smiled, and it was the worst thing Edmund had seen since the stars went out. "You have seen him. You know how little he misses the sky."

* * *

They gave her a night to decide, which was either mercy or theater, and Edmund suspected the Queen did not distinguish between the two.

A servant made of folded shadow led her through corridors of ledgered stone to a small chamber overlooking the court's great hall—the cathedral of souls, where ten thousand entries glowed on shelves like embers in ash. And there, at a high desk beneath the shelves, sat Tomas.

He was checking figures. That was the detail that broke something in her—not chains, not weeping, but a man with neat handwriting reconciling accounts, a candle stub burning beside him, his sleeve worn smooth at the elbow from years of the same motion. He looked up when the door opened, and his eyes were the eyes she'd seen in the hall: warm brown, familiar, and behind them nothing, a room swept clean and the furniture sold.

"You're the new broker," he said politely. "The Queen said you'd be staying the night. There's tea, if you want it. It's terrible. Everything here is terrible in a very consistent way."

Despite everything—despite the ledger and the hollow and the eleven stolen years—Edmund laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out. "That sounds like my brother."

"I don't have a sister." He said it without malice, the way you'd correct someone about the weather. "I don't think I ever did. There's a space where something should be, when I try to remember home. Like a tooth gone so long your tongue keeps checking."

The tongue keeps checking. Edmund sat down across the desk from him and wrapped her hands around the terrible tea because her hands needed occupation or they would do something foolish, like reach for him.

"What's your name?" she asked. "Not your function. Your name."

"Tomas." A pause. Something crossed his face like a fish passing under ice. "Though there was another one. I can't find it. I've been looking for eleven years and I can't find it, and the Queen says it doesn't matter, but—" He stopped, frowning at his own hands. "But I keep looking. Isn't that strange? Why would I keep looking?"

Because some things don't file away, Edmund thought. Because a soul isn't an entry in a ledger, no matter how fine the penmanship.

"Maybe it matters more than she thinks," she said.

"That's treasonous talk." He smiled as he said it, a small crooked smile she knew from a hundred childhood scrapes, from the time they'd stolen honey cakes from the shrine offering and blamed a raccoon. The smile was still in there. Whatever the Queen had taken, she hadn't found everything. Nobody ever finds everything.

They talked until the candle burned low. Or rather, he talked—about the orderliness of the court, about how the numbers balanced, about a dream he sometimes had of a valley between two ridgelines where the light came down gold through smoke, and how he always woke from it feeling like he'd lost something he couldn't name. Edmund listened and fed the fire of herself down to coals, hiding the bell-sound of Vael's fragment, hiding the name she wanted to say so badly her ribs ached around it.

Because she knew what the name would do. She could feel it—the fragment stirring under her skin whenever he spoke of the dream, reaching toward him like a hand toward a window. One word, their word, the ridiculous secret name two children had invented for each other over a raccoon-shaped shadow and a plate of stolen cakes, and something might wake in him. Or something might break. She didn't know which. That was the trap of it. Love, at this distance, was indistinguishable from a blade.

Near the end, he paused over his ledger and said, quietly, "You look at me like you're memorizing me. People don't do that unless they're saying goodbye."

"I'm deciding something," Edmund said.

"Decisions are the Queen's business. She makes them for everyone." He turned back to his figures, then added, almost too soft to hear: "I hope you decide well. Whoever you are."

* * *

At dawn—which arrived in the court as a dimming of the pale flowers, the closest thing to morning this place permitted—Edmund stood before the Queen's root-throne and gave her answer.

"Yes," she said. "I accept."

The Queen studied her for a long moment, and Edmund let her look, let her see exactly what a tired mercenary's surrender ought to look like: the sag of the shoulders, the wet shine held back by stubbornness, the hands hanging open and empty. All of it true. All of it also armor.

"Wise," the Queen murmured. "Grief makes such reasonable creatures of us. We will sign at moonrise, in the hall of ledgers. Until then, you are my guest. Wander where you like." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "You cannot leave. I trust that needs no saying."

"No," Edmund said. "I imagine it doesn't."

She walked out of the garden with her heart hammering a war-drum rhythm and her mind moving cold and clear as valley water in thaw. One night. One day. The signing at moonrise, when the Queen would bring the ledger itself, when every soul-entry in the cathedral would be gathered close for the ceremony—that was how courts worked, she'd learned that much in three days among the brokers. Power liked its treasures near when it made trades.

Which meant the ledger would be within reach. Which meant Sefa, somewhere beyond the wards with a smuggler's caravan and a forged manifest and more courage than any archivist should legally possess, would be watching for her signal.

It was a terrible plan. It was the only plan. She'd spent her whole life learning the difference, and mostly there wasn't one.

But first—first there was one thing she had to do, and she had to do it knowing the cost, and do it anyway.

She found Tomas at his desk in the great hall, alone among the glowing shelves, and she pulled the hood back from her hair and let him see her face in the full ember-light.

"I lied to you last night," she said. "About who I am. I'm going to tell you a story instead, and you won't believe it, and that's all right. Once there were two children in a valley between two ridgelines, and they stole honey cakes from a shrine, and when they got caught they swore the thief was a raccoon. A big one. A raccoon of unusual size and cunning. And they called each other Raccoon after that, for years, as a joke nobody else understood, and the younger one was you."

Tomas had gone very still. His pen had stopped mid-figure, leaving a blot on the page like a small dark wound.

"That's not in my records," he whispered.

"No. It wouldn't be."

"There's no entry for—" His breath caught. His hand rose halfway to his chest and hovered there, pressing against something invisible, something that hurt. "Why is there no entry? Everything has an entry. Where is it *filed*?"

"It isn't filed anywhere, Raccoon. That's the point of it."

The name landed in the quiet of the cathedral like a spark landing in dry grass.

For one heartbeat, nothing. Then his eyes changed—not filling with memory, nothing so kind—but flickering, guttering, like a lamp finding oil after long drought. Behind the emptiness something turned over, vast and slow, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound. His mouth shaped the word without voice. *Raccoon.* The blot of ink spread under his forgotten pen. The nearest shelf-embers brightened, all of them, ten thousand little souls leaning toward him like the flowers had leaned toward their Queen.

And far above them both, faint as a heartbeat through stone, Edmund felt Vael's fragment flare against her ribs—and heard, for the first time in eleven years of his silence, her god begin, very softly, to laugh.

Tomas looked up at her with eyes that were not yet his own and not entirely the Queen's anymore, and said:

"...I know you."

The candles guttered. Somewhere in the deeps of the court, a bell began to ring—an alarm, or a summons, Edmund couldn't tell—and she knew she had minutes, perhaps less, before the Queen came to see what her guest had done.

"I know," Edmund said, taking his hand. "Hold onto that. Just that. Hold on."
