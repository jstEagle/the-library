## 9. The Hollow Ember

The signing chamber smelled of ink and cold iron.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed—that a place built for the trading of souls should smell like a clerk's office. The second thing was the ledger itself. It sat on an obsidian dais at the center of the room, a book the size of a coffin, its cover stitched from something that had once been leather and was now only memory of leather. Pages turned by themselves, slow as breathing. Each page whispered.

*Don't listen to them,* Sefa had told her that morning, pressing a stub of chalk into her palm like it was a holy relic. *The pages will read your name back to you in your mother's voice. They did it to a broker I knew. He signed away his own shadow and thanked them for the courtesy.*

Edmund had no mother's voice to lose. She had Tomas's instead—a boy's laugh echoing off shrine walls, a brother teaching her to whittle arrows badly—and she suspected the ledger knew that too. Ledgers always knew what you owed.

The Queen-Below stood across the dais from her, robed in gray so deep it drank the candlelight. She did not look like a monster. She looked like a woman who had done arithmetic for a very long time and found the sums always came out in her favor.

"The quill," said the Queen-Below, and a servant—hollow-eyed, unaging, one of *them*, one of the taken—offered it on a cushion of black velvet. "One soul, freely given. Yours. And the voice we took from your god, returned to our keeping. In exchange, your brother walks out under his own name."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then he stays. He is content here, Captain Vasquez. You saw him." The Queen-Below's mouth curved without warmth. "Contentment is the kindest thing we sell."

*You saw him.* Yes. Edmund had seen him: Tomas in a seneschal's coat three sizes too fine, hands folded, eyes like windows with the shutters painted over. Content the way a held breath is content.

She thought about the wards. About forty-year intervals of peace, purchased and repurchased, generation after generation of some family's Tomas or Sefa or Edmund vanishing into the mountain while the valley above called it weather. She thought about Vael, silent her whole life, and the single word he'd spent whatever remained of himself to say: *run.*

She was very tired of running.

"I'll sign," Edmund said.

The Queen-Below extended the quill.

Edmund took it. It was heavier than it looked—the way guilt is heavier than it looks—and when its nib touched the open page, the ledger sighed. Somewhere in those whispering leaves, her name began assembling itself in her mother's voice, a voice she'd never heard, borrowed from somewhere, stitched together from other people's griefs.

"Before I do," Edmund said, "one condition. One night near my brother. Unwatched."

"A sentimental clause." The Queen-Below considered. "Granted. Sentiment makes the surrender sweeter. We've found it improves the flavor of the bargain."

*I'll bet you have,* Edmund thought, and did not say, because she still needed one more hour, and queens beneath mountains are not known for their patience with sarcasm.

* * *

They gave her a small room adjoining the seneschal's quarters, which was either kindness or a trap with better upholstery. Tomas sat by a window that showed no sky—only stone, softly lit from nowhere—and when Edmund entered, he rose with the smooth courtesy of a man greeting a business associate.

"You're the broker," he said. "From the surface negotiation."

"Tomas. It's me."

Something moved behind the painted shutters of his eyes. Not recognition. Less than that. A draft under a door.

"It's me," she said again, uselessly, because she'd rehearsed this moment on the road for six hundred miles and every version had ended with him saying her name. "When we were small, you called me—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "You called me Dill. Because I fell in the pickle barrel at Ferro's farm and came up smelling like a summer garden, and you laughed until you cried, and you never let me live it down, not once, not in twenty years—"

"Dill," Tomas said.

It wasn't remembering. It was the shape a word leaves in the air after the voice stops. But behind his eyes, something flickered—an ember in wet ash, stubborn, absurd, alive—and his hand rose halfway toward her face before his training caught it and folded it politely back into his lap.

"I'm sorry," he said, in the tone of a man apologizing for the weather. "Have we met?"

"No," Edmund said, and smiled, because if she didn't smile she was going to set fire to the room right then and there, ahead of schedule. "Not yet."

She stayed until the candles burned low. She told him stories he wouldn't keep: the arrow he'd whittled that flew sideways into Sefa's hat; the time they'd dared each other to touch the shrine bell and both run before it rang; the night the stars went out, and how she'd known, somehow, that it was about him. He listened the way stone listens. But twice—twice—his brow creased, faint as a fingerprint in wax, and both times Edmund catalogued it like treasure.

At the door, she paused.

"Hey, Tom. Whatever happens tomorrow—whatever you see, whatever you forget—it wasn't your fault. None of it. You were sold, not spent. There's a difference, and I need somebody in this world to know it even if that somebody isn't you."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. "That's very kind," he said, hollow and courteous, and then, quieter, almost puzzled, almost *his*: "Dill."

Edmund walked out before her face could break.

* * *

The signing was set for dawn, though dawn meant little in a cathedral with no windows. Candles were replaced. Servants filed in and arranged themselves along the walls like furniture. The Queen-Below took her seat beneath the dais's black canopy, and the ledger opened itself to a fresh page, and the whole court leaned in with the appetite of creditors.

Sefa stood among the servants, disguised in livery, chalk-stained fingers hidden in her sleeves. She would not meet Edmund's eye. Good. Sefa had never been able to lie with her face; looking at her now might undo them both.

"Captain Vasquez," the Queen-Below said. "The quill."

Edmund took it. Stepped to the dais. Looked down at the whispering page where her name was already half-written, patient as rot.

Here is what nobody in the court beneath the mountain knew, because they had spent centuries filing souls like currency and had forgotten what souls were *for*: a god's stolen voice does not stay quiet forever. It waits. It finds a crack. Edmund had carried Vael's fragment against her skin since the drowned temple, a mark that burned like a beacon to everything magical—and she had spent the entire descent wondering why the collectors' senses slid off her, why the hunters lost her trail, why the mark that should have made her prey made her instead a blind spot.

Now, standing over the ledger, she understood. The fragment wasn't calling out. It was *listening*. And there is nothing in all the worlds so dangerous as a piece of a silenced god, finally within earshot of the thing that stole the rest of him.

The ledger lay below her hand. The Queen-Below's stolen hoard of voices lay bound inside it—every tithe, every bargain, every silenced prayer, including, somewhere in the deep pages, the original theft: Vael's voice, pressed flat between entries like a flower in a murderer's book.

*Well, old man,* Edmund thought. *You told me to run. I'm going to do the opposite. Forgive me.*

She drove the quill not into the page but into the binding, and spoke the only word that mattered, the word the oracle had taught her, the word that was less a spell than a key turned in a lock:

"*Speak.*"

Vael's fragment ignited.

It started in her chest—a heat like sunrise swallowed whole—and poured down her arm and through the quill and into the ledger, and the ledger *screamed*. Not the pages. The book itself, the stitching, the memory-of-leather cover, all of it shrieking as a god's voice remembered itself and burned outward through everything it touched. Entries caught fire that were not paper—names blazed, bargains blazed, forty generations of quiet complicity blazed—and the whispers became wails became a sound like a valley's worth of people inhaling all at once.

Souls tore free.

Edmund felt them pass through her—she was the crack in the dam, the open door, the one living conduit in a room full of the hollow—and each one struck her like a wave striking a piling. A shepherd woman's laughter. A child's terror turning to wonder. An old man's long-held breath finally released. Hundreds. Thousands. Forty years at a time, four hundred years total, all of it ripping loose and streaming upward through the vaulted dark toward a surface none of them had seen since the day they were sold.

And then Tomas.

He came apart from the Queen-Below's service like a coat slipping off a shoulder, and his soul passed through Edmund's hands—she felt it, warm, real, *his*—and it was already forgetting. She watched it happen. Watched the ember of him gutter and rekindle clean, new-lit, unburdened, and in the rekindling, the pickle barrel went out of him, and the sideways arrow, and the dare at the shrine bell, and *Dill*, and her.

"No," she heard herself say, small, stupid, human. "No, wait—"

But that was the price. She had known the price. The ledger kept accounts, and even burning, it settled them: souls freed whole could not carry their chains with them, and she had been woven into his chains so deeply that freeing him meant unwinding her.

His soul rose. His body—elsewhere in the court, sleeping, she'd learn later—would wake empty of her and full of himself, and that was the trade she'd actually made, whatever she'd told the Queen-Below. Not her soul for his freedom. His memory of her for everyone's freedom, hers included.

Triumphant devastation. Both words, both true, neither sufficient.

The court convulsed around her. Walls of obsidian split like ice on a spring river. The hollow-eyed servants blinked, and blinked again, and began—one by one, like lamps relighting—to *see*. The Queen-Below was on her feet, gray robes smoking at the hem, and her face for the first time in centuries showed something other than arithmetic.

"What have you done?" she screamed, and the ceiling rained light. "The wards are dead, you fool—the valley will drown in it! Magic like a flood, everything you hid from coming home, and no wall, no silence, nothing between your people and the wide terrible world—"

"Yes," Edmund said.

Her voice came out scorched and steady. Vael's fragment banked low in her chest now—not a beacon, not a blaze, just warmth, like a hearth tended by someone who intended to keep tending it.

"That's rather the point."

The Queen-Below stared at her—at this mercenary captain smelling faintly of pickle barrels and godfire, standing in the ashes of four hundred years of careful bookkeeping—and Edmund watched her understand, slowly, the way mountains understand weather: that the age of the tidy bargain was over, and no ledger would ever hold what came next.

Somewhere far above, Edmund knew, stars were coming back on, one by one, like a city remembering itself.

She turned to find Sefa already running toward her through the smoke, chalk-white and laughing and crying, and beyond her, at the shattered edge of the court, the first true sunlight any of these souls had seen in lifetimes was pouring down the broken stair like water finding its old riverbed.

"Come on," Edmund said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she let it. "Let's go home. Whoever we are when we get there."

Behind them, deep in the convulsing dark, the last page of the last ledger curled to ash—and above the mountain, faint but unmistakable, a god cleared his throat.
