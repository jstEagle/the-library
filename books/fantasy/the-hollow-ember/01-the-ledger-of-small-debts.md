## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle of wax, and Cyrus Sandoval still had forty lines to copy before the bell of matins.

She did not look at the candle. Looking at it was a kind of despair, and despair was a debt like any other — one that compounded. Instead she looked at the page before her, the *Canticle of the Sealed Valley*, verse nine hundred and twelve, and set her pen to the vellum with the steady hand of eleven years' practice.

*And the founders said: let no ember cross the border, for what sleeps in the world beyond would wake in us.*

Her pen moved through the familiar shapes. She could have written this verse blindfolded, upside down, in her sleep. She had copied the *Canticle* four times entire, and portions of it more times than she could count, always in the same brown ink on the same rough vellum, always for the same reason: the temple paid three coppers per folio, and every copper went into the ledger.

The ledger sat open beside her inkpot. It was not a temple ledger. It was hers — or rather, it was her family's, which amounted to the same thing and had for as long as she could remember. The columns marched down its pages in her own careful hand: sums borrowed against sums owed, interest tallied by season, small payments recorded with the tenderness other people reserved for births.

*Paid: 4 coppers, autumn tithing.*
*Owed: 1,207 marks, 6 coppers.*

At her current rate — three coppers a folio, ten folios a week if she was swift, less when the winter made her fingers stupid — she would clear the debt in something over two hundred years. Her grandmother had begun paying it down at nineteen. Her mother had never once spoken of it aloud, but Cyrus had found the arithmetic scratched into the back of a cupboard door when she was a child, numbers going back four generations, each generation's total slightly smaller than the last, the way a tide goes out one grain of sand at a time.

She had asked her mother once, when she was young enough to still ask questions: *What did we buy?*

Her mother had been kneading bread. She had not stopped kneading bread. "Nothing," she'd said. "That's how debts work. You pay for nothing, forever."

Cyrus had thought it a joke then. She was older now, and she understood it was simply true, the way scripture was true — not because it made sense but because everyone with authority had agreed it did.

The candle guttered. Forty lines. She bent her head and wrote.

*And they sealed the valley with ash and vow, and the ember they hid within, and no word of magic shall pass the border again.*

Outside the scriptorium window, the valley lay under a sky the color of wet slate. The mountains that ringed them were black against it, their peaks lost somewhere above the cloud line, and between the mountains and the town the terraced fields went gold and gray in the last light. It was, Cyrus admitted to herself in the honest hour after midnight, beautiful. Everyone said so. Pilgrims came up the pass in summer just to stand at the border stones and look back at it, at the green quilt of the valley floor, and weep at how the founders had kept it safe.

Cyrus had lived her whole life inside the picture pilgrims wept over, and some nights she felt about it the way she imagined a fly must feel about honey.

There was nothing wrong with her life. That was the maddening thing. She had warm rooms in the scribes' quarters, work she was good at, a full stomach most days, and a name that meant nothing to anyone outside the valley, which meant safety. The wardens knew her face and nodded to her. The high priest had once complimented her lettering — *clean as cut stone, girl* — and she had carried those five words around for a month like coins in a pocket.

But there was a restlessness in her that had no name she dared write down. It lived somewhere beneath the breastbone, and it woke on nights like this one, when the candle was low and the verse was one she'd written before and would write again until her eyes failed, and it whispered: *is this all? Is this the whole of it — ink and debt and the same four verses until you die?*

She had learned to answer it the way her mother answered everything: with work. So she worked.

Thirty lines. Twenty-five.

It was somewhere around line twenty that her pen skipped, a tiny stutter of the nib, and a blot of ink bloomed on the vellum like a bruise. She swore under her breath — quietly; Brother Anselm slept two doors down and had ears like a bat — and reached for the scraper.

That was when she saw the folio underneath.

It was not hers. That was the first strange thing. Her stack of blank vellum stood where it always stood, at her left elbow, squared and neat. This sheet had slid out from beneath the stack, or perhaps from beneath the desk itself, and it was older than anything she used — soft at the edges, yellowed to the color of old teeth, covered in cramped script that was not the temple's hand.

She should have put it back. She knew exactly where it had come from, actually, now that she looked at it properly: the locked case at the end of the scriptorium, the one holding the restricted folios, the ones only the high priest and his archivists were permitted to touch. Someone had left the case unbarred today. She had noticed it that morning and said nothing, because saying things was how girls in the valley got reputations.

The sensible thing — the Sandoval thing, the indebted thing — was to slide it back under the desk and forget it.

Cyrus picked it up instead.

The restlessness under her breastbone turned over like something waking.

The script was dense, angular, a century old if it was a day. A title ran across the top in faded red: *The Ember Folio*. Below it, prophecy — real prophecy, the forbidden kind, the kind the temple taught had all been fulfilled or falsified long ago and none of it concerned the valley, because nothing concerned the valley, the valley was complete, the valley was safe.

She read fast, guiltily, the way she imagined people ate stolen fruit.

Most of it was the usual fog. *A crown that devours. A sister in exile. A kingdom gilded and rotting.* Kingdoms were far away; kingdoms were stories. But then her eye caught on a passage near the middle, and the room went very quiet around her, the way rooms do when something is about to change.

*A scribe of the hollow valley shall take up the pen,* the old ink read. *On the night when the twin stars cross the shepherd's staff, she shall be taken beyond the border, and the ember shall know its keeper.*

Twin stars. Shepherd's staff. Cyrus frowned. Every child in the valley learned the shepherd's staff — it was the constellation that hung over the eastern ridge in late autumn, the crook of bright stars the farmers used to time the last harvest. And the twin stars crossed it —

They crossed it tonight.

She knew this because she had seen them crossing on her walk up from the refectory, two white points sliding along the crook of the constellation like beads on a wire, and she had thought, idly, *pretty*, and thought nothing else of it.

She looked at the date scrawled in the margin beside the passage, in the same century-old hand. The old calendar reckoning took her a moment to translate — she was a scribe, translation was bread and butter — but when it came, it came all at once, and her mouth went dry.

Tonight. The margin said *tonight*. Written a hundred years before she was born.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, and her voice sounded very small in the dark scriptorium.

Prophecies were vague. Everyone knew that. Prophecies said *a king* and *a darkness* and *in the latter days*, and they fit whatever age you squeezed them into, which was why the temple kept them locked away — not because they were dangerous, the priests said, but because they were embarrassing. Old men guessing at clouds.

This was not vague. This was a date. This was a constellation and a date and a *scribe of the hollow valley*, and there was exactly one hollow valley in the world, and exactly one person awake in its scriptorium at this hour, and she was holding the proof in hands that had suddenly gone cold.

She told herself it was coincidence. She was very thorough about it. Coincidences were common; she copied dates all day; any clever person could match a star-crossing to a calendar. The candle flickered. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and went silent.

Then she heard footsteps on the stairs.

Heavy boots. More than one pair. At this hour, in the temple, there was only one kind of visitor who came in numbers and did not knock.

Cyrus looked down at the folio in her hands — at the red title, at the date that was also this night, at the words *the ember shall know its keeper* — and felt the strangest sensation, as though the sentence had turned around and was reading *her*, measuring her, finding her sufficient.

The footsteps reached the landing.

She slid the folio beneath her ledger, heart hammering, and picked up her pen as the door opened, as lamplight spilled across her half-finished verse, as three wardens in temple gray filled the doorway with the high priest behind them, his face pale and grave in a way she had never seen it, looking at her the way a man looks at a debt come due.

"Scribe Sandoval," he said. "Put down your pen."

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to take the night apart and examine its pieces — she would remember the strangest detail first: that her hand, obeying him, set down the pen without trembling. That some calm part of her, the part that had copied ten thousand verses in obedience, understood before the rest of her did that the ledger had finally called in its oldest entry.

But in the moment, there was only the candle drowning in its own wax, the wardens crossing the threshold, and the high priest saying words she would hear again in nightmares:

"It is the night written in the folio, child. You are the scribe of the hollow valley. And the law says you must go where the valley cannot follow."
