## 2. What the Valley Cannot Keep

They came for her at dawn, when the candle had burned to a coin of wax and her wrist still ached from the night's copying.

Cyrus heard the wardens before she saw them — the particular tread of temple boots on temple stone, four sets, unhurried. No one walked that way through the scriptorium at first light unless something was already decided. She set down her pen with great care, aligned it parallel to the page, and folded her hands as if she were merely resting between verses.

The door opened on Warden Hesk and three others whose names she knew from years of small courtesies: mended cloaks, ink stains blotted from shared tables, the thousand tiny debts of ordinary neighborliness. None of them would meet her eyes.

"Cyrus Sandoval." Hesk's voice was flat as a ledger line. "You are remanded to the custody of the High Priest. Come."

She came. What else did a scribe of the hollow valley do but come when called? Her legs carried her down the corridor past the drying racks where yesterday's scripture hung like pale laundry, past the novices' cells where someone was already chanting the morning psalm about the mercy of boundaries. *Blessed is the valley that keeps us. Blessed is the wall that holds back the wild.* She had copied that psalm nine hundred times. This morning it sounded like a lock turning.

The high priest's study smelled of cedar oil and old vellum, the smell Cyrus loved best in all the world, which made what happened there worse. Father Ambrose sat behind his desk, and the prophecy folio lay open before him — the forbidden one, the one she had touched only once, only read, only breathed upon. Someone had reported the disturbed dust. Someone always reported.

"Sit," he said.

"I can explain—"

"You read it." He turned the folio toward her. There was no anger in his face, which frightened her more than anger would have. There was something like apology, already prepared, already worn smooth from use. "Tell me what you found, child. In your own words."

Her own words felt dangerous now, so she gave him the safe ones. "A date. Tonight's date. In ink a century old."

"Yes." He closed the folio gently, as one closes the eyes of the dead. "And do you know why a century-old prophecy should name this night?"

Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "No, Father."

"Because it does not name a night. It names a person." He drew a single sheet from beneath the folio — a copy, in careful modern hand, of lines she had glimpsed and been dragged away from. "*When the ember stirs in the hollow valley, a scribe shall rise whose hand remembers what the founders buried. Take her beyond the border, for magic waits there, and magic knows its own.*" He looked at her over the desk, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "Scribe of the hollow valley. It could not be plainer. The law is older than I am, older than this temple. When the prophecy wakes, the named one goes beyond the border. That is not cruelty, Cyrus. That is obedience."

"Obedience to what? A book?" Her voice cracked on the word, and she hated herself for it. "I copied scripture for eleven years. I paid— my family has paid—"

"Your debt will be honored. The temple does not forget its obligations." He said it kindly, the way one speaks of weather or harvest yields, and something in Cyrus went very quiet and very cold. "There is a warden escort forming. You will be taken to the border at midmorning."

"Taken." She tested the word like a bad tooth. "Not asked."

"The valley cannot keep you." For a moment — half a heartbeat — something moved behind the priest's composure, some old grief of his own, quickly shuttered. "That has always been true of the ones it names. We only kept the knowledge of it from you, so you might live unafraid. I am sorry it was not longer."

*So you might live unafraid.* As if fear were the tax and ignorance the shelter. As if eleven years of smallness had been a gift.

They let her go to her mother's house under escort, to gather what she could carry. The walk took twenty minutes through streets she had known all her life, and every door along the way stayed shut. She heard the shutters. She heard the psalm rising louder from the temple, the congregation singing the walls strong around them, around everyone except her.

* * *

Her mother was awake, of course. Maren Sandoval had been awake since before dawn her whole life; sleep was another debt the family could not afford. She stood in the kitchen doorway in her good shawl, the one with the mended hem, and watched Cyrus come up the path between two wardens.

"They told me," her mother said, before Cyrus could speak. "Last night. After the priest sent word."

"Last night." Cyrus stopped at the threshold. "You knew last night, and you didn't come to the scriptorium."

"What would I have done there?" Her mother's voice was level. She turned back into the kitchen, and Cyrus followed because there was nowhere else in the world left to follow her. "Stand outside a door and weep? Weep the debt down? Weep the ledger clean?" She began, absurdly, to pack — wrapping bread in cloth, setting out the good needle and thread, items selected with the same economy she brought to everything. "You'll need these. The border towns charge for mending."

"Mama." The word came out younger than Cyrus intended, a child's word, and she pressed on anyway. "Eleven years. I copied until my fingers bled for this family. Doesn't that buy anything? Not a goodbye that means something? Not even—"

"It buys your cousin Tam the chance at a life," her mother said. "The debt transfers to him. The priest agreed this morning. Twenty-two years old, no craft, no calling — and now no debt either, once the temple forgives the balance for your removal." She smoothed the cloth over the bread as if smoothing a bedsheet for a guest. "Do you understand what that means? Your going pays for his staying. That is not nothing, Cyrus. That is arithmetic."

*Arithmetic.* Cyrus stood in the kitchen where she had grown up, in the house where every surface bore the marks of a life lived carefully, sparingly, counting — and understood that her mother was grieving. That this flat, transactional voice was the only shape her grief was permitted, the only language the ledger had ever taught her. It did not help. It was almost worse, seeing the love locked behind the numbers, present and payable and never once spent.

"Is there anything," Cyrus said slowly, "that you want to say to me? Anything that isn't about debt?"

Her mother's hands went still on the bread. For a long moment the only sound was the psalm drifting faintly from the temple, and the tick of the mantel clock that had run slow for as long as Cyrus could remember, so that the family had always lived a few minutes behind everyone else.

"Eat something before you go," her mother said finally. "The road is long."

It was not the sentence Cyrus wanted. But her mother cut the bread herself, thick slices, more generous than the household could spare, and put honey on them — real honey, hoarded honey — and Cyrus understood that this was the farewell. Not words. Rations. In the valley's grammar, this was *I love you*, written in the only ink they'd ever been allowed.

She ate. She could not taste any of it.

At the door, her mother pressed the needle and thread into her satchel and then, briefly, held her daughter's wrist — the scribe's wrist, the ink-stained wrist — hard enough to leave marks. "You were never ours to keep," she said, looking at the wall. "The valley just lent you. Everything here is borrowed, Cyrus. Learn that early and you won't be hurt by it."

Then she let go, and turned back to her counting, and that was the whole of it.

* * *

The escort formed at midmorning: six wardens, Hesk at their head, and Father Ambrose walking beside Cyrus as far as the road allowed. The valley wore its autumn best for the occasion — gold on the hillsides, smoke rising straight from chimneys, the air sharp and clean as a freshly cut page. It was beautiful the way a cell is beautiful to the prisoner who has never seen another room.

At the last farmstead before the border, Ambrose stopped.

"I'll say the words here," he told Hesk. "She walks the final stretch alone. The law requires it." He turned to Cyrus, and for the first time since the study, he looked at her fully — not as a prophecy fulfilled, not as a debt discharged, but as a girl he had watched grow up among the drying racks. "Child, I will tell you one thing the law does not require. The founders did not build the wall to keep magic out. They built it to keep something *in*. Something they hid. And prophecies, whatever else they are, have long memories." He pressed a small coin into her palm — an old custom, a traveler's blessing, worth almost nothing. "Whatever waits out there, remember: you were named by a book, but you were made by your own hand. Eleven years of copying teaches more than scripture."

Then he turned and walked back toward the valley, and did not look around, and she understood that this too was a kind of grief wearing duty's clothes.

The border itself was nothing — a line of standing stones gone grey with lichen, a gap in the hills where the road passed through. Beyond it, the world simply continued: more hills, more sky. It looked insultingly ordinary.

Hesk and the wardens halted at the stones. "We go no further," Hesk said. "No one crosses who lives inside the wall. Walk until you reach the shrine at the crossroads. Someone will find you there." He hesitated, and then, gruffly, added the only kindness the wardens' code permitted: "You copied fairer than any of us deserved to read."

Cyrus stepped between the stones.

The change was instant and total — like a deaf woman hearing her first thunderclap, like color flooding into a world drawn in charcoal. Magic did not trickle across the border; it *recognized* her. It poured into her fingertips where the ink stains were, sparking painful and bright, and the hair rose on her arms, and somewhere far off in her chest something that had been banked for a hundred years woke up and opened one burning eye.

She gasped, staggered, caught herself against a standing stone — and the stone warmed under her palm, humming, as if it had been waiting a century for exactly this touch.

Behind her, the valley. Silent. Sealed. Already forgetting her, perhaps; already singing its walls strong.

Ahead, the road ran down through country she had never seen, toward a capital she knew only from forbidden maps, where a king wore a crown that ate its wearer, and a prophecy waited with her name in its mouth.

Cyrus Sandoval, scribe of the hollow valley, wiped her eyes once — briskly, economically, the way her mother would have — and walked.
