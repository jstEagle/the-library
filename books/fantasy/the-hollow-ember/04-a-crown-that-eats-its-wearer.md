## 4. A Crown That Eats Its Wearer

They dressed her in borrowed silk.

Cyrus stood before a cracked mirror in a guest chamber of the palace of Vael Amaranth while a servant with careful hands pinned a grey-blue gown at her shoulders — temple colors, she realized. Someone had chosen them deliberately. The fabric was finer than anything she had ever touched, soft as moth-wing, and it made her feel like a forgery: scripture copied in gold ink on stolen vellum.

"You'll stand at the back," Vess said from the doorway, not looking up from the folio cradled against his chest. He had not let it out of his sight since the archive. "Behind the pillar. You watch. You do not speak. Above all, you do not *spark*."

"I don't know how to spark."

"Then we're safe." He glanced up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and still burning. "The court assembles for the king's weekly audience. He appears for one hour. He listens to petitions he no longer understands. And then he leaves, and everyone pretends they have seen a king."

"What am I looking for?"

Vess was quiet a moment. "You'll know," he said. "I'm sorry to say you'll know."

* * *

The throne room was gilded rot given architecture.

Cyrus saw it everywhere once she knew to look: gold leaf blistering off pillars that no one had repainted in years, tapestries faded to ghosts of hunts and coronations, chandeliers hung with perhaps half their crystals, the rest long since sold or stolen. The courtiers themselves were beautiful the way a painted face is beautiful — powder over pallor, jewels over frayed cuffs. They smiled at each other with the strained courtesy of people sharing a house with something dangerous.

She stood behind the pillar as instructed, hands folded, heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out.

The doors opened.

King Aldric entered alone.

He was young — younger than she had imagined, barely past twenty, with the kind of face that portraitists would flatter and poets would mourn. Dark hair, uncombed. A jaw still soft with boyhood. He wore black and silver, and above his brow, the crown.

It did not look like a crown. It looked like a wound that had learned to be ornamental — a circlet of dark metal, twisted like cooled candlewax, set with stones the color of old blood. As he walked the length of the hall, Cyrus felt the ember inside her stir for the second time since crossing the border, turning over in her chest like a sleeper disturbed by a sound.

The petitions began. A grain merchant begged relief from tariffs. A widow from the river districts asked for dredging crews before spring floods. A minor lord complained about wolves.

Aldric listened to all of it with perfect, terrible attention.

That was what undid her — not vacancy but its opposite. His eyes moved across each petitioner as though he were trying to drink them, to hold their faces somewhere they could not be taken from him. When the widow finished speaking, he said, gently, "Your husband's name?"

"Osric, Majesty."

"Osric," the king repeated, closing his eyes briefly, as if setting the name down somewhere fragile. "The dredging will be done. I will remember Osric."

A murmur ran through the court — approval, or pity; Cyrus could not tell which. She watched the king's hands, resting on the arms of the throne, and saw that they trembled very slightly, the way an old man's hands tremble.

And then it happened.

The king's gaze lifted from the crowd and drifted — idly, without purpose — until it found the pillar where she stood.

Something ancient looked out through his young face.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. His expression did not change; nothing so crude as a smile or a sneer crossed him. But behind his eyes, behind the tired boy, something *turned over* — vast and slow and patient, the way a leviathan turns beneath a boat, indifferent to whether the boat survives the wake. The ember in Cyrus's chest flared white-hot, and pain lanced up her throat and into her teeth, and she bit down on a cry and pressed her spine flat against cold stone.

When she dared look again, the king had turned away. He was saying something to his chamberlain. His shoulders were just a young man's shoulders.

But her fingertips were glowing faintly through her gloves, and she clasped her hands tighter and thought: *it knows me. Whatever wears him, it knows me.*

* * *

He is nine years old, and the valley smells of cut hay and river water.

His mother has brought him across the border in secret — this is forbidden, this is wonderful, everything forbidden is wonderful — and the wardens at the valley's edge bowed to her and pretended not to see the prince slip past the standing stones. Here there are no crowns. Here a boy can run through orchards with juice sticky on his chin and no one kneels.

An old woman teaches him to fold paper boats. Her hands are stained with ink, always ink, and she laughs at his clumsy folds and says, "A king who cannot make anything will only ever take things, little one. Learn to make."

He makes her a boat. It sinks immediately in the millpond. She laughs until she cries, and he decides that this — this exact sound — is the best thing in the world, and he will keep it forever.

He keeps it for six more years.

Then the crown comes down onto his head at his coronation, cold as a coffin lid, and the thing inside it wakes, and begins, patiently, meal by meal, to eat.

The paper boat goes first. Then the smell of hay. Then his mother's voice, then her face, then the fact of her. The old woman's laughter holds out longest — seven years, eight — because he hides it deep, buries it beneath other memories like contraband, and every night the crown digs it up and takes a bite, and every morning he tries to remember why he is grieving.

By nineteen, he remembers only that he loved something once, and that it is gone, and that the hunger behind his eyes is never, ever full.

* * *

"He saw me," Cyrus said.

They were back in Vess's rooms — a scholar's garret smuggled into the palace's eastern wing, walls of books, floor of books, a single window overlooking the inner gardens where frost was beginning to silver the hedges. Vess had gone grey around the mouth when she told him about the moment at the pillar. Now he poured wine into two cups with hands that were not quite steady, and pushed one across the desk to her.

"It saw you," he corrected. "There's a difference. Aldric hasn't truly *seen* anything in years. But the crown —" He sat down heavily. "The crown notices what feeds on magic. And you, scribe, are apparently a feast walking upright."

"Tell me what it is. All of it. No more riddles."

Vess drank. Set down the cup. And for once, the theatrical scholar fell away, and what remained was an old man who had lost everything once already and was afraid of losing the rest.

"Three hundred years ago," he said, "the founders of your valley didn't build a wall against magic. They performed a binding. They took a spirit — one of the old ones, older than kingdoms, older than names — and they bound it into an object, and they sealed the valley so it could never return to the wild places where such things live. The valley isn't a refuge from magic, Cyrus. It's a cage. And your whole life, you've been living on the lid."

"The crown."

"The crown. Passed hand to hand, king to king, always kept close, because the binding weakens with distance. And a bound spirit does what bound spirits do." He tapped his own temple. "It feeds. Not on flesh. On memory. Every king who wore it gave it decades of himself — first loves, first griefs, faces, names, the taste of bread, the sound of his mother's voice. Aldric was crowned at fifteen. Do the arithmetic."

Cyrus did, and felt sick. "He has almost nothing left."

"He has rituals instead of memories. He asks petitioners for their names because he cannot keep them. He orders the same dredging project approved every spring because every spring he learns of it anew." Vess's voice roughened. "I served in this court as a young man. I watched three kings hollow out. I wrote a paper about it — I called the crown what it is, a parasite — and they stripped my title and burned my chair for it. Truth is treason in a kingdom built on pretending."

"And the prophecy." She pulled the folio toward her, its worn leather warm under her palms. "The unfinished line. *And the scribe shall—*"

"*Shall unbind what was bound,*" Vess finished quietly. "That's the missing ending. I've spent thirty years reconstructing it from fragments, and I'd stake my life — I *have* staked my life — that the final clause reads some variation of those words. The spirit was bound by scribes, Cyrus. Founders' scribes, working in concert, sealing it into metal and blood-oath. What scribes bind, a scribe can loose. But not any scribe." He looked at her, and his eyes held something between awe and apology. "The text says *the scribe of the hollow valley.* The ember's keeper. You."

"Why me?" The question tore out of her rawer than she intended. "I'm nobody. I copied scripture for eleven hours a day to pay a debt that wasn't even mine. I've never — I don't know how to do *anything* except write neatly and keep accounts—"

"That," said Vess, "is exactly the point."

He rose and went to the window, silhouetted against the frosted garden.

"A hero would try to destroy the crown. Smash it, melt it, throw it in the sea. And the spirit would simply leap free — into whoever stood nearest, into the kingdom itself, into the land. Unbound carelessly, it becomes a plague. But a scribe doesn't destroy. A scribe *rewrites.* The binding is a text, Cyrus — a covenant written into the metal at the founding, in the founders' hand. If the right hand rewrites that covenant, the spirit isn't shattered or freed. It's *released properly* — sent beyond the world's edge, the way the old rites intended, whole and harmless. No plague. No new host. Just... an ending, finally, after three hundred years."

Silence. Somewhere below, a bell rang the hour, and Cyrus counted the strokes automatically, the way she had counted bells in the scriptorium all her life, and hated how much of her was still that girl.

"The price," she said.

Vess turned. "What?"

"There's always a price. Everything in the ledger costs something. Rewriting a three-hundred-year-old covenant with the ember of the founders — that doesn't come free." She heard her own voice go flat and steady, the voice she used for tallying sums she didn't want to think about. "What does it cost?"

Vess held her gaze for a long moment. Then he looked away, at the books, at the frost, anywhere else.

"I don't know," he said.

"You're lying."

"I'm *hoping,*" he snapped, and then gentled, instantly, guiltily. "Forgive me. The fragments mention... a toll. Paid in the only coin a scribe truly owns. I couldn't translate the word precisely. Some scholars say *blood.* Some say *years.*" He paused. "One fragment says *remembrance.*"

Cyrus looked down at her hands — the ink-stained nails, the callus on her middle finger shaped by a decade of pens. Hands that had copied ten thousand lines of other people's truths.

Outside, far off across the palace, a door boomed, and footsteps echoed in a corridor, and she thought of a young man asking a widow for her dead husband's name so he could carry it for one hour before it was taken from him too.

Pity, she found, was a small fire. It warmed, and it guttered, and it asked nothing of you but feeling.

Resolve was different. Resolve was a debt. And Cyrus Sandoval had never once in her life failed to pay a debt.

"When do I meet him?" she asked. "Properly. Alone."

Vess stared at her. "Cyrus — no. Not yet. You're not ready, and the crown already noticed you today, and if Isolde hears you're moving before—"

"She's coming tomorrow, you said. And the court burns archives and kings forget their mothers, and every day I wait is another day it eats him." She closed the folio and stood, and the ember pulsed in her chest like a second heartbeat, like agreement. "Teach me fast, Vess. Because I've decided."

"Decided what?"

She looked at the window, at the distant towers, at the wing where a hollow-eyed boy-king slept with a parasite on his brow.

"That I'm going to write him free. Whatever it costs."

From the shadows of the doorway behind them — a doorway neither of them had heard open — a dry, familiar voice said:

"Well. Isn't that convenient. Because I've come a long way to decide whether to help you — or to kill you."

They turned. Princess Isolde stepped into the lamplight, snow melting on her shoulders, a knife at her belt and her brother's hollow eyes staring out of her face.
