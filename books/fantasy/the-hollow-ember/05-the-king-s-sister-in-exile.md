## 5. The King's Sister in Exile

The house at the edge of the Saltfen marshes had once been a hunting lodge, and it wore its decay like a deliberate insult to the crown that had built it. Shutters hung by single nails. Moss climbed the chimney stones in green ropes. And on the door, burned into the wood in letters deep enough to catch rainwater, was a single word: *TRAITOR*.

"She did that herself," Vess said, dismounting. "The night of her exile. Said if the king wanted her branded, she'd do the branding honestly."

Cyrus stared at the word. Three days out from the capital, three days of Vess's coughing and the road's gray monotony, and she still flinched at every mention of the name Isolde — as if the princess were a fever the kingdom kept catching and never cured.

"You told me she tried to kill the king," Cyrus said.

"I told you she *accused* him." Vess swung down from the saddle with the care of a man whose ribs still remembered the archive fire. "Accusation is not a knife, scribe. Though in this kingdom, it cuts deeper."

The door opened before Vess could knock.

Isolde Aldrane was not what Cyrus expected. She was thirty perhaps, or thirty-five, with her brother's dark hair cut brutally short and his same hollowed cheeks — but where the king's face had been emptied by something eating it from within, hers looked carved by choice, by weather, by years of deciding exactly who she would be. She wore a man's coat over a woman's dress and a knife at each hip, and she looked at Cyrus the way a farrier looks at a horse brought to her with a limp.

"So," she said. "The valley finally coughed up its ember."

"You know why we've come."

"I know why *you've* come, scholar. I'm asking her." Isolde's eyes stayed on Cyrus. "Well? Say it yourself. What are you?"

Cyrus's hands curled at her sides. In them, faintly, the ache she'd carried since the border — the itch of light trapped under skin. "I don't know yet."

"Good answer." Isolde stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before something in the marsh sees you. Things here have noses for spark."

* * *

Inside, the lodge was one great room dominated by a table, and the table was covered in bindings — not books, though there were books, but *bindings*: knotted cords, wax seals cracked and resealed, strips of vellum stitched with red thread, iron rings blackened as if they'd been held in flame. Cyrus had spent her life copying scripture about the age when magic moved freely through the world, and here was its wreckage laid out like an anatomy lesson.

"Touch nothing," Isolde said, and then, without preamble: "Give me your hand."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to test whether you're the scribe the prophecy wants or just another girl the valley spat out, and I'd rather know now than after I've wasted a winter on you." She held out her palm, patient as stone.

Cyrus looked at Vess. Vess, who had claimed the prophecy was unfinished, who had bled for a book, who had not slept properly since the archive burned — Vess gave her a small nod that managed to be both apology and permission.

She put her hand in Isolde's.

The princess's grip was dry and strong, calloused along the fingers like a swordsman's. For a moment nothing happened. Then Isolde murmured something — old words, older than scripture, in a cadence Cyrus's copying hand knew but her mouth didn't — and the world *tilted*.

Light came out of her.

Not metaphor. Light, gold-white and hungry, pouring up through her arm, through their joined hands, throwing their shadows huge against the rafters. It didn't hurt, exactly. It felt like the moment a held breath finally breaks free — except the breath had been held for twenty-two years, by someone else, inside her, and it came out screaming. The cords on the table rattled. A wax seal split clean in two. Somewhere in the walls, the marsh went silent, every frog stopping at once.

Then Isolde let go, and Cyrus staggered back into a chair, gasping, her fingertips glowing faintly like banked coals.

"Well," Isolde said. Her voice was steady, but she was looking at her own palm as if it had touched something holy and blasphemous in the same instant. "That's more than a spark."

"What — what was that?"

"That was the founders' theft, answering to its own blood." Isolde pulled a cloth from her belt and wiped her hand, briskly, as if cleaning away evidence. "Your valley wasn't blessed, girl. It was a vault. They walled magic out of the world and hid the last living piece of it inside a bloodline, and your family has been paying rent on that secret ever since. Debt, they called it. I'd call it something else."

Cyrus thought of the ledger. Of the columns of small sums, the debts she'd copied and recopied, the arithmetic of her whole life. Something cold moved through her chest.

"Can you teach me?" she asked. "To control it?"

"I can teach you the binding-craft. Whether you control it is between you and whatever's inside you." Isolde turned to Vess. "And you. You want me to help her *unbind* the crown. Save my brother. Is that the shape of it?"

"The prophecy—"

"I've read your prophecy, Vess. I read it before you were disgraced, when I still had a seat at court and access to things you only dream about." Isolde's voice didn't rise, but the room seemed to tighten around it. "I know what it says. I also know what it doesn't say. So let me say plainly what you two have apparently been too polite to ask me."

She looked at Cyrus.

"I don't want my brother saved. I want him dead."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the fire to pop twice.

"He's being eaten alive," Isolde continued, pacing now, the lamplight sliding across her face. "You saw him. You stood in that throne room and you saw what wears him. Every year it takes more — first his childhood, then his mother's face, then his own name on some days. In ten years there will be nothing left to save. Nothing but a crown sitting on a corpse that still walks and signs edicts. I have watched that happen from exile for six years while the court called *me* mad for naming sorcery sorcery." She stopped. "A clean death is mercy. A clean death ends it. Your prophecy offers — what? A ritual. A chance. A gamble with the kingdom as the stake."

"It offers *him*," Cyrus said.

Her voice surprised her. Small, steady, valley-trained.

"He's a boy under there. I saw him. Whatever the crown is, he's still underneath it, drowning, and he knows it, and—" She stopped, because the next part was harder. "He loved the valley. Before. There's something in his memories of it — the orchards, the border stones. He used to visit as a child. Someone taught him the names of trees."

Isolde went very still.

"My grandmother," Cyrus finished quietly. "I think. I don't understand all of it yet. But he remembers kindness from my family, and I will not pay that back with a knife."

"You'll pay it back with your life, most likely."

"Then that's the price."

"No." Isolde crossed the room in four strides and took Cyrus's chin in her hand — not cruelly, but with the absolute authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed. Up close, Cyrus saw it: the resemblance to Aldric, exact and terrible, the same bones around the same hollow places. "Listen to me, scribe, because no one else will tell you the truth. Mercy is a door. The thing in that crown has been starving for six hundred years, and doors are how it feeds. It will find your compassion and it will *use* it. It will wear his face when you're weakest. It will offer you bargains shaped like kindness. And when you hesitate — even for a heartbeat — it will take everything."

She released her.

"I will teach you the binding-craft, because the prophecy chose you and I am not fool enough to fight fate twice. But hear this clearly: if you fail, I will finish it my way. And if your mercy costs my brother another year of that torment—" her jaw tightened, "—then I will consider you as much his enemy as the crown is."

"Understood," Cyrus said. And meant it, mostly. And hated herself a little for the small voice underneath that whispered: *but I'd still try to save you both.*

* * *

The lessons began at dawn and did not end until the candles drowned in their own wax.

Binding-craft, it turned out, was less like magic and more like scripture — which was the only reason Cyrus could bear it. Knots with grammar. Wax with syntax. The old craft spoke the world's structure aloud and then politely asked it to hold still. Isolde taught the way she did everything: without praise, without softness, correcting Cyrus's knot-work with a flick of her knife until Cyrus's fingers bled and learned.

"You copy text," Isolde observed on the third night, watching Cyrus bind a warding sigil into a strip of vellum. "That's why it comes easy. A scribe already knows that words are cages. Most people think language describes. Scribes know better. Language *confines*. That's all a binding is. A sentence the world can't argue with."

"And the unbinding?"

"A sentence the world can't refuse." Isolde's eyes flickered toward the window, toward the distant glow of the capital on the horizon. "Which is why it will cost you more than ink. Everything true does."

It was near midnight when Cyrus woke — not to sound, but to the absence of it. The marsh frogs had gone silent again.

She lay in her narrow cot beneath the eaves and listened. Below, Isolde's breathing, even. Vess snoring softly by the fire. Wind in the reeds. And then — footsteps. On gravel. Outside. More than one set, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had already counted the exits.

Cyrus slid from the cot without a whisper of sound, twenty-two years of temple discipline finally worth something. Through the gap in the shutter she saw them: four figures in the moonlight, faces masked, blades bare. Not crown guards. Worse — plainclothes, deniable. The kind of men a frightened court sends when it cannot afford witnesses.

They reached the door.

Cyrus did not think. That was the terrifying part, looking back — how little thought there was. Her hand came up, and the ember answered, gold light flooding her palm, and she pressed it against the doorframe and *spoke* — the first binding Isolde had taught her, the simplest one, the ward against entry — and the word left her mouth in a voice that was hers and older than hers, and the doorframe ignited with pale script, and the lead assassin's blade stopped one inch from the wood as if it had struck glass.

The crack of it woke the house.

What followed was ugly and fast. Isolde came off her cot with both knives drawn and fought like a woman settling six years of accounts; Vess, half-blind with sleep, hurled a lamp and screamed scholarly profanity; the assassins broke against the wards and the princess and fled into the marsh carrying their wounded, leaving behind one dropped dagger with the royal armory's mark filed off — but not well enough.

Afterward, Cyrus sat on the floor with her back against the scorched doorframe, shaking, her glowing hand cradled in her lap like something injured.

Isolde crouched in front of her. Blood ran from a cut above the princess's eye and she ignored it entirely.

"They weren't sent to capture you," Isolde said. "Those were killing strokes. Which means the crown knows what you are now — or the crown's *tenant* does." She studied Cyrus's face in the dying lamplight. "You bound that door alone. Untrained. Do you understand what that means?"

"That I got lucky."

"It means the thing in my brother felt you coming, and it is afraid." Isolde stood, and offered — grudgingly, like a coin pried from a fist — the ghost of respect. "We leave for the capital tomorrow. You need the original manuscript, and I need to see how bad he's gotten." She paused at the foot of the stairs, not turning around.

"Scribe. Tonight you chose to protect us instead of running. That was either courage or foolishness, and I haven't decided which." Her voice dropped. "But mark me — the next time that thing sends its knives, it won't send four."

In the silence after she left, Cyrus opened her hand. The light there had faded to embers, but in her palm, raised like a scar in new skin, was a single line of script she had never written and could not read —

and it was in her grandmother's handwriting.
