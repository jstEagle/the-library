## 6. Ink, Blood, and Borrowed Time

The archive smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old vellum, a smell Cyrus had known all her life without knowing it belonged to anything but the valley. Here it was different. Here the books breathed.

Vess had cleared a table near the window, where afternoon light fell in a dusty column across the prophecy's original manuscript — not the fair copy in the temple's possession, not the recitation-text every warden could chant from memory, but the thing itself: calfskin boards gone soft as cloth at the corners, pages the color of weak tea, and ink that seemed to sit *above* the surface of the parchment rather than sink into it. As if the words were still deciding whether to stay.

"Handle it by the edges," Vess said, not looking up from her own notes. "The original resents being touched. It has opinions."

"Books don't have opinions."

"This one dreamed itself into being, girl. Of course it has opinions."

Cyrus almost smiled. Three days in Vess's archive and she had learned the shape of the woman's moods the way she'd once learned the grain of temple paper: the sharpness that meant affection, the silence that meant fear. Vess was afraid now. She had been afraid since the attempt on Cyrus's life outside Isolde's door, though she hid it beneath lectures on marginalia and the taxonomy of prophetic hands.

"Read me what you see," Vess said. "Everything. Even what seems like nothing. Especially that."

Cyrus bent over the opening folio. The prophecy ran in a fine, disciplined script — a scribe's hand, she thought with a small private ache; whoever wrote this had been trained as she had been trained, ruled lines and measured letters, devotion disguised as craft. The famous verses passed beneath her eyes. *A scribe of the hollow valley shall come when the ember wakes.* The date. Her date.

Then, in the lower margin, something else.

Small letters, cramped, slanting hard to the right as if written in haste or in hiding. Not the same hand. Older, perhaps — or simply more tired.

"There's writing in the margin," she said. "It's been scraped and rewritten over, but under the palimpsest—" She stopped. Her breath went strange.

"What?" Vess's head came up sharply. "What do you see?"

"The ink is moving." That wasn't right either. The ink wasn't moving. The ink was *warming*, the way metal warms before it burns, and the warmth was climbing through her fingertips and up her wrists and into the place behind her sternum where the ember lived — the stolen spark the founders had hidden, the one that apparently slept inside *her*. "It knows me," she whispered. "Vess, the margin knows my hand."

"Read it aloud. Slowly."

She read:

*"The debt is paid in blood and kept in blood. We did not steal the ember — we carried it out so it could not be caged again. One line will hold it. One line will pay for it. Let the ledger never close until the scribe comes home."*

Below that, a single word, pressed so hard the stylus had torn the vellum: *forgive*.

Cyrus sat back. The room tilted gently, the way a boat tilts. "That's—"

"Whose hand?" Vess demanded, crossing to her. "Look at it properly. Whose?"

"I've seen it my whole life." Cyrus heard her own voice as if from far away. "On the debt-ledgers. On the permission slips for grain. On the letter she sent every solstice that I was never allowed to answer." Her grandmother's hand. The woman who had died when Cyrus was nine, who had held her once — only once, she remembered suddenly, violently, the memory arriving whole like a bird through a window — and said, *you are the page, little one, whatever they tell you you are only the pen.*

"She wrote this," Cyrus said. "Before I was born. Before the prophecy was even finished being written, maybe. My family's debt — the debt I've spent eleven years copying scripture to pay down — it was never money."

"No," Vess said quietly. "It was never money."

"It was *me*. They were paid to hide the ember in their bloodline. In *my* bloodline. The valley didn't lose its magic because the founders were cruel or foolish — they smuggled it out, they hid it in a child of children of children, and then they charged themselves a debt to explain why the family stayed poor and obedient and *watched*." She was standing now. She didn't remember standing. "Every warden who counted my copies. Every night my mother told me to write faster, the interest compounds, write *faster* — they weren't raising a daughter. They were keeping a vault."

"Cyrus—"

"And my grandmother *knew*. She wrote it down and hid it in the one book no one in the valley would ever be permitted to read, and she wrote *forgive*, and I—" The fury came up through her like floodwater through floorboards, finding every seam. Eleven years. Eleven years of candle-smoke and cramped fingers and her mother's cold arithmetic, and none of it had been punishment, or duty, or even greed. It had been *storage*. She had been shelved. "They let me believe I was small. They built a whole life of smallness around me like a wall, and it was a *sheath*."

Vess put a hand on her shoulder. For once the scholar had nothing scholarly to say.

Outside, somewhere below the archive window, a cart rattled past. Ordinary. Harmless. Neither of them moved for a long moment, and the light crawled slowly across the manuscript, and Cyrus became aware, distantly, that her fingertips were still glowing faintly gold, and that the marginalia had faded back beneath the scrape, satisfied, waiting.

"We need to copy it," Vess said finally, voice roughened. "All of it. Every margin, every scrape, every watermark. If the crown learns what your family did—"

"They already know." Cyrus wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, angrier that there were tears at all. "Isolde said the crown's agents have been asking after me since before I crossed the border. They didn't drag me out of the valley on a date and a hunch. They knew about the ember. Maybe they've always known."

"Then we work faster than they do." Vess was already pulling fresh paper toward her, already sharpening a pen with quick, vicious strokes. "You transcribe. I'll annotate. And Cyrus — if anyone comes through that door who isn't me or the princess, you burn the manuscript. Do you understand? Your spark can do it. Let it."

"You want me to burn a century-old original?"

"I want them to not have it. Grief is a luxury for afterward."

They worked. The light died by degrees; Vess lit lamps; the archive settled into its nighttime sounds, settling leather, ticking shelves, the wind worrying the high windows. Cyrus copied until her hand ached in the old familiar way, and found, strangely, that the ache comforted her — this much of her life had not been a lie. She really was a scribe. Whatever else they had made her, she had made herself this.

She was deep in the third folio, copying a passage about the crown-spirit's binding — *bound by consent, fed by consent, unbound only by a hand that consents to be emptied* — when Vess went very still at the window.

"Lamps," Vess said softly. "Out. Now."

Cyrus pinched them dark. Below, in the street, torchlight moved with the particular discipline of soldiers — not the loose sprawl of a patrol but a converging, deliberate geometry. Four men at the corner. Two more at the archway. A knock would come, or it wouldn't; they might simply be passing.

They were not passing.

Smoke first. It seeped under the door smelling wrong — sweet, oily, alchemical. Then orange light licked up the stairwell, and the shouting began below, voices ordering the building cleared, the building condemned, the building *cleansed*.

"The stairs are lost," Vess said. She was astonishingly calm. She swept the manuscripts off three tables into a leather satchel, working faster than Cyrus would have believed. "Back window. There's a roof, then a drop into the dyer's yard. You go first."

"Not without you."

"Don't argue with an old woman, it's undignified." But Vess wasn't moving toward the window. She was moving toward the door, toward the smoke, and she had the original manuscript — the prophecy itself — clutched against her chest with both arms like a child. "Someone has to convince them the archive is empty. Old scholars make very convincing ghosts. Go."

"Vess—"

"*Go.*" Then, softer, almost gentle, over the crackle of the burning stairwell: "You are the most important page in this kingdom, girl. Pages get carried to safety. That's the whole of the job."

Cyrus went — two steps — and turned back anyway, which is how she saw it.

The door burst inward in a bloom of fire, and figures came through masked in ash-black cloth, and Vess did not run. She stood in the middle of her burning life's work with the manuscript held high above her head, an old woman offering a book to the flames as if it were the flame's own scripture, and she said something Cyrus couldn't hear — something loud and contemptuous and unmistakably *scholarly* — and then the first blade took her through the side, and the second through the shoulder, and Vess went down folding herself around the book the way a mother folds around a child, so that when they pried at her arms the steel met her spine before it met the vellum.

Cyrus's spark detonated.

She didn't decide it. The ember decided for her — gold light screaming down her arms, out through the window frame, shattering glass and throwing two masked men backward off the roof-ledge into the yard below. Heat rolled off her skin. Somewhere in the fire a beam collapsed. She was on the roof, she was running, she was leaping the gap to the dyer's shed with the satchel banging against her hip, and behind her the archive burned like a struck match, and behind *that* — faint, terrible, threaded through the roar — she could hear Vess laughing.

Not screams. Laughing. Wild, wheezing, triumphant laughter, the laughter of a woman who had spent thirty years being laughed out of every lecture hall in the kingdom and had just made the crown send eight armed men and a bucket of alchemical fire to answer her footnotes.

Cyrus landed in the yard, rolled, came up bleeding from one palm, and ran.

* * *

Isolde's safehouse was a shuttered dye-works that smelled of mordant and secrets. Cyrus laid Vess on the long staining-table herself, with the princess's help, and watched the physician's face do the thing faces do when they are deciding how honest to be.

"The blade missed everything vital by the width of a prayer," the physician said at last. "But there's poison on the steel. Crown-issue. Slow. She'll have days, not weeks, and the days will hurt."

"Then we won't waste them," Isolde said.

Vess, grey-faced and slick with sweat, managed a smile. "Spoken like your brother's sister." She fumbled beneath her bloody coat and produced the manuscript — scorched at one corner, otherwise untouched, wrapped in her own body's sacrifice. She pushed it toward Cyrus with both trembling hands. "Yours. It was always going to be yours. Read the rest of the margins, girl. All of them. Your grandmother left you instructions, and I suspect—" a cough, wet and awful, "—I suspect the last page isn't a prophecy at all. I suspect it's a *draft*."

Cyrus opened the folio with hands that shook.

The final leaf was different from all the others. Fresher. The vellum pale, the ruling-lines crisp, and across the top, in her grandmother's hand — the same hand, unmistakable, patient, loving, damning — a single unfinished sentence:

*"Let the scribe take up the pen and write the ending thus:"*

And below it, nothing. Blank space. Waiting.

The ember stirred beneath Cyrus's breastbone like a thing waking hungry, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what kind of ink the blank page was waiting for.

Behind her, Vess whispered: "Now you know what it costs. Choose what you'll pay before someone chooses for you."
