## 7. The Thing Wearing His Crown

The summons came at the hour when even the palace candles seemed to doubt themselves — a folded card of grey vellum, no seal, no signature, only a single line in a hand so fine it might have been written by a spider walking through ink:

*Come alone. The west gallery. When the bells stop.*

Vess read it twice and went pale in a way that had nothing to do with his wounds. "Don't go."

"Kings don't summon scribes to kill them," Cyrus said.

"This one doesn't summon anyone," Vess said quietly. "That's rather the point."

But she went. She told herself it was duty, or curiosity, or the scholar's hunger that Vess had spent three weeks feeding in her — all the respectable reasons. The truth was simpler and she carried it down the cold corridors like a stone in her chest: she wanted to see the boy behind the crown again. She had seen him once, only once, on the night of her presentation at court — a flicker beneath Aldric's hollow eyes, quick as a fish under ice, gone before she could be sure. A boy who had looked at her as if he recognized something. As if he remembered a valley he should not have been able to remember at all.

The west gallery ran the length of the palace's oldest wing, hung with portraits of dead monarchs whose faces the restorers had long ago given up on. Time had eaten them evenly, kindly almost — except for the eyes. Someone, some century, had scratched out every pair of eyes.

She counted forty-one frames before the bells began.

They rang the third hour, and then — wrongness — they stopped mid-peal. Not faded. *Stopped*, the way a throat stops, and the silence afterward was so complete she could hear the dust settling on the portraits.

"You came alone."

Aldric stood at the far end of the gallery, before a window that showed nothing but fog. He was not wearing his crown.

Her breath caught. In three weeks of watching from galleries and processions and one disastrous court dinner, she had never seen him without it — not once, not even at table, where etiquette demanded bare heads. Without it he looked younger. Wronger. Like a portrait with the frame removed and nothing holding the edges together.

"Your Majesty." She bowed the way Isolde had taught her — shallow, watchful, hands loose.

"Do you know why I brought you here?" He turned. His eyes were the king's eyes, tired and kind and drowning. "I wanted to see the valley girl. I wanted—" His voice snagged, tore, resumed an octave lower, smooth as poured wax. "We wanted to thank you for your service."

The temperature of the room did not drop. It *tilted*. Cyrus felt it in her teeth, in the ember under her sternum that Isolde had spent weeks teaching her to bank — the spark flaring toward the thing in front of her like iron toward a lodestone.

"That's not him speaking," she said.

"No," agreed the thing wearing his mouth, with something like appreciation. "It isn't."

* * *

It did not move the way people move. Aldric's body stayed where it was, weight settled, hands loose at his sides — but the *attention* shifted, the way a lamp shifts when someone carries it through a room, and suddenly she felt lit from within, examined, catalogued.

"Scribe of the hollow valley," it said. "Do you know how long we have waited for your family? Four generations we have felt you across the border, a warmth behind a wall. Your grandmother hid you well. Your mother hid you better. And then you opened a folio you were not permitted to open, and the ink remembered your name, and here you are."

"The king," Cyrus said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "Where is he?"

"Sleeping. Dreaming. He does that more often now; there is less of him left to be awake." The mouth smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, which made it worse. "You have been asking our sister's questions. Reading our prophecy. Learning our unbinding." It pronounced each word carefully, a connoisseur tasting spoiled wine. "So let us speak plainly, scribe, because plainly is a language kings are rarely permitted."

It stepped closer. She did not step back. Some stubborn animal part of her understood that stepping back would be the first surrender.

"We know what you are owed."

The words landed like coins dropped into a well — she heard them fall and fall and could not find the bottom.

"Your ledger," the spirit said softly. "Yes, we have read it. We read everything written in this kingdom; it is all ours, eventually. Eleven thousand crowns against the Sandoval name. Three generations of copying scripture by tallow-light while the temple took its tithe and called it grace. Your mother, who sold her wedding ring to pay the interest. Your father, who died owing men who never spoke his name without spitting." It tilted Aldric's head, birdlike, gentle. "You dream of ledgers burning. We have watched you dream."

"How—"

"We are a crown. We sit six inches above the mind of everyone who enters this room. You would be astonished what leaks upward."

Cyrus's hands had gone cold. "What are you offering?"

"Release." The word came out of Aldric's mouth like a sigh, and for one disorienting moment it sounded like mercy. "One signature. One line in your beautiful hand: *the unbinding is refused, the binding stands.* Nothing more. And in exchange—" the spirit spread its arms, and the gallery's dead kings seemed to lean in around them, "—we will burn your ledger ourselves. Every debt, every interest, every mark against the Sandoval name, struck through in gold. Your mother freed. Your cousin freed. The temple's claim dissolved by royal decree before the next bell."

"And the price?"

"You already know the price. You have been pricing memories all week — we have felt you doing it, sorting them like beads." The voice softened further, went almost tender, and that tenderness was the cruelest thing in the room. "We ask only for the ones that hurt. The mother whose farewell was arithmetic. The father who died a debtor. The wardens' hands on your arms at dawn. The look on Vess's face when the archive burned. Keep everything sweet, scribe. Give us only the pain. It is a fair trade. It is the fairest trade anyone has ever offered you."

And the horror of it — the true horror, the one she would carry past everything that followed — was that for three full heartbeats, Cyrus wanted to say yes.

Three heartbeats. She stood in a gallery of blinded kings and felt the offer settle over her like a warm coat, and she thought: *my mother's face going flat and transactional, gone. The dawn arrest, gone. The debt, gone, and everyone I love freed besides.* It asked so little. It asked exactly what she had already been secretly deciding to give away for the unbinding — except the unbinding gave nothing back, and this gave everything, and no one would ever know she had chosen comfort over courage because there would be no *her* left who remembered choosing.

The ember under her sternum burned once, sharply, like a hand closing around hers.

She looked at Aldric's face — at the face above the face — and said, "Show me him."

"What?"

"The king. Show me Aldric. If you're so confident I'll take your trade, show me what I'd be abandoning." She made her voice a scribe's voice, flat and precise. "Or can't you? Is he too far under?"

Something moved behind the hollow eyes — irritation, or its ancient cousin. "You bargain boldly for a copyist."

"I count every stroke before I sign. That's what copyists do."

Silence stretched. Then Aldric shuddered, once, like a house settling, and the spirit — whether from pride or contempt or some boredom older than the kingdom — let go.

* * *

He surfaced gasping.

For a moment he was only a young man swaying in front of a fogged window, twenty-three years old and thin as a candle, gripping a marble plinth to stay upright. His eyes found her and they were wet and they were *his*, and Cyrus forgot every protocol Isolde had drilled into her and crossed the gallery and caught his arm.

"Don't—" Aldric's voice was rust and gravel. "Don't stand so close. It watches through me sometimes. Even now. Especially now." But his hand came up and closed over hers anyway, trembling, human, warm. "You're the scribe. The valley girl. You have ink on your — yes. Ink. Always ink." He laughed, a broken little sound. "I used to have ink on my hands too. Before."

"Before the crown."

"Before." His gaze drifted to the window, to the fog. "There was a summer — I was nine, maybe ten. My father sent me to the valley to be safe from court. Plague year. I lived with a miller's family near the eastern ridge and nobody knew who I was, and I—" his voice cracked wide open, "—I swam in the millpond. I ate bread with ash baked into it. There was a girl who taught me to skip stones. She had a gap in her teeth and she beat me at everything and she never once cared that I'd be king." He looked at Cyrus, and the recognition in his face was old grief, worn smooth as river glass. "You stand like her. Did you know that? You stand like someone who has decided the world will have to move first."

Cyrus's throat ached. "That was the spirit letting you remember. Bait."

"I know." He smiled, and it was terrible and true. "But it wasn't lying. That's the trap of it. Everything it shows me is real. It just keeps the real things hostage." His grip tightened on her hand. "Listen to me. Whatever it offers you — and it will offer beautifully — understand what it is. It isn't a parasite that eats memories. It's a collector. It *keeps* them. My mother's voice, my brother's laugh, the smell of that millpond — they're all still in there, in the dark, in the gold. It hoards us." His eyes were fierce now, the boy and the king surfacing together. "Unbind it. Burn the hoard if you must. Better scattered than caged. Promise me."

"Aldric—"

"It's coming back," he whispered. His whole body went rigid. "It's coming back, I can feel it climbing, please — whatever it says next, remember that I loved the valley, remember that someone in here chose you—"

His eyes went glassy mid-word, like a lamp snuffed.

* * *

When Aldric's face smoothed over, it did not become the spirit's face. That was the obscenity of it. It remained entirely, perfectly the king's — kind, weary, handsome — and the thing behind it simply looked out through the mask as through clean glass.

"You saw him," it said. "How generous of us. And now you will refuse us. We can see it assembling itself behind your eyes, scribe; refusal is such a young person's architecture. Very well. Let us not waste elegance on each other."

It released his arm from her grip with deliberate gentleness.

"But hear the shape of your future. You will run to the border with the princess and her knife. You will learn that the ink must be your blood. You will bleed a new ending into a book older than this kingdom, and you will pay in the coin you love most, and you may even succeed." The smile did not waver. "And we will be waiting in every shadow between here and the throne room, in every guard whose mind sits six inches below our gold, in every bell that rings between now and then. You have seen what we did to Vess's archive. That was a courtesy. A *warning shot*."

The temperature tilted again. The blinded portraits seemed to lean closer.

"We mark you now, Cyrus Sandoval, daughter of debt, vessel of the stolen ember. From this hour, everything in this kingdom that wears a blade knows your name. Sleep lightly. Trust slowly. And when you kneel at last over that folio with your wrist opened and your memories pouring out of you — know that we offered you kindness first. History should record that we offered."

Aldric blinked. Swayed. Looked down at his own hands as if he'd never seen them, and then at her, and his face collapsed into confusion and dawning horror — *what did I say, what did I do, how long was I gone* — and Cyrus backed away, because staying was more than her resolve could afford.

At the gallery door she stopped. She didn't turn around.

"For the record," she said to the room, to the thing in it, "the answer was always no. You just needed to hear it aloud."

Behind her, in a voice that was two voices braided into one, soft as dust falling:

"Yes. Now we both know where you sleep."

* * *

She walked back through the sleeping palace with the ember roaring under her ribs and her own pulse loud as siege drums, and she did not run until she reached Vess's door — and then she hammered on it until it opened, and pushed past him, and said the only words that mattered:

"It marked me. Get Isolde. We leave for the border tonight."
