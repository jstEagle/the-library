## 8. What We Bury to Survive

They rode through the night, and the kingdom let them go.

Cyrus did not trust that. She kept looking back at the road behind them — a pale ribbon unspooling through black pines — expecting at any moment to see torchlight, hounds, the possessed guards moving with that terrible synchronized grace. But the dark stayed empty, and the only sound was Isolde's horse breathing hard and her own heart keeping time like a metronome in a room where no one was listening.

By the third hour she stopped looking back and started looking inward instead, because something had changed there too. The mark the crown-spirit had pressed into her wrist — three small circles, like a brand made of shadow — ached with every mile they put between themselves and the capital. Not burning. *Counting.* She could feel it ticking down some ledger of its own, patient as interest.

"It knows where you are," Isolde said, without turning around. Her voice carried flat over the wind, stripped of everything but fact. "The mark is a leash. It won't pull you back. It will simply wait until you're close enough to matter."

"Then why does it hurt more the farther we ride?"

"Because it wants you to stop running." Isolde's silhouette shifted; Cyrus caught the gleam of her eyes over her shoulder. "Pain is the oldest argument there is. Don't listen to it."

They crossed the river at a ford no wider than a cart track, water dragging cold fingers at the horses' bellies, and on the far bank the land began to change. Cyrus felt it before she could name it — a thinning, a quieting, like a song sinking beneath the floorboards of a house. The air grew heavier. The sparks that had lived in her fingertips since the border crossing, those restless embers that woke whenever magic came near, dimmed to almost nothing.

"The valley," she said.

Isolde nodded once. "Your founders built a wall out of absence. No magic crosses it. Nothing goes in, nothing comes out." A pause, dry as old paper. "Except you."

They made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's hut within sight of the border stones — gray pillars furred with moss, standing in a crooked line across a bare hillside like the teeth of something long dead. Beyond them, the valley lay in darkness, and Cyrus found she could not look at it directly. It was not homesickness. It was worse: the feeling of being a letter returned to sender, unopened.

She built a small fire while Isolde saw to the horses, and when the princess finally sat down across from her, she did not take out maps or knives or any of the hundred practical things Cyrus expected. She took out nothing at all. She just looked into the flames for a long time, and Cyrus realized, with a strange lurch, that the woman was afraid.

"You said your exile was self-chosen," Cyrus said quietly. "In the capital. You said it like an accusation."

"I say most things like accusations."

"You also said the crown would use my mercy. That I'd get people killed for loving too easily." Cyrus fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "You never told me what *you* loved."

The silence stretched so long that Cyrus thought Isolde would simply refuse, the way she refused most things — elegantly, absolutely, like a door closing. But then the princess spoke, and her voice had none of its usual blade in it.

"I loved my brother. That's what no one tells you about exiles — we are not cast out. We walk out, carrying the ones we couldn't save." She turned her hands toward the fire, and Cyrus saw the old burn scars lacing her palms, silver-white in the firelight. "I was nineteen when I first understood what the crown was doing to him. I went to the court mages. I went to the priests. I stood up in the great hall during the Feast of Lanterns and accused the crown of sorcery in front of two hundred nobles, and do you know what happened?"

"They didn't believe you."

"They believed me." Isolde's mouth curved, and it was the cruelest expression Cyrus had ever seen her wear. "That was the problem. They believed me, and then they chose him anyway — chose the crowned thing wearing my brother — because a king who eats his own memories makes no demands. He signs whatever he's given. He remembers no grudges. He is the perfect ruler for people who want to be ruled." She flexed her scarred hands. "So I left. No one exiled me. I exiled myself, because staying meant watching, and watching meant becoming one of them — someone who sees the wound and calls it governance."

"And now?"

"Now I have spent eleven years learning how to kill a spirit bound in gold, when I should have spent them learning how to free it." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and terrible. "I wanted Aldric dead, Cyrus. I told you that on the first day and I have not lied to you since. Dead is simple. Dead is clean. Dead means I never have to look at my brother's face and wonder which parts of it are still his." She drew a breath. "But you bled for Vess. You refused the spirit's bargain when it offered you everything. And I have watched you choose mercy four times now when mercy was the stupid choice, and somehow you are still alive, so perhaps — *perhaps* — there is a fifth way, and it needs your kind of foolishness."

"That's the closest thing to kindness you've ever said to me."

"Don't grow accustomed to it." But Isolde reached into her coat, and what she brought out was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age. She unwound it slowly, ceremonially, the way one handles relics or wounds.

A knife. Small, plain-hilted, its blade dark as dried ink.

"This belonged to our grandmother," Isolde said. "Not mine and Aldric's — ours, yours and mine, if the marginalia in that manuscript told the truth. The Sandovals and the royal line come from the same root, cut apart when the valley was sealed. This knife was used to make the original binding. It has tasted prophecy before." She held it out across the fire. "It's yours. It always was."

Cyrus took it. The blade was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature — cold like a well, like deep water, like the space between stars. When her thumb brushed the flat, the ember in her chest stirred and answered, a low warm pulse, recognition passing between two old things.

"Why give me this now? What aren't you telling me?"

Isolde fed the fire another stick. When she spoke, she addressed the flames rather than Cyrus, as though the truth were easier to tell to something that couldn't flinch.

"Vess's translation was incomplete. My research wasn't. There is a final passage in the oldest recension — a colophon, scribes call it, the note at the end of a finished work." Her jaw tightened. "The binding cannot be broken from outside. No counter-spell, no knife, no army. The prophecy must be *rewritten* — the ending replaced, in the hand of the scribe who carries the ember. Blood for ink. The knife for the pen."

"All right," Cyrus said slowly. "Blood. I've bled before. I bled copying out penance psalms when I was nine."

"Not blood spilled. Blood *given*." Isolde finally looked at her, and there was no cruelty left in her face at all — only a bleak, unwilling tenderness. "The ink is memory, Cyrus. Every word you write in it costs you a memory you love. That is the price the founders set, so that no one would ever do this lightly. The spirit feeds on the king's remembered life. To unmake it, you must feed the page the same currency — willingly, knowingly, line by line. Whatever you write, you lose. Whatever you love most will be the first thing the ink takes, because the ink is honest and it knows the difference between what you cherish and what you merely keep."

The fire cracked. Somewhere beyond the border stones, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"How much?" Cyrus heard herself ask. Her voice sounded very far away, as though someone else were using it. "How much does the new ending cost?"

"A full ending. A true one." Isolde's voice dropped. "Vess thinks — and I agree — that to overwrite a century-old binding, the replacement must be written in the same weight. The original cost the founders their homeland, their names, their place in history. Your version will cost you—" she gestured, helplessly, at all of Cyrus— "*this*. Everything you carry that matters. Perhaps not all of it. Perhaps exactly all of it. No one has ever done this and come back to describe the arithmetic."

Cyrus looked down at the knife in her lap, and the mark on her wrist pulsed once, softly, like a reminder. *I am waiting,* it said, without words. *I can wait longer than you.*

* * *

She did not sleep. Instead, near dawn, she took out the folio — Vess's copy, wrapped against the rain, smelling of smoke and salvage — and read the prophecy through one last time as herself.

*And the scribe of the hollow valley shall come at the turning of the year, and the crown shall know its maker's hand...*

Her own handwriting stared back at her from the margins where she'd been annotating for weeks. She knew her letters the way she knew her breath. And it occurred to her, sitting in the gray light with the knife across her knees, that she had spent her whole life writing things down so they would not be lost — debts, psalms, inventories, the small stubborn records of a small obedient life. Now she would write the opposite. She would write a record designed to erase its author.

She tore a blank leaf from the back of the folio and laid it flat, and began, in pencil first, the way she drafted everything — making lists.

What she could afford to lose. The taste of temple bread. The sound of the bell at terce. The name of the warden who'd arrested her — she'd gladly surrender that one twice. The layout of the scriptorium. The color of the debt-ledger's cover.

Then the harder column. Her mother's face — already she flinched from it, already her hand shook. Vess's laugh, that startled bark that escaped him whenever he was delighted, which was often. The weight of the archive books in her arms. Isolde's voice saying *don't grow accustomed to it*. The valley itself, the green of it, the mute safe hush of it, home and prison both.

And underneath, at the bottom of the list, the entry she could not bring herself to write even in pencil: *the boy in the throne room's eyes, the flicker of him, the real Aldric reaching up through the dark like a drowning man.* Because if she lost that, would she still believe the freeing was worth it? Or would she wake tomorrow a stranger to her own sacrifice, holding a knife and a scar and no reason for either?

The pencil hovered. The mark on her wrist ticked. East of the border stones, the sky was going the color of old parchment, and somewhere behind them, in a gilded city full of borrowed faces, a king was forgetting one more thing he loved.

Cyrus picked up the ritual knife and set its point against her own forearm, not cutting yet — just resting there, cold and patient, asking its silent question.

"One more night," she whispered to it, or to herself, or to whoever was left to hear. "Let me choose what to bury."

And far away, in the dark of the capital, something wearing a crown smiled, and began, at last, to move.
