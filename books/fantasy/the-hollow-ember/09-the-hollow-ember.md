## 9. The Hollow Ember

The throne room doors opened without being touched.

Cyrus felt them part the way she felt everything now — through her skin, through the ember coiled behind her sternum like a second heart. The hall stretched out before her in columns of black marble veined with gold, and every torch along its length guttered at once, as if the room itself were inhaling.

At the far end, beneath a canopy of woven silver, King Aldric sat upon his throne. He was not alone. She could see that immediately, the way she had learned to see in these last desperate weeks: there was the boy-shaped man slumped in the seat, wrists thin over the arms of the chair, and there was the *other* thing draped over him like a mantle made of shadow and old hunger. It turned his head toward her slowly, the way a puppet turns when someone remembers to move it.

"Scribe," it said, in a voice like pages burning.

Behind her, Isolde drew the ritual knife from its wrappings. "Whatever happens," the princess said quietly, "do not stop writing. Not for me. Not for him. Not for anything you see."

"I know."

"You say that now." Isolde's mouth curved into something too sharp to be a smile. "Mercy always says that first."

Cyrus did not answer. She walked down the center of the hall with the folio open against her forearm, the way she had carried scripture across a thousand quiet mornings in the valley, and the familiarity of it steadied her more than any prayer could have. Her satchel held the knife-sharp stylus, the vials of prepared ink — though they both knew the true ink would come from somewhere no vial could reach.

The guards moved first.

They had been standing along the walls like statues, forty of them in the king's livery, and Cyrus had told herself they were merely men. Then their eyes rolled white, all forty pairs at once, and they came off the walls with the terrible smoothness of things that had never been men at all.

"Isolde—"

"*Write.*"

The princess met the first guard in the center of the floor, and the sound of steel on steel rang up into the vaulted dark like a bell being murdered. Cyrus forced her eyes down to the page.

The prophecy lay open before her, a century old, its final lines still waiting in the scholar's careful hand:

*And the scribe shall unmake what the founders bound, and the crown shall fall, and the price—*

The price. The price was the whole of it. Vess had explained it once, feverish and half-burned, gripping her wrist in the ruins of his archive: *Magic does not accept gifts, girl. It accepts payment. You want to rewrite fate? Fate will charge you by the memory.*

She pressed the stylus's edge to the pad of her thumb until blood welled, dark and glistening in the torchlight.

Then she began to write.

*The scribe shall unmake what the founders bound—*

The first word cost her the smell of the valley. It went out of her like breath leaving a body: wet stone and candle tallow and the green rot of the river in autumn. She gasped, pen faltering, and the world tilted — because some part of her had been leaning on that smell her entire life without knowing it, the way a child leans on a wall.

She kept writing.

*—and the crown shall fall—*

Her mother's face went next.

It did not fade; it was *taken*, ripped out with a pain like a hook behind her eyes, and Cyrus heard herself scream and did not recognize the voice. For one horrible instant she reached for the memory the way a drowning woman reaches for light — *Mama, the kitchen, the ledger, the cold goodnight kiss* — and found only a shape where a face should be, a warmth where a name should live. She knew she had loved someone. She could feel the love still, orphaned, howling in an empty room.

*Oh,* she thought, with the small clear part of her that was still a scribe, still counting costs in margins. *So this is what the debt was always going to be.*

She wrote on.

* * *

Isolde fought like a woman settling accounts.

Three guards down, her left arm hanging wrong, blood sheeting bright down her sword-arm, and still she laughed — a wild, ragged sound that echoed off the marble. "You see this?" she shouted at the throne, at the thing wearing her brother. "This is what your mercy buys! This girl is paying for your kingdom in pieces of herself, and you sit there and *watch*!"

The spirit answered through Aldric's mouth, and the voice was vast now, filling the hall, shaking dust from the rafters:

*She is paying for nothing. I offered her freedom. I offered her a clean ledger. They all choose the knife in the end — the founders chose it, the grandmother chose it, and this one chooses it too. They are so eager to bleed for other people. It is the most delicious thing about your kind.*

"Then starve," Isolde snarled, and cut down a fourth.

But there were too many. A blade caught her across the back and she went to one knee, and Cyrus saw it happen in the corner of her vision — saw the princess who had taught her binding-craft in a burned archive, who had given her a knife and her own shame, go down among the white-eyed dead-alive men.

Cyrus's hand shook. One line unfinished. One moment of mercy, and Isolde had warned her: *the crown will use your mercy, girl, it will find the soft place and push.*

*Do not stop writing. Not for me.*

She did not stop.

*—and the price of the unbinding shall be paid in full, by the hand that writes it, willingly, unto the last remembered thing—*

Vess's laugh left her then, and that was the worst one, worse than her mother's face, because it took the sound with it and left the silence shaped exactly like him. She could remember that he had laughed. She could remember that it had made her feel less alone in the world for the first time in her life. She could not remember how it sounded, and the absence sang.

*Goodbye,* she thought, toward whatever remained of him wherever memories go. *Thank you. I'm sorry I won't know why I'm thanking you.*

* * *

The spirit understood, then, what she was doing.

The hall went cold. The torches died to blue points. And the voice that filled the space was no longer wearing Aldric's throat — it was everywhere, inside her skull, inside the wet red lines of the folio, speaking in the tone of something ancient and suddenly afraid:

*Little ember. Little stolen flame. Do you know what you are? You are the piece we hid. The founders carved the magic out of the valley and folded it into a bloodline like a coin into a beggar's coat, and every generation has passed you down thinking yourselves poor. You are the treasure, child. You are the hoard. Stop this, and I will give you back every year they stole. Your mother's face. Your scholar's laugh. Your valley, whole and green. I will make you rich in remembering.*

For one heartbeat — one — Cyrus stood in the throne room of a dying king and wanted it so badly her knees nearly buckled.

Then she thought: *a blank page might let me begin again.* She didn't know yet that she would think it. But the self that chose love was already choosing, and it wrote:

*The ember returns to the world. The debt is ended. The crown is empty. Let the king wake.*

The spirit screamed.

It came out of Aldric like smoke from a doused fire, a column of shadow and hunger tearing free of the boy it had worn for years, and it lunged for her — for the folio, for the words still drying in her blood — and Cyrus raised the book between them the way Isolde had taught her, knife-hand flat against the cover, and spoke the binding-craft's oldest word:

"*Paid.*"

The shadow struck the page and came apart.

It unraveled into her, or she unraveled it — there was no telling which — a rush of cold and centuries pouring through the ember in her chest, and she gave it the only thing left worth taking: the valley itself. The border stones. The temple bells. The long gray afternoons copying scripture by candlelight, the whole small obedient life that had made her, gone in a single exhalation, and Cyrus Sandoval stood in a foreign throne room with no home in her memory at all.

The last line dried. The folio closed itself.

* * *

Silence.

Then a sound she almost didn't recognize: weeping. Raw, ugly, human weeping, and it was coming from the throne.

Aldric had collapsed forward over the arm of his chair, and he was crying like a boy, great shuddering sobs, his hands pressed to his face as if he could hold his own skull together. When Cyrus stumbled toward him he looked up, and his eyes were only eyes — brown, red-rimmed, terrified, *his*.

"It's quiet," he whispered. "It's — it's finally quiet. How long — how long has it been—" His gaze caught on her, on the blood on her hands, on the folio, and something in his face broke further. "What did it take from you?"

"I don't know," Cyrus said honestly. That was the truth of it, the whole truth: she had paid a fortune and could not itemize the loss. She knew she had loved people. She could feel the loves still, circling her like birds whose names she'd forgotten. "I don't know what I lost. That's the price. You don't get to keep the receipt."

Isolde dragged herself upright against a fallen guard, grey-faced, one eye swelling shut, and began — unbelievably — to laugh. "He lives," she said to the ceiling. "The wretched boy lives. I spent six years wanting him dead and the little scribe saves him anyway." She looked at Cyrus, and beneath the blood and the bruise her expression was almost gentle. "You're swaying, girl. Sit down before you—"

The floor tilted. The torches bloomed gold, then white, then nothing at all, and the last thing Cyrus Sandoval knew of the kingdom she had saved was the feeling of falling — and, faintly, impossibly, the smell of wet stone and candle tallow, rising up to meet her like a memory returning from somewhere she had never been.

Somewhere very far away, a temple bell was ringing.

She did not know why she was crying.
