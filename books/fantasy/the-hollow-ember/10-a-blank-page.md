## 10. A Blank Page

The first thing Cyrus knew was the smell of candlewax and vellum, and the knowledge that she had been crying.

She did not know why. The tears were already dry on her cheeks, salt-stiff in the creases of her face, as though she had wept for hours in a sleep that had taken something from her and left only the grief behind — grief with no name attached, like a letter whose seal had been broken and its contents removed.

She opened her eyes.

Stone ceiling. Familiar rafters. The little scriptorium cell above the temple archive, where the light came through one narrow window and fell across the desk in a bar of gold that moved, over the course of a day, like a slow hand turning pages. She knew this room. She knew it the way you know a room in a dream — intimately, and without knowing how you came to be there.

She sat up. Her body ached everywhere, a deep bone-tiredness, as if she had walked a very long way. Her hands were bandaged — both palms, wrapped in clean linen gone pink at the center. When she flexed her fingers, pain bloomed sharp and bright, and beneath the pain, something else: an echo, a memory of pain, as though the wounds remembered something she did not.

Her satchel lay on the desk beside a lit candle she did not remember lighting. She went to it because her hands went to it, before her mind could ask why, and found inside it the tools of her trade — pens, a knife for scraping, a stoppered inkpot — and one folio.

She did not recognize the folio.

It was old, its leather worn soft as cloth, its corners rubbed to bare board. Her fingers trembled on the clasp. *You know this,* said something small and certain in her chest. *You have held this before.* But when she opened it, the pages were blank. All of them. Dozens of leaves of good vellum, empty as snowfall, and on the last page — the very last — a single line of writing in a hand that made her breath catch, because it was her own:

*If you are reading this, begin again.*

She stared at it until the candle guttered.

*Begin again what?* she wanted to ask the page. *Begin from where? Who was I?*

But the valley around her answered nothing. Through the window she could see the terraced fields going gold in late-afternoon light, the temple bell tower, the smoke of evening fires beginning to rise — home, unmistakably home, and yet she stood inside it like a woman looking at a portrait of a stranger's family. She knew the names of things. She did not know their weight.

There was a ledger on the shelf above the desk. She took it down because scribes take down ledgers; it was reflex older than thought. It fell open to a page marked with her family name — *Sandoval* — and there, in the high priest's careful columned hand, a single line written across all the entries beneath it, striking them through:

*Debt forgiven by royal decree, in perpetuity. Paid in full.*

Paid in full. By a king. She read the line five times and felt nothing but a strange, hollow ringing, like a bell struck in an empty room. What king? What debt? What had been purchased with whatever "in full" meant?

Her palms throbbed.

She was still standing there, the ledger open, the folio in the crook of her arm, when she heard footsteps on the archive stairs — slow ones, uneven, the gait of someone favoring a healing wound — and then a knock at her open door.

* * *

The man in the doorway was gaunt and gray-stubbled, with ink stains permanent as birthmarks on his right hand and eyes that were red-rimmed, exhausted, and unbearably glad.

He looked at her the way people look at a sunrise they had stopped believing in.

"Hello," he said. His voice cracked on the second syllable and he cleared his throat and tried again. "Hello. My name is Vess."

"Vess," she repeated. The name landed somewhere deep and rang strangely, like a coin dropped into a well that never quite hit bottom. "I'm—"

"Cyrus," he said softly. "Cyrus Sandoval. I know."

"Do I know you?"

Something moved across his face — grief, quickly folded away, tucked into a pocket the way a scholar folds a damaged page to be mended later. "No," he said. "Not anymore. But I knew you. And I promised—" He stopped. Started over. "I promised that if you ever forgot, I would tell you the story from the beginning. May I come in? It's rather long."

She should have said no. Every instinct of a valley-raised scribe said *stranger, doorway, nightfall, no*. But her hands — her bandaged, aching, treacherous hands — had already stepped back from the threshold to let him pass.

He sat across the desk from her, in the student's chair, though he was clearly no student; he handled the chair like a man who had spent years being denied seats in better rooms. He set a bundle on the desk between them: another book, fat and battered, its spine repaired more than once.

"This is your story," he said. "Or the parts of it I could save. Some of it burned. Some of it—" his jaw tightened, "—some of it you spent. But I wrote down everything I could remember, and I remember nearly everything, which is either a blessing or the cruelest thing the gods ever did to me." He drew a breath. "Where would you like me to begin?"

"I don't understand any of this," Cyrus said. "I woke up and I don't— I know the valley, I know my letters, I know how to grind ink and rule a margin. But I don't know *me*. There's a hole where I should be."

"That's because you gave yourself away," Vess said simply. "Piece by piece. To save a king and a kingdom and, though she'd deny it under torture, a disgraced old scholar who didn't deserve it." His mouth quirked. "You paid with the only currency that mattered. Memories. The people you loved. The life you'd lived. You bled them onto the page until the prophecy was rewritten and the thing wearing Aldric's crown came apart like wet paper."

"Aldric," she said. The name echoed. Somewhere far below her conscious mind, something stirred — a flicker, a warmth, gone before she could turn toward it. "A king."

"The king. Younger now than he was, in every way that counts. Free." Vess's voice gentled. "He sent the decree forgiving your family's debt. He asked to come himself, but the council wouldn't allow it, so he wrote you a letter instead. It's in the book. So is Isolde's, though hers mostly says she told you so, and also that you're insufferable, and also — buried in the middle, where she thinks no one will notice — that she is glad, and that the knife is yours if you ever want it back."

"I don't know these people," Cyrus whispered. "Why does it hurt to hear about them?"

Vess looked at her for a long moment. In the candlelight his eyes were wet.

"Because forgetting isn't erasure," he said. "It's a room with the furniture covered in sheets. Everything's still there. You just can't see it. But you can feel the shape of it under the cloth." He laid his scarred hand flat on the battered book. "So. From the beginning, then. As promised."

And he began.

He told her about a valley where magic could not cross the border, and a girl who copied scripture by candlelight and tallied debts that were never hers. He told her about a forbidden folio and a date in century-old ink, and wardens at dawn, and a mother's farewell that was cold as a bill of sale. He watched Cyrus's face as he spoke, and whenever she flinched — and she flinched often, at things she could not remember and yet somehow recognized, like hearing a song you've never heard and knowing the next note — he slowed down, and softened, and went on.

He told her about the border of ash, and magic flooding back into her like water into a drought-cracked field, and how it had known her, sparked in her fingertips, called her by a name older than her own. He told her about a city of gilded rot, and a crown that ate its wearer, and a princess in exile with a knife and a grudge. He told her about marginalia in a grandmother's hand, and a stolen ember hidden in a bloodline, and archives burning, and blood on vellum.

He told her about the throne room. About guards with ancient light behind their eyes. About a princess holding a door against all of them, wounded, laughing, refusing to fall. About a girl kneeling at a lectern with a ritual knife and her own opened palms, writing a new ending while her memories left her one by one — a mother's face dissolving like sugar in rain, a laugh going out of the world, the valley itself fading to a sketch, an outline, a rumor.

"You kept one thing," Vess said quietly. "Do you want to know what it was?"

Cyrus could not speak. She nodded.

"The blank page," he said. "The last leaf of the folio. When the unbinding demanded everything, you argued with it — argued with a piece of living magic, mind you, haggled like a fishwife — and you won a single concession. One page, unwritten. Yours to fill however you chose." He tapped the folio still resting in the crook of her arm. "That one. You carried it out of the fire and across two kingdoms and back over the border, and you slept here in your old cell holding it like a child holds a blanket, and when they found you, your face was wet and you were smiling."

Cyrus looked down at the folio. At the last page. At her own handwriting, which she did not remember writing, saying *begin again*.

"Why?" she managed. "Why would I give up everything? I don't even— I don't know what I lost. How do you give up a whole life for strangers?"

"They weren't strangers," Vess said. "They were yours. That's the whole of it. That's the entire story, if you boil it down. A girl who had been given nothing her whole life was finally offered something worth having — people to love, work that mattered, a self that belonged to her — and when the price of keeping them was everyone else's freedom, she paid it without hesitating." His voice roughened. "I have studied prophecy my entire miserable life, Cyrus Sandoval. A hundred texts, a thousand interpretations. And the truest thing any of them ever said, you said yourself, bleeding, in a burning room. You said: *a scribe's hand, not a hero's. Heroes die for people. Scribes write them forward.*"

Outside, the last light was leaving the valley. The evening bell began to toll, and Cyrus found her lips moving with it, counting the strokes before she consciously decided to — her body remembering what her mind could not.

Vess rose, stiffly, and gathered his coat.

"It's late, and you've had a long day you don't remember," he said. "I'll be at the inn by the well — the widow Marla's. Come tomorrow, or don't. Come every day for a year, or never come at all. The story keeps. Stories are patient; it's people who aren't." At the door he paused, and looked back at her sitting in the candlelight with the blank folio in her lap, and his face did something complicated and tender. "For what it's worth — whoever you become next, I hope I get to know her too. The last one was the finest person I ever met. No pressure."

Then he was gone, and his uneven footsteps faded down the archive stairs, and Cyrus sat alone with the candle and the fog where her life had been.

* * *

She did not sleep.

She lit a fresh candle, ground ink she did not remember learning to grind, ruled a margin with hands that knew exactly what they were doing, and opened the folio to the blank page. The last page. The one she had fought magic itself to keep.

*If you are reading this, begin again.*

"All right," she whispered to her past self, to the ghost-girl who had paid so much. "From the beginning, then."

She dipped her pen.

And her hand moved — not hesitantly, not searching, but with the sure, flowing certainty of long practice, as if some part of her had been waiting in the dark of her forgotten mind for exactly this moment, pen in hand, page open, ready. The words came not from memory but from somewhere deeper than memory, from the place where the ember still glowed, and she wrote without knowing what she wrote until she read it back:

*I am Cyrus Sandoval, and this is the first page of everything I choose to keep.*

The candle flame leaned, though there was no wind. In the ink, just for a heartbeat, a faint warmth shimmered — gold, alive, familiar — and was gone.

Cyrus smiled at the page, at the fog, at the whole unwritten country of her future, and turned the sheet to keep going.
