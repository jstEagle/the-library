## 1. Charms for the Godless

The night market of Vell woke the way a cat wakes—slowly, one eye at a time, and then all at once with an air of having intended it all along.

August Thackeray had been setting up their stall in the Lantern Quarter for five years now, and they still loved this hour best: the moment when the lamplighters came down Copperlane Row with their long brass poles and touched flame to wick after wick until the whole street glowed like something spilled and golden. The smell of frying dough from Marda's cart. The clatter of dice cups from the gambling den two doors down, where the house always won but nobody ever seemed to mind. Somewhere above, a fiddler tuning strings in an open window, testing the same three notes over and over as if the fourth might be dangerous.

No magic in any of it. That was the point of Vell. That was the law, chiseled into the granite of every gatehouse in letters deep enough to read by touch: *No gods, no spells, no blood-price.* The city had outlawed the first thing and gotten the other two thrown in free, because that was how laws worked—they never stopped where you wanted them to.

August hung their charms on the pegboard one by one, arranging them just so. Luck-knots braided from river-reed and red thread, tight little diamonds of interlocked cord that were supposed to snag good fortune out of the air like flies in a web. Fever-charms: hollow clay beads packed with dried willowbark and meadowsweet, strung on leather cords, worn against the throat so the heat would "drain out through the neck." Wards-against-spite, which were honestly just knots with a clove tucked inside, but people believed in cloves the way they believed in luck, and belief did most of the work in this trade.

None of it was real. August had made very sure none of it was real.

They had spent five years learning exactly how much power lived in a knot tied by ordinary hands, which was none, and how much lived in a knot tied by hands like theirs, which had once been considerable—and they had learned to tie everything crooked. Deliberately, painstakingly crooked. A charm that worked too well drew questions. A charm that worked a little, or not at all, or only because willowbark actually *did* bring down a fever when you chewed it, drew nothing but coppers.

"Thackeray." Marda leaned across from her cart, flour to her elbows, squinting at the pegboard. "You've hung the fever-beads next to the spite-knots again. Old Tammer will have a fit. He says they quarrel."

"They're clay and string, Marda."

"He doesn't know that." She wiped her hands and lowered her voice, the way everyone in Vell lowered their voice for the next part, the ritual part, the litany of the godless. "Besides. You want the wrong person seeing charms hung too neat. You know what they say about the lower quarter lately."

"I know what everyone says." August straightened a knot they didn't need to straighten. "That prayers are getting answered. That the old shrines are warm to the touch. That a fisherman on the Salt Quay caught his drowned son alive in his nets after nine days." They kept their voice flat, bored, the voice of someone reciting nonsense they'd heard a hundred times. Inside, under the boredom, something older and colder turned over in its sleep and did not quite wake. "People say things. It's what people are for."

"Mm." Marda looked at them a moment longer than was comfortable. She had flour on her cheekbone and forty years of the market in her eyes, and she missed less than she let on. "Well. If anything strange happens tonight, you come stand by my cart. Strange keeps its distance from crowds."

*Strange,* thought August, *has been living in my chest for thirty years. It doesn't keep its distance from anything.*

But they smiled, and took the first coins of the evening, and the market closed over them like water.

* * *

The crowd came in waves, as it always did. First the early shoppers, practical folk buying fever-charms before they needed them the way sensible people buy umbrellas. Then the lovers, who bought luck-knots in pairs and tied them around each other's wrists with more ceremony than skill. Then the drunks, who bought whatever was closest and argued about the price afterward.

August sold to all of them with the same pleasant, forgettable patter. *Braid your wish into the knot, don't tell me what it is, a wish spoken aloud is half-spent.* It was a line they'd stolen from a hedge-witch in another life, in another country, before that country burned. It cost nothing and made people feel like the transaction went both ways.

It was nearly midnight when the child came.

She came alone, which was the first wrong thing. Six years old, maybe seven, barefoot on cobblestones that bit even through boots, wearing a man's shirt belted twice around her middle. Her hair had been cut raggedly short, recently, with something not meant for cutting hair. She stood at the edge of the stall's lamplight the way small animals stand at the edge of roads—decided, and terrified of the decision.

August had been a lot of things in thirty years, but they had never yet managed to stop noticing children standing at edges.

"You're open," the girl said. Not a question. A statement of fact delivered like a legal argument, as if August might try to claim otherwise.

"Barely. What do you need?"

"My brother's sick." She came closer then, all at once, the way children committed to things. "Fever. Three days. He shakes and then he burns and then he shakes. Mama's working the docks till dawn and I—" Her jaw set. Whatever she'd been about to admit—that she'd asked neighbors, that she'd been refused, that there was no one else—she swallowed it and said instead: "I have four coppers."

Four coppers was a quarter of what the fever-charms cost, and August knew without asking that those four coppers had been gathered coin by coin from somewhere a seven-year-old should not have been gathering coins at midnight.

"The fever-charms are four coppers tonight," August heard themselves say. Their own voice sounded far away. "Special price. Market's slow."

The girl dug the coins out of the knotted shirt-belt and counted them onto the counter twice, checking, and August picked out a fever-charm—the plainest one, willowbark and meadowsweet and clay, nothing in it but herbs and hope—and held it out.

And here is the thing August would lie awake turning over for weeks afterward, the thing they could not explain then and could not explain later: the knot holding the cord was tied straight.

Not crooked. Not the careful, deliberate, five-years-practiced crookedness that was as much a part of August's craft as the braiding itself. Straight. Tight. Even. Tied the way hands tied knots when hands remembered how, when some deep current underneath the person came up through the wrists and did the work while the mind stood aside whistling.

August stared at it. The girl, misunderstanding, snatched the charm and cradled it like a bird.

"It'll work?" she said. "Really work? Not just—" she wrinkled her nose, quoting some adult, "—'comfort.'"

"It's willowbark," August said. "Chew the beads if the fever climbs. It really works. It's the realest thing I sell."

Which was true, and safe, and exactly what an honest charm-seller in a godless city ought to say. But the girl wasn't listening anymore. She had pressed the charm between her small grimy hands, squeezed her eyes shut, and prayed.

August watched her lips move. No sound came out. Children's prayers were private; even the ones who'd been taught them out loud learned quickly, in Vell, to make them silent, because praying aloud in public could get a family a visit from the Wardens of the Quiet, and the Wardens did not comfort.

The girl's lips finished moving. She opened her eyes, nodded once, businesslike, and ran off into the dark between the stalls, bare feet slapping stone, gone before August could call after her—call what? *Come back, tell me your name, tell me who taught you to pray like that, tell me why the lamp just flickered.*

Because the lamp had flickered. August stood very still behind their stall and watched the little glass-flame lean hard to the left, though there was no wind, and straighten, and burn steady again, and burn—there was no other word for it—*attentive*. Like a dog that has heard its name in another room.

"No," August said quietly, to the lamp, to the knot, to whatever was listening. "No. We don't do that anymore."

The market roared on around them. Dice rattled. Dough sizzled. The fiddler upstairs finally found his fourth note.

August's hands, resting flat on the counter, would not stop trembling for the rest of the night.

* * *

They closed late and walked home the long way, which was habit rather than choice. Five years of habits, each one a small wall: the long way home, the crooked knots, the false name signed in the guild ledger, the accent flattened of every coastal vowel that might mark them as born within sound of the Sea of Glass. August Thackeray of Vell, charm-seller, unremarkable, harmless. The name they'd been born with belonged to a different person entirely—a person with lightning in their blood and a warrant with their description nailed to every gate from here to the coast, a person whose last storm had come down out of a clear sky and—

August stopped walking.

They were standing in front of the shrine.

It wasn't much of a shrine. That was the joke of it, the safety of it: it wasn't much of anything. A shallow alcove in the foundation wall of the old tannery, where rainwater collected and moss grew in the shape, if you squinted, of a woman kneeling. Some pious generation a century dead had carved a basin there and a name that erosion had eaten down to three letters: *V-S-A.* The Quiet Wardens knew about it and left it alone precisely because it was nothing—a damp hole in a wall that pigeons used. Faith needed grandeur to be dangerous. This had none.

Except that tonight, passing it at half past two in the morning, August could feel it from ten paces away.

Heat. Not metaphor. Actual warmth, radiating off wet stone in a city where the nights had turned cold enough for breath to show. August approached the way you approach a sleeping animal you know is not asleep, and looked into the alcove, and the breath went out of them slowly, like air from a punctured lung.

The basin was full of clear water. It had not rained in eleven days.

And floating on the water, arranged in a pattern too deliberate for driftwood, were cut flowers. White ones. August didn't know the variety—five years of studied ordinariness had pruned their knowledge of many things—but they were fresh, and their petals were dry, and the water beneath them steamed faintly in the cold air like a bath drawn for someone expected home.

Someone had prayed here. Recently. Someone had prayed here and the prayer had *landed*, had been received, had been answered with flowers and warm water in a forgotten alcove in a city that had spent a hundred years proving gods were stories.

August's hands rose without permission. Thirty years of muscle memory older than the exile, older than the false name, older than the vow—they rose the way they used to rise, palms out, fingers spread, the opening gesture of a calling, and the air above the basin *shivered*. Just barely. Just a heat-haze tremor where no fire burned. Somewhere very far away—or very deep—something enormous rolled over in its sleep, and August felt it the way you feel a ship change course while you sleep below decks: not seen, only known, suddenly and absolutely, that the direction of everything had changed.

They snatched their hands down and shoved them under their arms like a child hiding sweets.

"No," they said again, out loud this time, and their voice cracked on it. "I paid. Do you hear me? I *paid.*"

The alcove said nothing. The water steamed. The flowers floated, patient as an invitation.

August walked home fast after that, taking the long way again, doubling back twice, doing all the paranoid arithmetic of the hunted. And in their room above the chandler's shop, with the shutters barred and the single candle lit, they sat down at their workbench to do the one thing that always steadied them: tie knots. Crooked ones. Safe ones. Ordinary cord, ordinary thread, nothing in them but the tension of human hands.

The first knot came out straight.

So did the second. The third tied itself into a pattern August hadn't used since they were nineteen years old—a storm-lash hitch, forbidden in three kingdoms, useless for anything except calling wind down out of a clear sky—and August had to cut the cord apart with a knife to undo it, and their hands were shaking so badly they nicked a finger, and the bead of blood that welled up sparked.

Sparked. Blue-white, brief as a struck flint, gone before the eye could swear to it.

August sat in the dark with their bleeding finger cradled and their heart going like a drum at a hanging, and understood, finally, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the worst hour of the worst night, that the past was not knocking at the door.

The door was already open. It had been open for some time. Something had come in, quiet as weather, and was standing in the rooms of their life, touching everything, remembering them.

Outside, far off toward the river district, a church bell that had not rung in a hundred years rang once.

Just once. Then silence.

Then, thin and clear across the rooftops of the godless city, a child's voice—laughing, astonished, alive—crying out in the dark:

*"Mama! Mama, he's cold! His fever broke—he's asking for water!"*

August put their head in their hands.

In the basin of the forgotten shrine, twelve streets away, the white flowers began, very softly, to glow.
