## 3. Inquisitors at the Door

Sera had learned to measure the city by its silences.

Vell's lower quarter was never truly quiet—there were always the gulls arguing over the fish-market scraps, the creak of the tide-mills on the Weir, the endless argumentative music of a hundred households pressed shoulder to shoulder along the canal steps. But it had a particular silence for trouble, a held-breath hush that came before the doors closed and the shutters latched and the whole quarter pretended very hard to be empty.

She heard it now, three streets away, moving toward them like water finding the low places.

"Sera." August was already at the window of their rooms above the charm-stall, one hand parting the curtain no wider than two fingers. In five years she had never seen them move that fast without spilling something. "Get away from the glass."

"What is it?"

"Black cloaks. Six of them, maybe eight. Warrant-badges." August's voice was flat in the way Sera had learned meant fear wearing its work clothes. "Malvane's colors."

Sera crossed the room anyway, because she had spent nineteen years being told to stay back from windows and had never once managed it. Below, in the blue hour before full dark, the lantern-sellers were dousing their flames early. That was the tell. The night market never went dark—not for rain, not for funerals, not for anything short of plague. But tonight the stalls were folding like flowers at dusk, and the luck-knots and fever-charms hung abandoned on their racks, swaying in the harbor wind, little knots of colored thread turning slowly like small hanged things.

The inquisitors came up Candlemaker's Row in pairs, unhurried, the way men walk when they own the street they're walking on. Their cloaks drank the lamplight. At the corner where Old Marta sold her eel-pies, one of them stopped, consulted a slate, and pointed at her cart with two fingers. Two others broke off and surrounded it.

"They're checking prayers," Sera whispered. Her mouth had gone dry. "That's what the slate is. It's a resonance ledger—they can tell if a blessing's been answered within a certain radius. Mother Anselm told me about them. Before the convent fell, before—" She stopped. Before Malvane burned it, she did not say. Some sentences you kept in your chest like swallowed stones.

August hadn't moved from the window. In the last light their face was all planes and shadow, and Sera thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that she knew this person so well and so little at once. Five months since she'd collapsed half-dead on the shrine steps and been carried, delirious, to the only person in Vell who still left offerings out of habit rather than faith. Five months of shared bread and shared silence, of August teaching her which market guards took bribes and which took names, of learning to read the weather of their moods—and yet there were whole countries behind those eyes that August had posted signs around: *No Entry. Nothing Here But Weather.*

"August," she said carefully. "If they find the shrine—"

"They won't find the shrine." A pause. "They might find the charms."

"The charms are just thread."

"Yes," August said, and something in the way they said it made the hair rise on Sera's arms. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The knock, when it came, was polite. That was the worst part.

Four measured raps, patient as a physician. From the street below came the sounds of Old Marta protesting, then not protesting, then the wet scrape of her pie-cart being overturned into the canal. Someone wept in a doorway. The lower quarter held its breath and did nothing, because the lower quarter had learned what doing something cost.

August blew out the lamp, took Sera's wrist, and drew her into the back room where the charm-thread was stored—a closet of a place smelling of dye and salt and the tallow candles August refused to buy, insisting beeswax "behaved better." There was a trapdoor under the thread-racks. Of course there was. Sera had lived here five months and never known.

"Down," August breathed. "There's a passage to the catacombs. Don't light anything. Count your steps—if you reach forty without a turn, you've gone wrong."

"You're coming with me."

"I'm right behind you."

It was the first lie Sera had ever caught August telling, though she wouldn't understand it as one until later, and by then it would be too late to matter.

The trapdoor sighed open onto cold stone and the smell of old water. Sera lowered herself into the dark, hands finding rungs slick with moss, and had descended perhaps six steps when the door above opened and the polite voice said, pleasantly, "Charm-seller. How fortunate. We've questions about your merchandise."

And August Thackeray—who flinched at loud noises, who apologized to furniture, who once spent an entire evening coaxing a moth out the window rather than let it batter itself against the glass—said, in a voice Sera had never heard them use, calm as deep water: "Ask."

* * *

From below, sound traveled strangely. Sera crouched in the black with her ear against the underside of the floorboards, and every word came through muffled but intact, like voices heard through a wall of river.

"Your charms were found on the body of a child in Tanner's Lane." The inquisitor's voice was smooth, educated, the accent of the capital softened by years of giving orders. "Three days ago the child lay dying of lung-rot. This morning she stood up and asked for breakfast. Explain."

"I sell knots," August said. "Grief-knots, mostly. People tie them when they want to hold on to something. It's comfort, not cure."

"We opened one of your knots." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Inside the wrapping, beneath the thread, there are words. Old words. Pre-Vell words. Do you know what we call writing like that, charm-seller?"

Silence. Sera realized she was holding her breath and made herself breathe.

"We call it *prayer*," the inquisitor finished softly. "And prayer, in the Protectorate of Vell, is contraband."

"It's superstition," August said. "Thread and nonsense. If it cured anyone, I'd be rich instead of living over my own shop."

"Mm." Footsteps, unhurried, crossing the floor directly above Sera's head. Dust sifted down through the boards and settled on her lips; she tasted the upper room, the ordinary dust of an ordinary life. "You know what interests me? The child didn't just recover. When they asked her how, she said—" paper rustled— "*a lady made of rain stood at the foot of my bed and sang the rot out.* A lady made of rain. Now, I'm a simple man. I know three gods associated with rain, and all three of them are supposed to be dead. So either a dead goddess has taken up unlicensed medicine in the lower quarter—" the footsteps stopped, directly overhead, so close Sera could hear the man breathing— "or someone down here is channeling something they shouldn't have."

"I make thread knots," August repeated. "Whatever else happened, it wasn't me."

"No," the inquisitor agreed, almost gently. "I don't think it was you either. You're far too frightened, and the genuinely guilty are never frightened enough. But someone in this quarter prayed through your charms, and someone in this quarter has been leaving milk and honey on a drowned shrine by the Weir for the past five months, and I am paid to find connections between things. So we're going to search this room, top to bottom, and if I find so much as a dried petal out of place—"

What happened next, Sera would spend the rest of her life failing to describe properly.

Later she would say: *the light came up through the floor*. Later she would say: *it sounded like the sky clearing its throat*. Neither was true and both were true. What actually happened was this: somewhere below her, deeper than the catacombs, deeper than the bones of the city, something vast turned over in its sleep—and the floorboards above her head lit from beneath with a color that had no name, white shot through with green, the color of lightning seen through deep water—and every loose object in the room above rose an inch into the air and hung there, trembling, while outside the window the harbor itself seemed to stand up straight for the length of a heartbeat.

And through it all, threaded through the roar like a silver wire, Sera heard August's voice—no longer calm, no longer flat, but *ringing*, a voice with harmonics in it, a voice that belonged to storm-clouds and mountaintops:

"*Not this house. Not these people. Take nothing from this roof.*"

Then it was gone. The objects dropped. The lamps guttered and steadied. Somewhere outside, a dog began to bark, and then another, and the quarter exhaled all at once, a thousand doors creaking open on their hinges as people peered out to see whether the world had ended.

In the room above, the silence lasted four seconds. Sera counted them against her own pulse.

"Well," said the inquisitor at last, and his voice had changed entirely—all the pleasantness scoured out of it, replaced by something hungry and reverent and afraid. "Well, well. Sergeant—send the bird. Send it *now*. Code Ash-and-Lantern. Tell the Captain—" and here he laughed, a small disbelieving sound, "—tell the Captain Corvess that either he's the luckiest man in the Protectorate, or we've all just knocked on the door of the storm-caller Thackeray."

* * *

Sera did not remember climbing down the rest of the ladder. She remembered the cold of the catacomb water around her ankles, and she remembered running—barefoot, because shoes would echo—through passages that smelled of centuries, following the pale ghost-glow of August's hands.

Because August's hands were glowing. Faintly, unwillingly, the way a banked fire glows when the wind finds it: threads of that same impossible white-green light crawling between their knuckles, and August staring at their own fingers with an expression Sera had no shelf in her mind for. It wasn't guilt. It wasn't triumph. It was the look of a woman reading a letter from someone she had buried.

"You lied to me," Sera said. The words came out small and enormous at once.

August didn't stop walking. "Keep moving. They'll sweep the tunnels by morning."

"You *lied* to me. Five months. I told you things, August. I told you about the convent, about Mother Anselm, about the night the fires—I gave you everything I had left, and the whole time you were—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Do you know what they called you? In the convent, when I was a girl? *The Storm-Caller of the Broken Coast.* They used your name to frighten novices into obedience. *Pray quietly or the Thackeray will come.* Four hundred dead at Saltmere. They said you killed them with a word."

"I didn't kill them with a word." August's voice was hoarse. Still walking. The glow-light slid along the wet walls, making the catacombs swim. "It was considerably more than a word."

"That's not funny."

"No," August said. "It isn't."

They burst up out of the tunnel mouth into the reed-flats beyond the Weir, where the drowned shrine stood—Vessa's shrine, the little sunken alcove where Sera had left her offerings for five months, never guessing why the milk always seemed emptier in the morning, why the honey sometimes bore marks like fingertips. The sky above the harbor was enormous and starless, bruised purple, and far off across the water the beacon-towers of Vell's citadel were lighting one by one, wrong, out of order, a signal cascading.

August stopped at the shrine's edge. The glow in their hands had faded to embers, but Sera could still see it when she blinked—the afterimage burned into her vision, the shape of what August was.

"Say it, then," August said quietly. They weren't looking at her. They were looking at the water, at their own reflection, the way a person looks at a stranger. "You've earned it. Say whatever the convent taught you to say about me."

Sera opened her mouth. Closed it. Everything the convent had taught her was lined up behind her teeth like soldiers: *abomination, heretic, the reason the gods went silent, the reason Malvane rose promising a world scrubbed clean of the old blood.* Four hundred dead at Saltmere. She had built her whole life on the far side of that number.

But she had also built five months on the other side of it. On burnt bread and bad jokes. On August sitting up all night with her when the nightmares came, saying nothing, just *there*, the way no one else had ever managed to be. On the moth coaxed out the window.

"You protected Marta," Sera said finally. "You didn't have to. The flare—you could have stayed hidden, let them take the old woman's cart, let them tear the room apart. Nobody would have known. Instead you lit up like a second sunrise in a city where that's a death sentence, to keep them from hurting a neighbor." Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake. "I don't know what you are, August. I think maybe I never did. But I know what you *did*."

August turned. And in the dark, for one unguarded moment, Sera saw what lived underneath all the careful flatness—five years of running worn down to the quick, terror and exhaustion and something so rawly hopeful it hurt to look at, the expression of a drowning person who has just been told the shore might, *might*, take them back.

"The child in Tanner's Lane," August said. "The one who was healed. Did you pray for her?"

Sera frowned. "I—I light candles for everyone sick in the quarter. It's habit. Why—"

"Which charm did you use?"

"One of yours. A fever-charm. Blue thread." Sera's stomach dropped slowly, like a stone sinking in still water. "August. Why?"

August looked back at the drowned shrine, at the water where a goddess made of rain had once stood singing rot out of children, and their face did something complicated and grim.

"Because three days ago," they said, "I tied that knot. And when I tied it, I felt something answer. For the first time in five years, Sera, something *answered*—and I need to know if I called it, or if it's been calling *me*." They pulled the hood of their cloak up, and the last of the glow died in their hands. "Come on. By dawn, every inquisitor in the Protectorate will know my name. We need to be gone before they do."

"Gone where?"

August hesitated at the edge of the reeds, and Sera saw them glance—just once, quickly, helplessly—back toward the drowned shrine, as if the water had spoken a direction.

"There's a place," August said, "that I swore I'd never go back to. I think it's time I broke that oath too."

Behind them, across the black water, the citadel beacons burned their warning chain, and somewhere in the capital a bird was already flying with a message that would change everything: *the storm-caller lives.*
