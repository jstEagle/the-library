## 4. What the Goddess Left Behind

We ran through the lower quarter with the bells screaming behind us.

Not the market bells—those rang in rounds, patient and mercantile, marking the hours the way a good clock should. These were the alarm bells of Vell's garrison house, and they had a different voice entirely: high, ragged, urgent, like a child who has seen something terrible and cannot stop saying so. I had heard them twice in five years. Both times I had told myself it was nothing. This time I was running from them, so that lie was no longer available.

Sera kept pace beside me, her acolyte's gray robe hitched up around her knees, her bare feet slapping wet cobbles. She knew these streets better than I did—the shrine district had been her whole world for six years—and she pulled me left where my instincts said right, down a stair cut into the hillside, along a drainage channel where the smell of tanneries hung thick enough to chew.

"Not the river gate," she gasped. "They'll have the river gate."

"Then where?"

"The old chapel." She said it the way you'd say the name of a dead relative. "Vessa's chapel. The one they sealed."

I stopped so abruptly she nearly ran past me. "Sealed means watched."

"Sealed means *forgotten*," she said. "There's a difference, August. Inquisitors watch doors people might use. Nobody's prayed at that altar in thirty years. Nobody but me." Her chin came up, and there it was again—that stubborn, unreasonable faith that had gotten us both into this. "And nobody but Vessa is listening."

I wanted to argue. I was very good at arguing; five years of hiding teaches you to talk your way past every checkpoint of suspicion. But behind us, somewhere above the tannery row, a man shouted an order and boots answered in unison, and argument is a luxury paid for in time.

"Lead," I said.

* * *

The chapel of Vessa sat in a fold of the hillside like a coin dropped into a pocket and forgotten. You would never find it by looking. The approach was a collapsed goat path overgrown with bramble, and the door—if you could call it a door—was a slab of green stone set into the hillside, carved with a woman's face worn nearly smooth, so that she seemed less a goddess than a suggestion of one, a face remembered by rain.

Sera pressed both palms flat against the stone and whispered something too low for me to catch. For a long moment nothing happened, and I felt the old familiar relief begin to rise—I could say *see, dead is dead, let's find a cellar*—

Then the stone exhaled.

I don't know how else to describe it. The slab moved inward without grinding, without any sound at all, as if the hill had simply decided to open its mouth, and cold air rolled out smelling of wet earth and something else beneath it: ozone. Clean and sharp and electric. A smell I hadn't encountered in five years because I had made very sure never to make it myself.

It is the smell of a storm about to happen.

My hands started shaking. Not fear—or not only fear. It was the shaking of a body that recognizes something the mind has been ordered to forget. I stood on the threshold of a forgotten chapel and my skin remembered lightning the way a sailor's legs remember the deck.

"August?" Sera was already three steps down. "Come on."

Every sane part of me said *turn around*. Every sane part of me listed the costs in order: the raid above, the warrants being written, the gallows Malvane's magistrates kept polished for storm-callers specifically, the neat quiet life I had built out of luck-knots and fever-charms and careful smallness. Five years of smallness. Five years of being nobody, which is its own kind of prayer, and for five years no god had answered it.

I went down into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the chapel was smaller than its smell. A single vaulted chamber, maybe forty feet long, its walls furred with pale mineral bloom that caught what little light came through cracks in the ceiling and scattered it into a dim underwater glow. Rows of stone benches faced an altar that was not stone at all—it was a tree root, enormous and silver-gray, grown through the floor and ceiling and back again, dead wood preserved in some mineral embrace, shaped by centuries of patient water into the likeness of a woman with her arms open.

An empty garden bed ran along the eastern wall. Whatever had bloomed here once had been dust for decades.

"She was the growing god," Sera said quietly. Her voice changed in this place—lost its breathlessness, found a floor. "Before the war. Before the gods were put away. People brought their dead here and planted them, and Vessa turned grief into green things. That was the whole religion. Grief into green things." She touched the root-altar with two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "She wasn't a war god, August. She never wanted anything to do with storms or swords. So why do I keep hearing her say your name?"

"I don't know."

"You do know."

"I don't," I said, and it was almost true. What I knew was this: for five years I had starved my magic on purpose, fed it nothing, kept it buried under craft and routine until it was less a power than a scar. And for five years nothing had answered me—not my own buried storm, not any god, not the city's dead air. Then a child's fever broke through one of my charms, and a dying acolyte heard a drowned goddess speak my true name, and now I was standing in a sealed chapel that smelled like the inside of a thunderhead. Things were answering. They were answering *me*, and I had spent five years learning exactly why that was the most dangerous thing that could happen to anyone.

"Sera. When the goddess spoke to you. What exactly—" 

Light moved.

Not lamplight. It came from behind the altar, from a seam in the root-wood I hadn't seen, and it was white with a blue heart, the color of cloud-shadowed noon, and it pulsed once, twice, slow as breathing. Sera made a small sound and stepped back. I didn't step anywhere. My feet had become very interested in staying exactly where they were.

"It knows we're here," she whispered.

"It's a reliquary," I said. "Temples keep relics. It's probably just—"

"Just *what*, August? Finish the sentence."

I couldn't, because the seam was opening, and whatever was inside was brighter than sense allowed, and every hair on my arms was standing up, and underneath the terror there was that other thing, that awful homesick thing, the feeling of standing at an open window on the first warm night of spring. The seam parted along the grain of the dead wood, and the light poured out and took shape, and I understood that I had been lying to myself all the way down the stairs.

Because I knew this light. I had been born inside it.

A shard hung in the hollow of the altar—a splinter of something vast, no longer than my forearm, turning slowly. It looked like glass and moved like water and burned like nothing, giving light without heat, and when I looked directly at it I saw weather. Not a picture of weather. Weather itself, folded impossibly small: the spiral of a storm seen from above, winds circling a silent eye, and at the center of the eye a darkness that was not empty but *waiting*.

"That's not possible," I heard myself say. "That's not possible, that's—"

"The First Storm," Sera said. "Or a piece of it." She was crying without seeming to notice. "The oldest stories say the gods made it together, before the war, before everything. One storm that spoke every language, mortal and divine. They broke it apart when the war ended so no one could ever call it again." She turned to me, and her face in that blue-white light was young and ancient at once. "They missed a piece. Or someone hid one. Vessa hid one."

"Why would a growing god hide a weapon?"

"Maybe to keep it safe. Maybe—" Sera's voice cracked. "Maybe she knew someone would come looking someday, and she wanted it found by the right person first."

"No." I said it fast, before the light could finish whatever it was doing to the room, to the air, to the fine hairs on my skin. "No. Whatever you think this is, stop thinking it. I'm not the right person. There is no right person. That thing is a war, Sera. That thing is the reason Vell outlawed magic, the reason there are warrants with my description on them, the reason—" My voice did something humiliating. I steadied it. "I renounced. Do you understand? Five years ago I stood in front of witnesses and I gave it up. Swore it off, sealed it, buried it. There are papers. There's a *registry*. I am legally, officially, boringly powerless, and I have worked very hard to stay that way, because the last time I held power like that, people died. Children died. So whatever this shard wants—"

The shard flared.

The chamber went white. I felt it happen in my bones before my eyes finished closing—that deep sub-audible pressure, the feeling of a storm cell building ten miles off, the sky stacking itself into towers. And then a voice, and it was not a voice in my ears. It bypassed my ears entirely and happened somewhere behind my sternum, in the place where I had buried my own storm five years ago, and it used that buried thing like a door.

***Thackeray.***

My mother's name. My grandmother's name. The name of a line of storm-callers going back further than any registry, any city, any law. The name I had not spoken aloud since the trial.

***Child of the line. Come and be known.***

"I refuse," I said aloud, to a room, to a relic, to a dead goddess, to whatever was wearing her silence. "Do you hear me? I refuse. Find someone else. There's no one of the line left, I'm the last and I'm *finished*, I'm done, I bury my dead and I don't dig them up—"

***You were never asked whether to be born,*** the voice said, and there was something in it that was not cruelty and not kindness, only the terrible patience of weather. ***You are not asked now.***

The light struck me.

* * *

I have tried, in the weeks since, to describe the vision, and I have failed every time, because it was not like seeing. It was like remembering something that hadn't happened yet.

I stood in a field of ash under a sky the color of a bruise, and the First Storm hung above the horizon like a second horizon, turning, immense, alive. And it was not empty. It was full of faces—every caller who had ever raised it, every hand that had ever held the lightning, generations of Thackerays going back into the dark, and they were all looking at me, and none of them were angry. That was the worst part. I could have borne anger. They looked at me the way soldiers look at the last recruit mustered into a losing company: with pity, and apology, and relief that it isn't them.

*Heir,* they said, in a hundred voices that were one voice. *Last heir of the storm-blood.*

*I'm not,* I told them. *I'm a charm-seller. I sell luck-knots in a night market. I live above a dyer's shop. I pay my taxes.*

*You are the last,* they said, *and the storm does not care what you sell.*

And then the vision showed me the cost, because visions are honest in the way that gods are honest, which is to say completely and without mercy. It showed me the calling. Not the glory of it—the mechanics. To call the First Storm, a caller had to open themselves to it utterly, had to become the channel, and a channel is defined by what passes through it. Everything I was would pass through it. Every memory, every love, every name. The storm would take my history the way a fire takes a forest—to fuel itself—and what walked away from the calling would be a stranger wearing my body, standing in the ruins of my life, unable to remember why any of it had mattered.

I saw myself after. I saw a man with my face looking at Sera without knowing her.

I came back to myself on the chapel floor. Someone was holding my hand—Sera, kneeling over me, saying my name, my street name, *August, August, come back*—and the shard hung in its alcove, dimmed now, quiet, sated.

And on the back of my right hand, below the knuckles, a mark had appeared: a small spiral, blue-white, like a fingerprint of lightning seen from very far away. It did not hurt. That was somehow worse. It sat there with the calm permanence of a birthmark, of a brand, of a signature on a contract whose terms I had just read in full.

"No," I said. My voice came out scraped. "No, take it back. I refused. I said the words. You can't—you don't get to just—"

"August." Sera's grip tightened. "Your hand."

"I know about my hand!"

"It's spreading."

It was. Slowly, the way frost crosses a window, thin filaments of that same blue-white light were extending from the spiral, tracing lines along my fingers, up toward my wrist, mapping something. A circuit. A sigil. An inheritance, written on me in a script older than any law that had ever tried to forbid it.

Above us, muffled by thirty feet of hillside, the garrison bells were still ringing. Boots on stone, somewhere far off. Warrants being signed. And Dain Corvess—I didn't know his name yet, wouldn't learn it for another day—but someone, someone competent, someone who had hunted my kind before, was already riding toward Vell with orders, and every hour we stayed in this city the net drew tighter.

I lay on the floor of a dead goddess's chapel with a weapon older than the war branded into my hand, and I understood, finally, with total clarity, what inheritance actually is. It is not a gift. Gifts can be declined. It is a wound that was always yours, waiting to be opened, and the only choice you ever get is what you do while it bleeds.

Sera helped me sit up. She was still crying, but her jaw was set, and I realized she had already decided something while I was unconscious, and I was not going to like it.

"There's a way out of the city," she said. "Under the river. Old pilgrim tunnels. My mistress showed me once, before she died." She paused. "August... the vision. What did it show you?"

I looked at my marked hand. I thought about a stranger with my face, standing in ruins, loved by people he couldn't remember.

"It showed me the price," I said. "Get me out of this city, Sera. Before whoever they've sent finds us—because they will send someone, and when they do, I need to be gone. We both do."

She nodded and pulled me to my feet, and neither of us said the other thing, the thing we were both already thinking:

That the shard had chosen. And the shard did not care where we ran.
