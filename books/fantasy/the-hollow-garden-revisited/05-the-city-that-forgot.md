## 5. The City That Forgot

The catacombs beneath Vell smelled of drowned candles and old incense, and Sera hated every step of them.

Not because of the dark—she had spent six years in a convent where the only light came from votive flames and the goddess's silence. Not even because of the water, which rose past their ankles within a hundred paces of the entrance and kept rising, cold as a confession. She hated it because it worked. Because somewhere above them, Vell's night market was being torn open lantern by lantern, and the city that had outlawed magic was doing exactly what cities do when they are frightened: it was looking for someone to blame, and it had found August Thackeray.

"Keep your hand on the wall," August said ahead of her, voice low. "Left side. The right side has gaps."

"What's at the bottom of the gaps?"

"The sea, eventually."

Sera put her hand on the left wall.

They moved by feel and by the thin grey light of a charm-glass August carried cupped in one palm like a dying bird. It gave off barely enough glow to see each other's faces, and in that guttering half-light Sera kept studying them—the set of their jaw, the way their shoulders curved forward as if they could make themselves smaller than a person could be—and she kept trying to reconcile the exile she'd shared bread with for three weeks against the storm-caller the whole lower quarter was now whispering about. *Thackeray. The Thackeray. They found one of the faithful with a luck-knot that answered back, and the knot was warm, and the warmth was him.*

"You knew," she said finally. Her voice bounced strangely off wet stone. "In the shrine. When I told you Vessa spoke your name—you weren't surprised. You were *recognized*."

August didn't answer for a long time. The water sloshed. Somewhere far behind them, muffled through forty feet of earth and brick, a bell began to ring—not the market bell, not the tide bell, but the third bell, the one Vell rang only twice before, both times in Sera's lifetime, both times for fire.

"I knew what I was," August said at last. "I didn't know it still counted."

* * *

Three days earlier—or was it four? Time went strange underground—Sera had been kneeling in the ruined shrine of Vessa with her forehead against cold tile, doing the thing she had done every morning since the convent burned: praying to a goddess who did not answer, on the theory that faith which required answers wasn't faith at all but commerce.

She had heard the boots first. Then the torchlight, orange and wrong in a place that had known only candle-grey for a century.

"Inquisitor's warrant!" The voice filled the little shrine like smoke. "By authority of Lord Malvane, Regent of the Free Cities: any person harboring resurgent faithful, any person practicing unsanctioned devotion, is subject to detention and questioning. Come out. You will not be harmed if you come out."

Sera had pressed herself into the alcove behind Vessa's broken statue, heart hammering, and watched through the gap as grey-cloaked figures moved through the shrine with terrible efficiency—one checking the altar, one sweeping ash from the floor into a glass vial, one holding up a scrap of cloth she recognized, with a lurch, as her own sleeve, torn weeks ago on a nail.

And then the youngest of them, a boy barely older than she was, had stopped at the door and said, quietly, to his sergeant: "Captain Corvess is coming. He's bringing the warrants himself."

"Warrants for who?"

"The storm-caller." A pause. "It says here he's been in Vell five years. Selling charms." The boy laughed, a small disbelieving sound. "Five years under our noses."

Sera had not known then what a storm-caller was. She knew now. She knew it the way you know a word after it's been used to condemn someone you love.

* * *

The water reached their knees.

"Here." August stopped at a junction where three tunnels met, all of them black water and blacker stone. The charm-glass flickered. "We go left."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been down here before." August glanced back, and in the glass-light their face was all angles and exhaustion. "First year in Vell. I told myself I was learning the flood routes in case of fire. I told myself a lot of things that year."

Sera waded after them. "You were going to leave. Weren't you? Even before the raid. Before me. You were already packed."

August's silence was an answer.

"When?" she pressed. "When were you going to tell me what you are?"

"When we were far enough away that telling you couldn't hurt you." August's voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, like ice underfoot. "That was the plan, Sera. That was always the plan. Get you out of Malvane's reach and disappear again, and you'd hate me for vanishing instead of hating me for this."

"For what?"

"For what I did." August turned and looked at her fully for the first time since the shrine, and Sera saw that they had been waiting for this question the way a man waits for a wound to reopen. "You deserve to hear it from me and not from a warrant or a whisper. But not here. Not standing in the dark. Ask me when we're out."

"We're never out," Sera said. "Look around."

"Then ask me anyway."

* * *

She asked anyway. Later, on the long slope where the tunnel climbed toward the old tide-gates, with the water down to their shins and the air tasting faintly of salt and rot, she asked, and August told her.

Sera had expected a story shaped like the ones the convent taught—sin, consequence, penance. What she got instead was a story shaped like weather.

"There was a city called Bravene," August said. "Before Vell. Before the edicts. Back when the free cities still pretended magic was a neighbor rather than a disease. I was nineteen. I was already good—better than good; my mother's line ran back nine generations to the storm-bloods, and the power came to me early and came to me *loud*. And there was a fleet coming. Corsairs out of the Sable Reach, eleven ships, raiding the coast towns all summer. Bravene sent for help and the help that came was me, because I was cheap and I was fast and no one else would come."

The tunnel dripped. August walked and talked in a flat voice, the voice of someone reciting something memorized so thoroughly it had worn smooth.

"They anchored offshore to wait out a squall before the landing. Everyone in Bravene was on the walls watching, and I thought—I *thought*—I could end it before it started. One storm. One perfect storm, called down onto eleven ships at anchor, and no one dies on either side except the sea takes its toll and everyone goes home." August laughed, and it was the worst sound Sera had ever heard, because there was nothing in it. "I was nineteen. I believed storms could be aimed."

"What happened?"

"The squall was bigger than I read. Or I pulled harder than I should have. Both. The histories disagree and so do I." August's hand trailed along the wet wall, fingers leaving no mark. "The storm came down and it did take the ships. All eleven. And then it took the harbor. And then it took the seawall, and the seawall took the low quarter with it. Four hundred people, Sera. Fishermen. Children. People who had stood on those walls cheering me an hour before. I called lightning out of a clear sky over my own allies' heads and drowned a quarter of a city that had asked me to save it."

Sera stopped walking. The water swirled around her knees.

"And Vell—"

"Vell took in the survivors. Ten thousand refugees in a city built for six thousand. And the survivors told the story, and the story grew teeth, and within two years Vell passed the Edict of Quiet. No magic practiced, taught, sold, or sheltered. Anywhere in the city. On pain of the tide-pits." August finally looked at her. "I didn't write the law. But I'm the reason it has a name. Every quiet street in Vell, every charm-shop shuttered, every hedge-witch who fled inland—that's mine. I built the silence. I'm the most famous criminal in a city whose whole identity is pretending people like me don't exist."

"That's why you stayed," Sera whispered. "Of all the cities. You stayed in the one that banned you."

"Where else?" August said simply. "You don't get to run from a debt. You stay near it. You pay it in small installments. Luck-knots. Fever-charms. Five years of making sure nobody in the lower quarter went cold or sick or unlucky if I could help it, without ever letting them know why." A breath. "It's not penance. Penance implies an ending. It's just... installments."

Sera thought of the child in the night market—the cooper's daughter, burning with marsh-fever, whose mother had bought a fever-charm from a tired-looking stranger and gone home and found her daughter cool by morning. She thought of how the whole market had buzzed with it for days. *A prayer that answered back.* She had thought it was a miracle. Vessa stirring. The old gods waking.

Maybe it was. But maybe it was also just a debt being paid, one installment too many, until the payment itself had become indistinguishable from grace.

"August," she said carefully, "the fever-charm. Did you mean to—"

"No." Flat. Immediate. "I haven't called anything in five years. Whatever came through that charm, it wasn't me. It was—" August's hand went unconsciously to their chest, to the shard hidden under their shirt, the shard from Vessa's reliquary that had marked them like a brand. "It was *her*. Or it was the storm answering her. I don't know. That's what frightens me. I used to know exactly what I was capable of. Now I don't, and neither does anyone else, and Malvane's inquisitors are going to make everyone in Vell pay for my not-knowing."

* * *

The tide-gates were rusted half-open, and beyond them the tunnel mouth breathed cold sea air, and Sera allowed herself—for exactly four steps—to believe they had made it.

Then the light found them.

It came from outside, from the shingle beach below the cliffs where the catacombs emptied onto the grey morning shore: torches, dozens of them, and horses, and a wagon with a covered bed that Sera's stomach identified before her mind did, because convents have wagons like that too, for the dead. Grey cloaks. The sunburst-and-chain of Malvane's inquisition stitched in white at the shoulder.

And at their center, dismounting, a tall man in a captain's coat, dark-haired, maybe thirty-five, moving with the particular care of someone trying very hard to look unhurried. Even at this distance Sera could see he was not looking at the beach or the sea or his men. He was looking at the tunnel mouth. At them.

"He can't see us," August breathed. They had pulled back into the shadow of the gate, and Sera felt the temperature drop around them—not wind, not draft, something else, something that made the hairs on her arms rise and the water tremble in rings around her ankles. "The light's wrong. We're just shadow. We go back, we take the east fork, we're in the hills by dusk—"

"Sera." The voice carried across the water, across the rocks, impossibly clear, and it was the captain's voice, and it was saying her name. Not August's. Hers. "Sera of the Convent of the Drowned Bell. I have your file. I have your mother's address in Ostenmarch. I have a warrant that says *aiding and harboring*, and I am asking you—I am *asking* you—don't make me use it."

August went very still beside her. "You know him?"

"He knows *of* me," Sera whispered. "The convent reported runaways. He must have—" But even as she said it, she knew it wasn't true. The way he'd said her name. Not reading it. *Remembering* it.

"Dain Corvess," August said, and the name came out of them like a splinter. Of course. Of course the captain hunting them had a history with them; the world had been arranging that kind of coincidence since the shrine. "He was—he was at Bravene. After. He pulled bodies out of the water for three days. He was the one who testified." August's voice did something complicated. "He was also the one who argued the edict shouldn't be a death sentence. He lost that argument. He's been executing it ever since."

Below, Captain Corvess stood alone at the tideline, his men held back by some unspoken order, and shouted again, and this time his voice was not a captain's voice at all:

"Thackeray! I don't want the jar! Do you hear me? I will falsify whatever I have to—but if you run east, they'll send Kessrin's hounds next, and Kessrin doesn't falsify anything!"

Silence, except the sea.

August's hand found Sera's in the dark and gripped it, and Sera felt—not saw, *felt*—the storm wake under their skin, the old terrible inheritance stirring like a sleeper turning over, and she understood suddenly, completely, why Vell had outlawed magic, why the survivors of Bravene had needed the Edict of Quiet the way a burn victim needs clean bandages: not because power is evil, but because power is *weather*, and no one, no one, gets to decide whom the rain forgives.

"East fork," August whispered. "Now. Don't look back."

Sera looked back. Just once. The captain on the shore was watching the empty tunnel mouth, and he was not giving the order to pursue, and his face—in the torchlight, in the grey morning—was the face of a man grieving a city that had never actually been safe, only quiet, and mourning the last piece of his old life walking away into the dark with the woman he'd been ordered to bring back in a jar.

Then the dark took them, and the east fork swallowed the light, and somewhere behind them the third bell rang on and on over a city that had forgotten everything except its fear.

By dusk, Sera knew, the warrants would be nailed to every gate in Vell. By tomorrow, every road east would have eyes on it.

Which meant, she realized as the tunnel sloped upward toward the hills and freedom and the long road to Ostenmarch, that they would have to stop being fugitives and start being something worse:

A rumor. A storm-caller, a runaway acolyte, and a legend, moving through the free cities faster than any warrant could follow.

August squeezed her hand once, and said, "Stay close. It's going to rain."
