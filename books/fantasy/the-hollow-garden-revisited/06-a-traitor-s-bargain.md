## 6. A Traitor's Bargain

The order came at dawn, sealed in black wax that smelled of burnt hair.

Dain Corvess read it three times in the courtyard of the Inquisition's Vell garrison while the morning bells rang their flat, magicless note across the city. *By decree of Lord Protector Malvane: the fugitive known as the storm-caller is to be apprehended alive and delivered to the Bastion at Ostenmarch. Should capture prove impossible, recover the heart. A preservation jar accompanies this warrant.*

He looked up. A junior clerk stood at attention beside him, holding a small wooden case as if it were a sleeping snake.

"Alive," Dain said.

"Or the heart, Captain." The clerk's voice was carefully neutral. He was perhaps twenty. He had joined the Inquisition because it fed him, the way most of them had, and he had never yet had to think about what the work meant. Dain envied him with a violence that frightened him.

"Who signed this?"

"Undersecretary Vole. Countersigned by the Lord Protector's own hand." The clerk said it with something like awe. The Lord Protector did not countersign warrants for common heretics. He did not sign anything smaller than a war.

Dain took the jar out of the case. It was good glass, thick-walled, stoppered with waxed cork, sized precisely for what it was meant to hold. Someone in the Bastion's workshops had made it to specification. Someone had measured.

"Dismissed," he said, and the clerk fled gratefully into the grey morning.

Dain stood alone in the courtyard with the warrant in one hand and the jar in the other, and above him the bells of Vell rang on, toneless, law-abiding, safe. Five years ago those same bells had rung all night while the lower quarter burned.

*Alive,* the warrant said. Or the heart.

He put the jar in his saddlebag anyway. That was the first betrayal—not of August Thackeray, but of himself—and he didn't even notice it happening.

* * *

They gave him six men and a map with two red circles on it: the shrine district where the raid had gone wrong, and the flooded catacombs where the fugitives had presumably drowned, except that drowned bodies had not been found, which meant they hadn't drowned, which meant someone had failed, which meant Dain was cleaning up after a fool named Sergeant Hale who would be flogged for it by noon.

"Two fugitives," Hale reported, standing at rigid attention with a split lip and no shame at all. "The acolyte girl, Sera of the Convent of Small Prayers. And—" he hesitated, and Dain watched him decide whether to say it, "—a hedge-charmer from the night market. Older. Grey in the hair. We have a sketch."

Hale handed over the paper. Dain looked at it and felt the ground tilt very slightly beneath his boots, the way it always did when the past reached up through the floorboards.

The sketch was bad. It made the face gaunt where it had been sharp, old where it had been weathered, anonymous where it had once been the only face in any room. But the eyes were right—the sketch artist had gotten the eyes right, storm-grey with that permanent flinch around them, like a person perpetually braced against weather that had already passed.

Five years, and the Inquisition still couldn't draw August Thackeray properly. Dain wasn't sure whether that was incompetence or mercy.

"This is a night-market charmer," Dain said.

"Yes, Captain."

"And you believe this charmer is the storm-caller."

"The girl called them by name when we took her. She shouted it across the square. 'August, run.'" Hale shrugged. "We checked the registries. No August registered in Vell. Unregistered names are grounds for detention under the Resurgence Statutes."

"You detained an entire market square on the strength of a name."

"We detained four people, Captain. Three were released."

"And the fourth?"

"Escaped, sir. Through the catacombs. With the girl."

Dain folded the sketch along its crease, once, twice, until it was small enough to disappear into his glove. "You heard the name shouted," he said, "and your response was to check a registry."

Hale's jaw tightened. "The storm-caller is dead, Captain. Everyone knows it. Exile or execution, five years back. There hasn't been so much as a thunderstorm out of season since."

"No," Dain agreed quietly. "There hasn't."

But there had been a prayer answered through a fever-charm in the night market—that report had crossed his desk two weeks ago and he had buried it under routine filings, telling himself it was hedge-craft coincidence, telling himself a great many things—and now a woman who claimed to speak for a dead goddess had screamed a dead man's name across a Vell square, and the Lord Protector himself had countersigned a warrant.

Malvane believed. That was the thing Dain kept circling back to as he saddled his horse and checked his men's kit and gave the orders that sent them clattering out the north gate toward Ostenmarch road. Malvane, who had built an empire on the promise that magic was finished, that the gods were dead, that the world belonged to reason and rope—Malvane believed the storm-caller lived. You did not send a preservation jar after a rumor.

You sent it after a memory.

* * *

Dain rode out with his six men as far as the crossroads at Miller's Ford, and there he committed his second betrayal, which was also his first act of honesty in five years.

"Hale. Take the column on to the Ostenmarch fork. Work the river villages. I'm riding back to check the catacomb exits—if they surfaced anywhere, it'll be within a day's walk of the city, and they'll need food."

"Alone, Captain?"

"I've been doing this longer than you've been shaving. Go."

Hale went. Dain sat his horse at the crossroads for a long moment, watching the dust of the column settle, feeling the falsified orders sitting in his dispatch case like a stone: *Captain Corvess detached to investigate catacomb egress points. Remainder of squad proceeding per original directive.* Clean. Bureaucratic. The kind of lie that would hold for three days, maybe four, before someone in Vell asked why the captain hadn't reported in.

Three days. He turned his horse south, toward the drowned quarter, and understood that he had just chosen a side, and that he could not yet have said which one.

That was the worst part. Not the lying. The not knowing.

* * *

He found them at dusk, or rather he found where they had been—a culvert grate pried open with something thin and patient, mud footprints going east, two sets, one small and limping. He followed at a walk, letting the distance stretch, telling himself he was being careful, knowing he was being cowardly, doing both at the speed of a tired horse.

The purge came back to him the way it always did at dusk: not as a whole but as pieces, unbidden, in no order he could control.

The bell tower with the family inside it—he had ordered the door broken, and it was broken, and what came after was not his order but was done by his men in his name, and he had stood in the street below and listened, and written in his report that resistance was encountered and suppressed.

The acolyte, fourteen years old maybe, who had knelt in the ash of her convent and prayed aloud while they arrested her, praying not for rescue but for *them*, for the soldiers, and how Sergeant Brannic had laughed, and how Dain had not stopped the laugh, had not even frowned, had stood there with his face arranged into official blankness while a child prayed for the men dragging her away.

And August. Always, underneath everything, August—August on the steps of the Tribunal Hall in chains that weren't necessary, chains that were theater, looking out over the crowd with those storm-grey eyes and finding Dain in it, finding him instantly, the way you find the one lit window in a dark street, and saying nothing. Saying nothing at all. Not begging, not cursing, not naming him before the tribunal as the man who had drawn the maps of the lower quarter, who had marked which houses held the faithful, whose intelligence had made the purge efficient enough to earn a commendation.

August had gone into exile carrying every debt Dain owed, and had never once tried to collect.

*You could have damned me,* Dain had wanted to shout, afterward, drunk, alone. *It would have cost you nothing. One word. Why wouldn't you give me even that?*

Because that was the kind of person August was. That was the whole answer, and it had taken Dain five years and a warrant with a jar in it to understand that the answer was also a sentence: *there are people in the world who do not spend other people,* and August was one of them, and Dain was not, and the distance between those two facts was exactly the width of the gap between the man he was and the man he might have been, and it could not be crossed by regret. Regret was cheap. Regret was what cowards felt instead of acting.

His father used to say that. His father had been a butcher, an honest one, and had disowned Dain gently, almost kindly, the day he put on the black coat. *Regret is what cowards feel instead of acting.* He had never forgiven his father for being right.

* * *

He caught sight of them at full dark, on the far bank of the drainage canal, a fire no larger than a cupped hand—hedge-craft, careful, the fire of someone who knew how to make warmth without light. Two figures. One slight, curled up already asleep. One sitting upright, keeping watch, silhouetted against the tiny flame.

Dain dismounted forty yards out and walked the last of it on foot, boots silent in the canal mud, one hand nowhere near his sword, which was its own strange confession.

He stopped at the edge of the firelight and said, "Your wards are sloppy. I could have been anyone."

August moved faster than a person that age should move—up, turned, a knife out of somewhere, the small flame flaring green-white with a crackle that tasted of ozone and old violence. For one heartbeat the air itself leaned toward August like a dog hearing its name, and Dain felt his scars ache, all of them, the ones from the purge and the ones from before, and thought: *it's true, then. It's all true.*

Then August saw his face, and the lightning died in the air between them, and what replaced it was worse. Recognition. And grief. And something that might have been pity.

"Dain Corvess." August's voice was lower than he remembered, roughened by five years of disuse or five years of smoke. "They gave you my heart in a jar, I hear. Very tidy. Very Malvane."

"You know about the warrant."

"The goddess knows about the warrant. Apparently she knows everything now. It's exhausting." August lowered the knife but didn't sheathe it. Behind them, the girl—Sera—had woken and come up onto her knees, watching Dain with the flat, unblinking assessment of someone who had grown up in a convent and knew exactly what a black coat meant. "Say whatever you rode out here to say, Captain. Quickly. She needs sleep, and I'd rather not watch you do it."

"I falsified my report," Dain said. It came out clumsy, nothing like the speech he had rehearsed for thirty miles. "My men are on the Ostenmarch road. They're hunting river villages. You have three days, maybe four, before anyone checks my story. I know the army's route. I know where Malvane's supply trains are soft. I know—" his voice caught, and he hated it, and pushed through, "I know what I did. Five years ago. All of it. Every house I mapped. Every door I broke. And I am not asking you to forgive me, because I've met forgiveness and I don't qualify. I'm asking you to use me. I'm the best tool the Inquisition ever made, and I'm offering myself to the other side, badly, at night, in a ditch. That's the whole speech. That's everything I have."

The fire crackled. Somewhere down the canal a night bird called, absurdly domestic.

Sera spoke first. "He helped burn my convent," she said to August. Not accusing—reporting. Testing.

"He helped burn half the lower quarter," August said quietly. "I know. I was there."

"And?"

August looked at Dain for a long time. Those eyes, the sketch artist had gotten them right, and now Dain stood inside them and understood that he was being weighed—not against a standard, not against the purge, but against something simpler and more terrible: *is this useful, and can I live with it.* The arithmetic of survival. He had taught that arithmetic to a hundred interrogations. He had never been on this side of the table.

"You'll ride behind us," August said finally. "Always behind us. You don't touch the girl. You don't touch the shard." Something flickered across August's face at the word *shard*—pain, quickly mastered. "And when this ends, however it ends, you turn yourself in to whatever government is left standing and you tell them everything. Every house. Every door. You don't get absolution. You get a record."

"That's more than I hoped for."

"It's less than you deserve. It's what I can carry." August kicked dirt over the little fire, and the darkness came down gentle as a lid. "Sleep, Corvess. We ride at first light. And Dain—" the voice in the dark was almost too quiet to catch, "—if this is a trap, I want you to know that some part of me hopes it is. Because then I won't have to find out what you've become."

Dain stood in the dark with his heart going like a boy's and answered honestly, which was the third betrayal of the day, and the easiest:

"So do I."

* * *

They rode east before dawn, three riders and a secret, and behind them Vell's bells rang the hour in their flat, godless voices, and ahead of them the road ran toward Ostenmarch and the free cities that did not yet know they were already at war.

In his saddlebag, wrapped in cloth where no one could see it, Dain carried the warrant and the empty jar. He told himself he'd throw them away at the next river.

He didn't. Some habits of obedience go deeper than conscience, and a man choosing to betray his whole life does it badly, in installments, keeping receipts for the self he used to be.

But he rode east. That much, at least, he got right.
