## 7. Road of Small Mercies

The road out of Vell's drowned catacombs spat them into a world gone gold and green, all of it too bright after three days underground. August stood blinking in the morning light like something exhumed, and beside them Sera did the same—mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, the reliquary shard wrapped in oilcloth and strapped across her back because she had refused, with a stubbornness that would have been admirable under other circumstances, to let August carry it.

"Which way?" she asked.

August looked down the long scar of the Ostenmarch road, rutted by cart wheels, lined with waystones so old their inscriptions had worn to suggestions. "Away," they said. "Fast."

It was not much of a plan. It was, in fairness, more plan than they'd had in five years.

They walked. The first day they said almost nothing, because behind them Vell was still burning its incense of panic—watch-bells, criers, the distant drumbeat of boots—and ahead of them lay everything Malvane's warrants promised. August counted the waystones as they passed, an old habit from a life that had ended badly, and tried not to think about how many days it would take Dain Corvess to falsify his report and ride after them alone. Fewer than he thought. More than August wanted.

On the second day, Sera nearly killed herself.

She didn't mean to. That was the thing about hedge-magic that no one told you—it didn't care about meaning to. She had stopped at a stream to fill the waterskin, and August had turned away for perhaps ten heartbeats, checking the treeline out of habit older than sense, and when they looked back she was kneeling at the water's edge with both hands cupped around a wounded sparrow, whispering to it. And the whisper had *shape*. August felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm: a thinning in the air, a leaning, as if the world had tilted slightly toward the girl and her broken bird.

"Sera." They crossed the distance in four strides and closed their hands over hers, breaking the shape apart. The sparrow startled up out of her palms and vanished into the alders, winging crooked but alive. Sera stared at August with an expression caught between gratitude and fury.

"It was dying," she said.

"And now it isn't, and your nose isn't bleeding, and you're welcome." August let go of her hands. There was blood on her upper lip after all—a thin thread of it, already drying. "You were pulling from yourself. Do you understand what that means? You were paying with your own body. A little longer and you'd have paid with your heart."

"The sisters at the convent taught us prayer," Sera said, wiping her nose with the back of her wrist, leaving a rust-colored smear. "Prayer goes outward. You ask, and something answers. This—" she flexed her fingers, "—this is just me, talking to the world, and the world listening. It doesn't feel wrong, August. It feels like being *heard*."

"That's exactly what it feels like," August said quietly. "That's the trap."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, a slab of granite half-swallowed by moss, and August built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. Sera sat across from it with her knees drawn up, watching the flames with the particular intensity of someone assembling a question she wasn't sure she wanted answered.

"Teach me," she said finally.

"No."

"You just said I nearly killed myself doing it wrong."

"I did. And if I teach you, you'll do it less wrong, and then you'll do it more, and then one day an inquisitor will feel you doing it from three miles off and we'll both die on some anonymous hillside arguing about technique." August fed a twig to the fire. "No."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone of someone laying down a card she'd been holding since the shrine: "Vessa chose you. But she gave the shard to *me* to carry. Did you ever wonder why?"

August had wondered. They had wondered at two in the morning, staring at the ceiling of a smuggler's barn, and at noon, and at every moment the shard's weight shifted against Sera's spine. They wondered now, and hated it.

"She gave it to a girl who can't stop trying to heal things," August said. "That's not a recommendation. That's a warning label."

"Teach me properly, then, so I don't die carrying your goddess's luggage." Sera leaned forward, firelight catching the stubborn set of her jaw—the same jaw that had argued with dying acolytes' patience, with flooded tunnels, with August's every refusal since Vell. "Or refuse again, and I'll keep doing it my way, bleeding at streams, until I get it right or get it dead. Your choice. You seem to like having choices made *for* you, so here's one made against you."

For a long moment August only looked at her. Twenty years old, maybe twenty-one, convent-raised, running on faith and spite and whatever Vessa had poured into her in those last flickering minutes at the shrine. She reminded August of someone. That was the problem. She reminded August of themselves, thirty years ago, standing in a storm-swept temple telling an old woman that they could handle it, that power was just a tool, that they knew the cost.

They had known the cost. They had simply never believed they'd be the one to pay it.

"Fine," August said. "But we do it my way. Which means slowly, and small, and the first lesson is learning when *not* to."

Sera's whole face changed—relief and triumph and something softer underneath, quickly hidden. "When not to what?"

"When not to touch the world." August moved around the fire to sit beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and held out one hand, palm up. "Hedge-magic—the small craft, charms and knots and fever-work—isn't really magic at all. It's *asking*. You make a luck-knot, and you're asking the world to lean kindly on whoever wears it. Most of the time the world ignores you. That's good. Ignoring is safety. What you did at the stream was skip the asking and go straight to taking. You reached in and moved the current yourself."

"Is that so bad? It worked."

"So does a knife." August turned their hand over, showed her the palm's calluses, the fine white scarring along the thumb where a knot had once pulled tight enough to bite. "Every time you take instead of ask, you leave a mark. On the world, maybe. Mostly on you. The old hedge-wives called it *spending yourself*. You have a purse, Sera. It looks endless when you're young. It isn't."

She was quiet, turning her hand over to study her own palm as if she might see the ledger there. Then: "Show me the asking, then. Properly."

So August did. They found a smooth stone by the fire and taught her to hold it while thinking of nothing—not nothing exactly, but *low*, the mind settled like water left undisturbed. They taught her the first knot, the simplest one, a single loop of cord that meant *warmth* the way a word means warmth without being warm. Sera fumbled it six times. On the seventh try the loop came clean, and though neither of them felt anything dramatic happen—no thunder, no answering hum—August saw the slight easing in her shoulders, the way the cold seemed to give ground around her by a degree.

"That's it?" she asked, disappointed.

"That's everything," August said. "That's the whole religion of the small craft. You ask politely, and mostly nothing answers, and you go to bed grateful for the silence." They heard their own voice and recognized, too late, the bitterness braided through it. Sera heard it too. Of course she did. She had spent her life listening for gods in quiet places.

"What happened to you?" she asked. Not cruelly. Just directly, the way she did everything. "Before Vell. Before the exile. You talk about magic like it buried someone you loved."

August looked into the fire. The fire looked back, the way fires do, patient and indifferent.

"They'll be looking for us on this road," August said. "Dain Corvess knows me better than anyone alive. If I'm going to trust you at my back—and I am, apparently, because the goddess has a sense of humor—you should know what he'd be trusting you with." They drew a breath. It went all the way down and hurt on the way. "You should know why they call it the Hollowmere Massacre."

* * *

The fire burned low while August talked, and they fed it sticks whenever the words got hard, which was often, so that by the end there was a respectable pile of ash and a modest blaze and August's voice had gone rough as gravel.

"There was a town called Hollowmere," they began. "East of the free cities, in the hill country, back when the hill country still had towns. I was twenty-six. I'd been calling storms for eight years by then—not hedge-craft, real work, weather-shaping for farmers who paid in grain and gratitude. Rain where rain was needed. Hail turned aside. I was good at it. I was *proud* of it. Remember that part, because pride is the whole story.

"There was a drought that year. Bad one. Three seasons dry, wells going brackish, cattle dying in the fields. Hollowmere sent for me the way you'd send for a surgeon. And I came, and I looked at the sky, and the sky was empty—high pressure sitting over the hills like a lid, day after day, week after week. And I thought: I've broken harder skies than this."

Sera had gone very still. August made themselves continue.

"To break a sky like that you don't call a storm. You call *everything*. You reach down into the deep current—the big engine, the thing that turns all weather everywhere—and you pull. It's not a rope, it's not a lever. It's more like... waking something. And once it's awake, it doesn't want to go back to sleep, and it doesn't especially care what you meant." Their hands had started moving without permission, shaping the memory in the air. "I pulled. The storm came. It came *fast*—faster than any storm I'd ever raised, because the drought had charged the whole sky like a struck flint. And for about an hour, it was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing I have ever done. Rain fell on Hollowmere's fields and the people came out of their houses and stood in it with their arms open, and I stood on the temple roof and I thought, *this is what I'm for.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a nightbird called, once, and got no answer.

"Then the storm kept going," August said. "I told it to stop and it didn't stop. I *ordered* it—I used every binding I knew, every name, every knot—and it was like ordering a river. The deep current had come up through me and it didn't want to go back down. It wanted to spend itself. So it spent itself on Hollowmere. Hail the size of fists. Wind that took roofs off like lids off jars. Lightning that hit the granary, and the granary burned, and the burning jumped street to street because everything was wet and the wind was feeding it." Their voice did something strange on the next sentence, flattening out, going somewhere far away. "Forty-one people died. Nine of them children. I stood on the temple roof the whole time. I could have stopped. There was a way to stop it—I knew even then—but it meant letting the storm pass *through* me, letting it spend itself on my body instead of the town, and I was twenty-six years old and I did not want to die. So I didn't. I held on and I screamed orders at the sky and the sky ignored me, and when dawn came, Hollowmere was a black field with forty-one graves waiting to be dug."

Sera hadn't moved. Her face in the firelight was unreadable, which frightened August more than horror would have.

"They gave me a trial," August went on, because stopping now would be mercy and they didn't deserve mercy. "The storm-councils of the old line—they still existed then, barely. They stripped my rank. The free cities struck my name from every register. Vell took me in because Vell takes anyone who agrees to be nobody. Five years of selling luck-knots to people who will never know that the hands tying them once—" They stopped. Started again. "Once the sky did what I asked. That's the worst of it, Sera. Not that it disobeyed. That for one hour, it obeyed *perfectly*, and I loved it. I loved the power the way you love a person. And love like that doesn't check who else gets hurt."

Silence. The fire ate another stick. Sera looked at her hands, then at August, and when she spoke her voice was careful, feeling its way like a foot on river stones.

"The sisters taught us that power is a test," she said. "That the faithful are given strength to see what they'll do with it. And the ones who fail—" she hesitated. "We were taught to fear them. To report them. In the convent, if a novice showed unlicensed gift, there was a bell you rang, and the inquisitors came, and you were supposed to ring it. We practiced ringing it." She laughed, short and unhappy. "I used to lie awake wondering if I'd have the courage. And now I'm sitting at a fire with the most dangerous apostate in the free cities, and I helped pull him out of a tunnel, and I—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Forty-one people. Nine children. August, I *healed sparrows*. How am I supposed to—"

"How do you think I sleep?" August said. It came out harsher than intended, and they didn't take it back. "You don't. Or you do, badly, and you wake up and tie knots and sell charms and tell yourself the small mercies add up to something. They don't. They're just what's left. You become someone you have to apologize for, and the apology is the rest of your life, and no one ever accepts it, least of all you."

Sera lowered her hands. Her eyes were wet, but her gaze, when it met August's, had not wavered, and that was the moment August understood that the crack had gone through her faith and the faith had held anyway—which was somehow worse, and better, than either alternative.

"My convent burned," she said quietly. "Malvane's men burned it for sheltering relics. I watched Sister Anthea—she taught me my letters—stand in front of the reliquary and refuse to move, and they—" Her breath caught. She mastered it. "So I know what it is to survive something you should have been able to stop. I know the arithmetic of it. *If I had been stronger. If I had been braver. If I had been willing to die.*" She looked at August across the fire. "You weren't willing to die. And forty-one people paid for it. That's true. I believe you. I believe every word." She paused. "And you've spent five years paying, and now the goddess—whoever she is, whatever she wants—has put a weapon in your hands and called you heir. And you're terrified that if you pick it up, you'll love it again."

The words landed with the precision of a thrown knife. August sat very still.

"Yes," they admitted.

"Good," Sera said. "Be terrified. Sister Anthea used to say fear is the soul keeping its own ledger." She picked up the cord from her lap, the one with seven failed knots and one clean one, and began, deliberately, to tie an eighth. "Teach me the next one tomorrow. And the one after. Because if you're going to carry that shard to wherever it's going, you're not doing it alone, and I refuse to bleed at every stream between here and the end of the world."

August watched her fingers work the loop—clumsy, determined, alive—and felt something in their chest shift by a fraction, like ice remembering it could be water.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, moving fast through grey mist, and by midmorning the road had begun to fill with other travelers—carters, pilgrims, a tinker with a mule whose bells announced them a quarter mile in advance. Ordinary traffic. Except that August, watching the faces coming toward them, noticed that too many of the ordinary travelers were watching back. A carter who glanced twice. Two men in matching grey cloaks who walked past without speaking and then, fifty yards on, stopped to speak to each other.

"Bounty notices," August murmured. They angled their head down, tugged Sera off the crown of the road into the shadow of the hedgerow. "Ostenmarch posts them at the gates, but riders carry copies up and down the roads. Malvane's agents pay scribes to keep them fresh."

"Notices for what?"

"For a storm-caller traveling with a runaway acolyte." August risked one glance back. The grey-cloaked men had resumed walking—toward Ostenmarch, mercifully, not after them. Yet. "Description's probably wrong. They always are. 'Tall, gaunt, eyes like weather.' Very poetic. Useless." A beat. "Probably useless."

Sera's hand had found the shard's strap at her shoulder, an unconscious gesture August was beginning to recognize as her version of touching a talisman. "How far to Ostenmarch?"

"Three days, if the road stays kind." August looked north, where the hills folded down toward the river plain and the free city's smoke would be rising beyond the horizon, invisible and indifferent. "Three days to convince a council of merchants that a usurper is harvesting god-flesh and marching on them, using a face that's on every bounty board between here and the sea." They started walking again, pulling their hood lower. "I've been right before, Sera. Being right is how I killed Hollowmere. Let's hope the council likes my odds better this time."

Behind them, far to the south, thunder rolled—harmless, distant, ordinary weather.

Probably.

August walked faster.
