## 8. The Free Cities' Council

**AUGUST**

Ostenmarch smelled of salt and money.

August had been in cities that stank of both before—Vell had its fish-rot under the perfume of its merchant quarter—but Ostenmarch wore them openly, like a woman who has stopped apologizing for her age. The harbor district rang with the hammers of a hundred shipwrights. The terraced streets above climbed toward a citadel of white stone and green copper roofs, and at its heart stood the Council Hall, a dome so vast it seemed less built than grown, its windows stained in the colors of the six free cities: Vell's grey, Ostenmarch's blue, Corvane's red, and three others whose banners August had only ever seen on maps they'd once studied in another life, in another name.

They'd walked two days from the catacombs' mouth, sleeping in ditches, Sera's fever breaking on the second morning just as August had begun to fear the worst. Now she walked beside them through the crowd, hood up, clutching the wrapped shard beneath her cloak like a mother carrying an infant too precious to show. She kept glancing back at the road behind them.

"No one followed," August said for the fourth time.

"I know."

"You don't have to keep checking."

"I know that too." But she checked again anyway, and August let her. Fear was a thing you couldn't argue a person out of; you could only walk beside it until it got tired.

The Council Hall's public gallery opened at midday, and by then a queue of petitioners stretched down the marble steps—fisherfolk disputing net rights, a wool guild demanding tariff relief, a widow from the river towns holding up a dead child's shoe and weeping about sickness in the water. August watched the clerks move down the line with their ledgers and their stamps, sorting grief into categories, and felt something old and bitter rise in their throat.

"They'll hear us," Sera said. It wasn't quite a question.

"They'll *receive* us," August said. "There's a difference. I've petitioned councils before." They hadn't—not this body, not in this name—but the architecture of power was the same everywhere. You were heard exactly as long as you were useful, and no longer.

They took their place in line. The shard shifted against August's ribs when they moved, warm as a sleeping bird. In the five days since the reliquary, it had never gone cold. August had stopped trying to pretend that meant nothing.

An hour passed. Two. The sun crawled across the dome's oculus and laid a coin of light on the floor that crept toward them like something hunting. Sera recited prayers under her breath—the small ones, the household ones, the kind Vessa had supposedly loved—and August listened without meaning to, the way you listen to rain.

Then a clerk reached them. A young man with ink-stained fingers and the particular exhaustion of someone who has stamped four hundred petitions and expects to stamp four hundred more.

"Name and matter."

"Thackeray," August said, and watched the clerk's pen pause. Too late, they remembered the bounty notices, the descriptions, the way a name could be a door kicked open. They covered it fast. "*August* Thackeray. We come regarding the army of Malvane the Usurper, which crossed the Ashen Ford nine days ago and is marching west."

The clerk looked up. Around them, the queue went quiet in that particular way crowds do—not silence, but a leaning-in.

"Malvane is a southern matter," the clerk said. "The free cities are not party to his quarrels."

"His quarrel is with anyone who won't kneel. He burned three villages between the ford and the hills. He's harvesting—" August caught themselves, aware of the gallery, of the word *god-flesh* hanging in the air like a blade. "He's taking people. Whole villages. And he's coming here."

"Petition denied," the clerk said, already writing. "Next."

"We haven't even been *heard*—"

"Petition. Denied." The clerk's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about bureaucracy; it never needed to. "The Council does not act on rumor. If you have evidence—a survivor of standing, a magistrate's testimony—you may resubmit through proper channels. Next."

Sera stepped forward. "I'm an acolyte of the convent at Marrow's Bend. Our house was taken. I *saw*—"

"Faith testimony requires countersignature by a licensed keeper," the clerk said, and there it was again, that flatness, that wall. "There are no licensed keepers. There have been none since the Purges. Next."

August took Sera's arm and drew her back. Above them, in the tiered seats of the gallery, faces watched with the mild interest of people watching weather. A man near the rail leaned over to his companion and said, clearly enough to carry, "*Storm-caller*, wasn't it? Thought they hanged that one years ago."

Laughter, scattered and easy.

August stood very still and let the laughter wash past, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that they had made a mistake older than any mistake of tactics. They had come expecting a city to be afraid. But Ostenmarch wasn't afraid. Ostenmarch was *comfortable*, and comfort was the thickest armor ever forged. You could not cut through it with truth. You could barely dent it with corpses.

"Come on," they said quietly. "We're done here."

"But—"

"We're done, Sera."

* * *

**SERA**

Sera had believed in doors her whole life.

That was what faith was, really—that somewhere there was a door, and if you knocked rightly, with a clean heart and the right words, it would open. The convent had been a door. Prayer had been a knock. Even the goddess dying in her drowned shrine had been a door, terrible and half-collapsed, but a door nonetheless, and Sera had walked through it into a world where everything she'd trusted was burning.

She had thought the Council would be a door too. She had stood in that marble hall with the light falling through the colored glass like blessing, and she had thought: *these are reasonable people. Reasonable people listen.*

They had not listened. Worse—they had not even *refused* in a way that cost them anything. The refusal had been free. It had been paperwork.

Now she sat on the steps outside the Hall, in the long gold light of late afternoon, watching petitioners trickle out with their denials folded into their pockets like stones. August stood apart, arms crossed, looking at the harbor as though they could see the army in the masts and gulls.

"Say it," August said, without turning.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you're chewing on. You've been chewing since the clerk."

Sera pulled her knees up. "I keep thinking about the widow. The one with the shoe. She waited all day and they gave her a form."

"They gave us all forms."

"It's not right." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "People are going to die because a clerk didn't want to write it down."

"Yes." August turned then, and their face in the slanting light was hard and tired and, underneath the hardness, something that hurt to look at. "Welcome to how the world ends, Sera. Not with armies. With forms. With 'next.' With everyone assuming someone else will sound the alarm, right up until the alarm is standing in their streets."

"That's not fair. They can't just take every beggar's word for—"

"I know it's not fair. Fairness isn't a thing councils sell." August crouched, bringing their eyes level with hers, and for a moment the exile fell away and Sera saw the person underneath—the one who had taught her hedge-magic on the road with infinite patience, who had confessed a massacre with their hands shaking. "Listen to me. We have one more play. The harbormaster's office keeps copies of shipping manifests and border reports. Dain said Malvane's column would be visible from the coast watchtowers within the week. If we can get a witness—any witness with a name worth stamping—we force the hearing."

"Dain." Sera said the name carefully. The inquisitor who had knelt in the mud and offered them his shame like a bribe. He was supposed to meet them at the sign of the Salt Bell tonight. "You trust him?"

"I trust his hatred of Malvane. It's the most honest thing about him." August rose. "Go back to the inn. Rest. Eat something. I'll find you after dark."

"And where will you be?"

August hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a hairline crack, but Sera had learned to read them the way she'd once learned to read scripture—in the spaces between the words.

"August. Where will you be?"

"Making inquiries," they said, which was an answer the way a locked door was an answer, and walked away into the crowd.

* * *

**AUGUST**

The notice was nailed to the customs post at the harbor gate, and August found it because they had spent five years learning to see themselves everywhere.

It was good work, as such things went. The likeness was close enough—the sharp jaw, the storm-grey eyes, the hair worn too long for respectability. Beneath it, in the neat hand of a professional scribe:

*WANTED BY THE OFFICE OF THE USURPER'S TRUTH. AUGUST THACKERAY, STORM-CALLER OF THE HOLLOW GARDEN, FOR CRIMES AGAINST THE NATURAL ORDER AND THE MURDER OF ONE HUNDRED AND FORTY SOULS AT WICKFIELD. ALIVE FOR QUESTIONING. DEAD FOR REWARD. LAST SEEN FLEEING VELL IN COMPANY OF AN APOSTATE ACOLYTE.*

Below that, the reward figure. Below that, the seal of Malvane's Truth, the eye-and-flame that August had last seen branded into a neighbor's doorframe in Vell.

One hundred and forty souls. Wickfield. The numbers were wrong—it had been fewer, or more, depending on how you counted the ones who died later, of burns, of the sickness that followed the flooding—but the weight of them was exactly right. August stood in the harbor wind and read their own damnation in clean black letters, and felt the strange double vision that had haunted them since the reliquary: the guilt was theirs, and yet the name on the paper belonged to someone they had buried. Someone they had been trying very hard never to resurrect.

*Alive for questioning.* Which meant Malvane knew about the shard. Of course he did. The goddess had told them as much in the drowned dark: *he eats what gods leave behind, and you are carrying the largest meal left in the world.*

A shadow fell across the notice. A dockhand, broad-shouldered, squinting between the paper and August's face. Squinting. Comparing.

August moved before thought caught up—shouldered past him, head down, walking fast, then faster, cutting off the quay into the tannery lanes where the smell covered everything and the shadows were deep. Behind them, faintly, a shout. Not a cry of alarm yet. A question. *Wasn't that—?*

They climbed.

Rooftops were an old language and August still spoke it fluently; five years of hiding teaches you the grammar of eaves and drainpipes and the exact distance a body can cover in a running jump. They went up the tannery's rear stair and out across the low roofs of the dyers' quarter, and below them the city began to change key. The question became a shout became a bell—one of Ostenmarch's watch-bells, ringing the pattern August had hoped never to hear in a free city: *stranger in the walls, stranger in the walls.*

Not because of Malvane. Because of them.

From the ridge of a slate roof they looked back and saw them: watchmen with lanterns spilling out of the customs post, and behind the watchmen, civilians—dockhands, dyers, a fishwife still holding her knife—moving through the streets with the ugly purposeful energy of a crowd that has found a story to agree on. Someone pointed up. A stone arced past August's shoulder and shattered on the tiles.

*Right too early,* they thought, running. *That's the loneliest country in the world, and I'm its only citizen.*

Because that was the truth of it, the truth the council hall had shown them in stained glass: they *were* right. Malvane was coming. The villages were emptying. The god-flesh was being harvested and the First Storm's shard was warm against the ribs of the one person in the world the usurper feared, and none of it mattered, none of it could matter, because the messenger was a monster from a story mothers told, and monsters do not get to deliver warnings. Monsters get stones thrown at them.

Another bell joined the first. Then a third. The whole city waking to hunt one exhausted exile while the real wolf walked unhurried toward its gates.

August ran, and the shard burned against their chest like a second heart, and far to the south, past the harbor and the hills, thunder rolled in from a sky that had no clouds.

* * *

**SERA**

The Salt Bell was a tavern built into the bones of an old ship, keel-up, its ribs forming the rafters of a low smoky room. Sera sat in the darkest corner with a cup of small beer going warm and watched the door, and did not drink, and did not pray, because she had discovered in the last week that prayer had become a complicated act—like writing letters to someone whose address you no longer trusted.

Dain Corvess came in at dusk, disguised badly in a drover's coat, his inquisitor's bearing leaking through every seam. He dropped onto the bench across from her, scanned the room once with those graveyard eyes, and said, "Where's Thackeray?"

"Making inquiries." She repeated August's lie and watched him not believe it either. "Why? What's happened?"

"What's happened," Dain said, "is that a bounty notice went up at the customs post this afternoon. Fresh. Which means Malvane's agents are already in the city, which means the Council's denial wasn't ignorance, it was *purchase*. Somebody's been paid to keep the agenda clean." He turned his cup in a slow circle. "And it means your storm-caller is currently the most hunted person in Ostenmarch, because some dockhand with good eyes is telling everyone who'll buy a drink that the monster from Wickfield is walking around loose."

Sera's stomach dropped. "The bells—I heard bells—"

"Watch-bells. Crowd's out with lanterns." Dain drank, grimacing. "They won't kill them. Probably. Crowds like a trial. But the usurper's agents will use the chaos, and if they take Thackeray alive—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. *Alive for questioning* hung in the smoke between them like a body from a gibbet.

Sera was already standing. "Which way would they run?"

"Toward you, if they've any sense. You're the fixed point." Dain rose too, dropping coins on the table. "Roofs, probably. Thackeray moves like a cat that owes money. Head for the high streets near the dyers' quarter and look up."

They went out into the noise together—bells, shouting, the slam of shutters—and Sera ran through streets full of frightened light, lanterns swinging, faces turning toward her and away, everyone hunting and no one knowing what for. At the corner of Rope-walk she stopped, breathless, and looked up at the rooftops as Dain had said, and saw nothing but slate and chimney-smoke and the first stars.

Then, from the ridge of the tallest roof, a silhouette against the sky—coat whipping, balanced on the edge like a gargoyle deciding whether to fly.

"*August!*" she shouted, and the whole street turned to look where she pointed, and she understood her mistake half a heartbeat too late.

But August had seen her. And instead of running on—instead of vanishing into the maze of roofs where no crowd could follow—they turned along the ridgeline, straight toward her, dropping down drainpipe by drainpipe, because Sera was the fixed point too, and neither of them had ever learned how to leave the other behind.

They hit the ground three feet from her, breathing hard, face bloodied where a stone had grazed their cheek, eyes wild and bright with something that was not entirely fear.

"The council?" August gasped.

"They wouldn't listen. Dain says they've been bought."

"Of course they have." August laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "Of course. Why warn a city when you can sell it?"

Behind them, at the end of the street, torchlight rounded the corner. Ahead, the harbor gates were closing for the night watch. Between, a narrow lane climbing toward the old seawall, dark and empty and going nowhere anyone wanted to go.

Dain appeared at Sera's elbow, sword drawn, facing the torches. "Seawall," he said. "There's a smuggler's stair below the fort. It's a long drop and a cold swim, but it's out."

"Out where?" Sera asked.

August was looking south, past the walls, to where the cloudless thunder had rolled an hour ago—and now rolled again, closer, patient, like something that had all the time in the world and knew exactly where they were.

"Out," August said quietly, "to wherever the storm wants us. Because it's calling, Sera. And I think this time, we answer it—or it comes to collect."
