## 9. Where the Dead Gods Sleep

The shard woke before dawn.

August felt it through the leather of their pack, a pulse like a second heartbeat pressed against their spine, and lay still in the wet grass listening to it. Three weeks on the road from Vell and it had never done this. It had warmed, yes—when they crossed the old pilgrim road at Ostenmarch's edge, when Sera slept too near it and dreamed aloud in a language neither of them knew. But it had never *pulled*.

Now it pulled.

"Get up," August said to the dark shape beside the dead fire. "It's time."

Sera surfaced from sleep the way she always did, all at once, hands first, as if expecting the world to require catching. Her eyes found the pack. Found August's face. "You heard it too."

"It's been humming since midnight. Now it's singing." August shouldered the pack and tried not to let the weight of it show. The shard was no bigger than a fist, glass-dark, shot through with veins of silver that moved when you weren't looking directly at them. It had been silent stone in the reliquary beneath Vell. In August's keeping it had become something else entirely—a compass with an appetite. "Northeast. Toward the coast."

"The coast is drowned country." Sera stood, brushing grass from her acolyte's gray, which she still wore despite everything, despite Dain's warning that Malvane's men burned anyone in vestments on sight. "The maps show water past Greyhaven. Old water. The Sundering took it."

"I know what the maps show." August looked east, where the sky was going the color of an old bruise. "I also know that goddesses don't care about maps."

They walked.

* * *

The drowned country announced itself slowly, the way grief does. First the ground softened underfoot, then the trees changed—oaks giving way to black alder standing knee-deep in their own reflections—and then, around midday, the smell: salt and rot and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a bell.

Sera named it before August could. "A temple. There was a temple here."

"You can't know that."

"I can." She touched her collar, the frayed knot-work there, the last relic of her convent. "Every novice learns the litany of the lost shrines. The Temple of the Quiet Tide, dedicated to Vessa-of-the-Shallows. It went under during the Sundering. They say the goddess let it drown rather than let Malvane's predecessors loot it." She paused. "They say she's still down there. That fishermen hear singing on fog nights, and if you answer it, you don't come back."

"Charming faith you come from."

"It's not my faith." Sera said it quickly, too quickly, and then more quietly: "It wasn't. Before her. Before the shrine in Vell answered me back." She hugged her arms against the damp. "August. What if she wants something from us?"

"She already wants something. That's what gods do." August waded forward into the shallows, boots filling, the shard hammering now against their spine like a fist on a door. "The question is whether we can afford to pay."

The temple rose out of the water ahead of them—or rather, its bones did. Arches of pale stone broke the surface in a broken ring, furred with weed, gulls screaming off their crowns. Between the arches the water lay flat and green-black, so still it looked solid, and in its depths August could see the suggestion of steps descending, colonnades leaning, a whole architecture of worship folded into the deep like a letter no one had opened.

The shard went quiet.

That was worse than the singing. August stood chest-deep in the shallows—the water was warm, unaccountably warm, warm as breath—and felt the silence where the pull had been, and understood: *we have arrived; the rest is up to you.*

"There's air below," Sera said. She was pointing. Beneath the nearest arch, where the water met the stone, there was a seam of darkness that did not reflect the sky. "Look. The water bends around it. Like a door held open."

"Like a door held open," August repeated. "For five hundred years."

"For us, maybe." Sera's voice shook, but she stepped forward anyway, and August learned something about her then that they would carry a long way: she was afraid of nearly everything, and she walked toward it nearly every time. "She kept the door open for someone. Maybe it was always going to be you."

August wanted to say *don't put that on me*. Wanted to say it badly, loudly, the way they'd said everything badly and loudly since the shard marked them. Instead they filled their lungs and ducked beneath the arch, into the seam of dark, and the water closed over their head like a hand.

* * *

There was no drowning.

That was the first impossible thing. August went under expecting cold pressure, the burn of lungs, and instead found a corridor of air, dry and dim, winding downward through the temple's drowned heart. Water stood in walls on either side—vertical curtains of it, green-lit, fish drifting past like birds outside windows—and the passage between was floored with old mosaic, thousands of tiles forming waves, waves forming a woman, a woman dissolving into rain.

Sera came through behind him, gasping, soaked, alive. "It's her," she whispered. "The whole floor is her."

"Keep walking."

The corridor opened into the sanctum.

It had been a great hall once; now it was a cathedral of held water, the ceiling far overhead shimmering where the sea leaned in but could not enter. Candles burned along the walls—actual candles, wax and flame, though nothing here had tended them in centuries—and their light fell on an altar of white coral, and on the figure seated behind it.

She was small. That was the thing August had not expected. All the old icons showed Vessa vast, robed in tide, crowned in foam. This remnant was a woman-shaped absence, a silhouette drawn in salt and candlelight, translucent as breath on glass. Where her face should have been there was a slow shifting, like moonlight moving on water.

"*Storm-child,*" she said.

Her voice did not come through the ears. It arrived fully formed in the skull, in the voice of whoever you had loved best and lost—which for August was a cruelty, because it used their mother's voice, dead fifteen years, and they had to stand very still until the wave of it passed.

"Don't do that," August managed.

"Forgive me. I have so few voices left. I take the ones that fit." The remnant tilted her absent head. "You brought my heart home."

"This?" August lifted the shard from the pack, and it blazed in their palm, silver-veined, alive. "This is your *heart*?"

"A piece of it. The piece I cut away before I went to sleep, so that no one could wake me wrong." The candles guttered. "And now it has woken me anyway, because it found the one thing I never expected to exist again."

"Which is?"

"An heir." The word landed in the sanctum like a stone dropped down a well. "Storm-blood. The line of the First Callers. I thought it ended at the Sundering. I thought we had burned it out ourselves, in our shame." A pause, and the water-walls shivered. "But blood remembers. Even exiled blood. Even *renamed* blood."

August's mouth was dry. "I renounced it. Five years ago. There are papers. There's a registry in Vell—"

"Papers." For the first time, the goddess made a sound that might have been laughter, sad and distant, like surf heard through a wall. "Child, I watched you sell fever-charms in the night market. Do you know why they worked? Not the knots. Not the words. You poured storm into them without knowing, the way a spring pours water into a riverbed it has forgotten it dug."

Behind August, Sera made a small sound. August didn't turn. Couldn't.

"What do you want from me?" they asked. "Say it plainly. Gods always want something, and they always dress it up as destiny, and I am *done* being dressed."

The silence stretched long enough that the candles burned down a visible inch.

Then Vessa raised one translucent hand, and the water-walls became windows.

* * *

August saw a village at dusk. Smoke rising from chimneys, ordinary smoke. Then other smoke, blacker, and figures in white-and-gold moving through the streets with censers that did not smell of incense but of something meaty and wrong. They went door to door. At each door they prayed—and the prayers were *answered*. Light poured from the doorways. Villagers knelt weeping, healed of lameness, of fever, of years of pain, blessing the name of—

"Malvane," Sera breathed. "Those are his inquisitors. His new order. That's the miracle he performed at Corin's Ford—that's why the Councils started listening to him—"

"Watch," said the goddess.

The vision turned, and showed what the light was made of. Beneath the inquisitors' camp: cages. And in the cages, things that had been gods once—small gods, river-gods, hearth-gods, the humble divinities of wells and thresholds and first frosts—chained, hollowed, *drained*, their substance siphoned vessel by vessel into reliquaries shaped like censers. God-flesh, rendered down. Malvane was not performing miracles.

He was *spending* them. Spending the old gods like coin, buying himself heaven one village at a time.

"He means to finish," Vessa said. "He has the recipe now. Half-divine, he can walk through fire and raise the dead and make the harvests bend—but half is not enough for what he intends. He wants the First Storm. The primordial. The thunder that was old when the world was young. With that, he would not be a god among gods. He would be the only god. And he knows the shard exists, and he knows it will sing to its heir, and his hunters are already on the road."

"So we run farther. We go across the sea—"

"And he burns the free cities one by one until someone gives you up. You have seen what he does to villages that shelter the faithful. What do you imagine he does to cities that shelter *you*?"

August said nothing. The water-walls showed them more—Malvane's column on the march, ten thousand strong, banners of gold on white, and above it a sky that bent strangely, clouds circling the usurper's pavilion like moths.

"There is one weapon," Vessa said quietly. "One. The First Storm sleeps in the high places of the world, in the eye of the oldest tempest, and it can be called by storm-blood alone. Called, it will come. It will unmake Malvane's stolen divinity down to the last stolen drop. It will end this before it begins."

"Then call it yourself. You're a goddess. Call your own storm."

"I am a *remnant*, child. A candle-end. I could not call a summer shower." The translucent figure spread her hands. "Only you. Only the heir."

"Fine." August's heart was slamming. "What's the cost? There's always a cost. Name it."

And the goddess—who had worn their mother's voice, who had kept a door open five hundred years, who had waited in the dark and the salt for the one person alive who could save the world—did not flinch, and did not soften, and told the truth.

"The Storm does not distinguish between what it unmakes and what it saves. To wield it, you must stand in its eye and hold it, and it will pour through you like light through glass. It will take everything you are that is not necessary to the holding." A pause. "Your memories, storm-child. All of them. Everyone you have ever loved, everyone who has ever loved you—it will pass through you and out, and it will not come back. You will end the war and live, and you will not know why any of us kneel to you. You will not know *us*."

The candles burned. Somewhere far above, gulls cried over drowned stone.

"That's not a weapon," August said finally. Their voice sounded far away to them, like someone else speaking. "That's a grave with a job."

"Yes," said the goddess simply. "It is."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then hide deeper. Run farther. And in ten years, or twenty, the world belongs to Malvane, and the last free thing in it is a luck-knot seller who forgot how to be lucky." The remnant's light dimmed, gentled. "I will not pretend this is anything but horror, child. I have watched mortals make bargains with destiny for ten thousand years, and destiny always writes the contract in its own favor. I can only tell you the truth of the price. The choosing is yours. It was always going to be yours."

August looked at the shard in their palm—at the little storm sleeping in the glass, patient as winter—and thought of a night market in Vell, and a child's fever broken by knots they'd tied without thinking, and a girl in gray vestments who had followed them into flooded catacombs and out the other side and had never once asked them to be anything but what they were.

Everyone they loved. Everyone who had ever loved them.

Gone, in exchange for everyone else.

"Sera," they said, and their voice cracked on the name, and they hated themselves for the crack, because now she knew, now she could see the shape of the arithmetic in their face. "We need to talk."

But Sera was already shaking her head, already stepping forward with her fists clenched and her eyes bright and terrible, and August saw—with a horror that had nothing to do with the goddess or the storm—that she had understood the price completely, and was already, silently, beginning to calculate how she might pay it in August's place.

"No," August said. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"You'd do it for me," Sera said.

"That's not—"

"You'd do it for me, and we both know it."

Above them, five hundred feet of held ocean trembled against its invisible walls, and in the silence of the drowned temple, two people who loved each other began the argument that would decide the fate of the world.
