## 10. Sera's Choice

Sera had learned to count August's silences the way other people counted coins.

There was the silence of concentration, when they bent over a charm-knot and their fingers moved like water finding its level. There was the silence of grief, which came at dusk and sat with them by whatever fire they'd built, shoulders curved inward as if sheltering something small. And there was the new silence—the one that had arrived after the drowned temple, after Vessa's remnant had spoken the price aloud in a voice like rain on stone.

That silence was the worst one. It was the silence of someone doing arithmetic they didn't want anyone else to see.

*The storm can only be called by one willing to be unmade by it,* the goddess had said. *Unmade means unremembered. Every face. Every name. Every love. The caller walks out of the storm a stranger to everything that made them.*

And August Thackeray, who had already lost a city, a name, and five years of quiet, had simply nodded—nodded, as if paying with themselves were a debt they'd been expecting all along.

Sera lay awake in the loft of the waystation barn and listened to them not sleeping two rooms away. The shard of the First Storm sat wrapped in oilcloth in her satchel, and even through the layers she could feel it: a faint pressure against her thoughts, like a thumb resting on a bruise. It had been pressing harder every night since the temple. Or perhaps she was only listening harder.

She made her decision the way she'd once decided to run from the convent—not with courage, but with a kind of cold clarity that felt, in the moment, exactly like courage.

If the storm demanded memories, then let it take hers. She was twenty years old. She had a convent full of hymns she barely believed anymore, a mother whose face was already going soft at the edges in her mind, a childhood spent kneeling on cold stone. What did she have that August didn't? August had a whole life they'd built twice—once before the exile, once after—and people who would die for them, and hands that could make luck out of string. August had Dain Corvess riding somewhere behind them with a falsified report and a conscience he wore like an ill-fitting coat. August had a future worth remembering.

Sera had a debt. She'd been carrying it since the night market, since the prayer that answered back, since the dying goddess had pressed her hand against a stranger's door and said *this one, this is the heir.* She owed August everything, and debts were meant to be paid.

So she waited until their breathing finally slowed into something like sleep. Then she took the satchel, and the lantern, and went down into the frost-bitten orchard behind the waystation, where the apple trees stood black and bare against the stars like a congregation of the patient dead.

* * *

The shard came out of its wrappings slowly, reluctantly, the way a snake comes out of wintering.

It wasn't much to look at—a sliver of glass-dark storm no longer than her forearm, shot through with veins of white light that moved when she wasn't watching them directly. But the air around it changed. The frost on the grass began to breathe. Her breath came out in plumes that bent toward the shard instead of drifting upward, as if the little piece of the First Storm were inhaling the whole cold night.

Vessa's remnant had shown them how. Not with words—with images pressed directly into the bone: stand in open sky, hold the shard against your heart, speak your true name into it, and *ask*. That was all. Ask. The First Storm had been a god's weapon once, and gods, Sera knew better than most, answered prayers whether or not you understood what you were praying for.

Her hands shook. She told herself it was the cold.

"Vessa," she whispered, and the name fogged white in front of her. "Lady of the Hollow Garden. Keeper of small prayers. I'm the one who lit your candles when nobody else remembered you existed. I'm the one who found him for you."

The shard pulsed. Light crawled along its veins like lightning deciding where to strike.

"I know what you asked of him," she said. "I know what it costs. And I'm asking you to take it from me instead. He's the heir. He's the one who can end the war. But his memories—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and kept going. "His memories are the last good thing he has left. Take mine. Take all of them. I'll call your storm. I'll be unmade. Just—just leave him whole enough to grieve."

The shard went still.

For one long heartbeat the orchard was perfectly silent, and Sera thought, absurdly, *it's considering me.* Then the pressure against her thoughts became a fist.

The world tore open.

Not around her—*through* her. The shard drank her true name off her tongue like wine, and then it reached for everything else. She felt it happen: the smell of beeswax and wet wool that was the convent chapel, gone. Her mother humming while kneading bread, gone. Sister Anthea's voice saying *you ask too many questions, child*, gone, gone, gone—each memory lifted out of her like a splinter drawn from flesh, and each extraction left a hole that ached worse than any wound she'd ever taken.

She hadn't known there were so many. She hadn't known a person was *made* of them.

She was on her knees without remembering falling. Frost burned through her skirts. The shard blazed against her chest, white veins flaring, drinking, and somewhere very far away she heard thunder roll across a cloudless sky, and she thought, with the small clear part of her that was still herself: *oh. It's working. It's actually working. I'm sorry, August. I'm sorry I didn't say goodbye.*

Then hands closed on her wrists, wrenched the shard away, and threw it into the snow.

* * *

August's voice reached her before their face did—raw, cracked down the middle, shouting her name in a register she'd never heard from them, a sound like a door breaking.

"Sera! *Sera!* Look at me. Look at me right now."

She looked. August knelt in the frost in nothing but their shirt, hair wild, eyes enormous and terrified, and their hands were shaking as they gripped her face between them—her cheeks, her jaw, tilting her head back and forth as if checking her for cracks.

"What did you do." Not a question. A wound shaped like words. "What did you *do*, Sera?"

"The calling," she said. Her own voice sounded far away, thin as paper. "I asked it to take mine instead. It was working, August, it was—it was almost done—"

"It was almost done *killing you.*" They pulled her against their chest hard enough to hurt, one hand cradling the back of her head, and she felt their heart hammering through their ribs, fast and wrong. "Your lips were turning gray. You were—there was frost growing *out of your skin*, do you understand? The shard doesn't take payment gently from someone who isn't meant to carry it. Vessa said *heir*. She marked *me*. You tried to spend a coin that isn't yours and it nearly took the rest of you along with the memories."

"But it took some." She said it flatly, because it was true, and because she needed August to hear it. "Some of them are gone. I can feel the holes. My mother's face—I can't—I know I had a mother, I know I loved her, but her face is just—" Her breath hitched. "It's just a shape now. A shape where a face used to be."

August went very still.

When they spoke again, the fury had drained out of their voice and left something underneath that frightened her more: a terrible, exhausted gentleness.

"How many?"

"I don't know. Small things. Old things. Maybe more than small." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Maybe it was taking the cheap ones first. Maybe it was saving the expensive ones for dessert."

"Don't." August's arms tightened around her. "Don't joke about this. Please don't joke about this."

They stayed like that for a long time, kneeling in the ruined snow of the orchard, the dead apple trees standing witness around them. The shard lay twenty feet away in a drift, dark and dormant again, sulking like a banked fire. Eventually August retrieved it—wrapping it in three layers of oilcloth with hands that still trembled—and carried it back to her like something they both hated.

"You should be furious," Sera said.

"I am."

"You should be screaming at me."

"I was. Inside. For about ten years' worth." August sat down beside her against the trunk of the oldest tree, pulling their coat around her shoulders without asking, the way they always did, the way that had become so familiar she'd stopped noticing it until tonight, when she'd nearly lost the noticing forever. "But mostly I'm—" They stopped. Started again. "Do you know what I thought, when I saw you on the ground? I thought: *it's happening already. It's taking her and I'm too late.* I've watched this magic kill before, Sera. I know exactly what a body looks like when the storm decides it isn't finished with it. And yours looked—"

Their voice failed. In the five months she'd traveled with them, through raids and rooftops and drowned temples, Sera had never once heard August Thackeray's voice fail.

"So here's what happens now," they said, when they could speak. "You never touch the shard again. Not to move it, not to wrap it, not to look at it too long. If we're attacked, you run, and you let me handle whatever's chasing us, even if handling it means using the storm-blood, even if it means Malvane's hunters find us faster. Those are the terms. Non-negotiable."

"That's not fair—"

"No," August agreed quietly. "It isn't. Almost nothing about this is fair. Welcome to being the last people who remember the old gods; unfairness is the entire job description."

Sera hugged the coat tighter and stared at the frost. "You were going to do it anyway," she said. "The calling. You'd already decided. Before the temple, maybe. Before we ever left Ostenmarch. That silence you keep—that's not grief, is it. That's arithmetic."

August said nothing, which was an answer.

"When?" she pressed. "When did you decide to pay it? To let it take everyone? Me, Dain, the neighbors from the lower quarter, the child whose fever broke—everything?"

"The night my storm killed the ferry crowd." Their voice was level, which somehow made it worse. "Five years ago. I've been doing the arithmetic since before you were sworn in at your convent, Sera. The temple didn't tell me anything I didn't already owe. It just named the currency."

"And you weren't going to tell me."

"I was going to tell you eventually."

"*When?* The morning of? After? Were you going to walk into the storm and let me wake up next to a stranger?"

"Yes," August said simply. "Because that was the kindest version. Because if you argued with me beforehand, you'd try to stop me, or worse—you'd try to *replace* me, which you've just demonstrated you'll do without hesitation and without survival odds. Because I have watched people I love die for me once already, and I swore on the mass graves at Karst Ferry that it would never happen again." They turned to look at her, and in the starlight their face was stripped of everything—every deflection, every dry humor, every careful wall. What was left underneath was young and wrecked and honest. "So yes. I was going to lie to you. I've been lying to you since the temple. And I'm telling you now only because you forced my hand, and I want you to know that I'm sorry, and that I'd do it again, and that I hate myself for both of those things. That's the whole confession. That's everything in me."

Sera discovered she was crying. She hadn't noticed starting.

"This is what it's always going to be, isn't it," she said. "Us. Two people having the same argument forever. You trying to throw yourself on the pyre, me trying to drag you off it."

"Probably."

"And neither of us wins."

"Neither of us wins," August agreed. "Love's like that. It's an argument you keep losing, and you keep showing up to lose it anyway, because the alternative is—" They gestured at the empty orchard, the empty road, the empty world where the old gods used to live. "The alternative is winning alone."

A wind moved through the bare trees, and far away—so far it might have been imagination—thunder answered it.

Sera wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Then here's my counteroffer," she said. "You don't get to decide alone anymore. Whatever you choose, I choose too. If you call the storm, I'll be standing beside you when you do, and I'll be the first face you see when you come out the other side a stranger, and I will introduce myself to you as many times as it takes. And in exchange, you don't lie to me again. Not even kindly. Deal?"

August looked at her for a long moment. Something in their expression shifted—something settling, like a knot pulled tight.

"Deal," they said.

They walked back to the waystation together, shoulder to shoulder, leaving the shard buried in its snowdrift until morning. At the door, August paused with their hand on the latch.

"One more thing," they said, not looking at her. "The memory it took. Your mother's face. Sometimes—" Their jaw worked. "Sometimes the storm gives back change. Small mercies. If it ever offers you one, take it. Don't be proud the way I was proud. Pride is how I ended up owing a goddess my entire past."

Before Sera could answer, a light bloomed on the eastern horizon—far off, at the edge of the dark hills, where no farmhouse should be burning at this hour. Then another. Then a third, strung out in a line, moving south.

Moving toward Vell.

August's hand found hers in the dark, and squeezed once.

"That's not a patrol," they said. "That's a column. Malvane's not hunting us anymore."
