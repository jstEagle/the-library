## 11. The Inquisitor's Confession

Dain Corvess had been a soldier of the Inquisition long enough to know that the road always told you who was on it before you saw them.

Three days out of Vell, following the Ostenmarch way, the road told him about two travelers and a horse that limped. He read it in the mud at the ford—two sets of boots, one small, one broad, pressed deep where someone had stumbled—and in the hedge-row where a charm had been hung from a branch to ward off night-fever. Hedge-work. Sloppy, hurried, but alive in a way that made his teeth ache. Magic left a taste in the air like copper and rain, and Dain had spent eleven years learning to taste it.

He rode alone. That was the first lie he'd told himself—that it was strategy, that a single rider drew less notice than a warrant-party. The second lie was that he intended to bring them back at all.

His orders sat folded in his breast pocket, sealed with Malvane's black sun. *Bring the storm-caller alive. If alive is not possible, bring the heart.* He had read the words four times before leaving Vell and had not been able to stop hearing them since, spoken in the Registrar's dry voice: *the heart, Corvess, in a preservation jar, we'll provide the jar.*

They would provide the jar. That was what the world had become under the usurper. A place that provided jars.

The rain came at dusk, a thin cold drizzle off the western hills, and Dain pulled up beneath a leaning oak where an old way-shrine stood—or had stood. He knew this shrine. Every soldier who'd ridden the Ostenmarch way knew it. Travelers left bread there for Vessa-of-the-Roads, back when leaving bread wasn't heresy, and the little stone niche had always smelled of wildflowers no one could find growing anywhere nearby.

The shrine was rubble now. Not weathered—*broken*, methodically, the niche smashed inward, the flowers' memory scraped away with chisels. Someone had burned a fire against its base; the stones were black with soot. And nailed to the oak above it, half-dissolved by rain, hung a parchment bearing the black sun and a list of names.

He didn't need to read them. He knew them. He had written three of them himself, years ago, in a ledger in the Vell chapter-house, in his own careful clerk's hand. *Persons of resurgent faith, subject to inquiry.*

Dain sat his horse in the drizzle for a long time, looking at the broken shrine, and let himself remember something he had spent eleven years not remembering.

*Ashford Vale.*

His family's village. His mother's kitchen with the rosemary on the sill. His sister Maren, who had prayed to Vessa every morning of her life the way other people breathed, quietly, without thinking of it as faith—just habit, just love, just the small courtesy of talking to the world. When the purges came to the eastern provinces, Ashford Vale had been on no list. It was too small, too poor, too harmless.

Then Malvane needed a miracle.

Dain had been twenty-six, newly promoted, proud of his white-and-grey cloak. He remembered the dispatch that came through the chapter-house: *village of Ashford Vale preserved from plague by divine intervention; investigate source.* He remembered riding there with a full warrant-party and finding no plague at all—finding instead a village whole and laughing, and Maren running down the lane to hug him, and their mother saying, *the goddess heard us, Dain, she finally heard us,* and feeling the first cold thread of wrongness go through him because he had read the dispatch before the miracle happened.

The fire came three nights later. Not inquisitors—Malvane's own men, unmarked, quick and thorough. By dawn Ashford Vale was ash, and by noon the story was everywhere: *a village struck down by godless savages, then miraculously restored—the usurper's new order commands such power.* Except the village hadn't been restored. Only the story had. Forty-one dead, and their deaths walked the countryside wearing a god's face, gathering believers to Malvane like flies to honey.

Dain had investigated. That was the bitter joke of it—he had done his job *well*. He had traced the riders, found the unmarked company's paymaster, followed the coin to a quartermaster whose signature he recognized because he'd seen it on requisitions for the usurper's household guard. He had written it all down, sealed it, and carried it to his superior with his heart hammering like a young man's.

His superior had read it, looked at him over the top of the page, and said, "Corvess. Do you know what happens to men who find miracles inconvenient?"

And Dain—who had a mother's grave to think of now, and a sister's, and a career, and a coward's arithmetic running silently behind his eyes—had said, "No, sir."

"Neither do I," his superior said. "Let's keep it that way." And he had held out his hand for the report, and Dain had given it to him, and watched it burn in the brazier beside the desk, and gone back to his duties, and told himself for eleven years that there had been nothing else to do.

There had been nothing else to do. There had been nothing else to do. He had said it so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones, and he could carry them in his pocket without cutting himself.

Until tonight. Until a broken shrine in the rain and a list of names in his own handwriting, and the smooth words suddenly sharp again, cutting through everything.

He was still sitting there when he heard them on the road.

* * *

They came up out of the dark like ghosts arguing about directions. He heard the girl first—a low, urgent whisper, Sera's voice, he recognized it from the raid in Vell's lower quarter, though she wouldn't know his face from hers. Then August Thackeray's voice, rough with exhaustion: "We can't stop here. Corvess is behind us, and he doesn't ride slow."

"He's *behind* us?" A pause. "How do you know?"

"Because I know how he hunts. I taught him half of it."

Dain stepped his horse out of the oak's shadow, and the drizzle caught the grey of his cloak, and both of them froze—Sera with a knife already in her hand, which surprised him, and August with lightning gathering faintly around their fingers, which did not.

"Inquisitor," August said. The word came out flat as a blade laid on a table.

"Thackeray." Dain dismounted slowly, hands wide and empty, boots loud in the wet mud. "I'm alone."

"You're never alone. You people breed in each other's pockets."

"I burned my pockets." He kept his voice level, kept his hands where they could be seen. Eleven years of interrogations had taught him how a body reads another body, and right now August Thackeray's body was reading his for the draw, the shout, the signal to hidden crossbows. He couldn't blame them. He'd earned that reading a hundred times over. "No party. No warrants. No jar."

Sera's eyes flickered at that last word, and he understood she'd heard rumors of the jars. Good. Let her understand exactly what kind of man had been sent after them.

August's fingers stayed curled, storm-light leaking between them like breath in cold air. "Then why are you here?"

"I've been asking myself that for three days." Dain took one step closer, and the mud sucked at his boot, and he stopped. "I have a sealed order in my pocket with your name on it. Alive or your heart in a jar. I've had it since Vell. I've ridden past four places where I could have taken you—two of them you were asleep—and every morning I wake up telling myself tomorrow I'll do my duty."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I stopped at a broken shrine." He nodded back toward the oak. "You'll pass it, if you keep north. There's a list of names nailed to the tree. Three of them are mine—not my name, my *handwriting*. I wrote them into the inquiry ledger five years ago. Farmers. A midwife. A boy who sold charms at the market gate." His throat tightened around the next part, and he made himself say it anyway, because a confession delivered comfortably was no confession at all. "I helped build the machine that's hunting you, Thackeray. I wrote lists. I signed transport orders. I stood in rooms while people were questioned until they confessed to things they'd never done, because confessing was the only door out. And when I found proof that Malvane manufactured his first miracle—proof, in writing—I handed it to my superior and watched it burn, and went back to work."

The rain ticked softly on the oak leaves. Lightning still shimmered around August's hand, but dimmer now, uncertain, a held breath rather than a drawn weapon.

"What proof?" August asked quietly.

Dain closed his eyes. Here it was, then. The stone he'd swallowed eleven years ago, coming back up his throat with blood on it.

"Ashford Vale," he said. "Eastern provinces. My home village. My mother's, my sister's. Five years ago the usurper needed the faithful to believe the old gods had abandoned the people and only his new order could protect them—so he needed a village blessed, and then he needed it destroyed, and then he needed the destruction blamed on godless fanatics. Ashford Vale was all three. Blessed on paper, burned in fact, mourned in propaganda. Forty-one dead." He opened his eyes. "My sister prayed to Vessa every day of her life. She died in a fire set by the man who now claims the gods answer *him*."

Sera made a small sound. August didn't move at all, and Dain had been in enough rooms with dangerous people to know that stillness was worse than any flinch.

"I investigated," he went on. "Because I was good at investigating, and because some stupid part of me thought the truth was a thing the Inquisition wanted. I traced the burners to the usurper's own household guard. I wrote it down. I watched it burn in a brazier. And then—" his voice cracked, and he hated it, and he kept going, "—and then I spent five years being very good at my job, and telling myself there was nothing else to do. Until there was. Until you."

"Why tell us this?" August said. "What do you want?"

"I want—" Dain started, and stopped, because the true answer was ugly and he'd decided somewhere on the road that he was done with ugliness dressed up clean. "I want to be of use. That's all. I don't expect forgiveness. I don't expect you to turn your back on me, ever, and you shouldn't. I'm not asking to join whatever you're doing. I'm telling you Malvane's army marches on Vell within the month, and I know its route, its supply lines, its muster points, and the name of every captain in it, because I trained half of them. And I'm telling you that if you're going to do whatever it is you're going to do—" his gaze dropped, briefly, to the faint storm-light still breathing around August's knuckles, "—you should do it knowing what's coming, and I am the only man in Malvane's service willing to tell you."

Silence. The rain thickened, drumming on the oak, running down the broken shrine stones.

Sera spoke first, her voice thin and hard. "It could be a trap."

"It could," Dain agreed. "Test everything I give you. Assume I'm lying until the facts prove otherwise. That's more courtesy than I ever showed anyone in your position."

"That's not comforting," Sera said.

"No," Dain said. "Nothing I say will be comforting. I'm not a comforting man. I'm a man who helped break shrines and is standing in front of one trying to figure out how to be something else, and I don't have a word for what that is yet. Maybe you do. You're the faithful one."

Sera stared at him. Something moved behind her eyes—recognition, maybe, or pity, or the terrible kinship of people who had believed in institutions that lied to them. "Vessa teaches that shame is a seed," she said slowly. "Bury it shallow, water it honest, and it grows into purpose. Bury it deep and it rots into cruelty." She glanced at August. "He's burying it shallow. Finally."

"Generous," August murmured. But the lightning was gone from their hand now, and they were looking at Dain with an expression he couldn't parse—something between grief and appraisal, the look of someone weighing a bridge they weren't sure would hold.

"The route," August said at last. "Tell me."

Dain reached into his coat—slowly, telegraphing every motion—and drew out not the sealed order but a leather map-case, worn soft with use. He crouched in the mud, spread the map flat against the base of the ruined shrine, and weighted the corners with stones. Rain spattered the vellum immediately; he worked fast.

"Malvane musters at Karst Hold. Twenty thousand foot, six thousand horse, and the Godtouched companies—you've seen what those are, or you've heard. They march here." His finger traced the road. "Not straight to Vell. He swings west first, through the river valleys. Harvesting."

"Harvesting," Sera repeated, and the word came out of her like something broken.

"Villages with active shrines. Living faith. He takes the priests, the relics, the devout—alive. The Godtouched need... material." Dain's jaw worked. "I've seen the supply wagons. I signed manifests for them, God forgive me. Whatever he's building himself into, it's built out of people who prayed."

August had gone very still again. "When does he reach the valleys?"

"Nineteen days. Vell's walls inside the month." Dain looked up at them, kneeling in the mud at a dead goddess's broken altar, rain streaming down his face, and felt the strange lightness of a man who has finally set down something heavy even though it turns out to be on fire. "That's what I know. All of it. Take it, verify it, throw it away if it smells wrong. But take it."

August studied him for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Stand up, Corvess."

He stood.

"You knelt to a shrine," August said, "not to me. Keep it that way. I'm not a thing to kneel to, and neither is what's chasing me." Their eyes were tired and old and lit from underneath by something that frightened Dain more than any lightning had. "But you'll ride with us as far as the crossroads at least, because if Malvane's Godtouched catch you out here alone with that map, you'll die badly and uselessly, and I've seen enough useless dying."

Sera made a noise that might have been protest. August ignored it.

"And Corvess—" August turned, pulling their hood up against the rain. "Ashford Vale. Your sister. When this is done—if anything of this is ever *done*—I'll help you put her name somewhere it isn't a lie. That's not forgiveness. It's just carpentry. Somebody should build her a true marker."

Dain found, to his horror, that his eyes were burning, and that it wasn't the rain entirely. He bowed his head, once, and said nothing, because there was nothing in his mouth that wasn't either a sob or a lie.

They broke camp in the dark, three travelers and a limping horse moving north through the rain, past the shattered shrine, past the list of names dissolving off the nail. And it was Sera, walking beside him, who finally asked the question that had been waiting in Dain's chest all night:

"The Godtouched wagons. The manifests you signed. What exactly was in them?"

Dain looked north, toward the river valleys, toward the villages with their living shrines and their praying people, nineteen days ahead of the fire.

"Bones," he said. "Relics. And jars. Hundreds of jars." He paused. "One of them had my sister's name stenciled on the lid."

Nobody spoke after that. They walked faster.
