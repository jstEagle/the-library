## 12. Ash on the March

The column moved like a wound through the valley.

Dain Corvess had marched with armies before—Malvane's own, in the early years, when the banners still meant something and the men still believed they were restoring order rather than building a throne. He knew the rhythm of a host on the move: the creak of wagons, the low animal complaint of oxen, the way ten thousand boots turn earth into mud and mud into memory. He knew what an army looked like from a ridge at dusk.

He had never seen one that looked like this.

They lay flat among the gorse on the southern shoulder of Harrow Fell, the three of them, close enough to share breath. Below, Malvane's column stretched along the river road for the better part of a league—pikemen in black-lacquered scale, supply carts under oilcloth, and behind them, at the center of everything, the palanquin. It was carried by twelve men who did not sweat, though the day was warm, and it was shrouded in silk the color of drowned things. Even at this distance Dain could feel it. A pressure behind the eyes. A wrongness in the teeth.

"He's stopped again," Sera whispered.

He had. Third village in two days. The column halted at the edge of a settlement—this one called Merrow's Ford, if the crooked sign at the crossroads told the truth—and the soldiers did what soldiers do: nothing. They simply waited, weapons sheathed, while four figures walked ahead of them into the village. Four figures in white robes gone gray at the hems, faces hidden beneath hoods stitched with silver thread in patterns Dain almost recognized and wished he didn't.

"Harvesters," August said. The word came out flat as a blade laid on a table.

"You've seen them before?"

"No." August's jaw worked. "But I've seen what they leave."

* * *

They had been shadowing the column since dawn, keeping to the high ground where the gorse grew thick enough to swallow three travelers and a stolen army horse. Dain had led them off the pilgrim road at first light, using the route knowledge he'd offered on his knees in the ruined chapel—the shame of that kneeling still lodged under his ribs like a splinter he couldn't work out. *I know the army's march order. I know where the harvesters go. I know what Malvane is doing, because I helped him learn how to do it.*

He hadn't known, then. That was the lie he'd carried for six years and it had finally rotted through: *I didn't know.* He had known. He had chosen not to look. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was every village he'd certified as cleansed without walking past its first row of houses.

Now he watched, and made himself look.

The harvesters reached Merrow's Ford's well at the center of its square. They did not knock on doors. They did not need to. One of them raised both hands, hood falling back, and Dain saw the face beneath—

—and had to bite down on his own tongue to keep from crying out.

It was a woman's face. Old, kind, seamed with laugh lines, the sort of face you'd trust with your children or your dying. He knew it. Every child raised within fifty leagues of Vell knew it, from the little painted icons sold at market stalls: Saint Amethe of the Wells, who heard the prayers of mothers.

"That's not possible," Sera breathed beside him. "She's been dead for three hundred years."

"Something is wearing her," August said quietly.

Below, the false saint spread her arms, and the village answered. Doors opened. People came out—not driven, not herded, but *drawn*, stumbling into the square with their faces lifted like sunflowers toward a light that wasn't there. Dain watched a farmer drop his scythe in the dirt. Watched a woman carry a sleeping child into the open air. Watched old men kneel on cobblestones still wet from morning washing.

And the thing wearing Saint Amethe's face began to *listen*.

Dain couldn't see the prayers. But he saw their effect. The villagers' mouths moved—some weeping, some laughing, some simply opening and closing like fish—and with each spoken hope, something left them. Not blood. Not breath. Something else. Their color went first, then their certainty, then, visibly, horribly, their *shape*, bodies softening like wax held too near a flame. The farmer with the scythe collapsed forward onto his knees and stayed there, smiling, empty. The woman with the child swayed, and the child in her arms woke and began to cry, and the false saint turned her borrowed, beautiful face toward the sound with an expression of mild, patient appetite.

"They're taking the prayers," Sera said. Her voice had gone very small. "The gods used to answer prayers. This is—it's eating them instead. It's eating the *faith*."

"It's eating them," August corrected, and there was something in the storm-caller's voice Dain had not heard before. Not fear. Not even anger, exactly. Recognition. "Prayer isn't words. It's a person, poured out. That's what the old rites understood. You don't ask a god for help—you give yourself to it, and trust it to give back. These things take the giving and keep it. Every prayer those people ever prayed, every hope they ever spent—they're drinking it down and passing it up the column to the palanquin."

"To Malvane," Dain said.

"To whatever Malvane is becoming."

* * *

They stayed until full dark, though Sera begged to leave after the first hour and August refused, and Dain found himself refusing too, in a voice that sounded like his old commander's, cold and procedural, and hated himself for it. But someone had to count. Someone had to know the shape of it. If they were going to stand between this thing and the free cities—if anyone was—they needed to understand what they were standing against.

So Dain counted.

Four hundred villagers drawn into the square of Merrow's Ford. Three hours of harvesting. When it ended, the false saint folded her hands, bowed her head as if in benediction, and walked back down the road trailing her three silent brothers, and the column resumed its march, and the village stood empty behind them—not destroyed, not burned, merely *hollowed*. Lights still burned in windows. A dog still barked at nothing. But no one came to quiet it.

The supply wagons that followed the harvesters carried jars.

Dain had seen the jars before. In the inquisitorial vaults beneath the Citadel of Vell, sealed behind three doors and a vow of silence, there had been a chamber the novices called the Quiet Room. Preserved hearts in brine. Relics of the purges, catalogued and kept, because Malvane believed—had always believed—that faith was a substance, and substances could be stockpiled. Dain had signed the inventory sheets himself, twenty years old and proud of his neat hand.

He had thought they were trophies.

They were provisions.

"We should burn the wagons," Sera said when they finally withdrew into the dark hills, making camp in a shepherd's hut with a broken roof and a view of the river road below. She was sitting with her knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, staring at the distant lantern-light of the column. "One fire. All of it. We could—"

"With four hundred soldiers between us and the wagons?" Dain asked gently.

"Then we follow them to Ostenmarch. Warn the council. Show them *this*—" She stopped. Her voice cracked on the last word, and Dain realized she was holding something in her closed fist, and had been for some time.

August saw it too. "Sera. What is that?"

She opened her hand slowly, reluctantly, the way you open a door you already know what's behind.

A medallion. Small, pewter, worn nearly smooth by thumbs. Stamped with the sigil of a lily growing from a book.

Dain felt the bottom drop out of the evening.

"I saw it in one of the jars," Sera said. "In the wagon train. When the harvesters passed, one of the carts tilted, and the oilcloth shifted, and I saw—" She swallowed. "That's the mark of my convent. The Sisters of the Quiet Lily, at Candlemere. Where I was acolyte. Where I—" Her fingers closed around the medallion again, knuckles white. "Where I prayed every night of my childhood. Those are our relics. Our saints' medals. Our *prayer books*. They're in the wagons like—like salt pork. Like stores."

"Sera." August reached for her shoulder; she flinched away, then caught herself and let the hand land, and Dain watched the girl force herself to accept comfort the way you force yourself to swallow medicine. "When did you leave Candlemere?"

"Two years ago. Before the purges reached the coast. Mother Superior sent the novices away—she said the convent had grown *loud*, that prayers were being answered too easily, that something had noticed us." Sera laughed, a broken, awful sound. "We thought she meant the inquisitors. We thought she meant *you*, Dain. We ran from men with warrants, and all the time it was *that* down in the valley, learning our names."

"The harvesters don't have warrants," Dain said. "They don't need them. Malvane learned something in the early years—I helped him learn it, God forgive me. Faith leaves residue. Every answered prayer leaves a trace, like heat off a stone. His artificers built instruments to find it. Whole villages light up like signal fires to anyone carrying the right lens." He looked at his own hands in the dark. "Your convent wasn't loud, Sera. It was *lit*. And he harvested it the same way he's harvesting everything between here and Vell. Slowly. Efficiently. With a dead woman's face."

Silence, except for the wind in the broken roof and the far-off murmur of the army.

Then Sera stood up.

It was not a dramatic motion. That was what Dain would remember about it afterward, in the years when he let himself remember anything at all: how undramatic it was. A small woman in a travel-stained novice's habit rising to her feet in a ruined hut, wiping her face once with the heel of her hand, and saying, in a voice with no tremble left in it:

"I'm done running behind them."

August looked up. "Sera—"

"No. Listen to me." She turned, and in the thin moonlight through the broken roof Dain saw her face properly for the first time since the valley, and it frightened him more than the false saint had. Not rage. Something colder and far more durable. "I spent two years being sorry. Sorry for leaving, sorry for surviving, sorry for praying wrong or believing wrong or being the kind of person things like that can *use*. I hid in Vell's lower quarter and I lit candles for a goddess everyone forgot and I told myself small was safe." Her fist tightened around the medallion until Dain half expected blood. "Those were my sisters' medals in those jars. Maybe some of my sisters too. And I sat on a ridge today and *counted*, because counting felt useful, and I am finished with useful. I want to be *dangerous*."

"To what end?" Dain asked, and immediately wished he'd kept silent, because she rounded on him with the whole of her small furious weight.

"To the First Storm, inquisitor. To the thing your usurper is marching to steal. You told us the route. Fine. Now tell me which parts of it burn."

* * *

Later, when Sera slept at last—fitfully, the medallion still clenched in her fist—Dain sat awake beside the banked fire with August, watching the ember-light move across the storm-caller's face.

"She'll break," Dain said quietly. "Rage like that doesn't hold. It spends itself."

"Yes," August said. "And then what's underneath holds instead. I've seen it." A pause. "I've *been* it. You burn down a village in a single night of fury, and then you spend five years learning what fury costs, and what's left when it's spent is the only part of you that was ever real." The storm-caller's eyes reflected the fire, and for a moment Dain saw the old lightning in them, the thing he had loved across a barricade of duty six years ago, before the exile, before the purge, before everything. "She'll be stronger on the other side of tonight than either of us. Pray we're worth her strength."

Dain fed a stick to the fire. Beyond the hut, far to the south, the column's lanterns crawled along the river road like embers escaping a grate, and somewhere among them the palanquin moved under its silk the color of drowned things, and the jars rode in their wagons, and the road ahead bent, as all roads in this country now bent, toward Vell.

"There's something I haven't told you," Dain said. "About the route."

August turned.

"The council at Ostenmarch won't act—we both know it. But Malvane's march order has a flaw. A week from now the column funnels through the Cinder Pass, a defile two leagues long with cliffs on either side and one road through. He knows it. He's confident because no army stands against him anymore." Dain stared into the fire. "But the pass has an old shrine at its mouth. Pre-imperial. Dedicated to a goddess of thresholds and doors." He glanced sideways. "To Vessa."

August went very still.

"And here's the flaw," Dain continued, voice dropping. "The harvesters avoid it. Every shrine, every holy place on this road, they've entered and emptied—but not that one. My sources say the harvesters *refuse* it. Whatever's left of your goddess in that pass, Malvane's creatures are afraid of it. Which means it's the one place on this entire march where his power doesn't reach." He met August's eyes. "And it's the last ground before Vell where three people could possibly hurt an army of four hundred."

The fire popped. Down in the valley, faint on the wind, Dain thought he heard singing—thin, sweet, wrong, the sound of something wearing a god's face leading ten thousand men onward to steal heaven.

August rose, and looked south, and said: "Then we'd better reach the threshold before he does."
