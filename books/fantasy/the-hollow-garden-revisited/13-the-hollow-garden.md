## 13. The Hollow Garden

The valley announced itself long before we reached it.

We had been walking for two days through country that grew stranger with every mile—first the hedgerows going gray at their tips like old men's beards, then the streams running clear but tasting of ash, then the birds. Or rather, the absence of birds. Sera noticed it before I did; she always noticed the living things first. She stopped on the third morning, head tilted, listening to a silence that had no business being so complete in high summer, and said, "August. There's nothing singing."

By noon we could see why.

The valley opened below us like a wound that had been bandaged with gray cloth. Every tree stood bare—not dead, exactly, because dead things still have a shape to them, a dignity of fallen branches and curled bark—but *emptied*, as if something had drunk the green out of them and left the architecture behind. The grass was the color of old bone. A river ran through the middle of it, wide and slow, and where its water touched the banks it left no dark line, no wet gleam. The ground refused even that small intimacy.

Sera's hand found my sleeve. "Is this Malvane's doing?"

"No." I didn't know how I knew. I only knew that the blight here felt old, patient, settled into the soil like a family into a house they'd lived in for generations. "This is older than him."

The shard of the First Storm, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, had been warm since dawn. Now it was hot enough that I could feel it through three layers of cloth and leather, a heartbeat pressed against my sternum that wasn't mine. It had been pulling us north for days—tugging at my sleep, bending my dreams toward this one compass point—and now that we stood above it, the pulling stopped. It simply waited, the way a door waits.

"This is where she bloomed," Sera said quietly. "Vessa. The convent songs call it the First Garden. They say every flower in the world is descended from what grew here." She looked out over the bone-colored valley, the stripped trees, the river that wouldn't wet the earth, and her voice did something complicated. "They don't say what happened to it."

"Maybe nobody who saw it lived to write it down."

"That's not funny."

"I wasn't joking."

We went down into it.

* * *

The blight thickened as we descended, and it wasn't just the plants. Sound behaved strangely here. Our footsteps made no echo off the valley walls; our voices came out flat and close, as though the air itself had been muffled in wool. Even the shard's heat seemed to press inward instead of radiating, gathering itself around me like a second skin.

Halfway down the slope, Sera stopped walking and would not start again.

"August." Her face had gone the color of the grass. "I can't hear her."

"Hear who?"

"Vessa. I've heard her since the shrine—a little, a whisper, like someone praying in the next room. Every day since. Even when I doubted everything else, I could hear *that*." She pressed both hands over her ears, which of course did nothing. "Here there's nothing. It's not quiet, it's—it's like standing in a room where someone died."

I took her hands away from her ears and held them. Her fingers were ice. "Then whatever happened here happened to *her*, too," I said. "And maybe it's time somebody finally looked at it."

The temple—if that was the right word for it—stood at the valley floor near the river's bend. It had been magnificent once; you could tell from the bones of it. Columns of white stone fanned outward from a central hall like the petals of an enormous flower, and even now, bleached and cracked and half-swallowed by the gray earth, they kept the shape of something that had been built to hold joy. But the joy was gone. Moss that should have softened the stone grew in dead silver patches. The great doors had rotted to lace. And everywhere, carved into every surface in patterns so repeated they became almost invisible—the storm-spiral. My mark. My inheritance. Coiling up the columns, ringing the altars, spiraling across the shattered mosaic floor in lines of lapis that had once, I realized with a lurch, been blue as deep sky.

Storm-caller marks. In Vessa's garden.

I stood on that mosaic floor and felt the easy story I'd carried for five years begin, very quietly, to crack.

* * *

I need to explain what that story was, because I told it to myself so many times it had worn smooth as river stone, and I honestly believed it.

It went like this: Once, the gods walked the world, and mortals suffered under them—demanded sacrifices, hoarded worship, punished independence. Then mortals rose up, or the gods withdrew, or some mixture of both, and the world entered its long quiet. Magic faded. Prayers went unanswered. Cities like Vell outlawed the old arts, and that was *good*, that was wisdom, because magic was the gods' leash and cutting it set us free. Whatever power remained—in people like me, storm-blooded, heir to something ancient—was a leftover, a scar, a dangerous relic of a bad age. My own magic, the thing that had killed forty-one people in Corvane's harbor district, was part of that bad age. My exile was justice. My renunciation was progress.

That was the story. Mortals innocent, gods guilty, silence a victory bought with blood.

Standing in Vessa's ruined garden, with the shard burning against my heart and storm-spirals coiled up the goddess's own pillars, I felt the first hairline fracture go through it.

Because Vessa hadn't been a god of storms. Everyone knew that much. Vessa was the Green Mother, the Bloomer, the goddess of gardens and growth and gentle rain—the softest of all the old powers, the one the convents loved, the one whose prayers were about harvests and healing and children's fevers breaking at dawn. If *she* had carried the storm-mark, then the storm had never belonged to wrath alone. It had belonged to her too. To life. To the force that splits the seed and floods the field and tears the old growth down so the new can come.

Or—

Or the storm had belonged to whoever won the war.

I didn't know yet which answer was worse. I was about to find out.

"August." Sera's voice came from across the hall, thin and strange. She was standing before the central altar, where a basin the size of a wagon wheel sat sunk into the stone, dry as everything else. Except it wasn't dry anymore. Water was seeping up through the basin's cracks—black water, or maybe just water so deep it swallowed light—and rising, slowly, without spilling, without sound. "August, it's filling. Why is it filling? There's no spring here. There's no *anything* here."

The shard against my chest pulsed once, hard, like a fist knocking from inside.

"It's not filling for us," I heard myself say. "It's filling for *me*."

And the terrible thing, the thing I've never told anyone, is that some part of me wasn't afraid. Some part of me—some deep, buried, five-years-buried part—was *homesick*. That's the word I used in the dream, the night after the shrine: homesick. For power. For the storm. For the version of myself I swore I'd killed in Corvane's harbor. I stood in front of that black rising water and the renounced thing in my chest turned toward it like a sunflower toward light, and I hated myself for it, and I stepped forward anyway.

"Wait—" Sera caught my arm. "You don't know what it does. August. You *don't know*."

"I know it's been leading me here since the reliquary. I know your goddess spent her last strength getting this shard to me. I know Malvane is harvesting god-flesh three provinces away and building himself into something that makes the old gods look merciful." I turned my hand over and let hers go, gently. "And I know that whatever truth is in this valley, I'd rather it break me than keep hiding."

"You say that like the truth has never broken you before."

"It broke me in Corvane's harbor. I survived it by lying to myself about whose fault the war was." I looked at the water. It had nearly reached the rim now, and in its black surface I could see no reflection of the ruined ceiling, no reflection of Sera, no reflection of me. Only weather. Clouds moving in a sky that wasn't there. "Maybe it's time I let it finish the job."

I put my hands in the water.

* * *

The world came apart the way a knot comes apart when you finally find the right thread.

No pain. That surprised me most—I had braced for pain, for fire, for the white agony of the reliquary vision. Instead there was a sensation like being *unrolled*, like a map spread open on a table by hands far larger than mine, and then I was falling upward through cloud, through years, through the memory of the world itself, and Vessa was all around me.

Not a voice. Not a face. A presence the way spring is a presence—you don't see it arrive, you simply notice that everything has changed. And she was grieving. That was the first thing, the truest thing, the thing I would carry back up out of the water like a stone in my shoe: the goddess of every garden that ever grew was *grieving*, and had been grieving for longer than my language had words for it, and she showed me why.

She showed me the beginning.

I had imagined the ancient war the way everyone imagines it—mortal armies, divine wrath, fire from a sky full of offended gods. The truth was smaller and infinitely worse. There was no war, at first. There was a *garden*. This one. A world-garden, tended jointly, gods shaping the deep patterns and mortals tending the near ones, and the storm was the shared tool—the great renewal, called down together every generation to strip the tired growth and wake the seed-banks, death and birth braided into a single act that no one called violence because everyone consented to it.

And then the gods began to disagree about what the garden was *for*.

Some of them—Vessa showed me their shapes without names, vast and bright and wrong—decided mortals were the crop. Beautiful, brief, endlessly renewing, perfect. Others decided mortals were the gardeners, and the gods merely the weather. And the argument, which should have stayed an argument, curdled. One faction called the storm without consent. Another answered with a storm of their own. And the tool that renews became the weapon that unmakes, and the First Storm—the original, the pattern-storm, the one whose shard sleeps against my heart—was born not as a gift or a judgment but as an *escalation*, and everything after that was just the long, grinding, thousand-year horror of two sides refusing to be the first to stop.

The gods began it. Not mortals. Not ambition, not hubris, none of the tidy sins the priests taught. The gods began it, over a disagreement about ownership, and mortals—the fields, the cities, the children—were the ground it was fought on.

Vessa had tried to stop it. That was the last thing she showed me. She had stood in this valley between two warring pantheons and refused to choose, and they had made her refusal expensive: they had unmade her garden, poisoned her root-system through every soil in the world, left her blooming gray and empty for an age, as a lesson to any other god considering neutrality. Her blight was not decay. It was a *punishment*, maintained deliberately, lovingly, by her own kin.

And when the others finally burned themselves out or fled into hiding, when the long quiet fell and mortals told themselves the comfortable story—that they had won their freedom, that silence was victory—Vessa lay in her punished valley and listened to mortals tell it, and said nothing, because the truth served no one anymore.

Until Malvane started harvesting her dying kin for parts.

Until a runaway acolyte prayed at a drowned shrine and a dying goddess spent her final strength answering.

Until a storm-blooded exile walked into the water.

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees on the mosaic floor, soaked to the shoulders in water that was now clear and cold and ordinary, shaking so hard my teeth chattered. Sera had hold of me—both arms, gripping hard, her face wet, though whether from the splashback or from crying I couldn't tell.

"You were under for a minute," she was saying. "One minute, maybe less, you weren't breathing, August, I thought—"

"The gods started it." My voice came out scraped raw, like I'd been screaming underwater. Maybe I had. "Sera. The war. The whole war. The gods started it, and they punished her for trying to stop it, and everything—all of it, the exile laws, the purges, Vell, the inquisitors, everything we've run from—they built all of it on top of a lie that made *them* the victims." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "And I built myself on top of a lie too. All these years. Mortals innocent, gods guilty, my magic a curse from a bad old age. But it's not a curse, is it? It's a *tool*. Their tool. Their weapon. And I'm not an innocent who inherited a disease. I'm the heir of the people who started it."

"August—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Please. Let me—let me get to the end of it, because if I stop I'll never finish." I pulled the shard from my shirt. It glowed now, faintly, a blue-white pulse like distant lightning behind cloud, and it was beautiful, and for the first time since the reliquary I understood it completely and it made me sick. "This is the First Storm. The original escalation. The thing they used on each other until the world broke. And it doesn't care who calls it or why. It unmade gods, Sera. It will unmake Malvane. And it will unmake me, and it will do all of it with the same serene indifference, because that's what it *is*—it's the moment the gardeners decided the garden was theirs to burn."

Sera knelt in front of me. She took the shard out of my hand—carefully, reverently, the way she handled the convent relics she'd grown up with—and closed my fingers back around it.

"Then here's what I know," she said. Her voice was steady in a way that meant she'd fought for it. "I grew up on the convent stories. Vessa the Green Mother, gentlest of the gentle. And you know what? Everything she's done since I met her has been gentle. She answered a child's prayer through a luck-knot. She saved my life. She chose *you*—not because you're strong, but because you're the one person alive who knows exactly what the storm costs, and would never call it lightly." Her grip tightened. "So maybe the inheritance isn't innocence. Fine. Maybe it's complicity. But complicity means you're *in* it, August, and things that are in it can choose differently. That's the whole point of you. That's the whole point of everything we've walked through."

I wanted to believe her. God help me, I wanted it so badly my chest ached with it.

But the valley was still gray. The trees were still stripped. Vessa's punishment was still holding, an age after the war ended, and somewhere north an army was marching behind a man who had looked at the gods' crime and decided the solution was to become one of them.

And the shard pulsed against my palm, patient as a door, and through its glow I felt—faint, distant, unmistakable—something I had hoped never to feel again.

Thunder. Far to the south. Rolling toward us.

Malvane had found the valley too.
