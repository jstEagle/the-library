## 14. What We Bury, What We Keep

Sera had learned to read August the way she had once learned to read scripture—slowly, by candlelight, expecting the text to contradict itself.

There were the obvious signs. When August was afraid, they sharpened things: knives that didn't need it, a whetstone worn to a smile of steel, the edge of a wooden spoon once, in desperation, when Dain had taken their blades for cleaning. When August grieved—and Sera had seen them grieve twice now, once for the village they'd burned and once, strangely, for Vessa, whose death sat in them like a swallowed stone—they went quiet in a particular way, hands still, eyes fixed on middle distance, as if listening to something very far away. And when August was happy, truly happy, which was rare as rain in Highsummer, they hummed. Badly. Three notes of a song Sera didn't recognize, over and over, like a man tapping at a door he'd forgotten how to open.

Tonight there was no humming. Tonight August sat on the far side of the abandoned mill's cold hearth, turning Vessa's shard over in their fingers, and the silence around them had weight, had texture, like wet wool.

The shard had changed since the drowned temple. It had been sea-glass once, pale and patient. Now veins of silver crawled through it, brightening and dimming in a rhythm Sera refused to name because naming it would make it a heartbeat, and if the shard had a heartbeat then it was alive, and if it was alive then what August was holding was not a weapon but a wound that hadn't happened yet.

"You should sleep," Sera said.

"I'll sleep."

"You said that last night. You said it the night before. You're going to say it tomorrow, and then we'll be at the valley, and you'll have been awake so long you'll start seeing things, and I don't mean visions, I mean the ordinary kind, the kind that make people walk into rivers."

August's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. "You've gotten bossy."

"I'm an acolyte. Bossiness is liturgically required." She crossed the room and sat down across from them, tucking her legs under her, the way she used to sit in the convent dormitory when the older girls told stories after lights-out. Except those stories had all ended safely. *And then the saint went home.* *And then the god forgave them.* *And then everyone lived small and quiet and full of grace.*

No one had ever told her a story that ended with someone she loved being unmade.

"August."

"Don't," they said softly. Not unkindly. The way you'd say *don't* to someone reaching toward a hot stove. "Whatever speech you've been drafting for three days, Sera. Don't."

"I haven't been drafting a speech."

"You've been drafting a speech since the temple. You do this thing with your hands when you're composing. You fold them like a priest about to give a blessing." They set the shard down on the hearthstone between them, deliberately, like laying down a card. "So say it. Whatever it is. I'd rather be argued with than watched."

Sera's heart was going too fast. She made herself breathe the way Mother Osla had taught her—in for four, hold for four, out for four—the breath that was supposed to carry prayer, though lately her prayers had gone strange, addressed less to Vessa than to whatever part of Vessa still bothered to listen.

She reached into her collar and drew out the cord she'd worn since the convent. On it hung a small thing of bone and copper wire, no bigger than a thumbnail: a memory-charm. Hedge-craft, really, the kind of thing country wisewomen stitched for brides and midwives and soldiers going off to wars. You held it, you thought of a memory until it filled your whole skull, and the charm drank a little of it—not the memory itself, but its *shape*, its weight, the way a seal takes wax. And if your mind was ever broken, scattered, washed away—if something vast reached into you and rearranged what you were—the charm could give the shape back. A keel for a capsized boat. One plank of yourself, kept dry.

Mother Osla had given it to her the day Sera took her first vows. *You will lose things,* the old woman had said, pressing it into her palm. *Everyone does. Keep one thing safe against that day.*

Sera had never needed it. She had never lost anything, not really, not until a dying goddess spoke a stranger's true name in a ruined shrine and dragged Sera out of her small holy life and into this one—this road, this war, this slow-motion ending standing right in front of her wearing August Thackeray's tired face.

She untied the cord from her neck. It felt strange in her hands, lighter than it should have been, the way a key is always lighter than the door it opens.

"What's that?" August asked.

"A charm. My charm." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "It keeps memories. Well—one memory. It holds the shape of one memory safe, even if everything else goes."

August looked at the little bone-and-copper thing for a long moment. Then they looked up, and Sera watched them understand it, watched the understanding arrive like weather crossing a plain—first the stillness, then the darkening, then the wind.

"No," August said.

"It's not a request."

"Sera—"

"You're going to let the storm take everything." She heard her own voice crack on *everything* and hated it, hated the tremor, hated that she sounded seventeen instead of twenty-three, hated that grief had apparently decided to make her sound like every frightened novice she'd ever comforted. "Everything, August. Your mother's face. Your name. How to whistle. Whether you like honey in your tea. Every single thing you are is going to be poured out of you like dirty water, and you've known it since the temple, and you've been *fine*, you've been calm, you've been making jokes about it, and I can't—I cannot sit here and watch you decide that's acceptable."

"It's not acceptable. It's necessary. There's a difference."

"There isn't! Not anymore!" She was on her feet without remembering standing. "Necessary is what Malvane calls it when he burns villages. Necessary is what the convent called it when they locked away the relics. Everyone who does something monstrous has a word like *necessary* to hide behind, and yours is no better just because the monster is a storm."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, breathing hard. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. That's rather the entire problem."

August rose too, slowly, and for a moment they stood on opposite sides of the dead hearth with the shard between them, pulsing its slow silver pulse, and Sera thought absurdly of two people arguing over a grave.

"Give me the charm," August said quietly.

Something in Sera's chest lurched—hope and terror in the same instant, indistinguishable. "What?"

"If you're going to insist. Give it here. Let me see what it does."

She handed it over. Their fingers brushed, and she felt them trembling—August, who had faced inquisitors and floods and the voice of a drowning god without flinching, trembling over a scrap of bone and wire.

They turned it in the firelight. "This was yours. From your convent."

"From Mother Osla. She said everyone loses things eventually. She said to keep one thing safe against that day." Sera wrapped her arms around herself. "I never used it. I never had anything worth—" She stopped. Started again. "I have something worth keeping now. But it turns out it's not mine to keep. It's walking around wearing your face and refusing to sleep."

August was quiet for a long time. The shard pulsed. Somewhere outside, in the black hills above the mill, a nightbird called three times and got no answer.

"You understand what you're offering," August said at last. "If I take this, it means I've accepted it. The calling. The price. All of it. No more looking for another way."

"We both know there isn't another way. We've spent two weeks looking for another way. Dain's ridden half the free cities looking for another way." She gestured vaguely eastward, where Dain Corvess was somewhere in the dark, doing whatever disgraced inquisitors did to prepare for the end of the world—sharpening things, probably; it seemed to run in the company she kept. "The storm is the only thing that can unmake him. The storm only answers someone willing to be unmade. You're the heir. You're the last of the storm-blood line. There is no elegant version of this, August. There's only the version where you keep one piece of yourself, or the version where you keep nothing."

"And you'd give up your own memory-shape for that? This was your one protected thing. Your whole life, you guarded it empty."

"It wasn't empty." Sera smiled, and found to her surprise that the smile was real, that underneath the fear there was something else, something steady, something that had been growing in her for months without her noticing. "It was waiting. Osla said charms know what they're for before we do. Maybe that's why I never needed it before. Maybe it was always meant for this. For you."

"That's a terrible reason to give away the only sacred object you own."

"I'm an acolyte of a forgotten goddess whose entire theology is that sacred objects belong to whoever needs them most." She lifted her chin. "I think I'm on extremely solid doctrinal ground."

A sound escaped August—half laugh, half something much more fragile—and they pressed the heel of their hand briefly against their eyes. When they lowered it, they weren't smiling, but the terrible composure had cracked, and what showed through was younger and more frightened and more dear than anything Sera had seen in them.

"All right," August said. "All right. Show me how it works."

* * *

They sat close together on the floor, backs against the cold hearthstone, the shard pushed safely into its lead-lined box across the room. The charm lay in August's open palm, catching the light of the single candle Sera had lit—she'd wanted candlelight for this, wanted ritual, wanted the old shapes of holiness even if the holiness had mostly drained out of the world.

"Now," she said. "Hold it. Think of the memory. Not the facts of it—the *feeling*. The shape of it. Let it fill you up until there's nothing else in your head. The charm does the rest."

"What happens to the original memory?"

"Nothing. You keep it. The charm just keeps a copy of its shape. Like—" she searched for the image— "like a mold. If the statue shatters, the mold remembers what it looked like."

"And one memory comes back. Just one. Everything else stays gone."

"Yes."

August stared at the little charm. Then, quietly, in a voice Sera had never heard them use—a voice stripped of all its armor:

"How am I supposed to choose?"

The question hung in the smoky air. Sera had known it was coming. She'd had three days to prepare her answer, and she'd drafted speeches, yes, fine, August had been right about the hands—but none of the drafts covered this, the rawness of it, the naked arithmetic of a person deciding which single thread to pull out of the burning house of themselves.

"You don't have to choose tonight," she said.

"When, then? On the march? In the valley? While the sky is tearing open?" August laughed, short and bitter. "I've been trying to choose for days. Did you know that? Every night I lie awake and I go through it like a ledger. My mother teaching me to call the rain—that one, surely, that's the oldest, that's the root of everything. But then I think, no, the massacre, I should keep the massacre, because someone has to remember what I did, even if it's only me, even if the me that remembers hates the me that survives. But then I think—isn't that cruelty? Choosing my worst day as my only day? And then there's Vell. The night market. The luck-knots. Five years of being nobody, and it was—it was good, Sera. Being nobody was the happiest I ever was. Should I keep that? The happiness? Or the person I was when I was happy?"

Their voice was fraying now, the words coming faster.

"And then there's you. And Dain. And the temple. And the dream of the storm with a voice, which I hate, and which I also miss, which makes me sick to admit. And I lie there and I try to rank my own soul, and every ranking is wrong, and meanwhile the shard gets brighter every night, and I can feel it *waiting*, and I think maybe the truth is that I can't choose because choosing means admitting this is real, and as long as I don't choose, some part of me gets to pretend there's still a version of this where I survive it whole."

The silence afterward rang. Sera realized she was crying, silently, without drama, tears simply present on her face like weather.

"August," she said. "Take my hand."

They did. Their palm was damp and cold and shaking slightly, and she held on.

"You don't have to tell me what you choose," she said. "I don't want to know. Do you understand? Whatever you pick, it's yours. It's the one thing the storm can't touch, and it belongs to you alone, and I will never ask, and neither will Dain, and if you come out of this not knowing my name, I will introduce myself a hundred times if I have to, a thousand, and I will never once ask you what you kept. Because that's the point. That's the whole point. One thing that's *yours*. Untouched. Ungiven. Even to me."

August looked at her for a long moment. Candlelight moved in their eyes.

"When did you get wise?" they asked hoarsely.

"Somewhere between the flooded catacombs and the rooftop in Ostenmarch. It was a very educational month."

Another sound from them, wetter this time, almost a laugh. They closed their hand slowly around the charm, and Sera watched their knuckles whiten, watched them bow their head over their own fist as if in prayer—which, she supposed, was exactly what it was. The first honest prayer either of them had made in weeks, offered not to Vessa, not to the storm, not to any god at all, but to a scrap of bone and copper and the stubborn animal refusal of a human heart to surrender everything.

The candle flame leaned. Outside, far to the north, thunder muttered—distant, directionless, the kind of thunder that might be weather or might be omen. Both of them heard it. Neither of them moved.

After a while, August opened their eyes. The charm was warm in their fist; Sera could see it glowing faintly through the gaps between their fingers, drinking deep.

"It's done," August said quietly.

Sera's breath caught. "Already?"

"The charm knows what it wants. Apparently it always did." They looked at her then, and their expression was unreadable—tender and closed at once, a door with light behind it. They slipped the cord over their head and tucked the charm beneath their shirt, against their skin, where it would ride with them into the valley and the storm and whatever waited on the other side of both.

"Come on," August said, rising, pulling her up after them, voice rough but steady. "Dain will be back before dawn, and we've a long road tomorrow, and I intend to spend what's left of tonight sleeping, since apparently I have people now who count my hours."

They walked toward the mill's back room together. At the doorway August paused, just briefly, hand drifting up to touch the small hard shape under their shirt—an unconscious gesture, protective, the way a person touches a wound or a promise.

Then they were through the door, and the candle burned low, and Sera sat alone in the dark with the thunder rolling closer from the north, and she did not ask, and she would never ask, and all the rest of her life she would wonder.
