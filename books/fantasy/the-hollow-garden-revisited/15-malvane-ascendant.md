## 15. Malvane Ascendant

The ritual chamber had once been a granary. Malvane appreciated the irony. He had starved this city into submission three winters ago, and now its dead grain-silos held something sweeter than wheat: god-flesh, packed in salt and myrrh, stacked to the rafters like a harvest no farmer would ever dare name.

He stood at the center of it, barefoot on a floor of crushed marble, and let them begin.

The acolytes—no, he had stopped calling them that; *surgeons* was more honest—moved around him in their silent procession of nine, each carrying a reliquary the size of a heart. Inside each one, a fragment of something that had once been worshipped. A knucklebone of the Shepherd of Flocks. A dried petal from Vessa's drowned garden. A sliver of the River-Mother's spine, still faintly warm after four hundred years in a riverbed. The old gods had left so much of themselves behind when they went quiet, scattered through the world like coins dropped from a dying man's pocket, and for centuries no one had thought to pick them up.

Malvane had thought of nothing else for eleven years.

The first reliquary opened with a sound like a sigh. The flesh inside—gray-gold, glistening, wrong—lifted free and hung in the air between his hands, trembling as though it remembered being loved. He did not flinch. He had learned not to flinch somewhere around the fourth god.

"Begin," he said.

They began.

* * *

Pain was supposed to be part of it. Every text agreed on that—the mortal vessel must be broken open before divinity could pour in, the way a seed splits before the green shows. But no text had described what it actually felt like, because no text had been written by anyone who survived it, and the ones who hadn't survived had died too early to describe anything.

It felt like being unmade in the exact shape of himself.

The first fragment entered through his sternum. He felt it go—not as a wound but as an arrival, a tenant moving into rooms he hadn't known his body contained. His heartbeat stuttered, then doubled, then found a rhythm that was somehow both his own and older than his own, a drumbeat from before drums. Light leaked through the seams of him. He could see it from the inside: gold threading along his veins like frost forming in reverse.

The second fragment took his left eye. Not destroyed it—took it, replaced it, filled the socket with something that saw in every direction at once. Through his new eye he watched the surgeons work, and beneath their skin he saw the small bright animals of their souls, and beneath the floor he saw the bones of the city arranged like a diagram, and beyond the walls he saw the road south where an army waited for its master to become worth following.

The third fragment hurt most, because by then he understood what the pain was. It was refusal. The god-stuff did not want to be him. It remembered being vast and windless and adored, and it looked at the narrow, sweating, ambitious animal it was being poured into and it recoiled.

"Good," Malvane whispered, blood on his teeth. "Resist me. I have never lost to anything that resisted."

The fourth fragment. The fifth. Somewhere past the sixth he stopped counting and started listening, because the fragments were beginning to speak to one another inside him, in a language made of pressure and light, and what they were saying—what they were *arguing* about—was him.

One voice, cold and patient, said: *this one is a vessel, and vessels are permitted.*

Another voice, older and sadder, said: *this one is a thief, and thieves are burned.*

And a third voice, which he recognized with a jolt of horror and delight as his own, said: *this one is necessary.*

* * *

He woke on the granary floor with dawn coming through the arrow-slits and nine surgeons kneeling around him in attitudes of worship they had not planned. Their faces were wet. Later he would learn that everyone who looked directly at him in those first hours wept without knowing why—grief or awe or some nerve deep in the human animal recognizing, correctly, that it was in the presence of something that had eaten its own humanity to get here.

He sat up. His body reported itself to him piece by piece, and the report was strange. No hunger. No fatigue. The ache in his hip that had been his companion since the siege of Karst was gone, and he found he missed it, the way a man misses a limp that taught him caution. His hands were the same hands. That was the miracle and the obscenity of it: the same broad, scarred, ordinary hands, and yet when he flexed them, the air flinched.

He walked to the polished bronze mirror they had set against the wall—he had insisted on bronze, not silver; silver showed truth and he wanted, for now, only the truth he could bear—and he looked at what the ritual had made.

A tall man in his fifty-fourth year, gray at the temples, heavy through the shoulders, with the face of a provincial magistrate who had once been handsome. Except for the eyes. The left was still his: brown, tired, human, the eye that had read ledgers and death warrants and his daughter's letters. The right was molten. Gold moved behind it like weather seen from very high up, and when he turned it toward the mirror, the mirror began, very quietly, to smoke.

Half of him was divine. Half of him was rotting. He could feel both truths simultaneously, and neither canceled the other out. The god-flesh was alive in him, working, growing—but it was also *dying*, because divinity poured into a mortal vessel did not take root; it soured. Already he could smell it, a sweetness under his own skin like fruit going over. The texts had warned of this too, in the margins, in the handwriting of men who had gotten this far and no farther. *The vessel holds for a season. Then the season turns.*

A season. Perhaps two if he was careful. Perhaps less if he used the power as lavishly as he intended to use it.

Malvane laughed—a real laugh, rusty, startling the kneeling surgeons—and thought: *then I will do everything in a season.*

* * *

They brought him the reports while he dressed. He no longer needed sleep, which freed the nights for administration, and there was so much administration. Empire did not run on miracles. Empire ran on grain allotments and garrison rosters and the endless, grinding paperwork of making cruelty look like order.

"The storm-caller," said Chancellor Vey, standing at the respectful distance everyone now stood at, "was sighted in Ostenmarch ten days ago. The bounty notice did its work. They escaped the city by night. There were—" Vey consulted the page, and Malvane watched the man's throat move nervously around the word, "—witnesses who spoke of weather behaving strangely."

"Weather behaving strangely." Malvane smiled with half his face. In the bronze mirror, the golden eye did not smile at all. "In Ostenmarch. Where it has not rained in a month."

"Yes, lord."

"And the shard?"

"Gone from the reliquary site before our people reached it. Taken, we believe, by the same party. There is a theory among the field inquisitors that the heretic Thackeray is gathering relics—that the storm-caller intends to—"

"I know what they intend." He said it mildly, and Vey flinched anyway, because everything Malvane said now arrived with a faint pressure behind it, like the first drop before a flood. He had noticed this about himself and could not stop it. Words came out of him heavier than words should be. When he gave an order, men obeyed it faster than obedience required. When he thanked someone, they wept. Language bent around him now, the way light bends around a mass, and he had decided to find this useful rather than horrifying.

Horrifying could wait. Horrifying was for the season after this one, if there was one.

"They intend to call the First Storm," he said. "Of course they do. It is the only thing left that can kill me, and they are the only ones left who can call it. Do you know, Chancellor, that in eleven years of hunting, I have never once met August Thackeray? I have read their file until I could recite it. I have interviewed survivors of the massacre at Karst Ford until I dreamed in their voices. I have built my entire doctrine of the purges on the premise of that one dangerous life. And they were a child when I started, and now they are the last heir of the storm-blood line, and they are walking toward me across the free cities carrying the oldest weapon in the world." He flexed his fingers, watching gold light pool in his palm like honey. "I find I am looking forward to it. Is that wicked of me?"

Vey, wisely, said nothing.

"It is not wicked," Malvane answered himself. "It is the opposite of wicked. That is what none of you understand yet. What was the First Storm, Chancellor? Tell me what your histories say."

"A weapon of the god-war, lord. The thing that ended it."

"The thing that *won* it—for mortals. Yes. And how did mortals win a war against beings of impossible power?" He turned from the mirror. "Because the gods fought each other first. Because they spent themselves on their own jealousies and left their backs open. The histories call it a victory. I have read the casualty lists from that age, Chancellor. Whole coastlines. Whole *languages*. Victory." He picked up his gloves—plain leather, no ornament; he had always distrusted ornament—and drew them on over hands that glowed faintly at the seams. "The gods were never fit to hold power. They proved it in the oldest war there is. Mortals were never fit to hold power either—I have spent my life watching us prove it daily. But I am no longer entirely either one, and that is the point. That is the whole point. Something new, Vey. Something that has eaten both natures and kept the discipline of neither's failures. The world does not need a king. Kings are how we got here. The world needs a *cure*."

"You believe yourself the cure, lord."

"I believe myself the only candidate who applied." He smiled again, and this time the golden eye smiled too, and Vey took an involuntary step backward. "Have the column ready to march within the hour. We go to Vell."

* * *

Vell. He stood on the granary steps as the army assembled below him in the gray morning—forty thousand men, siege trains, the harvested god-relics in their lead-lined wagons humming like hives—and he let himself remember the city properly.

Vell, the magic-free city. The city that had outlawed wonder and called the ban wisdom. He had been young there once, a clerk's son with a clerk's son's hunger, memorizing the names of the old gods off a forbidden woodcut while other boys played at soldiers. He had prayed, once, at fourteen, alone in a stairwell, to any god that might be listening: *make me matter.* Nothing had answered. Of course nothing had answered; the gods had already gone quiet, already begun their long coward's retirement, leaving a hungry boy alone in a stairwell with nothing but his own ambition to grow into.

So he had done it himself. That was the whole biography, if you wanted it short. *No one answered, so I became the answer.*

And now the gods were stirring again—answering prayers through luck-knots in night markets, whispering their true names to runaway acolytes, leaving shards of themselves in drowned temples for exiled storm-callers to trip over. He had felt them waking for months, little tremors in the new geography of his flesh, and every tremor had confirmed what he had always known: they were unfit. They had abandoned the world and now they crept back into it sideways, through charms and children's prayers, offering hope the way a loan-shark offers coin. And the moment they were strong enough, they would want their thrones back. He had read enough history to know how gods resumed rule. It was always the same chapter, written in the same ash.

Not this time. This time there was something standing in the doorway with a ledger and a sword and half a god's fire in its skull, and it had done the arithmetic they had all refused to do: *power is only safe in the hands of the one willing to pay for it forever.*

He was paying. Look at him. He could feel the rot spreading even now, sweet and patient under the gold, eating him from the inside at exactly the rate he spent himself. Every miracle cost him a week of his remaining season. He had perhaps two hundred days left in which he was anything at all, and he meant to spend every one of them.

Below, the horns sounded the advance. Forty thousand throats took up the marching song, and the sound rolled up the hill and broke against him like surf, and the men nearest the granary fell to their knees without breaking stride, weeping, because their emperor's presence had become a kind of weather.

Malvane raised one gloved hand, and the sky over the eastern hills darkened politely, as if taking notes.

"To Vell," he said, and his voice carried to the rear of the column without effort, arriving in forty thousand ears at once like a thought placed gently in each skull. "The heretic storm-caller carries a relic that belongs to the future. We will take it back. And when the last of the old gods' leavings is ash in my hand, the world will finally be quiet—no prayers, no miracles, no masters in the sky. Only law. Only morning. Only us."

The column roared. The golden eye wept a single bead of light that sizzled on the stone at his feet. The brown eye stayed dry.

As he descended the steps to his horse, the rot shifted under his ribs, and for half a heartbeat he heard the fragments arguing inside him again—the cold voice, the sad voice—and then a third thing, new since the ritual's completion, thin and far away, like a voice down a well:

*a storm is waking in the west, and it knows your name.*

Malvane smiled, mounted, and rode at the head of his cure.
