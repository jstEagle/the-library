## 16. The Siege of Vell

**AUGUST**

The walls of Vell had never been built for war.

August stood on the rampart above the Salt Gate and understood that now, in the way you understand a thing only when it is already too late to matter. The wall was low—shoulder-height where the free cities' fortresses rose three men tall—and its stones were river-smoothed, pretty, chosen for their color rather than their strength. Vell had outlawed magic and called that armor enough. For five years the city had worn its peace like a charm against wolves, and now the wolves were coming up the Osten road with fire in their hands and something worse than fire behind them.

Malvane's column filled the valley from edge to edge.

August had seen armies before, but never one like this. The soldiers marched in silence—not discipline's silence but a held-breath silence, the hush of people walking beside something they did not dare look at. Above the ranks floated litters of black iron, each one curtained, each one carried by eight men who sweated even in the autumn cold. And from those curtains came a light. Not torchlight. A light like the memory of daylight, gold going gray at the edges, and when it touched the fields along the road the last of the harvest withered standing in its rows.

God-flesh. Harvested, bottled, paraded. Malvane was bringing his stolen divinity to the siege like a man bringing his own altar.

"He means to take the city before dark," Dain said beside him. The former inquisitor-captain had traded his white coat for boiled leather and a soldier's half-cape, and he looked wrong in both, like a word misspelled. He swept the valley with a captured spyglass. "He'll want the shard before nightfall. Whatever he is now, I don't think he sleeps."

"Then we disappoint him," August said.

Below them, the defense of the free cities was assembling itself out of desperation and pride. Ostenmarch had finally believed—too late, always too late—and sent what it had: pike levies in mismatched mail, merchant companies whose officers had bought their commissions, two hundred crossbows and perhaps forty men who knew how to use them well. The Vellish militia stood in the middle, shopkeepers and dockhands and one entire guild of glassblowers who had volunteered as a body because their kilns made the best fire-oil bottles in the coastlands. Sera moved among them with her acolyte's gray robe tied off at the waist, carrying water, binding blisters, saying small prayers over men who flinched from the words and then, shamefaced, asked her to say them again.

Five years ago, prayer in Vell would have gotten her arrested. Now the men reached for it like a rope thrown into floodwater.

"They're frightened," Dain said.

"Good," August said. "Frightened men hold walls. Brave ones charge out and die in front of them."

Dain almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. Nothing had reached his eyes since the council's messenger had brought the count of Malvane's vanguard and Dain had read it twice and folded the paper very small. Twenty thousand. Against six thousand, behind a wall built for beauty.

August turned back to the valley and felt the old thing stir under their breastbone—the storm-blood, the inheritance, the wound Vessa's shard had pressed into them in the reliquary beneath the drowned temple. It woke at the sight of that gray-gold light the way an old dog wakes at a stranger's tread. *Soon*, it seemed to say. *You know what soon means.*

Not yet. Not until there was nothing else. That had been the whole of the plan, if a plan could be called a thing assembled from refusal: hold the walls, bleed the column, buy the hours. And if the walls failed—

If the walls failed, then August would climb the tower of the old lighthouse temple, the highest stone in Vell, and call the First Storm down out of the sky's cold vaults, and be unmade by it. Memory by memory. Name by name. They had spent three nights deciding which single memory to keep, and had failed all three nights, and had told no one, not even Sera. Especially not Sera.

The first horn sounded from the valley at midmorning.

It was not a war horn. That was the horror of it. It was a choir—a sound like a hundred voices singing one long unbroken note, and every voice was slightly wrong, pitched just past human, and the note went through the wall's stones and through the flesh of everyone standing on them. Men dropped their spears to cover their ears. One leapt from the rampart without screaming, as if the note had simply lifted him over the edge.

"There it is," Dain said quietly. "That's what answered the prayers in the villages. That's what's wearing the god's face."

The gray-gold light above the litters brightened. And across the whole width of the valley, Malvane's army began to walk toward the wall—not running, not charging, walking, in step, in silence, under that wrong sweet note—as though they had all been promised, personally, that no harm could come to them.

* * *

**DAIN**

Dain Corvess had helped burn a village once, and he had told himself a story about it for eleven years, and he had come to Vell to stop telling stories.

Now he stood on the Salt Gate with a borrowed sword and four hundred men who trusted him because he had once worn the white coat of the Inquisition and therefore must know things about the enemy, and he watched the story he'd helped write come home. The silent ranks. The curtained litters. The way the light bent toward living things, hungry as a tongue toward a wound.

"Hold until they reach the ditch," he said, and his voice carried down the wall the way it had carried orders in another life. "Fire-oil on my mark. Crossbows loose at fifty paces. Nobody breaks the line for anything you see or hear. *Anything.*"

Because that was the trick of it—he'd learned this much from the reports he'd falsified, from the nights he'd spent reading what the Inquisition had gathered and pretended not to understand. The god-light got inside you through hope. A man in the ranks would hear his dead wife's voice in that choir-note, would see his burned village green and whole in the glow, and he'd step off the wall to walk to it. Two of Ostenmarch's crossbowmen had done exactly that during the skirmish at the ford, walking down off the embankment into the enemy's arms, smiling.

So Dain had given the order no commander should have to give, and hated himself cleanly and efficiently for it, the way he'd practiced: *if any man steps toward the light, cut him down before he reaches the parapet.*

The army reached the ditch at noon.

The glassblowers earned their guild's legend in the first hour. Their fire-oil bottles arced down into the packed ranks and bloomed orange, and for a while the silent march became something else—men burning, men screaming, the choir-note stuttering as if the thing behind the curtains found pain interesting. The crossbows worked in relays. The pike levies braced. Twice the assault broke against the gate like water and flowed back, and the defenders on the wall cheered themselves hoarse, and Dain did not cheer, because he had counted the litters and none of the curtains had opened.

In the third hour, one did.

He saw it from the gate tower: a litter set down in the shadow of the siege line, its bearers kneeling, its curtain drawn back by hands that were not quite hands anymore. What came out was man-shaped and larger than a man, and it was *wrong* the way a portrait is wrong—features assembled from too many faces, skin with the gray-gold light moving underneath it like blood under paper. It walked to the wall alone. Arrows went into it and stayed there and did not matter.

It put one hand flat against the river-smoothed stones.

The wall did not crack. It *melted*—a span of ten feet slumping like wax left in sun, defenders sliding down with it, and through the gap came the silent ranks at last, and the battle stopped being a defense and became arithmetic.

Dain did the arithmetic fast. The gap had to be held for one reason only, and the reason was climbing the far hill behind the city with a gray-robed girl and a storm-caller who carried the end of the world in a lead-lined case: the tower of the lighthouse temple needed time. Time was the only currency left in Vell, and someone had to pay.

"Glassblowers!" he roared. "With me! Everyone else—*hold the line!*"

He went down into the breach with four hundred men and a borrowed sword, into the smoke and the wrong sweet singing, and the last coherent thought he had room for was not of August, not of the purge, not even of the village he had burned. It was absurdly small: *I never did learn to pray properly. Sera tried to teach me. I hope somebody says one over me anyway.*

Then the god-thing turned its assembled face toward him, and Dain Corvess, disgraced inquisitor, traitor to two masters, keeper of one true report, set his feet in the breach of the city he had once helped make afraid, and paid.

* * *

**AUGUST**

From the tower of the lighthouse temple, August watched the breach swallow the Salt Gate and knew, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the plan had died at noon.

They could see the fighting only as light and smoke and a churn of movement where the streets met the wall, but they could feel the rest. The storm-blood heard the city the way a mother hears a house at night—every cry catalogued, every collapse measured. And under it all, growing, the gray-gold presence: Malvane himself, still on the hill, still waiting, letting his harvested thing open the city the way a man lets a dog open a door before he enters.

"August." Sera, breathless, soot-streaked, a militia cloak over her robes and blood on her sleeve that she hadn't mentioned. She'd run the whole way from the wall. "The gate's gone. Dain took the glassblowers into the breach and—" Her voice caught. "Nobody's come back out."

August closed their eyes. Opened them. "How long can the streets hold?"

"An hour. Maybe less. They're not trying to win, August, they're—they're herding. Pushing everyone toward the harbor quarter, away from here." She swallowed. "Toward the tower. He knows where you are. He's known since the ford."

Of course he had. You did not hunt a storm-caller across five cities without learning to listen for the weather in her bones.

August looked at the lead-lined case at their feet, and at the shard inside it when they opened the lid—a splinter of the First Storm, black glass shot through with moving light, humming against the palm like a trapped bird. Vessa's voice lived somewhere in it, or had; the goddess was mostly silence now, and what remained of her had spent itself in warnings.

*When you call it*, she had said, in the drowned temple, in the hollow garden, *it will take everything you remember. Every face. Every name. You will stand in the rain afterward and not know why the people around you are crying.*

"I have to go up," August said.

"I know." Sera's chin came up, and August saw her doing the thing she always did—refusing the easy grief, refusing to let a hard moment be simple. "And I'm coming with you."

"No."

"You don't get to spend yourself alone. That's not how any of this works. I carried prayers for a goddess nobody remembered; do you think I don't know what it costs to be the last one who believes?" Her hands were shaking. Her voice wasn't. "You'll need someone to say your name after. Someone has to be standing there who knows who you were, or the price doesn't mean anything—it's just erasure. Let it mean something."

August wanted, with a violence that frightened them, to say yes. And because they wanted it, they said, "Someone has to carry the case if I fall on the stairs. Someone has to live to tell the free cities what actually happened here, since apparently that job falls to people like you and Dain." They closed the lid. "Stay below the tower, Sera. In the temple. When the sky tears open, get under stone."

"And when you come down and don't know my face?"

The question landed like a knife laid flat against skin—not cutting yet, just resting there, patient.

"Then you'll tell me," August said. "You're good at telling stories. Start practicing."

Sera looked at them for a long moment, and whatever she saw in August's face made her stop arguing, which was more frightening than any argument. She nodded once. Then the tower shuddered, dust sifting from the joints of the ancient stones, and the shouting in the streets below changed key—from resistance to rout—and there was no more time for anything.

August climbed.

* * *

**SERA**

The temple stairs were behind her and the burning streets were everywhere else, and Sera ran into them anyway.

She had meant to stay. She had sworn it, standing in the temple's nave with the shard-case heavy in her arms because August had thrust it at her at the last moment—*in case I fall on the stairs*—and she had meant to stay. Then the western door had burst in with the smoke behind it, and through the smoke came militiamen running, and behind the militiamen came the silent ones, and behind the silent ones came the light.

She had taken the case and gone out the harbor door, because the case could not be taken, and because August was at the top of the tower and the tower was suddenly on the wrong side of a burning city.

Vell burned the way a beautiful thing burns—thoroughly, brightly, without any dignity at all. The glassblowers' quarter went up like a festival, every window pouring colored fire. The harbor warehouses caught in a chain of soft concussions that Sera felt through the soles of her feet. People ran past her in both directions, some carrying children, some carrying nothing, one old woman carrying a cat that had clearly decided death was preferable and was making its opinion known. Nobody questioned the gray-robed girl with the lead box. In a burning city, everyone was carrying something.

The choir-note came again, closer now, and Sera felt it try to get inside her—the way it had at the ford, offering her convent, her novices, the smell of bread in the refectory, her whole small lost life rebuilt in gold light. She did what she had taught herself to do. She said Vessa's name—not a prayer, just the name, like a handhold on a cliff face—and the false sweetness slid off her.

*Hold on*, she told the goddess in the case, or the case itself, or herself. *We're not finished.*

The street to the tower square was blocked. Of course it was. A harvest-wagon burned sideways across it, and beyond it the silent ranks moved in their terrible unhurried file, herding the fleeing toward the harbor. Sera pressed into an alley, then another, mapping the city the way she'd mapped the catacombs under it—by dead reckoning and stubbornness. The heat came in waves. Somewhere close, a building's roof gave way with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

She came out of the alleys into the tower square from the north, from the direction no one was watching, because no one was watching anything but the tower itself.

August stood at the top of it, on the old beacon platform, silhouetted against a sky that had begun to do something skies should not do. Clouds were arriving from every horizon at once, spiraling, stacking, turning the afternoon the color of a bruise, and lightning moved inside the cloud-mass like thoughts behind a forehead. Below the tower, filling the square, advancing up the temple steps with the patience of tide, came the silent ranks—and before them, parting them like a shepherd parts dogs, walked the assembled-faced thing from the breach, and behind *it*, at last, entering his own victory at a walk, came a figure in robes of gray and gold whose outline hurt to look at directly.

Malvane. Half-divine and rotting, ambition curdled into hunger, wearing his apotheosis like a coat two sizes too large for what remained of the man.

Sera looked at the stairs—three flights of open stone between her and the platform, every inch of them covered—and at the case in her arms, and at the sky.

"Dain," she whispered, because he had taught her the word for this, the inquisitor's word for a moment when every option is a loss and you simply choose which loss you can survive: *triage*. "Wherever you are. I hope somebody's saying a prayer over you. Here's mine."

She shifted the case to her hip, pulled her hood up against the ash, and started for the stairs at a dead run, straight into the light.

Behind her, above her, the First Storm opened its eye.
