## 17. The First Storm

The tower of Saint Verity's had no business still standing. It was the oldest thing in Vell—older than the walls, older than the city's name, a needle of black stone raised by hands that predated every god the free cities remembered. August climbed it through smoke and falling ash, rung by rusted rung of an iron stair that sang under their boots like a struck wire.

Below, Vell burned in patches. Malvane's siege engines had broken the eastern wall at dusk, and now his half-divine soldiers moved through the streets with light leaking out of their seams, their eyes glowing like coals behind glass. The ragged defense—the free cities' last army, Dain's word for it, and Dain was dead—had folded like wet paper. August had watched the gate fall from a rooftop two streets away. Had watched Dain Corvess plant himself in the breach with a stolen standard and a sword he barely knew how to use anymore, and hold it long enough for three hundred people to run.

He hadn't come back out.

August didn't let themselves think about it. Thinking about it would crack them open before they reached the top, and they needed to be whole—or as whole as a person could be who was about to be unmade.

The shard hung at their throat on a cord of braided hair. Sera's hair. She'd cut it herself on the road from the drowned temple, grinning through a split lip, saying, *If you're going to carry a goddess around your neck, she should at least smell like someone who likes you.* The shard was warm now. It had been warm for hours, warming as the sky above Vell darkened in ways that had nothing to do with night.

Because the sky was tearing.

August saw it clearly from the tower's shoulder: a seam of white-gold light splitting the clouds directly over Malvane's command position, wide as a river, and through the seam came things that were not quite lightning and not quite wings. Malvane was ascending in real time. August could feel it in their teeth, in the old scar tissue along their palms where the storm-blood ran closest to the skin—a great dragging pull, like the tide going out before a tsunami, all the loose divinity in the world being sucked toward one hungry man.

*Hurry,* said the shard, or the thing inside the shard. Vessa's voice was thinner than it had been at the drowned temple, worn down to a whisper of a whisper. *Hurry, little heir. He is drinking the sky.*

"I'm hurrying," August muttered, and took the last flight of stairs three at a time.

The platform at the top was bare except for wind. Vell spread out beneath them, burning and beautiful, the city that had taken them in when no one else would have, the city that had outlawed the very blood in their veins and sheltered them anyway because laws in Vell were more suggestion than covenant. Five years of luck-knots and fever-charms. Five years of small magic, safe magic, magic shrunk down until it fit inside a life.

Five years of pretending the storm wasn't still there, curled at the base of their spine, dreaming.

August walked to the center of the platform and looked up into the torn sky.

Every lesson they'd ever learned screamed at them not to do this. You don't call lightning while standing on the highest point in a city. You don't open yourself to power this size without anchors, without circles, without a dozen acolytes to bleed for you if it goes wrong. And this wasn't lightning. This was the First Storm—the one that had existed before the world had edges, the one the gods had used on each other in the war that taught mortals to fear prayer, the one that had been buried in Vessa's garden when the goddess chose surrender over victory.

The storm could only be called by someone willing to be unmade by it.

Vessa had said it plainly, in her drowned temple, her voice coming up through water that shouldn't have been there: *It will take everything you are. Every face you know. Every name you love. You will stand in the aftermath holding nothing but the fact that you once held something.* And August had said yes anyway, because Malvane was harvesting villages and wearing god-flesh, because Sera had nearly died trying to spare them this, because some prices you pay so other people don't have to.

But knowing the price and standing at the counter were different things.

August closed their eyes. Found the storm where it always lived—not in the sky, not in the shard, but in the memory of rain on a roof in a village that no longer existed, in the sound their mother made when she laughed, in the first time they'd ever called thunder and felt the world answer like a dog hearing its name.

"Vessa," they said aloud. "Last request."

*I am listening.*

"When it's done—if anything of me is left—don't tell me what I paid. Let me be happy being empty."

A pause. The wind grieved around the tower.

*That,* said the dying goddess, *is the cruelest kindness anyone has ever asked of me. Yes.*

August opened their hands.

* * *

Calling the First Storm was not like calling ordinary weather. Ordinary weather you summoned; this you surrendered to. August reached down into themselves, past five years of careful smallness, past the exile and the market stall and the charm-knots, down to the original wound—the day the storm had first answered them, eight years old, standing in a flooded field with their arms out and their heart hammering *again, again, again*—and they stopped holding it back.

They stopped holding *anything* back.

The shard ignited against their sternum. Light poured off it in sheets, and August heard Vessa scream—not in pain, in release—as the last of her poured out of the crystal and into them, a goddess spending her final coin on one mortal's gamble. Power hit August like the sea hitting a seawall. Their feet left the stones. Their hair lifted. Somewhere below, the fighting stuttered as both armies looked up.

"FIRST STORM," August said, and their voice was not only their voice—it was thunder given grammar, it was the sky remembering its oldest word. "I AM THE LAST OF THE STORM-BLOOD. I PAY THE PRICE. COME AND TAKE IT."

The sky answered.

It did not come down so much as arrive—everywhere at once, a pressure change so vast that ears bled across three districts, a darkness deeper than night rolling over the tear in heaven like a hand closing over a wound. The First Storm had a color August had no name for, a green-black-violet that the eye kept trying to file elsewhere. It had a sound underneath its sound, a subsonic voice that spoke directly to bone.

And it had attention. That was the worst of it. The storm *noticed* August—turned its vast regard upon the small figure hanging in the air above the tower—and August felt like a candle flame being studied by the ocean.

*You called,* it said, in a voice that was every voice August had ever lost. *You knew the cost.*

"I know the cost."

*Then hold nothing back. Not even the holding.*

Malvane rose to meet it.

He came up out of his command camp trailing gold fire, his body already half-transfigured—too tall, joints wrong, his skin luminous and rotting at the edges both at once, divinity and decay in a body that couldn't house either. His face was still human enough to show fury. "THIEF," he roared, and his voice shook shingles loose in the streets below. "That power is MINE. I bled for it. I harvested a GOD."

"You harvested corpses," August said, and the storm spoke with them, doubling the words across the whole sky. "There's a difference."

He struck first. Of course he did—he'd built his whole ascension on taking what others carried. A lance of stolen sunfire crossed the distance between them, and August didn't dodge; they opened, and the First Storm drank it. Malvane's own attack went into the vast green-black dark and did not come back, and for one heartbeat the usurper's glowing face showed something ancient and human: fear.

Then they fell on each other, and Vell's sky became the old war again.

* * *

Fighting as the storm's fist was not fighting. It was *being fought with*. August had no sense of their body anymore—they were everywhere the storm was, a will threaded through wind and pressure and light. When Malvane hurled a handful of harvested prayers—screaming ghosts of answered wishes, weaponized faith—the storm unwove them midair, and August felt each one go, little lives, little hopes, dissolving clean.

When Malvane seized a spire of the tower and ripped it free to throw, the storm caught it and returned it, and the usurper learned what it was to be struck by his own theft.

But he was strong. Gods, he was strong. Half-divine and getting more so by the minute, drinking the ambient terror of a burning city, feeding on the prayers of people who thought the end had come and were praying to *anything*. Each prayer made him brighter. Each brightening made him less Malvane and more the thing he'd been building: a god-shaped hunger wearing a man's grudges.

"You don't understand what I'm offering!" he shouted across the storm-wracked dark, and there was real conviction in it, which was the most horrifying thing yet. "The gods abandoned you! They let the world rot! I am the answer to every unanswered prayer—I am what comes AFTER the gods! Join me, Thackeray! Your line was made to serve the divine. Serve the new one!"

"The gods began the war," August said. "I know. I've seen it." The Hollow Garden's truth sat in them like swallowed glass. "And you're not the answer to it. You're just the same hunger with a newer face."

The storm gathered itself. August felt it gathering—felt the whole weight of the primordial dark pulling back like a wave retreating before it breaks, and understood with total clarity what was coming, and that they would be at its center, and that this was the part where the price began.

"VESSA'S HEIR," the storm said, and its voice was almost gentle, which broke August's heart. "IT IS TIME. LET GO OF WHAT YOU ARE SO THAT YOU MAY BE AN INSTRUMENT."

Below, tiny as an ant, a figure was climbing the tower's outer stair. Sera. August could see the shard-light reflected in her upturned face—no. The shard was here, against their chest. What she carried was the memory-charm, the little knot of braided thread pressed to her lips as she climbed, screaming words the wind stole.

*One memory survives,* she'd told him in the valley. *Choose well. Choose—*

"Don't watch," August whispered, though she couldn't hear. "Please don't watch."

Then they let go of everything.

* * *

Unmaking, it turned out, happened in reverse order of importance. The storm took the trivia first, and it took it gently, the way snow takes a footprint.

August felt their knowledge of charm-knot patterns go—the seven-fold luck weave, the fever-braid, gone like breath off a window. Then smaller things. The taste of Vell's night-market wine. The name of the innkeeper who'd never asked questions. Which stair creaked. Where they'd hidden a spare key. An entire afternoon spent learning to juggle, wasted and warm, dissolved.

*This is fine,* August told themselves, hanging in the heart of the storm while it wound itself around Malvane like a fist around a coal. *This is the cheap part.*

The storm struck Malvane fully, and the world went white-green-black, and in the roar August heard the usurper scream—not with a voice but with everything he'd stolen, a thousand harvested souls crying out of him at once as the First Storm unwove his apotheosis thread by thread. God-flesh boiled away. Stolen divinity scattered like startled birds, freed, fleeing home to the quiet places. Malvane shrank as he died, shrinking from monument back to monument's ambition back to man, and the last thing visible of him was his face, human again, astonished, mouthing *but I was going to fix everything* before the storm took even that.

And then the storm turned to August, and began collecting the debt.

Names went next. That was how August knew the cheap part was over. The innkeeper's name had been trivia; these were *names*, load-bearing ones. Dain Corvess—the gate, the standard, the man who'd knelt in the mud and confessed his shame and offered his knowledge and died holding a door—went first, sliding out of August like a splinter, leaving not grief but a strange smooth absence, a socket with nothing in it. August grabbed for it and their hands closed on fog.

"No," they said. "No, wait, I just got him back, we were—we were *going to be*—"

The storm did not pause. It was not cruel. That was somehow worse. It simply continued, thorough as erosion.

Sera.

The name held longer than any of them. August felt the storm test it and move on, test it and move on, and in those repetitions August lived a lifetime: the shrine, the dying acolyte who claimed a goddess had spoken their true name, the road to Ostenmarch, hedge-magic lessons by firelight, the argument in the valley, the charm of braided hair, *choose well, choose—* They held onto it with everything left, which was not much, which was less every second.

*You may keep one,* the storm said. *One. The bargain allows it. Choose.*

One. Out of everything. One single thing to carry into an empty life.

August's mind, fraying, flailing, reached for faces. Their mother's laugh—that was gone already, they realized, gone somewhere in the last minute, and they couldn't even mourn it properly because mourning required the memory of it. Dain. The village. The rain on the roof. All of it dissolving, all of it fog, all of it fog except—

Except one moment, burning brighter as everything else dimmed. Not a face. Not a scene. Something smaller and stranger: a voice in the dark of a ruined shrine, a dying girl's cracked whisper speaking a name August had never dared say aloud, their true name, the storm-name, spoken like it was a good thing. Like it was a *gift*. The feeling of hearing it. The feeling of being, for one instant, entirely and correctly named.

Not a person. But a person had made it. And a person would still be standing there tomorrow, and the day after, visiting a stranger in a garden.

*Choose,* said the storm, and the sky was already clearing, and August was already forgetting that they were forgetting.

"That one," August said—or tried to say; they weren't sure the words ever made it out. "Keep me that one."

The storm took the rest.

* * *

Rain fell on Vell. Real rain, ordinary rain, the first honest weather the city had seen in days, putting out fires street by street with a patience older than any god. The torn seam in the sky knit itself closed. The half-divine soldiers collapsed in the streets like puppets with cut strings, weeping, human again, alive—which was perhaps the strangest mercy of all.

On the highest tower of Saint Verity's, a body lay on the black stones, breathing.

After a long time, it stirred. Sat up. Looked at its hands—scarred palms, callused fingers, hands that had made things, surely, hands that knew knots of some kind. Looked at the burning-then-drowning city below with no recognition at all and no fear either, just the flat clean wonder of a newborn eye.

At its throat hung a crystal shard, cold now, empty, dead as sea glass. Around its wrist was tied a small braided charm of dark hair, and the fingers found it without instruction and held on.

The person stood, swayed, and looked east, where the clouds were breaking apart around a washed and silver dawn. Something in the far distance rumbled—just thunder, only thunder, the tail of a storm that had already forgotten them both.

The person smiled at it, for no reason they could have named.

And below, on the tower stair, a young woman with a split lip and ash in her hair stood frozen with her hand over her mouth, watching, waiting, carrying the whole story alone.
