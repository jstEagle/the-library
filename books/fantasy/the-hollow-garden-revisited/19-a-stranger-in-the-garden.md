## 19. A Stranger in the Garden

The temple had drowned a thousand years ago and no one had bothered to tell the stones.

August learned this by touch before they learned it any other way—kneeling in the black mud where the flooded nave had been, fingers closing around something smooth and rounded. A paving stone, worn concave in its center, as if generations of knees had pressed their prayers into it. They set it aside with the others they'd pried loose that morning, arranging them in a careful row along the dry lip of the sunken floor, because a garden needed paths and paths needed stones and this was a problem with an answer, which was more than August could say for most of their life.

Most of their life was a blank page with one line written on it.

*You were loved,* the woman named Sera had told them, the first morning, when August woke on a cot in a stranger's house with mud under their fingernails and nothing behind their eyes but fog. *That's all you need to know to start.*

It wasn't, of course. But it was enough to plant things.

August stood, knees cracking, and surveyed the drowned temple as it slowly became something else. The roofless walls still stood in places, green-slicked granite rising from shallow water like the ribs of some patient beast. Rainwater had pooled in the nave for centuries, thick with silt and lily roots and the pale bones of fish that had found their way in through channels no map remembered. But the eastern end—the apse, Sera called it, using words from her old life—stood above the waterline, and there the light came down through the broken vault in long dusty columns, and there August had begun.

They had carried soil up from the floodplain in baskets, trip after trip, day after day, until the apse held two feet of good dark earth. They had cleared the silt from the drainage channels so the water ran where they wanted it instead of where it pleased. They had planted what the season allowed: beans and squash, yes, but also marigolds and feverfew and a row of sunflowers along the south wall that leaned toward the light like congregants toward a sermon.

An old woman from the village had watched them work for three days before she finally spoke.

"You're building a church," she'd said, not unkindly.

"I'm building a garden."

"Same thing, maybe." She'd shrugged. "Depends what you're praying to."

August hadn't answered, because they didn't know, and because the question had lodged somewhere under their ribs like a splinter they couldn't find the edge of. Praying. The word meant nothing to them and everything at once, the way certain other words did. *Storm.* *Vell.* *Malvane.* Names from a war they'd apparently ended, spoken to them gently by Sera and Dain in the first bewildered weeks, and every name landed like a stone dropped into deep water—they saw the ripple, felt the weight sink, but never touched bottom.

Dain had come once, in those early days, standing awkwardly at the garden's edge with his arm still bound from Vell's gate. He'd looked at August for a long time with an expression August couldn't read—grief wearing courtesy like a mask—and said only, "You look better without the lightning."

Then he'd left, and Sera had told him never to come back, and August had been too busy being confused to ask why.

Now, six weeks into the work, August wiped their hands on their trousers and climbed out of the nave onto the dry flagstones, where the morning had warmed enough to make the whole ruin smell of wet stone and green growth. Somewhere beyond the western wall, the river muttered. Birds nested in the collapsed vault. It was, by any honest measure, beautiful.

It was also full of ghosts August couldn't see, and some mornings the not-seeing hurt worse than seeing would have.

There was no reason for that. That was the strange part. Grief without a face, homesickness without a home. Some mornings August woke with their heart hammering and the taste of ozone on their tongue, and lay staring at the ceiling of the cottage Sera had rented them, waiting for a memory that never came, until the feeling drained away like water through the temple's old channels and left them empty and calm and faintly ashamed, though of what, they couldn't say.

Sera arrived at midmorning, as she did every day, coming down the overgrown processional road with a basket on her hip and her hood pushed back. She was small and quick and serious, with ink-stained fingers and the habit of watching August when she thought August wasn't looking—a look August had learned to recognize even if they couldn't interpret it. It was the look people gave a fire that had burned down to embers: relief and vigilance in equal measure.

"You've moved the stones again," she said, setting down her basket.

"They fit better on the north path. The ground slopes wrong on the east side; rain will pool against the wall and rot the bean roots." August hesitated. "Is that right? It sounds right."

"It's right." Something crossed her face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "You always knew ground. Slopes, drainage, where water wanted to go. You used to—" She stopped herself, the way she stopped herself a dozen times a day, mid-sentence, mid-memory, redirecting like a cart horse refusing a gate. "Never mind. I brought bread."

"Sera." August came closer, brushing dirt from their palms. "You do that constantly. Start to tell me something and swallow it. I'm not going to break."

"I know you're not."

"Then why?"

She busied herself unwrapping the bread, tearing off a piece, handing it over. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't quite. "Because some things are heavier than bread, and you asked me once—" A breath. "You told me, before, that if you ever forgot, I should let you be new. That being new was the whole point. And I promised I'd try. So I'm trying. Badly. But trying."

August took the bread and ate it and didn't push, because pushing felt like kicking a door that someone had locked for a reason—and yet.

"And if I changed my mind?" they said quietly. "If I decided I'd rather be heavy?"

Sera looked at them then, fully, and August saw that her eyes were red-rimmed, that she'd been crying recently or often or both, and that she smiled anyway, the way people smile at funerals when they mean it.

"Then I'd tell you everything," she said. "Every terrible, wonderful thing. And you'd hate me for a month and thank me after. But not today. Today you planted sunflowers, and today you're new, and I think—" Her voice caught and steadied. "I think today you get to just be the person who planted sunflowers."

* * *

The afternoon went to work, and work was the only prayer August understood.

They hauled water from the river in yoked buckets, two trips for each bed, muscles burning in a way that felt honest. They turned compost that smelled richly of decay becoming food. They found, half-buried in the apse wall, a carved frieze they'd been carefully ignoring for weeks—figures in procession, faces weathered blank, and at the center a woman holding what might have been flowers or might have been lightning—and they made a decision they'd been circling for days.

They planted a rosemary bush directly in front of it.

"There," August said aloud, tamping the soil. "Let her watch something grow."

The goddess—if that's who the carved woman was; Sera had mentioned a name once, *Vessa*, and the name had slid off August's mind like rain off oilcloth—could watch the beans climb and the bees work and the small ordinary miracle of things taking root in a place built for worship and drowned by time. If gods existed, surely this was the worship they'd actually want. Not kneeling. Gardening.

Unless gods were the reason for the hollow feeling.

It came on them sometimes in the late afternoon, when the light went gold and long across the water. A sense of having been larger once. Of hands that had held something enormous and set it down, and now didn't remember what it was, only the shape of the ache where it had rested. August would stand in the shallows of the old nave with the water cold around their ankles and feel the ache like a missing tooth—the tongue returning and returning to the gap.

Today the feeling came with company. Today, standing knee-deep among the drowned columns, August heard thunder.

Far away, past the hills, past the horizon, a sound like a great door rolling shut. The sky to the west was clear. The thunder came anyway, once, twice, and then a third time, softer, almost polite, and August's body reacted before their mind could object: their hands came up, palms open, fingers spread, in a gesture they had absolutely never been taught.

The air tasted of ozone. The hairs on their arms lifted. For one impossible heartbeat, August felt the storm—not the distant thunder but *storms*, plural, all of them, everywhere, a vast sleeping animal whose breathing they could hear—and felt that it knew them. Felt that it had missed them. Felt, with a certainty that bypassed every rational objection, that if they spoke a single word—

A fish broke the surface. The moment shattered. Their hands dropped.

August stood in the cold water shaking, heart slamming, and did not speak the word, mostly because they didn't know it, and partly, in some chamber of themselves deeper than memory, because they did.

When Sera found them twenty minutes later, sitting on the apse steps wringing out their boots, she read their face in an instant. She'd gotten good at that. Or she'd always been good at it. There was no way to know.

"Thunder," August said. "From a clear sky. And my hands did something."

Sera sat down beside them. She didn't touch them, though her hand hovered near theirs for a moment before withdrawing, a moth deciding against a flame. "What did they do?"

"Opened. Like I was catching rain." August laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's mad, isn't it? I'm a gardener. I grow beans. Why would my hands want to catch a storm?"

For a long moment Sera said nothing. August watched her decide something—watched the internal argument move across her face like weather—and braced for another swallowed sentence.

Instead she said, "Because they used to. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before you gave it up. All of it. Everything you were." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, made herself small in a way that seemed practiced. "You paid an enormous price, August. The largest price there is. And the world is better for it—I need you to know that, the *world is better*—but the price doesn't stop existing just because you can't remember paying it. Your body remembers. Bodies are stubborn that way."

"The storm," August said slowly. "I ended a war with a storm."

"You ended a war with yourself, mostly. The storm was just how it came out." She glanced west, where the sky stayed innocently blue. "And it's over. Whatever that was just now—it's an echo. Echoes fade."

"Do they?"

"Yes," Sera said, with such fierce certainty that August understood immediately it was a wish dressed as a fact, and loved her a little for it, and was frightened a little too.

*Loved her a little.* The thought arrived without permission and settled in comfortably, as if it had always lived there. Which was absurd. She was a stranger. A kind stranger, a daily stranger, a stranger who brought bread and knew about drainage and cried when she thought August wasn't looking—but a stranger. You didn't love strangers.

Except apparently you could trust them completely, instantly, against all evidence, and August had since the first moment of waking, and when they'd tried to examine that trust it had held firm like bedrock under topsoil, and perhaps that was its own kind of love, or the seed of it, or the grave of something that had come before.

"Sera," August said. "Why do you come every day? Truly. Not the bread answer."

She was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence. Then: "Because someone should witness what you're building. Because you shouldn't be alone with the echoes. Because—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "Because I made a promise to someone I loved very much, and keeping it is the only way I know to keep loving them, and I'm aware that makes no sense to you, and I'm sorry, and I'll bring bread tomorrow too."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in six weeks," August said. "Which isn't saying much, given the competition. But it counts."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and bright—and for a moment the whole ruined temple felt less like a grave and more like a beginning.

* * *

Evening came down soft. Sera left at dusk, as she always did, walking back up the processional road toward the village, and August stood in the gateway of the drowned temple and watched her go, and felt the familiar double vision: a stranger's silhouette against a stranger's sunset, and underneath it, faint as a watermark, the outline of someone irreplaceable.

They went back inside and lit the small lamp they kept in the apse and did the last rounds. Beans staked. Slugs salted. The rosemary watered. The carved goddess watching over it all with her blank weathered face and her flowers-or-lightning.

At the edge of the garden, where the old temple floor gave way to wild ground, August had left one patch untouched—a square of original mud, silted and weedy, that they'd fenced off with string on the first week without knowing why. Sera had seen it and gone very quiet and never asked them to dig it. Tonight August crouched at its border and studied it, as they did most nights.

Something was buried there. They were nearly sure. Not treasure—something worse and better. A box, maybe. Papers. An offering. Every instinct they possessed said *leave it*, and every instinct also whispered that the instinct itself was a message from a person they used to be, addressed forward through the erasure, like a note tucked into a coat pocket: *Don't. Not yet. Maybe not ever.*

Or maybe it was just mud.

August pressed one palm flat against the cool earth, the way you'd check a fever, and felt nothing but damp and dark and the slow patient stirrings of seeds already in the soil, waiting for spring, waiting for a reason.

"Whatever you are," they murmured to the buried thing, to the blank-faced goddess, to the echo of thunder rolling somewhere beyond the hills, "you can wait. I'm planting a garden."

The wind moved through the broken vault. Far to the west, so distant it might have been imagination, the sky flickered once—heat lightning, probably, or a god turning over in sleep—and went dark again.

In the morning there would be weeds, and water to haul, and bread arriving with a woman whose name August trusted more than their own reflection. In the morning the work would continue, which was the same as saying the healing would, which was the same as saying the living would.

But tonight, for just a moment, before sleep, August knelt in the doorway of the drowned temple with dirt under their nails and an ache in their chest shaped like everything they'd lost, and they whispered the only prayer they knew:

*Let what I planted outlive what I forgot.*

Somewhere far away, something old and tired and kind heard them—and began, at last, to let go.
