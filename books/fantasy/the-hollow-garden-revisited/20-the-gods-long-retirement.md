## 20. The Gods' Long Retirement

The first god to leave was a river.

Sera heard about it from the ferryman who worked the Graywater crossing below Vell's lower quarter—a broad, patient man named Osk who had once, before the war, paid a copper each dawn to the spirit of his boat. He came to her door on a morning thick with river fog and stood there wringing his cap in his hands like a man rehearsing bad news.

"It went in the night," he said. "The old one. The voice under the water. I've talked to it thirty years, Keeper, and last night I said my thanks like always, and there was nothing back. Just water." He looked at her with an expression she had come to know well over the past months—not grief exactly, and not fear, but the particular loneliness of a man whose conversation partner has moved away without leaving an address. "Did I do something wrong?"

"No," Sera said, and found that she meant it. "You did everything right. It isn't punishment, Osk. It's retirement."

He turned the word over like a coin of doubtful minting. "Gods retire?"

"These ones do."

She made him tea because he looked like a man who needed something warm to hold, and while he drank it she told him what she had learned in the drowned temple, in the weeks after the storm: that Malvane's harvest had not merely fed on the old gods but *bound* them, stitched their dying flesh into his ascension until they could not fade even when fading was all they wanted. That August's storm had torn those stitches out. And that now—freed, unharvested, unneeded—the gods were doing the thing gods had perhaps always dreamed of doing, if anyone had ever thought to ask them.

They were going quiet. Not dying. *Quieting.* Slipping out of shrines and riverbeds and thunderheads and into the world's small places—the hush under snowfall, the pause between lightning and sound, the moment when a child stops crying because someone picked them up. They were becoming less like voices and more like weather. Less like masters and more like mercy.

Osk listened to all this, turning his cap. "So I won't hear him again."

"You might feel him," Sera said. "On a good crossing. When the current helps you along for no reason you can name."

The ferryman nodded slowly, and something in his shoulders loosened. Before he left he pressed a copper into her hand—the same offering he'd given the river for thirty years—and said, "For the work, then. Someone ought to keep paying."

That was how Sera learned what her new office actually was. Not a priestess. The gods didn't want worship anymore; she suspected they never truly had, or had grown out of it the way children grow out of being carried. What they wanted—what Vessa had wanted, whispering through the shard in those last bright terrible days—was *custody*. Someone to tend the small prayers people still insisted on making. Someone to receive them, weigh them, answer the ones that could be answered by human hands, and hold gently the ones that couldn't.

Someone to be, in short, what the gods had been, except mortal, and present, and required to do the actual work.

Vell's council had fought her on the license for three months. Magic was outlawed; faith had been suspect since the purge; and here came a former acolyte of a forgotten goddess asking for official sanction to run a shrine. But the siege had changed the arithmetic of the city. People remembered the burning streets. They remembered Dain Corvess holding the gate alone for eleven minutes against things that used to be men, and they remembered that he had done it so a storm-caller could climb a tower and pay a price no soldier should have to name. When Sera stood before the council in her plain gray robe and said, *I am asking to keep prayers, not to cast spells*, the vote wasn't close.

She kept the copper from Osk in a dish on the shrine's windowsill. By midsummer there were forty-seven coppers in the dish, and she counted them every evening the way other treasurers counted gold, because each one meant a person who had decided the quiet was worth tending.

* * *

The shrine itself had been a granary once, then a tannery, then nothing much—a stone building in the lower quarter with a sagging roof and a door that stuck unless you lifted the handle as you pushed. Sera had chosen it deliberately. Grand temples invited grand expectations, and grand expectations were precisely what the old gods were retiring from. She wanted a place where a person could come in off the street smelling of fish guts or dye vats and not feel the need to apologize for it.

She swept the floor every morning. She kept a fire going in the cold months. She hung no images of Vessa—the goddess had asked for none—but above the hearth she set the only relic the temple had surrendered willingly: a single dried flower from the hollow garden, brown and papery and impossibly intact after a year, which botanists from the university had offered twice to buy and which Sera had refused twice with a politeness that fooled no one.

People came. That was the part that still astonished her, a year on. They came at odd hours, mostly—dawn and dusk, the hinge-times—and they rarely stayed long. A dyer whose husband drank. A boy who couldn't sleep since the siege. An old woman who came every ninth day to say thank you for something she refused to specify, on the grounds that gratitude named aloud lost half its strength. Sera listened. Sometimes she gave advice, which people took or didn't. Sometimes she gave bread, which everyone took. And sometimes—this was the work within the work—she simply received the prayer, wrote it in her ledger in her careful acolyte's hand, and let the person watch her do it, because she had learned that half of what prayer had ever provided was the experience of being *heard*.

The ledger filled. She was on her third volume now. Late at night she would read back through the entries and find the shape of a city in them: its fears, its stubbornnesses, its small unreasonable hopes. *Let my daughter's fever break. Let me find work before winter. Let me stop dreaming about the tower. Let me forgive my brother, or failing that, let me stop hating him, whichever is easier.*

Once, near the front of the second volume, in handwriting shakier than her own because her hands had been burned and bandaged at the time, there was an entry that read: *Let August live. Nothing else matters. Take anything else.*

Sera had written it herself, kneeling in the ash outside the tower, while the First Storm still rang in the stones. She had meant *take anything else* literally. She had been prepared, in that moment, to trade every memory she owned, every future she'd imagined, for the sound of August breathing.

The gods had taken neither. They had taken August's memories instead, and left Sera whole, and she had spent a full season angry about that arithmetic before understanding that no one had taken anything. August had *paid*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and she still had to remind herself of it on the days the unfairness rose in her throat like bile.

August lived. That was the entry's answer, and the only one that mattered, and every morning when she walked to the garden she repeated it to herself like a lesson she refused to unlearn.

* * *

The garden sat where the drowned temple had been, in the low valley south of the city that maps still labeled the Hollow Garden though nothing about it was hollow anymore. August had gone out there six weeks after the storm—walking, alone, carrying a spade borrowed from a neighbor whose name August did not remember borrowing it from—and had begun, without any stated plan, to dig.

By autumn there were beds. By spring there were rows of beans and cabbages and, in the corner nearest the ruined altar-stone, flowers: marigold, borage, a straggling attempt at roses that August defended fiercely against all criticism. The blight that had poisoned the valley for a thousand years was gone—pulled out root and branch by the First Storm, or by Vessa's last breath, or by some transaction between the two that no living soul could reconstruct—and the soil underneath was black and generous and smelled the way soil is supposed to smell.

Sera visited daily. She told herself it was stewardship: the keeper of small prayers ought to keep an eye on the one place where a god had actually lived. But honesty, which she practiced in her ledger and tried to practice everywhere else, compelled her to admit the plainer truth. She went because August was there. Because August smiled at her across the bean rows with the open, unguarded smile of a person meeting a stranger they inexplicably trusted, and that smile was both the sweetest and cruelest thing in Sera's world.

"Your roses are dying," she called one morning, setting down her basket on the wall.

"They're *resting*," August called back, straightening up with dirt to the elbows and sun in their hair. "There's a difference. Everything that comes back does it slowly. You'd know—you're the expert on slow returns."

It was August's joke, not hers, and it landed the way those jokes always landed: lightly, harmlessly, with a blade inside. August made them often without knowing why. The dead god's gardener, joking about resurrection. Sera laughed anyway, because the alternative was not laughing, and she had learned which of those she could survive.

They worked together through the morning—weeding, watering, arguing companionably about whether cabbages needed more room than August gave them (they did; August disagreed; the cabbages declined to comment). At noon they ate bread and hard cheese on the wall, legs dangling, and August asked, as August asked most days, in the tone of someone inquiring about distant weather:

"Tell me something about before. Anything. You always pick good ones."

And Sera, who had been waiting all week for the question, reached into her basket and brought out the ledger.

Not the third volume. The second—the one with the ash-stained cover, the one with her own burned-hand entry near the front. She had thought about this for days. She had almost brought the first volume instead, the night-market volume, the beginning-of-things volume, because beginnings were kinder. But kindness was not the same as truth, and if she was going to tell August who August had been, she was going to start where the weight was.

"What's this?" August asked, wiping their hands carefully before taking it, because August was careful with books even without remembering why.

"My work journal. From the war year." Sera watched August's face as the words landed—watched the flicker of something that wasn't memory but was adjacent to it, the way a scar aches in cold weather. "I thought today I'd read you a real one. Not a story. Something I wrote while it was happening."

August held the book like it might be hot. "Will it hurt me?"

"I don't think so. I think—" Sera paused, choosing. "I think it might feel like putting on a coat you forgot you owned. Heavy, but yours."

August nodded once, and opened the book to the ribbon Sera had placed there, and read silently for a long moment. Sera watched their lips move slightly around the words.

"*Let August live,*" August read aloud at last, very quietly. "*Nothing else matters.*"

"That's mine. From the night of the storm. I was outside the tower. My hands were burned—I'd tried to carry the shard up and it—" She stopped. Some details were for later chapters. "I wrote it down because I couldn't say it out loud to anyone, and the ledger was the only thing listening."

August looked up. There was dirt on their cheek and an expression on their face that Sera had never seen before—not the polite blankness of the amnesiac, not the warm stranger-smile, but something older surfacing through both, like a riverbed showing through shallow water.

"I don't remember writing this," August said. "But I remember—" A frown. A hand pressed flat against their own chest, as if checking for a heartbeat or holding one in. "When you read it. There's a feeling. Like a bell struck somewhere far off. I get it around you sometimes. I thought it was just—" They stopped, and Sera watched them decide, with visible effort, to finish honestly. "I thought it was just that I trust you and didn't know why. But it's more than trust, isn't it. This book says it's more."

"Yes," Sera said. Her throat was tight. "It's more."

August closed the ledger and held it a moment longer, thumb moving over the stained cover as though reading it by touch. Then, without any ceremony at all, in the voice of a person deciding something practical:

"Then I'm keeping this feeling. Whatever else gets rebuilt or doesn't. This one stays."

"The gods aren't taking anything else," Sera said. "They've retired, remember? You're allowed to just—have things now. Slowly. We're rebuilding."

"Rebuilding," August repeated, tasting the word, and looked out over the garden—the black soil, the struggling roses, the ruined altar-stone wearing a crown of wildflowers it had never asked for. "Good. I like work. I think I've always liked work. Don't tell me if I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong," Sera said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest that had been locked since the tower. "You were the hardest worker I ever knew. You once dug an entire drainage channel through a flooded catacomb in a night because a stranger's cellar was flooding."

"A stranger's cellar," August said, delighted. "See, that's the kind of detail I want. Was I heroic about it?"

"You complained the entire time."

"Better and better."

They sat on the wall in the sun, the ledger between them like a shared meal, and Sera felt the day arrange itself into something she would want to write down later—which, she realized, was itself a form of prayer, and one she was now licensed to perform.

Below them, in the valley, the wind moved through the bean rows with a sound almost like speech. Almost, but not quite. The gods were quiet these days. But Sera had noticed something in the past month, small enough that she hadn't yet put it in the ledger, hadn't yet told anyone, hardly dared to tell herself:

The quiet was changing key.

Where Vessa's garden had been silent as a held breath, there was now a hum underneath things—the bees, yes, and the wind, yes, but also something beneath the beneath, a faint vast patience, like a congregation settling into pews. The gods were not gone. They were *nearby*. Retired, the way a lighthouse keeper retires: still living beside the sea, still watching the water, no longer obliged to light anything.

And lately—only in the last few weeks, only at dusk, only when she was alone in the shrine counting coppers—Sera had begun to hear, at the very edge of hearing, a sound she knew better than her own name.

Thunder. Far away. Patient. Not answering anything.

Just rolling.

She looked at August—at the dirt on their hands, the sun in their hair, the stranger who had chosen to keep one feeling and was keeping it with both hands—and decided, for now, to write none of this down.

Some prayers, she was learning, you carry a while before you speak them.
