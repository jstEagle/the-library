## 21. Thunder, Far Away

The first year ended the way most days in Vell had come to end: with dirt under August's fingernails and the smell of rain that hadn't arrived yet.

They were kneeling in the third bed of the garden—the one Sera called the stubborn bed, because nothing in it grew without argument—when they felt it. Not in the sky. In their hands.

A pressure. A gathering. The particular weight of air that has decided to become something else.

August sat back on their heels and looked at their palms as if they belonged to a stranger. The trowel lay beside them, its wooden handle darkened by a season of use. Their hands were gardener's hands now. Calloused differently than they used to be, they'd been told. Softer at the fingertips. They had no memory of what storm-caller's hands felt like, which was perhaps the point of them.

But the hands remembered anyway.

*Here*, said something old and wordless in the marrow of them. *This is how it begins. This is how you gather the sky into your fists.*

Above the drowned temple's broken dome—which was not drowned anymore, because August had spent eight months hauling water out of it bucket by bucket, and if anyone asked why, August could only say it seemed like the thing to do—the clouds had gone the color of wet slate. A wind came up from the east carrying the smell of the far hills, of ozone and crushed grass, of a storm still hours away over the free cities but coming all the same.

The first storm of the season. The first since—

Since the tower. Since the burning streets. Since whatever August had done up there that everyone refused to describe to them in full, as though grief were a courtesy they owed a stranger.

Their fingers curled. Just slightly. An involuntary thing, like a sleeper's twitch.

And for one heartbeat—one single, suspended heartbeat—August Thackeray knew exactly how to call it.

Not remembered. *Knew.* The way a body knows how to breathe. The knowledge rose through them whole and enormous: the shape of the gathering, the words that weren't words, the place behind the sternum where a person opens themselves like a door and lets the sky come through. It stood in them complete, vast and terrible and beautiful, a cathedral built in an instant out of lightning and loss.

It would be so easy.

That was the horror of it. Not the power—they'd been told about the power, warned about it, mourned it on their behalf by people who loved someone they couldn't remember being. The horror was the ease. One breath. One opening. The storm rolling toward Vell would answer like a hound hearing its name, and August would stand up out of the dirt with lightning in their fists and the world would remember what they were.

And then it began to take its price again. August could feel that too, in the same heartbeat, the way you can feel a blade before it cuts: the storm reaching for the shelves of them, for the person who taught them to tie luck-knots, for a voice saying a true name in the dark, for—

August opened their hands.

Let go. Breathed out. Unmade the door before it finished opening.

The knowledge drained away like water through loose soil, and what remained was just a gardener kneeling in a garden under a graying sky, heart hammering, palms empty, eyes wet without knowing why.

* * *

Sera found them there twenty minutes later, when the first fat drops began to spot the dust.

"You're not working," she said, standing in the doorway of the temple with her keeper's satchel slung across her body, the one with the licensed sigil of Vell stitched on the flap. She still wasn't quite used to the sigil herself; August had watched her touch it absently a hundred times, the way a person touches a tooth that used to hurt.

"Storm's coming," August said.

"I know. I walked through half of it getting here." She came down the row, skirts darkening at the hem, and crouched beside them. Her face did the thing it always did when she looked at August closely—the small tightening around the eyes, the quick inventory, the fear she never voiced anymore because voicing it hadn't helped in a year. "You're pale."

"I'm always pale."

"You're *paler*." She put her hand on their forehead, professional and gentle, checking for fever the way she'd checked a thousand acolytes in another life. "Tell me."

August hesitated. This was the arithmetic they lived inside now: every truth told to Sera was a truth given back to a person August had no memory of loving, and every truth withheld was a small cruelty to the person who had carried them out of the fire. There was no clean side of that ledger. There was only choosing, daily, which debt to pay.

"The storm," August said finally. "When it got close. My hands—" They flexed their fingers, watching them like evidence. "I knew how to call it, Sera. For a second. All of it. Like it had never left."

Sera went very still. Rain ticked against the leaves of the bean rows. Somewhere beyond the temple walls, in the town that had grown up around the reclaimed garden, a shutter banged and a child shouted happily at the weather.

"And?" she said quietly.

"And I let it pass." August turned their hand over, palm up, showing her the emptiness. "It wanted things, Sera. When it touched me. I could feel it reaching for—" They stopped. Swallowed. "For the things I have. Whatever's left of me. It counted them."

Sera took their hand. Held it open between both of hers, studying the palm as though she might read the storm's fingerprints there.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Letting it go?"

The honest answer was complicated, so August gave her the true one instead. "Ask me tomorrow. Some days I'll say yes."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I've got." August managed a smile. "I don't remember who I was. But I know who I am, and this morning I was a man who was going to get the beans staked before the rain. That's not nothing. I worked hard to be that man. Or—I didn't work, exactly. Someone paid for it." They watched her face do the flinch it always did at that. "I'd like to keep him. If it's all the same to the sky."

Sera laughed—a short, wet sound—and pressed their hand closed again, folding the fingers down over the emptiness as if she were sealing something inside.

"Then keep him," she said. "Come on. Storm's here. We'll stake the beans tomorrow."

* * *

They sheltered in the temple's eastern alcove, which August had roofed with salvaged tile and which Sera had, over the course of the year, filled with the small apparatus of her strange new office: candles for prayers nobody official sanctioned, a shelf of written petitions tied in bundles, a chipped statue of Vessa that some farmer had dug out of his field and brought here like a stray cat, hoping somebody would feed it.

The rain came down in earnest, drumming on the tiles, silvering the light. Through the archway they could see the garden drowning gloriously, everything drinking, everything green going greener.

Lightning flickered over the far hills. Thunder followed, long and low, a sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

August's hands ached.

Not painfully. More like the ache of an old injury before weather changes—a ghost of a wound, reporting in. They kept their hands folded in their lap where Sera wouldn't see, and listened to the storm walk across the free cities, and thought about the strange arithmetic of anniversaries.

One year ago today—or near enough; Sera kept the exact date and August had asked her not to tell them—something had happened on a tower in Vell. Malvane had died. The war had ended. Cities that had been bracing for a generation of darkness had instead gotten a harvest festival and a treaty and, eventually, a licensed keeper of small prayers. August had done it. Everyone agreed on that. August had saved them all, and the price had been everything August was, and the world considered that a bargain well struck, and August—who was the only person alive who couldn't remember either side of the transaction—had learned to nod along.

But sitting there in the candle-smell and the rain-noise, watching lightning stitch the hills, August understood something they had never managed to say aloud:

They were proud of the person who'd climbed that tower. Proud the way you're proud of a soldier in an old story. And they grieved him the same way. And neither feeling belonged to them properly, because both belonged to a man whose face August knew only from reflections and other people's descriptions—a tall man, Sera said, with a temper and a laugh and terrible taste in poetry; a dangerous man, Dain had said, once, before he stopped visiting; a good man, everyone said, always, in the tone people use for the dead.

The thunder rolled closer. August's hands ached, and ached, and then—slowly, like a tide going out—stopped.

"Tell me something," August said.

Sera looked up from relighting a guttered candle. "Anything."

"Not the big story. I'm not ready for the big story. Something small. From before."

She considered, flame trembling on the wick in the draft. Then she smiled, and it transformed her face the way it always did—ten years younger, five years lighter.

"The night market," she said. "The first time I saw you. You were selling fever-charms off a blanket, and you'd priced them wrong—far too cheap—and this old woman argued you *up*. Insisted on paying double. You were so offended." Sera's laugh caught. "You said, 'Madam, I am a craftsman, not a charity,' and she said, 'Boy, I've seen your knots. You're a charity.' The whole market heard it."

"I sounds insufferable."

"You sounded terrified," Sera said softly. "You smiled the whole time, but your hands shook when you made change. I remember thinking: there's a man hiding from something, and he's doing it very badly, and he's still kind to children and rude to grandmothers, and I hope whatever he's hiding from never finds him." She looked at the candle instead of at August. "I was wrong about the last part. But I've never been wrong about the kindness."

Outside, the storm reached the hills above the valley and broke against them, rain lashing the tiles, the whole temple roaring softly with it. Lightning lit the garden white—every leaf, every stake, every drowned furrow printed for an instant in silver—and August watched it and felt nothing in their hands at all.

Just peace. Ordinary, unremarkable, hard-won peace, sitting in them like a seed that had finally taken.

* * *

The storm passed in the night, as storms do, and morning came up rinsed and gold.

August was in the garden early, alone, staking the beans as promised. The stubborn bed had survived. Everything had survived. That was the quiet miracle of gardens, August had learned: they forgave neglect, rewarded attention, and asked nothing of you that you couldn't give with two hands and a morning.

They were tying the last knot—plain garden twine, a loop and a hitch, no charm in it at all—when they noticed the figure at the temple gate.

A traveler, standing in the mud of the road, hood back, travel-stained. A woman, gray at the temples, with the bearing of someone who had ridden hard and slept little. She wasn't looking at the garden. She was looking at August with an expression August had learned to dread over the past year: recognition without acquaintance, grief without context, hope and apology fighting for the same face.

People came sometimes. People who had known the person August used to be. They always stood like that, at a distance, as if crossing the fence line might break something.

But this woman didn't wait politely, the way the others did. She pushed open the gate, walked up the row, and stopped an arm's length away, and when she spoke her voice was rough with road-dust and something older than road-dust.

"They told me you wouldn't know me," she said. "They told me not to come. I've spent a year obeying that." She swallowed. "My name is Maren Thackeray. I'm your sister. And I need to tell you about the night our mother died—because there's something you buried even deeper than us, August, and I think it's starting to wake up."

The twine slipped from August's fingers.

Far away, over the hills, thunder answered.
