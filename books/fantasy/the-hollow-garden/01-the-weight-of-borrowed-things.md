## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The soldier's name was Aldric Vane, and he had a mother's kitchen folded up inside him like a letter he'd never dared to open.

Elodie found him at the Drowned Lantern, three days after his company staggered through Hollowmere's southern pass, frostbitten and loud and grateful for any fire that didn't move. He was the youngest of them, barely old enough to shave, and he sat apart from the others the way young men do when they're homesick and ashamed of it — nursing a cup of mulled wine he wasn't drinking, staring into it as though the answer to something might float up.

She'd watched him for two evenings before she made her approach. That was the craft of it. Anyone could take a memory; Elodie had seen gutter-thieves do it badly, ripping memories out like drawers yanked from a dresser, leaving people lopsided and confused, missing years they couldn't name. The brokers paid nothing for damaged goods. But a memory *lifted* — eased out whole, with its edges intact, so that the man who lost it only felt a soft absence, a room in his mind where the furniture had been quietly sold — that was worth coin.

And more than coin. It was worth the thing Elodie actually lived on, which was other people's lives.

"Rough crossing?" she asked, sliding onto the bench beside him with the ease of someone who belonged there. She wore her second-best shawl, the grey one, because grey said *harmless* in a way that red never managed.

Aldric blinked at her. Up close he was all elbows and chapped ears. "The pass," he said. "The pass was — yes. Rough."

"You're a long way from home."

He laughed, short and bitter. "You could say that. We marched through two kingdoms and half a winter to get here." He turned the cup. "My mother thinks I'm garrisoned at Callow Ford. Nice and safe. She'd weep if she knew where I actually am."

"And where's home?"

"Briarlow." He said it the way people say the names of places they love and would deny loving. "It's nothing. A village. You've never heard of it."

"Try me."

So he told her — about the millpond that froze green instead of white because of the copper in the water, about the innkeeper who watered his ale and everyone pretended not to know, about the hill behind the church where you could see the whole valley at once if the mist lifted. And then, because he was young and lonely and she had asked exactly the right questions in exactly the right order, he got to the kitchen.

"Oh, you'd laugh," he said, going pink.

"I promise I won't."

"It's just — my mother's kitchen. It smells like thyme and bread and the woodsmoke from the stove that never draws properly, so there's always a haze, and my mother waves a towel at the window every morning like she's fighting a duel with the smoke and losing. There's a chip in the third floorboard that catches your heel if you walk too fast, and she refuses to fix it because my father chipped it the week they were married and she says the house wouldn't be the house without it." He smiled down into his cup, helpless. "When it's cold on watch, that's what I think about. The chip in the floorboard. Isn't that stupid?"

"It's not stupid," Elodie said, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it. "It sounds like the least stupid thing in the world."

Here is what no one tells you about taking: it is easy. Easier than lying, easier than stealing bread. You need only touch skin — a hand, a wrist, the back of a neck, anywhere bare — and find the thread. Everyone's minds are full of threads, bright ones and dull ones, and a practiced hand can feel the difference. The kitchen-memory glowed in Aldric like a coal banked under ash. Warmth. Thyme. A woman waving a towel at smoke and losing, laughing while she lost it.

*Forgive me,* Elodie thought, the way she always thought it, the way a prayer works when you don't believe anyone's listening.

She laid two fingers on his wrist beneath the table, and drew.

It came away clean. Whole. The chip in the floorboard, the bad-drawing stove, the smell of thyme — all of it, packed up small and bright, hers now. Aldric's face went briefly distant, the way faces do, and then he frowned faintly, patting his pockets as though he'd misplaced something, though he hadn't, though there was nothing left to misplace.

"Sorry," he mumbled. "I — lost my thought."

"Happens to everyone," Elodie said gently. "Another wine?"

"No. No, I should—" He stood, swaying slightly, looking around the tavern as if trying to remember why he'd come. Then he looked at her, and for one terrible moment his eyes were wet. "Do you ever miss somewhere you can't remember? Like a word on the tip of your tongue, but it's a *place*, and it's—"

"Every day," said Elodie, who missed nowhere, having nowhere to miss.

He nodded slowly, satisfied by this in some way she couldn't fathom, dropped a coin too many on the table, and went off to rejoin his company, lighter by one kitchen and not knowing why.

Elodie sat alone with the warmth of it glowing behind her ribs, and felt — as she always did in the first hour after a lift, before the hunger came back — rich beyond description.

* * *

Maren Hale kept her shop on Tallow Street, wedged between a coffin-maker and a dyer whose vats turned the alley air the color of bruised plums. The sign above the door read HALE & ASSOCIATES, FINE RECOLLECTIONS, which was broker humor, since Maren had no associates and nothing about her was fine. She was a broad, square woman with spectacles she didn't need and a voice like a drawer of cutlery being opened carefully.

"Well?" she said, before Elodie had even shut the door. "What have you brought me? Please say it isn't another fisherman's first catch. I have eleven of those. Eleven. I could wallpaper the back room with fishermen."

"A mother's kitchen," Elodie said. "Complete. Village called Briarlow. Copper pond, crooked stove, a floorboard with sentimental value. The owner was a soldier, young, and he loved it so much he cried talking about it."

Maren's eyebrows went up over the useless spectacles. "Sentimental value in a *floorboard*. Oh, that's good. That's very good." She rubbed her hands together. "Grief-adjacent domestic warmth, military provenance, verifiable place-name. The collectors will fight over this. Sit. Let me look at it."

The inspection was a ritual Elodie both dreaded and craved. Maren took Elodie's hand in both of hers — her palms dry and cool as paper — and closed her eyes, and for a moment Elodie felt the broker riffling along the edges of the memory the way a jeweler checks a stone for flaws.

"Mm. Clean lift. Edges intact. The stove detail — lovely, nobody remembers stoves, it's all hearths and candles with the amateurs, but a stove says *lived-in*." Maren released her hand and squinted. "You didn't damage him?"

"He'll feel a gap. A soft one. He'll fill it with something."

"They always do." Maren said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. She opened the strongbox under the counter — an ugly iron thing older than the street itself — and counted out coins. "Twelve silver. Fourteen if you throw in whatever else you got off him."

"That was everything."

"Everything? A whole evening and you took one memory?" Maren clicked her tongue. "You used to work a table like a harvest, girl. First love, worst fear, the face of whoever raised them — three lifts, four, and gone before the stew arrives. Now you take one and sit there sipping wine like a lady at a funeral."

"The quality's better."

"The quality's better," Maren agreed grudgingly, "but arithmetic doesn't care about quality. Twelve silver." She pushed the coins across, then paused, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for business that smelled of opportunity. "Anything else stirring? Anything odd come through with the soldiers? Rumors, curiosities, things that don't add up?"

"Odd how?"

"Odd how I pay extra." Maren leaned forward. "There are buyers south of the pass asking after strange goods. Not the usual collectors — these ones don't want kitchens. They want *wrongness*. Memories that shouldn't exist. People who shouldn't exist." Her eyes, small and quick behind the glass, held Elodie's a moment too long. "You hear anything like that, you come to me first. I'll make it worth your while."

"There's nothing wrong in Hollowmere," Elodie said. "That's rather the point of it."

"Mm," said Maren Hale, who knew better, and counted out twelve silver, and did not offer fourteen.

* * *

Elodie's hovel clung to the hillside above the frost line like a barnacle with pretensions. It had one room, one window, one chimney that smoked sideways whenever the wind remembered to blow, and a view — on clear nights — of the whole grey bowl of Hollowmere below: the town huddled around its fires, the border stones standing pale and patient in their ring, and beyond them, always, the white country where the world went on without permission from anyone.

Magic could not cross those stones. Nothing crossed them easily. The valley had been sealed since before anyone living could remember — a pocket of the world tucked away from enchantment, safe from it, starved of it, depending on whom you asked and how cold they were. What Elodie knew was simpler: inside the stones, no spell worked, no wonder grew, and the only magic left in Hollowmere was the kind she carried in her own hands, the small theft of other people's brightness. She was, as far as she knew, the last candle in a valley that had outlawed fire.

She lit her lamp, hung her coat on its nail, and sat down at the table where she kept her collection.

Not coins. Not jewels. Memories — hundreds of them, sorted and shelved in the only vault that couldn't be robbed: her own head. She had a system. Kitchens and hearths went in the warm corner near the lamp. Sea-voyages slept along the north wall of her mind, salt and gulls and rope-creak. First kisses were kept separately, in a little locked drawer she visited rarely, the way you visit a grave or a cake you're saving. There were weddings in there, and funerals, and one entire childhood belonging to a shepherd boy from the eastern hills — lambs, a dog named Sorrel, a willow tree you could climb straight into the sky — that she took out sometimes on bad nights just to remember what it felt like to be somebody's son.

Tonight she took out the soldier's kitchen.

Thyme. Woodsmoke. The duel with the towel. She walked through it slowly, the way you walk through a house you're thinking of buying. The chip in the floorboard caught her heel, right where he'd said it would, and she felt the specific love of it — a flaw kept because keeping it was a form of devotion — and something in her chest pulled tight, like a knot drawn one degree closer.

This was the arrangement, and it had been the arrangement for eleven years: she owned hundreds of lives and had none of her own. Every borrowed life fit her perfectly for an hour and then hung wrong forever, the way borrowed coats hang wrong. She knew the taste of forty mothers' cooking and could not remember the face of the woman who'd fed her. She owned a wedding from the inside — the nerves, the terrible poetry, the groom's cold hands — and had never once been looked at the way that groom looked at his bride. She was the richest pauper in Hollowmere, a magpie's nest of other birds' eggs, and on nights like this one the wealth sat in her like stones.

"Twelve silver," she said aloud to the empty room, because saying things aloud was the closest she came to conversation most weeks. "For a whole kitchen. Maren Hale will sell that to some fat collector for sixty, and he'll put it on at dinner parties and weep artistically, and none of them — not one of them — will ever have chipped the floorboard themselves."

The lamp flickered. Outside, the wind was changing. She'd learned to read Hollowmere's weather the way sailors read water, and the wind had that particular hush in it, that held-breath quality, that meant snow coming — the first snow, maybe, the early one, the one that closed the passes and settled the valley into its long white silence.

Elodie banked the lamp, put the soldier's kitchen carefully back in its warm corner — she was not a monster; she kept her goods in good condition — and lay down under blankets that smelled of wool and ash.

She was nearly asleep when she heard it.

Footsteps on the frost-brittle grass outside her door. Small ones. The steps of someone light enough that the ground barely noticed them, climbing past the frost line, past every sensible boundary, to the one hovel in Hollowmere where a person with nothing to gain and everything to lose might still open a door.

Then: a knock. Three taps, polite as a bird pecking.

Elodie lay very still in the dark, her heart doing something complicated against her ribs. Nobody climbed to her hovel. Nobody knocked. In eleven years, the only visitors she'd had were creditors, weather, and once a goat with opinions.

The knock came again. Three taps. Patient. Waiting.

And underneath the sound of it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, Elodie heard something that made her sit bolt upright in the dark — a voice, thin and high, speaking words she should not have known, in a language that had been dead longer than the valley had been sealed.

She knew it anyway. She knew it from a dying king's stolen last hour, shelved in the far corner of her mind, in the drawer she never opened.

The child outside her door was speaking Verdant.
