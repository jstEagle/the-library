## 2. A Knock Before the Snow

The first snow of the season came to Hollowmere the way bad news always did: quietly at first, then all at once.

Elodie felt it coming in her teeth, which was a thing she'd never told anyone because it sounded like madness and also because there had never been anyone to tell. The valley's air went thin and glassy, the way it did before a snowfall, and every sound carried too far — the creak of her roof beams, the tick of the stove, the small dry whisper of memory-spheres shifting in their velvet-lined drawers beneath her bed. Three hundred and eleven of them now. She'd counted twice that morning, the way she counted every morning, as if one might have wandered off in the night to live a life of its own.

She was stirring a pot of barley soup that would last four days if she was disciplined and two if she wasn't, when the light through the single window changed. Not dimmed — *changed*, the way it does when something large moves between you and the sky.

She set down the spoon.

Nobody climbed to Elodie Voss's hovel. That was rather the point of living above the frost line, in a house perched on the shoulder of Cinder Hill like a barnacle on a drowned ship. The brokers came to her, down in the market town of Lowmere, where the lamps burned yellow and honest people pretended not to know what she sold. Up here there was only her, the wind, and a view of the valley floor that was beautiful in the way a locked box is beautiful — everything visible, nothing reachable.

The knock, when it came, was so soft she almost missed it under the wind. Three taps. Small ones. The kind of knuckles that hadn't finished being knuckles yet.

Elodie stood very still, spoon in hand, and conducted the brief internal argument that had governed her entire adult life.

*Don't answer it.* This voice sounded like Maren Hale, brisk and sensible, with her ledger-keeper's eyes. *Nothing good knocks on doors up here. Nothing knocks at all, that's the beauty of it.*

*It's a child,* said another voice, smaller, one she didn't let speak often. *That's small knuckles.*

*Children grow into adults who remember who helped them and who didn't. And adults talk. And talk, in this valley, is currency worse than none.*

*It's snowing,* said the small voice. *First snow. Nobody travels in first snow unless they're fleeing something or lost something or—*

"Oh, hush," Elodie told herself out loud, and put down the spoon, and opened the door.

* * *

The child stood on her doorstep like something the storm had decided, halfway through its work, not to finish.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, though it was hard to say — hunger and cold flatten children into a sort of agelessness. She wore a coat that had been fine once, deep green wool gone grey at the seams, embroidered at the cuffs with leaves so stylized they might have been feathers or flames. No hat. No gloves. Hair the color of wet river stones, plastered to her face. Snow had built itself into little drifts on her shoulders, and she hadn't brushed it off, which meant either that she'd been standing there a long time or that she'd stopped noticing her own body somewhere along the road.

Her eyes were what stopped Elodie's breath.

They were green. Not the green of valley eyes, which was really a muddy hazel pretending ambition. This green was old. It was the green of the underside of leaves, of moss on north-facing stone, of a color that had been waiting patiently underground for someone to move the rock. And they were looking at Elodie with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: the flat, calculating watchfulness of someone deciding whether the person in front of them was a threat, a meal, or a door.

"Well," Elodie said. "You're not a broker."

The girl said nothing. Her mouth stayed closed, lips faintly blue.

"Are you mute, or merely well-mannered? I've known both. The well-mannered are rarer." Elodie sighed at herself, already losing the argument, already stepping aside. "Oh, come in, then, before the storm changes its mind about sparing you. But mind you wipe your feet. I have exactly three rugs and I'm fond of all of them."

The girl crossed the threshold the way a deer crosses a clearing — weight forward, ready to bolt. Inside, she stopped in the middle of the single room and turned slowly, taking inventory: the stove, the pot of soup, the narrow bed, the curtained alcove where the drawers were, the window with its view of the whole valley lying below like a held breath.

Elodie watched her watch, and felt a prickle of professional unease. That was not a child's survey. That was a thief's survey, or a soldier's, cataloguing exits and valuables.

"Sit," Elodie said, pointing at the stool by the stove. "There's soup. Don't ask what's in it; the answer is barley and optimism."

The girl sat. She did not touch the bowl Elodie filled for her until Elodie ate a spoonful first, demonstratively, and even then she only ate after watching Elodie's throat move when she swallowed. Clever, Elodie thought. Careful. Someone had taught her that poison-watching, or life had.

They ate in silence for a while, wind worrying the eaves. The girl ate precisely — no gulping, no wolfishness, each spoonful measured as though rationing had been bred into her bones. When the bowl was empty she set it down, aligned its handle with the table edge, and folded her hands.

"Right," Elodie said. "Names. I'm Elodie. Elodie Voss. You may have heard nothing about me, which is how I prefer it." She waited. The girl looked at her. "Your turn. Name? Any name? Made-up names accepted. I once knew a man called Turnip and he answered to it happily for forty years."

The girl's mouth opened.

What came out was not Hollowmere speech. It was not anything. The sounds were low and round and layered, vowels stacked inside vowels like rings inside rings, and they moved strangely in the small room — Elodie felt them in her sternum, a hum, a resonance, as though the words were less spoken than *rung*, like bells made of wood.

The girl said perhaps six words. Then she stopped, and looked at Elodie with those impossible green eyes, and waited.

"I haven't the faintest idea," Elodie said honestly. "You're speaking — is it a language? It sounds like a language. It sounds like a language that's been buried a long time and doesn't know it's dead."

The girl tilted her head. Something flickered across her face — not disappointment exactly. Recalculation. As if she'd expected the words to do something, and was adjusting her plans around their failure.

And Elodie — Elodie's blood went cold, and then strange, and then cold again.

Because she *knew* that language.

Not knew it — she couldn't have conjugated a verb in it to save her soul. But she had heard it. Once. In someone else's head, which was the only place Elodie had ever heard anything worth hearing.

Three winters ago, a dying man had come through Hollowmere. A traveler, half-frozen, fevered, past saving by anything the valley's herbwives could brew — magic couldn't cross the border stones, but neither could much mercy, and the man had been beyond both. He'd had nothing left to sell except his memories, and Elodie had bought the lot for a handful of coins and a promise of a warm room for his final night. Standard trade. She'd lifted them gently, the way she always did at the end — the whole bundle, kitchen-warm, mother-scented, the works — and what remained behind in him was just enough quiet to die in peace.

Among his memories had been a king.

Not a real king — that's what Elodie had told herself for three years, sorting the sphere onto her shelf between a fisherman's wedding day and a midwife's worst night. A *memory* of a king, secondhand, the traveler must have seen some mummers' play, some pageant, some mad street preacher dressed in rags and crowned with wire. Because in the memory, the king lay dying in a hall of white pillars overgrown with living green — vines heavy with fruit in winter, flowers opening as fast as frost could form — and the king had been speaking to someone kneeling at his side, and the language he spoke was *this* language, these exact rounded bell-tones, and the last word he said — the one he repeated, the one he pressed into the kneeling figure's hands along with something small and dark and seed-shaped —

The word had been a name. Or a place. Or a plea.

*"Vaelith,"* the girl had said, just now, among those six impossible words. Elodie was nearly sure of it. *Vaelith*, the way the dying king had said it, with the same falling cadence, the same grief worn smooth by repetition.

Elodie put down her own spoon very carefully, because her hands had begun to shake, and shaking hands near memory-spheres was how accidents happened.

"Say that again," she said. "The third word. The one that goes down at the end."

The girl studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she said it again: *"Vaelith."*

And the spheres in the drawer beneath the bed — all three hundred and eleven of them — hummed.

It was very soft. Soft enough that Elodie might have imagined it, might have blamed the stove, the wind, her own guilty conscience. But she had spent eleven years handling stolen memories, and she knew their behavior the way a shepherd knows sheep. They slept. They murmured sometimes, dreams leaking through the glass, but they did not *answer*. They were goods. Inventory. Dead men's Tuesdays and living women's weddings, packed away in velvet, inert as stones.

All three hundred and eleven of them had just leaned toward the sound of that word like flowers toward sun.

* * *

The girl watched Elodie the whole time Elodie crossed the room and knelt at the drawer and drew out the king's memory without quite deciding to. Some instincts bypass deliberation entirely.

The sphere sat in her palm, warm as always, faintly gold, swirling with the slow inner weather of a stranger's life. Normally she saw nothing in the swirls — reading a memory required a broker's lens, or a buyer's willingness, and Elodie had never been able to afford either for her own stock. But tonight, held near the girl, the swirls quickened. Green threaded through the gold. For half a heartbeat Elodie smelled rain on hot stone, cut grass, something flowering where nothing should flower.

She looked up. The girl was looking at the sphere. Not hungrily — that was the terrible part. Longingly. The way you look at a lit window when you're out in the cold. The way, Elodie thought with a lurch, *I* look at other people's lives.

"That's a dying man's last evening," Elodie heard herself say. "He was nobody. A tinker. He mended kettles. And somewhere in his life he saw — or dreamed, or invented — a king in a garden that wouldn't freeze, saying a word I now suspect isn't from any mummer's play." She turned the sphere over. "And you say that word. And my entire livelihood leans toward you like a dog hearing its name."

The girl reached out — slowly, telegraphing the movement, the way you reach toward a strange cat — and touched the sphere with one fingertip.

The memory flared.

Light poured between the girl's fingers, green-gold, and for one instant the whole hovel was a summer afternoon: Elodie felt grass under feet she didn't have, warmth on shoulders she'd forgotten she owned, and somewhere a woman laughing, and the laughter was—

Then it stopped. The sphere dimmed back to its ordinary drowsy gold. The girl snatched her hand back as if burned, eyes wide, and shook her head rapidly — *no, no, sorry, sorry* — and Elodie realized her own mouth was open and her heart was slamming and she was gripping the edge of the drawer hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

"It's all right," Elodie said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "It's all right. That's never happened. Eleven years, and that has never happened."

Outside, the snow thickened against the window, erasing the valley one field at a time. Down there, in Lowmere, the brokers would be closing their shutters and lighting their lamps and settling in with their ledgers, and tomorrow morning Maren Hale would hear — Maren always heard — that a stranger had come up Cinder Hill in the first snow. A child. Alone. Speaking nonsense.

Maren paid well for nonsense. Maren paid best of all for things that hummed.

"You can sleep by the stove," Elodie said, dragging her mind back to the room, the girl, the immediate and considerable problem of having invited trouble across her own threshold. "One night. The storm will keep you anyway; no one walks in first snow. Tomorrow you'll tell me — somehow, we'll manage *somehow* — where you came from and where you think you're going, and then we'll decide what to do with you. 'We' meaning me, since you appear to be approximately eight and possibly royal and definitely trouble."

The girl blinked at the torrent of words. Then she did something that undid Elodie more thoroughly than the humming spheres had: she smiled. Small, crooked, exhausted, but unmistakably a smile, aimed straight at Elodie like a gift she had no right to give.

"Don't," Elodie said. "Don't do that. I'm not nice. Ask anyone. Well — don't ask anyone, actually, but rest assured the consensus exists."

The girl curled up by the stove in her leaf-embroidered coat, and within minutes she was asleep, breathing slow, snow melting out of her hair and darkening the flagstones. Elodie sat across from her with the king's memory in her lap and watched her, and did not sleep at all.

Once, near midnight, the girl stirred and spoke in her sleep — one word, soft, the falling cadence again.

*Vaelith.*

And in the drawer, three hundred and eleven stolen lives turned over together, like a field of flowers finding the sun, and Elodie Voss sat alone in the dark holding a dead man's Tuesday and understood, with the certainty of the truly doomed, that by morning everyone in Hollowmere who bought and sold other people's pasts was going to want this child — and that she, fool that she was, had already decided otherwise.
