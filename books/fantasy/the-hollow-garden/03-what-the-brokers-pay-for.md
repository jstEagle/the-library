## 3. What the Brokers Pay For

Maren Hale kept her counting room at a temperature she described, to clients who shivered, as *bracing*. The truth was that cold sharpened her. Cold made the numbers stand up straighter on their ledgers. Cold reminded her that everything soft in this world was merely something that hadn't yet been properly priced.

She was pricing a soldier's homesickness when they came.

The memory sat in its little leaded glass vial on her scale, glowing the dull amber of woodsmoke and bread, and it was worth perhaps four marks — five if the soldier's mother had died recently enough to make the longing fashionable. Grief appreciated. That was the first lesson of the trade, and Maren had learned it before she'd learned her letters.

The bell above her door rang, badly, as it always did. She didn't look up.

"Elodie Voss," she said. "You're early. Either you've found something wonderful or you're desperate, and your face says both."

"Good morning to you too, Maren."

Maren looked up then, and her pen stopped moving.

Elodie Voss stood in the doorway of the counting room wrapped in that threadbare grey coat she refused to replace, holding out a vial like a woman offering a bouquet. Behind her — half-hidden by the doorframe, peering around it with the particular caution of small animals and children — stood a girl.

A child. Perhaps seven years old. Dark-haired, thin as a winter branch, dressed in clothes that had crossed more borders than most couriers ever would. And looking at Maren Hale with eyes that were not afraid.

That was the first wrong thing. Children were always afraid of the brokers' house. They were taught to be. The mothers of Hollowmere told their little ones that if they lied, the memory-brokers would come and take the lie out of them, leaving them hollow as gourds. It was excellent for business. Maren had never once corrected the rumor.

But this child looked at her the way one looks at weather. Noting it. Waiting to see what it would do.

"And who," said Maren pleasantly, "is this?"

"Nobody," said Elodie, too quickly. "Found her on my step during the first snow. She doesn't talk. I'm keeping her warm until I can figure out where she belongs, which is elsewhere, ideally soon." She set the vial down on the counter with a small decisive click. "Soldier's memory. His mother's kitchen. You'll want it — he wept when I took it, so you know it's good stock."

Maren picked up the vial and held it to the lamp. Amber, yes. Woodsmoke, bread. But beneath it, threaded through the whole thing like a hair in milk, ran something else — a faint green shimmer, barely there, the color of light through leaves.

Her heart did not speed up. Maren Hale's heart had not sped up in nineteen years of trade; that was rather the point of her. But her attention narrowed, the way it did over a miscounted ledger, and every part of her went very quiet and very fast.

"Where did you take this?" she asked lightly.

"Off a caravan man at the Sable Rest. Why?"

"No reason. It's got an odd cast to it. Greenish." She turned the vial. "You know how the Court-touched memories used to fetch. Before the war made them unfashionable."

"The war was sixty years ago, Maren. Nothing's unfashionable forever except honesty." Elodie glanced back at the child, who had drifted into the room now, silent as spilled flour, and was studying the shelves of vials with an expression of grave professional interest. "Don't touch anything," Elodie told her, in the tone people use when they already know it won't work.

The child did not touch anything. She simply stood before the great case of Maren's finest stock — the sealed vault-vials, the ones behind glass — and looked at them. And Maren watched, with the detached fascination of a woman watching a card trick she couldn't catch, as three separate vials on the top shelf flickered. Just once. Just softly. Like lamps acknowledging someone walking past.

Like things recognizing their kin.

"Well," said Maren.

"She does that," said Elodie wearily. "Lamps do it at home too. Candles. I thought at first she was pinching them out somehow, but no. They just... brighten. Is that a problem? Should it be a problem?"

*Should it be a problem.* Maren nearly laughed. It was like asking whether a lit fuse should be a problem while standing on the powder.

"Not at all," she said warmly. "Children are full of odd static. Valley-born brats make milk sour if they cry hard enough, or so their mothers claim. Sit down, Elodie. Let's talk price. And the girl may have a honey drop, if she likes. I keep them for clients' children."

"I don't want your honey," said Elodie, "but she'll have one, because she hasn't eaten properly since—" She stopped herself. "Because she hasn't."

Maren fetched the tin from the drawer by the register, and here she performed a small experiment that cost her nothing and might, she suspected, cost the world a great deal. She held the tin out to the child directly — not to Elodie — and watched.

The child took a honey drop. Looked at it. Looked up at Maren. And then, without any warning at all, she spoke.

Not words Maren knew. A low, liquid language, all vowels and green shadows, two syllables maybe three, offered up like a coin between strangers. Her voice was rusty from disuse — Maren heard that much even in a tongue she couldn't parse — but it was unmistakably a *thank you*, or a greeting, or a warning. With children those were often the same word.

"What did she say?" asked Elodie, startled. "She hasn't made a sound in four days."

"No idea," said Maren truthfully. "Lovely though, isn't it? Sounds like water over stones." She smiled her best smile, the one that had closed a hundred bargains, and crouched slightly to bring herself nearer the child's level. "What's your name, little one?"

The child considered her for a long moment. Then she reached out — quick as a pickpocket, and Maren would know — and touched two fingers to the back of Maren's hand.

And gave her a memory.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. Maren had handled ten thousand memories; she knew their textures the way a cloth merchant knows wool. This one came in whole and blazing: a garden under a sun that no longer existed anywhere in the world, trees heavy with fruit out of season, fountains running warm, and everywhere — everywhere — people. Laughing, singing, arguing about pruning. A court alive. A court *green*. And through the middle of it walked a king with frost already in his beard, smiling anyway, because his gardeners had promised him spring was a promise and promises were kept here—

Gone. Less than a heartbeat. Maren was standing in her cold counting room with a honey tin in her hand and her composure in ruins, and the child was watching her with those unafraid eyes as if to say: *there. Now you know what I am. What will you do about it?*

"Maren?" Elodie's voice, sharp with suspicion. "You've gone white. Whiter. You're the whitest thing in this valley and you've gone whiter."

"Sugar," said Maren smoothly, setting down the tin. "Goes straight to my head these days. Now — the vial. Four marks, and I'll throw in a jar of pickled plums, because I'm feeling generous and your coat has holes in it I could post letters through."

They haggled. Elodie was good at it — she always was, hungry people usually are — and Maren let herself be beaten down to four and a half mostly because her mind was elsewhere, assembling, calculating, catching fire quietly behind her eyes.

The Verdant Court. Dead sixty years, drowned in the long winter, its king frozen mid-sentence on his throne, its gardens glass. Everyone in the trade knew the story: the Frostbound had won, magic had fled the world's skin, and the valley of Hollowmere was one of the dead places, the blind spots, where nothing grew enchanted and nothing escaped. The brokers' entire fortune rested on that fact. Memories were the only magic left in Hollowmere, and memories could be weighed.

And a child had just handed Maren Hale a living fragment of the Court itself, free of charge, across a counter, over a tin of honey drops.

The seed. It had to be. The old texts all agreed the Court had hidden its last seed before the fall — a living kernel of the deep green, carried out by whoever was quickest and smallest and least likely to be searched. Sixty years the Frostbound had hunted it. Sixty years the brokers had dismissed the hunt as a fairy story, because fairy stories don't weigh out on scales.

Well. Fairy stories paid better than soldiers' kitchens.

"Elodie," Maren said, as casually as she had ever said anything, "where exactly did you find this child? On your step, you said. During the snowfall. Was there anyone else? Anyone who left her? A note, a token, anything?"

"A blanket with strange stitching. Why?"

"Just establishing provenance. It matters for resale value." *It matters enormously,* she did not say. *It matters to people who freeze the blood in your veins.*

"Provenance," repeated Elodie, with the flat look of a woman who stole for a living and recognized the smell of a bigger theft being planned. "Maren. If you're thinking what I think you might be thinking—"

"I'm thinking about pickled plums," said Maren, "and that you should take two jars, and that you should come back next week with whatever else you've lifted off that caravan, because the greenish cast is going to be worth chasing." She counted out the marks, slid them across, and added, lightly, so lightly: "Bring the girl, if you like. I'll pay for the company. It gets lonely in here."

Elodie hesitated. Maren watched the hesitation travel across her face — suspicion, need, suspicion again, the eternal arithmetic of the poor — and finally settle into a shrug.

"We'll see. Come along, Wren."

"Wren?" said Maren. "You've named her?"

"She was named. I'm calling her Wren until she tells me different." Elodie scooped up the coins, took the child's hand, and paused at the door. "Maren. Whatever you're calculating behind those eyes — leave her out of it. She's a lost kid, not stock."

"Of course," said Maren, meaning none of it, and smiled, and waved, and watched them go out into the grey street, the tall thief and the small silent miracle, hand in hand like something out of a ballad.

Then she locked the door, drew the blinds, and allowed herself, for precisely eleven seconds, to shake.

* * *

The sending took an hour to prepare, and Maren prepared it the way she did everything: carefully, and with gloves.

Beneath the counting room, below the vaults, below even the cellar where the worthless memories were stored in barrels marked FLOUR, there was a room her grandfather had built in the war years, when Hale & Daughters had still taken commissions from both sides and seen no contradiction in it. The room was lined with river ice packed in sawdust, and in the middle of it, in a block of ice older than Maren, slept a single white raven.

Not dead. Never dead. The Frostbound did not kill their messengers; they *kept* them. The raven's heart beat once a minute, slow as a dripping eave, and its dreams — Maren had checked, early on, out of professional curiosity — were all white.

She pressed her palm flat against the ice and spoke the old words her grandfather had taught her, the ones that tasted like biting a coin.

"Wake up, darling. We've found it."

The ice sighed. Cracks spidered out from her hand, delicate as fern fronds, and inside them the raven opened one eye — black, ancient, unimpressed — and waited for the rest.

"It's a child," Maren said. "Girl, seven years or so, mute in our tongue, speaking the high green speech. Carries the seed — I felt it myself, a true fragment, not a rumor, not a forgery. She's in the keeping of a memory-thief called Voss, Elodie Voss, works the caravans, no family, no faction, no spine worth mentioning. They're at the frost line, north hovel row. I can hold them in place a week, perhaps two, with softness. After that I can't promise."

The raven tilted its head. In the ice, frost bloomed outward from its eye like a flower opening in reverse.

"Yes," Maren said. "I want my payment honored. The valley concession, the border tolls, and the archive rights, as discussed with your people before the thaw failed. I am not doing this for love of either side. I am doing it because I intend to be the richest woman between the mountains when the dust settles, and I intend to be *alive*, which means whichever side wins owes me."

Frost spread. Somewhere far away, beyond the border stones, beyond the blind valley, in halls of standing ice where the old enemy of everything green had waited sixty patient years, Maren liked to imagine a door opening.

The block split cleanly down the middle. The raven stepped out onto the frozen floor, shook itself once — shedding sixty years of stillness like a coat — and looked at her with an expression she chose to interpret as respect.

"Don't eat anyone on the way out," she advised. "We're meant to be respectable."

It went up the ice-shaft in a whisper of wings, and Maren stood alone in the cold dark, listening to it fade, and began — because she was who she was — to calculate.

Two weeks. She would raise Elodie's prices, flatter her, become her best client, keep the pair close and comfortable and *observed*. She would send her nephew Tamlin to loiter near the north hovels — the boy owed her money and was too stupid to ask why he was loitering. She would draft three letters: one to the Frostbound liaison at the Grey Ford, one to her insurers, and one, short and vicious, to the other brokers of Hollowmere informing them that the Voss woman's recent stock was contaminated and best avoided, which would starve Elodie of buyers and drive her straight back into Maren's arms.

Contaminated. Maren smiled at the word in the darkness. The only contamination in this valley was hope, and she had just cornered the market on it.

Above her, muffled through floors of stone and timber, the ordinary sounds of Hollowmere went on: cart wheels, church bells, children shouting in the lane. Somewhere up there a thief was feeding a lost girl supper, entirely unaware that the coldest war in history had just reopened its account in her name.

Maren climbed the stairs, unlocked her door, turned the sign to OPEN, and sat down at her ledger.

Four marks for a soldier's kitchen. She wrote it in a fair, upright hand, blotted it neatly, and reached for the next vial.

Business, after all, was business.
