## 4. The Valley That Magic Forgot

The child slept through the second night in Elodie's hovel, curled on the pallet like a comma at the end of a sentence nobody had finished writing.

Elodie did not sleep. She sat by the peat fire with her knees drawn up and her tea gone cold, watching the small shape breathe, and turned the problem over the way she turned everything: as a question of value. A mute child who spoke a dead tongue was worth nothing to the brokers — Maren Hale paid for memories, not mouths. But a child who had appeared out of the first snowfall of the year, walking a road that no one used anymore, carrying words that Elodie had only ever heard once, in the memory of a dying king?

That was worth something. The trouble was that Elodie couldn't decide whether *she* was the something, or whether the child was bait, or whether the child was simply lost, and Elodie — who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of caring about no one — was about to find out which.

"You snore," she told the child, because saying it aloud made it less true that she'd been listening to the breathing all night instead of sleeping. "Like a small kettle."

The child did not stir. Snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below the frost line, Hollowmere's lamps were being lit one by one, and somewhere among those lamps, Maren Hale was drinking her evening wine and asking her usual question — *anything come down from the high passes?* — and Elodie realized with a lurch that she had already decided to lie about the child, and had decided it without noticing herself deciding.

That was new. That was inconvenient.

* * *

In the morning she fed the child barley porridge with the last of her honey, mostly because the child looked at the honey jar with an expression of such naked, uncomplicated longing that Elodie felt her whole transactional worldview wobble on its foundations.

"Greedy," she said, and gave her more anyway.

The child ate with her hands cupped around the bowl as though it might be taken back, and when she finished she said something in that rusted-bell language — two syllables, soft ones — and pointed at herself.

"Wren," Elodie guessed. It sounded like a bird's name. It sounded like a word for something small that survived hard weather.

The child's face opened like a window. She pointed at herself again, firmly. *Wren.* Then she pointed at Elodie, eyebrows raised, and waited.

Elodie hesitated. Names were currency here too; she hadn't given hers away freely since she was nineteen. But there was no broker within ten miles, and the fire was warm, and the child was looking at her with the terrible patience of someone who has walked a very long way and is not going to be put off by a woman's stubbornness.

"Elodie," she said finally.

Wren repeated it — *Eh-loh-dee*, with the vowels slightly wrong, slightly older than wrong, as though the name had been pronounced this way before Hollowmere existed — and then smiled, and Elodie discovered that being smiled at by this particular child produced a sensation dangerously similar to being *paid*.

She stood up briskly and began wrapping bread in cloth. Enough sentiment. There was a thing she needed to know, and there was exactly one place in the valley where she could learn it.

"We're going for a walk," she announced. "Don't touch anything. Don't eat anything. If anyone asks, you're my niece and you're simple." Wren tilted her head. "Simple means — never mind. Come on."

* * *

The border stones stood half a day's climb above the frost line, where the valley pinched itself shut against the world.

Everyone in Hollowmere knew the rule the way they knew the taste of their own water: magic does not cross. It had been true so long it had stopped being a fact and become a feature, like the cold or the gray light. The Court's green wonders lived beyond the stones, in the wide world, and inside the valley a person could walk from one end to the other and never feel so much as a shiver of anything uncanny. That was why the memory trade worked, Elode supposed, though she'd never thought it through before. In a land where nothing *happened*, a stolen afternoon in a sunlit kitchen was worth a week's bread.

She had been to the stones perhaps four times in her life. Travelers came up the old pass sometimes — merchants, pilgrims, the occasional fool chasing rumors of the Verdant Court — and their memories were richest near the border, still warm with the world outside. You worked the pass, you sold to Maren, you went home. Nobody lingered at the stones. They were tall and gray and faintly wrong, like teeth in a jaw that had bitten down on something long ago and never let go.

The snow was knee-deep on the last stretch, and Wren climbed it like she'd been born on mountains, which, Elodie reflected, she probably had been. Twice the child stopped dead and stared at the peaks to the north with an expression Elodie couldn't read — grief and recognition tangled together — and twice Elodie pretended not to notice, because noticing would mean asking, and asking would mean answers, and answers were the one commodity she wasn't sure she could afford.

At the top, wind. The stones rose out of the drifts in a broken arc, taller than a man, each one carved — though the carving had worn to suggestion — with leaves. Ivy, oak, some flowering branch Elodie didn't know. Under the snow and the centuries they looked less like markers than like mourners.

"All right," Elodie said, to Wren, to herself, to the wind. "Here's how it works. Nothing strange happens past these stones. Ever. People have tried for three hundred years. So if I'm wrong about you — if whatever's in you is what I think it is — then this is where we find out, and if I'm right, we're both in a great deal of danger, and I'd like to state for the record that I object to all of it."

Wren looked up at her. Then, deliberately, the child reached into the collar of her ragged coat and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a seed.

It was small. That was the first absurdity — after the dying king's fevered litany, after the dead tongue, after a child walking out of a snowfall that had swallowed the road behind her, Elodie had expected something dramatic. But the seed was the size of a plum stone, dark brown, unremarkable, the kind of thing you'd sweep off a floor. Except that it seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it, and except that when the wind gusted, the seed did not swing on its cord the way a physical object should. It leaned. Toward the border stones. Like a dog straining at a leash.

Wren held it out flat on her palm and looked at Elodie with an expression of absolute, terrifying trust.

"Oh, don't do that," Elodie said. "Don't look at me like I'm somebody who helps people. I steal things for a living. I once sold a grandmother's funeral to pay for boots. Good boots," she added honestly, "but still."

Wren did not lower her hand.

Elodie blew out a breath that the wind stole immediately. "Fine. Fine. But we do it my way. Not over the line — nothing goes over the line. We'll set it down just short of the stones, on this side, where it can't possibly matter, and if nothing happens we go home and I sell you to the tannery—" Wren's eyes went round — "*joke*, that was a joke, saints preserve me—"

She cleared a patch of snow at the base of the nearest stone, on the valley side, careful to leave a full arm's length between the spot and the invisible line everyone could feel but no one could see. Then she knelt, took the seed from Wren's palm — it was warm, which was the second absurdity, warm as a living thing in air cold enough to crack leather — and pressed it into the frozen ground.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. Three hundred years of nothing happened. Elodie sat back on her heels and laughed, a short ugly laugh of relief, and reached to dig the seed back up—

—and the ice under her fingers moved.

Not melted. *Moved.* It flexed upward, gently, the way a blanket lifts when someone underneath turns over in sleep. A hairline crack ran out from where the seed lay, then another, then a lattice of them, fine as lace, and through the cracks came a color that Elodie had not seen in the valley in her entire life, a color she knew only from other people's stolen summers.

Green.

A stem came up through the ice like a needle through cloth. It was thin and trembling and utterly impossible, and it rose, and it unfurled two leaves, and then — with the unhurried confidence of a thing that has done this ten thousand times and been interrupted only briefly — it bloomed.

The flower was white with a throat of gold, and it was the size of Elodie's thumbnail, and it was the most violent thing she had ever seen.

Because the cold *fought* it. She felt that — felt it in her teeth, in the old scar on her palm, in some sense she didn't have a name for. The valley's long winter pressed down on that small blossom with the whole weight of three hundred years, and the flower simply declined to care. Frost crept toward it and slid off its petals like rain off oiled wool. The air around it shimmered, very faintly, the way air shimmers over a hearth.

And the warmth — Elodie could feel it from where she knelt, a hand's breadth of warmth spilling off six inches of stem, in a valley where warmth had to be carried in iron stoves and paid for in peat.

Beside her, Wren made a sound. It was the first sound Elodie had heard her make — small, cracked, half a sob — and the child dropped to her knees in the snow and pressed both hands to the ice on either side of the flower, and where her palms touched, the frost bloomed backward into fern-patterns of clear water, and beneath the ice, Elodie saw, dimly, other seeds waking. Seeds that had lain under the border stones for three hundred years, patient as debts.

"No," Elodie whispered. "No, no, stop — stop it, put it away, that's not — you can't do that *here*—"

But even as she said it, terror and wonder were fighting inside her chest, and wonder was winning, and that frightened her more than the flower did.

Here was the arithmetic she'd built her life on: Hollowmere was poor, gray, and safe. Magic did not cross the border, and neither did armies, and neither did she. Everything she owned she had carried in through the front door of other people's minds, and everything she wanted she kept locked in her own head, and the system worked, it *worked*, it was small and cold and it worked.

And now a flower was breaking the rules six inches from her knee, and the rules were breaking back, and Elodie Voss understood — with the clarity that comes only at the exact moment it becomes useless — that if Maren Hale ever learned what this child carried, brokers would not be the end of it. Brokers were middlemen. Beyond the stones, in the wide world, there were the heirs of the Court and the servants of the Frostbound, and they had ended a war three hundred years ago the way you end a war: incompletely, and with a treaty instead of a victory. A living seed of the Verdant Court, walking around loose in the one place magic couldn't reach? That wasn't merchandise. That was a match thrown into a powder store.

"Come away," she said, and her voice came out strange and quiet. "Wren. Come away from it now."

Wren looked up at her, tears freezing on her cheeks, and did the cruelest possible thing: she smiled. Radiant, certain, *grateful*. As though Elodie had given her something. As though this were the beginning of something instead of the end of safety.

Elodie got up, brushed snow from her skirts with hands that shook, and made herself look north, past the stones, to where the pass descended into the wide world.

She had never considered leaving. It wasn't courage she lacked, exactly — she'd stolen memories out of soldiers' heads, which takes a kind of nerve — it was that leaving had never been a *line item*. Outside the valley there was no market for her skills, no Maren, no arrangement, no walls. Outside there was only the world, vast and magical and indifferent, and Elodie had spent eleven years making herself small enough that the world couldn't get a grip on her.

But the flower burned white and gold at her feet, and the ice sang its hairline songs, and somewhere under the snow a hundred sleeping seeds were turning over in their long sleep, and Elodie thought: *the valley forgot magic, but magic has apparently not forgotten the valley.*

And she thought, further down, in the cellar of herself where she kept the things she didn't examine: *if magic lives in her, why was she sent here? Of all the gray corners of the world — why to the one thief who knows the dying king's language?*

The wind shifted. Far below, thin as a whetted knife, a bell rang in Hollowmere — the brokers' bell, the one that meant a rider had come up the pass, or word had come down from the stones, or simply that Maren Hale wanted attention and always got it.

Wren's head snapped toward the sound. And then the child did something else new: she grabbed Elodie's hand. Not for guidance. For *reassurance*. Her fingers were small and chapped and strong as roots, and she held on as though Elodie were the fixed point and the rest of the world were weather.

Elodie looked down at their joined hands, then at the flower, which was already folding its petals as the child's attention withdrew — patiently, deliberately, going back to sleep like a soldier who knows the watch isn't over.

"We need to get off this mountain," Elodie said. "And then, Wren, you and I are going to have a conversation, even if I have to learn your whole cursed language to have it."

They started down. Behind them, under the ice, the seeds waited.

And far below, the bell rang again — twice this time, which meant *come*, which meant Maren Hale had heard something, somehow, and wanted everyone with goods to declare them.

Elodie walked faster.
