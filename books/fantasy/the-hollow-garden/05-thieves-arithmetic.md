## 5. Thieves' Arithmetic

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

Elodie knew every kind of knock Hollowmere had to offer. There was the broker's knock — three measured raps, patient as a ledger line. There was the neighbor's knock, apologetic, half-swallowed by wind. There was the drunk's knock, which was less a knock than a slow collapse against the door. And there was the knock of someone who wanted something from you specifically, which arrived with a certain rehearsed cheerfulness, like a smile you could hear.

This knock was none of those. This knock was two soft taps, almost shy, followed by a silence so complete that Elodie might have imagined it — except that Wren, sitting cross-legged on the floor sorting buttons by a color system that existed nowhere but inside her own head, went very still. The girl's fingers stopped mid-air over a brass coat button, and she turned her face toward the door with the unblinking attention of a deer that has heard something in the brush.

Elodie set down the memory-vial she'd been polishing — a good one, a fisherman's recollection of his daughter's first steps, still bright after six years in glass — and crossed the room without letting the floorboards speak.

She looked through the gap in the shutters.

Three figures stood in the blue snow-light of her dooryard. She knew two of them on sight, because knowing them was part of her trade. Ostler Bramm, broad as a barn door, who collected debts for the brokers when debts needed collecting with fists. And beside him, small and neat as a sewing kit, Perren Quill, whose job was never violence but always preceded it. Quill carried a leather case the way a physician carried instruments, and inside that case were vials, and inside the vials were other people's lives, and he handled them all with the same gentle, terrible competence.

The third figure Elodie didn't know, and that frightened her more than the other two together. A tall woman in a gray traveling cloak, hood back, hair the color of old pewter, standing in the snow without any impression that the snow was cold. She wasn't looking at the door. She was looking at the chimney smoke rising from Elodie's roof, and her expression was the expression of a person reading a letter they had written themselves long ago.

"Miss Voss," Quill called out, pleasant as Sunday. "We know you're home. Your fire's lit, and Bramm can smell soup."

Bramm nodded confirmation of this, gravely, as though soup-smelling were one of his certified skills.

Elodie's mind did what it always did under pressure: it began counting. It was an old habit, a thief's habit, the arithmetic of escape. One window at the back, painted shut since autumn. One door, opening inward, which meant whoever came through it would have to turn their shoulders, and turning shoulders took a heartbeat. One child, mute, who could not be told to run and could not scream if caught. One back way down the scree that was really no way at all unless you were a goat or desperate enough to become one.

The arithmetic did not favor her. It rarely did. That was the whole secret of surviving Hollowmere: you didn't win, you just lost more slowly than everyone else.

"One moment," she called back, in the voice of a woman who had nothing whatsoever behind her door but soup. "I'm decent."

"You're never decent," Quill said warmly. "That's why we get along."

* * *

She turned from the shutter and found Wren watching her. Not afraid — that was the remarkable thing, the thing Elodie would think about later, much later, when fear had become a country she lived in. The child simply watched, gray eyes steady, hands folded in her lap among the buttons, waiting for information the world refused to give her in words.

"We have visitors," Elodie said, and then felt foolish, because saying it aloud changed nothing. She knelt, which put them nearly eye to eye — the girl was seven, maybe eight, small for it — and did the only thing she could think of. She reached out and touched the girl's temple with two fingers, the way she'd seen the dying king touch his son in the stolen memory where Elodie had first heard this dead tongue spoken, and she poured a memory through the contact.

Not a big one. She couldn't afford big ones anymore; she'd learned that much in the weeks since the snowfall brought the child to her door. But she had a small one, cheap and useful: the memory of a market crier in some southern town shouting *run*, the whole word, the whole feeling of it, feet already moving. She gave Wren the shape of it — *run, hide, don't come out until I come for you* — wrapped in the image of a cellar door and a candle stub.

Wren's eyes widened. Then she shook her head.

Firmly. With the particular stubbornness of children who have decided something and will not be undecided by adults, armies, or weather.

"No," she said — not in words, she had no words, but in the flat set of her mouth and the way she picked up her button jar and hugged it to her chest like a shield. *No.* I am not hiding in a cellar. I have hidden in cellars. Cellars are how the others were found.

Elodie didn't understand the last part, not fully. But she understood *no*. It was, she reflected, the first opinion the child had ever expressed to her, and of course it had to be the most inconvenient one available.

"Fine," Elodie whispered. "But stay behind me, and if I say the word —" she pressed the market-crier memory deeper, made it loud — "you go out the back even if I'm not behind you. Promise me."

Wren considered this. Then she held up one small hand, palm out, in a gesture Elodie didn't recognize but accepted anyway, because it had the unmistakable gravity of an oath.

The knock came again. Politer this time. Which meant they'd heard her moving around, and were being courteous about the fact that courtesy was now a formality.

* * *

She opened the door before they could break it, because a broken door was a bill she couldn't pay.

"Master Quill. Master Bramm." She stepped into the doorway and let her body fill it, which was a trick — she was a thin woman, and the doorframe was generous — but confidence is mostly posture. "You've caught me at supper. I'd offer you some, but it's turnip, and I like you both too much to do that."

Quill smiled his seam-splitting smile. Up close he smelled of cedar oil and cold iron, the broker's perfume. "Miss Voss. May we come in? It's bitter standing."

"It's bitter everywhere in Hollowmere. That's rather our civic identity." She didn't move. "What brings the honor?"

"The honor," Quill said, "is a missing item." He produced a slate from his coat, and on the slate, in Maren Hale's own upright hand, was a description. *Child, female, approx. seven years. Arrived valley's edge at first snow. Mute. Speaks unknown tongue. Reward negotiable upward.*

"Seen her?" Quill asked.

"I see lots of children," Elodie said. "They bring their parents' memories to sell. Sad little economy, ours."

"This one wouldn't be selling. This one would be *bought*." He said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. "Maren pays well for retrieval, Miss Voss. Better than well. Life-changing well. And she pays a great deal less for obstruction, though I'll grant the second figure is harder to spend from a cell."

Behind Quill, the tall woman in gray shifted her weight, and the snow beneath her boots — Elodie saw it clearly, and wished afterward she hadn't — did not crunch. It *melted*, just slightly, just at the edges, as though the ground itself were flinching away from her.

Frostbound. Or close enough to it that the distinction didn't matter. Magic couldn't cross the valley's borders — everyone knew that, it was the one law Hollowmere had never needed enforcement — but things could walk in wearing magic like a scent on a coat, and this woman wore it like a fur.

"I haven't seen her," Elodie said.

"Mm," said Quill.

"I certainly haven't seen her *in my house*," Elodie continued, warming to it, because lying was also arithmetic and she was good at sums, "which I assume is your next question, so let's save time. No child here. Just me, my turnips, and my late husband's collection of buttons, God rest him, wherever he ran off to."

It was a good lie. It had detail, pathos, a joke. Quill's eyes flicked past her shoulder into the room — one heartbeat, two — and Elodie felt the whole structure of her evening balance on a knife-edge, and then:

Wren sneezed.

A small sneeze. A kittenish sneeze, from somewhere behind the woodpile near the hearth, muffled by a blanket that Elodie had absolutely not left draped there that morning.

Quill's smile spread another inch. "Bless you," he said, to the woodpile.

"Bless the cat," Elodie said instantly. "I have a cat."

"You've never mentioned a cat."

"She's shy. We're both private people. It's why we get along."

Bramm laughed — a single bark, genuinely amused — and then looked at Quill, and the look between them was brief and professional and settled everything. Quill sighed, the sigh of a man who takes no pleasure in his work, which was itself a lie he told daily with great conviction.

"Miss Voss," he said. "Here's how tonight goes. You step aside, we collect the item, Maren pays you a finder's fee for your trouble, and nobody remembers anything unpleasant. Or Bramm steps forward, and we find out how attached you are to your teeth. I genuinely hope for the first one. You're one of my favorite suppliers."

"And if I make you a different offer?" Elodie said.

Quill tilted his head, interested despite himself. Brokers were, at bottom, merchants, and merchants could not help listening to an offer.

* * *

Here is what Elodie owned, if you counted honestly, and she was forcing herself to count honestly now, in the space of about four heartbeats while Quill waited with merchant's patience.

She owned four hundred and eleven memories in glass. She owned a hovel above the frost line that leaked at every joint. She owned a coat with good buttons, a kettle, a bed, and the accumulated expertise of fourteen years of theft.

And she owned one memory that was worth more than all the rest combined.

She had taken it nine years ago from a dying pilgrim on the high pass — a man who had walked out of the white country with frost in his beard and wonder in his eyes, and who had traded her, for a cup of broth and a warm floor, the single most beautiful thing she had ever held. It was a summer. Not a memory *of* a summer — a whole summer, entire, from first thaw to first leaf-fall, experienced from inside: the green coming up through the mud like a shout, the taste of the year's first strawberry, rain that fell warm, a night sky thick with stars over a flowering field, and threaded through all of it the feeling — Elodie had no better word, she had tried for nine years to find one — the feeling of the world being *alive on purpose*. Of warmth as a choice someone had made, and kept making, out of love.

It was the memory she lived in, on bad nights. It was the reason she had never quite managed to believe Hollowmere's winter was natural. It was, though she'd never have admitted it sober, the closest thing she had to a soul.

Brokers had offered her a house in Vellum for it. Plural brokers. Maren Hale had once offered her a house *and* a title.

"Name your price," Quill said now, because she had paused too long, and pause reads as greed.

"A night," Elodie said. "You leave now. You report to Maren that the child passed through days ago, heading south, alone, and that the trail's cold. You buy me one night's head start, and tomorrow morning I'm gone and whatever you find or don't find is no longer my concern."

Quill actually laughed. "That's not an offer, Miss Voss, that's a eulogy."

"It comes with payment." Her mouth was dry. Her heart was going like a trapped bird. Somewhere behind her, Wren was watching, and Elodie found she could not look at the child while she did this, so she looked at Quill instead, which was easier, because Quill's face was designed to be looked at. "One memory. My best. The pilgrim's summer. You know what it is. Everyone in the trade knows what it is. Maren has wanted it for nine years."

The change in Quill was immediate and total, and it was worse than anger. His smile stayed exactly where it was, but everything behind it reorganized, the way a shop reorganizes when a rich customer walks in. Behind him, the gray woman went very still, and the snow around her boots stopped melting, as if even the Frostbound's ambient dread had paused to listen.

"The Verdant summer," Quill said softly. "Genuine? Unspoiled?"

"Taken fresh off a living mind. You can verify the depth yourself."

"That memory is worth ten of you," Quill said. "It's worth this whole rotten valley. Maren would give you —"

"I don't want what Maren would give me. I want a night. Take it or leave it, Master Quill, because the cat is getting restless and so am I."

He studied her for a long moment. She watched him do the arithmetic — thieves' arithmetic, brokers' arithmetic, it was all the same sum with different numbers — weighing a treasure beyond price against one mute child and one thin liar in a doorway. She watched him decide that the treasure was real, because greed is a faith and he was devout. She watched him decide that she must be running *toward* something, some bigger buyer, some cut of the reward, because no one gives away their best thing for a night's head start, no one sacrifices, sacrifice isn't a thing people do, it's a thing that happens to them.

That was the flaw in his arithmetic, and she clung to it like a rail in a storm. Let him believe she was crooked. Crookedness was believable. Kindness would have tipped him off.

"Done," Quill said. "The memory now. Verified."

* * *

Taking a memory from someone was easy. Fourteen years of practice easy. You touched their temple, you found the thread, you pulled, and it came away clean as a splinter, and the other person usually sighed with relief, because most people were glad to be rid of their worst things.

Giving one away was different.

She sat across from Quill at her own table, Wren pressed against her side — the child had abandoned the woodpile and planted herself there, jaw set, daring anyone to object — and she pressed her own temple with her own two fingers, and she pulled.

It did not come away clean. It came away like a tooth. Like a rib. It came away with roots, and she felt every root tear, and she made a sound she was not proud of, low in her throat, and Quill — to his credit, or perhaps simply out of self-interest — did not rush her.

The summer came up out of her in a long silver thread and coiled into the vial he held ready, and the room got colder, or she did. The green went out of the world. Not the actual world — the world outside was winter either way — but the world *behind her eyes*, the reserve, the treasury, the place she retreated to. She felt it empty, and the emptiness had a shape, and the shape was exactly the size of one perfect summer, and she understood suddenly, viscerally, why the old stories said the Verdant Court had died of grief rather than of war.

Stealing hurt other people. Giving hurt *you*. Nobody had ever told her that. It seemed like important information. It seemed like the sort of thing that should be carved over the valley gate.

Quill held the vial up to the lamplight and looked into it, and for one unguarded instant his face went young and wondering, and Elodie hated him for it, and pitied him, and understood him completely, because she had worn that same face for nine years.

"Well," he said quietly. "Well." He corked it, cradled it, tucked it away in his case with the reverence of a priest handling relics. Then he stood, and the merchant came back into his face like a shutter closing. "A night, Miss Voss. Not a minute more. If we find you south of the pass by dawn, the deal sours."

"Understood."

He paused at the door. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I've wondered for years what you were saving that one for." He glanced, briefly, at the small gray-eyed girl glaring at him from Elodie's side. "Now I know, I suppose. Strange arithmetic, yours."

Then they were gone, three sets of footprints filling with snow, and the gray woman's footprints filling faster than they should have, melting and refreezing, melting and refreezing, like the ground trying to spit her out even as it bore her weight.

* * *

Elodie stood in the middle of her hovel and felt the hole inside herself where the summer had been, and it was enormous, and it whistled faintly in the draft like an empty chimney.

Wren tugged her sleeve. When Elodie looked down, the child pressed two fingers to her own temple, then reached up toward Elodie's — *give me one, show me what it cost* — and Elodie caught her wrist gently and shook her head.

"Not that one," she managed. Her voice came out scraped. "There isn't a that-one anymore."

Wren frowned. Then, with the terrifying practicality of children, she climbed onto the chair, took the chipped kettle down from its hook, filled it with snow, and set it on the fire, and pointed at Elodie, and pointed at the chair, and pointed at the kettle again. *Sit. Tea. Now.* It was, Elodie thought dimly, the most fluent conversation they had ever had.

She sat. The tea, when it came, tasted of nothing much, as tea does, and she held the cup in both hands and stared at the fire and tried to inventory what remained. Four hundred and ten memories. A coat. A kettle. A lifetime of other people's best days, and not one of them hers.

And yet.

And yet the hole where the summer had been was not entirely empty. Something had rushed in behind the departing memory, quick as water finding a channel — the weight of a small body leaning against her arm, the fierce pressure of a child's grip on her sleeve, the absurd image of Wren guarding a jar of buttons like a dragon's hoard. New things. Unstolen things. Things that had happened to her instead of being taken from someone else, and that therefore belonged to her in a way nothing else in the house did.

"Giving hurts differently," she said aloud, testing the sentence, finding it true. "Nobody warns you."

Wren looked up at her, and — whether she understood the words or merely the tone — she leaned her head against Elodie's ribs and stayed there.

Outside, the snow kept falling, soft and indifferent, erasing the footprints of everyone who had ever stood in that yard. One night. They had one night, and a goat-track down the scree, and a border of stones that magic could not cross — except, apparently, when it walked in wearing a child.

Elodie finished her tea, packed her coat pockets with vials the way other travelers packed bread, and took down her mother's old walking staff from behind the door, which she had not touched in fourteen years and which, she noticed for the first time, was carved — worn nearly smooth, but carved — with leaves.

She decided not to think about that yet. There would be time on the road. There had better be.

"Wren," she said. "Get your buttons. We're leaving."

The child was already holding them.
