## 6. Over the Stones

The border stones of Hollowmere did not look like much. That was the valley's great joke, if it had one: a ring of gray standing rocks, lichen-crusted, sheep-nibbled, no taller than a man, and every one of them humming with a silence so complete that birds refused to nest there. Travelers came up the pass expecting a wall of thorns or a gate of black iron. What they got was a line of stones you could lean against while you caught your breath — and then they stepped past them and forgot why they'd come, forgot the road behind them, forgot everything that had ever made them interesting.

Elodie had spent twenty years on the inside of that forgetting. She knew exactly what she was walking toward: a world where magic worked, which meant a world where memory-brokers like Maren Hale could reach her, where the Frostbound could find a girl carrying the last seed of the Verdant Court, and where Elodie Voss — thief, fence, hovel-dweller, coward by any honest accounting — would be the most conspicuous woman in three kingdoms.

She went anyway. She was still deciding whether to be proud or furious about that.

"Right," she said to Wren, who stood at her elbow in a coat two sizes too big, salvaged from a dead peddler's cart and belted with rope. "Before we do this, some ground rules."

Wren looked up at her with those dark, patient eyes. The child hadn't spoken since arriving at her door — couldn't, apparently, not in any tongue the valley held — but she had learned, over their week of hiding and running, to say things another way. Elodie lent her a memory the way you'd lend a shawl: *here, cold night, put this around your shoulders.* And Wren would take it, hold it, and hand back something of her own through it — a feeling pressed into the memory like a flower into a book.

It had started by accident, three nights ago, when Elodie had given her the soldier's mother's kitchen — bread rising under cloth, steam on the window, a wooden spoon worn smooth as river stone — just to keep the girl from crying. Wren had taken it and returned it changed. In the memory now there was an extra chair pulled up to the table, small, with a cushion on it, as though someone had always been expected to sit there. Elodie had sat awake until dawn trying to decide whether that counted as a message or a wound.

"Rule one," Elodie said, holding up a finger. "We don't stop between here and the treeline, no matter how impressive anything looks. Rule two—" a second finger, "—if I say run, we run. Not walk briskly. Run. Rule three." She hesitated. She had been composing rule three all morning and it kept coming out wrong. "Rule three is that you tell me if it hurts. The lending. If it hurts."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she reached out and touched Elodie's wrist, and asked for a memory.

Elodie gave her the ferryman's — the one about his daughter learning to whistle, all wet lips and furious concentration, until the whole river knew her triumph. It was a light one. She'd learned to sort them: the heavy memories she kept wrapped in wool at the bottom of her pack, the light ones near the top for lending. A strange new economy. For twenty years she had only ever taken. Now she was running a lending library out of her own skull, and the interest she collected came back in things like this:

The whistling daughter turned toward Wren in the memory and grinned, and taught *her* to whistle, and the two of them stood on the ferry deck making terrible sounds while the father laughed so hard he had to sit down on his own crates.

And underneath it, unmistakable, a meaning pressed flat and simple: *I understand rules. Rules are how people who love each other keep each other.*

"Well," said Elodie, gruffly, because her voice had gone unreliable. "Good. Excellent. You've grasped rule three better than I have."

* * *

They climbed through the last of the pines as the morning fog burned off, and the trees grew sparser and stranger, bent inland as though flinching from something beyond the ridge. Elodie felt the change before she saw the stones — a thinning, like altitude, like the moment before a storm when your teeth ache. Twenty years of living above the frost line had taught her body the valley's moods, and the valley was holding its breath.

The border stones stood in a ragged arc across the saddle of the pass, gray and patient, furred with lichen the color of old pewter. Snow lay unbroken on either side of them. No tracks crossed here. Nothing crossed here. Things arrived in the valley already emptied, and left the same way, and the stones kept whatever difference there was between those two conditions to themselves.

Wren stopped dead.

Elodie watched the girl's face do something complicated — fear and recognition tangled together, the look of someone seeing a house they'd been carried away from as an infant. Wren's small hand found Elodie's sleeve and gripped it, and without asking, without ceremony, she reached for a memory.

Elodie gave her the dying king.

She hadn't meant to. It was the heaviest thing she owned, the one she never sold because no broker paid fairly for grief that size, and it rose in her unbidden — the throne room going gold with evening, the old man's hands trembling on the armrests carved as oak branches, and his voice cracking on words in that same dead tongue Wren had spoken at her door. *The garden sleeps. Find the gardener's daughter. Tell her—* and then nothing, because the memory's owner had died before the king finished, and Elodie had bought a sentence with its ending torn off.

Wren took it and went very still. Her lips moved. No sound came out, but Elodie saw the shape of the words, and they were the king's words, and the girl knew them — knew them the way you know a lullaby, whole, with the ending intact.

Then Wren pressed the memory back into Elodie's hands, and it was different.

The throne room was the same, the dying king the same, but now the light through the high windows fell across a figure standing in the shadows by the door — a young woman in gardener's clothes, dirt on her knuckles, holding a child's hand. And the child was Wren. And the woman was looking straight out of the memory at Elodie, and her face was—

"No," Elodie said aloud. "No, that's not mine. That's not in mine. I've had this memory eleven years, I know every corner of it—"

But the woman's face stayed, patient, familiar in a way that scraped somewhere below thought, and Wren was tugging her sleeve and pointing through the stones, urgently, *come, come*, and there was no time to stand in a pass arguing with a stolen memory about whether it had grown a door.

"Rule two," Elodie managed. "Run."

* * *

Crossing the line of stones was like walking through a curtain of cold water that never quite broke over your head. One step, the valley's hush pressing on her ears. Another step, and —

Elodie Voss fell to her knees in the snow on the far side of the border and discovered that she had been deaf her entire life.

Not deaf, precisely. But the world on the far side of the stones was *loud*. Magic didn't hum here; it roared, it sang, it chattered like a market crowd. It was in the ground under her palms, a deep green pulse like a heartbeat the size of the horizon. It was in the snow itself, which wasn't snow, she realized, it was light given a place to rest, each flake a small held note. It was in the air, in the trees down the slope — real trees, oak and rowan, their branches bare but *aware*, leaning toward the sun with the slow attention of sleeping cats. Everything was connected to everything else by threads she could suddenly see, silver-green filaments running from root to soil to stone to sky, and threaded through it all, everywhere, the smell of growing things. In winter. Under snow. The land was asleep, not dead, and it dreamed loudly.

"Oh," she said. It came out very small. "Oh, that's — that's completely unfair."

Twenty years in Hollowmere. Twenty years of a world heard through wool, tasted through gauze, and she had called it quiet. She had called herself practical. She had built a whole life in a muffled room and told herself the muffle was the world.

Wren crouched beside her, worried, and offered back a memory — the kitchen again, the bread, the extra chair — wrapped now in a question: *is it too much?*

"It's not too much," Elodie said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle because her eyes were doing something embarrassing. "It's too little. It was always too little. I just didn't—" She stopped. Breathed. The green pulse of the earth moved under her knees like the flank of an enormous breathing animal. "Help me up. If I kneel here admiring the dirt all day we'll both freeze, and I refuse to die in a world with this much color in it."

* * *

They came down off the pass as the afternoon slanted long and amber, and Elodie learned that her senses were not done betraying her.

She could taste the wind and it tasted of places — pine resin and far-off woodsmoke and, faintly, absurdly, oranges, from some orchard leagues south that the wind had visited on its way. She could hear a stream under the ice and hear the fish under the stream. When she brushed her hand along a rowan trunk as they passed, the tree noticed. There was no other word for it. A slow, woody attention turned toward her fingertips like a face, and something traveled up her arm that was either warmth or welcome or both, and she snatched her hand back and wiped it on her coat.

"Don't touch things," she told Wren, who was touching everything.

Wren, in fairness, had cause. The land knew her. Elodie watched it happen: the snow softened where the girl walked, not melting, just yielding, the way a mattress yields. Birds — actual birds, in January — dropped out of the bare branches to hop along in their wake, keeping a respectful distance, singing what Elodie could now plainly hear was *greeting*, layered and conversational. Once, crossing a frozen meadow, Wren paused over a drift and pressed her palm flat against it, and beneath the ice something green stirred, unfurled, and went back to sleep, leaving a fern-shaped shadow in the snow like a signature.

"You're a walking scandal," Elodie said. "Every broker from here to the coast would pay a kingdom for you. Which is why we're walking very fast and not telling anyone, ever, anything."

Wren handed her back a memory — the whistling daughter, repurposed, the two of them on the ferry deck — but changed again. This time the whole river was listening. Every reed, every mooring post, every fish under the hull, all of them turned toward the terrible joyful noise, and none of them minded it.

*I'm loud here,* the memory said. *You're loud here too. Isn't it wonderful?*

"It is not wonderful," Elodie lied. "It's dangerous, and it's exhausting, and my head feels like a kettle." It was wonderful. She was grinning like an idiot and she knew it and she let it happen, because there was no one to see except a mute child and several hundred attentive trees.

* * *

Dusk found them in the shelter of a hedgerow — an actual hedgerow, hawthorn and briar, grown thick and deliberate, and Elodie understood with a jolt that it had been *planted*, laid out by hands, generations ago, a wall made of patience. Someone had tended this. Someone had wanted a boundary here and grown it instead of building it, and the hedge remembered being planted, and the memory of it was a low contented hum like a dog dreaming by a fire.

Everything remembered here. That was the other thing the valley had starved her of without her knowing: a world where nothing was forgotten, where even stones had tenure. In Hollowmere, memory was currency because it was scarce, hoarded, stolen, sold. Out here memory was weather. It fell on everything equally.

Elodie sat with her back against the hedge, shared out the last of the bread and the dried apples, and tried not to think about the woman's face in the dying king's memory. Tried, and failed, and decided to fail productively.

"That woman," she said. "In the throne room. The gardener." Wren went still, apple halfway to her mouth. "You showed me her on purpose. At the stones." She kept her voice level, the way she did when a mark was about to lie to her. "You know her."

Wren set down the apple. She reached for Elodie's hand, and this time she didn't take a memory so much as open one — hers, offered palm-up, the first time she had ever given instead of borrowed.

It was fragmentary, a child's memory, all sensation and no order: being carried through snow at night, urgent strides, a heart beating fast under an ear pressed to a shoulder. A voice — the gardener's voice, low and fierce, speaking the dead tongue into the dark. And threaded through it, repeated like a refrain, one phrase that Wren had been carrying since before she could talk, the way you carry a song whose words you don't know:

*Find the gardener's daughter. Find the gardener's daughter. Find the gardener's daughter.*

The memory closed. Wren looked at her, dark eyes steady in the dusk, and pointed one mittened finger — deliberately, gravely, with the terrible accuracy of children — at Elodie's chest.

The hedge hummed. Somewhere down the valley, a fox barked once and went quiet. Elodie became aware that every thread of green in the landscape, every filament she'd marveled at all day, had gone still — the whole listening world holding its breath, waiting to see what she would say.

"I think," she said finally, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "that we should get to that inn Tam's map mentions before I have to think about a single word of this."

Wren nodded solemnly, picked up her apple, and took a bite.

But as they packed up and moved out under the first stars — real stars, close and ringing with light — Elodie caught the girl glancing back at her, small and satisfied, like someone who has planted a seed of her own and intends to wait.

Down the darkening road, past the humming hedge, a light was burning in a window. And nailed above the inn door, just visible in its glow, hung a sign shaped like a tree in leaf.
