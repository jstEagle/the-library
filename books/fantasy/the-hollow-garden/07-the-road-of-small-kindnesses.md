## 7. The Road of Small Kindnesses

*green green green was the first word and it was not a word at all but a color that had a name before names were cold*

Wren walked beside the tall one — *Elodie*, the lending-memories said, *Elodie who takes, Elodie who sells* — and Wren did not say this with her mouth because her mouth kept other words now, old words, seed-words. But she could put things into the space between them, small things, like dropping pebbles into a still pond, and watch them ripple across the woman's face.

She dropped the first pebble on the second morning.

*Pebble:* a courtyard where the fountains ran honey instead of water, and a boy with mud on his knees laughing because the bees had gotten into his sleeves.

Elodie stumbled on nothing. "What — what was that?"

Wren shrugged, which in every language means the same thing: *I don't know, I only have it.*

They were out of Hollowmere now, past the border stones, into a country where the air itself seemed to remember being alive. Wren felt it the way you feel a blanket someone has just laid over you while you sleep — not as an event, but as a warmth arriving from outside yourself. The magic here didn't hide. It sat in the hedges like birds. It pooled in the wheel-ruts of the old road. When Elodie wasn't looking, Wren let a dandelion open early just to see the woman's face do the thing it did.

The face did the thing.

"You can't just *do* that," Elodie hissed, glancing up and down the empty road as though the dandelion might be reported to authorities. "Someone will see."

Wren pointed at the sky, the road, the three crows, the distant smudge of hills. *See who?*

"Someone always sees," Elodie muttered, and pulled her hood lower, and walked faster, and Wren understood then that the woman carried her valley inside her like a stone in a shoe. She had been walking on it so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her foot.

*Pebble:* the smell of bread that sang when it came out of the oven. Actually sang. A low round note, like a monk humming through flour.

Elodie stopped dead in the road. "Bread does not sing."

Wren grinned. It was the best game. She had spent her whole life — all six years of it, or however many; counting was a court art and she'd been bad at it even before the fall — she had spent her whole life surrounded by people who treated wonder like a rationed food. Here was a woman who had never once been given any, and Wren had pockets full.

*Pebble:* a queen who juggled pears at midwinter because the official entertainment had frozen solid, literally frozen, mid-song, and the whole court had laughed until the ice cracked off the windows.

"She *juggled*?" Elodie said. Then, catching herself: "This isn't real. These are memories. You're showing me memories." But she said it the way people say *it's only rain* while standing in a downpour.

They walked. The road was called, according to a leaning signpost, the Old Green Way, though the green had mostly retired. Frost furred the hedgerows. Their breath went up in little ghosts. Wren counted their footsteps in the old tongue — *one, two, three* — words that tasted like sap and sunlight, and wondered if the tall one would ever want to learn them.

On the third day, Wren stopped playing games.

Because the road bent around a hill, and on the hill stood a tree, and the tree was wrong.

It was an oak, or had been. Now it was glass. Every branch, every leaf, frozen mid-shiver into clear grey crystal, and inside the crystal — Wren pressed her palm against the trunk and the pebbles turned to knives —

*Not a pebble. An avalanche.*

---

*the court in the season of the last summer, and everyone pretending not to count the days*

*a hall of living wood where the pillars grew new rings each year and the children carved their names and the pillars healed by morning, forgiving*

*her mother's hands, soil-black, pressing a seed into Wren's palm: keep this, little wren, keep this where no frost can find it, and if I cannot come for you, find the one who was hidden, find the gardener's daughter, she will—*

*(the memory frayed there, burned at the edges like paper held too near a candle)*

*then the white coming over the mountains like a tide that did not stop for doors, and the singing — oh, the Frostbound sang beautifully, that was the worst of it, they sang while the garden went grey*

*the king on the steps with his crown of thorns that bloomed rather than bled, saying: we do not fight winter with winter, we wait it out, we are patient, we are seeds — and the white reaching him, and his last word not a command but a name, a small name, a child's name—*

*running. carried. cold. a door in a mountain. hands passing her like a lantern from dark to dark, each pair of hands warmer than the last, each face more frightened*

*and then the valley, the hollow valley where magic went to die, and the silence, and the silence, and the silence—*

---

Wren came back to herself sitting in the ditch with Elodie's coat around her shoulders and the woman's face very close and very pale.

"Don't do that again," Elodie said, and her voice shook, which was interesting, because Wren had thought the woman's voice was incapable of shaking. "You were gone. Your eyes went somewhere and you weren't — you were just *gone*, and there was frost coming off you like smoke, and I thought—" She stopped. Repacked her face into its usual shape, the one like a locked drawer. "Don't do that again without warning me."

Wren leaned against her. Just briefly. Just to see if the tall one would flinch.

The tall one did not flinch. The tall one went stiff as a fence post, then slowly, grudgingly, like a gate deciding to open, softened about four percent.

"There's a village ahead," Elodie said into the middle distance. "Or a farm. Smoke. We need food, and you need — whatever it is you eat. And we need to not talk about kings."

But that night, wrapped in a haybarn that smelled of mouse and safety, Wren sent one more pebble across the dark. Not a game-pebble. A true one.

*Pebble:* the courtyard again, the honey fountains, the muddy-kneed boy — but older now, and holding a baby, and saying to the baby in the old tongue: *this is your cousin Wren, and someday she will be insufferable, and you will love her anyway.*

Elodie sat bolt upright in the straw.

"That boy," she whispered. "I've seen him. Not — not like this. In a memory I took, years ago, from a dying man. He was old. He was in a kitchen, and he was calling for his mother, and he was speaking *that language*, and I sold the memory and I never—" Her breath went ragged. "Wren. Who is he? Who was he?"

Wren looked at her for a long time. The barn was dark and the mice were rude and somewhere far away the winter was sharpening its knives.

She reached out and, very gently, gave the tall one the answer the only way she could: the feeling of the boy's hand in hers, big and warm, and the sound of his voice saying *little wren* in the old tongue, and underneath it, worn smooth by time, the shape of a truth:

*he was the last of us besides you and me. and he died in a valley that forgot his name.*

Elodie made a sound Wren had never heard a grown person make — small and cracked, like an egg with something alive still inside it.

"The soldier," she said. "The soldier whose mother's kitchen I stole. That was — he was *Court*. He was one of yours. And I sold his dying to a broker for coin." She laughed, and it was a terrible laugh, the kind that hurts the laugher. "Of course I did. Of course."

Wren crawled over the straw and did the boldest thing in her entire life, bolder than the running and the carrying and the door in the mountain. She took the woman's calloused thief-hand and pressed it flat against her own small chest, where the seed slept under her ribs like a coal.

And she showed her — not a pebble this time, not a game, but the plain unadorned fact at the center of everything, the message she had crossed a kingdom to deliver, the whole reason for the silence and the snow and the long frightened road:

*you are the gardener's daughter. you are the one my mother named. i was sent to find you. i found you. please don't make me have found you for nothing.*

Elodie pulled her hand back as if the seed had bitten her.

"No," she said. "No, you're wrong. I'm nobody. I'm a thief from a hole in the ground. My mother was—" She stopped. Started again. Stopped again. The locked-drawer face was failing her; Wren could see the drawers sliding open behind her eyes whether she wanted them to or not. "My mother left me in Hollowmere when I was small. That's all I know. That's *all anyone knows.*"

*Pebble:* a woman with soil-black hands and Elodie's exact stubborn chin, laughing, turning away from a doorway, looking back one last time.

Elodie made the egg-crack sound again and then was quiet for a very long time, lying in the straw with her back to Wren, and Wren pretended to sleep and watched the woman's shoulders shake in the moonlight and learned something important: that belonging, for people who have never had it, arrives like hot water on frostbitten skin. It hurts worse than the cold did. They flinch from it. They say *no, take it back, I was fine.*

Wren resolved, with the full gravity of six years of wisdom, to simply keep applying warmth until the tall one stopped flinching.

In the morning, Elodie was brisk and brittle and bought them porridge at a crossroads inn with coins she counted twice, and did not mention kings or mothers or gardens, and Wren let her have her drawer.

But at the edge of the inn's yard, an old man mending a fence looked up as they passed, and looked again, and his mallet fell from his hand into the mud.

"You," he said. His voice was strange, half wonder and half dread. "Girl. Where did you get that cloak-pin? That was Court work. That was *before*." He limped toward them, favoring a leg that had clearly argued with a war. "You'd better come inside. Both of you. There are people who pay good money for news of the Court, and worse people who pay better for everything else — and one of them passed through here not three days ago asking after a woman and a child."

He held the door open. Behind him, a fire crackled, and a kettle sang — actually sang, a low round note, and Elodie stared at it as though it had personally betrayed her.

"Well?" the old man said. "Coming in, or shall I shout your descriptions to the road?"

Elodie's hand found Wren's shoulder. Tight. Deciding.

They went in.
