## 8. Ashgrove Inn

The inn appeared out of the snow like something remembered rather than seen.

One moment the road was white nothing, and Wren's small hand in Elodie's had gone from cold to that frightening not-cold of a thing giving up, and then — there. A squat stone building hunched against the hillside, its roof sagging under two feet of snow, its single window glowing amber as if the light inside were warm butter. A sign swung above the door on a bracket gone green with age, and though the paint had flaked away to ghosts, Elodie could just make out the shape of an ash tree.

"Ashgrove," she read aloud, her voice cracked from disuse. "That's — that's a good omen, I think. Probably."

Wren looked up at her. The girl hadn't spoken — couldn't, or wouldn't; Elodie still wasn't sure which — but she had developed, over three days of shared memories passed between them like coins under a table, a whole vocabulary of looks. This one said: *you are lying to comfort yourself, and I have noticed.*

"You're very cheeky for someone who can't talk," Elodie muttered, and knocked.

The door opened before her knuckles fell a second time.

The man standing in it was built like something meant to hold up bridges. He was sixty at least, with a beard gone entirely to frost-white and a face so weathered it looked less like skin than like a map of somewhere difficult. He wore a leather apron over a wool shirt, and he held a cleaver in one hand with the easy, terrible familiarity of a man who had once held other things.

He looked at Elodie. He looked at Wren. He looked at the snow behind them, where their footprints were already filling in, erasing them, as though the world itself were trying to pretend they'd never come.

"Deserters don't knock," he said at last. His voice was gravel poured slow. "They crawl. So either you're not deserters, or you're polite ones."

"We're not—" Elodie began.

"Girl's lips are blue," the man said. "Argue inside."

* * *

The interior of the Ashgrove Inn was one large room, low-ceilinged and fire-warmed, smelling of woodsmoke, onions, and something medicinal that Elodie decided not to ask about. A pot bubbled over the hearth. A cat — enormous, orange, and missing half an ear — regarded them from atop a barrel with the expression of a customs official.

"Sit," the man said. "Not you." This to Wren, who had made for the fire. "You, by the fire, absolutely. You look like a frozen wren."

Wren blinked. Then, slowly, she smiled — the first full smile Elodie had seen from her — and pointed at herself, and then at the mantelpiece, where a little carved bird sat among the clutter: a wren, wooden, worn smooth by handling.

"Huh," the man said. "Clever."

"She understands more than she says," Elodie offered, hovering. "She can't speak. There's something wrong with her voice, or—"

"There's nothing wrong with her voice," the man said, ladling soup into bowls without looking up. "I've heard that silence before. It's not emptiness. It's held breath." He set the bowls down hard enough to slop. "Eat. Then we'll talk about why a valley-thief and a Verdant child are walking my road in the dead season, because I'm retired, and retired men only get visited for bad reasons."

Elodie's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "Verdant—"

"Eat first," he repeated. "Bad news keeps. Good soup doesn't."

They ate. The soup was potato and leek and salt, and it was, without competition, the best thing Elodie had ever tasted, though she suspected part of that was the three days of snow and half-rations. Wren ate like a small polite wolf. The cat descended from the barrel, inspected Wren with great ceremony, and then installed itself in her lap with the air of a decision being finalized.

"His name is Turnip," the man said. "He likes maybe four people in the world and hates everyone else, so don't take it personally when he leaves."

"He can stay," Wren did not say, but her whole body said it, curled around the cat like a wall around a garden.

"I'm Tam," the man said finally, settling into the chair opposite with the groan of a man whose knees kept their own records. "Tam Vetch. And you're going to tell me your names, and then you're going to tell me the truth, because I spent thirty years reading lies for a living and I can still taste them."

"Elodie Voss," Elodie said. "And this is Wren. She came to Hollowmere in the first snow. I've been — looking after her."

"Mm," said Tam. "And how does a memory-thief out of Hollowmere come to be *looking after* anything? Last I knew, your kind sold everything, including the looking-after."

The words landed harder than they should have, mostly because they were true. Elodie put down her spoon.

"How do you know what I am?"

"Because I know what the brokers pay, and I know what kind of person walks out of that valley with broker-money in her pocket and frostbite on her boots." Tam's eyes were pale grey, the grey of old ice, and they didn't blink enough. "Also because Maren Hale sent word ahead through three hands that a thief and a child went over the border stones, and described your face well enough that I'd know it at a funeral. You're being hunted, girl. I'm surprised you didn't know."

Elodie's stomach dropped through the floorboards. "Maren — she took my payment, she let us go—"

"She let you go *out the gate*, not off the leash." Tam leaned forward. "Why do you think the road's been empty? Why do you think every farmhouse has its shutters closed? Word travels faster than feet out here. Every soul between here and Vellum knows there's a price on a mute child, and most of them need the money worse than they need their neighbors' goodwill."

Wren had gone very still. Turnip, sensing the change, pressed his head up under her chin.

"So run," Tam said. "Or hide. Or — no. No, you won't, will you." He sighed, a long sound like wind leaving a barn. "Because she's got that look. They all had that look. Sit down properly, Elodie Voss. You're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

What Tam told them, he told slowly, in the manner of a man unpacking something heavy from a box he'd hoped never to open again. He talked with his hands sometimes. Once he got up and fed the fire though it didn't need feeding.

"I was Frostbound," he said. "You know the word?"

"I know it the way you know a word from a nightmare," Elodie said. "It's in the memories I've stolen. Soldiers say it. Old people say it. Nobody says it *happily*."

"That's about right." Tam turned his bowl in his hands. "Before the long winter, there were two courts. The Verdant Court — growing things, green things, the turning of seasons, all that living machinery nobody thinks about until it stops. And the Frostbound, who were never evil, whatever the songs say. We were *cold*. That's all. Cold is patient, cold is quiet, cold believes that anything that must end should end sooner, cleanly, without fuss. Winter isn't cruelty. Winter is efficiency."

"And the war?" Elodie asked quietly.

"The war was about whether the world gets to keep changing." He laughed, short and awful. "That's the whole of it. All those battles in the songs, the heroes, the betrayals — underneath, it was the Court saying *everything blooms and rots and blooms again*, and us saying *why not just stop, once, beautifully, forever*. Like arguing with a river about whether it should be allowed to reach the sea."

He was quiet a while. The fire popped. Wren watched him with her whole face.

"We lost," Tam said. "Not the way stories say. We lost because the Verdant Court chose to end itself rather than let the war go on. The last of them gathered at their court — you'd know the place as ruins now, glass and stone under the ice — and they poured everything they were into one seed. One seed, holding the whole living memory of the Court. The idea of spring, compressed into something you could hold in two hands. And then they scattered their people to hide it, and the winter came down like a lid, and it hasn't fully lifted in a hundred years. That's the long winter. Not weather. Weather is just its shadow."

"And the seed," Elodie said. Her mouth had gone dry.

"The seed needs a keeper. Living blood of the Court, grown around it, carrying it, keeping it alive until the world's ready." Tam looked at Wren, and his voice gentled in a way that frightened Elodie more than shouting would have. "Someone hid that child in Hollowmere, where magic can't reach and the Frostbound can't feel her. Someone spent years making sure she'd be invisible. And now she's sitting in my kitchen eating my soup, and every power left in this world knows she exists."

"But she's a *child*—"

"She's a *key*," Tam said. "And keys don't get to stay children, that's the grief of it. Listen to me, girl, because I buried friends over this and I'd rather not bury anyone else." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight made his face into something carved and old. "The Frostbound will come for her, and they won't hurt her — that's the trap. They'll offer her thrones. They'll offer *you* things. Your mother, your past, your heart's desire, whatever the brokers dug out of you. They are very, very good at offering. And the moment you take anything, you're theirs, because the offer was never the point. The point is that you *want*."

"And the brokers?" Elodie whispered.

"Worse. The brokers don't want her for either side. They want her as *product*. A living seed of the Verdant Court, harvested and bottled and sold in pieces — spring by the dram, summer by the vial. Maren Hale would sell the sunrise if she could find the jar for it." He shook his head. "Both sides will use her, Elodie. The only question is whether anyone will ever ask her what *she* wants. And she can't answer, can she? Not in any tongue the world remembers."

Silence. Wren set down her spoon. Very deliberately, she climbed down from her chair, crossed the room, and stood in front of Tam. She looked up at him — this enormous ruined soldier of the losing side — and she reached out and touched his scarred hand.

Then she showed him.

Elodie felt it happen, felt the memory leave her own keeping and pass across the space between them, the way they'd been trading fragments for three days: a kitchen, a mother humming, bread rising under a cloth. But Wren wasn't showing Tam the borrowed thing. She was showing him something of her own — and Elodie caught the edge of it as it passed, one glinting sliver —

— a garden under glass, rows of sleeping seeds, a woman with Elodie's own dark hair kneeling in the loam, planting, singing in the dead tongue, and a small girl hidden behind a hedge watching her mother work—

The vision was gone before Elodie could grab it. It left her shaking, her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out.

Tam had gone white. He stared at Wren, then at Elodie, and something moved across his face that might have been recognition or might have been pity, and both were unbearable.

"What did she show you?" Elodie demanded. "What was — I saw — there was a garden, there was a woman—"

"Girl," Tam said quietly, "what did you say your mother did?"

"I don't—" Elodie's voice cracked. "I don't remember my mother. That's the whole — that's why I steal, that's why I *sell*, because I woke up in Hollowmere at seven years old with nothing, no past, no—"

She stopped.

The fire crackled. Turnip yawned. Outside, the snow fell on the ash tree over the door, patient as everything else in the world.

"Oh," Elodie said, very small.

"They didn't erase you," Tam said gently. "They *hid* you. There's a difference, and it matters, and I think you already know what it is." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to a chest beneath the window. From it he drew a roll of oilcloth, tied with string, and laid it on the table between them. "Map. Roads to Vellum, roads to the court, roads that don't exist officially. Thirty years I've kept it for a night I thought would never come."

"Why give it to us?" Elodie asked. "You're retired. You said—"

"Retired means the war's over." Tam looked at Wren, and then at Elodie, and his old grey eyes were wet. "Nobody told me it was just *paused*."

He turned to the window then, to check the weather, he said — but Elodie saw him stand there a long moment, one hand flat against the glass, looking north toward the ice, like a man listening for footsteps he'd been dreading for thirty years.

And far off, past the hills, past the borders of the map, something answered: a light on the horizon that was not dawn, moving, deliberate, and coming on like winter itself remembering the way.
