## 9. Hunters in the Hedgerow

Tam Vetch had spent forty years learning how to be unremarkable, and he had gotten rather good at it.

He'd been a scout in the Frostbound's third cohort once — quick, quiet, forgettable, the sort of man officers described as "adequate" and then couldn't pick out of a line of six. When he deserted, that adequacy saved his life. Nobody chased adequate men very far. He'd walked out of the war with nothing but a bad knee, a talent for hedgerows, and an inn at the crossroads of nowhere, and for thirty years he had polished all three into a life.

The inn was called Ashgrove, which was a joke only Tam understood, since there hadn't been an ash grove within fifty miles since the winter came down off the mountains and ate everything green. He kept the name anyway. A man ought to keep one joke going as long as he could.

So when the woman and the child came up his road through the sleet — the woman walking like her boots were full of stones, the child riding her back like a small determined barnacle — Tam did what unremarkable men do. He looked busy. He swept a step that didn't need sweeping and waited to see if trouble would pass him by.

It did not pass him by. It stopped at his gate, dripping.

* * *

"You'll want soup," he said, before either of them could speak. "And you'll say you don't, because travelers are liars about soup. Come in."

The woman — Elodie, she gave reluctantly, like the name cost her something — looked around his common room the way a cat looks around a dog's house. The child looked around it the way a cat looks around anything: with total sovereignty.

"I can pay," Elodie said.

"Everyone says that too." He ladled out three bowls. "Sit. Eat. If you're running from someone, eat faster."

Elodie's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "I'm not—"

"Girl, you've got frostbite on your ears, mud to the knee, and you flinched when I said *someone*. I was a scout for eleven years. I know what running looks like. It looks like soup eaten standing up." He nodded at her bowl. "Sit down. Running people who sit are harder to read."

She sat. The child sat beside her and ate with the focused violence of someone who had known real hunger recently enough to respect it.

Tam asked no questions that night, which was its own kind of question. But he watched. He watched the way the girl's hands moved when she wasn't thinking — little curling gestures, like fingers remembering vines. He watched Elodie watch the door. And he watched, once, when the fire guttered low, the girl glance at it and the flames stand up straighter, just for a breath, like soldiers hearing their captain's boot on the stair.

Tam put down his cup very slowly.

"Well," he said, to nobody, in the old tongue of the Frostbound camps, the one they'd used for jokes the officers weren't meant to hear. "*There's a thing I haven't seen in thirty years.*"

The girl turned her head. She looked at him — really looked, the way you look at a locked door — and then she made a sound. Not a word. A shape of a word, half-formed, green-colored somehow even though it was only air.

Tam's eyes stung, which he blamed on the smoke.

"Ah," he managed. "Right. Yes. More soup, then."

* * *

They stayed two nights, which became four, which became a week, because that is how kindness works — not in decisions but in drifts, like snow against a fence.

By the third day Tam knew most of it, or enough. The valley below, the brokers, the seed the girl carried in whatever place seeds live inside people. He knew because Elodie told him in pieces, always watching his face for the moment he'd decide she was more trouble than soup.

"That moment doesn't come," he told her, when she finally said it all out loud by the fire. "I waited thirty years for it to come with my own conscience, and it never did. You get used to carrying a thing you should have done differently. Some mornings it's the only warm thing you own."

"What should you have done differently?" Elodie asked. She was direct like that. He'd decided he liked it, mostly.

"I stood at the edge of the Verdant Court the night it fell," Tam said. "Third cohort, holding position, waiting for orders that never came because the officers had already run. There was a gardener on the wall — a woman with dirt to her elbows and a pruning knife, fighting soldiers with a *pruning knife*, if you can credit it. She looked right at me. Right through the frost on my visor. And she didn't beg. She just looked disappointed, like I was weather she'd been hoping would miss her garden." He turned his cup. "I could have opened the postern gate. One bolt. It wouldn't have saved the court. But it might have saved the gardeners. I did the arithmetic of a soldier instead. Adequate, again. Always adequate."

Elodie said nothing, but the girl — Wren, Elodie called her, a name borrowed from somewhere, everything about them was borrowed from somewhere — reached over and put her small hand flat on Tam's wrist. Warmth went through him like brandy.

"Don't," he said gruffly, and didn't move his arm away.

* * *

On the sixth morning, Tam's hens refused to come out of their coop.

Now, Tam had been reading hens longer than he'd ever read maps, and hens were honest creatures. They didn't care about politics or war; they cared about foxes, hawks, and men in coats that smelled wrong. Three hens pressed to the back wall of the coop, staring at the treeline with round yellow judgment, meant one thing:

Someone was coming up the south road who did not want to be seen.

He counted the signs after that, and the signs obliged him. Crows lifting off the hedgerow in ones and twos, moving away from the road rather than along it. His dog — a vast, useless, beloved animal named Turnip — standing at the gate with her hackles up, making a low sound he'd only heard twice before, both times before bad company. And under it all, faint as a toothache, the particular silence of people being careful.

Brokers' hunters. Or worse. With a Verdant seed traveling, possibly much worse.

Tam filled the kettle first. Whatever else happened, a man should die with a full kettle on — it was the principle of the thing. Then he went and knocked on the wall of the room where Elodie slept, and when she opened her eyes he put a finger to his lips and said, quietly, in the tone he'd used for eleven years to move soldiers without waking the enemy:

"Up. Boots. Back door. No lamp."

She was good. He'd give her that. She had Wren bundled and awake in under a minute, no questions until they were in the kitchen, and then only one, whispered: "How many?"

"Can't tell yet. Four, maybe five. Coming careful, which means professionals." He was already pulling boards off the wall behind the flour barrel — a cavity he'd built the first year he owned the inn, because a deserter who doesn't build hiding places is just a soldier waiting to be found. Inside: a wrapped bundle, oilcloth, tied with waxed cord.

"Take this," he said.

"What is it?"

"A map. My map. Thirty years of roads — the real ones, the shepherd tracks and the dry fords and the passes the Frostbound think are closed. And marks where the old Court roads still run underneath, if you know how to walk them." He pushed it into her hands. "Follow the ash marks. I carved them myself, years ago, on trees that were already dead. Nobody notices a dead tree."

"Why are you giving me this?"

"Because you're going to need it, and because I'm not." He said it lightly, and hated how easily lightness came, after all these years of practice. "Kettle's on. Go through the kitchen garden, over the wall, into the sheep pasture. Don't take the road until the second mile. And Elodie—" he caught her shoulder, "—the girl eats first. Whatever happens. Promise me something small and keepable."

Elodie's jaw did something complicated. "Come with us."

"Ha. No. My knee and I have an arrangement: we stay within sight of this inn, and it forgives me for the other things." He smiled at her, and found, to his surprise, that it was mostly a true smile. "Besides. Someone has to answer the door. It's what inns are *for*."

* * *

They came at dusk, which showed a certain lack of imagination. Professionals, yes, but broker-bred, not Frostbound-trained; the Frostbound would have come at the hour when even dogs despair.

There were five of them. Tam watched them arrange themselves in his yard with the neat, greedy patience of men who had done this many times to people with fewer friends. Their leader was a narrow woman with a broker's gray cloak and a memory-lantern swinging at her belt — the kind that held harvested recollections in glass, and glowed faintly with other people's best days. Tam had seen those lanterns in the war, carried by requisition officers. He had never gotten used to them. A light made of stolen sunsets. It seemed to him the rudest possible magic.

He opened the door before they knocked. Unremarkable men answered doors promptly; it disarmed people.

"Evening," he said. "You'll be wanting beds. I've got two rooms and the loft, and I'll warn you now the loft has opinions."

The leader smiled the smile of her profession. "Innkeeper. We're looking for a woman and a child. Passed this way, perhaps."

"Half the world's a woman and a child these days. Birth rates being what they are."

"They'd be in a hurry. The woman dark-haired, sharp-eyed. The child mute."

"Mute children are ten a penny too," Tam said. "I get a dozen a season, all of them mysteriously unable to hear the word *wash*. You're welcome to check the loft, but mind the third step, it bites."

She studied him. He let his face do what it had learned to do across forty years: nothing at all, pleasantly. Behind his ribs his heart was going like a drum at a parade, but hearts were private, and faces were public, and a man controlled what he could.

"We'll check the loft," she said.

"Mind the step."

Two of them went inside. Tam remained in the yard with the leader and two others, and the evening got very long and very cold, and Tam thought about the kettle, which was by now certainly boiling, and about whether that counted as dying with a full kettle on, and decided it did, provisionally.

Then, from the loft, a shout: "Empty! Beds cold!"

"Cold?" the leader said softly. "Cold how?"

"Went out hours ago, by the ashes."

The leader turned back to Tam, and her smile fell away like a mask slipping its string. "Innkeeper. Where is the child?"

"Now," Tam said, "that's an interesting question, and I'll tell you why it's interesting. Because the answer depends entirely on who's asking, and for whom, and what happens to the child afterward. If the answer were 'safe,' I'd tell you plain. Since I suspect the answer is 'a lantern,'" — he nodded at the glass at her belt, glowing with somebody's wedding day — "I'm afraid the answer is: gone."

Something shifted in the yard. The two hunters' hands went to their belts. The leader sighed, genuinely, almost sadly.

"Deserter," she said. "Vetch, isn't it? Third cohort. We have your file."

"You have everyone's file. It's very flattering. Do I look taller in it?"

"The seed comes back with us, Vetch. The woman too, if she's worth the rope. You — you can walk away. You've walked away before. We know how good you are at it."

And that — that was the mistake. Not cruelty; cruelty he'd have respected. But they had read his file and drawn the obvious line through it, and the obvious line was wrong, because files don't record the night a gardener looks at you through frost and you spend thirty years failing to un-see her.

"No," Tam said. "As it happens, I've gotten quite good at the opposite."

* * *

Later — much later, in the way of stories told by survivors of other people's last stands — the details would blur. But some things stayed sharp as frost on wire.

He remembered that the yard was the worst possible ground for them and the best he could offer, all at once: hedgerow on three sides, gate on the fourth, and Tam Vetch, adequate at everything, standing in the middle of it with a woodcutter's axe and thirty years of knowing every root and rut of that yard by heart.

He remembered putting out the first man's lantern-light with a thrown pot, because darkness was his oldest friend and he introduced them gladly.

He remembered the hedge itself — the blackthorn he'd spent a decade coaxing into a thicket, on the theory that a coward plants thorns — closing around two hunters like a fist, and how, for one impossible moment, the thorn branches had moved *eagerly*, and somewhere far away a seed in a fleeing child had felt the pull of old allies and leaned toward them in her sleep.

He remembered the leader, competent and cold, and the moment she understood the fight was lost, and chose — as brokers always choose — the ledger over the battle. "Mark the inn," she said to the runner. "Burn what's worth burning. The seed will surface again, and we will be there when it does."

He remembered the axe catching him under the ribs as he covered the gate, and how it hurt less than he'd expected, and how the strangest grief of it was the kettle, surely boiled dry by now, whistling faintly in an empty kitchen.

And he remembered lying in his own doorway, in the blood-warm snow, while the hunters withdrew to regroup, and finding — this was the ridiculous part, the part he'd have laughed at any other time — that he was not afraid. Thirty years he'd assumed the end would find him mid-flinch. Instead it found him mid-kindness, which was, on reflection, a better posture.

Footsteps, soft and fast. Elodie, white-faced, Wren clutched against her, having disobeyed every instruction he'd given — of course she had; he'd have liked her even more for it if liking weren't about to become inconvenient.

"Tam—"

"Ah. No. None of that." He fumbled at his coat, and she understood, and pressed his hands still. "Map's with you. Good. Listen, then, since you won't let me be mysterious about it. The ash marks — follow them past the White Ford, don't trust the bridge, it's watched. And the girl—" his breath caught, and Wren leaned close, and laid her hand on his chest, and warmth bloomed there, brief and green and utterly unlike anything a body should hold, "—ah. Oh, that's *lovely*. Tell her—"

He looked at Wren. Wren looked at him. And in the old camp-tongue, the joke-language, the language of men who had once served the winter and lived to be ashamed of it, he said: "*Tell her the gardeners win.*"

Wren's face crumpled and shone at once, and she made her half-word, her green-shaped sound, and pressed it into his hands like something being returned.

"There," Tam said. "Adequate. Finally."

* * *

They buried him at dawn under the blackthorn hedge he'd grown, which Elodie said was fitting and Wren insisted, by taking Elodie's hand and pressing it to the hedge until a single early blossom opened out of season among the thorns, small and stubborn and absurdly white.

Elodie stood a long time in the cold with the oilcloth map heavy under her arm. She had stolen hundreds of memories and never once been given one freely, and now the last thing anyone would ever give her was a dead man's roads and a dead man's guilt, and she found she valued them more than everything in her hoard.

Behind them, smoke rose thin from Ashgrove Inn, where the brokers burned what was worth burning and found, in the end, that the only thing of value in the whole building was the absence of the people who'd left it.

"Come on," Elodie said hoarsely, and took Wren's hand. "We have miles of dead trees to find."

And far to the north, in a city of kept things, a gray-cloaked woman set a memory-lantern on a broker's desk and said, "The seed lives. Send word to Corvane."
