## 10. The City of Kept Things

The road to Vellum smelled like other people's Sundays.

Elodie noticed it first as a warmth on the wind — woodsmoke and roasting onions and something sweeter beneath it, baked apples maybe, or the particular golden smell of bread that has been left to rise in a sunny window. Hollowmere had never smelled like that. Hollowmere smelled of frost and wet stone and the inside of her own hood, which after eleven years was not so much a smell as a fact of existence.

Wren walked beside her with her small hand fisted in the hem of Elodie's coat, and every hundred paces or so she would stop dead, point at some ordinary thing — a crow, a milestone, a puddle with a rainbow skin of oil — and look up at Elodie with an expression of such ferocious delight that Elodie felt briefly obliged to be delighted too.

"It's just a puddle," Elodie said, for perhaps the ninth time since dawn.

Wren pointed again, insistent.

"All right. It's a very good puddle."

This seemed to satisfy her. The child had no words yet that Elodie could understand — though over the days since the border stones, they had built a small country of their own out of gestures and borrowed images. When Wren wanted to communicate something too large for pointing, she would press her palm flat against Elodie's wrist, and a memory would bloom between them: green light through leaves, a hall of singing stone, hands planting something precious in dark soil. In return, Elodie had learned to lend her small warm things — a memory of honey on bread, of a fire crackling, of snow seen from indoors rather than out. It was, Elodie reflected, the first time in her life she had ever given anything away without being paid, and the strange part was how little it emptied her. Lending, it turned out, was not the same as losing.

The city announced itself before they saw it: a hum, low and constant, like a hive heard through a wall. Then the road crested a hill, and there it was.

Vellum sat in a bowl of hills like a coin at the bottom of a purse. From this distance it looked almost gentle — pale walls, slate roofs, a scatter of towers — but Elodie knew what the hum was. She had grown up on stories of Vellum, told in the memory-brokers' back rooms of Hollowmere by men who had made their pilgrimages there and come back richer and somehow smaller. Every soul in that city carried a ledger. Memories were minted there like coins, weighed there like grain, spent there like water. Somewhere in those pale walls were vaults where a person could walk in poor and walk out having lived another man's wedding, another woman's grief, another child's whole summer.

And somewhere in those vaults, Elodie thought with a lurch, might be records. Ledgers. Names.

"Right," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "Rules."

Wren looked up.

"Rule one: I talk. You don't." She crouched to the girl's eye level. "Not because you can't — because you shouldn't. A mute child is sad. A child speaking a dead tongue in a city where dead tongues are worth money is a *commodity*. Do you understand commodity?"

Wren considered this, then pressed her hand to Elodie's wrist. What came through was not a word but a feeling: a market stall, bright awnings, and beneath it a sensation of being *looked at* — measured, priced, appraised. Wren knew exactly what a commodity was. She had been one longer than Elodie had been free.

Elodie swallowed. "Well. Good. We're agreed, then."

* * *

They came through the Westgate at midday, and the hum resolved into voices.

Vellum's streets were narrow and tall, the buildings leaning toward each other overhead like gossips, and everywhere — everywhere — were the brokers' marks. Little brass plaques beside doors: MEMORIES BOUGHT AND SOLD. FAIR RATES FOR CLEAN EXTRACTIONS. A shop window displayed its wares the way a confectioner might: small glass vials glowing faintly, each labeled in a fine hand. *First Snowfall, Age Six. The Smell of Your Grandmother's House. Learning to Whistle.* People drifted along the street with the unhurried air of the comfortably indebted, pausing at windows, comparing vials.

Elodie kept her head down and her hood up and did what she had done in a hundred towns: became furniture. Furniture was safe. Furniture had no face worth remembering.

It worked beautifully for nearly two hours.

In that time she found them a room above a dye-shop — paid for with a soldier's memory of his mother's kitchen, which stung less than it once would have, because she had already begun keeping a list, inked on the inside of her own forearm, of everything she meant to give back. The kitchen was third on the list. The landlady took the vial without ceremony, held it to the light like a jeweler, and handed over a key with a smile that said *good business* in any language.

It also worked badly in one specific way, which was that Wren had never in her life been furniture, and did not intend to start now.

"Stop *touching* things," Elodie hissed, hauling the girl away from a fruit stall for the fourth time. "You're meant to be invisible. Invisible means you don't pick up the pears."

Wren pointed at the pears with great dignity, as if to say these pears had personally requested her attention.

"You cannot eat evidence," Elodie said. "That's rule two."

She bought the pears. She was weak; she knew it; she filed it away under things to feel guilty about later, in the spacious wing of guilt she maintained for exactly this purpose.

They were crossing the square by the old fountain — a broad cobbled space where a memory-fair was setting up, stalls going up like tents before a battle, hawkers calling out their stock — when Elodie felt it.

A prickle. The particular prickle of being *known*.

She had felt it before, in Hollowmere, whenever a broker's runner looked at her too long. But this was different. This was older and heavier, like a hand laid on the back of her neck. She turned, casually, the way you turn when you are absolutely not turning —

— and saw him.

He stood at the edge of the fountain, an old man selling roasted chestnuts from a cart, and he was staring at her as if she were a ghost wearing his daughter's coat. He was perhaps seventy, weathered brown as an old boot, with a white beard trimmed short and eyes gone pale as river ice. And Elodie knew him.

Not from life. From inventory.

Tomas Aldery. That was the name on the vial. Memory #44 in her collection, extracted outside Harrow Ford nine years ago, sold within the week to Maren Hale: *a harvest evening, age nineteen, dancing in a barn with a girl named Sera while the fiddles went mad and the cider went round*. One of her earliest lifts. One of her cleanest. He had been drunk and grieving and lonely by a fire, and she had taken the best night of his youth out of him like a splinter, and he had wept afterward without knowing why, and she had walked away into the dark feeling nothing at all.

Except now she was standing in front of him, nine years later, wearing her own face, and his chestnut tongs had gone still in his hands.

"*Sera?*" he said.

The world narrowed to the space between them. Around them the fair went on setting up, the fountain went on chuckling, the pears went on weighing down her bag, and none of it mattered, because Tomas Aldery was looking at her with nine years of hope rising in his ruined face.

"No," Elodie said quickly. "I'm sorry. You've mistaken me for—"

"Sera." He came around the cart, slow, one hand out. "You'd be — what, thirty now? Twenty-nine?" His voice cracked on the number. "They told me you went south. They told me—" He stopped, close enough now that she could see the tremor in his hand, and his brow folded. "No. No, that's not right. Why isn't that right?"

Because I have your memory of her, old man, Elodie thought, sick. Because I know her laugh better than you do now. Because when you dance with her in your mind, you're dancing with my stolen goods.

"I'm not her," Elodie said, and hated how gently it came out. "I'm sorry. I have one of those faces."

But he wasn't listening anymore, because Wren had stepped slightly forward — protective, absurdly, this scrap of a girl squaring up to shield Elodie — and the old man's gaze dropped to her, and softened, and then sharpened all at once.

"That mark," he said quietly. "On her wrist. My grandmother had that mark. Said it was from the old country, before the winter." He looked up, and the hope in his face had changed shape entirely — cooled, concentrated, become something more dangerous. "You're not from here at all, are you, miss. Either of you."

"We're passing through," Elodie said. "Thank you for the directions."

She took Wren's hand and walked. Fast. Not running — running was a confession — but fast, cutting through the fair stalls, past a hawker crying "*Clean joys! Gently used sorrows! Best rates in the western quarter!*", past a woman trying on someone else's childhood like a hat.

Behind her, she did not hear him follow. That frightened her more than pursuit would have. A man who hoped does not chase; a man who hopes *waits*, and tells people, and people in Vellum who are told things about unusual children think immediately of brass plaques and fair rates.

* * *

The room above the dye-shop was small and smelled of vinegar, and Elodie barred the door and sat on the bed with her elbows on her knees and her head in her hands, doing arithmetic.

One: the old man had seen her face. Two: he had connected her face to a stolen memory — which meant he remembered the *gap*, the hole where Sera's night used to be, and holes make people curious, and curiosity in Vellum was currency-adjacent. Three: he had seen Wren's wrist, and the mark there, the small green whorl like a fingerprint of a leaf, and he had known it, and knowing things got people paid.

"He'll tell someone," she said to the ceiling.

Wren, sitting cross-legged on the floor shelling pears with intense concentration, looked up.

"Some broker will come asking after a woman with my face and a marked child, and they'll pay him for the telling, and he'll take the money because he's poor, and I won't even blame him, because I did worse than that to him and called it business." Elodie laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what I did to that man? I took the single best night of his life. He's been walking around with a hole in him shaped like a barn dance for nine years, and today he saw the thief wearing the missing piece."

Wren set down the pear. She crossed the little room, climbed onto the bed beside Elodie with the unselfconscious gravity of the very young, and pressed her palm flat against Elodie's wrist.

What came through was not comfort. It was a question, asked in the language of images: *a vault door, iron-banded, standing open; and beyond it, shelves and shelves of small glass lights, each one a life; and a pair of hands — Elodie's hands — reaching in, not to take, but to set something back.*

Elodie stared at her. "How do you know about the vaults?"

Wren only looked at her, patient, the way she looked at puddles and crows and other miracles, and Elodie understood that the question hadn't been *how do you know* but *when will you go*.

"I can't," Elodie said. "Returning them takes it out of you — Tam said so, and Tam didn't lie about much. Every memory I give back is a piece of me I don't get to keep. And I need—" Her voice caught on the word she always reached for. *Need.* Need what? Safety. Leverage. A self. "I need what I have. It's all I am."

Wren tilted her head, and sent one more image, small and devastating: a garden in winter, and a gardener refusing to plant because the seeds were *too precious to spend*, and the garden staying bare, and the seeds staying perfect, forever, in their drawer.

"Whose side are you on?" Elodie muttered.

Wren smiled — the first true smile Elodie had seen from her in two days — and went back to her pears.

* * *

Dusk came down over Vellum like a lid, and the lamps of the memory-fair lit one by one below the window, each stall glowing with its hoard of other people's yesterdays. Elodie watched the crowds drift through the light: the rich buying brightness, the poor selling theirs, everyone walking around full of holes and paying to fill them.

Somewhere down there, she thought, were vaults. Somewhere down there was a ledger with her name in it, and entries for every lift she'd ever made — forty-four, sixty-one, ninety, a hundred and twelve — and somewhere, possibly, if the brokers kept what they couldn't sell, was a vial labeled with a name she had never dared speak aloud in eleven years. *Elodie Voss, age four. A kitchen. A woman singing.*

She had never stolen her own past back because stealing it felt like admitting it existed, and admitting it existed meant admitting it had been taken, and she had built her whole survival on the cleaner lie: that she had simply never had anything at all.

A knock at the street door, downstairs. Soft. Twice.

Elodie went cold. The landlady wouldn't knock — she had a key and no manners. Brokers knocked softly when they wanted you to open willingly.

Wren had gone utterly still, pear forgotten, her eyes on the door and her small body coiled like a spring. On her wrist, faintly, the green mark had begun to glow.

Another knock. And then — muffled through the floorboards, in a cracked old voice Elodie knew from a stolen harvest evening nine years gone:

"Miss. I know you're up there." A pause. "I don't want money. I want to ask you somethin', and then I'll leave you be." Another pause, longer. "It's about the girl's mark. There's a vault under the Fair Exchange keeps things nobody's paid for. Things *confiscated*. And I've seen what's in the drawer marked V-O-S-S."

Elodie's heart stopped, started again, wrong.

"I only want to know," the old man called up, gentle as snowfall, "if you're the one who's been carryin' my Sera's night around all these years. Because if you are, miss — I'd like it back."
