## 11. A Debt in Someone Else's Name

The first thing Elodie learned about returning a memory was that it hurt in the wrong direction.

Stealing had always felt like plucking a plum — a small resistance, then sweetness. Giving back was like swallowing a stone you'd been carrying so long your body had mistaken it for part of you. The soldier's memory of his mother's kitchen, the very one she'd sold to Maren Hale all those years ago and bought back at ruinous cost from Vellum's vaults, sat behind her ribs now like a coal she couldn't put down.

Wren watched her work with that unnerving stillness children have when they are actually paying more attention than adults ever suspect. They were lodged above a dyer's shop in Vellum's Tanners' Quarter, where the smell of mordant and old wool covered two travelers who had no business being anywhere. The room was one bed, one chair, one window with a view of a wall, which Wren seemed to find endlessly fascinating.

"It's all right," Elodie said, mostly to herself. "You don't have to watch."

Wren did not look away.

Elodie closed her eyes and did the thing she had spent three days learning how to do: she turned the theft around. Not pulling — pressing. She found the memory's edges, the way it fit against her own mind like a key cut for someone else's lock, and she pushed it home.

The kitchen went out of her like a lamp going out. For a moment she could still feel its warmth fading through her fingers, the ghost-smell of bread, the sound of a woman humming off-key — and then it was gone, and somewhere across the city a soldier named Aldric Vane would wake tomorrow remembering his mother's kitchen as though he'd never lost it, though he didn't know he'd lost it, because that was the cruelty of stolen things: you only missed them once they came back.

Elodie sat down hard on the bed. Her hands were shaking. She felt lighter and emptier at once, like a house after the furniture's gone.

"That's eleven," she said aloud, because saying it made it arithmetic instead of grief. Eleven memories returned in four days. Eleven owners found or tracked down through the ledgers Tam's map had led her to — Vellum kept records, of course it did; a city where memories were currency kept books the way other cities kept grain. Eleven people who had walked around with holes in their lives and never known why.

Eleven passages bought, too, in a way. Because word traveled strangely in Vellum. A cobbler whose dead wife's laughter had come back to him told his neighbors about the pale woman who asked nothing. A ferryman restored to the memory of his daughter's face carried them upriver free of charge and wept the whole way without knowing quite why. Restitution, it turned out, opened doors that coin could not.

It also emptied her. Each return took something with it — not just the memory but the calluses it had grown on her soul, the little comforts she'd built atop it. She had lived inside other people's lives so long that giving them back was like being evicted eleven times over.

Wren crossed the small room and, with great solemnity, pressed something into Elodie's hand. A dried apple slice, saved from their provisions, slightly fuzzy with age.

"I can't eat that, I'm not—" Elodie started, and then understood, and laughed despite everything. "For me. Thank you." She ate it. It tasted like almost nothing and like everything.

* * *

The problem with restitution, Elodie discovered on the fifth day, was that Vellum remembered her better than she remembered herself.

She was standing in the Vault District — a neighborhood of grey stone and quieter silence, where the memory-brokers kept their deep stores — wearing the face and papers of a woman called Sera Ilvane, whose identity Elodie had purchased honestly enough with a returned memory's goodwill. Sera Ilvane was a licensed appraiser. Sera Ilvane could walk into Hale & Associates' counting-house without raising an eyebrow.

Maren Hale's operation in Vellum was larger than her valley branch, and colder. The clerks here wore grey gloves. The vaults below held memories in glass and silver, each one labeled in a hand so fine it looked like frost.

Elodie had come for a specific ledger entry. She had spent two evenings copying names from the public registry — the registry of unclaimed stock, memories whose owners were dead or unfindable, the ones brokers sold cheap because no one grieved for them. And there, in the column marked *provenance unknown*, she had found an entry that stopped her breath:

*Item 4471. Female child, approx. six years. Orchard in high summer. Seller: unnamed. Acquired: Hollowmere border station.*

Her own childhood. The orchard. The one memory she had never sold, never traded, never touched — except that she had, hadn't she? Long ago, before she understood what she was doing, before Maren Hale taught her the trade properly. She must have brought it to the border station as a girl, raw and stupid with grief, and some broker with neat handwriting had taken it from her for the price of a meal.

She had been carrying a copy of it all these years without knowing — the way you carry a scar and forget the wound. But the original, the true depth of it, sat in a vault forty feet beneath her feet.

"Appraiser Ilvane?" A clerk had materialized at her elbow, polite as a knife. "You've been standing at the registry for some time. May I help you locate stock?"

"I'm looking," Elodie said, and her voice did not shake, which she counted as the day's single victory, "for item 4471."

* * *

They let her see it. That was the terrible part — how easily they let her see it. Appraisers viewed unclaimed stock all the time; it was how valuations were set. The clerk led her down stairs that grew colder with each flight, past doors with silver seals, into a viewing room where a single glass vessel sat on black velvet under a lamp.

Inside swam a light the color of late afternoon.

"Orchard scene, provenance unknown," the clerk recited. "High quality. Unusually intact emotional register. We've had three offers. Curious piece — whoever held it originally seems to have been a natural, if undeveloped. There's a secondary thread woven through it, very faint. Some of our examiners think it might be—"

"May I view it?" Elodie said. "Alone?"

He hesitated, then bowed and left her. Protocol, probably. Or perhaps even clerks in grey gloves preferred not to stand near certain memories too long.

Elodie lifted the vessel. The light inside stirred, recognized her — she felt it, the way a dog recognizes a footstep on the stair — and pressed itself against the glass like a child at a window.

*An orchard in high summer. A woman on a ladder with apples in her apron, laughing, calling down: catch me one, little wren, catch what falls. A man's voice off-stage, warm and indistinct. The smell of grass going gold. The feeling — the feeling underneath everything — of being loved so completely it wasn't even noticeable, the way fish don't notice water.*

And threaded through it, faint as a watermark: green. Growing things. A whisper of the Verdant Court, in the hands of the woman on the ladder, in the way the apples fell just slightly too sweetly, just slightly too generously, as if the tree liked her.

Her mother had been a gardener. Not a farmer. A gardener.

Elodie stood in the cold room with her whole childhood in her hands and understood, finally, what Wren had been trying to tell her since the border stones — since the flower through the ice, since the fragments of the falling court, since the night Wren had pressed her small palm flat over Elodie's heart and said, in the dead tongue, a word Tam had translated with tears in his eyes before he died:

*Keeper.*

Not Wren. Wren was the seed. The seed needed a keeper. And the Court had sent its last living seed not into the wilderness at random, but to the daughter of the woman who had tended its gardens — hidden in the one place magic could not reach, the one place the Frostbound would never think to look, because Hollowmere forgot magic the way the sea forgets rain.

"They're coming for her," Elodie said aloud to the empty room. "That's why the brokers want her. That's why Maren sent word beyond the valley." And Corvane's name, which Tam had spoken of with loathing, sat in her mind like a stone in a shoe.

The clerk knocked. "Appraiser? Your time is concluded."

Elodie set the vessel down. She looked at it the way you look at a door you cannot yet open.

"One question," she said. "If a piece of unclaimed stock were to be... claimed. By a private party. What would that require?"

"A certified claimant's seal," said the clerk, "or a broker's license. Or," and here his voice developed the particular smoothness of a man quoting prices he enjoyed, "sufficient payment. Item 4471 is valued at four hundred crowns. Provenance pieces run high. Sentimental demand, you understand."

Four hundred crowns. Elodie had eleven crowns and a dried apple's worth of goodwill.

"Thank you," she said, and walked up the stairs into the grey daylight, doing arithmetic that made thieves' arithmetic look like a children's song.

* * *

"You want to give away MORE?" the ferryman's wife said, when Elodie explained her plan that evening in the dyer's loft. Bess Ondrey was a broad, practical woman whose husband's restored memory had made her curious rather than suspicious, which in Vellum was nearly heroic. "Love, you've got shadows under your eyes like bruises. Every one you give back takes a bite out of you. Everyone can see it."

"Not everyone," Elodie said. "People who owe me. People I've helped. Their testimony carries weight here now — you've seen it. If enough of them vouch for a claimant's petition..."

Bess snorted. "Petitions cost money too."

"Less than four hundred crowns." Elodie spread the map Tam had given her, weighted at the corners with spools of dye-blue thread. "There are thirty-one entries in the unclaimed registry whose owners aren't dead — they're scattered. Displaced by the war, moved north, lost track of. The brokers can't sell those memories at full price because the owners might surface and file claims. Dead weight on their ledgers. But if I find the owners and return the memories *myself*, publicly, witnessed..." She tapped the map. "Every return is a story. Every story is a voice for my petition. And every owner who gets their life back becomes a reason the magistrates can't refuse me."

"And what do you get out of it besides tired?"

Elodie looked at Wren, who was asleep sitting up, the way children do, mid-thought, her head tipped against the wall, her small hands curled open.

"Passage," Elodie said. "Out of this city with what's mine. And maybe—" she almost didn't say it, "—maybe learning how to be a person who gives things back. I'm told it's possible."

Bess studied her for a long moment. Then she stood, rolled up her sleeves, and pulled a ledger of her own from under the bed — names, routes, debts, favors, the underground cartography of any city.

"Well then," she said. "First one's easy. There's a weaver in Northgate who's been half-mad for six years. Lost the memory of her wedding day to a broker during the famine winter. Sold it to feed her kids, thought she was selling something smaller. Been grieving a day she can't remember ever since. My sister knows her. We'll go tomorrow."

"Bess." Elodie's throat was tight. "Why are you helping me?"

Bess shrugged enormously. "Because my husband hums again while he works. He didn't used to. Didn't know he'd stopped." She jabbed a finger at Elodie. "That's what you people take, isn't it? Not the big things. The humming."

* * *

The weaver's wedding day went home to her on a Tuesday morning, in a room full of looms and sunlight, and the sound the woman made when it settled back into her was not crying and not laughing but something older than both, and Elodie had to sit down afterward and be given tea by strangers while her hands refused to stop shaking.

The second return cost her the taste of cinnamon. She hadn't known until it was gone that she still had it, that every stolen kitchen had left a residue, and now the world's baking smelled like wax and paper.

The third cost her the color of a particular evening sky over a harbor she'd never visited, and she caught herself mourning it, and hated that she mourned it, and mourned it anyway.

By the seventh, the pattern was clear: each return took the memory and a tithe of whatever surrounded it. She was hollowing out like a gourd. Wren noticed before anyone. The child began leaving things in Elodie's pockets — buttons, pebbles, a feather, a twist of blue thread — small treasures deposited silently, an economy of gifts that required no language at all.

"You're worried about me," Elodie said one night, holding up the feather. "I'm the one who's supposed to be worried about you."

Wren pointed at Elodie's chest, then at the window, toward the Vault District, toward item 4471 sleeping in its glass. Then she made a gesture Elodie had learned to read: both hands cupped, opening slowly, like something blooming.

*Almost,* the gesture said. *Soon.*

"Yes," Elodie said. "Soon. Nine more returns. Nine more voices. Then the petition, then the claim, then we leave this city before Corvane's people realize where we are." She smiled, and it was mostly real. "I'm going to buy back my own childhood with other people's happiness. There's a sentence I never thought I'd say."

Wren considered this gravely. Then she climbed onto the bed, arranged herself against Elodie's side with the frank entitlement of the very young, and fell asleep.

Elodie sat in the lamplight, lighter than she'd been in twenty years and poorer than she'd ever been, and found — to her considerable annoyance — that she was humming.

She didn't know the tune. It came up from somewhere below the thefts, below the hovel above the frost line, below everything she'd built her survival on: a few notes of a song a woman had once sung off-key in a kitchen, in a memory Elodie had sold to Maren Hale for three silver coins and a hot meal, long before she knew what she was selling.

Except — she went very still — she had returned that one. Eleven days ago. To Aldric Vane, the soldier. It was his mother's kitchen. His mother's humming.

So why did she know the tune?

The lamp guttered. In the glass-dark window, Elodie saw her own reflection and, superimposed faintly upon it, as though the city's thousand kept memories were leaning close to look: a woman on a ladder in an orchard, laughing, calling down words in a tongue no one in Hollowmere had ever spoken.

The same dead tongue Wren spoke.

Elodie's hands went cold. Four hundred crowns suddenly seemed like the smallest of her problems. Because if the humming was hers — if the tune had been hers all along, sold and resold and copied through a hundred strangers' minds until it leaked back to her like water through stone —

Then item 4471 wasn't just her childhood.

It was proof. And somewhere in Vellum, in a vault with a silver seal, sat the one document that could tell her exactly who her mother had been — and exactly why the Frostbound had spent twenty years making sure no keeper's line survived.

Nine more returns, she told herself. Nine more days.

Outside, far off toward the river, a bell began to ring — not the hour, but the alarm pattern, the one that meant brokers' hunters were moving through the streets with warrants in their gloves.

Elodie blew out the lamp and picked up Wren, who woke instantly, silent, already reaching for her shoes.

"So much for nine days," Elodie whispered.
