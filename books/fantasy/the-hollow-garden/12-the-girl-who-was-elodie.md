## 12. The Girl Who Was Elodie

The vault beneath the Vellum Memory Exchange smelled of cedar and cold iron, and Elodie hated it with a thoroughness that surprised her.

She had spent eleven days returning memories. Eleven days of walking through Vellum's grey-lit streets to the doors of strangers, pressing her palm to their foreheads, and giving back what she had taken — the cooper's wedding day, the midwife's first delivery, the fisherman's one perfect morning on open water. Each return left her lighter and emptier, like a house losing rooms one by one. She had told herself it was penance. She had told herself it was arithmetic: a memory spent was passage bought, a favor earned, a door opened.

But standing now before the vault's inner door, watching the archivist work his ring of small brass keys, she understood that something else had been happening too. Somewhere in this building, in one of these drawers, was a memory with her own name on it.

"You said you had it," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You said you'd found it."

The archivist — a narrow man named Pellwick with spectacles thick as bottle-bottoms and the patient air of someone who had catalogued ten thousand sorrows — did not look up from his keys. "I said I'd found *a* memory matching your description. Earliest recollection, female donor, deposited sixty-odd years ago by a broker operating out of Hollowmere." He turned the last key. The lock made a sound like a swallowed word. "I should warn you, madam. Memories that old, kept that long in iron... they don't always come back whole. Sometimes they come back *opinionated*."

"Opinionated."

"They develop preferences," Pellwick said gravely. "For being remembered properly. You'll want to sit down."

Wren was already sitting. The girl had claimed the single stool in the corner of the reading room and sat upon it with the absolute stillness she brought to everything, hands folded in her lap, green eyes tracking every movement in the room. Since the border stones, since the crossing, she had grown more present somehow — the magic humming under her skin seemed to have loosened something in her, and though she still did not speak aloud, she no longer needed to. When she wanted to tell Elodie something, she simply *placed* it, the way you'd set a cup carefully into someone's hands: an image, a warmth, a fragment of feeling borrowed from the memories Elodie carried and reflected back, polished, made legible.

Right now she placed nothing at all. She only watched. But there was something in her watchfulness that made Elodie's chest ache — a kind of held breath, as if the girl had been waiting for this room, this drawer, this exact hour, for her entire silent life.

Pellwick drew out a shallow tray lined with grey felt. Upon it lay a vial no longer than a finger, its glass clouded with age, and inside the glass a curl of silver light that moved when you looked at it — slowly, drowsily, the way smoke moves in a room where no one has spoken for years.

"There," he said softly. "Sixty-one years in storage. Donor name unrecorded. Broker's notation reads—" he squinted at the tag wired to the neck of the vial— "*girl-child, valley-born, earliest.* That's all we know."

"That's all I need," Elodie said.

She reached for the vial and stopped. Her hand hung in the air above it, trembling faintly, and she could not have said why. She had handled hundreds of memories. She had lifted them from soldiers and widows and children, had rolled them between her fingers like coins, had weighed them against bread and firewood and the price of another winter. This was the same thing. It was only glass and light.

It was not the same thing at all.

*You're afraid,* Wren placed gently in her mind. Not mocking. Wondering.

"Of course I'm afraid," Elodie muttered. "I'm about to meet myself."

She picked up the vial. It was warm. Not body-warm — hearth-warm, bread-warm, the specific temperature of a kitchen on a morning when someone who loves you is cooking — and the strangeness of that nearly undid her before she even began. She uncorked it. The silver light rose out like a sigh, hovered a moment in the air between them, and then, finding her, poured itself home.

---

The kitchen was yellow.

That was the first thing — the first true thing she had ever known, and it arrived whole: *the kitchen was yellow*. Yellow walls gone soft at the corners where little hands had touched them, yellow light through a window over the basin, yellow steam rising off a pot of something with rosemary in it. A floor of wide grey stones worn smooth by feet. A table scarred with knife-marks and candle-drips and one deep gouge shaped like a crescent moon, which a very small person had made with a very forbidden knife and never confessed to.

And her mother was singing.

Elodie stood in the memory the way you stand in a river — everything moving around her, carrying her, too much to hold and too lovely to let go. Her mother stood at the basin with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and her arms were brown and strong and flecked with soil that never quite washed out, and her voice moved through a song in a language that made the window-light bend. Not the dead tongue Wren spoke. Older. Greener. A language that sounded less like words than like things growing.

*Mama,* said a voice, and Elodie realized with a lurch that it was her own — a child's voice, high and imperious, coming from somewhere near the floor. *Mama, the roses are talking again.*

*What are they saying?* her mother asked, not turning around, smiling at her own reflection in the water.

*They're complaining. They say the beans are rude.*

*The beans ARE rude,* her mother agreed solemnly. *I'll have a word with them.*

And the child laughed — Elodie laughed, six years old or seven, laughing in a garden kitchen while her mother's song went on underneath everything like roots under a lawn — and the memory began, gently, terribly, to give her more.

Her mother's name was Isolde. Her hands were gardener's hands; she could coax a cutting to root in bare stone, and the plants *did* talk to her, in the way that rivers talk and old houses talk, and she had never once thought this strange because everyone she knew could do it. Everyone she knew lived in a court of living wood and flowering stone, where the halls were grown rather than built and the throne was a tree so old it had opinions about weather. Her mother was a gardener there. Not a great lady, not a mage — a gardener, which in that country was perhaps the same thing. She tended the winter-beds. She kept the last blooms alive through the Frostbound's slow advance, year after year, war after war, holding a green thread against the white.

And then the winter won.

Elodie felt it happen the way you feel a bone break — from the inside, all at once, wrongness flooding everywhere. White in the corridors. White on the throne-tree. Voices going quiet one by one, some fleeing, some freezing, some *kept* — and her mother running, running through falling gardens with a child bundled against her chest, running toward the one place in all the world where magic could not follow, because magic could not cross it, because it was a hole in the world's fabric, a valley where the Court's power died at the stones—

Hollowmere. She was hiding her daughter in Hollowmere.

*It can't reach you here,* her mother whispered — and Elodie heard the whisper twice, once as the child heard it, muffled against wool and terror, and once as the woman heard it now, understanding at last what it had cost. *Nothing grows here and nothing remembers here, and that is exactly why you will be safe. Forget me if you must. Forgetting is a kind of wall.*

*No,* said the child. *I won't forget. I'll remember every day. I'll remember your song and the yellow kitchen and the rude beans—*

*I know you will, my heart. That's why I'm doing this instead of you.*

And then — Elodie felt it coming and could not stop it, could not look away, could not even close her eyes inside her own past — her mother lifted her hand, and her hand was full of a soft green light, and she pressed it to her daughter's forehead, and she took the memory away herself.

Not stole. *Kept.* Held it safe the way you hold a seed through winter, meaning all along to plant it back.

*I'll return it to you someday,* Isolde whispered, and the memory frayed at the edges like cloth in wind. *When the frost breaks. When it's safe to be who you are. Until then — be small, my love. Be plain. Be nobody's daughter. Grow up crooked and unnoticed, like a weed, and live.*

The yellow kitchen folded itself away. The song faded. And the last thing the memory gave her, the very last, was a fragment of her mother's face — young, exhausted, dirt-streaked, radiant — and a sound behind her mother's voice that Elodie only now, sixty years too late, understood: her mother had been crying while she smiled.

---

Elodie came back to the reading room on her knees.

She didn't remember falling. Pellwick was hovering with a glass of water and the expression of a man deciding whether fetching help would violate confidentiality. Wren was beside her instantly, both small hands pressed flat against Elodie's back, and through those hands poured not comfort exactly but *recognition* — the girl's whole body shaking, her eyes streaming, silently weeping as though she had seen the memory too.

"Wren." Elodie's voice was gravel. "Wren, look at me."

The girl looked. And Elodie saw it now, saw it everywhere at once — the green of her eyes, the particular shade of it, the same green as the light in her mother's hand. The way the seed in the girl's chest answered her mother's magic the way a tuning fork answers a bell. Sixty years. The Court had fallen sixty years ago, and her mother had hidden her here, and the Court had endured just long enough, somewhere, in some frozen root or kept heart, to send one last messenger across the world to find her.

*You,* Wren placed, and her thought was not a word but a shape, a certainty, vast and simple as sunrise. *We were sent for you. Not a keeper. Not a stranger. You. The gardener's daughter. The heir of the winter-beds.*

"I'm not an heir," Elodie said hoarsely. "I'm a thief. I've spent my whole life stealing other people's mothers' kitchens because I didn't have one—"

*That's WHY,* Wren pressed, urgent now, gripping her shoulders with surprising strength. *Don't you see? The theft-gift — the taking — it isn't Hollowmere's curse. It's HER gift, twisted by the valley. Your mother could hold memories safe. Could keep a seed alive through winter. You inherited the keeping, and the valley taught it to steal. You've been doing the family work your whole life, badly, in exile, without knowing—*

Elodie laughed. It came out cracked and wet and half-hysterical, and she didn't care. "Badly. Yes. Thank you."

But underneath the laughter something enormous was settling into place, tectonic and slow. All those years of transactional loneliness. All those hundreds of lives in jars. She had thought herself hollow — a hollow girl from a hollow valley — and it had never once occurred to her that hollowness could be *made*, deliberately, by a loving hand, as shelter. She had mistaken her emptiness for an absence of self. It had been a protection all along. A wall built of forgetting, exactly as her mother promised.

And now the wall was down, and behind it was a garden.

"The brokers," she said suddenly, standing, wiping her face with the heel of her hand. "If my mother was Court — if I'm — then Maren Hale didn't want Wren for the seed alone. She wanted the seed *and* the keeper. The pair of us. That's why she let us leave Vellum so easily. That's why the hunters keep missing us by hours." She looked at Pellwick, whose face had gone carefully blank in the way of men who have lived through regime changes. "Who else knows what's in your ledgers?"

"Madam," Pellwick said delicately, "I am an archivist. Knowing things is my profession. Telling them is my ruin." He hesitated. Then, quietly: "There was an inquiry. Three weeks ago. A woman, grey coat, paid in old coin. She asked after any deposits connected to Hollowmere, sixty years or older. I told her nothing, because there was nothing yet to tell — your request only came yesterday. But she left a token. Said if anything matching her description surfaced, I was to send it to her, and she'd make it worth my retirement."

He slid a small object across the table. A chip of white ice, cut faceted like a gem, that did not melt and did not chill and hummed faintly against the wood.

Elodie did not touch it. She knew Frostbound craft when she saw it. Corvane's people had been ahead of her the entire time — had known, perhaps, before *she* had known, that the heir of the winter-beds was walking around Vellum giving back stolen memories like a madwoman.

"Maren Hale," she said.

"Or someone wearing her manners," Pellwick agreed.

Wren tugged her sleeve. When Elodie looked down, the girl placed an image in her mind with terrible clarity: the ruined court, the glass garden, the empty throne of green — and beside it, overlaid, the yellow kitchen, her mother's soil-dark hands, the two images fused into one meaning:

*Your mother may still be kept. Frozen, not dead. The Frostbound keep their prisoners. If we reach the court — if we plant the seed — spring returns, and everything the winter holds, it must release.*

Everything the winter holds.

Elodie stood very still in the cedar-and-iron smell of the vault, holding a memory she had waited her whole life to receive, delivered sixty years late by a mother who had meant to bring it back herself. Somewhere north, in the white country, in the frozen heart of a dead court, a woman with gardener's hands might be standing in stasis, waiting, still meaning to finish that sentence — *I'll return it to you someday* — not knowing her daughter had finally come to collect.

"Well then," Elodie said, and her voice did not shake at all, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "We're going to the court. Tonight. Before they realize what they've lost."

Pellwick cleared his throat. "Madam? There is one more thing. The inquiry — the grey-coat woman — she wasn't alone. She had a child with her. Small, dark-haired, silent." He adjusted his spectacles, uncomfortable. "I mention it only because the child looked at me, quite directly, and mouthed a single word. I assumed it was nonsense. Some northern dialect."

"What word?"

"*Found,*" said Pellwick. "She mouthed *found.*"

Outside the vault windows, far to the north, the sky had begun to go the color of bruised snow.
