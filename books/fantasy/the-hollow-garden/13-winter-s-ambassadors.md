## 13. Winter's Ambassadors

Corvane had crossed the White Country eleven times in his long service to the Frostbound, and he had learned one truth above all others: cold was not cruel. Cold was honest. It told you exactly what it wanted from you — your warmth, your breath, your stubborn animal insistence on continuing — and it took nothing you did not, in the end, surrender willingly.

People, by contrast, lied beautifully.

He thought about this as his sleigh crested the last ridge above Vellum and the city of Kept Things spread out below him like a spilled jewelry box, all candlelight and slate roofs and the amber glow of memory-vaults burning through frosted windows. Somewhere down there was a thief named Elodie Voss. Somewhere near her was a mute child carrying a seed that could undo three hundred years of careful, patient winter. And somewhere between those two facts was Corvane's work, which was, as always, to make a lie so comfortable that the truth seemed like the cruelty.

"Envoy," said his driver, a broad-shouldered woman named Sif who had been frozen at thirty for longer than most kingdoms lasted. "The brokers have lit the signal fires. They know you've come."

"Of course they do," Corvane said. "Brokers know everything except when to stop." He adjusted his gloves — white leather, stitched with silver thread, the only vanity he permitted himself — and regarded his reflection in the sleigh's polished rail. A lean man, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that innkeepers trusted and children did not. He had spent centuries cultivating that face. Trust was a tool like any other, and tools deserved maintenance.

"And if she refuses?" Sif asked.

Corvane smiled at his reflection. "Then we do what winter does, my dear. We wait. We are very, very good at waiting."

* * *

Maren Hale received him in the back room of her brokerage, which smelled of tallow, old paper, and the particular metallic sweetness of memories stored too long without rotation. She was a compact woman with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who had appraised ten thousand souls and found them all slightly overpriced. Corvane liked her enormously. She reminded him of himself before the frost got into his heart and simplified things.

"Envoy Corvane." She bowed the precise degree required by protocol — deep enough for an ambassador, shallow enough for a rival. "You honor my establishment."

"Your establishment honors me by not being on fire, which is more than I can say for the last three brokerages I visited." He removed his gloves finger by finger. "Where is she?"

"The child? Two streets east, in a safehouse behind the chandler's. The thief is with her."

"You let the thief keep her?"

Maren's mouth twitched. "I let the thief *think* she's keeping her. There's a difference, Envoy, and the difference is why I'm rich." She poured two glasses of plum wine — real plum wine, smuggled, absurdly expensive — and pushed one toward him. "She won't give the girl up easily. I've watched Elodie Voss for years. She sells everything, keeps nothing, trusts no one. But this child has gotten under her skin like a splinter. You can see it when she looks at the girl. She goes soft in the face. It's frankly embarrassing."

"People usually are," Corvane said. He sipped the wine and found it excellent, which annoyed him; pleasure was a debt the body collected later, with interest. "Tell me what you know of the mother."

"Maren Hale's files are not free."

"Maren Hale's cooperation is not optional."

They regarded each other across the table, two professionals acknowledging the fiction that either of them had leverage. Then Maren laughed — a short, genuine bark — and opened a ledger bound in green leather gone soft with handling.

"Lyra Voss. Court gardener, third rank, Verdant Court. Present at the planting of the last wards. When the Court fell, she didn't run north or south like the others. She ran *in* — into Hollowmere, where magic couldn't follow. Clever. The valley eats enchantment, so she hid her daughter there and went out again to draw pursuit away." Maren turned the page. "The Frostbound caught her at the border stones. She fought. She lost. And because she was Court-born and knew the seed-lore, the Queen ordered her kept rather than killed."

"Kept where?"

"The Amber Gallery. Under stasis. Three hundred years, give or take." Maren tapped the ledger. "She's still there, Envoy. Still breathing. Still twenty-six years old, if the records are right. The Queen calls her an asset. I call her the finest bargaining chip ever minted."

Corvane turned his glass slowly on the table, watching the wine catch the lamplight. "And the daughter knows?"

"Not yet. But she will. Thieves always go looking for their own past eventually — it's the one thing they can't steal from anyone else." Maren closed the ledger. "So. What's the play?"

"The play," Corvane said, "is honesty."

Maren stared at him. "I beg your pardon?"

"Honesty, Hale. The cruelest weapon we have. Everyone expects the Frostbound to lie, to threaten, to freeze their livestock and crack their wells. So instead, I am going to walk up to Elodie Voss, sit down across from her like a civilized man, and tell her the absolute truth: her mother is alive, she can have her back, and all it costs is one small child who was never hers to begin with." He set down the glass. "No threats. No tricks. Just arithmetic. Let her do the rest herself."

"That's monstrous," Maren said admiringly.

"It's efficient. Threats make people brave — have you noticed? Back a creature into a corner and it finds teeth it never knew it had. But give a person a *choice*, a real one, with a real prize on one side and a stranger's life on the other..." He shook his head sadly. "Most people would sell their own shadow for less. And this one has spent her whole life selling other people's yesterdays. She already believes everything has a price. I'm simply going to quote her one."

* * *

He found her two days later, because he made her come to him — or rather, he made the circumstances arrange themselves so that finding each other looked like accident. There was an art to ambushes that wore the clothing of coincidence, and Corvane had written several chapters of it.

The chandler's safehouse backed onto a small square with a dead fountain at its center, and in the dead fountain, impossibly, a single crocus had forced its way up through a seam in the ice. Small wonder. The child's presence leaked warmth the way a cracked jug leaks water. Corvane stood beside the fountain in the blue hour before dawn, hands folded, breath steaming, and waited.

Elodie Voss came out of the safehouse just after first light, a thin woman with a thief's economy of movement and dark circles under her eyes that spoke eloquently of sleepless nights. She saw him immediately — good instincts — and stopped, one hand drifting toward the knife at her belt.

"You're not a broker," she said.

"No. Brokers dress better and smell worse." He inclined his head. "Elodie Voss. My name is Corvane. I am an envoy of the Frostbound Court, and I've come to make you an offer."

"I don't want anything from the Frostbound."

"Everyone wants something from the Frostbound. Warmth, mostly. It's rather our whole portfolio." He gestured to the fountain steps. "Sit with me five minutes. If you dislike what I say, you may throw me into the fountain. It appears to be thawing; you'd be doing me a kindness."

Something flickered across her face — the ghost of humor, quickly suppressed, the way a starving man suppresses a smile at a feast he isn't invited to. She did not sit. But she did not leave, either, and Corvane counted that as a door opening.

"The child," he said. "Wren. You know what she carries."

"I know she's a little girl who talks in a dead language and makes flowers grow through ice. That's all I need to know."

"Then let me tell you the rest." He kept his voice gentle, almost bored, the tone of a man discussing weather. "That flower through ice — you've seen it. Imagine a forest of it. Imagine spring itself, bottled and breathing, in the chest of a child. The Verdant Court made her kind to be gardeners of seasons, Voss. Wren is the last living seed of that court. Wherever she takes root, the long winter ends. Our Queen has held the world in frost for three hundred years, and this child is the key to every lock she ever built."

"Then you'll kill her," Elodie said flatly.

"We could. It would be wasteful and it might not even work — seeds are stubborn things. No, Voss. We don't want her dead. We want her *unplanted*. Kept. Asleep, somewhere cold and quiet, for another three hundred years, until the world forgets her the way it forgot everything else." He paused, and here he let the gentleness drop away, letting her see the machinery underneath. "Which brings me to my offer."

Behind her, in the safehouse window, he glimpsed the child — small, pale, watching them with eyes the color of new leaves. Elodie shifted, unconsciously placing herself between Corvane and the glass. Interesting. Very interesting. Maren had been right about the splinter.

"Your mother," Corvane said, "is alive."

The silence that followed had texture. He watched it land on Elodie Voss like a physical blow — watched her thief's composure crack along fault lines she hadn't known were there.

"You're lying."

"I never lie in negotiations. Lies are for people who lack collateral." He produced a small object from his coat: a pressed flower, browned with age but unmistakably a court-lily, the kind grown only in the Verdant gardens. "This was found in her pocket when we took her. Lyra Voss carried it out of the falling court. She has carried it — or we have kept it for her — ever since. She is in the Amber Gallery beneath our Queen's hall, asleep in a moment that hasn't moved in three hundred years. Not aging. Not suffering. Waiting." He extended the flower. "She was twenty-six years old, Voss. She is twenty-six years old now. You could speak to her within the month."

Elodie did not take the flower. Her hand rose halfway and stopped, as though the air itself had thickened. "What do you want for her?"

There it was. Not *is it true*. Not *how dare you*. *What do you want for her.* Maren had been right: this woman believed in prices the way priests believe in gods. Corvane felt something that in a warmer man might have been pity.

"One child," he said. "Delivered to my escort at the valley border, unharmed, unwoken. In exchange, your mother walks free — truly free, beyond our reach, beyond everyone's. A life for a life. A family restored for a family that was never yours." He softened his voice further, pouring honey on the blade. "Think of it honestly, Voss. You found Wren on your doorstep in a snowstorm. You have known her a matter of weeks. Your mother has been frozen in the dark for three centuries because she hid *you* — because she loved you enough to spend her whole life buying your safety. Which debt is larger? Which name is carved deeper?"

He watched her face as he said it, and he was very good at watching faces, and what he saw frightened him slightly — not because it showed weakness, but because it showed strength gathering. Grief, yes. Temptation, absolutely; he could see the mother blooming behind her eyes, a woman she had never known, alive, reachable, *real*. But underneath it, something stubborn was taking root, the way the crocus had taken root in the fountain ice. He recognized it with professional dismay. It was the same look he had seen on defenders of fallen cities, on mothers standing between wolves and cradles. The look of a person deciding that some ledgers do not balance.

"You've done this before," Elodie said quietly. "Made offers like this."

"Many times."

"How many took the deal?"

Corvane considered lying, found it pointless, and answered honestly. "More than half."

"And the ones who refused?"

"Winter happened to them," he said. "It happens to everyone eventually. I merely schedule it."

She looked at him then — really looked, the way thieves look, cataloguing, weighing, finding the seams in a mark's coat. "You think I'll give her up because I'm a thief. Because everything I've ever touched, I've sold."

"I think," Corvane said gently, "that you know better than anyone alive what things cost. And I think that knowledge is a gift. Most people learn it too late."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The crocus in the fountain trembled in a wind that hadn't existed a moment ago. Behind the window glass, the child pressed one small palm flat against the pane, and Corvane felt the warmth of it even from here, faint and insistent, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"I need time," Elodie said.

"Three days," Corvane replied, rising, retrieving his gloves. "After that, my offer remains, but my patience doesn't. There are others who want the child, Voss — brokers, zealots, worse. The Queen's mercy is the gentlest thing currently hunting you. Remember that when you weigh your arithmetic." He tucked the court-lily back into his coat, then paused, and — because cruelty was an art and he was a craftsman — added softly:

"She has your chin, you know. Your mother. I've seen her face often enough. Sleep well, Elodie Voss."

He walked away across the square without hurrying, because hurried men looked like men who feared refusal, and he did not fear refusal. Refusal was simply the first act of a longer negotiation, and winter had never once lost a negotiation. It only ever ran out of negotiators.

Behind him, faint as a rumor, he thought he heard the sound of ice cracking in the fountain — one clean note, like a bell, like a beginning.

He did not look back. But he noted it, in the ledger of his long memory, under the heading he reserved for problems: *the thief hesitates too long, and the ice is listening.*
