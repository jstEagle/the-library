## 14. What Mothers Would Ask

The tent was warm.

That was the first cruelty of it, Elodie thought. After weeks of frozen roads and hedgerow ambushes and Wren's small body burning with fever against her back, the Frostbound ambassador's pavilion should have been a block of ice. Instead it glowed. Braziers burned without smoke, and the air smelled faintly of juniper and hot stone, and the rugs underfoot were deep enough to swallow her boots to the ankle. Somewhere beyond the canvas walls, the blizzard that had chased them across the White Country howled like something starving, and here it could not touch them at all.

Corvane sat in a chair carved from pale wood, hands folded on one knee, watching her with the patience of someone who had waited out centuries and considered this meeting a pleasant diversion. He did not look like winter. He looked like a well-fed scholar, silver-haired, kind-eyed, wearing grey wool instead of armor. That was its own warning. Nothing that cruel ever dressed for the part.

"Sit," he said gently. "Please. You're swaying."

"I'm standing fine."

"You're standing the way a fence post stands after a flood." He gestured again, and though every instinct in her screamed that the chair might be a trap, might be iron, might be anything — her knees simply gave out, and she found herself sitting in it, holding Wren against her chest where the girl's fever-heat pulsed like a second heartbeat.

Wren had not spoken since the hedgerow. She hadn't spoken in words since Hollowmere, but she had communicated plenty — through lent memories, through the language of images Elodie passed into her mind like coins slid under a door. Now there was nothing. Just heat, and shallow breathing, and the seed — Elodie could feel it too now, a faint green pulse beneath the girl's ribs, dimming like a lamp running out of oil.

"She's dying," Elodie said. Not a question. She wanted it said aloud, in front of him, so he couldn't pretend otherwise.

"Yes," Corvane agreed, with what sounded horribly like sympathy. "The cold is patient, but she is not. The seed burns her from inside trying to keep her alive, and it is losing. Another day, perhaps two." He leaned forward. "I can stop that."

"By taking it out of her."

"By *harvesting* it," he corrected, "which sounds monstrous only because you haven't heard the terms yet. The Court would have called it gathering. The Court called many terrible things gardening." His smile was thin and old. "But I'm not here to argue theology with a thief. I'm here because I can give you something no one else in this world can."

Elodie said nothing. Her hand moved without permission to the pouch at her belt, where Tam's map lay folded, and where — deeper, wrapped in oilcloth — the memory she'd taken from the broker's vault in Vellum sat like a coal that never cooled.

She hadn't opened it. Three days on the road, and she hadn't opened it.

Corvane watched her hand and understood everything, because of course he did. Men like him always did.

"You know what I'm offering," he said softly. "You felt it the moment my envoy spoke the word *stasis*. Your mother is not dead, Elodie Voss. She has been sleeping in our keeping for twenty-two years, unaged, unwithered, preserved as carefully as a gardener preserves a cutting through frost. One word from me and she wakes. One word from you — *yes* — and you walk out of this tent with her arm in yours by morning."

* * *

They brought her mother in an hour later.

Elodie had asked for proof. It seemed reasonable. It seemed like the sort of thing a careful person would demand, and she had spent her whole life being careful, being transactional, being the woman who checked the weight of a coin twice before biting it. So she demanded proof, expecting a lie, expecting some glamour, some stolen face worn by a stranger.

Instead they carried in a block of ice the size of a coffin, clear as window glass, and inside it —

Inside it was a woman with dirt under her fingernails.

That was the detail that broke Elodie open. Not the face, though the face was hers, or nearly — the face Elodie had spent years failing to remember, the face that lived now only in the sealed memory she couldn't bring herself to open. Not the hair, dark going grey at the temples, braided back the way working women braid it. But the hands. The hands were cupped slightly, curled around nothing, and the nails were rimmed with soil, and there was a smear of green stain along one thumb that no amount of stasis had cleaned away, because the Frostbound preserved bodies but apparently not their dignity, and thank all the mercies for that, because it meant it was real. No one would invent dirt under the fingernails. Liars made queens. Only truth kept a gardener.

"Maren Hale told us where to look for you," Corvane said quietly. "Your mother hid you well, but she left a trail. Mothers usually do. They can't help it." He paused. "You may touch the ice. It won't wake her. Nothing wakes her but our consent."

Elodie touched the ice.

It was cold, of course. But underneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, she felt something — a warmth, a slow green pulse, the same rhythm she'd felt in Wren. The rhythm of the Verdant Court. Of the seed. Of whatever ran in her own blood, dammed up for twenty-two years behind the borders of a valley that magic forgot.

Her mother's eyes were closed. There was a small crease between her brows, the kind that came from squinting at seedlings in bad light, and Elodie thought, absurdly, *she looks annoyed*, and then she was laughing, or crying, or both, kneeling on the rug with her forehead pressed to the ice while the most dangerous man in the world stood over her saying nothing, because he didn't need to say anything. The offer was doing its own work.

*Stay,* said the ice. *This is real. This is yours. You've spent your whole life stealing other people's mothers' kitchens. Here is your own.*

* * *

Here is what Elodie did not tell Corvane: she had already decided.

She decided it somewhere between touching the ice and hearing him name the price, in that gray space where grief makes arithmetic easy. The trade was simple. A child she had known for a handful of weeks, a child who was cargo, a mission, a seed-carrier, a stranger who spoke a dead tongue — traded for a mother. For a childhood returned. For the answer to the question *who am I* that had gnawed at her since the first memory she ever stole.

Thieves' arithmetic. She was good at thieves' arithmetic. It had kept her alive in Hollowmere for twenty years.

So when Corvane said, "You need only walk away. Leave the girl. We are not monsters; we will keep her comfortable, and the harvest is quick," Elodie nodded. She felt herself nod from very far away, the way she'd once watched herself lift a soldier's memory of his mother's kitchen and feel nothing at all.

"I need water," she heard herself say. "For her fever. Before I go. If you want her alive for your harvest, she needs water."

A kindness, even now. Even in betrayal, some reflex of care. Corvane smiled and signaled, and a servant brought a clay jug, and Elodie knelt beside Wren to lift her head and give her drink.

Wren's eyes opened.

They were fever-bright and unfocused, and for a moment they saw nothing, and Elodie thought, *good, she won't know, she'll never know what I—*

And then Wren looked at her.

Not with accusation. That was the unbearable part. Elodie had braced for hatred, for fear, for the look a mark gives a cutpurse. Instead Wren looked at her the way she had looked at her the first night in the hovel above the frost line — with a mute, absolute, idiotic trust, the trust of a creature that had walked out of a dead kingdom carrying the last living seed of everything it loved and had chosen, out of everyone in the world, to knock on *this* door.

And then, because she could not speak, Wren did the thing she did. She reached up with a shaking hand and pressed her fingers to Elodie's wrist, and gave.

It wasn't a memory Elodie recognized. It was older than any of her thefts. It came flooding in raw and untranslated: green light through leaves, the smell of turned earth after rain, a hall full of voices singing in the dead tongue, and a garden — *the* garden — the Verdant Court in its summer, vast and ridiculous and alive, fountains running, banners of living ivy, and everywhere children, dozens of children, running between the beds with watering cans, and one woman moving among them with dirt under her fingernails, laughing, showing a small dark-haired girl how to press a seed into the earth.

*Press it deep,* the woman was saying, in the dead tongue, in a voice Elodie knew the way you know a voice you've heard in dreams your whole life. *Deep, and then let go. What you hold too tight never grows.*

The memory ended. Wren's hand fell away from her wrist.

Elodie knelt there with the water jug trembling in her grip, and understood, finally and completely, what she had almost done. Not sold a child. Sold the garden. Sold the last summer. Sold the woman in the ice — because that was the joke of it, the beautiful vicious joke — the woman in the ice had *built* that garden, had pressed seeds deep and let go, had hidden her daughter in a valley that magic forgot precisely so that no one could ever be traded for anyone, and here was her daughter, twenty-two years later, ready to do exactly that.

*What you hold too tight never grows.*

She looked at the ice. Her mother's face, annoyed and asleep and waiting. Twenty-two years of stasis, and a morning walk arm in arm, and answers to every question she'd ever hoarded.

She looked at Wren. Feverish, fading, trusting, *hers* — not owned, never owned, but given, the way seeds are given.

"Oh," Elodie said, to no one, to both of them. "Oh, you clever, cruel old man."

"Elodie," Corvane said, and his voice had lost its warmth, which meant it had finally become honest. "Decide."

She stood. Her legs held this time. She picked up the water jug, walked to the ice, and — slowly, deliberately, holding his eyes the whole time — poured the water out onto the rug.

Then she turned to Wren, gathered her up — all elbows and fever and ninety pounds of dying seed-carrier — and settled her against her back, tying her there with the rope she'd used to cross the border stones, the rope that had already carried them over one impossible line.

"The deal," Elodie said, "is declined."

"You're making a mistake."

"Probably. I make them constantly. Ask anyone whose memories I've stolen." She was moving toward the tent flap now, and Corvane had not risen from his chair, and somehow that was worse than if he had. "But here's the difference between you and me, ambassador. Everything I ever took, I took because I was empty. And I've learned — late, expensively, at other people's cost — that filling yourself up with taken things doesn't work. You just end up hollow with company." She shifted Wren's weight. "You've been keeping my mother for twenty-two years and you still don't understand her at all. She'd be ashamed to be your bargaining chip. She'd probably bite you."

For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's kind eyes. It might have been respect. It might have been the beginning of the order that would send his hunters after them into the blizzard.

"Walk out that flap," he said quietly, "and the mercy ends. The storm, the brokers, my host — everything comes down on you before dawn. The girl dies in the snow, and you die beside her, and my offer expires forever. Is that what your mother would ask of you?"

Elodie stopped with her hand on the tent flap.

She thought about it honestly, because she owed her mother honesty. *What would you ask?* Not the ice-woman, not the fantasy — the real one, the one with dirt under her nails, the one who had pressed her daughter deep into a forgotten valley and let go.

She knew the answer. She had known it since a flower bloomed through ice at the border stones.

"You've got it backwards," Elodie said. "You think she'd ask me to choose her." She pushed the flap open, and the blizzard roared in like a living thing, filling the warm tent with white fury, snuffing braziers, sending Corvane's servants staggering back. Elodie stood in the doorway with the storm tearing at her and the girl burning on her back, and she smiled — a thief's smile, sharp and true.

"She'd ask me to plant something."

She stepped out into the white, and behind her she heard Corvane rise from his chair at last, and call for horses, and the long winter began to fall in earnest.
