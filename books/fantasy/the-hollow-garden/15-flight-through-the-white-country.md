## 15. Flight Through the White Country

The white country did not want them.

Elodie had known this in the abstract way one knows that rivers are wet. But knowing it in the body — in lungs that burned like swallowed coals, in boots that sank to the knee and held on like a creditor's hand — was another matter entirely.

Behind them, three days out of Vellum, the broker-hunters were still coming. She could not see them; nothing moved under that flat grey sky except snow and the two small figures wading through it. But she could feel them, the way you feel a debt collector's knock before it lands. Maren Hale's people traveled light and fast and never stopped for anything so sentimental as a sunset.

Ahead of them, somewhere past the frozen river country, lay the ruined Court. Tam's map said four days' walk. The map had been drawn by a man who then died holding a bridge against six hunters, and Elodie suspected he had been an optimist about distances.

"Tell me again," she panted, "why we couldn't have taken the road."

Wren looked up at her from beneath a hood crusted with frost. The girl's answer came the way all her answers came now: she pressed her mittened hand against Elodie's wrist, and a memory bloomed between them — *the road is where they will look* — accompanied, as Wren's communications always were, by a stray sensory garnish. This time it was the smell of bread. Elodie had no idea why. The child's mind arranged its messages like a housekeeper who insisted every parcel be wrapped in something pleasant.

"Yes, thank you," Elodie said. "Very helpful. The road is where they'll look. I'd worked that out myself, actually, but it's good to have confirmation."

Wren smiled. It was a small smile, worn thin by cold, but it was there.

They walked on.

* * *

*Cold is a country,* Wren thought, because thinking in words was still new to her, still a game she played with borrowed shapes. *Cold is a country and it has eaten everything.*

She remembered — truly remembered, from before — what warmth had been. Not the memory Elodie lent her, though she kept that one close, worn smooth like a river stone from handling. That one was a kitchen: copper pots, a woman humming off-key, steam fogging a window, someone's hands kneading dough while flour drifted down like patient snow. Wren visited it nightly now, the way other children visited dreams. She had begun to think of the humming woman as hers, which was a strange thing to do with a stolen thing, but the Court had fallen and its rules had fallen with it, and a girl was allowed one kitchen.

Her own memories were older and stranger. Green halls. A king whose voice sounded like wind through wheat. Her mother's face, which she could no longer hold onto — it slid away whenever she reached for it, the way water slides through fingers, and each time it slipped further.

And beneath everything, dimming, the seed.

She felt it the way she felt her own heartbeat: a green pulse behind her ribs, fainter each day. The cold pressed on it. The cold pressed on everything here. In the valley magic had been locked out; here in the wilds it was simply being strangled slowly, patiently, the way winter strangles a garden — not with violence but with time.

That morning she had coughed, and the breath that left her had glittered. Not like ordinary breath. Like pollen. Tiny motes of light drifting away on the wind, and when they landed on the snow they made small brown circles, like scorch marks, or like the ghosts of flowers.

Elodie had seen it too. Elodie had gone very quiet, and then very busy, checking straps and buckles and pretending her hands weren't shaking.

Wren didn't tell her that the seed had started whispering at night. That it spoke in the old tongue, the dead tongue, the one that lived only in Wren's mouth now. That it said things like *plant me* and things like *I am tired* and once, terribly, *let me sleep.*

She kept walking instead. One foot. Then the other. The kitchen hummed in her chest, off-key, and she matched her steps to it.

* * *

By the third night the wind found its teeth.

They sheltered in the lee of a fallen waystone — one of the old border markers from before the war, its carved face worn to a suggestion, its inscription legible only to historians and the dead. Elodie got a fire going from damp scrub and half a stick of Tam's precious pitchwood, and the fire was less a source of heat than a rumor of heat, a small orange argument against the dark.

Wren sat too still.

That was the thing that frightened Elodie. Cold made people fidget, stamp, hug themselves — the body begging. Wren had stopped begging. She sat with her hands in her lap and watched the fire with the mild, distant interest of someone watching weather in another country.

"Right," Elodie said briskly, because briskness was the only tool she had ever trusted. "Supper." She produced the last of the travel bread, which had achieved a texture best described as architectural, and broke it in half. Wren took her piece and chewed without appetite, mechanically, and Elodie felt something twist in her chest that she recognized, with distaste, as fear.

She had spent her whole life taking things from people. She knew the mechanics of it intimately — the little silver pull behind the eyes, the way a life came loose like a ripe fruit. Giving back, these last weeks, had been harder, stranger, a muscle she'd never used. But this — sitting across a dying fire from a dying child who carried the last living hope of a dead court, unable to do anything but watch —

This was worse than both. This was having nothing to steal and nothing to give.

Except.

Elodie looked at her own hands. Thief's hands. Hoarder's hands. Hands that held, if she was honest, several hundred lives' worth of other people's warmth, sorted and shelved in the vault behind her eyes like a miser's pantry.

"Wren," she said. "Come here."

The girl shuffled over. Elodie took her cold hands in her own and closed her eyes and went digging.

Not deep. Not the valuable things — those were for buying passage, for bribes, for survival. She went to the shallow shelves, the ones she'd never bothered selling because they were worth nothing to brokers. A fisherman's memory of his daughter falling asleep against his shoulder while he mended nets. A baker's memory of pulling the first loaf of a morning, cracking the crust open while it was still too hot to touch. An old woman's memory of dancing at her granddaughter's wedding, badly, joyfully, with her shoes off.

Small things. Warm things. The kind of inventory a person accumulates without noticing, the way a hearth accumulates heat.

She pushed them gently across, one at a time, like feeding a fire twig by twig.

Wren gasped. Her eyes flew open, wide and dark, and Elodie watched the color come back into her face in a slow tide — not health exactly, but interest. Appetite. The particular animation of a person who has just been handed a hot bun.

"The wedding one," Wren said aloud — her first word in days, cracked and rusty — and then, in images: *again, again.*

"You're supposed to be resting, not demanding encores."

*Again.* And there was the smell of bread wrapped around it, because apparently even urgency got gift-wrapped in this child's mind.

Elodie sighed the sigh of the fundamentally defeated and gave her the wedding dance again. Then the loaf. Then the nets, and the sleeping daughter, and a third thing she hadn't meant to give — a soldier's memory of his mother's kitchen, the very first memory she'd ever stolen, sold long ago to Maren Hale and yet somehow still hers in the way a scar is yours. She gave Wren the humming. Just the humming.

Wren fell asleep mid-note, warm for the first time in three days, breathing evenly, and the seed behind her ribs pulsed — Elodie would have sworn it pulsed — a shade greener than before.

Elodie sat back against the waystone and stared at the fire and understood, with the clarity of the utterly exhausted, that she was going to keep doing this. Every night. Whatever it cost. She had spent twenty years filling herself with other people's lives because her own was empty, and now she was going to pour all of it into this small stubborn vessel until either spring came or she was hollow as a winter reed.

"Well," she told the fire. "Thieves' arithmetic."

The fire crackled, noncommittal.

* * *

On the fourth day the hunters nearly had them.

It happened at the river crossing — a broad grey expanse of ice that Tam's map marked cheerfully as FORDABLE IN THAW, which was the sort of joke only a dead man could fully appreciate. They were halfway across when Elodie looked back and saw them: four figures in broker-grey, moving along the far bank with the unhurried confidence of people who know their prey has nowhere to go.

Maren Hale's people. And beside them, wrongness in the shape of a man — tall, pale, dressed in white that wasn't quite white, moving without disturbing the snow. Frostbound. The brokers had made common cause with the enemy, or perhaps had always been the enemy's gloves.

"Off the ice," Elodie breathed. "Now. Slowly. As if we're — I don't know — admiring the view."

Wren pressed a memory against her palm: *they will send him ahead. The white one. He does not walk on ice. He walks on winter itself.*

"That's comforting. Thank you."

*I am being helpful,* said the image of bread.

They reached the far bank and ran, which in waist-deep drifts meant something closer to aggressive flailing, and the white figure came onto the river behind them without hurry, and the ice sang under his feet — a high, thin note, like a wineglass rubbed at the rim.

Then the world cracked.

Not the ice. The air. A sound like a great green bell struck once, deep underground, and the white figure stopped as if he'd hit glass. For a moment — Elodie saw it clearly, would see it for the rest of her life — the snow around the man blackened and curled, and through the blackened snow, impossibly, a single shoot of green speared upward, unfurled one leaf, and froze.

The seed. Wren's seed, answering threat the way a guard dog answers a knock.

The white figure regarded the frozen leaf for a long moment. Then he turned around and walked back the way he'd come, unhurried, and the singing note faded, and the four grey figures on the far bank stood very still, the way people stand when they are recalculating.

Wren swayed. Elodie caught her before the snow did.

"What did you *do*?"

Wren's answer came slow, heavy, freighted with exhaustion: *it used me. I didn't ask it to.* And then, smaller, wrapped in the smell of bread and something new — fear: *it is getting louder. It doesn't want to wait anymore.*

Elodie looked north, toward the horizon where Tam's map promised the ruined Court, and saw the sky above it doing something sky should not do. A darkness gathering, low and vast, like a bruise forming over the bones of the world. Not cloud. Clouds moved. This sat.

The long winter, finishing its fall.

"Then we don't wait either," Elodie said, and hauled the girl up, and they went on into the gathering dark — faster now, because behind them the ice was singing again, and ahead of them the sky was learning how to end the world, and somewhere between the two, if the map and the mercy of strangers held, there was a garden made of glass with room in its heart for one more seed.

One more seed, and one thief's whole hoarded life.

She tried not to count what that would leave.
