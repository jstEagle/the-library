## 16. The Empty Throne of Green

The last mile, Wren walked on her own feet but not entirely in her own body. She drifted like a leaf deciding which way to fall, and Elodie kept a hand fisted in the back of the girl's coat because she had learned, over the frozen weeks behind them, that Wren's feet would keep going even when the rest of her wanted to stop.

"Nearly there," Elodie said, for perhaps the hundredth time. "I can feel it."

That was true, and it frightened her more than the hunters or the cold ever had. Hollowmere had been a valley of silence — magic couldn't cross its borders, and neither could anything else worth having. But out here, past the stones, past Tam's grave with its cairn of honest rocks, the world hummed. Elodie felt it in her teeth, in the soft place behind her sternum, like standing too close to a beehive made of music. And the closer they came to the old court, the louder the humming grew, until it wasn't a hum at all but a voice, wordless and vast, calling something home.

She only hoped it was calling for Wren.

The road climbed through a forest of trees she had no names for — black-barked giants wearing sleeves of snow, their branches hung with icicles that caught the grey light and turned it green. Green light, in winter. Elodie had stopped remarking on such things somewhere around the third day out of Vellum, when a river had flowed uphill beside them for an hour out of what she could only assume was stubbornness.

"There," Wren said.

It was the longest sentence she'd managed in two days, and it was one word, and Elodie could have wept for joy at it.

The court rose out of the hillside like a shipwreck that had decided, after long consideration, to become a mountain. Walls of pale stone, taller than any building in Vellum, crowned with towers whose spires had snapped off clean, as if some enormous hand had come along and pinched them. Snow lay over everything in a smooth white shroud. But the strangeness — the thing that made Elodie's breath catch and hold — was that the snow lay *green*.

Not dirty, not mossy. Green. A deep glassy emerald, as though the whole court had been dipped in stained glass and left to set. The drifts against the walls gleamed like bottle bottoms. The frozen fountain in the outer courtyard held water that had stopped mid-leap, a sculpture of ice and light, and every surface of it glowed faintly from within, the way a firefly does just before it decides against glowing.

"Oh," said Elodie, who considered herself a woman of words and found she had exactly one.

Wren pulled free of her grip and ran.

Elodie let her. There was nowhere in this place for the girl to come to harm that the place itself hadn't already suffered, and besides, Elodie's legs had begun shaking somewhere around the border stones and hadn't properly stopped. She followed at her own pace, boots crunching on glass-snow, through an archway wide enough to drive a cart and four oxen through, into the heart of the court.

* * *

It had been a garden. That was the first thing, the thing that hit her sideways, in the ribs, where a person keeps the soft parts. Not a throne room, not a fortress — a garden. The great hall of the Verdant Court had been open to the sky once; now a ceiling of ice arched overhead, thick as cathedral glass, filtering the daylight into something underwater and holy. Rows of planting beds stretched away into the gloom, each one raised, each one shaped with care, and every single one of them empty. Not dead — dead things rot, and there was no rot here. Empty the way a stage is empty between performances. Waiting.

At the far end stood the throne.

It was not gold. It was not stone. It was a tree — or had been — grown into the shape of a seat by someone who understood that patience is the most powerful magic there is. Its branches curled up and over like protecting arms. Its roots spilled down from the dais and ran out across the floor in silver-green veins, and Elodie saw, following them with her eyes, that those veins went everywhere. Into the beds. Up the walls. Out under the archway toward the whole sleeping world.

Every vein was dark.

Except one. At the center of the throne, where a heart would be if trees had hearts, something small pulsed with a light so faint Elodie thought at first her eyes were making it up. It flickered. It guttered. It was, unmistakably, dying.

And Wren was climbing the dais toward it with her hands outstretched.

"Wren—" Elodie started forward, then stopped, because the girl hadn't touched it yet. She stood before the throne with her palms hovering an inch from the bark, head bowed, and from her small body came a sound Elodie had never heard her make: a low, wavering note, like a struck bell remembering how to sing.

The pulse in the wood answered. Flickered brighter. Held.

Then Wren swayed, and Elodie moved without deciding to, catching her before she folded onto the steps.

"I'm all right." Wren's voice was thin as onion skin. She was shivering, and not from cold — the seed inside her, the living heart of everything this court had been, was straining toward its throne like a dog toward a door it knows. "It wants to go home. I want to let it."

"So let it," Elodie said. "That's why we came. That's the whole — that's the entire point of the frozen misery we've walked through, Wren. Plant it. Go on."

Wren looked up at her, and there was something in the child's face that Elodie had seen before, in mirrors, in the moment before she lied to a traveler about the road ahead. Pity. Pity, and the particular gentleness of someone about to tell you the price of something you've already promised to buy.

"It needs a keeper," Wren said. "To plant it. That's how it works. That's how it always worked."

"Fine. You're the keeper. You've carried it across half the world—"

"Not me." Wren shook her head slowly. "Keepers don't carry the seed, Elodie. Keepers *are* the ground it grows in. The seed takes root in a remembered life. All of it. Everything a keeper is — every memory, every name, every face they love — goes into the soil, so the garden has something to grow *from*. That's what my mother told me. That's what the tongue says." She tapped her own temple. "*Vess-ah-nui.* One who gives their whole self so that green may return. It's not a title. It's a price."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere overhead, the ice ceiling creaked, settling, patient.

"How much of a life?" Elodie asked, and heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used at bargaining tables, the voice that never paid a copper more than it must.

"All of it," Wren said simply. "That's why the court fell, partly. When the Frostbound came, the last keeper was already old, and she'd given so much over the years keeping the winter back — little pieces, seasons' worth — that there wasn't enough of her left to plant a new spring. So she hid the seed instead. Sent it away. Sent it—" Wren stopped. Looked down at her own hands. "Sent it to be carried by whoever could reach the valley. Because magic can't cross the borders. But a child isn't magic. A child is just a child until the seed wakes."

Elodie sat down on the dais step. It seemed wise, suddenly, to be sitting.

"And if nobody plants it?"

"The seed dies. The winter finishes falling. Not just here — everywhere. Hollowmere was only the first valley to forget. It won't be the last." Wren sat down beside her, small and grave, and leaned her head briefly against Elodie's shoulder, the way she'd begun doing sometime on the Road of Small Kindnesses, unasked, unforgiven, unstoppable. "But Elodie — it doesn't have to be you. We can find someone else. Someone old. Someone who's already losing their memories anyway, who wouldn't miss—"

"Don't," Elodie said.

"—who'd call it a mercy—"

"*Don't.*" Sharper than she meant. Wren flinched, and Elodie closed her eyes and breathed until the sharpness wore down. "I'm sorry. It's only — I've spent my whole life taking memories from people, Wren. Selling them. Spending them. I know exactly what a memory weighs, because I've carried hundreds of them, and I know exactly what a person becomes when the important ones are gone, because I've watched it happen from the outside, and I have never once — not once — thought of it as a mercy."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "What about your own? Do you know what those weigh?"

Elodie laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I wouldn't know. I never had any. That was rather the whole tragedy of me."

"You did," Wren said. "You do. I've felt them. When you lend me memories to talk — the kitchen with the blue door. The woman singing with flour on her hands. Those aren't stolen, Elodie. Stolen things feel different. Cold at the edges. Those are yours. They've been yours the whole time, buried under everything you took to fill the hole where they should have been."

Elodie stared at her. The hall hummed around them, that vast wordless voice, and beneath it now she could almost hear — faintly, absurdly — a melody. A woman singing. Flour on her hands.

"We'll speak of that later," Elodie said, because if she spoke of it now she would come apart on the steps of a dead king's greenhouse, and there was work to do. "First: options. I didn't cross a frozen country to sit on a stair. What are our options?"

* * *

They were, it developed, three.

Option the first: find another keeper. This required finding a person willing to surrender their entire inner life, within days, before the seed guttered out entirely, in a ruined court at the edge of the map, while broker-hunters and Frostbound closed in from every direction. Wren listed these obstacles with the air of a merchant describing a slightly damaged bolt of cloth.

"Right," said Elodie. "Option the second."

"Run farther. Find help. The free cities might—"

"Might. In spring. If the seed lasts. Which it won't." Elodie got up and paced the length of the empty planting beds, her footsteps ringing oddly on the glassed earth. "Option the third, then. Tell me what happens. Exactly. To the keeper."

Wren hugged her knees. "They don't die. The garden keeps them — that's the kindness in it, my mother said. The keeper becomes part of the court. Their memories become the soil, their — their self becomes the roots. They live as long as the garden lives." A pause. "But they don't remember being anyone. They wake up the morning after the planting with nothing. A new person, in an old body, in a blooming garden, with everyone they ever loved gone from them like a dream."

"Like waking from Hollowmere," Elodie murmured. "Where I spent thirty years owning everybody's yesterdays and none of my own." She stopped pacing. She looked at the throne, at the tiny guttering heartbeat of it, and felt something in her chest do a strange, quiet arithmetic.

Here was the joke of it — and there was a joke, there always was, the world was a cruel comedian with excellent timing — here was the joke: she had built her whole life on other people's memories. She had hoarded them like a miser, hundreds of lives in jars and vials and locked boxes, soldiers' kitchens and merchants' weddings and farmers' funerals, and she had told herself it was survival, transactional loneliness, the only warmth available in a valley that forgot. And now the choice stood in front of her wearing its true face: give away the hoard, all of it, the stolen and the buried-alive alike, and become soil. Become spring. Become a woman who woke in a garden knowing nothing, owing nothing, *having* nothing —

— and having, for the first time, given everything.

"It's not nothing," Wren said quietly, watching her face. "Whatever you're thinking. It isn't nothing. The garden remembers *that* you loved, even if it doesn't remember whom. My mother was very clear on that. She said the keeper wakes up kind."

"That sounds insufferable."

"It sounded nice to me."

Elodie huffed out something that was nearly a laugh. "Of course it did. You would say that. You gave away your voice before I ever met you, you impossible child — don't look surprised, I worked it out in Vellum, no mute child crosses a war-torn country alone carrying a dead language and a live god-seed unless she's already paid something enormous for the privilege." She crouched in front of Wren. "So here's what happens now. We sleep. We eat the last of Tam's waybread, which tastes like sweetened chalk and I will miss terribly. And tomorrow, if the seed still burns and the hunters haven't arrived — and they will arrive, Wren, Maren Hale does not travel slowly and Corvane does not travel at all, he sends weather — tomorrow I will decide."

"You've decided already," Wren said. "You decided on the stairs. You get a particular face. Like a cat that's knocked something off a table on purpose."

"I do not have a face like that."

"You absolutely do. Tam said so."

"Tam," said Elodie, "is dead, and cannot testify." But her voice caught on his name, on the cairn of honest rocks a hundred miles behind them, on the cost of kindness made literal, and Wren reached out and took her hand, and neither of them said anything else for a while.

* * *

Night came early, as it did now, the long winter pressing its advantage. They made camp in the lee of the throne itself, because the dark veins in the floor gave off a faint residual warmth, the ghost of green, and because Wren wanted to be near the seed and Elodie had stopped pretending she wanted anything else.

Elodie lay awake a long time, listening to the ice ceiling tick and settle, doing sums she already knew the answer to.

She thought about the soldier whose mother's kitchen she'd sold to Maren Hale all those months ago — the first theft in this story, though hardly the first of her life. He'd wept when it was gone, and hadn't known why, and had thanked her for the company. She thought about the vault in Vellum, and the small lead box with her own name scratched on it in her mother's hand, the box she'd carried out under her coat and never opened, because opening it meant admitting it existed, and admitting it existed meant admitting she'd been somebody before she was a thief.

Tomorrow, maybe, she'd open it. Tomorrow, maybe, she'd pour the whole box into the dark veins of a dead garden and watch a tree grow out of everything she'd ever been.

Or Corvane would come first, with his polite voice and his impossible offers, and the choice would be taken out of her hands, and she'd spend eternity wondering whether she'd have been brave.

The seed pulsed in the dark. Once. Twice. Small and stubborn as a candle in a doorway.

"Go on, then," Elodie whispered to it. "Burn till morning. I'll be here."

And far off, beyond the broken towers, beyond the green-lit snow, the wind changed direction — and began, very softly, to sing.
