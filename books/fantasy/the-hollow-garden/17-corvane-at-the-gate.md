## 17. Corvane at the Gate

Corvane had not felt cold in three hundred years, which was, he often reflected, the great irony of his existence. The Frostbound were named for winter, sworn to it, sustained by it, and yet somewhere in the long centuries of service he had simply stopped noticing. Cold was a country he lived in. You did not remark upon your own floorboards.

What he felt now, standing at the shattered gate of the Verdant Court with a thousand soldiers at his back and snow falling like an apology no one would accept, was something colder than cold.

Doubt.

"You're doing the face," said Vess, his second, from beside him. She was a broad woman with frost in her eyelashes and the conversational delicacy of a thrown brick. "The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic you don't like."

"I am always doing arithmetic I don't like. It's the job."

"No, this is worse arithmetic. This is *moral* arithmetic." She squinted up at the walls of the dead court, glassed over and gleaming faintly under the storm-light, like a garden that had been dipped in sugar and forgotten. "You think she'll refuse."

Corvane did not answer immediately. That was answer enough, and they both knew it, and Vess had the grace to pretend otherwise by examining her gauntlets with tremendous interest.

He had come to offer mercy. That was the official word for it, stamped into the treaty-scrolls and spoken in the honeyed register the Ambassadors' Office preferred: *mercy*. In practice, the offer was this: give us the child, and we will return to you the woman who has been asleep in our keeping since before you could walk. Your mother, whole and unfrozen, in exchange for one small girl who was always going to die out here anyway.

It was, Corvane had to admit, an elegant cruelty. He had helped draft it. There were phrases in the third clause he was rather proud of.

And yet.

He looked at the gate — at the two figures silhouetted against the pale light leaking through the glass garden beyond it. One tall, one small. The thief and the seed. The woman who had spent her life taking memories from travelers, and the child who carried the last living thing the old world had made.

"Envoy," said a voice behind him, crisp as new ice. "She's coming to the wall."

* * *

Maren Hale walked like a woman who had never once doubted that the ground would hold her. Corvane had recruited her eleven years ago, when she was a memory-broker with sharp eyes and sharper ledgers, and in all that time she had never surprised him. She wanted wealth, then influence, then immortality of the only kind Hollowmere's gray economy permitted: to be indispensable. He had offered all three. She had shaken his hand with fingers that smelled faintly of ink and other people's lives.

Now she stood at the base of the gate, snow gathering on her broker's coat, looking up at Elodie Voss as though inspecting merchandise.

"You came," Corvane said quietly, beside her. It was not a question, but he let it hang like one.

"I keep my accounts," Maren said. "You should know that better than anyone." Her breath fogged. "She'll listen to me. We've done business for years. She trusts me the way a fish trusts a hook — because it's always been there and hasn't killed her yet."

"That's a grim way to describe loyalty."

"It's an accurate way to describe Hollowmere." Maren's jaw tightened, just slightly, the only tell she'd ever shown him. "I've known that girl since she was nineteen and terrible at haggling. This isn't personal. It's just the largest transaction of my career, and I intend to close it."

"And if closing it breaks her?"

Maren looked at him then, and for a moment Corvane saw past the broker to the girl from the valley markets, the one who had learned early that everything soft gets taken. "Everything breaks everyone eventually, Envoy. I'm merely offering her a version where she gets her mother back first."

Corvane inclined his head. It was, he thought, a very good line. He wondered if she'd rehearsed it on the march north, or if cruelty simply came to some people pre-polished.

"Then go and say your piece," he said. "And Maren — the Ambassador's terms stand. Nothing more, nothing less. If you improvise generosity, I will disavow you before your body hits the snow."

"I wouldn't dream of improvising generosity," Maren said. "It doesn't pay."

She walked toward the gate alone, her boots crunching a rhythm into the silence, and Corvane watched Elodie Voss watch her come, and tried to read the thief's face across forty yards of falling snow.

He couldn't. Which troubled him more than he cared to admit. He had negotiated with archdukes whose faces were open books compared to hers.

"She's going to refuse," Vess said again, at his shoulder.

"You've said that twice."

"I'm going to keep saying it until you admit I'm right, because I want it noted in the record that I called it." She shifted her weight. "Envoy — between us. Do *you* want her to take it?"

Corvane considered lying. Vess had earned too many winters beside him for that.

"I want this finished," he said. "Three hundred years of holding the line against a court of gardeners, and for what? They're dust. Their throne is a greenhouse full of glass flowers. And still the old men in the White Hall wake up screaming about vines." He exhaled, and the breath left him in a long silver ribbon. "A war ends when someone plants the last seed or burns it. I was sent to make sure it's burned. Or failing that—" he nodded at the gate, at the small figure beside the tall one, "—that it's ours, potted and leashed. Either way, the waking stops."

"And the mother?"

"The mother is real," Corvane said. "That's what makes it work. We don't deal in lies, Vess. Lies melt. We deal in true things arranged cruelly." He straightened his coat. "Come. Let's go and be merciful at them."

* * *

The parley happened at the gate itself, because Corvane had learned long ago that thresholds mattered to people like Elodie Voss — people who lived their whole lives on the wrong side of borders. He stopped ten paces out, close enough to speak without shouting, far enough that his honor guard's presence was architecture rather than threat. Mostly.

"Elodie Voss," he said. "I am Corvane, Envoy of the Frostbound, and I come with the last honest offer anyone will ever make you."

Behind the gate, the child — Wren, the scouts called her — pressed close to Elodie's side. She was smaller than the reports suggested. Reports usually got numbers right and missed everything else; they had failed to mention that when she looked at Corvane, she did so with the flat, unblinking assessment of someone deciding whether he was weather or wolf.

"You'll forgive me," Elodie said, "if 'honest' and 'Frostbound' sit strangely together in a sentence."

"They do in most sentences," Corvane agreed. "But consider: I could have burned this gate an hour ago. I have a thousand soldiers and you have a dying child and a garden made of glass. Instead I'm standing in the snow discussing terms. That is either honesty or extraordinary arrogance, and I promise you arrogance would have been cheaper."

He heard Vess make a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Elodie's arms were around the girl now. Protective. Instinctive. *Bad sign*, Corvane thought, in the detached professional part of his mind. Mothers and mothers-to-be were the hardest negotiations there were, and this woman wasn't even the child's mother, which meant she had none of the exhaustion and all of the ferocity.

"Say your terms," Elodie said.

"Maren," Corvane said. "Tell her."

* * *

Later, Corvane would replay Maren Hale's performance and grudgingly concede it was masterful. She did not plead. She did not threaten. She stood in the snow with her hands folded like a woman presenting a ledger, and she spoke to Elodie Voss in the language of their shared valley: cost, weight, arithmetic.

"He's telling the truth about your mother," Maren said. "I verified it myself, years ago, before I ever sent the first message south. Lyra Voss. Court gardener. Taken at the fall, held in stasis at the White Hall. Alive, Elodie. Not a memory of alive. *Alive.* Sleeping, but sleeping is just waiting with better posture."

Elodie flinched. Corvane saw it — a small, involuntary movement, like a door blowing open on a warm room.

"You knew," Elodie said to Maren, and her voice had gone very quiet, which Corvane had learned, in three centuries of negotiations, was infinitely more dangerous than shouting. "All these years. You bought memories off me. You sold me wine at midwinter and asked after my health. And you knew."

"I found out four years ago," Maren said. "And yes. I said nothing, because saying something would have gotten us both killed and accomplished nothing. Now it accomplishes everything." She took one step closer to the gate. "Listen to me. You cannot win this. Look behind me. Count the banners. The seed dies tonight whether you plant it or freeze — Wren is fading, Elodie, I can see it from here, the poor mite is translucent. But your mother doesn't have to be collateral. Hand the child over, walk out under envoy's protection, and by spring thaw you'll be holding her hand in a warm room while the rest of the world does whatever the rest of the world does."

"You'd give us safe passage," Elodie said. "Both of us. Out of the valley. Free."

"Under seal," Corvane confirmed. "The oldest seal we have. Break it and every Frostbound oath-bound is released from every restraint — including mine."

"There it is," Elodie said softly. "The hook inside the bait. There's always a hook."

"The hook," Corvane said, "is that we are asking rather than taking. I'd have thought, of all people, a thief would recognize the difference."

Something moved behind Elodie's eyes — not anger. Something older and sadder. And then the child did something that Corvane, in all his long career, had not been prepared for.

Wren stepped forward. Away from Elodie's arms. Out into the open, onto the trampled snow between the gate and the host, small enough that the wind pushed her sideways. And she looked up at Corvane with those grave, ancient eyes, and she opened her mouth, and what came out was not the dead tongue of the Verdant Court.

It was a memory. His.

He knew it instantly — the taste of it, the shape. A kitchen. A low fire. A woman's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists, humming a tuneless song. *His* kitchen. His mother's, from before — from the human life he'd had before the ice took the name he was born with and gave him a title instead. A memory he had surrendered when he took the white oath, the way they all did, the way the Frostbound paid for their endless winters: everything warm, handed over, locked in the vaults beneath the White Hall.

The child was showing it back to him. Deliberately. As a question.

*Is this what you are? Is this what you chose?*

Corvane stood very still. Beside him, he heard several soldiers shift uneasily; they'd felt it too, the brush of something green moving through the frozen air, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.

"Wren," Elodie said sharply, and pulled her back. "Don't. Whatever you're doing — don't waste it on him."

But it was too late, and they all knew it, and Corvane found that his throat had gone tight in a way it had not gone tight since the last century he'd had a throat worth the name.

"Well," he managed. "That's a new negotiating tactic."

"Envoy," Vess murmured, warningly.

"I'm fine." He was not entirely fine. He cleared his throat. "Elodie Voss. The offer stands until moonrise. After that, the storm comes down — a real one, the kind the old songs warn about — and my mercy goes with it. Think on it. Think on her." He nodded at the gate, meaning the mother who slept three hundred miles south. "Some debts you pay forward. Some you pay back. This one you could pay *sideways*, and everyone walks away breathing."

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and in her hair, and she did not brush it away, and Corvane understood suddenly that she had stopped caring about small discomforts — that she was already somewhere past them, somewhere the cold didn't reach, doing arithmetic of her own.

"Thank you for the honesty," she said at last. "It's rarer than you'd think, even among thieves."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a *not yet*," Elodie said. "Which from me, in this valley, is practically a marriage proposal. Ask me again at moonrise."

Corvane bowed — a shallow, correct bow, the kind that conceded nothing — and turned back toward his lines. Behind him he heard Maren's voice drop low and urgent, wheedling, insistent, the broker working her oldest mark one final time, and he heard Elodie's answers getting shorter, and shorter, and quieter.

Vess fell into step beside him. "Well?"

"She asked for until moonrise."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer she has left," Corvane said. Above the ruined court, the clouds had begun to turn the color of iron, and the wind had picked up a low note, like a drawn bow. He had commanded storms before. He knew their overtures. "Get the men under cover. When it breaks, nothing negotiates — not me, not her, not the seed. Whatever happens next happens fast."

"And if she refuses?"

Corvane looked back once. At the gate. At the tall woman and the small one, framed in the dying light, leaning into each other like two trees grown too close to fell separately.

"Then," he said, "we find out what a gardener's daughter does with nothing left to lose. And Vess — I've read the old accounts of the last war."

"And?"

"The gardeners won the opening battle of it with a single rosebush." He pulled his collar up against the rising wind. "Wake me the moment the sky changes."

Far to the south, past the valley, past the border stones, the first thunder of the long winter's final argument rolled up over the mountains — and the snow began, gently, terribly, to fall harder.
