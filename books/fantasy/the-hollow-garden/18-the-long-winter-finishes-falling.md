## 18. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The blizzard came at dusk, the way the worst things always did — politely, almost apologetically, and then all at once.

Elodie had been watching the horizon all day from the shelter of a fallen archway, and she had told herself the gray smear along the northern sky was just cloud. She had told herself that about a great many things in her life: that the brokers were merely businessmen, that stealing memories was merely trade, that she could carry Wren to the old court before the weather turned. She was, she reflected grimly, an accomplished liar even to herself.

Then the wind arrived ahead of the snow like a herald clearing its throat, and the temperature dropped so fast her breath cracked in the air, and there was no more lying to be done.

"Wren." She shook the girl's shoulder gently. "Up. We have to move."

Wren didn't move.

That was the first truly frightening thing. The child had been slowing for two days now, yes — stumbling, sleeping too long, her small hands cold as river stones no matter how many warm memories Elodie poured into them — but she had always opened her eyes when Elodie spoke. Now her eyes stayed shut, lashes rimed with frost, and her breathing came in shallow, fluttering pulls, like a bird deciding whether each breath was worth the trouble.

And in the crook of Wren's arm, wrapped in cloth, the seed had gone dim.

Elodie had seen it glow every night since they'd crossed the border stones — a soft green pulse, patient as a heartbeat, the last living thing the Verdant Court had ever made. Tonight it barely flickered. When she lifted it, it weighed nothing and felt like everything: a held breath, a promise running out of air.

"No," Elodie said, to the seed, to the weather, to whatever ancient machinery of war and winter had decided this was the moment. "No, you don't. We walked three hundred miles for you. You will not quit on me in sight of the door."

The court was close. Tam's map said so; the glass-topped hills said so; the strange, sweet smell beneath the ice said so, like a garden remembered by the ground itself. An hour's walk in fair weather. In this?

She looked at Wren's face, slack and blue-lipped, and understood that there would be no walking at all unless something changed immediately.

*Think,* she told herself. *You are a thief. Thieves are practical.*

Practical. Right. She had a pack with half a loaf going stale, a knife, a waterskin, and a head full of other people's lives — hundreds of them, sorted and shelved like jars in a pantry. A soldier's mother's kitchen, bread rising under a damp cloth. A fisherman's wedding morning, gulls screaming over the harbor. A midwife's hundred safe deliveries. A king dying in a garden, saying words in a dead tongue that had turned out to be the beginning of everything.

And underneath all of it, locked where she kept the thing she'd never once looked at directly: her own childhood. Her mother's face. The garden behind the wall. The reason any of this had ever happened.

*Not that one yet,* said the hoarder in her chest, the voice that had kept her alive in Hollowmere for twenty years. *Anything but that one.*

"Elodie," Wren whispered.

Her eyes were open after all — slits of green, unfocused, finding Elodie's face by sound alone. Her lips were moving around shapes in the old tongue, the language she'd arrived speaking, the language Elodie had learned piece by piece from a stolen memory of a dying king. Elodie caught only one word, and it was one she knew:

*Plant. Plant. Plant.*

"I know," Elodie said. "I know, little wren. We're nearly there. Just hold on. Hold on, and I'll figure out the rest, because figuring things out is what I do, and I am very good at it, and modest besides."

Wren's mouth twitched. Even half-dead, the girl could hear nonsense when it was offered to her. It was, Elodie thought absurdly, the closest thing they had to a shared joke, and she clung to it like a rope over a cliff.

Then the snow arrived in earnest, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

She carried Wren on her back, arms hooked under the girl's knees, the seed tucked between their bodies where her own warmth might fool it into lasting. The blizzard erased the world within minutes. Sky became ground became sky. The wind found every gap in her clothing and filled it with knives. She walked by faith and by counting — two hundred steps, rest, two hundred steps — because direction meant nothing anymore and stopping meant sleeping and sleeping meant dying, and she had read enough stolen minds to know exactly how dying went.

Somewhere in the third hour, she began talking. Not to Wren, who couldn't hear, and not to herself, exactly. To the seed.

"You're a stubborn little thing, aren't you," she panted. "I respect that. I've been stubborn my whole life. Stubborn enough to stay in a valley that ate people's futures, because leaving was scarier than starving. Stubborn enough to steal instead of ask. Stubborn enough to think that if I collected enough other people's happiness, some of it would rub off." She laughed, and the laugh froze on her lips. "It doesn't, by the way. In case anyone asks you. It just sits there. Like silverware in a drawer. Beautiful and useless and never once used at supper."

The seed pulsed. Faintly. Almost sarcastically.

"Oh, don't judge me. You've been asleep in a jar for a hundred years."

But the pulse gave her an idea, and the idea was so simple and so terrible that she stopped walking entirely, standing in the white roar with the wind trying to peel her off the world.

Memories fed Wren. Warm ones — kitchens, summers, hands braiding hair. Every time the girl had faltered on the road, Elodie had lent her one, pressing it across the strange bridge they'd built between their minds, and Wren had brightened like a lamp given oil. That was how they'd talked, in the early days. That was how they'd become *them*, whatever they were now.

The seed was magic. Wren carried magic. And magic, in the world beyond the valley, ran on memory the way lamps ran on oil — Tam had said as much, the night before he died holding the hedgerow for two strangers. *Everything green remembers,* he'd said. *That's what the Court was. A memory the land kept of being alive.*

The seed wasn't dimming because it was broken. It was dimming because it was hungry, and the cold was eating faster than a sleeping child could feed it.

Elodie stood in the blizzard and did thieves' arithmetic, the only mathematics she had ever been good at.

*I can't carry her and walk and fight the cold all at once. But I can feed it. I have food. I have a whole pantry of food.*

*And it will cost me every jar I open.*

She thought of Maren Hale's office in Hollowmere, the ledger with its neat columns, the way Maren had smiled when she priced a memory: *A summer is worth a winter, dear. A childhood is worth a house.* Everything in Elodie's life had always had a price attached, and she had always been the one collecting.

Now she stood at the counter on the other side.

"All right," she said aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle. "All right. But we do this in order. Cheapest first. I'm not a saint, I'm a shopkeeper."

* * *

She knelt in the snow, laid Wren down against her chest, and cupped the seed in both hands. Then she closed her eyes and opened the first jar.

The soldier's kitchen. The oldest theft she owned, the one that had started everything — a young man's memory of his mother's kitchen, bread rising under a cloth, steam on the window, a woman humming off-key. Elodie had sold it to Maren Hale for coin, bought it back years later for triple, and told herself the price was business.

She pressed it toward the seed and let go.

It left her the way blood leaves a cut — not painfully, exactly, but with an awful sense of *outwardness*, of something that had been part of the container becoming part of the world. For one shining second she stood in that kitchen again: the hum, the yeast-smell, the woman turning to say something ordinary and kind. Then it was gone, and she was kneeling in a blizzard, and the seed was glowing green as new grass.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it works."

The seed flared. Warmth spread up her arms. Beneath her, Wren's breathing deepened by one degree, which was one degree more than nothing.

"Right," Elodie said, and her eyes were wet and freezing on her cheeks. "Cheapest first, I said. So."

The fisherman's wedding. Gone — gulls and salt and a bride laughing into the wind, spent in a heartbeat of green light. The midwife's deliveries, a hundred first cries, poured out like water from a jug. A shepherd's knowledge of stars. A schoolteacher's patience. A drunkard's one perfect evening, his daughter asleep on his chest, which he'd sold to forget and which Elodie returned to the dark because it deserved better than a drawer.

Each one hurt differently than stealing ever had. Stealing had felt like lifting a purse — quick, thrilling, a little shameful, easily forgotten. This felt like pulling teeth from her own head while they were still healthy. This felt like watching her house burn room by room and choosing which room to stand in.

And still she opened jar after jar, because Wren's breathing steadied with every one, and the seed burned brighter, and somewhere ahead — she could feel it now through the soles of her boots, a faint thrumming like a heart under the floorboards — the old court was answering.

"You should see this," she told Wren, whose cheek was pressed to her collarbone. "You should see what you look like when you're fed. You're lit up like a festival. Your mother would be—" Her voice snagged. "Whoever your mother was. Whoever trusted you to the road. They'd be proud."

Wren stirred. One frozen hand crept up and closed around a fold of Elodie's coat, weak as a kitten's grip, and held on.

*Hold on,* Elodie agreed silently. *We're both holding on.*

* * *

The storm worsened. Of course it did. Somewhere behind them, she knew, Corvane's host was marching in this same white hell, and Maren Hale with them, and every broker's hunter who'd ever smelled money in a mute child. The blizzard might even be theirs — the Frostbound had ways, Tam had warned, of asking the winter for favors. But Elodie found she had stopped caring about what was behind her. There was a clarity in spending everything you had: no weight left for fear.

The problem was the shelves were emptying.

She inventoried what remained, kneeling in the drifts with the seed blazing against her palms, and the arithmetic turned ugly. A handful of minor thefts — a baker's dawn routine, a nun's certainty, a thief's (a colleague's, really) pride in a clean job. And then the deep shelf. The ones she'd sworn never to spend.

Her mother's face.

She had carried it out of Hollowmere without ever looking at it straight-on, the way you carry a mirror covered in cloth. She knew what was under there. Chapter twelve of her own life, the one the brokers' vault had given back to her in Vellum: a garden behind a high wall, a woman with soil-blackened fingers and her same sharp chin, pressing a bundle into a stranger's arms. *Keep her past the stones. Don't tell her what she is until the world is ready to be kind about it.* The last living gardener of the Verdant Court, hiding her daughter in the one place magic couldn't reach, because the valley's blindness was the only cloak left.

Wren hadn't been sent to find the seed's planter. Wren had been sent to find *her*. All those miles, all those returned memories, all that penance — and the answer had been sleeping in her own skull the entire time, wearing her mother's face.

If she fed that memory to the seed, she would plant the garden and lose the gardener both. She would save the world and forget why she'd bothered.

"There has to be another way," she said. "There's always another way. I've talked my way out of everything my whole life. Talk to me. Somebody talk to me."

The wind answered. The seed pulsed — softer now, the feast already fading, hunger creeping back in at the edges. And Wren, half-conscious against her chest, murmured something in the old tongue, and this time Elodie understood it perfectly, because it was a phrase from the dying king's memory, the one she'd built her whole understanding of the dead court upon:

*A garden is not grown from gold. It is grown from what you are willing to bury.*

"Oh, that's cheating," Elodie whispered. "Bringing my own education back at me. That's low."

But her hands were already moving, tucking the seed into the crook of Wren's neck where it could drink warmth, gathering the girl up, standing. Because the thrumming under the snow was louder now, unmistakable — the court, the walls, the glass garden, close, so close — and because the truth was she had known since the border stones what the ending would cost. Tam had known. Maybe even Maren had known, in whatever shriveled place Maren kept her knowing.

The long winter finished falling around them, vast and white and final, and Elodie Ashgrove walked into it carrying the last two things she owned: a dying child, and a memory of her mother's face.

She intended to arrive with both.

What she did when she got there — whether she was brave enough, or desperate enough, or simply out of coins — the storm would decide, and the storm was in no mood for mercy.

Ahead, through a tear in the snow, she saw them: gates of black iron furred with ice, and beyond them, pale as bone under the drifting white, the ruined court waiting with its empty throne of green.

The seed, against Wren's neck, pulsed once — twice — like a knock at a door.

"Coming," Elodie said. "We're coming. Keep the kettle on."
