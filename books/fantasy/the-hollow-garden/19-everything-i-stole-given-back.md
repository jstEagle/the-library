## 19. Everything I Stole, Given Back

The blizzard came down like a verdict.

Elodie felt it before she saw it — a pressure in the air, a hush that pressed against her ears the way water presses against a drowning woman's chest. The sky over the ruined court went the color of old pewter, and then the snow began, not falling so much as *arriving*, whole battalions of it, driven sideways on a wind that howled through the glass garden like something grieving.

Wren was dying.

There was no gentler word for it. The girl lay in Elodie's arms at the base of the dead throne — a throne of woven branches turned to clear glass by some ancient catastrophe of cold and magic — and she was light as a bundle of dry sticks. Her lips had gone blue. The seed, which Elodie could see now without even trying, a small green star pulsing beneath the girl's skin just below her collarbone, had dimmed to an ember about to go out.

"No," Elodie said, for perhaps the hundredth time since they'd stumbled through the shattered gates. "No, you don't. You walked all this way. You don't get to stop here."

Wren's eyes opened a sliver. In them, Elodie saw the memory she'd lent her that morning — one of her own stolen ones, a soldier's memory of his mother's kitchen, bread rising under a cloth, steam on a window. Wren had been living inside it for three days now, breathing it like air, because the cold had taken everything else from her except what Elodie could give.

And that was when Elodie understood.

Not slowly. Not with dawning horror or noble resolve. It arrived all at once, the way a debt collector arrives, the way winter arrives: completely.

*The seed eats memories.* Tam had told them that, in his warm kitchen with his maps spread out like a confession. *That's what it is, girl. A memory made flesh. The Verdant Court didn't run on spells and sacrifices. It ran on remembering. Every gardener fed it their whole life, everything they were, and in exchange it kept the world alive.*

She had thought he meant it as history. A story about other people, long dead.

She looked down at Wren, whose breath was coming in small, ragged pulls, and she thought: *I have hundreds of lives. I have been hoarding them like a dragon hoards gold, and I never once spent a single coin of them on anything that mattered.*

"Well," Elodie said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word, which was embarrassing, but there was no one to be embarrassed in front of except a dying child and a blizzard, and neither of them would judge. "Well. I suppose this is what it's for."

* * *

She knelt in the snow at the foot of the glass throne and laid Wren gently against its base, tucking her own coat around the girl like a cocoon. Then she pressed both palms flat against the frozen earth.

It was colder than anything she had ever touched. Colder than the border stones. Colder than Maren Hale's counting room, where warmth went to die. She felt the frost reach up her arms like climbing ivy, and she almost pulled away, and then she thought of the kitchen — the soldier's mother's kitchen, the first memory she'd ever stolen, back when she'd been too young and too hungry to understand what she was taking — and she stopped fighting.

*Here,* she thought, toward the seed, toward the dim green star in the dying child's chest. *Take this one first.*

The memory left her the way a bird leaves a hand.

One moment it was hers — the smell of yeast and woodsmoke, the woman humming off-key, flour on her forearms to the elbow — and the next it was simply *gone*, and where it had been there was a clean bright ache, like the space where a tooth used to be. And beneath her palms, deep in the frozen ground, something answered.

A single thread of green unspooled through the ice.

Elodie laughed. It came out half sob, half wonder. "Oh," she said. "Oh, you're *hungry*. All right. All right."

She gave it the baker's memory next — the one where he burned his first hundred loaves and learned to read dough by touch instead of clocks. Then the fisherman's dawn on the grey sea, gulls screaming, his daughter asleep against his back. Then the midwife's memory of forty years of first cries. Then a shepherd's summer, a weaver's wedding, a thief's one honest day.

Each one left her lighter. Each one left the ground greener.

The threads of green multiplied beneath the snow, spreading outward from her knees in slow fractals, and where they passed, the glass garden began — very quietly, the way a secret begins — to change. Frost retreated from the glass branches in retreating rings. Somewhere overhead, a pane of the great shattered dome sang a low, resonant note, like a struck bell, like a held breath finally released.

"You're doing it," Wren whispered. Her voice was paper-thin. Her eyes were open now, wide and dark and fixed on Elodie with an expression Elodie had no stolen memory to name. "You're really—"

"Don't talk," Elodie said. "Save your strength. This is my job. I'm very good at taking things; turns out I can do it in reverse."

It wasn't entirely true, and they both knew it. Giving hurt differently than stealing ever had. Stealing had been easy — a slipping, a sliding, a door opening where no one stood guard. But giving was a *rending*. Every memory tore free with little roots attached, roots that had grown into her during all these years of keeping, and each one left a wound that bled nothing but absence.

She gave the memory of the border stones and the flower blooming through ice — her terror, her wonder, both at once.

She gave the memory of Maren Hale's counting room, the click of the abacus, the broker's smile like a drawer closing.

She gave the memory of Tam's kitchen, the map spread out, the old deserter's hands shaking as he poured tea for two runaways he owed nothing to. *Hold onto that one,* she begged the seed silently. *Keep that one somewhere safe. He died for us. Don't let him be forgotten twice.*

The garden heard her. She was almost sure it did. A thread of green curled up around the base of the throne and stayed there, thickening, like a hand placed gently over a grave.

* * *

The blizzard reached the gates.

She felt Corvane's host before she saw them — a wrongness in the wind, a silence inside the storm's roar, the particular cold of things that had once been men. Through the white curtain she made out shapes: tall, pale, armored in rime, moving with the terrible patience of winter itself. And at their head, a figure in a cloak of unmelting frost, eyes like chips of the sky before dawn.

Corvane. Come to collect.

Elodie did not stop giving memories. There wasn't time, and anyway, she found she no longer particularly cared.

"Elodie Voss." Corvane's voice carried through the storm without effort, the way a knife carries through cloth. "The Court is dead. What you wake will not thank you. It will only begin the war again — and this time, there will be no valley to hide in, no borders to cower behind. Give me the child. Go home. Live."

"I don't have a home," Elodie said. Her voice sounded strange to her — thinner, emptier, already partly someone she hadn't finished becoming. "I had a hovel full of other people's lives. That's not the same thing." She pressed her palms harder into the earth. "And I'm going home right now, as it happens. This is it. This is the home part."

She gave the memory of her mother.

It rose up in her like a tide — the only memory she had ever recovered rather than stolen, the one torn from a broker's vault in Vellum, the one that had rewritten her entire life in a single afternoon. Her mother in the Court gardens, sleeves rolled, dirt to the elbows, laughing at something her father said. Her mother pressing a kiss to a small forehead and whispering, *stay hidden, stay small, stay safe, my Elodie.* Her mother turning to face the coming Frostbound so her daughter could run.

She gave it the way you give a heart: all at once, or not at all.

The ground *detonated* with green.

Not gently anymore. Threads became vines became rivers of growing things, bursting up through the glass and the snow, and the great dome above them rang like a cathedral of bells, and every shard of frozen garden caught the green light and threw it back tenfold until the whole ruin burned emerald in the heart of the blizzard. Snow fell onto leaves instead of stone, and the leaves did not care, and the snow, meeting living green, simply *stopped being winter* — melting midair, becoming rain, becoming mist, becoming the smell of soil after the first warm storm of spring.

Corvane's host faltered.

It was not a battle. Nothing so dramatic. They simply — stopped. The tall pale shapes halted at the garden's edge as if they had walked into a wall of something worse than armies, and Elodie, watching through the storm, saw what it was that stopped them:

Living things.

A vine had reached the nearest Frostbound soldier and wrapped, gently, around his boot. Where it touched, the rime armor softened. Underneath — Elodie squinted through the green-gold light — underneath there was a face. Old. Tired. Human, once, maybe still. The soldier looked down at the vine the way a man looks at a flower growing through a grave, and he took one step backward, and then another, and the whole line wavered like a candle flame.

"They remember," Wren breathed beside her. The girl had dragged herself upright against the throne, the seed in her chest blazing now like a second sun. "They were gardeners once. Before. Some of them. They *remember*—"

"Then let them remember," Elodie said, and gave the seed everything else.

* * *

She emptied herself the way you empty a house before a long journey: room by room, shelf by shelf, deciding nothing is worth carrying if it means leaving anything behind.

The kitchens went. The thefts went — every mark, every traveler, every soldier and merchant and pilgrim whose life she had lifted and sold, she gave them all back to the earth, and the earth took them and grew roses. The loneliness went, the transactional gray years above the frost line, the hovel with its hundreds of bottled lives and none of her own. The fear went. The hunger went. Even the names went — *Maren Hale, Tam, Corvane, Hollowmere* — dissolving like sugar in warm water.

Last of all, when she was nearly transparent, when she was more absence than person, more spring than girl, she held the final memory up to the light and looked at it a long moment.

*Why I came.*

The dying child at her door. The first snowfall. The knock before the snow. The whole reason, the beginning, the debt that started everything.

If she kept that one, she would know who she had been. If she gave it, she would never know why any of this happened — only that it had, only that she was here, only that the world was turning green.

Somewhere far away, Corvane was shouting orders that no one followed. Somewhere closer, Wren was crying — actually crying, the silent child who had never once made a sound, weeping openly into the rain-warm air.

"Oh, stop that," Elodie said fondly. "I haven't gone anywhere. I've just... been filed elsewhere."

She gave the last memory away.

The garden ignited.

Green fire without heat roared up through the glass throne, through the dome, through the ruins of the Verdant Court, and the blizzard broke apart around it like a lie exposed, and the sky — the actual sky, pewter and iron for as long as anyone could remember — split open down the middle and showed blue.

Spring did not arrive. Spring *erupted*. Blossoms burst from branches that had been glass an hour ago. Grass swallowed the courtyard in waves. Birds — birds! — wheeled overhead, shrieking songs that had not been sung since before the long winter, and every Frostbound soldier at the gates dropped to their knees, not in defeat but in something older than defeat, something like homesickness, something like grief finally allowed to end.

In the center of it all, the seed rose out of Wren's chest like a leaf of pure light, drifted down to the newly-living earth at the foot of the throne, and planted itself.

A tree began to grow. Fast, impossibly fast, silver-barked and blossom-heavy, and Wren stood beneath it with her face turned up and tears streaming, and she laughed — laughed aloud, her first sound, high and startled and joyful, as if she'd been saving it her whole life for exactly this moment.

Elodie tried to laugh too.

She found she couldn't quite remember how.

She found, in fact, that she couldn't quite remember much of anything at all. The world was very beautiful and very new and she was standing in it like a woman at the edge of a lake she has no memory of swimming across. She looked down at her hands — they seemed nice hands, capable, dirt-stained — and she felt a strange, floating peace, the peace of a page before the first word.

The girl beneath the tree turned and looked at her. The girl's face changed — joy collapsing into something stricken, something desperate — and the girl ran to her, catching her before her knees decided the ground was a good idea, and the girl was saying something, saying it over and over, a word, a name—

"*Elodie.* Elodie, look at me. *Look at me.*"

The name landed somewhere deep, deeper than memory, in a place that hoarded nothing and forgot nothing either.

Elodie blinked up at the weeping stranger under the blossoming tree, at the green world ringing with birdsong, and smiled the smile of someone meeting a dear friend for the very first time.

"I'm sorry," she said gently. "Have we met?"
