## 20. Spring, Unremembered

Wren dreams in green.

Not the green of the seed-dreams, which were always urgent, always a door held open against a wind — but the slow green of things that have nowhere else to be. Moss-green. Lichen-patience green. The green of a leaf deciding, without hurry, to uncurl.

In the dream she is very small and standing in her mother's hands, except her mother's hands are made of light, except they are not her mother's hands at all but branches, except it does not matter, because in the dream being held is being held, whatever holds you.

Then the dream lets go, gently, the way you set down something you mean to pick up again.

And Wren wakes.

* * *

The first thing is the smell.

She has never smelled spring before — not truly, not with her whole nose. In the valley there was only cold-smell and smoke-smell and the stone-smell of Elodie's hovel, and once, unforgettable, the green-smell of a flower blooming through ice at the border stones, which had been like hearing your own name called from very far away.

This is that name, sung by a whole choir.

Soil and sap and something sweet she has no word for, and underneath it all the wet dark breath of earth waking up. Wren lies still and breathes it in the way other children breathe after crying: greedily, gratefully, as if the air itself might leave if she isn't careful.

The second thing is warmth. Real warmth, sun-warmth, lying on her skin like a hand that means no harm.

She opens her eyes.

Sky. A sky with *blue* in it, blue like a memory someone gave her once — one of Elodie's borrowed things, a soldier's summer, a lake so bright it hurt — except this sky is hers, this sky is here, this sky is not borrowed at all. Clouds move across it slowly, fat and white and unhurried, as though nothing in the world has ever chased them.

Wren sits up.

She is lying on a bed of moss — actual moss, deep and springy, the kind of bed a story would invent for a princess and then apologize for not being grand enough. Around her, the ruined court rises in its arches and broken colonnades, but the ruin is *ruining differently now*. Vines pour through the shattered windows like green water finding its level. Flowers crowd the cracks in the glass floor — the glass floor that was a garden turned to garden again — pushing up between the panes in reckless profusion, purple and white and a yellow so bold it seems to be showing off. Where the throne stood, or where Wren guesses the throne stood, because she was never here while it was a throne and only knows it from the dying king's memory, from the words Elodie lent her in the dark of the road — where the throne stood, there is now a tree.

It is not a large tree. It is perhaps taller than Tam's inn was, though Tam's inn is ash now and the tree is *here*, and height feels like an unfair way to measure anything anymore. Its trunk is pale silver-barked, and its leaves catch the light and let it go again, and beneath it the snow lies in a ring of meltwater, and beyond the ring, further than Wren can see, the white country is retreating. She can almost hear it go. A long sigh of ice giving up, water moving under the frost, the whole frozen world exhaling after two hundred years of holding its breath.

The seed. She remembers the seed. She remembers it dimming inside her like a coal going out, remembers the cold climbing her arms, remembers —

Remembers Elodie feeding it everything.

Wren's chest goes tight and small. She scrambles up, moss slipping under her bare feet — when did she lose her shoes? — and turns in a circle, searching the blooming ruin, calling the only name that matters in every language she has, which is to say she opens her mouth and makes no sound at all, because the name is not a word, the name is a face, the name is chapped hands and a crooked smile and the taste of stolen bread shared in a hedgerow, and none of those things can be shouted.

*Elodie.*

* * *

She finds her by the tree.

The woman is sitting at its base with her legs stretched out and her head tipped back against the silver bark, asleep in the way of the utterly exhausted, which is less like sleep and more like falling. Her clothes are stiff with old frost and torn at both sleeves. There is dirt on her hands — real dirt, black and rich, ground into the creases of her knuckles — and her hair has come loose from its braid entirely, spread around her shoulders like something poured.

Wren stops three steps away and looks at her, and looks, and looks.

The face is the same. Of course it is the same. Faces do not empty out; Wren learned that on the road, learned it in the worst hour of the white country when Elodie had given away so much of herself that she'd looked at Wren with polite blankness and asked, gently, *who are you traveling with, little one?* — and Wren had taken the woman's own hands and pressed them to her own cheeks and waited, waited, until something behind the blankness stirred like a fish under ice and Elodie said, uncertainly, *Wren? That feels right. Why does that feel right?*

But the face is the same and the person is different, and Wren stands there in the flowering ruin learning the difference.

The woman stirs. Frowns in her sleep. Mumbles something — Wren catches *not the blue ones, take the blue ones* — and then her eyes open, gray and clear and unclouded, and they find Wren, and they do not know her.

Wren watches the not-knowing happen. It is a strange thing to watch. There is no cruelty in it, no rejection. The woman simply looks at her the way you look at a bird that has landed near you: with interest, with a kind of startled pleasure, with no history whatsoever.

"Oh," the woman says. Her voice is rough with sleep. "You're real. I half thought I dreamed you." She rubs her eyes. "Did I dream you?"

Wren shakes her head slowly.

"Good," the woman says, with great seriousness, as though this settles an important matter. "I'd hate to have wasted a dream that good."

And then — and this is the part Wren will carry for the rest of her life, the part she will tell the valley children years from now when they ask what it was like, the part that makes her stop mid-sentence every time because there is no word for it even in the old tongue, especially not in the old tongue, which has a hundred words for grief and only this one gesture for what happened next — the woman scoots over on the moss and pats the space beside her.

An invitation. For a stranger. In a ruin at the edge of the world.

Wren sits down.

* * *

They learn each other the way weather learns a landscape: gradually, and by touch.

The woman — who says her name might be Elodie, *it sounds right when I say it, like a bell rung in an empty room, but I don't know why it's mine, only that it is* — cannot speak much of the old tongue. But she hums, and her humming is the court's humming, the green-melody that used to live in the seed, and when Wren hears it she has to press both hands over her mouth because the melody is *home*, home before home fell, home when the gardens sang themselves awake each morning, and the woman is humming it without knowing what she hums, the way you breathe without knowing you breathe.

*Where did you get that song?* Wren asks her, in the old tongue, and then remembers, and takes the woman's hand instead, and gives her the memory the way Elodie taught her to give: gently, palm to palm, a small warm thing passed over.

The woman goes still. Her eyes widen. When the memory settles into her — the memory of the court singing, which Wren kept folded small all this time precisely for a moment like this — the woman laughs, one short surprised note.

"I know that place," she says. "I've *been* there. Or — no." She frowns, pressing her fingers to her temple. "I've been somewhere like it. There was a kitchen. Someone's kitchen. Bread on the table and my feet cold on the flagstones and someone laughing —" She stops. Looks at Wren with sudden fierce attention. "Was that yours? Did you give me that?"

Wren nods.

"It's warm," the woman says, wonderingly. She touches her own sternum, where the memory sits. "Things aren't usually warm. I keep noticing that. Everything's warm now. Is that normal?" She glances around at the blooming ruin, the melting snow, the impossible tree. "None of this is normal, is it. I feel like I should remember why any of this is happening, and instead I mostly remember how to prune roses."

She says it lightly, but Wren sees the flicker underneath — the vertigo of a person standing in their own life without a floor. And Wren understands, with a child's terrible clarity, exactly what was paid for all this green. Every flower opening in the glass floor is a morning in a soldier's mother's kitchen. Every vine on the broken windows is a first kiss, a father's voice, a name spoken over a cradle. The woman emptied herself out like a cup poured to the last drop, and the garden drank, and the garden lived, and the woman woke up in paradise with nothing in her hands.

Wren takes her hand again. Gives her another memory: the hedgerow, the stolen bread, the two of them laughing with flour on their faces while hunters passed close enough to touch.

The woman receives it and her whole face changes — softens, sharpens, becomes *someone*. "Oh," she breathes. "Oh, that's — we ran together. You and me. Didn't we." Not a question. She grips Wren's hand harder. "I don't remember it, but I *know* it. It's like knowing a room with the lights off. I know the shape of everything and none of the furniture." Her gray eyes fill, and she laughs at herself, embarrassed, wiping her face with her dirty wrist. "Look at me. Crying over bread I can't remember eating. You must think I'm mad."

Wren shakes her head. Leans, carefully, the way you lean toward a fire you're afraid of losing, and rests her head on the woman's shoulder.

The woman freezes. Then her arm comes up around Wren, hesitant, then certain, pulling her in.

"Well," the woman says into her hair, thick-voiced. "I don't know who you are. But I'm not letting go of you. Whoever I was, she'd agree with me about that. I'd bet the garden on it."

* * *

They build a day the way birds build nests: from whatever is at hand.

The woman turns out to know things her missing self would be proud of. She cannot say how she knows them — *they're just in my hands,* she says, flexing her fingers, puzzled and pleased — but she knows how to test soil between her fingers, how to read which buds will open first, how to talk to the tree in a low murmuring voice that makes its new leaves tremble as if laughing. By afternoon she has cleared a circle around the throne-tree and planted, from seeds found in the pockets of a rotted gardener's coat hanging in a ruined gatehouse, three neat rows of something that may be beans and may be miracles.

"You'll want to eat," she tells Wren, bossy and gentle at once. "Growing people need feeding. I decided you're growing people. I've decided a lot of things today; it's easier than remembering them."

Wren eats. There are fish in the melt-streams, stunned and slow from the long winter, and the woman catches them with a patience that looks practiced, and roasts them over a fire she builds on the second try, and salts them from a pouch that was in the coat too, along with the seeds, along with a map, folded and refolded a thousand times, soft as cloth.

The map. Wren sees it and her heart lurches — Tam's map, the dead man's gift, the roads inked in a deserter's careful hand — and she reaches for it, and the woman hands it over without question.

"Do you know whose this is?" Wren asks, in gestures, in the old tongue, in the press of memory she offers: Tam's kitchen, Tam's laugh, Tam standing in the hedgerow turning to face the hunters so that two strangers could run.

The woman takes the memory. Sits with it a long moment, firelight moving on her face.

"Yes," she says quietly. "No. Both." She touches the map's worn folds. "Someone gave us this. Someone stayed behind so we wouldn't have to." She looks up, and her eyes are clear and grieving at once, grief with no floor under it, mourning a man she cannot recall. "I don't remember him. But I owe him. Isn't that strange? Being in debt to your own forgotten life." She folds the map closed and presses it back into Wren's hands. "Keep it. Somebody trusted you with things. I can tell, because you hold everything like it's breakable and precious at the same time. That's not how thieves hold things."

Wren's throat closes.

Because that is the truth of it, the truth she has been carrying since the white country: the woman who stole hundreds of lives is gone, spent, poured into the roots of a tree. What woke in her place is someone new — someone who plants beans and catches fish and pats the moss beside her for a stranger — and yet not new at all, because the kindness was always there, buried under the hoard, the way the court was buried under the ice. Spring doesn't make the garden. Spring just stops holding it down.

That night they sleep beneath the tree, wrapped in the gardener's coat, and the woman tucks the coat around Wren first, fussing, muttering about drafts and thin shirts, and Wren lies listening to her heartbeat and thinks: *this is what the seed wanted.* Not thrones. Not courts. This. Someone staying.

* * *

On the third morning, travelers come.

Wren hears them first — footsteps on the melt-softened road, many feet, cautious — and tugs the woman's sleeve, and they stand together in the flowering gateway as the first people from outside the white country come up the ruined avenue: ragged, frostbitten, blinking in the green light like creatures emerging from a cave. Valley folk. Wren recognizes the coats, the wary walk, the look of people who have spent generations believing the world ends at the border stones.

The folk stop when they see the garden. One old man falls to his knees in the mud, and no one laughs at him.

And at the back of the group, pushing forward, comes a figure Wren knows — a girl Wren's age, stocky and scowling and alive, wearing a broker's coat turned inside out so the fine lining shows, and carrying, clutched to her chest like contraband, a small wooden box.

The girl stops dead when she sees Wren. Her scowl collapses into something younger.

"You're alive," she says. "You absolute little — you're *alive*." She shoves through the crowd, thrusts the box at Wren. Inside, nested in wool, sit a dozen seeds: brown, ordinary, patient. "From the vaults. From *her* vaults. Hale kept seeds, did you know that? Kept them locked up for the Frostbound like they were jewels. Well." The girl's chin lifts. "Vellum decided otherwise. Word came down the roads faster than winter could follow it — the ice is breaking everywhere, do you understand? Everywhere. People are walking out of the valley for the first time in two hundred years and they're following the green, and the green leads *here*." She looks past Wren, at the woman crouched by the bean rows, and her voice drops. "Is that her? The thief? They're saying she burned herself down to light this place. They're saying she won't ever remember."

Wren looks back at the woman — at Elodie, who might be Elodie, who is humming her green song to the beans and waving cheerfully at the arriving strangers as though hosting a festival she planned herself.

"No," Wren answers, aloud, in the old tongue, and then translates it into the only language that matters, taking the box of seeds and holding it against her heart:

*She won't remember. So we will remember for her. All of us. Starting now.*

And somewhere behind them, faint as a bell rung in an empty room, the woman begins to hum.
