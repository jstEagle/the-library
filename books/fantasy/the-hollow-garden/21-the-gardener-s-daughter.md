## 21. The Gardener's Daughter

I do not remember my name.

That is the first thing I know each morning, and it is not so terrible as it sounds. I wake in a room with green light on the walls and a blanket that smells of thyme, and I lie there for a moment in the pleasant fog of being no one at all, before the scraps arrive.

They arrive the way birds come to a feeder. One at a time. Unbidden, but faithful.

*A woman's hands kneading dough* — that was the baker's wife from Vellum, the one whose memory I took for three coppers and a lie about my dying sister. It comes back to me now as warmth in my knuckles, the particular ache of good work. *A boy running through a barley field with his arms out like wings* — that was Tam's, given back to me by his niece, who pressed it into my palm outside Ashgrove Inn and said, "He'd have wanted you to have it. He talked about you two for weeks." *The smell of ink and a father laughing at his own terrible joke* — Maren Hale's, of all people, left in my keeping along with her confession, though whether she meant it as an apology or an accusation I still cannot say.

And one scrap that belongs to no one else. A garden. Glass where flowers should be. A small girl hiding under a workbench while her mother hummed a song in a dead tongue.

That one came back on its own. The seed remembered me, even when I could not remember myself.

* * *

Wren found me on the third morning after the planting, when I was sitting in the courtyard of the risen court trying to decide whether I was a person or merely a place where memories pooled.

She did not speak. She had never spoken, not once in all our flight across the white country — but she sat down beside me on the warm stone, took my hand, turned it over, and studied the palm as though reading something there. Then she looked up at me with those grave dark eyes and said, in the old tongue, a word I somehow knew meant *gardener.*

"I don't know if I am," I said.

She frowned at me with tremendous disapproval, the way only children and cats can frown, and said another word. This one I knew too, because it lived in the same scrap as the humming: *daughter.*

"That's not the same thing," I told her.

She shrugged, magnificently unconvinced, and went off to investigate a fountain that had spent forty years as ice and was now, rather embarrassingly, gushing water all over the flagstones. And that was that. Wren decided things the way weather decides things, and arguing with her was like arguing with rain.

So I stayed. Not because I chose it, exactly — at first. Because someone had to pull the nightshade vines off the eastern trellis, and because the glass garden at the court's heart was sprouting things through its cracked panes that needed naming, and because every time I thought about walking out the gates, some returned scrap would rise up in me — the barley field, the kitchen, the mother's song — and remind me that a person is not a thing you find whole. It is a thing you grow.

I have since made my peace with choosing it on purpose.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about having your memory emptied into a seed: the world does not stop needing you to be somebody. The cooks in the rebuilt kitchens want to know if you take your tea sweet. The masons ask your opinion on where the new wall should run. Wren wants to know, urgently and daily, whether you think the fountain fish are lonely.

At first I answered everything with "I don't remember," which became tedious even to me. So I began to answer differently. I began to answer as the person the scraps suggested.

The baker's wife's hands knew bread, so I baked, badly at first and then adequately, and then — I am told — well. Tam's barley field gave me patience with slow things, which is most of gardening. From a soldier whose mother's kitchen I stole a lifetime ago in Hollowmere, I kept the habit of setting two cups out even when expecting no one, because loneliness, he had taught me without ever meeting me, is a room with only one cup in it.

Some days a scrap arrives that doesn't fit. A memory of drowning, from a ferryman I robbed on the mountain road. A wedding, from a bride whose face I wore for a week in Vellum to pass through a checkpoint. These are harder. They are not mine, and they never will be, and holding them feels like wearing a coat cut for someone else's shoulders. But I have learned to hold them gently anyway, the way you hold a bird — firmly enough that it isn't lost, loosely enough that it can leave.

Because that is the other thing nobody tells you: the scraps leave. When their true owners come looking — and more of them come every season now that the roads are open and the brokers' vaults stand empty — I give them back. Each one goes out of me like a breath, and where it was, something new takes root. A little space, tended and waiting.

I used to think a self was a hoard. Now I think it is a garden. You do not own what grows in it. You just keep it alive, and it keeps you.

* * *

In the autumn of the first year, a traveler came up the mountain road with frost still on his boots, though the valley below him had been green since spring. He stood at the gate a long time, hat in hand, looking at the walls that had been ruin and were now stone again, warm stone, faintly gold in the afternoon light.

"You're her," he said finally. "The one who planted it."

"I'm told I am," I said. "I don't remember doing it."

He blinked. Then he laughed — a startled, honest laugh — and said, "That may be the first honest thing anyone's said to me in this country." He introduced himself as a seed-merchant from the southern valleys, and he had walked a long way because his grandmother used to sing songs about this place, and he wanted to see if it was real.

It was real. He stayed three days, helped me dig a new bed for the winter squash, and left with a satchel of seeds and a promise to send word of how they fared in warmer soil.

After he went, I stood in the gate a long while myself, looking down the road. Somewhere past the border stones lay Hollowmere, my valley, the place that magic forgot and that I had fled with a child in my arms and nothing in my head but borrowed lives. I wondered if the frost line had moved. I wondered if the brokers' hall had been converted into something useful — a school, perhaps, or a very ironic bakery.

I wondered if I would go back and look.

I decided I didn't need to. Not yet. Maybe not ever. The girl who was born there had done her running; the woman who remained had other work.

* * *

Winter came, as winters do, but it was an ordinary winter — snow that melted, cold that ended — and the court wore it lightly, like an old scar that aches in damp weather and is otherwise forgotten. We kept the glass garden heated with lamps and stubbornness. Wren learned to read, in three languages, none of which she would speak aloud except the dead one, which she taught me patiently, word by word, correcting my accent with the merciless precision of an eight-year-old.

My mother's name, I learned, had been Isolde. Wren knew it. Wren had known it all along, carried it across the mountains in her silence like a coal cupped in bare hands, waiting for the day I could hear it without breaking.

"Isolde," I practiced, in the greenhouse, among the tomatoes. It tasted like a name. It tasted like mine.

* * *

There is one visitor I have not yet mentioned, because I did not know how to mention her until now.

She came in the second spring. A tall woman, gray at the temples, with a gardener's forearms and a stranger's face. She stood at the gate and wept without making any sound, and when I asked her name she said, "Brannoc. I was your mother's friend. I was the Court's last herald." She looked at me the way you look at a house you grew up in, long changed. "You have her hands," she said. "I'd know them anywhere. She had the only hands in the Court that could wake a cutting that everyone else had given up on."

We sat in the courtyard until dark, and she told me pieces — not memories, I couldn't take those anymore, wouldn't take them, but stories, which are memories worn smooth by telling. How Isolde had smuggled me over the border stones in a basket of roots. How she had gone back to hold the gate so the Frostbound wouldn't follow. How the last thing she said was not a farewell but an instruction: *let her grow toward whatever light she finds.*

"Whatever light she finds," Brannoc repeated, looking around at the courtyard, the fountain, the child asleep against my shoulder with dirt on her knees. "Well. She'd have been insufferably pleased with herself."

I laughed until I cried, which I am told is the correct order of things.

Before she left, Brannoc pressed something into my palm: a cutting, wrapped in damp moss. From the mother tree at the heart of the old court, grown impossibly tall now, its branches full of white blossom out of season.

"For wherever you plant yourself," she said. "Though I suspect you've already chosen."

I had. Of course I had. I planted it by the gate, where travelers would see it first, and where I could prune it on mornings when my own scraps ran thin and I needed reminding that things grow back.

* * *

Which brings me to tonight, and the reason I am writing this at all — scratching these words by lamplight in the gardener's journal, in a hand that is slowly becoming my own instead of an echo of everyone else's.

Because yesterday, the seed-merchant's letter arrived, and it was not alone.

His squashes thrived, he wrote. His neighbors asked for seeds. His neighbors' neighbors asked. And then a letter came to him from further south, from a village near the old Frostbound marches, where the thaw has not reached and the wells freeze in July, and the people there have heard rumors of a court that grows and a gardener who gives things away, and they are asking — timidly, hopefully, the way you ask about miracles — whether the rumors are true, and whether she might spare some seeds for a place the winter never left.

I read the letter twice. Then I read it to Wren, who listened with her chin on her fists and her eyes going brighter and brighter, and when I finished she jumped up, ran to the seed chest, and began sorting packets with the terrifying efficiency of a general mobilizing troops.

"We'll need the hardy ones," she signed, in the gesture-language we invented between us during the flight, the one that needs no voice. "And someone will have to show them how. Seeds die without a keeper."

"Yes," I said. "Someone will."

She looked at me. I looked at her. Outside, the mother tree dropped a petal onto the snow, white on white, patient as a promise.

The winter is broken, but it is not defeated. It waits at the edges of the maps, in the places the thaw hasn't touched, and it will wait as long as it must. And somewhere out there is a village with frozen wells and a rumor of hope, and a road I have never walked, leading away from the only home I can remember — which is, I suppose, the perfect condition for finding out who I am next.

I put down the pen. I picked up the seed chest.

"Wren," I said, "pack for cold weather."
