## 22. Seeds for the Next Winter

The schoolhouse had been a granary once, and before that a chapel to a god whose name even Tam had never learned. Its walls were stone gone soft with moss, its roof mended with green copper that Wren swore sang on rainy days, though Elodie suspected the girl was romanticizing. The bell rope still hung by the door. Nobody rang it anymore; there was no need. The children of Hollowmere came when they came, trailing snowmelt and excuses, and if they were late it was usually because they had stopped to argue with a fox or watch something bloom.

Elodie stood at the front of the room with a basket on her hip and twenty-six faces looking up at her, and she thought — not for the first time — how strange it was that she had ever been afraid of children.

"Again," said a small boy named Bram, who had a cowlick like a thistle and the stubbornness of a mountain goat.

"You've heard it four times."

"Fifth times the one that sticks. My gran says so."

"Your gran also says you can cure warts with a stolen sock, so perhaps we shouldn't lean too hard on her scholarship." But Elodie smiled as she said it, and the children laughed, because they knew her now — knew that the sharpness was only the shell around the warm thing, and that the warm thing was always offered in the end.

She held up her hand and spoke the old tongue, slowly, shaping each word the way Wren had taught her to shape them: not spoken so much as *planted*, each syllable set into the air like a seed pressed into soil.

"*Veth-ala. Suren-veth.* Grow well. Grow toward the light."

The words did what old words do in a room full of young mouths: they took root. By midwinter half the valley's children could count to ten in a language that had been dead for a hundred years, and by spring they were arguing in it, which any teacher will tell you is the true sign of fluency.

Wren sat at the back by the window, as she always did, mending a net or whittling or simply watching. She was seventeen now, tall and quiet, with her mother's dark eyes and a scar on her palm from the winter she'd nearly died crossing the White Country. She didn't speak much in any tongue. She had never needed to. When she did speak the old language, the words came out whole and living, like birds released from a cage, and the younger children would go silent just to listen.

"Tell them the story," Bram demanded, as he demanded every day. "The true one."

"The true one is long," Elodie said.

"We're not going anywhere."

It was true. None of them were going anywhere, and for the first time in the history of Hollowmere, that was a choice rather than a sentence.

* * *

The valley had changed more slowly than the world outside it, but it had changed. The border stones no longer hummed with that dead-air silence; magic crossed them now the way weather crossed them, sometimes welcome, sometimes not, mostly indifferent. The brokers' trade had withered almost to nothing — who would buy a memory when you could grow your own? Maren Hale had left the valley eleven years ago with two sledge-loads of stock and no buyers, and rumor said she'd opened a shop in Vellum selling ordinary things: needles, salt, secondhand boots. Rumor also said she was terrible at it. Elodie tried not to enjoy that rumor too much, and failed.

Some things had not changed. The frost line still crept a little further down the valley each year, patient as arithmetic. The winters were shorter than they had been in the old days — weeks instead of seasons — but they came, and every few years they came hard, and the oldest people in Hollowmere would stand at their doors watching the snow and remember, without wanting to, what a winter that never ended had felt like.

That was why the seeds mattered.

Every autumn, Wren went up to the ruined court — the garden of glass and green fire where a woman had emptied herself out so that spring could be born — and gathered what grew there. Seeds from plants that existed nowhere else in the world: frost-blooms that opened in the coldest dark, hearth-moss that kept a wall warm, the pale gold wheat called *mother's-patience* that ripened in sixty days of thin sun. And every autumn, Elodie wrote letters — real letters, in her own careful hand — and the seeds went out with them, wrapped in waxed cloth, carried over the mountains by traders and pilgrims and once, memorably, by a very determined goose.

To the north country, where the Frostbound's descendants farmed land that had been frozen for generations. To Vellum, where a memory-broker's vault had been converted into a public library, free to anyone, because the woman who ran it remembered being someone whose memories had not been free. To the scattered villages of the Verdant diaspora, who had forgotten they were a diaspora until a package arrived containing seeds and a letter in the old tongue, and an old woman had read it aloud and wept.

"You could keep them," Tam's granddaughter said once, helping pack the parcels. She was named Sorrel and had Tam's broad hands and his habit of asking uncomfortable questions gently. "The seeds. Grown here, we'd never have another bad year. Never."

"And in fifty years," Elodie said, tying off a knot, "one blight, one bad summer, one fire — and everything that survived the long winter dies in a single season. No." She tucked a packet of frost-bloom between two layers of cloth. "A thing kept dies. A thing given away keeps itself."

Sorrel had considered this with the serious face of the very young, then said, "That's what my grandda always said about stories," and Elodie had felt, briefly and warmly, that somewhere beyond whatever lay beyond, Tam was laughing at her.

* * *

On the last day of the teaching season, when the light through the granary windows had gone honey-colored and the children were restless as bees, Wren rose from her window seat and did something she almost never did.

She taught.

Not with words. She never used many of those. She walked to the front of the room, and she held out her hands, palms up, and she looked at the children until they understood that she wanted theirs, and then — one by one, shyly, giggling — they gave them.

And she showed them.

It was not the great magic. It was not the planting of courts or the breaking of winters. It was the small kind, the kind that had come back into the world along with everything else: a snowdrop opening in a child's cupped hands, white petals unfolding from nothing, from warmth alone. Each child got one. Each child got to feel it happen — that tickle of life answering attention, the way a cat answers its name — and the room went so quiet you could hear the copper roof singing in the wind, if Wren was right that it sang, and by now Elodie believed her.

"That's yours," Wren said, in the old tongue, and then again in the valley speech, because some things deserved saying twice. "It doesn't belong to me. It doesn't belong to anyone. That's what makes it grow."

Bram stared at his snowdrop with the expression of a boy whose entire worldview has just been rearranged. "Can I eat it?"

"No," said Elodie.

"Can I—"

"You may plant it. You may water it. You may talk to it, if you must, though I'd keep the conversation dignified." She crouched to his level. "And in the spring, when it blooms, you'll give its seeds away. All of them. Even the ones you want to keep. *Especially* those."

"Why especially those?"

Because, she thought, I spent thirty years keeping exactly those, and it nearly made me into Maren Hale. Because I once owned nine hundred lives and could not remember my own mother's face. Because the hoard is a hunger that eats the keeper first.

"Because," she said, "that's the whole lesson. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

They walked home together in the blue hour, teacher and student, though neither of them used those words anymore and hadn't for years. The path climbed past the frost line, past the hovel where Elodie had lived her hoarded, hollow decades — it was a drying shed now, full of hanging herbs, which everyone agreed was the most useful it had ever been — and on up to the cottage they'd built together on the shoulder of the hill, with its wide south windows and its garden terraces cut into the slope.

The garden was asleep under its first snow. It would wake. It always woke now. But Elodie paused at the gate anyway, as she did most evenings, and looked at the white shapes of the beds, and felt the old vigilance stir and settle. Some habits were winters of their own. You didn't defeat them. You just got better at dressing for the cold.

"Wren." She said it in the old tongue: *Reth-suren*. Little bird. The name had been Wren's own invention, all those years ago, back when she'd had no other way to say *I am here*.

Wren stopped. Waited. She had learned, over seventeen years, that Elodie's pauses meant either trouble or tenderness, and that it was best to let her get to whichever it was in her own time.

"When you found me," Elodie said. "At my door. In the snow. You couldn't speak. You had nothing but a seed and a dead language and a king's dying wish. Do you ever wonder what would have happened if I hadn't opened the door?"

Wren considered this with the gravity she brought to all questions, which was considerable. Then she signed as she answered, her hands moving in the fluid grammar she'd invented for herself long before Elodie learned it: *You opened it. That's the story. There isn't another one.*

"There's always another one. I've stolen enough lives to know. There's a version where I sold you to Maren Hale and bought a house in Vellum and died rich and forgotten. There's a version where Corvane—" She stopped. Some names still carried cold. "There are a hundred versions, and in ninety-nine of them, everything I love is ash or ice. And the only difference between that version and this one is that I opened a door."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked softly down through the branches of the rowan tree by the gate.

Then she signed, slow and deliberate, making sure each word landed:

*Doors open both ways.*

Elodie laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and pulled the girl into a hug that Wren endured with the tolerant stiffness of a cat being appreciated against its will, and returned anyway, as she always did, one arm settling around Elodie's shoulders like a branch coming to rest.

Inside, the kettle went on. Inside, the fire caught. On the mantel, in a place of honor, sat a small clay pot of valley earth, and in it, the last seed of that year's gathering — not sent outward, not planted in the court, but kept, just one, because even stewardship was allowed one indulgence, and because next spring a child would need something to practice on.

Outside, the snow fell on the sleeping garden, on the terraces, on the valley below with its lit windows and its learning children, on the border stones that hummed faintly, all night, like a held breath finally let go.

The long winter was broken. It was not defeated. Somewhere past the mountains it waited, patient as arithmetic, and someday — in Wren's lifetime, or her children's, or their children's — it would test them again.

But the seeds were out in the world now. Hundreds of gardens, hundreds of hands, hundreds of doors that might open.

And in a schoolhouse that had been a granary that had been a chapel, chalk marks on a slate board spelled out tomorrow's first lesson in a language older than the frost:

*Suren-veth. Veth-ala.*

Grow toward the light. Grow well.

*The End.*
