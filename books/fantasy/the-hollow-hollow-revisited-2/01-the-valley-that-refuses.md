## 1. The Valley That Refuses

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a country that does not exist, and I have spent thirty years learning to hear it.

It sounds like lamplight. It sounds like the scratch of a nib on good vellum, and the small wet pause when you dip for ink. Tonight it sounded like all of that, plus the criers below my window, which is to say: tonight the silence had competition.

"By order of the Crown," the man bawled, his voice bouncing off the shutters of Hollow Street the way a stone skips off still water, "all persons harboring or concealing the gift of sorcery shall present themselves for assessment. Names are known. Mercy is offered to those who come forward. To those who do not—"

He let the sentence hang there, unfinished, which was the cruelest part. A finished threat has edges you can grip. An unfinished one just gets into everything, like damp.

I did not look up from my map.

I should explain what I was drawing, because even I find it difficult. It was a map of a country I have never seen. This is not the boast it sounds like; cartographers who only copy what exists are clerks with better pens. My work—the work I have done quietly, in this valley where magic cannot follow and therefore cannot find anyone—has always been elsewhere. I map realms that exist only in memory. Someone remembers a place so hard, so long, that it leaves a residue, a shape in the world's fabric, and I draw that shape. Coastlines of things that never happened. Rivers through countries that were never born. I am, if you like, an historian of places that refused to occur.

The country on my desk tonight had no name yet. They usually tell me their names eventually, the way a cat tells you its name by ignoring every wrong one. This one was being stubborn. It had mountains drawn in a hand heavier than mine, as though the memory behind them had been held by someone angry, or grieving, or both. There was a valley at its center—I notice I always give them valleys—and the valley had a door marked on it in no ink I could account for. The line simply appeared on the vellum some nights, faint as breath on glass, and I had learned not to touch it until I understood whose breath it was.

Below my window, the crier moved on toward the square, and the unfinished sentence moved with him.

Here is what I believed, for thirty years, about the Hollow Hollow: that its name was a joke and then a prayer and then simply a fact. Magic cannot follow you here. Whatever the gift is—whether it lives in blood or bone or that unlit room in the mind nobody can furnish—it stops at the valley's rim like a dog at an invisible fence. The gifted came here for generations and became millers and midwives and mendicant priests, and their gifts went quiet as banked coals, and the world forgot them, and forgetting, we told ourselves, was safety.

Invisibility. I used the word often. I used it the way comfortable people use words, without tasting it.

I heard boots on the stairs at half past eleven.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only penance I know. Half past eleven. I remember because my candle had guttered twice and I had trimmed the wick and said, aloud, to no one, "Half past eleven, and still she sings." Because Marta Ovelin, my neighbor across the landing, sang while she worked. She mended lace, which requires either extraordinary eyes or extraordinary patience, and she had neither, and she sang anyway—old songs, valley songs, the kind with a hole in the middle of the verse where a name used to go. Everyone in the building knew her voice the way you know weather.

The boots stopped at her door.

I put down my pen. I will not pretend I did not consider picking it back up. There is a version of me—a better draftsman and a worse man—who kept working, who reasoned that a map in progress cannot be interrupted, that the door-line might fade, that there was nothing to be done anyway. That version of me existed for perhaps four seconds. Then I crossed the landing.

Marta's door was already open. Two men stood inside her room in the posture men adopt when they are being gentle on purpose, which is the least gentle posture there is. One held the list. I saw it only a moment—a folded paper, official cream, a column of names in a clerk's patient hand—and I saw, because the light was bad and the paper turned, that the names were not many. Seven? Eight? A short list. A list made by someone who knew exactly whom they wanted.

"Marta Ovelin," the one with the list read aloud, though she was standing right there, as if the saying of it made it true and her mere presence were a clerical error.

"You have the wrong woman," Marta said, and her voice was steady, and I thought: she has rehearsed this. We have all rehearsed this. Thirty years of rehearsal and not one performance.

"The list says otherwise."

"The list," said Marta, "is a piece of paper," and something flickered at the edge of her—something I had never seen in forty years of knowing her, something like heat seen through a shutter—and the lamp in her room bent its flame sideways, and the man with the list smiled the way a fisherman smiles when the line goes taut.

"There she is," he said softly.

They took her shawl off its hook for her. That detail will outlive me. They were courteous about it, almost tender, holding the shawl open like valets, and Marta put her arms through the sleeves because the alternative was worse, and she looked at me—standing in her doorway in my nightshirt, ink on my fingers, the great invisible man of the Hollow Hollow—and she did not ask me for anything. That was the worst of it. She looked at me the way you look at weather. She had known, all along, exactly what I was worth in a crisis, and she had never once expected more.

"I'll finish your map," I said, and immediately wished the words dead. It was nonsense. She had never asked for a map. But her face did something then—not gratitude, nothing so kind—recognition, maybe. As if I'd finally said something true, which was that I would stay here, in my room, with my lamp, doing my small immortal work, while they walked her down the stairs.

"Mind the western passes," she said. "They're steeper than you think."

And she laughed—one note of it, dry as a snapped thread—and went down between the two men, singing. Not loudly. Just enough to fill the stairwell, the song with the hole in the middle where the name went, and I stood in her doorway until the sound stopped, and then I stood there longer, because stopping seemed like another thing I'd be complicit in.

* * *

Back in my room, the map waited with the patience of the nonexistent.

I sat down. I picked up the pen. My hand was steady, which offended me; hands ought to shake at times like these, and mine went on drafting coastlines as if the world were orderly. The unnamed country spread under the lamp, mountains and rivers and that valley at its heart, and the door-line at the valley's center, faint as ever, patient as ever.

And then I saw it.

The valley on my map—the invented one, the remembered one, the one belonging to some stranger's grief—was shaped wrong. Or rather, it was shaped right, and that was the problem, because I knew the shape. The curve of it, the way the river pinched at the north and splayed at the south, the notch in the western rim like a bite taken from a loaf—

That was home. That was the Hollow Hollow. The valley where magic cannot follow, the valley erased from every crown survey, every trade atlas, every pilgrim's guide—the valley that appears on no map but mine, and here it was appearing on a map of a country I had never seen, drawn in my own hand without my knowledge, and the door-line sat precisely where the chapel stands. Where the chapel has always stood.

I set the pen down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

Thirty years I believed the valley hid us. Invisibility as mercy, absence as armor. But maps do not lie about shape, and the shape on my desk said something else entirely: that the Hollow Hollow was not hiding. It was hidden. There is a difference, and the difference has a maker. Countries do not erase themselves from atlases by accident. Somebody had unwritten us—or we had unwritten ourselves—and the only thread left tying the valley to the world ran through my lamp-lit desk, through my pen, through a door I had been drawing for months without once understanding I was drawing it.

Below, in the square, the criers had gone quiet. Somewhere a wagon rolled toward the valley's rim, carrying Marta Ovelin and whatever the list intended for her, and the valley let it pass, the way it lets everything pass, refusing and refusing.

I looked at the door-line on the map, and the door-line, faint as breath on glass, looked back.

Then someone knocked at my door—three soft raps, unhurried, from a hand that clearly believed it had every right to be there—and a voice I did not know said, through the wood:

"Cartographer. We need to talk about the chapel."
