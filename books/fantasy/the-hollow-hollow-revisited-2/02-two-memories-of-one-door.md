## 2. Two Memories of One Door

I did not sleep after they took Marta. Sleep, I have found, is a country the crown cannot annex but can embargo, and that night no ships came in. Instead I sat at my desk with every map of the Hollow Hollow spread around me like a hand of losing cards, and I asked each of them the same question: *where is your door?*

Maps are honest the way drunks are honest. They tell you what they know and nothing more. Mine showed streets I had walked ten thousand times, the chapel with its crooked bell-tower, the millpond green as a bottle bottom — but no door. A door implies an outside. My maps were drawn by a man who had never once believed there was one.

Which was when someone knocked. Three knocks, spaced like a confession: here, here, here.

I opened the door to a woman holding a lantern in one hand and a crown warrant in the other, which struck me as a thorough way to be both salvation and threat in a single gesture.

"Ulyana Dunmore," she said. Not a question. Huntresses don't ask; they confirm.

"You've the advantage of me," I said, which was true, though her face itched at something in my memory, like a name heard through a wall.

"Sera Voss." She turned the warrant over, showing me its blank back. "Third Company. Formerly." The lantern threw her shadow long across my threshold, into my house, uninvited. "I'm told you draw places that don't exist."

"I draw places that exist very quietly."

"The Hollow Hollow," she said. "The valley under the valley. You've mapped it."

Here is what you must understand about cartographers: we lie fluently with our hands. "I've mapped a valley," I said. "It's mostly mud and opinion."

Her smile had no warmth in it, only edges. "Then you won't mind showing me where the door is."

* * *

Sera Voss remembers the millpond.

This I learned within the hour, because she would not stop saying it. We stood in my study while she pointed at my finest work — twenty years of ink, the valley rendered so faithfully you could smell the rain off it — and she put her finger down on the millpond like a woman pinning a moth.

"There," she said. "That's where it stands."

"Nothing stands there," I said. "That's where children drown their regrets and fishermen drown their lunches."

"The door. To outside. I walked through it when I was eleven." Her voice did something complicated on the last word, a hinge swinging both ways. "My mother put my hand on the iron ring and told me to count to nine and not look back. I counted to seven. I've spent thirty years regretting it."

"And yet," I said, tapping the chapel on my map, "the door is here. Under the chapel floor, beneath the third pew from the back, the one with the loose board everyone pretends isn't loose. I've known since I was a boy. Old Wren showed me."

"Old Wren," Sera repeated, flat as a coin left on rail tracks. "There's no Wren in the Hollow Hollow. There's no chapel either. There's a mill, Ulyana. There has always been a mill. The pond fed three farms and a ferry crossing, and the door stands at the deepest point, under the water, and you reach it at low water on the ninth night of the month—"

"It's under the chapel."

"The millpond."

"The chapel."

We glared at each other across my own cartography, two people arguing over the geography of a place neither of us could prove, which is, I suppose, the national sport of the Hollow Hollow.

And then the ink moved.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only prayer I have left. My map did not smudge. It did not fade. The river on it — the slow brown river I have drawn so many times I could do it blind, drunk, and dead — *changed course*. It bent eastward, toward the millpond, obedient to something Sera Voss remembered harder than I did. And the chapel, my crooked beloved chapel, shivered on the page like a reflection in disturbed water.

"Oh," said Sera softly, in the voice of a woman watching her worst suspicion confirmed.

"What did you do?"

"Nothing." She was pale. Huntresses, I note, are not accustomed to being frightened by stationery. "Ulyana. How long have you been drawing this valley?"

"Twenty years. Give or take."

"How long has it been *unmade*?"

I told her the truth, which was that I didn't know, which was that nobody knew, which was the whole point of the Hollow Hollow — a valley that could not be found, only inhabited, where the gifted hid from hunters by hiding inside a forgetting so complete even the forgotten couldn't feel its edges.

She sat down slowly in my good chair, which I resented, and looked at me with something worse than hatred: recognition.

"They didn't erase us," she said. "Did they. The crown. They tried, and the valley — the valley hid itself. It pulled itself out of the world like a hand out of a glove." She laughed, one dry note, no humor in it anywhere. "Thirty years hunting the gifted, and I never once asked where we all came *from*. I grew up there, Dunmore. Before it went dark. And then I grew up again, somewhere else, with a mother who wasn't my mother, remembering a pond that doesn't exist — except now it does, because *you drew it*, you absolute fool, your maps gave it back to me—"

"Your memory is giving it back to *me*," I snapped, because the river was still bending, still crawling eastward across twenty years of my careful lies. "Do you understand what you're doing? A map is an agreement. Between the drawer and the place. And you're renegotiating."

"Good," she said. "Renegotiate toward the pond."

"Absolutely not."

* * *

From Sera Voss's side of the night, which she kept to herself for years afterward and told me only once, drunk, in a country that had never heard of either of us:

She had followed the list to the Hollow Hollow expecting ash. Third Company always left ash. But the valley had refused them — roads looping back on themselves, compasses spinning like drunk dancers, and the names on the list going soft in their mouths, unreadable, until Captain Reyes screamed at his own handwriting. She had defected somewhere in that fog, or maybe before it; she'd stopped being able to tell. All she knew was that the valley was dying — she could feel it unraveling, streets forgetting themselves — and that a man who drew impossible maps was either its murderer or its midwife.

She hoped midwife. She'd killed enough murderers.

What she hadn't expected was to stand in his study and see her childhood hanging on his walls, wrong in every particular, and to feel it *correcting itself under her eyes* — the mill rising out of the ink where the chapel should be, the water climbing, her mother's voice coming back to her in a language she hadn't spoken since she was eleven.

*Count to nine. Don't look back.*

She had counted to seven. Thirty years, and the debt was still open.

* * *

By dawn we had stopped arguing and started measuring, which is how all wars end and most marriages begin. We took my maps and her memories out to the valley itself, and that was where things got genuinely unpleasant.

Because the landscape couldn't decide whose recollection to wear.

The road to the chapel became, halfway along, the road to the millpond, and then both at once — a lane running in two directions simultaneously, hedgerows splitting like tongues. The bell-tower rose above the trees and did not rise above the trees. I watched a signpost try to say CHAPEL LANE and MILL WAY and finally settle on a word in neither alphabet, a word that made my teeth ache to read.

"Stop it," I told Sera.

"I'm not doing anything!"

"Your remembering is doing something. Reel it in."

"You reel in yours first!" She grabbed my sleeve, and where her fingers landed, my skin remembered a burn scar I have never had — a hunter's brand, faded, old. I felt her flinch too, feeling my life bleed backward up her arm: lamplight, Marta's laugh, the particular loneliness of a man who maps what he cannot leave.

We let go of each other like scalded children.

"This is what happens," she said quietly, "when a country has two witnesses and no ground truth. It can't hold both. It's choosing, Ulyana. Or rather—" she looked at the doubled road, the impossible tower, the pond that was becoming real enough to reflect a moon that had set hours ago, "—it's asking us to choose for it. One memory gets to be true. The other gets erased. And whichever one wins—"

"—rewrites everything else," I finished, because I am a cartographer and I know what a legend does to a map. "Including us."

Somewhere behind us, down a street that had looped past the same bakery four times, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Captain Reyes had found a thread he could pull, or Sera's defection had been reported, or the valley's unraveling had finally torn wide enough for the crown to see through the hole.

Sera drew her blade — habit, I think, more than hope — and looked at me.

"The pond," she said. "Low water. Ninth night. That's tonight."

"The chapel," I said. "Third pew. Loose board."

The doubled road shuddered beneath our feet, unable to hold us both any longer, and began — I swear this on my last blank sheet of vellum — to fold.
