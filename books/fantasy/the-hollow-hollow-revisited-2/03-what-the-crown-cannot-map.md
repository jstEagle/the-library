## 3. What the Crown Cannot Map

I had been a huntress for eleven years before I understood that the crown's maps were a kind of lie so thorough they circled back around to honesty. They showed nothing where the Hollow Hollow was. No valley, no river, no millpond. Just blank vellum and a small printed note: *terrain unsuitable for settlement.* I used to think it was laziness on the part of the royal surveyors. Then I grew up, learned which questions got people burned, and thought it was policy.

Now, running through streets that would not hold their shape, I understood it was neither. It was mercy — or something wearing mercy's coat.

"They're at the bridge," Ulyana said behind me. He said it the way he said everything, like he was reading it off a chart he hadn't quite finished inking. "Two of them. Maybe three. The bridge is—" He stopped. We both stopped. Because the bridge was gone.

Not destroyed. Gone, in the way a word goes when you've been holding it on your tongue too long. Where the stone arch should have crossed the Alderstream there was simply more street, looping back on itself, and we were standing where we'd stood four minutes ago, outside the baker's, except the baker's sign now read only B—R'S, the letters flaking off the wood like dead skin.

"Ulyana," I said carefully, "how many times have we passed this bakery tonight?"

He looked at me. He had ink on his fingers and terror behind his eyes, and between those two things he managed a shrug. "Once."

"We've passed it three times. The bread smell changes each time. First it was rye. Now it's honey cakes." My mother made honey cakes. That memory was the only reason I could still see her face, and I gripped it the way you grip a rope over a drop. "The valley is forgetting itself. Street by street. Name by name."

"That's not how places work."

"No," I agreed. "That's how countries work. And this one has been dying for thirty years, and none of us noticed because noticing requires remembering, and remembering—" I gestured at the sign, at the looped street, at the whole patient unraveling of everything. "Remembering is exactly what this place forbids."

* * *

Here is what I knew that Ulyana did not, and what I had not told him because telling him felt like handing a stranger my mother's letters: I was born in the Hollow Hollow. Before it was unmade. Before the crown came with its censuses and its quiet lists, and the valley — all of it, every roof and grave and gossiping fence line — folded itself up like a letter never sent and hid inside the one thing no army can search: a memory.

The gifted didn't flee the valley. The valley fled itself. It erased every road out, every map, every official record, and it survived by living only in the heads of the people who remembered it truly. Which meant the country's continued existence depended on a few hundred scattered minds keeping faith with a place the world insisted wasn't there.

And then the witch-hunters came for the rememberers. Naturally. We were always the ones they came for.

"Your maps," I said to Ulyana as we ran again, taking a different lane that I prayed would stay put. "You draw places that exist only in memory. You told me that your first week here, like it was a charming quirk. Like it was a hobby."

"It is how I earn my bread," he said, offended.

"It's how the valley earns its breath. Don't you see it yet? Every map you've ever drawn of this place — every lamplit night bent over that desk of yours — you weren't recording anything. You were *holding it together*. You're the thread, Ulyana. The crown didn't erase the Hollow Hollow from their charts. The valley erased itself, and you—" I caught his sleeve, pulled him into an alcove as boots rang past on cobbles that were, briefly, real cobbles. "You've spent twenty years unknowingly doing the most dangerous work in the kingdom. Keeping a dead country alive out of sheer stubbornness and lamp oil."

He was quiet for a long moment. In the alcove dark his face was all angles and disbelief, and I watched the idea land on him the way grief lands: not all at once, but in stages, each one heavier than the last.

"My neighbor," he finally said. "Marta. They took Marta last night. Her name was on their list." His voice cracked down the middle. "I heard them reading names under my window and I blew out my lamp and I told myself invisibility was safety. Twenty years believing the valley protected me by making me forgettable. But it wasn't protection, was it. It was *use*. The valley kept me hidden the way you keep a tool hidden. Safe until needed."

"Yes," I said, because lying to him now seemed obscene. "And now it needs you."

* * *

The chapel found us rather than the other way around. That sounds mad written down, but by then madness was simply the local weather. One moment we were cutting through the tanner's yard; the next, the yard was gone and we stood in the chapel square, and the chapel itself was coming apart like a loaf in slow motion — walls softening, windows bleeding light, the bell tower leaning at an angle no honest tower would choose.

Inside, it was worse. Or better, depending on your theology. The pews had become suggestions of pews. Through the east window I could see not sky but the millpond, silver and still, exactly as I remembered it — and beneath its surface, faint as a watermark, the outline of a door.

"There," I breathed. "The door stands in the millpond. I told you. I've *told* you since the day we met."

"And I have told you," Ulyana snapped, and for once the mild cartographer sounded like a man with a spine, "that the door is under this chapel. Under the altar stone. I have mapped it a hundred times. I have *drawn* it a hundred times." He yanked his satchel open with shaking hands and spread his maps across the altar — dozens of them, years of them, and every single one placed the exit precisely here, beneath our feet.

Behind us, the chapel doors burst inward.

Four hunters. I recognized two by their grey cloaks; the third carried the list, and even at this distance I could see the names glowing faintly on the parchment, the way hunted names do when the hunting is nearly done. The fourth I recognized worse than any of them: Captain Ilse Marchand, who had trained me, who had taught me to read a fugitive's trail, and who had never once in her life been wrong about where someone would run.

"Voss," she called, conversational, as the ceiling groaned above us. "And the mapmaker. How fitting. Two ghosts and a church that can't decide to be a church."

"The valley is dying," I called back. "Doesn't that bother you? You're standing in a place that's choosing to stop existing rather than let you find it. Doesn't some part of you respect that?"

"I don't chase places, Voss. I chase people." She drew her blade. "Places don't bleed."

The floor tilted. Somewhere above us a beam gave way, and dust fell like snow, and through the window I watched the millpond flicker — once, twice — like a lamp running out of oil. Like a country running out of believers.

Ulyana stared down at his maps, and I saw him arrive at the terrible arithmetic. His memory said chapel. Mine said pond. The valley could not survive both truths, and it could not survive either one alone much longer. Someone had to be wrong about home. Someone had to give up the truest thing they had.

"I know which one is real," he whispered. Not to me. To himself. To the maps.

"How?" I asked. "How can you possibly know?"

He looked up at me, and there was something in his face I had only ever seen in mirrors, back when I still dared own one — the look of a person about to pay for something with themselves.

"Because," he said, "one of these memories isn't mine to keep."

He picked up the pen.
