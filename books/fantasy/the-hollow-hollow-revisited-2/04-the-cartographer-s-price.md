## 4. The Cartographer's Price

There is a particular cruelty in the way a memory leaves. It does not pack its bags or say goodbye at the door. It simply stops being there, the way a tooth stops being there, and your tongue keeps going back to the gap, probing for something that has no business being absent.

I am telling you this now, at the beginning, because by the end of this chapter I will not remember why it matters.

The chapel was coming apart around us. Not falling—unwriting. The stones did not crumble; they forgot how to be stones, going soft and grey as wet paper, and through the gaps where windows had been I could see the valley doing the same. The millpond hung in the air like a held breath. The lanes had looped themselves into knots. Somewhere out in that unraveling, witch-hunters with a list of names were discovering that the names were fading off their page faster than they could read them.

Sera Voss stood beside me at the altar, holding her memory of the door the way one holds a bird—gently, and with the constant fear it will beat itself to death against your cupped hands.

"Say it again," she said.

"The millpond," I said. "You grew up swimming to it. You said the water went cold over your head like a lid closing, and beneath the silt there was an archway, and beyond the archway—" I stopped. My own memory insisted: *beneath the chapel floor, under the third flagstone, the one with the crack shaped like a river delta.* That was where I had always known the door to be. I had mapped it forty times. I had inked it in lamplight while the criers called the gift-hunt below my window, believing that a map of a hidden thing was a kind of protection.

"Your version is wrong," I told her, not unkindly. "It has to be wrong."

"And yours?" she said. "Ulyana. Look at the chapel."

I looked. There was no floor. There was no millpond either, come to that—there was only the raw material of a place that had stopped being remembered correctly by anyone, waiting for someone to decide what it had been.

That was the moment I understood the shape of the choice, though I would spend the rest of my life pretending I made it bravely rather than simply quickly. A country exists only while someone remembers it truly. Two of us remembered it differently, and the difference was tearing it apart, and the hunters were coming up the hill, and behind me in the shadow of the unwriting nave huddled perhaps thirty people whose faces I knew and whose names I had spent years carefully not writing down, because names on paper are how hunts begin.

One of those memories could open the door. Not both. And whichever memory opened it, the opener would pay for it entire.

"It should be mine," Sera said. "It's my home too. I was born under that millpond's lid of water."

"You were born here," I agreed. "And then the crown took you, trained you, sent you back with a list. Your memory of this valley has been handled by other people, Sera. It's been turned over and examined and corrected. Mine hasn't. Mine is just mine—small, stubborn, wrong about everything except the one thing that matters." I paused, and hated myself for the pause, because it sounded noble and nobility is often just vanity wearing a borrowed coat. "Also, I'm the cartographer. Drawing doors is my trade. If anyone is going to pay a price in memory, it should be the man who hoarded them like coins."

She didn't argue. She was a huntress once; she knew when a fight was already lost, and she had learned, somewhere between the crown's service and this hillside, that some surrenders are gifts.

So I knelt on the last solid patch of chapel floor—the flagstone with the crack shaped like a river delta, which existed now only because I still believed in it—and I unrolled the map I had been making all my life without admitting it. Every map I'd ever drawn of the Hollow Hollow had been a rehearsal. This was the performance.

* * *

Here is what I can tell you about the drawing, because I wrote it down afterward in a hand I barely recognized as mine, on a page I found later in my own coat:

*I drew the door from her memory. The archway under the millpond. The cold like a lid. The silt parting. I drew it true to her telling, line by line, and as each line went down, something went out of me.*

*I felt the lamplight go first. Not the lamp—the light. The specific gold of it on my desk, the color I had mixed from three pigments because no shop sold that exact shade. Gone. I looked at the memory of mixing it and found a smooth blankness, like a wall repainted.*

*Then faces. Marta who sold me candles. The boy who chased geese along the millrace. My neighbor, taken in the night, whose name was already on the list—I reached for her name and found the list instead, the hunters' list, the only record left, and even that was burning away at the edges as the valley unwrote itself.*

*Last, the reason. Why I hid. Why I stayed invisible in a valley of the invisible. I have written here that I don't remember, but that is a lie of omission, because omission requires knowing what was omitted. I don't even have the gap. The page where the reason lived is not blank. It is not there.*

I set down the pen. The archway stood on the paper—not drawn, exactly, but *insisted upon*, the way a word repeated until it loses meaning can suddenly mean more than it ever did. Water poured across the parchment without wetting it. The flagstone beneath me remembered being the bottom of a pond.

"Go," I said to the thirty shadows in the nave. "It opens outward. Swim up. Don't look back at the valley—it won't be there, and seeing that will cost you more than it cost me, because unlike me, you'll keep the memory of losing it."

They went. Some wept. One old man saluted the empty air where the altar had been, which I thought was correct.

Sera went last. At the lip of the archway she turned and looked at me—at what remained of me—and whatever she saw, she didn't flinch from it. "Come through," she said. "You'll be a stranger everywhere. Better a stranger somewhere real than a ghost here."

"I'll follow," I said. "Someone has to close the door behind us. Hunts end badly when doors stay open."

A lie. The door closed itself the moment the last of them passed, folding shut like a book, and the valley finished forgetting itself, and I stood alone in a white nothing that was not even darkness, because darkness is something and this was less than that. Then the nothing pushed, gently, the way a tide pushes, and I came through after all—into cold water, upward, past a lid of surface, into air.

* * *

We emerged into a living country that had never heard of me.

That is not sentiment. I checked. I walked into the first town with my coat full of maps and asked after the Hollow Hollow at the market, at the tavern, at the records office, and received in return a range of expressions from polite puzzlement to the particular pity reserved for men who ask after places that sound like riddles. A clerk showed me a survey of the whole region, beautifully done, and the valley was there—but as ordinary farmland, ploughed for centuries, owned by families with long dull names. No dead country. No erasure. Whatever the Hollow Hollow had been, this world had never needed to hide it, because this world had never hunted anyone for their gifts. Or if it had, it had gotten over it, the way children get over fevers.

Sera met me at the town well on the second morning. She had been asking her own questions, and her answers were worse than mine, because she remembered a life here—a childhood, a mother, a street—that this country also had no record of. We compared notes and found we were both ghosts of the same nowhere, and there was a strange comfort in that, the comfort of two people shivering near the same small fire.

"We owe them," she said, meaning the thirty. Meaning the ones whose names I no longer knew but whose passage I had bought with everything I was. "Justice owed, not given. Someone should make sure they landed somewhere kind."

"They landed here," I said. "This is where they came out."

"Then someone should make sure *here* stays kind." She had huntress eyes when she said it. Old habits. "Countries forget. People forget. Maps are how you make forgetting difficult."

I had lost every memory of the valley. What I had kept—what no sacrifice could reach, because it wasn't memory but craft—was the ability to draw. So I sat down at the well's edge with fresh paper, and I began a new map.

Blank.

Every inch of it blank, and I want you to understand that a blank map is the most frightening thing a cartographer can own. It is a confession that you know nothing, an admission that everything you were has been spent. But it is also the only honest document in the world, because every other map is a record of things that may no longer be true, and a blank page is a promise that you intend to find out.

Sera sat beside me. She had taken up whittling, of all things, a knife and a piece of driftwood from a river this country actually had, and she worked it in slow strokes while I stared at nothing with professional intensity.

"What will you put on it first?" she asked eventually.

"I don't know yet. That's rather the point."

"That's a terrible answer from a professional."

"It's the only honest one." I dipped my pen. Held it over the white. "Though—if I'm being truthful, which I'm told is becoming fashionable—I suppose I'll start with the well. Everything else so far has started there."

She laughed, short and real, and the driftwood in her hands was beginning to look like a door, though she claimed that was coincidence.

I drew the well. Then the road out of town, because roads are promises. Then, at the edge of the paper, where the country thinned into country nobody had surveyed, I drew a dotted line and stopped, pen hovering, because the dotted line wanted to become something and I did not yet know what.

Behind us, in the market square, a crier had climbed the steps of the courthouse. I hadn't noticed him arrive. He unrolled a scroll, and his voice carried the way criers' voices do, flat and official and impossible to ignore:

"Hear ye! Hear ye! By order of the Crown—"

Sera's knife stopped mid-stroke.

"—a reward is offered for information concerning persons arriving unregistered within the county—"

The dotted line on my map trembled under my pen. This country had never heard of the Hollow Hollow. But crowns, I was beginning to suspect, are the same everywhere, and somewhere behind that courthouse door, someone had a list.

"Ulyana," Sera said quietly, "they're calling names."

I looked down at my blank map, at the single dotted line escaping toward the unmapped edge, and I understood that the price I paid had not bought an ending. It had bought a beginning—which is, I have since learned, considerably more expensive.

I pressed the pen to the paper and followed the line.
