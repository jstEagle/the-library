## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The mist came first, as it always did, rolling down from the shoulders of the mountains before the sun had properly risen — a slow white tide that swallowed the terraced fields, the slate roofs of Hollowmere village, and at last the great black arch of the Gate itself. Sable Quintana had watched this ritual of the valley for twenty-six years, every morning of her life, and she had never once grown tired of it.

Magic could not follow the mist in. That was the whole point of the place, though no one in Hollowmere spoke of it plainly. They said *the valley keeps what it keeps*, or *the fog is thick today, best stay home*, or nothing at all. Children were taught to sweep their doorsteps clean of feathers, because a feather might be an omen, and omens were a kind of magic, and magic did not belong here. The valley forgot things on purpose. That was its nature, and its gift, and its law.

Sable was the exception. She remembered everything.

She climbed the switchback path with her satchel of tools bumping against her hip: the brass scraper for lichen, the jar of lime-wash, the soft horsehair brush, the little iron stylus worn smooth by three generations of keepers' hands. The path was steep and wet and lined with boundary stones, each one carved with a spiral that meant, in a language older than any spoken now, *nothing passes*. She touched the third stone as she passed it, as her mother had presumably once done, as Maren had taught her to do before she could reach the top of it. A habit. A greeting. A promise.

The Gate stood where the valley narrowed to a throat between two cliffs, and it was not a gate in any way a stranger would recognize. There were doors, yes — twin slabs of some dark, dense stone that no chisel in living memory had managed to scratch — but they had not opened in Sable's lifetime, nor in her grandmother's, nor, if the records were true, in four hundred years. The arch above them rose forty feet, black as a drowned ship's hull, and across its face ran carvings so old and so deep that rain and lichen had made them into something more like weather than writing. Spirals within spirals. Hands with too many fingers. A mouth, open, that was either singing or screaming depending on the light.

Sable set down her satchel, knelt on the flat stone before the threshold, and began.

First the sweeping — birch twigs bound with red thread, drawn outward from the center, always outward, never toward. *What gathers must be gathered away,* Maren used to say, standing over her small self in the grey dawn, correcting her grip. *You are not inviting anything. You are showing it the road out.* Then the lime-wash, thinned with rainwater caught in copper bowls (iron soured it; everyone knew that much even if they'd forgotten why), painted along the base of each door in a line unbroken. Then the inspection: every inch of the arch examined by eye and fingertip, every spiral traced with the stylus to feel for cracks, for warmth, for that faint wrongness beneath the skin of the stone that keepers called *the itch* and wrote about only in cipher.

It was work that took most of the morning and all of a lifetime. Sable loved it the way one loves breathing — without thinking about it, until something makes you gasp.

This morning there was frost. Early autumn frost, silvering the boundary stones and stiffening the moss in the Gate's deep carvings, which was unusual enough that Sable paused with her hand flat against the arch's cold cheek. Frost came late to the valley, held warm by some quality of the air that no one had ever explained to her satisfaction. She scraped at the carving with her thumbnail and the ice flaked away, and beneath it the stone was —

Warm.

Only barely. Only the way a hearthstone is warm an hour after the fire dies. But stone in this valley did not hold heat, because heat was a kind of energy and energy was a kind of magic and magic did not belong here. Sable crouched, pressed both palms to the arch, closed her eyes, and listened the way Maren had taught her to listen: not with ears but with the flat of the soul, the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth.

Nothing. The usual nothing. The profound, engineered, blessed nothing that lived inside the Gate like water inside a well.

She told herself the warmth was her own hands. She told herself a great number of things that morning.

* * *

Hollowmere woke below her as she walked back down: chimney smoke rising straight in the windless air, the clatter of the mill wheel, old Tamsin's goats complaining their way up the high pasture. The village was built in a horseshoe around the millpond, forty-odd houses, a meeting hall, a shrine with no god in it — just an empty alcove where offerings of bread and salt were left for *whoever keeps us*, a phrase that made Sable's chest tighten every time she heard it, though she'd never been able to say why.

Maren was in the kitchen garden when Sable reached the house they shared at the village's edge, pulling the last of the beans with hands that were still strong despite the knuckles gone wide with age. She was not Sable's mother. Everyone knew that, and no one said it, and after twenty-six years the distinction had worn smooth as a river stone. Maren had found her, or been given her, or chosen her — the story changed depending on Maren's mood and how much cider had been drunk at the harvest feast — and that was all anyone needed to know.

"You're late," Maren said, without looking up. "Frost on the stones?"

"A little." Sable hung her satchel on its peg. "Early, though."

"Mm." Maren's hands went still among the bean vines. Just for a moment. Then they moved again. "Well. Seasons don't read our almanacs. Come eat."

They ate barley porridge with honey at the table by the window, and Maren talked about the winter stores and whether the Hedleys' boy would finally be old enough to help with the slaughter, and Sable answered in the right places, and neither of them mentioned the thing they always didn't mention, which was everything.

It was only when Sable was leaving, her hand on the door latch, that Maren spoke without turning from the sink.

"Sable. Did you touch the arch today? With bare hands?"

"I inspect it every day. You know that."

"I know it." Maren's voice was very level, the way it went when she was holding something down. "And how did it feel?"

Sable hesitated. In twenty-six years, Maren had never asked that question. Keepers reported cracks, discoloration, the itch. Never feelings. Feelings were for the keeper's private journal, and the journal was burned at midwinter, pages fed one by one into the shrine's cold alcove, a ritual Sable performed alone and had long ago stopped pretending to understand.

"Cold," Sable said. "Cold as always."

Maren nodded slowly, still facing the window, and the light caught the lines around her eyes, and Sable thought — absurdly, briefly — that her guardian looked afraid.

"Good," Maren said. "Mind the frost, then. It lies."

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the ledger.

Every keeper kept one: a heavy book bound in grey hide, its pages ruled by hand, recording the Gate's condition day by day, year by year. Sable's shelf held nine of them in her own hand and thirty-one in others', going back past the founding of the village, past the fall of the kingdom whose coins sometimes turned up in plowed fields, past the edge of written history and into pages of cipher that no one alive could read — except, apparently, the keepers, who learned it as children without ever being told they were learning it. Sable had copied those oldest pages out in her ninth year, letter by letter, and understood them perfectly, and had never been able to explain to anyone, including herself, how.

*Day 9,412 of my keeping,* she wrote, in the cipher that looked to ordinary eyes like weather. *All lines sound. No itch. Lime renewed. Frost early — note for the almanac.* She paused, stylus hovering, and then added, because the ledger demanded honesty and honesty was the keeper's first law: *Arch warm at the western shoulder. Faint. Possibly nothing.*

Possibly nothing. She underlined it twice, which in the cipher meant *I am lying to myself and I know it.*

The afternoon light came slanting through the archive room's single window — a room beneath the meeting hall that officially stored grain records and unofficially stored everything that mattered — and Sable worked in its amber glow until her hand cramped. When she finished she sat for a while with her palm flat on the closed ledger, listening to the silence of the valley settle around her like snow.

That was when the prayer came through.

Later — in the terrible weeks that followed, in the exile and the burning and the becoming — Sable would try to describe it a hundred times, to Oren, to Kestrel, to the empty air, and never quite manage it. It was not a sound. It arrived already inside her, the way cold arrives, the way grief arrives: suddenly present in a place it should not be able to reach. It was thin and ragged and desperate, worn down by distance the way a pebble is worn by rivers, and it slipped through the seal the way light slips through a crack under a door.

A woman's voice. Young. Hoarse with crying.

*Sable. Sable Quintana. If anyone can hear me — they say the gods answer again, they say the dragons are dying, they say the fire is going out — Sable, please, I dreamed your name, please—*

Gone. As fast as it came, snuffed out like a match pinched between wet fingers. The silence of the valley closed back over the hole it had left, and Sable sat in the archive room with her heart slamming against her ribs and the taste of someone else's terror on her tongue.

Her name. Not *keeper*, not *the gate*, not the formal invocation in the cipher that she half-knew and had never dared use. Her name. Spoken by a stranger beyond the mountains who had never seen her face, carried through four hundred years of perfect silence, delivered to the one person in the world whose entire existence depended on that silence holding.

Sable's hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to someone else — someone whose mother had died at this gate, someone who had been raised to tend a lock without ever asking what it locked, someone who had just heard the world outside knock for the first time in her life and speak her name like it had known it forever.

*They say the fire is going out.*

She knew, distantly, from books, from the oldest ledgers, that dragon-fire had sealed the Gate in the first place. That somewhere beyond the valley the last dragons still breathed, or had breathed, and their flame was woven into the seal like warp threads in cloth. The keepers' cipher mentioned it only glancingly, the way one mentions the foundations of a house — *as long as the fire endures, the seal endures* — and no keeper in recorded history had ever needed to think further than that.

*Going out.*

Outside, the evening bell rang from the meeting hall, calling the village to supper, to lamplighting, to the small safe rituals of a people who believed themselves forgotten by everything larger than weather. Sable rose on legs that felt borrowed. She climbed the stairs. She stepped out into the blue dusk, and the mist was rising off the fields again, patient and white and total, and the valley was forgetting another day as it had forgotten thousands of them.

But the Gate, she thought, looking up at the black arch on its cliff, faintly visible through the thinning fog like a doorway drawn in charcoal.

The Gate remembered.

And tonight, for the first time in four hundred years, something had gotten through to it — or through it — and that something knew her name.

Far to the west, past the mountains, past the kingdoms she knew only from maps, a woman was still praying. Sable could almost feel her, a small hot point of desperation on the edge of perception, like a star you can see only by not looking directly at it. And beneath that prayer, deeper, older, patient as stone, she felt something else begin to stir. Something vast turning its attention toward the valley the way a sleeper turns toward a sound.

The mist rolled in. The valley forgot.

Sable Quintana stood in her dooryard, holding her own elbows against the cold, and for the first time in her quiet, ordered, carefully bounded life, she was afraid — not of what the Gate kept out, but of what, after all these years, had finally begun knocking.
