## 5. Hollow-Born

The stairs beneath the Hollow Gate had not been built. They had been *grown* — or so the old stories said — each step a pale rib of stone that curved up out of the valley's bones like something once alive and now only patient. Sable had descended them exactly twice in her life: once at seven, when Maren caught her on the fourth step and carried her back up shaking with a fear she never explained; and once at nineteen, when the seal flickered during an eclipse and every Keeper before her was dead and there was no one left to tell her no.

Tonight made three.

Oren went first, his stolen lantern held low, its light swallowed three steps behind him as though the dark down there drank rather than merely lacked. He moved like a man who had spent years in places where silence was a predator — weight on the outside of his feet, breath shallow, one hand trailing the wall. The wound in his side had been bound that morning with Maren's linen, though neither of them would say her name aloud in front of the other.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," he murmured, not turning around.

"Because you said the sigil on my wrist is a brand of office." Sable kept her voice flat. Her voice had been flat for two days, since the mob, since Maren's face across the firelight, since the lie she'd told with her mother's calm. "And because you looked at it the way a man looks at a debt."

"I looked at it the way a priest looks at scripture he hoped was metaphor."

"Same thing."

He huffed something that might have been a laugh in a kinder life. Then the stairs ended, and the lantern's light finally found purchase, and Sable stopped breathing.

* * *

The undercrypt was not what she had imagined, which was nothing, which had been the point. She had spent twenty-six years not imagining this place the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. Now it resolved around her all at once: a chamber vast enough that the lantern could not find its far walls, floored in the same ribbed pale stone, and everywhere — *everywhere* — writing.

Not carved. Grown. Lines of script rose from the floor and climbed the pillars and arched overhead like migrating birds frozen mid-turn, tens of thousands of characters in a hand that began precise and grew, over what must have been generations, more hurried, more cramped, more desperate. And threaded through all of it, running along the grooves between the words like sap through wood, was a faint silver luminescence that pulsed.

Sable knew the rhythm without counting. It matched her heartbeat exactly.

"Keeper," Oren breathed. He'd gone still in the way of men who have seen temples and know when they've walked into one. "This is a ledger."

"It's a room."

"It's a *ledger.*" He raised the lantern, following a line of script up a pillar until the light lost it. "Devotional accounting. I've read fragments — smuggled pages, burned fragments, things my order denied having. The old faith kept records of what the gods were given. Prayers received. Devotion rendered. Payment owed." His voice had gone thin. "I thought the fragments were forgery. Nobody builds a religion out of bookkeeping."

"Somebody built this one out of it."

She knelt where the nearest script met the floor and put her fingers to it. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm, not hearth-warm — *blood*-warm, the temperature of a living body, and beneath her fingertips the pulse quickened, just slightly, like a sleeper stirring at a familiar touch.

The characters under her hand were older than the rest, deeper, written in that first confident hand. She couldn't read them. But she didn't need to, because beside them, grown into the stone at the height of a kneeling woman's eyes, was a sigil she had known her whole life.

It sat on her wrist right now. It had been pressed into her skin by Maren's thumb and cold ash the night after her mother died, when she was too young to remember being branded, and she had been told it marked her as Keeper, as servant of the gate, as the valley's shield.

Here, in the oldest writing, at the beginning of everything, the same mark appeared inside a larger design — and the larger design was unmistakably a person. A figure rendered in lines of script instead of flesh, arms spread, standing in a doorway. The doorway was the gate above them. The figure stood *in* it, filling it, and every line of prayer-script in the entire chamber flowed toward that figure like water finding a drain.

"She's the anchor," Oren said quietly. He had come to stand beside her, and he was not looking at the figure. He was looking at her wrist. "Every account I ever found spoke of the anchor in the third person. *The anchor holds. The anchor must be willing. The anchor must be fed.* I assumed it was a beast. A sacrifice. Something they kept."

Sable's mouth had gone dry. "Kept where?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The silver pulse under her fingers beat against her heartbeat, and the heartbeat did not change, because it was not echoing the room. The room was echoing her.

* * *

They followed the script backward through time — or forward; Oren argued it was forward, the hand degrading generation by generation, confidence curdling into haste. Sable let him argue. Words were easier than understanding. They moved along the walls by lantern-light, and the chamber gave up its history in installments, and each installment took something from her that she did not get back.

The earliest entries were almost serene. *Year of the First Sealing. The gods came hungry to the valley and the gate refused them, for the gate is not a door but a throat, and a throat may swallow.* Sable read that line four times. *A throat may swallow.* The gate did not keep the old gods out. It took them in. It took their hunger in, and their devotion, and the prayers that fed them, and it held all of it the way a dam holds a river — and the dam, the script explained in language that grew less certain with every century, was not stone.

The dam was a woman.

*The anchor binds. The anchor bleeds willingly into the lock, and her blood remembers, and what remembers cannot be unmade.*

"The Keepers aren't priests," Oren said. His voice had changed over the last hour, shed its caution, gone raw. "They're not even guards. They're — maintenance. Tending the seal. Your rituals, the ash, the salt, the prayers you don't send — you weren't worshipping the gate, Quintana. You were *feeding* it. Keeping the anchor nourished so the lock stays fat and slow and shut."

"My mother's rituals." Sable heard her own voice from very far away. "Maren's rituals. The ones I do every dawn."

"Someone has to feed the lock." He paused. "Or someone has to *be* the lock."

The next section of wall stopped them both.

The handwriting here belonged to a single author — she could tell, because it broke mid-sentence and resumed in the same slanting, urgent hand for page after page of stone, a woman writing faster and faster as though racing something. The date-forms shifted. The entries shortened. And then, set apart from the rest in characters grown deep and deliberate, as if carved with the last of a lifetime's strength:

*My daughter lives. Let it be written where no one will look, because everyone looks at the gate and no one looks beneath it: my daughter lives, and I am the gate, and these are the same sentence.*

Sable did not remember sitting down. She was on the floor of the undercrypt with the warm stone pulsing under her palms and the lantern guttering, and Oren was saying her name — not Quintana; her name, *Sable*, which he had learned two days ago and never used — and she could not make her eyes stop moving across the words.

*I was chosen because I volunteered, and I volunteered because someone had to, and the council called it holy and I call it arithmetic. The gods are starving. Starving gods eat the world. So the world feeds them slowly, through me, a meal measured out over a lifetime instead of devoured in an afternoon. That is the sealing. That is the mercy. That is the lie we tell so the arithmetic can sleep at night.*

*A child born of the anchor carries the anchor's blood. The blood remembers. My daughter will be able to open what I am closing. I have asked the council to bind her power away, to bury it, to let her live a small life in a safe valley, and they have agreed, because the council agrees to everything except telling the truth.*

*If you are reading this, you are either a Keeper who has finally dared the stairs, or you are my daughter, and you have finally dared the stairs. Either way: I did not die protecting the gate. There was nothing to protect me from. I died becoming it. Every dawn ritual you perform, you perform for me. Every prayer you refuse to send, I drink instead. I am not sorry. I would do it again. But I wanted someone, somewhere, in the dark under everything, to know that I chose it — and that I am sorry anyway.*

*Be small, little one. Be safe. Be ordinary. The world outside will beg you to be otherwise, and the begging will sound like holiness, and holiness is how the hungry feed.*

The lantern went out.

For a long moment there was only the dark and the pulse — her pulse, the room's pulse, indistinguishable now, and Sable understood with a clarity that felt like falling that it had always been indistinguishable. The seal had not been flickering during the eclipse when she was nineteen. It had been *reaching*. The gate had known its next anchor was walking on the stairs above it, and it had been waiting, patient as stone, for her to grow into her inheritance.

"Sable." Oren's hand found her shoulder in the dark. Warm. Human. Startlingly so. "Say something."

"She's still there."

"What?"

Sable stood. Her legs shook and she let them. "The script. The newest of it — the last hand, the one that isn't finished. Look at the dates, Oren. The ledger doesn't end. Anchors die and anchors are replaced, and the record continues, and the last entry—" her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through— "the last entry is dated the year I was born. And it says *the anchor endures*, present tense. Present tense, Oren. Twenty-six years later."

The darkness seemed to lean in.

"You're saying your mother—"

"I'm saying the gate doesn't kill its anchors. It *keeps* them. It keeps them the way it keeps the gods — slowly, patiently, forever." Her hands had closed into fists without her permission, and her nails bit into the sigil on her wrist, and the pain was good, the pain was hers, the pain was the one thing in this room that belonged to her alone. "She's not dead. She's *in there*. Holding. For twenty-six years she has been holding the door with her body while I lit candles for her ghost and Maren told me she died a hero."

Oren was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was the voice of a man setting down a weight he had carried too far. "In the seminary they taught us the sealing was a wall. A miracle. The one clean thing the old faith ever did." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "It's not a wall. It's a hostage. It's been a hostage for a thousand years, and the ransom is paid in daughters."

"Not anymore."

"Sable—"

"She wrote *be small*. She wrote *be ordinary*." Sable turned toward where she knew the oldest script stood, the first hand, the confident one, the figure in the doorway with its arms spread wide. She could see it now even in the dark; the silver glow had brightened, or her eyes had changed, or the room had decided she was ready. "Well. I was small. I was ordinary. I lied to the woman who raised me and hid a bleeding stranger and broke every rule I was given, and the world outside came knocking anyway, and the dragons are dying, and the gods are answering prayers, and the lock is starving because nobody told me I was supposed to be feeding it a *person*."

The pulse around her quickened. She let it.

"So I'm done being the secret. I'm done being the buried thing. Whatever my blood can open, I want to know how it opens. Whatever my mother is holding, I want to know what it costs her — and what it would cost to take her place, or take her *out*, or burn the whole rotten arithmetic down." She heard her own voice fill the crypt and did not recognize it and did not care. "Grief is a fuel, Oren. My order taught me to bank it like a fire. Tonight I'm spending it."

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of grown stone, the Hollow Gate hummed — a note too low for most ears, but Sable had been hearing it her whole life without knowing what it was. It sounded different tonight. It sounded like recognition.

Oren struck light back into the lantern with hands that were not quite steady. Its glow returned, and the chamber blazed with it, ten thousand years of bookkeeping, and in the silver pulse of the walls Sable saw the newest script — the unfinished entry, the present tense — shimmer, just once, like a surface disturbed by something rising from far below.

And beneath the last line, in characters finer than any others in the chamber, grown so recently the stone around them was still smooth, a single new sentence had appeared that had not been there when they descended:

*You came down. Oh, little one. You came down.*

Sable read it twice. Then she turned to Oren, and her voice, when it came, was quiet and absolutely level, the voice of a woman standing on the far side of a door that could never again be closed.

"We leave for Cinderhold at dawn. And on the way, you're going to tell me everything your order knows about opening gates."
