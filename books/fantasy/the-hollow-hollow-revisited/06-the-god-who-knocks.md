## 6. The God Who Knocks

The body had been a good choice.

Vessia turned her borrowed hands in the grey light of dawn and admired them: a queen's hands once, soft as candle wax, now travel-worn and cracked at the knuckles from the long road south. The pilgrim queen had prayed for three days and nights on the steps of her own drowned temple before Vessia had come to her, and she had wept with gratitude even as the god poured herself in through the cracks of her — that was the thing mortals never understood about being chosen. It felt like grace right up until it felt like drowning, and by then the water was already inside you.

She flexed her fingers. The body obeyed. It always obeyed, now.

*Good bones,* she thought. *Good lungs. A voice that people had spent a lifetime learning to kneel to.* That last mattered most. Devotion soaked into flesh like smoke into wool; you could wash a garment a hundred times and still smell the fire. When this mouth spoke, people heard authority before they heard words. It was a useful inheritance, and Vessia wasted nothing.

Before her, the valley opened like a cupped palm.

Mist lay over it in slow rivers, pooling in the folds of the hills, and through the gaps she could see green fields stitched with stone walls, a village huddled around its well, terraces climbing the slopes where someone — some small diligent mortal life — had planted barley in rows straight enough to be prayers themselves. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere a dog barked, thin and distant, a sound that arrived a half-beat late, as though the valley were slightly out of step with the world.

And at the valley's throat, where the road narrowed between two leaning stones, the air itself stood up and said *no*.

Vessia stopped at the boundary without meaning to. Her feet simply would not carry her past the line where the mist thickened, and when she pressed forward anyway — gently, experimentally, the way one tests a locked door — the world pushed back. Not violently. Not with fire or thunder. With *absence*. The power in her went quiet, like a singer stepping into a room built of felt. She could feel the edges of herself going numb, the way a hand goes numb in snow, and beneath the numbness something older than numbness: hunger, waking, and finding nothing to eat.

"Ah," she said aloud, and her stolen voice was warm with interest. "There you are."

The Hollow Gate. She had known of it, of course — the gods had all known of it, in the way prisoners know of the wall they cannot see. A seal raised in an age when the old hungers had grown too loud, too greedy, when the sky had been full of wings and fire and the ground had rung with worship until the mountains themselves grew tired of listening. Someone had shut the door. Someone had paid for it. And for centuries the gods had starved outside it, dwindling, dozing, feeding on scraps — the stray prayer that slipped a crack, the dying wish, the desperate bargain struck at some forgotten shrine.

Scraps kept them alive. Scraps did not keep them *whole*.

Vessia sat down on the boundary stone itself, arranging the pilgrim queen's grey skirts with a hostess's care, and looked out across the mist at the little valley that had forgotten her.

It was not anger she felt. Anger was a mortal weather; it blew through and left the sky clean. What she felt was the patience of a root splitting stone — the slow, total certainty that what is closed can be opened, given time, given pressure, given thirst.

"You've done very well," she murmured to the valley, to the seal, to whatever watchful thing might be listening behind that wall of silence. "So well. All these years, keeping us out. Keeping everything out." She smiled, and the smile did not reach the eyes of the body she wore, because smiles are a surface language and she had never needed surfaces. "But walls don't only keep things out, little gate. They keep things in. And nothing that lives likes to be kept."

* * *

She learned the valley's rhythm from the ridge above, the way a spider learns a web by its tremors.

The villagers rose before the sun. They worked the fields, mended the walls, argued cheerfully about drainage ditches and the price of wool. In the evenings they gathered, some of them, at a low stone building near the well — a shrine, though they had let it go bare, its alcoves empty, its altar swept but unadorned. They did not pray there so much as *remember* praying there, the way one remembers a dance learned in childhood: the steps remained, the music gone.

No gods answered here. Of course none did. Magic could not cross the boundary; she could feel that truth pressing against her like glass. Whatever force the gate wielded, it did not merely bar the way — it unmade the divine at the threshold, thinned it, starved it. Anything of hers that entered that silence would arrive as nothing.

But.

Vessia tilted her head, watching a woman — broad-shouldered, silver-streaked hair, hands like tree roots — stand at the empty shrine after the others had gone. The woman lit no candle. There were no candles. She simply stood, palms flat on the cold altar, lips moving.

Vessia reached out — carefully, delicately, the way one slips a knife through the seam of a letter — and listened.

*Keep her safe,* the woman was saying. Not to any god. To the valley itself, perhaps, or to the dead, or to no one. *Keep Sable safe. I know I promised her mother. I know. Just — keep her safe.*

Vessia's breath caught — a habit of the body, borrowed along with the lungs, and she found she liked the sensation, the small human stutter of it.

*A name.*

Prayer was food, but names were doors. A god could live a century on the crumbs of anonymous devotion — *please, please, please*, shapeless and flavorless, gruel for the starving. But a named prayer was different. A named prayer had a hook in it. Somewhere in that mist-locked valley walked a woman called Sable, loved fiercely enough to be prayed for over an empty altar, and love, Vessia knew, was the oldest hinge in the world.

She settled back against the boundary stone and began, with the leisure of an immortal, to work.

* * *

It could not be done all at once. She was honest with herself about that; vanity was for younger gods. The seal was strong, woven from blood and will and something older than either, and the valley's silence was absolute at its center. But a seal is a made thing, and made things have seams, and seams are where the water gets in.

She started small.

A child in the eastern fields lost a lamb, and wept into his hands at the fence line — close to the boundary, closer than he knew, close enough that his grief crossed the invisible line like heat crossing a windowpane. Vessia caught it. She held the small wet rag of his sorrow in her hands and turned it over, savoring it — grief was rich fare, grief and hope braided together, the finest dish mortals made — and then she gave something back.

Not much. A nudge. A whisper of herself threaded through the seam of the world, guiding the lamb's bleat along the wind, turning the boy's head at the right moment toward the bramble hollow where the foolish creature had tangled itself. He found it, whooping, and ran home shouting that the valley had shown him where to look, and his grandmother smiled and said the valley was kind, and neither of them thought another thing about it.

But Vessia thought about it. She rolled the moment over in her mind like a sweet. The first taste in centuries that had not been a scrap snatched from a gutter. She had *given*, and the giving had passed through the seal like light through fog — thinned, yes, diminished, barely a breath of her true self — but *passed*. The gate barred bodies. It barred gods walking in glory. It did not seem to bar a kindness small enough to fit through a keyhole.

"Everything has a size," she told the mist, fondly. "Even miracles."

Through that first week she practiced, patient as tide. A fevered man dreamed of cool water and woke to rain. A young farmer, despairing over a blighted field, found the blight stopped at one neat row, as if drawn there by a careful hand. A widow who prayed — not to anyone, just *prayed*, the word worn smooth as a river stone — slept that night without dreaming of her husband's face for the first time in a year.

Each gift cost her. Each one arrived thinner than the last effort should have allowed, wrung down to almost nothing by the passage through the seal, and each time she had to rest afterward, crouched in the body's small human weariness, licking the wound of expenditure. But the gifts landed. That was the miracle — *her* miracle, the one she hoarded jealously. They landed, and they took root, and roots, she knew, grow in the dark where no one is watching.

And the villagers began, without knowing why, to feel watched over.

* * *

On the ninth day, the priest came.

She felt him before she saw him — a smudge of wrongness moving fast along the southern road, a man running with the particular desperation of the hunted. Vessia rose from her seat on the boundary stone and drifted, unhurried, to the ridge above the road, and watched him come: gaunt, wild-eyed, robes torn to rags, carrying in his arms something wrapped in sacking that clinked like broken bells.

Behind him, at a distance no mortal eye could bridge, walked her problem.

She had sent him weeks ago — a vessel, a servant, a thing that had once been a man and had prayed itself hollow until there was room in it for something else. It had served adequately. It had tracked the priest across half the realm, following the trail of stolen relics, and it had nearly had him twice. But now it had followed him here, to the valley's edge, and Vessia watched it stop at the boundary stones exactly as she had stopped, watched it press against the silence and recoil, watched it stand there swaying like a puppet whose hand had paused.

It could not cross either. Nothing of theirs could.

"Well," Vessia said softly. "Isn't that interesting."

The priest staggered to the boundary stones and fell against them, gasping, and even from the ridge she saw the moment the valley's silence took hold of him — the sag of relief, the way a hunted animal goes limp when it finally crosses into ground its predators cannot enter. He looked back at the thing pursuing him. It looked at him. Neither moved.

Then the mist shifted, and figures came down from the upper fields — villagers, armed with nothing more dangerous than scythes and suspicion, drawn by some instinct toward their border. And among them, walking ahead of the others with her chin set at an angle the woman at the shrine would have recognized, came a girl.

Vessia went very still.

She knew the walk before she knew the face. She had spent nine days listening to the valley's prayers, sifting them like sand for gold, and she knew the name that recurred in them — *Sable, keep Sable safe, Sable says the boundary must hold, Sable tends the gate* — and here she was, tall and dark-haired and grave, kneeling beside the fallen priest with her hands already at his wounds.

And on her wrist, as she reached, the sleeve falling back — a sigil. Branded, old, deliberate. Even at this distance, even thinned by mist, Vessia felt it like a toothache: the mark of the keeper's line. The mark of the blood that had closed the door.

Hunger rose in her so suddenly and so completely that the borrowed body trembled with it. She had to sit down on the wet grass and breathe — a human trick, breathing, marvelously distracting — until the shaking stopped.

*There you are,* she thought, and the thought was not a thought at all but a vast slow gravitational pull, the whole of her attention narrowing to a single point in the mist below. *There you are, little lock. Little key. All this time I wondered what the gate was made of, and it walks, it breathes, it kneels in the mud bandaging a thief.*

The girl helped the priest up. The villagers murmured. And Vessia watched, with the patience of a root splitting stone, as the girl broke the first rule of her whole careful, sealed, silent life: she touched an outsider. She brought him toward the boundary. She stepped across the line herself, back into the valley, carrying contamination on her hands.

"Oh, Sable," Vessia whispered, tasting the name fully for the first time, letting it dissolve on the stolen tongue like sugar. "You're going to be so much easier than I expected."

Below, the thing that had been a man turned from the boundary stones and began, unhurried, to walk away. It would report. It would wait. It had eternity, and so did she.

Above the valley, the mist curled and thickened, and if any of the farmers glancing up from their fields noticed that the evening light had gone strange and golden, and that the air smelled faintly of incense where no incense burned, they only smiled, and felt blessed, and went in to supper.

Vessia settled against her stone to wait, and to feed, and to knock — softly, softly, like water through stone.

The girl would come to her. They always did, in the end. Every prayer was a thread, and every thread led somewhere, and somewhere in that forgetting valley a woman named Maren was even now standing at an empty altar, asking the dark to keep Sable safe.

"So shall I," Vessia promised the mist, smiling with the pilgrim queen's beautiful mouth. "So very safely. You'll see."
