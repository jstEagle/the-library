## 8. The Dragonslayer's Debt

Kestrel March had a system for mornings like this one: drink until the ceiling stopped moving, then decide whether the day was worth the trouble of standing up.

The ceiling of the Drowned Lantern was not moving. That was a bad sign. It meant she'd only had four cups last night instead of the six required to make the world honest.

She lay on the bench she'd claimed as a bed — the innkeeper had stopped renting her rooms two years ago, after the incident with the hearth and the tax collector, and Kestrel had stopped asking — and she watched dust drift through a crack in the shutters like it had somewhere better to be. Everyone in Cinderhold had somewhere better to be. That was the whole point of a border city. It sat at the seam between the settled lands and the ash-wastes where dragons had once ruled, and everyone in it was either running from something or waiting for something to run from them.

Kestrel had been both for eleven years.

She sat up. Her head filed a formal complaint. She ignored it with the practiced ease of a woman who had once taken a wyvern's tail across the shoulder plates and kept swinging.

On the table beside her bench sat the evidence of last night's mistake: an empty bottle of Ashwine, red as old blood, and a folded paper sealed with wax the color of a healing wound. She remembered taking it. She remembered the man who'd given it — soft hands, temple cologne, the particular smile of someone who believed he was doing you a favor. She did not remember agreeing to anything.

She broke the seal anyway. Curiosity was the only appetite that ever survived her hangovers.

*The bearer of this writ is retained by the Congregation of the Answered Prayer,* it read, in a clerk's careful hand, *to locate and terminate one Sable Quintana, styled the Gate-Witch, currently residing within the city of Cinderhold. Payment: two hundred crowns upon presentation of proof. The Congregation thanks you for your service to the faithful.*

Two hundred crowns. Kestrel read the number twice, because numbers like that didn't come for killing witches. Numbers like that came for killing things people loved.

She turned the paper over. Blank. She held it up to the dusty light, looking for the watermark, the sigil, the tell of who exactly had decided a gate-witch was worth a small fortune. Nothing. Just the wax seal, which showed a stylized flame cupped in two open hands — the mark of Vessia's new congregations, the ones that had sprouted through Cinderhold in the last season like mold through bread.

Eleven years ago, Kestrel March had been the youngest sworn blade in the Order of the Broken Seal, the dragonslayers' brotherhood, and she had believed three things with her whole heart: that dragons were the enemy, that the Order's word was law, and that duty was a debt you paid forward until it killed you.

Then came the Caldera Compact, and the order's masters selling dragon-eggs to the temples of Vessia while telling their blades the clutches had died naturally, and Kestrel learning what duty looked like when you peeled back its skin. She'd spoken up. She'd spoken up loudly, in front of witnesses, with evidence. The Order had responded by stripping her rank, burning her name from the rolls, and sending her into the world with nothing but her sword, her scars, and a reputation as the traitor who loved lizards more than people.

So no, she didn't kill for churches anymore. Churches were just orders with better singing.

But two hundred crowns was two hundred crowns, and the witch was *in the city*, which meant Kestrel could at least find her, look at her, and decide whether the Congregation's money was worth the Congregation's smell. There was a version of this where she took the coin and vanished east before anyone could ask questions. She told herself that was the version she was considering.

She strapped on her sword. It was called Verity, because her father had named it when she was nine and she'd never had the heart to rename it, even after it stopped meaning anything. She pulled on her coat — grey wool, patched at the elbows, the Order's crest torn off the shoulder so long ago the holes had become part of the design — and went out into the morning.

* * *

Cinderhold burned brighter every day. That wasn't a metaphor; the city had always been named for its foundries, but lately the light coming off it seemed wrong, too golden, too warm, like the sun itself had started taking sides.

Temples stood where taverns used to be. Kestrel counted four on her walk down the Foundry Road alone — white stone, unweathered, built fast and built loud, each one crowned with the same emblem: the flame in the cupped hands. Pilgrims clogged the gutters, kneeling in the filth to pray, and here was the thing that made the hair on her arms stand up: they were being *answered*. She'd seen a woman's withered hand straighten mid-prayer outside the third temple, fingers uncurling like ferns in springtime. She'd seen the crowd around that woman weep and claw at each other to touch the hem of her dress. She'd seen the woman herself, afterward, sitting alone in an alley with an expression Kestrel recognized from funerals.

Miracles, it turned out, had a cost, and the cost was written on the faces of the saved.

Kestrel kept her hood up and asked her questions quietly. The gate-witch wasn't hard to find. Half the city was talking about her — the stranger who'd come out of nowhere with a priest, the one whose wrist bore the old sigil, the one the Congregation said had come to *steal the prayers*. Fear made its own maps. By noon she knew the witch was lodging at the Wayfarer's Rest near the east gate, that she traveled with a fugitive of the Vessian clergy named Oren, and that she'd been seen at the ash-market buying dried meat in quantities that suggested a long road ahead.

Which meant they were leaving soon. Which meant if Kestrel was going to look this woman in the face, today was the day.

She told herself she wanted to see what kind of person was worth two hundred crowns to a church. She told herself a lot of things. Eleven years of practice had made her very good at it.

* * *

The Wayfarer's Rest smelled of tallow and wet dog, which put it a full class above most establishments Kestrel frequented. She took a table in the corner with her back to the wall and ordered the cheapest thing that came hot, and she waited, because waiting was half her trade and the half she was still good at.

They came down at dusk. She spotted the priest first — Oren, the runaway cleric, hollow-cheeked and watchful, moving like a man who'd learned that crowds were weather and weather killed. Then the witch.

Sable Quintana was younger than Kestrel expected. That was the first wrong thing. Witch-hunters knew the type: the older ones had a hardness around the eyes, a way of holding their bodies like fortresses. This one moved like a girl who'd grown up in a valley where nothing had ever reached for her — until recently, apparently, because there was a flinch in her now, a constant low-grade readiness, the look of someone whose whole world had broken open inside a month.

And on her left wrist, bare above a wrapped cuff, the sigil.

Kestrel felt it like a hook behind her sternum. She knew that mark. Every child raised in the Order knew that mark — it was chalked on training-yard walls, branded into doctrine, carved over the door of the hall where Kestrel had sworn her oaths at fifteen with her hand on cold iron and her future in her teeth. The Hollow Sigil. The mark of the First Sealing. The sign every dragonslayer learned to salute and never, ever question.

Except the Order had questioned it plenty, privately, in the years before Kestrel's fall. The Order had dug into what the gate really was, what fed it, what it cost — and then buried what it found and sold the eggs anyway. And here was the sigil walking around on the wrist of a farm-girl witch with a price on her head, wearing the same mark that had been burned off Kestrel's own records like a brand off stolen cattle.

The witch looked up. Their eyes met across the common room, and Kestrel saw her go still — saw her nudge the priest, saw the calculation happen, saw her do the bravest and stupidest thing available to her, which was to stand up and walk over.

"You've been watching us since the ash-market," Sable Quintana said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles weren't. "If you're from the Congregation—"

"I wouldn't wear this coat to a stoning." Kestrel tipped her head at the torn shoulder, the missing crest. "Sit down before you make a scene, gate-witch. I'm deciding something, and you're going to help me decide it."

Oren drifted close, hands visible, a cleric's old habit of de-escalation worn thin. "Deciding what?"

"Whether you're worth two hundred crowns dead." Kestrel let that land. Let the color leave the girl's face. Then she slid the writ from her coat and laid it on the table between them, face up, so they could read their own murder in a clerk's neat hand. "That's what your life costs in Cinderhold right now. I want you to understand that I know. And I want you to answer one question honestly, because liars are the only thing I kill for free."

Sable stared at the writ. Then at her. "What question?"

Kestrel reached across the table, slow, telegraphed, and turned the girl's wrist gently until the sigil faced the lamplight. Up close it was worse — not inked but *set*, the lines faintly raised, like a scar that had been there since birth and only recently learned its shape.

"The Order of the Broken Seal," Kestrel said. "Who marked you? Who gave you the Hollow Sigil?"

"No one living," Sable said.

Something in Kestrel's chest went very quiet and very cold. Because there was one other way to bear that mark, one way the old texts whispered about and the Order denied in public and researched in private, and it involved bloodlines, and it involved the gate, and it involved the specific kind of sacrifice that the Order's masters had spent eleven years pretending hadn't happened.

Kestrel laughed. One note, no humor. "No," she said. "They never do."

* * *

They talked for three hours, and by the end of it Kestrel had drunk nothing at all, which was its own kind of miracle, and hated how much she believed them, which was a different kind entirely.

The priest told it first — the answered prayers, the god feeding on devotion, the dragon-fire guttering like a candle in a doorway. He talked like a scholar and flinched like a heretic, and everything he said matched the documents Kestrel had seen in the Order's vaults the night she'd blown her life apart. The witch filled in the rest — the valley, the seal, the undercrypt, the bloodline — in a flat, controlled voice that cracked exactly twice, and Kestrel recognized that voice too. It was the voice you used when the truth was too heavy to carry any way but carefully.

"So," Kestrel said finally, turning her cup in circles on the table. "Let me say this plainly, because I'm a plain woman. A god is eating the world's prayers. The dragons are dying because the gate is starving them. Your blood can change that, maybe, somehow, and the church wants your head for it. And you two are planning to walk east, into the ash-wastes, to find a dragon that may be dead already." She looked at Sable. "And you don't have a swordsman."

"We have each other," Oren said.

"That's sweet. It's also a eulogy." Kestrel leaned back. Here was the arithmetic she'd been doing all evening, the sum that wouldn't resolve. Two hundred crowns on one side. On the other side: the sigil, the Order's buried truths, the eggs sold to temples, the whole rotten ledger of her disgrace — and underneath all of it, the simple stupid fact that this girl wore the mark Kestrel had bled for and been betrayed by, and wore it like a wound she hadn't chosen.

Duty was a debt you paid forward. Her father's saying. She'd spent eleven years trying to default on it, and it had followed her into a tavern in Cinderhold and sat down at her table wearing a farm-girl's face.

"If I come with you," Kestrel said, "I'm not your friend. I'm not your protector. I'm settling an account. The Order ruined me for telling the truth about the gate, and every mile east is a coin off that debt. When it's paid, I walk. Understood?"

Sable studied her for a long moment — long enough that Kestrel felt catalogued, weighed, and unexpectedly not found wanting. "Understood," the witch said. Then, quieter, with something that wasn't quite a smile: "For what it's worth, I'm not a witch."

"You're wearing a sigil that makes churches nervous and gods hungry. You're a witch. Congratulations."

It earned her a real almost-smile, gone in a blink, and Kestrel looked away from it because it was dangerously close to warmth and she had a reputation to maintain with herself.

* * *

She was gathering her coat when the bells began.

Not the foundry bells — those rang in patterns, work-song rhythms every child of Cinderhold knew in their bones. These were temple bells, all of them at once, every congregation in the city ringing in unison, and beneath the bronze Kestrel heard something else: voices. Thousands of them, pouring into the streets, chanting a name she'd only heard whispered in the writ-room and the wine-shops.

*Vessia. Vessia. The Mother of Answers.*

She went to the window. Below, the street was filling — pilgrims first, then neighbors, then ordinary folk drawn out of their doors like iron filings after a lodestone, faces tilted up toward the great new temple on the hill, where a light was rising that had nothing to do with lamps or fire. It was gold and warm and it made Kestrel's scars ache the way a storm makes old breaks ache, and she thought, with the clarity that only arrived when trouble was already at the door: *she's here. The god herself, or close enough. And she knows the witch is in this city.*

Behind her, Oren had gone the color of tallow. "That's a summoning," he said. "That's a city-wide summoning. They'll sweep every lodging, every cellar—"

"They'll start with the places strangers sleep," Kestrel said. She slung Verity across her back and felt, for the first time in eleven years, like the sword and her purpose were pointing the same direction. "Back door. Now. And gate-witch — keep that wrist covered. Tonight, the whole city is looking for your mark."

Sable pulled her cuff down. In the window's strange golden light, the three of them moved for the rear of the inn as the chanting swelled, and Kestrel March understood, with the resignation of a woman who had never once won a bet against her own conscience, that the debt had just gotten larger.
