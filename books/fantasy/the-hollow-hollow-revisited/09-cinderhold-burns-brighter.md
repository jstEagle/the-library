## 9. Cinderhold Burns Brighter

The city had changed in three days.

Kestrel March noticed it the way a soldier notices a battlefield gone quiet — not with her eyes first, but with the skin at the back of her neck. Cinderhold had always been loud: the clang of the forges, the bawling of cattle driven through the Ashgate market, the eternal argument of the river barges below the terraces. That noise was still there. But underneath it now ran something else, something that made her hackles rise like a dog before a quake.

Singing.

Not tavern singing. Not work songs. Hymns — low, sweet, and everywhere, drifting from doorways and alley mouths and the open windows of houses whose owners used to curse the gods by name.

She stood on the balcony of the Gilded Wyrm's third floor, a cup of watered wine she had no intention of drinking sweating in her hand, and counted temples.

Three days ago there had been one shrine to Vessia in Cinderhold, tucked behind a tannery where the smell covered the incense. Now there were seven. She could see four of them from where she stood: white stone rising out of soot-black neighborhoods like teeth out of rotting gum, each one crowned with the same sigil — an open hand cradling a flame that burned downward, into the palm. Each one built fast. Too fast. The newest, down by the tanners' row, still had scaffolding on its western face, and yet she'd watched a hundred worshippers file inside at dawn, singing, stepping over the masons' abandoned tools as if the tools themselves had been blessed away.

Temples didn't rise overnight. Faith took years to build. Unless someone was doing the building *for* them.

"Drinking before noon again, March?" called the innkeeper's boy from the stairwell.

"Watering it," she said, and he laughed and clattered off, and Kestrel looked back at the white temples and felt the old familiar shape of dread settle over her shoulders like a yoke.

She had been paid half up front to kill a gate-witch. She had spent two days failing to do it — first because the witch had turned out to be a hollow-eyed girl with a brand on her wrist that Kestrel knew better than her own scars, and then because the priest traveling with her had looked at Kestrel across a tavern table and said, *You were Order of the Broken Seal once. I can see it in how you hold your left arm. Protecting the old wound.* And then he'd bought her dinner, which was more than her own order had ever done after they burned her name off the rolls.

She hadn't killed them. She hadn't warned them either, not properly. She'd told them the city wasn't safe, told them to move on, and then she'd walked back to the Gilded Wyrm and drunk herself stupid trying not to think about the sigil on Sable Quintana's wrist — the same sigil that had been branded onto the shoulders of every knight who ever served under Serah Vane, the same sigil Kestrel had carried for eleven years until the day the order decided she was worth less than the coin a rival house paid them to look away.

*Failed by duty twice over,* she thought. *Once when I kept it. Once when they cast me out for keeping it too well.*

Below, the hymn-singing swelled. A procession was forming in the market square — white-robed, faces serene, carrying candles that burned with a flame the wrong color, gold shot through with veins of violet. At their center walked a woman in gray, barefoot on cobblestones slick with forge-water, and the crowd parted around her like water around a keel.

Kestrel had seen that walk before. Not the woman — the *walk*. The unhurried certainty of someone who has never once needed to hurry because everything comes to her eventually.

She set down the wine. She went and got her sword.

* * *

Finding them was insultingly easy. They weren't hiding. They were in the temple district — the *temple district*, which three days ago had been a row of shuttered warehouses and was now a canyon of white stone and violet candlelight — standing in front of the largest of the new buildings, and Oren was arguing theology with a priestess while Sable Quintana stared up at the facade with an expression Kestrel recognized from mirrors: the look of someone reading their own death warrant written in a language they almost understood.

"—and I'm telling you," Oren was saying, his voice carrying that gentle, reasonable tone he used on everyone, the tone that made people underestimate him right up until he outmaneuvered them, "the Lady Vessia answers prayers, yes. But ask yourselves what happens to the prayers that go *unanswered*. Where does the difference go? Devotion isn't a well, sister. It's a wound. And something is—"

"The Lady does not take," said the priestess, smiling with perfect serenity. "The Lady gives."

"She gives the way a spider gives silk," Kestrel said, stepping out of the crowd.

Both of them turned. Sable's face did something complicated — relief, wariness, and beneath it, carefully buried, the flicker of hurt that said Kestrel's warning-and-disappearance had landed harder than intended. Good, some petty thing in Kestrel's chest noted. Let it land. Caring about people got you burned rolls and a shallow grave.

"Sister Mavelle," Kestrel said to the priestess, giving the white robes a lazy once-over. "Nice vestments. New?"

"The Lady provides," Mavelle said.

"I know the cut. It's Dragonslayer auxiliary issue, dyed and re-hemmed. Your whole choir is wearing my order's surplus." She let that sit. Around them, the crowd's singing had faltered into an uneasy murmur. "So here's my question, sister. When your Lady 'provides' — who's she providing *from*?"

Mavelle's smile didn't waver, but something behind her eyes did — a shutter closing, a calculation running. "You should come inside," she said warmly. "All of you. The Lady welcomes even the doubtful."

"She would," Kestrel said. "That's the whole problem."

She hooked a hand into Sable's sleeve and Oren's collar and walked them both out of the square, not hurrying, exactly, but moving with the particular pace of a woman who knows precisely how many seconds it takes armed men to reach a given doorway. Behind them the hymns resumed, sweeter than before, and Kestrel did not relax until they'd turned three corners and the white stone was out of sight.

"You came back," Sable said.

"Don't make it strange."

"You disappeared for two days."

"I was thinking." Which was true, though what she'd mostly been thinking was that she should leave Cinderhold alone and let the gate-witch and her priest dig their own graves, and that thought had lasted approximately as long as it took her to see the violet-veined candles. "And I was wrong to just warn you and walk off. So consider this me correcting the error. You need to be out of this city tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

"Why?" Oren asked. He wasn't offended; he was already working, she could see it, filing her urgency against everything he knew. "What have you seen?"

"Seven temples in three days. Congregations in the thousands. Priests wearing my order's castoffs, which means someone with access to Seal armories is funding this, which means either the order's gone over to Vessia or someone's wearing its corpse like a coat." She kept her voice flat, report-flat, the way she'd been trained. "And last night I watched a man pray at the tanners' shrine for his wife's fever to break. It broke. This morning he was at the shrine again, praying for his *neighbor's* wife. And the neighbor's wife died."

Oren went very still. "The Lady gives," he murmured, "and takes the difference."

"And here's the part that matters to you specifically." Kestrel stopped walking and turned to face Sable fully. Up close, the girl looked worse than she had two days ago — thinner, shadowed, like someone sleeping badly in a world that wouldn't stop whispering. "There's a price on you. Not the one I was offered — that was private, quiet money. This one's public. Posted at the Ashgate this morning. 'The gate-witch who bars the Lady's mercy.' Fifty crowns. There'll be a hundred by week's end, and by then it won't be sellswords coming for you. It'll be *congregations*. Every soul in this city who's had a prayer answered will want a piece of the woman who's keeping the rest of their wishes locked behind a valley wall."

Sable's hand drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the brand on her wrist. "They don't understand what opening it means."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "They understand what *not* opening it means. Their mother's fever. Their dead son. Their failed harvest. That's all they understand, and it's enough. Fear of the gods never built a mob, witch. Hope builds mobs. Hope with a body count."

* * *

They nearly didn't make the Ashgate.

The trouble started at the tanners' row, where the newest temple's scaffolding had become a pulpit. A preacher stood forty feet up on the planks, and his voice rolled over the neighborhood like weather: *"She hears you! She has ALWAYS heard you! While the dragons hoarded fire and the gate-keepers hoarded silence, SHE heard every prayer you wept into your pillow — and now she ANSWERS—"*

The crowd around the scaffolding was thick and joyful and utterly without malice, which was what made it dangerous. Kestrel had fought fanatics before, in the border wars; fanatics had edges, angles, things you could push against. This was worse. This was a thousand people simply, sincerely certain that the woman walking among them in a hooded cloak was the reason their prayers had weight but no wings.

A child saw Sable's face under the hood. Children always saw.

"*Witch!*" the child shrieked, with the pure delight of a kid spotting something forbidden, and the joy in the crowd curdled in the space of a single breath.

Kestrel moved before thinking, the way she'd been trained to, the way that had gotten her expelled — putting herself between the crowd and Sable, sword half-drawn, and Oren's hand closed on her wrist. "*No*," he breathed. "Steel makes martyrs. Walk."

They walked. The crowd flowed around them, closing, and the faces were terrible not because they were cruel but because they were kind — concerned, sorrowful, *compassionate*, a sea of neighbors wishing to save her soul whether she consented or not. Someone pressed a violet candle into Sable's hands. Someone else began to sing, softly, right beside her ear, a hymn about mercy, and Sable's step stuttered because — Kestrel saw it — the girl was listening for her mother's voice in it, or dreading that she'd find it.

Then the press of bodies ahead parted, and there stood Sister Mavelle, flanked by six of the faithful, blocking the lane to the Ashgate. Smiling.

"The Lady asks only one question of you, keeper," Mavelle said, and her voice carried impossibly, the way voices carry in dreams. "How many must die of unanswered prayer before your silence becomes the greater sin?"

And the awful thing — the thing Kestrel would chew on for weeks afterward — was that Sable hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. Long enough that Kestrel understood the offer had teeth, that somewhere in the girl's grief-starved heart the question had found purchase.

Then Oren stepped forward, into the space between Sable and the priestess, and said, mildly, "One more dies today if we don't clear this lane, sister. Yours, if your Lady permits it — mine, if she doesn't. Either way, the answer to your question is *one*. It's always one. That's how they get you." And he smiled his gentle, infuriating smile, and for a moment Mavelle's serenity cracked into something older and hungrier underneath, and the crowd gasped as one —

— and the temple bells began to ring.

Not alarm bells. *Vespers.* The whole city's new temples, ringing in unison, calling the faithful to evening prayer, and the crowd's attention shattered toward the sound like glass toward a hammer. In the sudden eddy, Kestrel grabbed a fistful of priest and gate-witch and hauled them through a tannery yard, over a fence reeking to the gods and back, down a drain-cut alley she remembered from a job five years ago, and out through a gap in the Ashgate's customs wall that the guards hadn't patched since the last time she'd bribed them not to.

They ran. All three of them, past the boundary markers, up the eastern road into the hills, until Cinderhold lay below them glowing — and Kestrel meant that literally. The city was *glowing*, seven white temples lit from within like lanterns, violet light bleeding up into the dusk, and the hymns audible even here, even at distance, a sound like a tide coming in.

Sable doubled over, hands on knees, breathing hard. Oren leaned against a milestone, laughing quietly — the slightly unhinged laugh of a man who has just realized how close the edge was.

"You could have taken the fifty crowns," Sable said to Kestrel, between breaths. "Easier than this."

"Easier, sure." Kestrel looked at the burning-bright city, then at the two fools wheezing beside her — a priest who argued with gods in market squares and a girl who carried a seal in her blood and a graveyard in her eyes. Her chest did something uncomfortable and unfamiliar, something adjacent to warmth. "But I've worked for people who pay in coin, and I've worked for causes that pay in ash, and I'm done with both. You two are paying in something else."

"What's that?" Oren asked.

"No idea yet. I'll let you know when it clears." She shouldered her pack and started up the road. "Come on. We've got maybe a day before the congregations organize, and I know where we're going."

Sable caught up, frowning. "Where?"

"There's a dragon left in this world. Half-dead, asleep in a caldera shrine three weeks' east. My order kept records on it — the last living flame, the thing holding the hunger back." Kestrel glanced back at the glow of Cinderhold, at the downward-burning flame on every white spire. "If anyone knows why your gate is failing and what happens when it breaks, it's the one creature old enough to remember the gate being built."

Behind them, the hymns rose with the dark, sweet and endless, and Kestrel March walked faster.
