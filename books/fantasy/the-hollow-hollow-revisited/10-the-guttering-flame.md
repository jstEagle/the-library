## 10. The Guttering Flame

The mountain had a heartbeat.

Oren felt it first as a tremor in the soles of his boots, three days out from Cinderhold, when the road gave up on switchbacks and became a goat track stitched into black rock. A slow pulse beneath the stone, patient as a sleeping man's breath. Kestrel felt it too — he saw her stop mid-stride, one hand pressed flat against the cliff face, her expression doing something complicated that she would later deny ever having done.

"That's not seismic," she said. "That's *her*."

"Ysolde," Sable said. She said the name the way other people said prayers.

They had climbed for two days through country that grew stranger with every mile. The ash-fall that had haunted the lowlands thinned here, but what remained of it lay in drifts against the rocks like grey snow, and where it lay, nothing grew. Where it did not lie, everything grew wrong — trees with bark like hammered bronze, grass that chimed faintly when the wind moved through it, a stream that ran uphill for thirty paces before thinking better of it. Magic pooled in this place the way water pools in a basin. Oren could feel it pressing against his skin, filling his mouth when he breathed, so thick that his old training — the seminary's careful walls of doctrine and silence — felt like a paper screen in a flood.

He had been a priest of the Quiet Order for twenty-two years. He had spent those years teaching that magic was a wound in the world, that the gods were dead or dreaming, that prayer was a discipline of the self and never a transaction. He had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold your weight: completely, without thinking about it.

Then the prayers had started being answered. And now he was climbing a volcano to wake the last dragon in creation, and the floor was gone, and he was falling, and the only thing he knew for certain was the girl walking ahead of him with a sigil branded into her wrist that he had seen once before, carved into the oldest wall of the oldest crypt beneath his own seminary, in a place the brothers called the Foot of the First Question.

Sable Quintana walked like someone carrying something breakable. She had walked that way since they'd fled Cinderhold — since the temples, since the mob with candles, since Kestrel had put an axe through the temple door and told them all to run. But today there was something else under it. Today she kept glancing at the summit, and each time she did, her hand drifted to her wrist, thumb tracing the seal-mark without seeming to know it was doing it.

"You feel it," Oren said, catching up to her.

"I've felt it since we crossed the border." Her voice was low. "Like something's... listening. Not Vessia. Something older. Tired." She looked at him. "Is that what dragons are? Tired?"

Oren thought about lying to her. He was getting better at noticing when he wanted to; that was one thing the road had taught him. "I don't know," he said. "I've never met one. None of us have. That's rather the point of them."

Ahead, Kestrel snorted. "I've met one." She didn't turn around. "From a distance. At Emberfall, the year they sent my order to cull the wyrm broods. You don't meet a dragon, priest. You stand in a field while the sky decides whether you're worth the trouble." Her hand went to the axe at her hip — not the temple-door axe anymore; she'd traded up in Cinderhold, a proper war-axe with a haft wrapped in dragon-hide leather, which Oren suspected was either a joke or a penance, and knowing Kestrel March, both. "Most days the sky decides no."

"And you?" Sable asked quietly. "What did the sky decide?"

Kestrel walked on for ten paces before answering. "It decided I was better used broken." And then, because that was all the honesty she permitted herself per day: "Keep moving. We're losing light."

* * *

They made camp in the throat of an old lava tube, and Oren took first watch, sitting at the tube's mouth with his back against warm stone, watching the caldera glow above them like a banked hearth. It wasn't firelight. He'd spent enough years around sacred fires to know the difference. This light moved wrong — it breathed. Long slow swells of amber rising from the crater's rim and sinking again, in time with the pulse he'd felt in the rock.

Behind him, Sable wasn't sleeping. He heard her turn over, and turn again, and finally give up and come sit beside him, wrapping her cloak around her knees.

"Tell me about her," she said. "Ysolde. What does the doctrine say?"

Oren almost laughed. "The doctrine says dragons are instruments of the Adversary, that their fire is the last corruption of the elder world, and that the saints drove them beyond the sea for the good of mortal kind." He rubbed his eyes. "The doctrine also says the Hollow Gate was built by righteous kings to imprison the old gods forever, and I watched a woman walk out of that valley who carries its lock in her blood. So perhaps we can agree the doctrine and I are no longer on speaking terms."

"What do *you* say?"

He considered the breathing light. "I say that everything I was taught about the gate was written by people who needed it to be a wall. Walls are simple. You defend them, you trust them, you sleep well behind them. But I've read the oldest liturgies, Sable — the ones the Quiet Order keeps locked because they don't scan right, don't rhyme right, don't *mean* right. And in the oldest ones, the gate isn't called a wall." He hesitated. "It's called a mouth."

Sable went very still beside him.

"A mouth feeds," she said.

"Yes."

Above them, the caldera pulsed. Once. Twice. Like something counting.

"We should sleep," Oren said, and neither of them slept.

* * *

The last stretch of the climb took them through the shrine.

Oren hadn't known to expect a shrine. But as the dawn came up grey and gold behind the eastern peaks, the goat track delivered them onto a broad shelf of rock just below the crater rim, and there it was: a ring of standing stones, each one taller than three men, each one carved — no, Oren corrected himself, staring — each one *grown* into shape, the way a tree grows around a fence wire. Dragons coiled up their lengths in relief, wings folded, heads bowed inward toward the center of the ring, where a shallow bowl of black glass held the ashes of what might have been ten thousand offerings. Feathers. Bones. Whole skeletons of goats and deer, arranged with care. And newer things: soldiers' tags strung on wire. A child's shoe. Letters, hundreds of letters, weighted down with stones, their ink bleached by weather but their words still legible if you knelt close enough.

*Please. Please. Please.* The same word over and over, in a hundred hands, across fifty years.

"People have been praying here," Sable said softly. She knelt at the edge of the bowl and lifted one letter, turned it toward the light. "Since the gate closed. Since the miracles stopped." Her thumb moved across the faded ink. "'Holy Ysolde, keeper of the last flame, my daughter is sick and the priests say only dragon-fire—'" She stopped. Set the letter down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something that might go off.

"They came here because everywhere else stopped working," Kestrel said. She stood at the ring's edge, arms crossed, not entering. Oren noticed she hadn't crossed the line of stones. "And then the answers started coming from somewhere else. From *her*." She spat, offhandedly, in the general direction of the whole southern sky, where Vessia was. "You can watch it happen in Cinderhold. Temple fills up, shrine empties. Why wait fifty years for a dragon to maybe wake up when a pretty lady in white will heal your kid *today*, no questions, just bring her your Sundays?"

"Did anyone get healed here?" Sable asked. "At the shrine. Ever?"

Kestrel was quiet a moment. "My order's records say Ysolde answered three petitions in the last century. Three. Each one cost her a year of sleep, they said. Each one burned a piece of her hoard — her *fire*." She looked up at the crater. "Dragons aren't stingy, priest. That's the lie my order told so we'd feel righteous killing the broods. They're spendthrifts. Every miracle is money out of a purse that doesn't refill. And hers—" she nodded at the mountain, at the dying pulse of it, "—hers has been emptying for four hundred years."

Oren looked at the letters. At the child's shoe. At the three miracles bought with three years of an ancient life, and the thousands of prayers that had gone unanswered not because they were unheard but because the purse was empty, and felt something in his chest crack along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"Then we shouldn't wake her," he said, and heard his own voice come out strange. "If waking her costs her—"

"She's already awake," said the mountain.

* * *

Later, Oren would try to describe the voice and fail. It was not sound, exactly. It arrived the way heat arrives from an open oven door — through the stone, through the soles of the feet, through the teeth. The standing stones rang with it, a low harmonic that Oren felt in his sternum, and the ash in the offering bowl stirred and rose in a slow spiral, and the breathing light above the crater rim flared bright as noon.

Then the far side of the caldera opened one golden eye the size of a chapel window.

She had been there the whole time. That was the thing Oren's mind kept snagging on, afterward — that the entire mountainside they had climbed, the ridges and the scree fields and the shelf of the shrine itself, had been *her*, scaled in grey and ember-dark, folded so perfectly into the landscape that the eye slid off her like rain off oilcloth. As she moved — and the movement was less a movement than a geological event, tectonic plates rearranging themselves with intent — Oren understood that the mountain's heartbeat had never been a metaphor. It had been her heart. They had been walking up a living creature for two days, and it had let them.

Ysolde raised her head over the crater rim, and the dawn bent around her horns.

"*Little thief,*" she said, and Oren realized with a lurch that she was looking at Kestrel. "*You smell of the order that unmade my children. You carry their steel. Come closer, and we will see how fast a dragonslayer can pray.*"

Kestrel did not come closer. She also did not run, which Oren suspected was the braver of the two options available to her. "I carried their steel for nineteen years," she said. "I buried it in a bog outside Emberfall the day I walked. If you want a debt collected, lizard, collect it from the ones still wearing the badge."

"*Lizard.*" The eye narrowed, and the humor in it — and there was humor, ancient and dry as the ash in the bowl — was somehow more terrifying than rage would have been. "*Four hundred years, and still they send me the honest ones last.*" The great head swung, ponderous, until the golden gaze settled on Sable.

Everything in Oren screamed to step between them. He did not. Some instinct older than his training told him that stepping between a dragon and Sable Quintana would be like stepping between a river and the sea.

"*Ah,*" Ysolde said. Softly. Terribly. "*There you are. Little anchor. Little lock. I wondered which of you the gate would spit out when it began to starve.*"

"You know what I am," Sable said. Not a question. Her voice shook, but she held the dragon's gaze, and Oren saw her hand stop reaching for her wrist and instead close around it, gripping the sigil like a hilt.

"*I helped build it,*" Ysolde said. "*Come inside, keeper's daughter. All of you. What must be said must be said once, and I have not the fire to say it twice.*"

* * *

Inside meant down — down a stair cut into the caldera's inner wall, into a cavern where the walls were veined with channels of dim gold light, like veins around a failing heart. The air was hot and sweet and old. In the cavern's center, half-buried in centuries of her own shed scales, lay the hoard: not gold, Oren realized, though there was gold, but *fire*. Coals the size of houses, banked and dimming, each one pulsing faintly, and each pulse a little weaker than the last. He counted the rhythm without meaning to. It was slowing. He could hear it slowing, the way you can hear a clock winding down in a silent room.

Ysolde curled herself around the hoard the way a mother curls around a child, and regarded them across the embers.

"*Ask,*" she said.

"The gate," Oren said. His voice sounded very small in the vast dark. "The Quiet Order teaches it was built to imprison the old gods. To keep them out of the world."

"*Your order teaches many comfortable things.*" A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils, red-edged, guttering. "*Tell me, priest. When your brothers built their walls around their little truths — did they ever wonder why a prison needs to be fed?*"

Oren's mouth went dry. "Fed."

"*A god is hunger given a name,*" Ysolde said. "*Vessia. Ur-Moloch. The Choir Below. They do not eat bread. They eat devotion — belief, prayer, love, the small daily worship of small daily lives. Before the gate, they fed freely, and the world was a table, and mortals were the meal. Your ancestors could not kill them. Hunger cannot be killed; it can only be starved, and starving a god takes an age, and an age is a long time for a mortal world to survive unguarded.*" The golden eye blinked, slow. "*So they built a mouth instead of a wall. The Hollow Gate does not keep the gods out, little priest. It keeps them *in* — sealed in the hollow places, in the spaces between, where the only sustenance that reaches them is what leaks through the seal. A trickle. A ration. Enough to keep them dormant, not enough to let them wake. The gate was never a prison.*"

"*It was a feeding trough,*" Kestrel said flatly. "*A slow drip. Keep them sedated on scraps so they never get hungry enough to break out.*"

"*Now the slayer understands.*" There was no pleasure in it. "*And what happens, little ones, when the drip slows? When the seal grows thin, because the blood that anchors it grows scarce, and the prayers of a desperate world come hammering at the other side like fists on a door?*" She lowered her head, and the light of the dying hoard moved across features that might once have been beautiful in the way mountains are beautiful. "*The gods begin to taste the feast waiting outside. And they begin to answer. A miracle here. A healing there. Devotion poured out like wine, and every drop seeps back through the cracks and fattens them for the day the seal fails entirely. Your Vessia is not invading your world, children. She is *being invited*, one answered prayer at a time, by every soul who kneels at her altars.*"

Silence, except for the slowing pulse of the hoard.

"And the dragons," Sable said. Her voice was steady. Oren marveled at it. "Dragon-fire. The doctrine says it's corruption. But it's not, is it. It's the other thing. The thing the gate was cutting off."

Ysolde regarded her for a long moment. "*Your mother asked the same question, standing where you stand,*" she said, and Sable made a small sound, and Oren's heart broke a little for both of them. "*Dragon-fire is the elder flame — the first fire, the one that predates the gods, that burns without consuming because it does not feed on anything. It is the only force in creation a god cannot eat. It is the only thing they fear. When the gate closed, the flow of magic between the hollow places and the living world was severed, and we who live in the living world began to starve alongside the gods we starved. Four hundred years, and my fire has eaten nothing new. I bank it. I ration it. I answer three prayers a century and hate myself for the extravagance.*" The great head dipped lower. "*I am the last, keeper's daughter. The others went to sleep and did not wake, or flew into the deep places to die where the order could not mount their skulls on walls. What you see around you is the final reserve. And it is nearly spent.*"

"How nearly?" Kestrel asked.

"*Soon,*" Ysolde said, "*you will be able to count the pulses between my heartbeats. And when the last dragon-fire gutters out, there will be nothing left in the world that hunger fears. The seal will fail — soon after, or long after, but certainly — and the gods will rise from their hollow places fat with four centuries of rationed devotion and a starving world's desperate love, and there will be no one left to tell them no.*"

The words settled over the cavern like ash. Oren found he was looking at the floor, at the shed scales, at the small perfect architecture of a dying species, and thinking of the seminary crypt, of the carvings no one had been allowed to study, of twenty-two years spent guarding a truth that was itself a guard, set in place by people who had decided that a comfortable lie was kinder than a survivable truth.

"So that's it," Sable said. "Open the gate fully, and the dragons live — the magic comes back, the fire refills. And the gods come through with it." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "Or keep it shut, and starve them forever, and starve everything else with them. Those are the choices. Feed the hunger or kill the world."

"*Those,*" Ysolde agreed, "*are the choices your ancestors left you. They were cowards with good intentions, which is the most dangerous combination flesh produces.*" A pause. The hoard pulsed. Slower. "*Although.*"

Oren's head came up. "Although?"

The golden eye turned to him, and then to Sable, and something moved behind it — something old and careful, the look of a creature deciding how much truth a fragile vessel could hold before it cracked.

"*Your mother stood in this cavern eleven months before she died,*" Ysolde said. "*She asked me a question, at the end, that I have been turning over ever since, the way one turns a stone that does not sit right. I told her I did not know the answer. That was a lie, keeper's daughter, and I am old enough now, and tired enough, that I find I have no appetite left for lying to the last person in the world whose blood is the lock.*"

Sable stepped forward. "Tell me."

Outside, far to the south, so faint it might have been imagination, the sky flickered — a pulse of white-gold that did not belong to any dawn. Kestrel saw it. Oren saw her see it, and saw her hand close on her axe.

Ysolde drew one long breath, and the whole cavern breathed with her, and the dying coals flared once, briefly, like a candle deciding to matter.

"*The gate opens both ways,*" she said. "*But a gate is not the only thing that can stand in a doorway.*"

And far below them, deeper than the hoard, deeper than the roots of the mountain, something knocked — three slow blows, patient as water through stone — and Ysolde's head snapped toward the sound, and for the first time in four hundred years, the last dragon in the world looked afraid.
