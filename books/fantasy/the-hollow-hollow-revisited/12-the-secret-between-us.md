## 12. The Secret Between Us

The road out of Cinderhold smelled of incense and rot, and Kestrel March had learned, in three days of walking, to tell them apart by the weight they left in her throat.

Incense was the temples' doing — Vessia's temples, white-walled and quick as mushrooms after rain, rising in the burned lots and abandoned granaries of every town between the border and the mountains. The rot was everything else. The answered prayers. A village where every well gave sweet water and every child woke screaming from dreams that were not their own. A farmstead where the wheat stood tall and golden and whispered names when the wind moved through it, and the farmer who'd prayed for a good harvest now slept with wax in his ears and a sickle under his pillow.

Kestrel had seen enough miracles in her life to know what they cost. She'd been paid in them once.

She walked at the rear of their small company, half a dozen paces behind Oren and Sable, and she watched Sable's back the way she used to watch a dragon's wings — for the tremor before the strike.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation shrine, one of the old roadside chapels the dragonslaying orders had maintained back when dragons needed maintaining and gods knew better than to answer. The roof was mostly gone. The altar stone remained, cracked down the middle, and someone — decades ago, by the moss — had carved a sigil into its face.

Kestrel saw it first because she'd been trained to see it: a circle broken by a vertical line, the line crowned with three descending bars.

The mark of the Order of the Broken Seal.

Her order. Her ruin.

"Kestrel." Oren's voice, low. He'd followed her gaze. "You don't have to—"

"I know what it is." She crouched beside the altar, running her thumb along the carved bars. The stone was cold in a way stone shouldn't be, even in autumn. "I cut this same mark into my own skin when I took my vows. Twenty-two years old, drunk on ceremony, certain I was joining the only honest war left in the world."

"And now?" Sable asked, from the doorway.

Kestrel didn't turn. "Now I know we weren't fighting a war. We were keeping a ledger. And somebody upstairs decided the numbers didn't matter anymore."

She said it lightly. She did not feel light. Because here was the thing she hadn't told them — the thing she'd been carrying since Cinderhold like a stone sewn into her coat lining:

In Cinderhold, in the temple district, in the great white basilica that had swallowed the old dragonslaying hall whole, Kestrel had found the archives. Not all of them. The zealots had burned most of what the order kept about the gate — but fire is a sloppy librarian, and Kestrel had spent two nights picking through the charred remnants of the sealed stacks while the faithful sang hymns forty feet above her head.

And she had found a folio. Thin. Water-stained. Sealed with the Broken Seal itself.

Inside, in the cramped hand of some long-dead quartermaster of the faith, was an inventory. Ritual components. Quantities. And a single line, underlined twice in faded red ink, that Kestrel had read eleven times and still felt in her teeth:

*The anchor's blood, freely given and fully spilled, does not seal the gate. It opens it. All the way. Both ways.*

Both ways.

That was the sentence that had been sitting in Kestrel's chest for four days, pressing on her lungs every time Sable laughed at one of Oren's terrible jokes, every time Sable shared out the bread without being asked, every time Sable looked at Kestrel with that open, unguarded trust that said: *you are one of us now.*

Because Kestrel understood what the line meant. She'd spent enough years around dragon-lore to understand it. The gate didn't just hold the old gods behind it. It held the dragons too — or rather, it held the source of dragon-fire, the deep well from which all dragonflame drew, the fire that was guttering now because the gate, aging, failing, starved of its anchor's blood, was pinching that well shut. Ysolde wasn't dying of old age. She was dying of thirst, a hundred miles upstream from a dam.

Sable's blood, spilled freely and fully, would burst the dam.

It would flood the world with fire again. Wake the starving dragons. Fill Ysolde's lungs with flame until she blazed like the dawn of creation.

And it would tear the gate wide open, and Vessia and her hungry siblings would come through it like a tide through a broken seawall, and everything — the valley, Cinderhold, the whispering wheat, the screaming children, all of it — would drown.

Free the dragons. Unleash the gods. The same act. One key, two doors, no way to turn it halfway.

And Sable Quintana — stubborn, kind, self-sacrificing Sable, who had already crossed the boundary of the only home she'd ever known to save strangers, who had already learned her mother died becoming part of the gate — Sable would do it. Kestrel had known her barely two weeks and she knew it absolutely. If Sable learned that her blood could save the dragons, she would give it. Freely. Fully. Gladly. She'd probably apologize to whoever had to clean up afterward.

Which meant Kestrel had a choice, and the choice was this: tell the truth and let Sable decide, or keep the secret and find another way.

There was no other way. That was the problem. Kestrel had turned the folio over in her mind a hundred times looking for one — a partial opening, a controlled release, some clever slayer's trick to siphon the well without breaking the dam. There was nothing. The old order had known it too; that was why the folio was sealed. That was why, she suspected, the order had fallen apart in the first place, why its grandmasters had scattered and its halls had burned: because somewhere, generations ago, they had realized the truth about the gate and could not bear it, and the truth had rotted them from inside like fruit around a worm.

*Keeper's blood opens both ways.* The order hadn't been destroyed by dragons or by gods. It had been destroyed by arithmetic.

And now Kestrel March, disgraced, drunk-for-most-of-a-decade, failed keeper of that same rotten arithmetic, held the numbers again. And she was doing the exact thing that had broken her order.

She was hiding them.

* * *

"You're quiet," Sable said, later, when Oren had taken first watch and the fire had burned down to a red heart of coals.

Kestrel looked up. Sable sat across the coals from her, wrapped in her threadbare valley cloak, her wrist — the branded sigil, the keeper's mark — resting on her knee. In the firelight the brand looked almost fresh. Almost wet.

"I'm always quiet," Kestrel said. "You've met me."

"You're quiet *differently* tonight." Sable's eyes were steady. She had a way of looking at people that made lying feel like a physical act — like lifting something heavy with your bare hands while they watched. "Back in Cinderhold you disappeared for two nights. Oren thought you were drinking. I told him you wouldn't."

"Glad one of us has faith."

"Where were you?"

Kestrel weighed the lie. It came easily, which frightened her more than the truth did. "The archive halls. My old order's records. I wanted to see if there was anything about the gate we could use — a ritual, a reforging, anything." That much was true. "There wasn't. Just ash and hymnals." That much was not.

Sable nodded slowly. Something flickered across her face — not suspicion, exactly. Worry. "Kestrel. Whatever you found. Or didn't find. You'd tell me? If it mattered?"

The coal-light popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called, three notes falling like stones down a well.

Kestrel thought about the folio hidden in the false bottom of her pack, wrapped in oilcloth, riding against her spine like a second knife. She thought about the red-inked line. She thought about her grandmaster, Aldous Vane, standing in the ruins of the order's council hall twenty years ago, telling his slayers the order was finished, telling them *we kept secrets to protect each other until the secrets ate us*, and then walking into the sea rather than explain further.

She thought about how much she loved — and the word arrived unbidden and unwelcome and true — how much she loved these two impossible people, and how love, in her experience, was just duty that had gotten personal, and duty that got personal always ended with someone holding a knife over someone else and calling it mercy.

"Yeah," Kestrel said. "If it mattered, you'd hear it from me first."

Sable smiled. It transformed her whole face, tired as it was. "Then we're fine."

*We're fine.* Kestrel held the smile until Sable rolled into her bedroll, and then she let it die, and she sat alone with the coals and the nightjar and the folio against her spine, and she understood with perfect clarity that she had just done the thing. The first cut. The one her grandmaster must have made, once, long ago, telling himself the same story she was telling herself now: *not yet, not like this, she deserves better than this truth, I'll find a gentler way.*

There was no gentler way. There was only the way, and the lie, and the distance growing between them like water seeping through stone.

She pulled her pack close and rested her hand on it, feeling the oilcloth's edge through the canvas. Tomorrow they'd reach the mountain roads. Three days after that, if the maps in her head still served, the caldera. Ysolde. The last dragon, sleeping herself to death above a well that Sable's blood could open — and that Kestrel's silence was helping to close.

Maybe that was the answer, she told the coals. Maybe the gate should stay shut. Maybe a world without magic was a fair price for a world without Vessia. Maybe the dragons were a lost cause and the kindest thing was to let them sleep forever and never tell the girl with the branded wrist that she was carrying the cure and the catastrophe in the same veins.

But she remembered Ysolde from the stories — the oldest living creature in the world, older than the order, older than the gate, who had burned back the gods' first hunger when the world was young. She remembered the dragonslayer's oath she'd sworn at twenty-two, drunk on ceremony: *the fire endures, and so do we.* She'd broken every other clause of that oath. She'd broken it so thoroughly they'd struck her name from the rolls and broken her sword across their knee in front of the assembled chapter.

But she had never stopped believing the fire should endure.

And it was going to gutter out, and she was going to be one of the last people alive who knew why, and she was going to have chosen her own comfort over the truth, and her own cowardice over the girl across the coals who trusted her.

The nightjar called again. Kestrel didn't sleep. She sat up through the small hours doing the arithmetic her order had died of, and every sum came out the same, and every sum required blood, and every blood was Sable's, and the only variable Kestrel controlled was how long she could stand to carry the secret before it ate through her like acid through a scabbard.

Near dawn, Sable stirred and murmured something in her sleep — a name, maybe, or a prayer. Kestrel caught only the shape of it.

She tightened her grip on her pack, and told herself she'd find a third way.

She was starting to understand that her order had probably told themselves that too.
