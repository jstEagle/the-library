## 14. What Maren Never Said

*The first letter was never meant to be sent.*

Maren wrote it the night after she pulled the infant from the boundary stones, her hands still smelling of ash and milk and something older, something like lightning trapped under ice. She wrote it by tallow-light while the baby slept in a basket that had been meant for turnips, and when she finished she read it once, wept once, folded it once, and pushed it beneath the loose flagstone by her hearth where she kept the things that could not be allowed to exist.

She did not know yet that there would be more letters. She only knew that if she did not write the truth down somewhere — somewhere outside her own skull, which grief was already rearranging like furniture in a dark room — she would wake one morning believing her own lie completely, and then no one in all the world would remember what had actually happened at the Hollow Gate.

*Someone has to remember,* she wrote. *Even if it is only paper.*

---

*To whoever finds this, or to no one, or to me —*

*The woman who died at the gate tonight was named Elira Quintana. She came out of the mist at moonrise carrying a child against her chest, and she was already burning. Not with fire. With light. It came off her skin like heat off a forge, and where she walked the grass silvered and died, and she said — she said, "The gate needs an anchor and I am done being one. Take my daughter. Raise her away from all of this. Let her be ordinary. Promise me."*

*I promised. God forgive me, I promised before I even understood what I was promising.*

*She pressed the child into my arms and walked back into the seal-light and the valley forgot her name before her body finished coming apart. That is the way of this place. The valley forgets. By morning no one will remember a woman named Elira ever lived here, because the gate takes memory as payment, always has, and everyone will believe the child is mine — a sister's daughter from over the mountains, dead of fever, poor thing.*

*I will believe it too, eventually. That's why I'm writing this. So that somewhere, in ink, it stays true:*

*Sable Quintana is not my niece. Her blood is the lock on the Hollow Gate. Her mother did not die protecting the valley. Her mother died *becoming* it.*

*And I let her walk back in, because she asked, and because the child was crying, and because I am a coward who loves children and cannot bear to watch them starve.*

*Maren*

---

* * *

The second letter came eleven years later, written fast, in the hand of a woman whose knuckles had begun to ache in cold weather.

*Sable asked me today why she doesn't look like me. She's eleven and sharp as a thorn and she stood in my kitchen with flour on her nose and said, "Aunt Maren, your eyes are brown and Da's were brown and mine are gray as gate-mist, and nobody in this whole valley has gray eyes, so where did I get them?"*

*I told her from her mother. Which is true, and a lie, both at once, the way most true things are in this valley.*

*She said, "What was she like?"*

*I said, "Brave," and went back to kneading bread so I would have an excuse to hit something.*

*Here is what I did not say: Your mother was like the moment before a storm breaks. Here is what I did not say: You have her hands exactly, and when you're angry they do the same twitching thing at the fingers, and every time I see it I have to sit down. Here is what I did not say: The gate sigil on your wrist isn't a birthmark, child, it's a brand, it's a *lock*, and you are the key wearing itself as skin, and I have spent eleven years praying you never ask the question whose answer would unmake the ordinary life I swore on your mother's ashes to give you.*

*I gave her jam instead. She rolled her eyes. Eleven-year-olds are merciful that way. They accept jam as an answer to everything.*

---

* * *

There were years when Maren wrote nothing. Years when the lie sat comfortable as an old boot, when Sable was apprenticed to the wardens, when the girl took up the tending of the Hollow Gate with a solemnity that made Maren's chest hurt in a way she could not name without opening the flagstone and reading what was underneath.

Then came the year of the dry summer, and the letter she wrote shaking.

*Sable found the first prayer today.*

*She came home white as salt and said, "It whispered my name, Aunt Maren. Through the seal. Nothing gets through the seal." And I held her hands — those hands, her mother's hands — and told her it was the wind, it was nothing, it was the valley settling.*

*But I knew. Oh, I knew. Because I have kept one other secret besides yours, Sable, and it is this: your mother did not go quietly into that light. In her last moments, before the seal took her, she looked at me over your head and said, "If it ever starts to fail — if the prayers start getting through — don't let her fix it. Don't let her learn what she is. A keeper who doesn't know she's a key can't be used as one."*

*So now the prayers are whispering your name through a failing seal, and I am sitting in my kitchen doing the arithmetic of cowardice, and the sum keeps coming out the same: either I tell you the truth and hand you to whatever is knocking, or I keep lying and pray the seal holds longer than my silence does.*

*I have never been good at praying. I promised your mother I'd give you an ordinary life. I intend to keep that promise if it kills us both.*

---

* * *

* * *

Twenty-two years of letters lay beneath the flagstone, and Sable Quintana read them all in a single night in a safehouse above a dyer's shop in Cinderhold, by the light of a lamp Kestrel had stolen from a temple of Vessia, which Kestrel considered a fitting use for it.

But this chapter belongs to Maren, and Maren was four hundred miles away, and on the night her daughter read the truth, Maren was standing in her kitchen holding a courier's intercepted satchel and understanding, with terrible clarity, how the truth had traveled.

---

The courier had come at dusk, one of the new ones, the ones with the too-bright eyes and the small gold suns pinned at their collars. Vessia's faithful were everywhere now, even seeping into the valley's trade road like water finding cracks. He'd handed over the parcel with a smile that was a half-beat too warm and said, "From a well-wisher. For the keeper's mother."

Inside the oilcloth: a bundle of letters. Not copies. *Originals.* Every letter Maren had ever written and hidden beneath her hearthstone, tied with the same twine, smelling faintly of her own smoke.

And a note, in handwriting like spilled honey:

*You hid them so well, Mother Quintana. But faith hears everything, and our lady eats secrets the way she eats prayers. We thought you should have them back — and that the girl should have them too. Truth wants to be free. Our lady says so. Our lady gives people what they want.*

*Consider this a gift. Consider what she might give the rest of the world, if only someone would open the door wide enough.*

*— A friend of the hungry god*

Maren sat down. Her legs simply declined to hold her anymore, and she sat in her own kitchen, in the house where she had raised a stolen child into a keeper, and understood what had been done.

They hadn't stolen the letters to hurt *her*. They'd stolen them to hurt *Sable* — no. Worse. To *help* her. That was Vessia's whole terrible method; Oren had explained it, gasping, on the night he collapsed at the boundary stones: the old gods didn't break doors, they *gifted* keys. They answered wishes until the wisher opened the door themselves and called it their own idea.

Somewhere out there, past the mist, her daughter was reading twenty-two years of lies-by-omission, and every word of them was true, and every true word would land like a knife.

"She'll hate me," Maren said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the flagstone with its hollow underneath, to the ghost of a woman named Elira who had trusted her with the only precious thing she had.

The kitchen, being a kitchen, said nothing.

So Maren did what Maren had always done, which was the only thing she knew how to do. She got up. She packed bread and hard cheese and the good boots, and she took down her winter cloak though it was high summer, because she had decided, somewhere between sitting down and standing up, that she was done communicating with her daughter through paper.

Twenty-two years of letters. Twenty-two years of loving a child at arm's length through a floorboard. If Sable wanted to scream at her, she would stand there and take the screaming. If Sable never wanted to see her again, Maren would stand at the boundary stones until the mist closed behind her. But she would not let the last true conversation between them be conducted by courier, intercepted, weaponized, honey-handed strangers who served a thing that ate devotion and called it love.

"You don't get to have this," she told the note, and struck a match, and burned Vessia's gift-giving in the sink like the poison it was.

Then she wrote one more letter. Short. The shortest of her life. She left it on the table where any neighbor checking on the house would find it, addressed to no one, saying only:

*Gone after my daughter. If I'm not back, tell her the jam was always an apology.*

---

* * *

Four hundred miles away, Sable finished the last letter near dawn.

Kestrel found her on the roof of the safehouse with the bundle in her lap and her eyes red-rimmed and dry, which Kestrel had learned was worse than crying. Oren slept below, his wounds wrapped, his breathing thin but stubborn.

"Courier intercept?" Kestrel asked, dropping onto the shingles beside her. "Vessia's lot?"

"They delivered my whole childhood back to me like a returned parcel." Sable's voice was flat. "My mother didn't die protecting the gate. She died *becoming* it. And Maren knew. Twenty-two years, Kestrel. She tucked the truth under a floorboard and fed me jam."

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. Down in the street, temple bells rang the hour, and somewhere a crowd sang Vessia's name in sweet, starving harmony.

"My order did the same to me," Kestrel said finally. "Found out what they'd built me for on the worst day of my life, and every elder I'd loved had known all along. I burned their hall down. Didn't help. Turns out the fire doesn't care whose fault it was." She looked sideways. "You going to burn anything?"

"No." Sable turned the last letter over in her hands. "That's the worst part. I read them all, and every page is her choosing me. Over and over. Lying to my face for two decades because my mother asked her to, because she thought an ordinary life was the only life worth having." A breath that shook slightly at its edges. "I'm furious at her. And I understand her completely. Both at once. I didn't know you could fit both in one body."

"Family's chosen twice," Kestrel said. "Once by whoever grabs you first. Once by you, after you know everything. Second one's the real one."

Sable looked east, toward the mist-locked valley she could no longer enter, toward a woman who was, at that exact hour, lacing up good boots she hadn't worn since winter.

"She'll come," Sable said. "I know her. She'll come."

"And if Vessia's faithful follow her?"

"Then we'd better reach the Broken Seal's ruins before she reaches us," Sable said, standing, folding the letters away against her heart like armor made of paper. "Because I am not losing the woman who raised me to a god who thinks truth is a door prize."

Below them, Oren coughed himself awake, and the day began.
