## 16. Ritual of Ash and Salt

The ruins of the Order of the Broken Seal crouched in a fold of the Greymantle Hills like something that had knelt down to die and never finished.

Sable felt it before she saw it — a hush in the air, a pressure against her ears like descending into deep water. Magic had soaked into this place for centuries and then bled out all at once, and the land had never forgotten how it felt to be full. The stones remembered. She could feel them remembering, faint and mournful, the way an empty house remembers voices.

"Careful on the stairs," Oren said from behind her.

He was leaning on Kestrel's shoulder more than his own legs would allow. The wound in his side — Vessia's zealots had put a consecrated knife there, a blade blessed so thoroughly with answered prayers that it burned like dragon-fire — had stopped bleeding only because Oren had packed it with ash from Ysolde's scales and prayed over it with words that were not quite prayers. He was gray around the mouth. Every breath cost him something he could not spare.

"I'm always careful," Sable said.

"No," Kestrel muttered. "You're really not."

They came down through what had been the order's cloister, its arches broken at uneven heights, its courtyard floor cracked into a mosaic no one could read anymore. Weeds grew in the seams — but not ordinary weeds. These had grown pale and strange in the old magic, silver-veined, shivering without wind. Salt crusted everything. The sea was forty miles east, but salt lay on these stones as if the ocean had once come here specifically to mourn.

"The refectory," Oren said, pointing with his chin toward a low building whose roof had half fallen in. "The ritual chamber is beneath it. The order kept it hidden even from most of its own members. Only the anchors and their seconds were permitted below."

"And you?" Sable asked. "You were permitted?"

Oren smiled, and it hurt him to do it. "I was a priest who asked too many questions about why our gods needed feeding. They let me sweep the chamber. That was my punishment." He paused at the top of the stairs, one hand flat against the wall, gathering himself. "It was also my education."

The stairwell spiraled down into the earth, and the temperature dropped with every turn. Sable counted ninety-one steps. At the bottom, a door of blackened iron stood ajar, swollen in its frame, and beyond it —

She stopped breathing.

The chamber was round, perhaps thirty paces across, and its walls were carved floor to ceiling with names. Tens of thousands of them, in scripts she did not know and some she almost did, spiraling upward into shadow. In the center of the floor, a ring of white stone had been set into the rock — a perfect circle, wide enough for three people to stand within — and around that ring ran channels cut into the stone, dry now, waiting, their purpose unmistakable.

Ash channels. And beside them, narrower ones, already dusted white with ancient salt.

"It's a hearth," Sable whispered. "It's built like a hearth."

"Everything the old orders built was a hearth or a cage," Kestrel said. "Usually both." She eased Oren down onto a bench carved into the wall, and he went gratefully, folding like a man setting down a weight carried too far. "Where do we start, priest? You swept this place. You must have memorized every crack."

Oren closed his eyes. When he spoke, it was in the cadence of recitation, words worn smooth by repetition.

"The Ritual of Ash and Salt. To reforge a seal upon the Hollow Gate. It requires four things: the blood of the anchor line, freely given. The flame of a living dragon, freely lent. The salt of faithful tears — not grief alone; grief is common. Faithful tears. Tears shed by someone who believes the sacrifice will matter." He opened his eyes and looked at Sable, and she wished he hadn't. "And a willing anchor. A soul that consents, fully and without coercion, to be bound into the gate. Not killed by it. *Bound.* The gate does not take life. It takes everything else."

The silence after his voice was the loudest thing Sable had ever heard.

*Not killed by it. Bound.*

Her mother had been bound. Her mother had walked into a circle very much like this one, in a valley very far away, and consented, and the gate had folded her in like a page pressed shut in a book, and Sable had grown up sweeping her mother's grave-marker every morning without knowing that the marker was a courtesy, a lie told to a child, because you cannot bury someone who is still holding up the sky.

"How long," Sable said. Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of being proud. "How long does an anchor last?"

Oren didn't answer.

"Oren. How long did my mother last?"

"Twenty-two years," he said quietly. "The length of your life, Sable. She lasted exactly as long as she chose to. Anchors endure until they let go. Your mother let go when she knew you were safe — when she knew you'd never be called upon to follow her." He looked down at his ruined side. "I'm sorry. I've known since the sigil. I should have told you sooner. There was never a moment when telling you would have hurt less."

Kestrel had gone very still by the wall. Sable didn't look at her. Later she would understand that Kestrel had gone still because she had read this ritual in the order's stolen archives weeks ago, in some border-town inn, by candlelight, and had known since then what it required — and had said nothing. But that understanding was for later. In this moment there was only the chamber, and the ring of white stone, and the spiral of ten thousand names climbing the dark.

Sable walked to the edge of the circle and stood there, looking down.

"So it's me," she said. "It was always going to be me. My blood opens the gate, my blood seals it. Mother first, daughter after. A dynasty of doors." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Vessia offered me my mother back if I opened the gate. And all along, the price of saving everyone else was becoming her."

"Sable—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me there's another way. Don't tell me we'll find something in the texts. I'm done with hope as a strategy. Hope is how Vessia farms people." She turned to face them both. "We do this tonight, while Ysolde still has fire left to lend us and before Vessia finds this place. I give my blood. Ysolde gives her flame. Maren—" her voice caught on the name, and she pushed through it, "—Maren shed enough faithful tears at the boundary stones to salt a harbor, and I brought a vial of the ground where she stood. That leaves the anchor, and the anchor is me. So let's stop pretending otherwise and get to work."

* * *

They worked through the hours of dusk.

Oren directed from the bench, his voice growing thinner by the hour, and Sable marveled at him even as she grieved him — because he had memorized all of this, every step, during years of punishment-sweeping, storing it away like grain against a famine he'd always known was coming. He taught her the preparatory inscriptions. He taught her which channels took the salt and which took the ash. He taught her the words of invitation, spoken to the gate itself, because — this surprised her — the gate was not a thing. It was a listening. "You don't command a gate," he said. "You ask it. It has been asked so many terrible things. Be gentle with it."

Kestrel ground the salt and laid the lines. She worked in silence, jaw set, moving with the economy of a woman who had spent her life doing precise, unforgiving work — dragonslaying, it turned out, was mostly geometry and patience, and those skills transferred. Twice Sable caught her watching the anchor's circle with an expression Sable couldn't read, and twice Kestrel looked away first.

By full dark the chamber was ready. The channels gleamed. The inscriptions Oren had chalked along the walls had begun to glow faintly, responding to the proximity of Sable's blood — she had given a few drops to prime the sigils, and they drank it eagerly, hungry things, and she tried not to think about how hungry everything in this world was, gods and gates and seals alike.

"We need Ysolde's fire," Sable said. "How do we call her?"

"You don't," said Kestrel. "You wait. She said she'd come when she felt the priming. Dragon-fire senses its own kind of hunger." She checked the blade at her hip out of habit, though there was nothing in these ruins to fight but ghosts. "Which means she's close. Which means Vessia might be closer."

"Then we don't waste time standing here discussing it," Sable said, and stepped toward the circle.

"Wait."

Kestrel's voice. Not loud. But it stopped Sable the way a hand on the shoulder stops someone walking in their sleep.

Kestrel came forward into the glow of the inscriptions, and up close Sable saw that she was trembling — Kestrel March, who had faced a wyrm on the Scorchfield flats with a broken arm and hadn't trembled — and her hands were fisted at her sides like she was holding herself together by force.

"I need to say something," Kestrel said, "and you're going to want to interrupt, and I need you not to."

"Kestrel—"

"*Please.*"

So Sable waited.

"I read the archives," Kestrel said. "Weeks ago. Cinderhold, the night before the temples started burning incense on every corner. I found the reforging rite and I read what it required and I understood it completely, and I hid it from you." She said it plainly, no softening, and Sable felt the words land like small stones thrown hard. "Because that's what I do. That's what my whole order did — we hoarded truths like dragons hoard gold, and we told ourselves it was protection, and every single person we 'protected' ended up destroyed by the truth arriving late. I watched it happen. I helped it happen. I was exiled for the one time I told the truth early instead of late." Her voice frayed. "I swore I'd never hide a thing again. And then I hid the biggest one of all, because — because I couldn't bear the shape of it. Because I love you two idiots, and loving people turns out to be the one thing I never trained for."

"Kestrel," Sable said softly, "it doesn't matter now. We're past it. The anchor is chosen, the rite is ready—"

"That's the thing." Kestrel laughed, wet and awful. "That's exactly the thing. Listen to the rite, Sable. Really listen. It requires a willing anchor. A soul that consents *fully*, without coercion." She stepped closer. "You are not willing. You are resigned. I've watched resignation for twenty years in every tavern mirror between here and the coast, and I know it like my own face. You're walking into that circle because you think it's your debt — your blood, your gate, your mother's path. You're walking in because you think no one else *can*. And a gate anchored in resignation won't hold. Oren, tell her. Tell her what happens to an unwilling anchor."

Oren's silence was answer enough.

"The binding fails," he said quietly. "Or worse. The gate takes the consent literally. An anchor who goes in unwilling becomes a prisoner, and prisoners become cracks, and cracks become doors." He met Sable's eyes. "Your mother sang as she walked in, Sable. I've seen the records. She was afraid, and she went anyway, singing, because she wanted — more than anything in the world — to keep you safe. That's why she held for twenty-two years. Willingness isn't the absence of fear. It's the presence of something stronger than the fear."

"Then I'll find it," Sable said. "I'll find whatever she found. I just need—"

"You need time," Kestrel said. "Time to make peace with it. Time to choose it instead of falling into it. And we don't have time, because Vessia is coming and Ysolde is dying and the world is starving." She drew a long breath. "But I have time. Or I have nothing else, which amounts to the same thing."

Sable felt it then, cold, sliding up her spine ahead of understanding.

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the rite doesn't specify whose soul." Kestrel's voice was steady now, terribly steady, the steadiness of a decision already made. "It requires a willing anchor. It doesn't require the anchor to be of the bloodline — that's for the *opening*, Sable, the blood is the key, but the anchor is just the lock's hand. Anyone can hold a door shut if they mean it." She looked at Oren. "Tell her I'm right."

"She's right," Oren whispered. "God forgive me, she's right. Any willing soul. The order preferred bloodline anchors because they were convenient, not because they were necessary—"

"Convenient," Kestrel repeated. "Like me. Disgraced, unattached, no family to mourn her except a priest who's already dying and a gate-witch who'll get over it." She grinned, and it was the grin Sable had first seen in a Cinderhold gutter, sharp and crooked and alive. "I spent eleven years believing my order threw my life away for nothing. Let me spend the rest of it proving my life was worth something after all."

"No." Sable heard her own voice crack apart on the word. "No. Absolutely not. Kestrel, you don't get to — we found each other, we *found* each other, that's the whole point of everything since the boundary stones, and you don't get to end it by—"

"By saving you?" Kestrel stepped right up to her, close enough that Sable could see the tear tracks cutting through the ash on her cheeks — faithful tears, Sable thought wildly, there are the faithful tears, shed in advance, and hated herself for the thought. "Listen to me. Listen. If you go in that circle tonight, you go in grieving and resigned and half in love with the idea of joining your mother, and the gate will take you the way it took her — and in twenty-two years, give or take, it will let you go, and there will be no one left to anchor it, and everything we've bled for comes undone. But if I go in—" her voice finally broke, all the way through, "—if I go in, I go in *choosing* it. Freely. Gladly. Because you two made my life worth the living and I want to pay it back with the only coin I've got. And a willing anchor holds, Sable. For decades. Long enough for the world to learn to live without miracles. Long enough for someone smarter than all of us to find a better way."

"This isn't yours to decide—"

"Neither was it yours!" Kestrel flared. "You decided in that crypt under the valley, alone, that your blood made it your burden! You didn't ask us! Well, welcome to my side of the argument!"

Above them, faintly, through ninety-one steps of stone, the night changed.

Sable felt it first as a wrongness in her teeth. Then the silver-veined weeds in the courtyard above began, all together, to sing — a thin, sweet, devotional sound, like a congregation of one note. Then the inscriptions on the chamber walls flickered and dimmed, as if something vast had passed between them and their light source, blotting out not the sun but something deeper than sun.

Oren struggled upright off the bench, one hand pressed to his wound, his face gone paper-white.

"She's here," he breathed. "Vessia. She found the ruins. She's been following the primed sigils — our blood led her straight to us." He looked at Sable, then at Kestrel, and in his eyes was the terrible arithmetic of a man running out of everything. "We have minutes. Perhaps less. The ritual has to begin now — the circle has to close before hers does, or everything ends tonight."

"Ysolde?" Sable said.

"Coming," Kestrel said. Far overhead, through the broken roof and the ruined cloister and the singing weeds, a red-gold light kindled in the eastern sky, growing. "Hold on, old girl. Hold on."

Sable stood at the lip of the circle. Her blood was already in the channels. The gate — the real gate, miles north, and this echo-chamber too — was listening. All she had to do was speak the words of invitation. All she had to do was win the race against her own heart, find willingness before Vessia found the door.

Kestrel moved.

Sable saw it too late — saw the dragonslayer's boots cross the chalk line, saw her plant herself dead center in the ring of white stone, arms spread, facing outward toward the stairwell like a soldier holding a pass — and heard her say, clear and calm and absolutely certain:

"*I am willing. I am the anchor. Begin.*"

The channels lit white. The salt began to burn blue. And the last thing Sable saw before the light took the chamber was Kestrel turning her head, finding her eyes through the blaze, and mouthing two words with a crooked, breaking smile:

*Thank you.*

Then the gate began to open — and something on the other side of the world began, patiently, joyfully, to knock.
