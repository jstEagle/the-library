## 21. Visiting Hours

The road to the valley took Maren four days on foot and cost her two of the coins she had been saving for a winter coffin. She was seventy-one years old, and her knees announced every mile like a town crier, but she walked it anyway, because the alternative was to die in her chair with the letter still folded in her apron pocket and the apology still unsaid.

She had read Sable's letter eleven times. She knew that was excessive. She had raised a girl who counted things — stitches, steps, the seconds between a lie and its confession — and somewhere along the way the habit had taken root in Maren too. Eleven times. The ink had not changed between readings. The words still said: *I know what you kept from me. I know why. Come to the stones when you are able. I can be there for a little while now. Only a little while.*

Only a little while. That phrase did something to Maren's chest that she refused to name, because naming it would have meant sitting down by the roadside and weeping until dark.

* * *

The valley looked the same as it always had, which felt like an insult.

Mist still pooled in the hollows like spilled milk. The pines still stood shoulder to shoulder on the ridgelines, black-green and patient. The river still ran cold and quick over its stones, indifferent to everything that had happened since a woman named Elira Quintana had walked into a gate of ash and salt and never walked out again. The world outside had caught fire with miracles and fanaticism; dragons had burned their last reserves above Cinderhold; a god had gone hungry at the threshold and been sent back to slumber. And here was the valley, mist-locked and green, wearing the same face it had worn for two hundred years.

Maren stopped at the first boundary stone and set down her pack because her hands were shaking.

The stones were older than the village, older than the order, older than any story anyone had ever told her about them. They stood in a broken ring where the meadow met the treeline, gray and lichen-spotted, unremarkable to any eye that didn't know what they were. Maren had passed them ten thousand times. She had taught Sable to count them — nine standing, one fallen, the fallen one split clean through — back when counting was a game and not a prayer.

Now the ninth stone was no longer split.

It stood whole again, seamless, faintly warm to look at, as though it remembered fire. And around it, the air had a quality Maren had no word for except *attended*. Like a room someone was waiting in.

"Sable?" she said, and hated how small her voice came out.

Nothing. Mist. A crow somewhere up-slope, complaining.

Maren sat down on her pack and waited, because waiting was the one skill seventy-one years had perfected. She ate half a heel of bread. She watched the light move. She rehearsed what she would say and then threw away every version of it, because every version sounded like either a lawyer or a ghost.

It was near noon when the mist began to move wrong.

Maren had lived beside this valley her entire life. She knew how its fog behaved — slow, sullen, hugging the ground like a tired animal. What happened at the ninth boundary stone was nothing like that. The mist gathered there the way breath gathers on glass, drawn inward, condensing, taking weight. It thickened. It silvered. And out of it, slowly, the way a figure emerges from behind a curtain, stepped a young woman made of weather.

Sable.

Not quite. Maren's heart lurched and corrected itself. This Sable was translucent at the edges, her outline soft as steam, her hair drifting as if underwater. But her face — her face was exactly the face Maren had last seen fleeing through the gate with a priest's blood on her hands and a dragonslayer's arm around her shoulders, three years ago now, or four. Time moved strangely out here. Maren had stopped trusting her own arithmetic.

"You came," the figure said. Her voice sounded like wind finding a crack under a door.

"Of course I came," Maren said. "You asked."

"I wasn't sure you'd—" Sable stopped. Even made of mist, she could not finish a sentence she'd started in anger. Some things survived apotheosis. "I'm glad you came," she said instead.

Maren got up off her pack, joints cracking, and closed the distance between them. She stopped an arm's length away, suddenly shy of the boundary, unsure whether touching the keeper of the Hollow Gate was sacrilege or simply impossible.

"Can I—"

"If you try to hug mist you'll fall over," Sable said, and there — there it was, dry as tinder, the old humor, the voice of a girl who had once told Maren that prayer was just talking to yourself with better posture. "But you can sit. Sit close. It's easier when you're close. Everything's easier to hear."

Maren lowered herself onto the grass beside the warm stone, and Sable settled across from her, cross-legged, hovering a finger's width above the ground, and for a long while neither of them spoke. The valley breathed around them. The crow gave up its complaints.

"I buried your mother's letters," Maren said finally. "Did Kestrel tell you? Under the plum tree, in a tin. In case anyone ever needed proof of who you were." She smoothed her skirt over her knees, an old gesture, a hiding gesture. "Then I dug them up and sent them to you, and then I spent three years deciding whether that was the bravest thing I ever did or the cruelest."

"It was both," Sable said gently. "Most true things are both."

"I told myself I was protecting you." Maren's voice wobbled and she let it; she was too old and the road had been too long to spend strength on steadiness. "That was half of it. The other half — the half I've only said out loud once, to a priest who was dying — is that I couldn't bear to be the woman who replaced her. If you'd known I wasn't your blood mother, you might have loved me less. Or worse, you might have loved me the same, and I'd have had to live knowing the love was charity."

"Maren." Sable leaned forward, and the mist of her nearly touched Maren's knee, and where it hovered the skin went warm, like sunlight through a window. "I learned your hands before I learned my own name. I learned to bake from watching you. To swear. To mend. When I dreamed of my mother before I knew anything — do you know whose face I saw?"

Maren shook her head, though she suspected.

"Yours," Sable said. "Every time. Whatever Elira gave me, whatever runs in the seal and the stone and the fire — *you* raised the person who could hold it. She made the key. You made the keeper." A pause, and then, quieter, with the careful flatness of someone delivering a verdict they've rehearsed: "I'm not angry anymore. I want you to know that. I was furious for a year. Kestrel says I was insufferable. Then Oren died, and I found out fury is just grief wearing armor, and I got tired of wearing it. So. I'm not angry. But I'm also not finished. Some things don't get finished. They just get carried lighter."

Maren laughed — a wet, cracked sound — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "You always were too honest for your own good."

"I'm a door now," Sable said. "Honesty is structural."

* * *

They talked until the shadows lengthened, and the talking was nothing like the grand reconciliation Maren had composed on the road, all sweeping speeches and absolution. It was smaller than that. Better than that. It was Sable asking after Widow Corrin's goats, and Maren reporting that the goats had eaten the widow's laundry line twice and were therefore, in the village's estimation, possessed. It was Sable asking whether the chapel bell had ever been recast, and Maren saying no, and Sable saying *good*, because she liked the way it rang slightly sour, like the bell disapproved of sermons on principle. It was Maren confessing, in a small voice, that she still set two cups out some mornings out of pure habit, and Sable going very still, and saying, "So do I," though what furniture a living gate kept was beyond imagining.

Once, a pilgrim came up the western path — a thin man with a staff and the sunburnt patience of the long-walking devout — and Maren stiffened, but Sable simply dimmed, folding herself down into the stone like heat sinking into a hearth. The pilgrim knelt at the boundary stones and pressed his forehead to the ground and prayed, quietly, for his daughter, who was sick in a city whose name Maren didn't catch.

When he had gone, Sable rose again.

"He knows prayers aren't answered here," Maren said. "Everyone knows. Why walk all this way to pray at a gate that doesn't open?"

Sable was quiet a moment. "Because being heard isn't the same as being answered," she said. "That was Vessia's great trick — making the world forget the difference. People used to come here just to say things aloud where nothing could take advantage of them. I keep those prayers, Maren. All of them. I tend them like—" she smiled faintly, "—like a garden. Most of them are small. *Let her fever break. Let him write back. Let me be brave tomorrow.* I can't grant a single one. But I can hold them so they're not carried alone." She looked at Maren, and the mist of her eyes had an expression in it that was painfully, humanly tired. "That's the job. Nobody warned me the job was mostly listening."

"That's most jobs worth having," Maren said. "I listened to a valley for forty years. It's called keeping house."

Sable laughed, and the sound moved the mist in rings, like a dropped pebble in still water.

* * *

The sun was leaning hard toward the ridge when Kestrel March came up the eastern path carrying a hammer, a bag of nails, and a plank of roof-beam over one shoulder like a soldier shouldering a pike.

She was grayer than Maren remembered, and heavier through the shoulders in the way of women who have started doing physical labor for grief rather than wages. She wore no order sigil anymore. She wore a plain leather apron, scarred with burn marks, and she walked with the rolling gait of someone who has decided, permanently, that hurrying is for other people.

"Don't get up," she said to Maren, dropping the beam in the grass with a thud. "You've walked four days. I've walked thirty feet from my half-built cottage. We are not peers today."

"You're building it already?" Maren said. "You only broke ground last month."

"The walls are up. Roof next week, gods willing and the timber straight." Kestrel squinted westward at the sun, then at the mist-shape seated among the stones, and her rough face did the thing it always did around Sable — softened by degrees, reluctantly, like ice deciding to trust the thaw. "She wanted a place within sight of the gate. Said pilgrims shouldn't find a dead valley. Should find somebody home."

"Somebody home," Maren repeated softly.

"Aye." Kestrel sat down heavily beside her, produced a flask, considered it, and put it away unopened — a small war she fought daily, and won daily, and never mentioned. "There'll be a room facing east. Toward the stones. For visitors. For—" she shrugged elaborately, "—anyone who comes."

"For you," Sable said, "it will be for you, you enormous coward, just admit—"

"It's for *visitors*," Kestrel said with dignity, and Maren laughed until her ribs hurt, and Kestrel pretended to be offended, and Sable's laughter scattered silver through the mist, and for a few minutes the three of them were simply three women on a hillside in the evening, arguing about roof pitch, and it was the closest thing to holy Maren had felt since childhood.

Later, when the light went amber and long, Kestrel said, more quietly, "Oren's grave is on the far slope. Facing the gate. I planted rosemary because he'd have hated something sentimental." She turned the hammer over in her hands. "Some evenings I tell him about the day. Out loud. Feels stupid. Do it anyway."

"Do it anyway," Maren echoed. "That's the whole religion, isn't it. That's all any of us ever managed."

Kestrel looked at her sideways, and something passed between them — recognition, two keepers of other people's secrets, acknowledging each other across the gulf of everything they'd each failed to say in time.

* * *

The manifestation was failing. Maren could see it happening — Sable's edges fraying, her voice arriving a half-breath late, the way a candle gutters not from lack of wax but from lack of evening left.

"You're fading," Maren said. "Go rest. Whatever resting means for you."

"Not yet." Sable held up a hand, and the hand trembled like water in wind. "One more thing. I need to give you something, and I need you to actually take it, because if I ask Kestrel to hold it she'll lose it in the construction site."

From the fold of her misted sleeve — sleeves, even now, even as architecture — she drew a small object and set it on the boundary stone. It solidified as it crossed: a wooden cup, small, handleless, the kind children use. Its rim was chipped. Its glaze was the ugly brown of the village potter's early years, before he learned proportion.

Maren knew the chip. She had chipped it herself, washing up, the week after she brought a silent, staring, orphaned child home from the gate-side, and had cried about it absurdly, because the cup had been Elira's, and breaking it felt like losing another piece of her.

"You kept this," Maren whispered. "All this time. Through everything."

"I kept *you*," Sable said. "This just came along." Her form flickered, steadied. "Listen. Before I go. The reason I asked you to come isn't finished business. Well — it's partly finished business. But there's something else." Her gaze drifted to the south, where the mountains stood up blue-black against the sunset, and her expression changed into something Maren had never seen on her face before, not in childhood, not in exile, not even at the moment of her binding.

Uncertainty.

"The prayers I tend," Sable said slowly, "they've started changing. Lately — these past few moons — more and more of them are coming from the south. From past the caldera. Prayers in an accent nobody in this kingdom speaks. And yesterday morning—" her voice dropped, and the temperature around the stone seemed to drop with it, "—yesterday morning I heard one that wasn't a prayer at all. It was a knock. Three times, deliberate, like knuckles on wood. From underneath. From the deep roots of the gate, down where even I haven't gone."

Kestrel had gone very still. "Underneath the gate there's nothing. There's never been anything. That's the whole point of the gate."

"Yes," Sable said. "That's what I thought too." The mist of her was thinning fast now, streaming off her shoulders into the dusk like smoke returning to a fire. "Vessia starved and slept. Ysolde burns steady. The world is healing — everyone says so, everyone writes it in letters, the temples are emptying, the miracles stopped rotting. So explain to me," and here her voice cracked, just once, the keeper of the Hollow Gate sounding suddenly and entirely like the girl Maren had raised, "why something down in the dark is knocking. And why — Maren — why every time it knocks, I feel it *recognize* me."

The last of her frayed apart. The boundary stones stood empty in the blue dusk, warm and silent and attended.

Maren picked up the little chipped cup and held it against her chest, and looked at Kestrel, and Kestrel looked back.

"Well," Kestrel said at last, reaching for her hammer and getting to her feet with a groan. "Roof can wait a week."
