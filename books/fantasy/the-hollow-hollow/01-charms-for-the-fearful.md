## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The knot had to be tied wrong before it could be tied right.

That was the first thing Xanthe Voss had learned at their mistress's knee, back when the lessons were called lessons and not crimes: a luck-knot worked honestly was only cord. It needed the flaw in it—the deliberate slip, the loop that looked like a mistake—to give the magic somewhere to live. *Magic is a guest,* Mistress Odile used to say, her needle flashing through the hemp like a minnow through reeds. *And guests will not stay in a house with no cracks.*

Xanthe's fingers remembered the lesson even now, five years after the house burned and the mistress with it. They sat behind their market stall in Greyharbor's Salt Row with a length of grey wool across their knees, tying a knot wrong on purpose, and around them the morning market roared and haggled and pretended, as Greyharbor always pretended, that everything was fine.

It was a good lie, as lies went. The fish stalls were loud. The bread carts smelled of rye and woodsmoke. A woman argued cheerfully over the price of eels two stalls down, and somewhere a fiddler mangled a reel badly enough to be charming. Salt wind came off the harbor carrying gull-cries and tar and the far-off groan of ships.

And between the bakery cart and the cooper's awning stood three men in ash-grey coats with iron pins at their collars, moving through the crowd the way oil moves through water.

Witch-finders.

Xanthe did not look up. That was the second lesson: never look. Looking was a kind of reaching, and reaching left fingerprints on the world that people like these could feel—or claimed they could, which amounted to the same thing when there was a pyre at the end of it. So Xanthe kept their eyes on the wool, kept their hands slow and stupid, kept their breathing even, and let the small warm thing coiled behind their breastbone go utterly still.

*Be cord,* they told themselves. *Be nothing but cord.*

The witch-finders stopped at the charm-seller's row.

Of course they did. Salt Row was where the fearful came—fishwives wanting fair winds, mothers wanting fever-charms, old men who'd buried too many sons and wanted to believe the dead were somewhere warmer than dirt. Fearful people bought knots. And knots were technically legal, so long as they were *just knots*, just craft, just superstition sold to the simple—which was exactly the kind of distinction that made witch-finders linger.

"Morning," said the nearest one. He had a voice like a boot heel on gravel, and he said *morning* the way other men said *confess*. He was working his way down the row of stalls, lifting charms between two fingers as though they might bite him. "Licenses."

Xanthe's stall was last in the row, tucked against the cooper's fence where the shadow fell early. They had time. They used it—one more pass of the wool, one more deliberate flaw set into the weave, the little crack where a guest might stay if a guest ever dared come. Then they reached beneath the counter and produced the license: a square of stamped vellum reading **XAN VOSS — CORDWORK & TRIFLES**, issued by the Office of Occupied Trade, seal intact, fee paid.

*Xan.* Not Xanthe. The name had been shortened the way everything in Greyharbor got shortened—rations, rights, lives. It was safer that way. *Voss* was already dangerous enough to carry; half the old families had scrubbed the name off their doors when the border provinces fell, and Xanthe wore theirs like a stone in the shoe, painful and private and impossible to put down.

The witch-finder arrived and held out his hand without greeting. Xanthe gave him the license. He read it slowly, though Xanthe would have wagered their whole stock that he could barely read at all, and while he read, his companions drifted—not toward the stall, but around it. One circled wide to the left. One drifted right, past the eel-woman, who had gone suddenly quiet about her prices.

They did this because they wanted you to notice. Noticing made people sweat. Sweat made people stammer. Stammering was nearly as good as confession.

Xanthe counted the knots on their display rack instead. Eleven luck-knots, four storm-braids, one marriage-cord that had been sitting unsold for a month because no one in an occupied city wanted to promise anyone anything forever. Their hands stayed loose. Their heart stayed slow. Behind their ribs, the warm coiled thing slept on, or seemed to.

"Cordwork," the witch-finder said at last. He turned the license over, looking for something on the back that wasn't there. "You tie knots."

"I tie knots."

"For luck."

"For decoration, mostly." Xanthe smiled the smile they'd practiced in a cracked mirror until it fit their face like a well-worn glove: pleasant, slightly dull, the smile of a person with nothing hidden anywhere. "Sailors buy them. Sailors are superstitious. I don't ask what they think the knots do. It's bad for trade to argue with customers."

Something flickered at the corner of the man's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been the beginning of the question that ended with rope and pitch. In Greyharbor those two things lived very close together.

He set the license down. Then, instead of leaving, he picked up a luck-knot from the rack and held it to the light, turning it, and Xanthe felt the day tilt a single degree toward disaster.

Because this knot was *good*. This was one of the ones they'd worked at midnight, unable to sleep, hands moving on their own, the flaw in it so fine and the weave so true that any hedge-witch in the old days would have whistled—and witch-finders, whatever else they were, were trained to know the difference between a sailor's trinket and the real thing. Odile had warned them about this too. *Your worst enemy isn't your power, child. It's your pride. Pride makes you show your work.*

"It's a pretty pattern," the witch-finder said. "Where'd you learn it?"

"My mother."

"She sell charms too?"

"She sold fish." This was true, in its way. Odile had sold everything, out of a shop in Vellamere, before the fall—charms and cures and quiet counsel, and once, Xanthe suspected, things considerably larger than charms. "I learned the knots from her. The rest I taught myself off a peddler's pamphlet."

The witch-finder looked at them then, fully, for the first time—a long flat look, the look of a man weighing meat. Xanthe met it the way one meets weather. Inside, behind the breastbone, the coiled thing stirred, drowsy and indignant, wanting to rise, wanting to *show*—

*Xanthe pressed it down.* Down, down, into the cellar of themselves where it had lived for five years, fed on scraps, allowed nothing. It hurt, the way holding your breath hurts. It had always hurt. They had simply gotten good at hurting quietly.

"Hm," said the witch-finder, and dropped the knot back on the rack.

And that should have been the end of it. That should have been the whole of the morning: fear, endurance, survival, the ordinary miracle of being overlooked. The three ash-grey coats moved on down Salt Row toward the fortune-teller's tent, where the pickings would be easier, and Xanthe exhaled for what felt like the first time since moonrise, and the market noise closed back over the stall like water over a dropped coin.

Then the child came.

She couldn't have been more than seven, small enough that she had to rise on her toes to see over the counter, with a smudge of flour on one cheek and her hair escaping its braid in every direction. She looked left and right with the exaggerated stealth of the very young, then slapped something onto the counter and whispered, with tremendous gravity:

"For my da. He works the walls. Night shift. I want him to come home."

Xanthe looked at the coin.

And the world went narrow and ringing, the way it does in the moment before a wave breaks.

It was an old coin—old the way ruins are old, worn nearly smooth, its stamping faded to a ghost. But ghosts hold their shapes. And this ghost was a circle broken by nine lines radiating inward, and at the center, small as a seed, a hollow ring.

The covenant sigil. The Keeper's mark.

*Their* mark. The mark that had been branded into the lintel of Odile's shop in Vellamere, the mark stitched inside every coat Xanthe had owned since childhood, the mark they had burned out of their own belongings one terrible night five years ago while the smoke of the shop still hung over the city. The mark of the family Voss, going back further than records bothered to go. The mark that meant, in a language almost no one living could read: *bound and binding, keeper and kept.*

No one minted these anymore. No one had minted them in a generation. This coin should have been melted, buried, forgotten—and instead it was in a child's fist in occupied Greyharbor, offered up for a luck-knot with the solemnity of someone paying in gold.

"That's real old money," Xanthe heard themself say. Their voice came out steady. Five years of practice. "Can't take it. No one'll give you change."

"It was my gran's." The child pushed it a half-inch closer, fierce. "She said keep it secret but it's lucky, and Da needs luck more than secret. Please. You've got the good knots. Marta says yours are the ones that actually work."

*Marta says yours are the ones that actually work.*

Down in the cellar of themselves, behind the breastbone, the coiled thing opened one eye.

Xanthe's hands were moving before their mind caught up—old habit, older than fear, the hands of a person raised to answer need before anything else. They took a storm-braid from the rack, then set it back and took plain cord instead, because a storm-braid was too fine, too recognizable, and began to work. Wrong first. Then right. The flaw slid into the weave, small and dark, a door left open a crack.

They did not let anything flow through it. They *wanted* to—they could feel the want like thirst, like the ache of a singer forced mute—but wanting was all they permitted. The knot was only cord. It would keep the girl's hope, which was most of what any knot had ever kept anyway.

"There." They tied it off and held it out. "For your da. Tell him to wear it under his shirt, where the rain can't get it."

The girl traded the coin for the knot with the care of someone handling both a relic and an egg, and then—because children are sent into the world to undo us—she looked up at Xanthe with her flour-smudged face and said:

"You've got the same one. On your wrist. Gran had it too."

Xanthe's blood stopped.

Slowly, without deciding to, they looked down at their own left wrist, where the cuff of their sleeve had ridden up as they worked—and there it was, inked years ago in Odile's kitchen, the small hollow-ring sigil they covered every single day and had somehow, today, failed to cover. Five years of perfect caution, undone by a sleeve and a moment of generosity.

The girl was still watching them, waiting, unafraid. Children didn't know yet that some marks were worth burning for. That was the whole horror of the occupation in a single small face: the law had made a crime of remembering.

"Your gran," Xanthe said, very quietly. "What was her name?"

"Maren. Maren Ostrey." The girl frowned, sensing the change in the air without understanding it. "She died in the winter. She said if ever I met someone with the circle-mark, I was to—" The frown deepened. She was digging through memory for words handed down like the coin, words whose meaning had worn off but whose weight remained. "She said I was to tell them the door's still shut but the hinge remembers. That's all. I thought it was just a saying."

The market roared on. Gulls cried. Somewhere the fiddler found the tune at last. And Xanthe Voss stood behind a stall of harmless cord with their heart hammering against five years of locked doors, hearing a dead woman's message arrive by way of a seven-year-old courier: *the door's still shut but the hinge remembers.*

Odile had said things like that. Odile had been full of sayings that weren't sayings.

"Thank you," Xanthe managed. "Tell your da to come home safe."

The girl grinned, pocketed nothing—for the knot was already clutched to her chest—and ran, braid flying, back into the bright noise of the market, carrying her relic and her errand both.

Xanthe pulled the cuff down over the sigil. Their hands, they noticed distantly, were shaking.

*Coincidence,* they told themselves. Old coins survived. Old families scattered. Grandmothers said strange things on their deathbeds. There was no reason—no reason at all—to feel the ground tilting beneath the flagstones, no reason for the coiled thing behind their breastbone to have woken fully now, pressing upward like a plant toward light it had been denied too long, no reason for the morning to feel less like an ending endured and more like a first page.

Except that the witch-finders had come to Salt Row twice this week, when once a month was usual.

Except that the patrols along the harbor wall had doubled since the dark of the moon was announced.

Except that Odile had made Xanthe memorize, in the last winter of her life, the location of a cache buried outside the city—*for when the stars go out, child*—and had refused, absolutely refused, to explain what she meant.

Xanthe looked down at the old coin lying on the counter, its hollow ring catching the grey northern light.

Then, with the whole of Salt Row watching or not watching, they closed their hand around it, swept their knots into their satchel, and went to find out what their dead mistress had left them.

* * *

The cache was a long walk and a longer climb, up through the tanner's quarter where the smell alone discouraged visitors, past the ruined watchtower the occupiers hadn't bothered to garrison, to a shelf of granite above the sea-cliffs where Xanthe had dug one frightened night five years ago and never once returned.

They dug now with bare hands, heart keeping time with the waves below.

The oilcloth bundle came up heavy. Inside: a few coins, a knife, a folded cloak—and a letter, sealed with wax, bearing the press of a signet Xanthe knew better than their own heartbeat.

The hollow ring.

The paper was yellowed, the ink brown, the handwriting Odile's—sharp, slanted, impatient, alive. Xanthe knelt on the cold granite with the wind pulling at their hair and read:

*Xanthe. If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you are desperate, or we are both about to be. Forgive me for the silence. Silence was the only armor I could leave you.*

*You are not what I told you. You are more. Our family does not sell charms—we keep a covenant, the oldest one, the one that holds the border between this world and the Other. Every star you have ever wished on is a knot in that covenant, and the Keepers' blood is the cord. I was the last. Now, I think, it must be you.*

*If the wards fail—if the stars go out—you must reach the Hollow Hollow before the next dark moon. Take the coast road. Trust no council, trust few friends, and trust the mark when strangers carry it: the old families remember, even the small ones. Even the granddaughters.*

*I am sorry, child. I hid you to save you. It seems I only saved you for this.*

*Burn nothing. Hide nothing. Not anymore.*

*—O.*

Below the cliffs, the sea went on grinding the shore, patient as it had been before any covenant, indifferent as it would be after. Overhead the sky was pale and empty in the way of late afternoon, the sun a smear behind cloud, and the first stars hours away from showing or not showing.

Xanthe sat back on their heels, the letter trembling in their grip, grief and fury and—God help them—hope rising together like floodwater.

Above Greyharbor, the sun touched the horizon.

And the moon, rising fat and full over the occupied city, began, very quietly, to gutter.
