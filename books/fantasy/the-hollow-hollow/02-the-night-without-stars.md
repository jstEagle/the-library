## 2. The Night Without Stars

The luck-knots sold out by dusk.

Xanthe counted the coins twice in the back room of Maren's chandlery—thirty-one coppers and a bent silver, enough for bread and lamp-oil and a week's rent on the cot above the tannery—and told themselves, as they did every night, that this was enough. This small life. These small charms that were not really charms at all, only knots tied in a certain way with a certain thought held behind the teeth, harmless as a prayer, deniable as a sneeze.

Five years since they had last let their magic show. Five years since Greyharbor's walls had come down under Vane's siege engines and the witch-finders had come marching behind them with their iron censers and their lists of names. Xanthe had learned to be small in that time. Learned to sell luck to the fearful without ever letting a spark of it touch their own fingertips where anyone could see.

They were trimming the lamp when the moon rose wrong.

It came up over the harbor rooftops the color of an old bruise—not silver, not white, but a deep wounded violet, as though something on the far side of the sky had pressed its thumb against it. Xanthe stood very still, scissors in hand, and watched it climb, and felt the hair rise along their arms because violet was not a color moons made. Violet was a color wards made, when they strained. When they *bled*.

"No," they said aloud, to no one. "No, it's too soon."

The border wards had been failing for years—everyone who still remembered the old songs said so, muttered it over their beer like a confession—but failing was a slow thing, a sagging fence, a rusted hinge. Failing was not *this*. This was a held breath. This was the moment before a dam spoke.

Xanthe blew out the lamp and went up the outside stair two steps at a time, out onto the chandlery's flat roof where the drying racks stood empty and the whole of Greyharbor lay spread below them like a spilled jewelry box: ten thousand hearth-fires, the harbor lanterns strung along the mole like a rosary, the watch-towers burning their green signal-beacons against the coming dark.

And above it all, the first stars beginning to prick through the violet sky.

Xanthe had grown up on stars. Everyone had. But most people looked at them the way they looked at rain—as weather, as furniture, as things that simply were. Xanthe had been taught better, once, by a woman with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a closing door. *Stars aren't lights, child. They're stitches. The sky is a seam, and every star is a knot in the thread that holds your world to the other one.*

Mistress Ilvane had never explained what happened if the stitches pulled loose.

At moonrise exactly—the bruised moon clearing the eastern wall of the citadel, fat and wrong and throbbing faintly like a wound breathing—every star in the sky went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Guttered*, all at once, the way candles gutter when a door slams somewhere deep in a house, and the darkness that rushed in behind them was not ordinary dark. It was thick. It had texture. It fell over the city like a wet blanket and Xanthe heard, distantly, from the direction of the old Northgate and the border road beyond it, a sound like the world's largest sheet of ice cracking—a long, singing, splintering groan that went on and on and did not stop so much as run out of air.

Then the screaming started.

It came up from the market district first, then the fish-rows, then everywhere at once, a tide of human terror with no single shape. Dogs howled. Bells began to ring—every bell in Greyharbor, watch-bells and chapel-bells and the great cracked doom-bell of St. Verity's that hadn't sounded since the plague year, all of them clanging out of rhythm with each other, a chaos of bronze. Below Xanthe, in the street, people poured from their doors holding candles and children and nothing, standing in the starless dark and staring up at a sky that had become a smooth black lid.

And through the cracks—

That was the thing Xanthe would remember for the rest of their life, however long that turned out to be. Not the panic. Not the bells. The *light*.

Because the wards hadn't just failed. They had shattered, and shattered things have edges, and along every edge of the broken border—high above the Northgate, above the citadel, in seams across the sky itself that only eyes like Xanthe's could see—fae light was bleeding through. It came through like water through a cracked jar: slow, luminous, impossibly colored. A green that did not exist in mortal pigment. A gold like honey held up to a dying sun. It pooled in the air above the rooftops in slow-moving veils, and where it touched, things changed. Frost ferned across a chimney in high summer patterns. A gutter-spout bloomed with tiny silver flowers that screamed, softly, when the wind moved them. Somewhere down by the harbor a ship's figurehead turned its wooden head and wept pitch.

"By the nine," whispered someone on the next roof over—an old man in a nightshirt, gripping his chimney pot, tears cutting tracks through the soot on his face. "It's beautiful. Oh, gods, it's beautiful, it's *coming through*—"

Xanthe wanted to tell him to get inside. Wanted to tell everyone to get inside, though inside would save no one from what was coming through those seams. But the words wouldn't come, because at that moment Xanthe's own magic woke up.

It had slept five years. Five years of careful starvation, of tying power into knots and selling it off in scraps, of keeping the well capped with iron discipline and iron fear. And now the wards were gone—the great wards, the continental ones, the ones whose sheer pressure had always lain over the world like a hand pressing down on everything wild—and with the hand lifted, everything in Xanthe that had been pressed down came *up*.

It started in their chest. A warmth like swallowing a coal. Then their hands began to shine.

Not glow, precisely—glow was gentle, glow was firelight. This was *light*, true light, pouring out through the skin of their palms and the veins of their wrists in threads of white-gold, the same light as the stars that had just died, and Xanthe looked down at their own shining body with a horror that had no bottom, because they knew what this was. They'd known since they were nine years old and Mistress Ilvane had caught them lighting candles with a glance and gone white as whey and gripped their shoulders hard enough to bruise.

*"Never let them see. Whatever happens, whatever the world does, you never let them see what you are."*

And now the whole sky could see.

Below, someone shouted. Xanthe couldn't make out the words over the bells, but they saw the pointing hands, dozens of them, hundreds, all aimed upward—and not at the sky. At the roofs. At *them*. A woman in the street dropped her candle. A watchman turned his lantern toward the chandlery and the beam found Xanthe standing on the rooftop wrapped in starlight like a bride in her own funeral shroud.

"*Witch-finder!*" someone screamed, and the word ran through the crowd like fire through dry grass. "*Witch-finder! Witch-finder!*"

Xanthe ran.

There was no dignity in it. They went down the outside stair three steps at a time with their hands still blazing, tried to smother the light inside their coat and failed—the fabric lit up from within like a paper lantern, useless, absurd—and burst out into the street into a river of fleeing bodies all moving away from the harbor, which meant Xanthe, running toward it, was swimming upstream through a flood of elbows and screams and abandoned shoes.

The magic would not go down. That was the terrifying, exhilarating truth of it. Every time Xanthe reached for the cap, the well simply overflowed around it; five years of drought and now a flood with no banks. Light leaked from their fingertips and left little drifting motes in the air behind them, and a child snatched at one as Xanthe passed and laughed—a bright, shocked, delighted laugh, the only laugh in all that darkness—and Xanthe felt the mote settle into the child's cupped hands like a tame firefly and *stay there*, glowing, and had no idea whether they had just blessed the child or doomed her.

The harbor square was worse. Half of Greyharbor seemed to have gathered there, pressed up against the seawall, staring east across the black water to where the horizon should have been. Where the border had run. And there, out over the sea, the fae light was thickest—a vast aurora of impossible colors writhing up from below the horizon line like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and within it, if you had the eyes, shapes. Towers. Sails. Antlers. A procession of lights moving along a road that existed in no mortal map, coming closer, or perhaps not coming closer, perhaps merely becoming visible, which might be worse.

"The stars," an old fishwife was saying, over and over, in a flat calm voice that was somehow more frightening than screaming. "The stars are gone. The stars are gone and they're coming. I told my mother, I told her fifty years ago, I said the treaties won't hold forever—"

"They'll blame us," said the man beside her, and his voice cracked. "You know they will. Vane will say the charm-sellers did it. He's been waiting for an excuse to burn us all for years and now the sky's handed him one gift-wrapped—"

Xanthe flinched as if struck, and pushed on through the crowd, hunched, hood up, hands jammed in pockets where the light showed between the knuckles like cracks in a furnace door. Toward the tannery district. Toward the cache.

Because there was a cache. There had always been a cache—Mistress Ilvane had drilled that into them too, in the last year of her life, when her cough had gone from nuisance to verdict: *When it breaks, girl—or boy, or whichever you decide to be that decade—when it breaks, you don't think, you don't pack, you go straight to the hollow under the third pier of the old ferry dock, and you take what I've left you, and you run.* Xanthe had assumed she meant bandits, or witch-finders, or famine. Some ordinary catastrophe.

She had never once said *what* she'd left. She had only smiled, that terrible knowing smile, and said: *"You'll know it when you see it. And it will break your heart, and then it will save your life, and probably in that order."*

The old ferry dock was a ruin of rotten pilings at the mouth of the Tanner's Run, half-collapsed, officially condemned, unofficially the territory of eel-potters and smugglers who paid the watch to look elsewhere. Xanthe slipped down the mud-slick stairs between the warehouses, heart hammering, light still bleeding through their coat seams, and crouched beneath the third pier where the tide had eaten a hollow into the pilings' base years ago. Their fingers found the loose stone by touch alone. Underneath: an oilcloth bundle, wax-sealed, cold as a corpse.

Xanthe knelt there in the dark under the dying city, in the smell of tar and salt and rot, and broke the seal with shaking, glowing hands.

Inside lay three things. A knife with a blade of pale glass-like material that drank the light from Xanthe's fingers and gave nothing back. A ring of keys stamped with sigils Xanthe didn't recognize—no. Recognized *too* well. Sigils from the margins of Ilvane's books, the ones she'd burned pages of whenever Xanthe asked. And a letter, folded twice, sealed with a drop of red wax pressed with a mark Xanthe had seen only once before in their life: on the locket Ilvane wore and never opened, and on the family tombstone in the country churchyard where Xanthe had buried her.

The Voss mark. Xanthe's own family mark, the one they'd spent five years scrubbing out of every record, every register, every memory.

The paper trembled in Xanthe's hands. Above, through the gaps in the pier planks, the false aurora pulsed, and the bells rang on, and somewhere far off a new sound joined them—the deep horn-call of the citadel, three notes repeated, the signal reserved for invasion.

Xanthe read the letter by their own light.

*If you are reading this, then the night has come, and I am sorry for every hour I let you believe you were hiding instead of waiting.*

*My name was not Ilvane Marsh. My name is Ilvane Voss, and I was the last Keeper of the Covenant, and you, my darling, are the seal.*

Xanthe stopped reading. Looked up through the pier-planks at the starless, bleeding sky. Down at their own hands, still shining, still refusing to be hidden, and felt—for the first time in five years, in the middle of all that terror—something that was not fear at all.

Purpose has a taste, it turns out. Like cold iron and hot tea.

They folded the letter, took up the knife and the keys, and ran north, toward the border, while behind them Greyharbor burned its candles against the dark and the witch-finders' drums began to beat.
