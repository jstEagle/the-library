## 3. What the Mistress Left

The city burned its own light that night, and Xanthe ran through it like a thief.

Greyharbor had gone mad in the hours since the stars died. Fires on the Middling Stairs, fires on the fish-quays, fires in the temple district where the priests of the Ninefold had thrown open their reliquaries and were burning candles by the hundred, as if wax could fill a hole in the sky. The harbor itself was worst. Where the water met the dark, something pale and wrong was bleeding through—a greenish glow that came from beneath the waves, from the drowned crossing at the harbor's mouth, and the fishing fleet had put out toward it with lanterns swinging, because sailors would rather sail into a wound in the world than sit ashore and wait for it to close.

Xanthe kept to the shadowed side of the streets, hood up, luck-knots still strung across their chest under the coat like a bandolier of small confessions. Every knot held a thread of their magic. That had been the design—Mistress Ellery's design, five years ago, when she'd taught them to braid intention into hemp until the power sat quiet inside the weave instead of leaking out through their skin. *A vessel holds water,* she used to say. *A river floods. Be the vessel.* But tonight, in the market square, when the first crack of fae light had split the darkness over the clocktower, every knot on their body had lit up like festival lamps, and Xanthe had stood in a crowd of screaming strangers glowing like a struck match.

They had run. Of course they had run. And behind them, in the crowd, someone had shouted the word—the word that got people burned—and the witch-finders' horns had begun to sound.

Now the horns were everywhere, braided through the city's panic, and Xanthe took the long way home: down Tanner's Cut where the reek covered all other smells, along the drainage channel where no lanterns hung because no one wanted to see what floated there, up through the rookery where the old tenements leaned together like conspirators. Their lungs burned. Their hands, shoved deep in their pockets, still glowed faintly at the knuckles, and they pressed them against their thighs to smother the light.

*Five years,* they thought, ducking under a sagging clothesline. *Five years of being nothing but a charm-seller. One night.*

The tenement on Corvis Lane had no door worth breaking; the lock had been broken since before Xanthe rented the room, and the landlady pretended not to notice who came and went at strange hours. Xanthe took the stairs three at a time, shouldered into the narrow room that smelled of hemp and tallow and the ghost of tea, and threw the bolt out of habit even though the door wouldn't have stopped a determined cat.

For a moment they just stood there, chest heaving, listening to the city scream below.

Then they moved—not to the bed, not to the window, but to the hearth. The fireplace hadn't drawn properly in years; Xanthe had told the landlady the chimney was blocked, and she'd shrugged, and that was the whole of the lie that guarded the most important thing Xanthe owned. They knelt, worked their fingers into the soot-caked seam at the back of the firebox, and pulled. The false brick came away with a grinding whisper, and cold air breathed out of the cavity beyond, smelling of earth and old stone.

Inside lay a canvas satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, and on top of it a letter sealed with grey wax.

Xanthe's hands stopped working.

They knew that seal. Everyone in Greyharbor knew it, or had once—it was the sigil stamped on the coin the child had paid them with that morning, the mark that had turned their blood to ice water in the middle of a market day. A circle broken by seven points, each point crowned with a star. The Voss mark. *Their* mark, the one stitched inside every coat they'd ever owned, the one their mother had inked onto their wrist when they were small enough to still believe such things were ordinary.

But the letter wasn't in their mother's hand. It was in Ellery's.

*She's been dead eight months,* Xanthe thought, and the grief arrived all at once, fresh as the day they'd found her—found the chair by the window, found the blanket over her knees, found her hands folded and her face peaceful in a way it never was while she lived, because Mistress Ellery had carried pain the way other women carried keys, always on her person, always rattling. *Dead eight months, and she is still handing me tasks.*

They broke the seal with a thumb that shook only a little, and read by the light of their own traitorous hands.

*Xanthe.*

*If you are reading this, then the night has come, and I am sorry for it—sorry that I am not there, sorrier still that I spent your childhood teaching you to hide instead of teaching you to stand. I told myself it was mercy. I tell myself many things.*

*You will want to know what happened tonight, so I will give you the truth plainly, because you have earned plainness if anyone has. There was a covenant, old as the border between this world and the Otherworld, older than any kingdom now standing. It was not a wall. People think of wards as walls, but walls are for prisons. The covenant was a promise—a marriage, if you like, between the mortal realm and the fae, each holding the other's hand so that neither fell into the dark. The stars you saw last night were not lights. They were the visible face of that promise, the seal-fire showing through the skin of the sky. When the covenant held, the stars burned. Tonight they went out because the covenant has broken.*

*I know because I was its Keeper. The last of nine, and before me the last of four, and before that fewer still. We did not guard the covenant, child. We anchored it. Our blood was the knot at the center of the promise, and the promise fed on us slowly, the way a hearth feeds on wood, and we let it, because the alternative was a sky with nothing in it. I have watched three Keepers go before me—my mother, my aunt, the woman I loved—and I swore when my time came I would not feed another generation into the fire. So I let the knots rot. I let the order dwindle. I taught you craft instead of covenant, hiding instead of standing, and I called it keeping you safe.*

*It was cowardice. I name it so you need not.*

*But hear the rest. The covenant can be reforged. Not patched—reforged, made new, and the blood that can do it is yours. You are the last of the Voss line, and the Voss line was the first to swear, back when the oath was cut into a living tree at the world's hinge. The place is called the Hollow Hollow. Do not look for it on maps; maps are how the wrong people find holy things. I have left you what you need to find it—the road-song, the tokens, the names of those few who remember the old crossings. Bramble Ferrow can take you as far as the edge of the Otherworld, if her price is paid; she is half-fae and twice stubborn, and she owes me a debt she will pretend she does not.*

*Go before the next dark moon. After it, the cracks will widen past any mending, and what bleeds through will not be light forever. The fae are starving on the far side of a dead sky, Xanthe. Starving things come through cracks, and they do not knock.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop writing, because my hand hurts and you always hated when I lectured. What I did to you—hiding you, hollowing you—I did out of love, and love does not make it right. If you choose to go, go as yourself. All of yourself. I have watched you sell luck-knots to frightened people for five years and call it nothing, and it was never nothing. You have been keeping small covenants your whole life. This is only the large one.*

*Your mistress, who loved you badly and well,*
*Ellery*

Xanthe read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting each sentence land like a stone dropped into still water.

Outside, the horns had changed pitch—closer now, or maybe just more numerous. Somewhere down Corvis Lane a woman was shouting names, over and over, a roll call against the dark. The green glow from the harbor had strengthened enough that it seeped under the door and lay across the floorboards like spilled poison.

*My blood can reforge it.*

They looked at their hands. The glow had settled into the lines of their palms now, tracing them like a mapmaker inking coastlines, and for the first time in five years Xanthe didn't press the light away. They let it burn. It hurt to look at, the way mirrors hurt after you've avoided them a long while—not because it was ugly, but because it was honest.

*She hid me. She hid me so well I hid myself, and she knew, and she was sorry, and she did it anyway.* Anger rose in them, hot and sudden—anger at Ellery, at the dead Keepers who'd let the knots rot rather than find another way, at a world that made a child's inheritance something to be buried behind a false brick. The anger crested, and Xanthe let it crest, because they had spent five years practicing not-feeling and tonight the practice was over.

And underneath the anger, cooling slowly into something harder and more useful: purpose.

Because here was the thing Xanthe understood, kneeling in soot with a dead woman's letter in their glowing hands: Ellery had been wrong about one thing. She'd written *I let the knots rot* as though the choice had been hers alone, as though the covenant belonged to the Keepers the way a house belongs to its owner. But Xanthe had sold charms to the fearful for five years. They knew what a luck-knot was worth to the woman who bought it against her husband's drowning, to the boy who bought one before his first voyage, to the child who'd paid with a coin bearing Xanthe's own family mark as though the sigil were simply *money*, as though the Voss star had sunk so deep into Greyharbor's soil that children spent it without knowing what it was.

The covenant had never belonged to nine Keepers. It belonged to everyone who'd ever tied a knot against the dark and half-believed. The Keepers had just been the ones who held the ends of the rope.

*So hold the ends,* Xanthe thought. *Or find out who else can.*

They packed by feel and by glow: the satchel, the oilcloth, the letter folded against their heart where the luck-knots hung. From the bottom of the cache they took the things Ellery must have placed there years ago—a whistle carved from rowan-wood, cold to the touch; a ring of seven iron tokens, each stamped with one point of the broken circle; and a scrap of vellum dense with a song written in notation Xanthe didn't recognize, though when their fingers brushed it the melody rose unbidden in their memory, in a voice they'd almost forgotten, humming over the wash-basin on some ordinary morning years ago. Ellery had sung it to them as a lullaby. She'd hidden the road in a cradle-song and waited eight months for the night it would matter.

"Damn you," Xanthe said softly, to the empty room, to the dead. "Damn you, and thank you."

Then they banked the glow in their hands—not smothering it, not hiding it, just folding it small, the way you fold a flag rather than abandon it—and went to the window.

Corvis Lane was empty, but the emptiness had a texture. Boots on cobbles two streets over, moving in formation. The horns again, nearer. And at the mouth of the lane, where it opened onto the Tanner's Cut road, a lantern swung—once, twice, deliberate, the rhythm of a search pattern. Witch-finders swept in threes. Xanthe counted the shadows crossing the lane's entrance and counted three.

The back wall of the room shared a roofline with the dye-house next door, and the dye-house backed onto the drainage channel, and the drainage channel ran downhill toward the harbor gates, which were open tonight because half the guard had deserted to check on their own families. Xanthe had walked this escape in their head a hundred times without ever naming it an escape route. Ellery had taught them that too: *always know your second door.*

They slung the satchel across their chest, opposite the luck-knots, and paused with one foot on the windowsill.

The room looked very small from the outside of it. Five years of small magics and careful silence, of being the charm-seller on the corner with quick fingers and no history. On the table sat the unfinished knot they'd been working that morning, meant for no one, tied out of habit. Xanthe crossed back, picked it up, and set it on the sill facing the street—an unclaimed charm for whoever climbed through the window after them, luck to the fearful, one last small covenant left behind like a coin on a dead man's eye.

Then they went out into the dark, hand over hand along the wet shingles, toward the harbor and the road east and a smuggler named Bramble Ferrow who owed a debt she would pretend she did not.

Behind them, the witch-finders reached Corvis Lane and found a locked room, a cold hearth, and a single finished luck-knot waiting on the sill, glowing faintly, like a signature.

By the time they kicked the door in, Xanthe Voss was already a rumor moving south through the drainage dark, following the smell of the sea, carrying a dead woman's song and the last living seal of a broken world.

The hunt had begun. So had everything else.
