## 4. Witch-Finders at the Door

The orders came at dawn, written in Aldric Vane's own hand and sealed with the black sun of his new Protectorate.

Captain Sera Dune read them twice by the light of a tallow candle in the barracks at Greyharbor's Salt Gate, because she could not believe them the first time.

*All practitioners of hidden arts within the walls of Greyharbor are to be taken alive where possible,* the first line said, and that was standard enough. She had been taking charm-sellers and hedge-witches alive for three years, since the border wards began to fray and the Protectorate decided someone must be blamed. *Where resistance is offered, force is authorized.* Also standard. She had broken arms for less than resistance.

It was the third line her eye kept snagging on, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth.

*Practitioners under the age of fourteen are not to be taken. They are to be made example of.*

Made example of. The words were Vane's—she recognized the careful, clerkish hand—but the phrasing was the Inquisitor General's. Corvus Ashe did not say *burned*. He never said *burned*. He said *cleansed*, *rendered*, *made example of*, as though children were a lesson written in chalk that rain had smudged and needed rewriting.

Sera set the paper down. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself with a kind of distant clinical interest, the way she noticed everything: the candle guttering, the frost ferning up the inside of the window, the sound of her thirty witch-finders drilling in the yard below, boots striking cobbles in unison like a slow enormous heart.

She was thirty-one years old. She had joined the Order at sixteen because her village had lost eleven people to a blood-mad hedge-coven when she was nine, and she remembered her mother's face afterward, and she had wanted, with the whole dumb fierce wanting of a child, to be the one who stood between the innocent and the uncanny. That wanting had carried her through twenty years of oaths and iron and cold mornings exactly like this one.

*They are to be made example of.*

She picked the order back up and read it a third time, as if the words might have rearranged themselves into something lawful.

They had not.

Outside, Greyharbor woke under a sky that still had no stars in it. Five days now since the night they all went out. The city had not slept properly since; it moved through its days with the raw-eyed wariness of a wounded animal, and every rumor fed it—the wards were dead, the fae were coming, the fae were already here wearing human faces, the Protectorate had done it deliberately, the stars would return if only the right blood was spilled. That last rumor had grown teeth fastest. Sera had watched it grow. She knew whose mouth it had come out of.

She buckled on her sword, pinned the silver sun of the Order to her collar, and went down to make her soldiers ready to hunt children.

* * *

Xanthe Voss heard the drums at midmorning and knew what they meant before their mind finished translating them.

Three beats, pause. Three beats, pause. The sweep-rhythm. The witch-finders were coming through the Warrens door by door, and there would be no market today, no luck-knots, no small honest trade in thread and hope. There would be a net drawn tight across the district, and everyone in it sorted into two piles: the clean and the guilty.

Xanthe stood in the doorway of the room they rented above the dyer's shop on Tanner's Lane and looked at the life they had built in five years of hiding. It fit in a satchel. It always had. A change of clothes, a knife, a bundle of half-finished knots, the little brass scales for weighing charms. And beneath the floorboard under the bed, wrapped in oilcloth: the letter from Mistress Ellery, which Xanthe had read so many times in the four days since finding it that the creases had gone soft as cloth.

*You are the last seal, child. Your blood can bind what is broken. Come to the Hollow Hollow before the dark moon, or do not come at all, but do not let them take you, for your blood in the wrong hands would unmake far more than it mends.*

The dark moon was nineteen days away.

Down in the street, the drums stopped. In the silence after, Xanthe could hear the other sound, the worse one: boots. Many boots, moving in pairs, stopping at doors.

They slung the satchel across their chest, pried up the floorboard, and hesitated over the letter. Take it or leave it? If the witch-finders found it, it was a confession, a map, a death warrant with directions. If they left it—

If they left it and died, no one would ever know.

Xanthe took it. They were many things—a liar, a coward by their own harsh accounting, a person who had spent five years folding themselves smaller and smaller until they could pass through the world without leaving a shadow—but they were not going to die having thrown away the only true thing anyone had ever told them.

Then they blew out the lamp, though it was morning, and went down the back stair into the dye-works' reeking steam, where old Marta the dyer looked up from her vats with eyes that missed nothing.

"They're sweeping the Lane," Xanthe said.

"I know." Marta wiped her hands on her apron, leaving violet stains. "Been coming since dawn. Girl two streets over—they took her mother already." Her jaw worked. "The girl ran. They'll run her down."

"Marta—"

"Don't." The old woman held up a stained hand. "Don't thank me, don't apologize, don't make it a moment. There's a hatch behind the drying racks. Goes to the culvert under the Lane. Comes up past the tannery wall. You go, you don't look back, and you don't come back here again, you understand? Whatever you are—and I've known what you are since the week you moved in, child, I've dyed wool forty years, I know when a color won't take till someone's whispered to it—you go be it somewhere else."

Xanthe's throat closed. Five years of buying thread from this woman, of fixing her rheumatism with knots she pretended were just *good knitting*, of being a neighbor, and Marta had known the whole time and had simply—kept the secret. Kept it the way you keep a bird in winter: warm, and quiet, and safe.

"Thank you," Xanthe said anyway, because some things couldn't not be said.

"Go," Marta said. "*Go.*"

* * *

The culvert smelled of dye-water and rot and came out, as promised, beyond the tannery wall, in a weedy lot where the city's unwanted things accumulated—broken carts, dead cats, and now Xanthe Voss, crawling up out of the ground like something planted.

They got their bearings. The sweep was moving east to west along the river districts; the Salt Gate barracks lay north; the harbor lay south, and south meant ships, and ships meant passage out of Greyharbor, except that Xanthe had no money for passage and no destination yet either, only a letter and a deadline and a word—*Hollow Hollow*—that sounded like a place from a nursery rhyme gone wrong.

They had gotten perhaps fifty yards along the wall when the whistle shrilled.

Not for them—not yet. Two streets over, a woman's voice rose in a scream that cut off abruptly, and then the whistle again, and Xanthe understood: they were running the girl Marta had mentioned. Running her down through the lanes like hounds running a hare.

And Xanthe—who had spent five years perfecting the art of not seeing, not hearing, not knowing, who had made an entire self out of looking away—stood at the mouth of an alley with their heart slamming and their feet refusing to move toward safety.

*Do not let them take you,* Ellery's letter said.

But the screaming hadn't stopped. It had only gotten farther away, and higher, and younger.

"Oh, damn you," Xanthe whispered—to the letter, to Ellery, to themselves—and turned toward the sound.

* * *

Sera Dune found the girl in a dead-end yard behind the ropewalk, backed against a wall of stacked hemp, breathing in great tearing sobs.

She was maybe eleven. She had a charm-knotted bracelet on one wrist—the crude kind, the kind sold in markets for a copper, luck-threads and nothing more, the magical equivalent of a rabbit's foot—and she had been seen buying it, and someone had informed, because someone always informed, because fear made informants of everyone.

"Please," the girl said. "Please, it's just a bracelet, my gran bought it, please—"

Behind Sera, Witch-finder Osk held the fire-pot. He was a big man, dull-eyed, obedient, the best kind of soldier and the worst kind of person, and he was already unstopping the pot because the order was the order and Osk had never once in eight years asked what the order was for.

"Sergeant," Sera said quietly. "A word."

Osk looked up, firelight dancing orange in his flat face. "Captain?"

"The bracelet. Show me."

He brought it over. Cheap work. Luck-thread. She turned it over in her gloved fingers and felt—nothing. Not even the ghost of a working. Half the luck-knots sold in Greyharbor were fraud, thread and superstition, and the Order knew it, and burned the sellers anyway, because the point had never been magic. The point was that people should be afraid.

"She wore it," Osk said. "That's practice of hidden arts under the Act. Doesn't matter if it works, Captain. Wearing's the crime."

"I know what the law says, Sergeant."

"Then we're clear to proceed." He turned back toward the girl, and Sera stood in the yard behind the ropewalk with the worthless bracelet in her hand and twenty years of oaths sitting on her shoulders like stones, and she heard herself say:

"No."

Osk stopped. Turned. The other finders in the yard—there were six of them—went very still, the way men do when the ground shifts underfoot.

"Captain?"

"The order says practitioners under fourteen are to be made example of." Sera heard her own voice from a long way off, level, captainly, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "I have reviewed the case. The subject possesses no working. The article is inert. Under the Act's second clause, prosecution requires demonstrated practice. There is none. Case dismissed. Cut the bracelet off her and send her home to her mother."

"That's not—" Osk's brow furrowed, the effort of thought visible. "The Inquisitor General said—"

"The Inquisitor General," Sera said, "is not here. I am. Dismissed case, Sergeant. Unless you'd care to file a formal objection with the Order's tribunal, citing your superior officer's ruling, in writing, and wait in the city a fortnight for the answer while the sweep proceeds without you."

She watched him wrestle with it. She watched him lose. Osk had never filed anything in his life.

"...Dismissed," he muttered, and snatched the bracelet from her hand, and knelt by the girl with a knife to cut it away, and the girl flinched from him so hard she cracked her head on the hemp bales, and Sera turned away before she had to watch any more of it.

*Case dismissed.* As if a ruling could put back what the morning had already taken—as if the girl would ever sleep soundly again, would ever see a silver sun-badge without her stomach dropping. Sera had saved her from the fire and left her in the ash, and both of them knew it.

But her hands, when she looked down at them, were shaking.

That was new. Twenty years, and her hands had never shaken. Not at the coven-burnings, not at the drownings, not at any of the hundred small cruelties she had certified as lawful. She flexed her fingers inside her gloves and thought, with the clarity that comes in the space between one heartbeat and the next: *something has changed, and it isn't the law. The law has always been like this. It's you.*

Five days without stars. A city full of terrified people. An order signed in a dead man's calm handwriting. And somewhere in the middle of it, a crack had opened in Captain Sera Dune, and she could feel cold air moving through it, and she did not know yet whether it was a flaw in the wall or a door.

* * *

Xanthe reached the ropewalk yard too late to matter and hid anyway, pressed into the shadow of a rain barrel, watching through the gap between barrel and wall as the witch-finders marched the girl out—alive, whole, sobbing but alive—and sent her stumbling off toward the lanes.

Alive. Xanthe let out a breath they felt in their ribs. Alive, because the tall captain with the grey streak in her hair had said *no* in a voice like a closing door.

Xanthe knew that face. Everyone in Greyharbor knew that face. Captain Sera Dune, the Order's best hunter, the one they sent when the quarry was clever. Three times in five years Xanthe had slipped a net she'd drawn, and three times she had been close enough to touch, and once—two winters ago, in a snow-choked alley—she had looked directly at Xanthe across ten feet of falling white, and her eyes had narrowed, and then she had turned away, and Xanthe had run, and had never understood why.

Now they watched her stand alone in the yard after her men filed out, motionless, staring at nothing, and thought: *she knows. She's known for years. And she keeps letting me go, and I have never once wondered hard enough why.*

The thought should have been comforting. It was terrifying. A kindness from the Order's best blade was a debt with teeth.

Xanthe slid back along the wall, meaning to melt away south toward the harbor, and made it four steps before the voice came from behind them, dry as winter grass:

"You can stop pretending to be a barrel, Voss."

* * *

Sera watched the charm-seller turn slowly, hands rising in the universal gesture of the caught, and studied the face she had been studying for five years without admitting it.

Younger than her reputation suggested. Sharp-boned, wary, dark-haired, with the particular stillness of a person who had practiced being overlooked until it became a discipline. And under the grime and the market-stall shabbiness, that faint luminescence Sera had caught twice before and told no one about—the shimmer beneath the skin, like moonlight through milk. The mark of real blood. Old blood.

"You know my name," Xanthe said.

"I know everyone's name. It's my job." Sera walked closer, boots crunching on rope-ends and hemp dust, and stopped out of lunging range, which was courtesy, and kept her hand off her sword-hilt, which was also courtesy, and watched the charm-seller notice both courtesies and be more frightened, not less. Good. Frightened people didn't do anything stupid. "Here's how this goes. I'm going to ask you questions. You're going to answer them honestly, because I will know if you lie—I've had five years of practice at you, Voss—and depending on your answers, I'm going to decide whether I arrest you."

"And if I run?"

"Then I arrest you tired."

Something flickered across the sharp face—not quite humor, but adjacent to it. "Fair enough."

"Why did you come toward the scream?" Sera asked, and watched the question land harder than any blow she could have struck. "Every instinct you have says run. You've survived five years of my sweeps by running better than anyone in this city. Today you ran *toward* it. Toward six armed finders and a fire-pot. Why?"

Xanthe was silent a moment. When they answered, their voice was low and rough. "Because I was tired of listening."

"Tired of listening."

"To people scream and doing nothing. Yes." The chin came up, defiant and scared at once. "Is that a crime now too?"

Under the old law, no. Under the new law, everything was a crime; the law had become weather, it fell on whoever stood outside. Sera looked at this ragged, foolish, brave nobody who had run toward a fire-pot for a stranger's child, and thought of the third line of the order in her pocket, and felt the crack in her widen.

"When the stars went out," she said. "Five nights ago. Something happened in the Warrens that night. Lights. Witnesses talk about lights coming off a person like sparks off a grindstone, near the old ward-stone on Cooper's Row. My reports list seventeen possible sources. Sixteen are dead or fled." She paused. "One is standing in front of me trying to look like a barrel."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Liar," Sera said, almost gently. "Try again."

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the city another whistle shrilled, and another, the sweep grinding on through the districts like a millstone, and between the two of them in the weedy yard the truth sat waiting to be picked up or stepped around.

"I didn't do it on purpose," Xanthe said finally. Barely above a whisper. "When the wards broke, it—it pulled. Like a hook behind my ribs. Five years I've kept it down, Captain, five years I've been nobody, and in one night it all came up out of me like blood from a cut, and I couldn't stop it, and now—" Their breath hitched. "Now they'll have felt it. Not just your finders. *Them.* The ones on the other side of the cracks. And whatever comes through those cracks is going to come looking for me, and I have somewhere I need to be before it does, and I am begging you—not for mercy. For time."

Sera stood very still.

Everything in her training said: *arrest*. Everything in her oath said it. This was the quarry of her career, the source on Cooper's Row, the practitioner whose capture would earn her the Inquisitor General's favor and a promotion and probably a commendation signed by the Protector himself. All she had to do was whistle.

Instead she heard herself say: "Where?"

"What?"

"Where do you need to be? Before what comes looking?"

Xanthe stared at her. "You're asking me to confess my destination to a witch-finder."

"I'm asking because if you tell me, and I let you walk out of this yard, then in about an hour I'm going to have to write a report saying I searched the area and found nothing. And if I'm going to commit treason, Voss, I'd like to at least know what I'm committing it *for*."

The word hung in the air between them. Treason. Neither of them reached for it or pushed it away. It simply was, the way the starless sky was, a fact of the new world.

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, they drew something from inside their coat: a letter, soft with handling, and unfolded it just far enough for Sera to see the top line in a spidery old woman's hand.

*To the last seal, when I am gone—*

"Ellery Marsh," Sera said involuntarily. "The herb-wife on Bell Lane. We questioned her last spring. She gave us nothing. We reported her as clean." And then, colder, in her own ears: *We reported her as clean because I chose to believe her, and she was the last Keeper, and I shook the hand of the covenant's death and called it diligence.*

"You knew her?"

"Everyone knew her. She sold cough remedies." Sera's mouth was dry. "What does the letter say?"

"It says that the covenant that used to hold the borders—the one your Protectorate pretends never existed—is broken. It says the stars didn't fall, they were *stolen*, and they're being stolen still, hoarded in camps north of the river where your Order delivers the raw material." Xanthe's voice shook, but their eyes didn't. "It says there's a place called the Hollow Hollow, and a dark moon nineteen days from now, and a choice. And it says my blood is the key to all of it, which is why I need time, and why I need to not be in your net, and why—" they faltered, and steadied— "why I think, Captain Dune, that you already knew you weren't going to arrest me. You just needed me to give you a reason."

Sera said nothing. The wind moved the hemp stacks. Far off, the drums started again, three beats and a pause, sweeping toward the ropewalk.

"Nineteen days," she said at last. "Where does your road start?"

"The free cities. Vellamere, if I can get there. There's supposed to be a smuggler who runs the marsh crossings—half-fae, they say. Ferrow's her name."

"Bramble Ferrow." Sera almost smiled; it hurt her face, like a muscle long unused. "She'll rob you blind."

"So I've heard. I'm hoping to be robbed by someone with a boat." Xanthe edged backward, toward the gap in the wall, the satchel riding their hip. "Captain. The report you're going to write. Make it boring. Boring reports save lives."

"Voss." Sera waited until the sharp eyes came back to hers. "The third line of today's order. Do you know what it says?"

"No."

"Good." She meant it. "Keep it that way. Go south along the tanner's ditch, not the open lots—the sweep's pickets are lazy on the water side. And Voss—" the name came out of her before she could weigh it, carrying twenty years of dead villages and burned charms and one girl alive behind the ropewalk because a captain had said *no* for the first time in her life— "whoever you meet on this road. Whoever swears to help you. Ask them what they're willing to lose. The ones who can't answer are the ones who'll sell you."

Xanthe looked at her a moment longer—looking, Sera realized, at the first witch-finder in history to give tactical advice to her own quarry—and nodded once.

Then they were gone, over the wall and down into the ditch-shadow, quick and quiet as water finding its level, and Captain Sera Dune stood alone in the yard behind the ropewalk with a worthless luck-bracelet in her pocket and treason in her mouth, tasting it.

It tasted, she discovered, like the first breath after a fever breaks.

She straightened her collar, pinned the silver sun a little crooked, and went to write a boring report.

* * *

By nightfall the report was filed, the sweep had passed on to the River District, and Greyharbor settled into its fifth starless dusk, torches lit at noon-dark, doors barred at sundown.

In a culvert boat poled south through the tanner's ditches by a bribed fisherman, Xanthe Voss opened Ellery's letter one more time and read by feel, by memory, by the faint glow their own skin cast on the page in the dark—that glow again, stronger now, harder to hide, the mask gone for good.

*Nineteen days,* the letter might as well have said. *Nineteen days, and a smuggler, and a road, and every finder in the Protectorate between you and the Hollow Hollow.*

Somewhere behind them, a bell rang the alarm-hour, and Xanthe did not look back.

Ahead, far off across the black marshes where the crossings waited, something glimmered—brief, cold, and blue-white, like a star fallen into the fen and drowning there.

Like a star that was still being stolen.

The fisherman saw it too, and crossed himself with an oar, and poled faster.
