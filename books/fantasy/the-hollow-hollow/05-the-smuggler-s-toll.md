## 5. The Smuggler's Toll

Bramble Ferrow smelled the trouble before she saw it.

That was the fae half of her, the part her mother had left in her like a coin under a pillow—ears that caught whispers three rooms off, a nose that read fear the way sailors read weather. Fear had a smell: copper and wet wool and something faintly sweet underneath, like fruit going bad. The Saltcellar stank of it tonight.

She leaned against the doorframe of her own back room, arms crossed, watching the stranger who'd paid old Wren too much silver for a corner table and then sat there like a person waiting to be arrested. Hood up. Shoulders drawn in. Hands wrapped around a cup of small beer they hadn't touched in an hour.

*Witch,* said the smell. *Hunted witch.*

Bramble had made a good living for eleven years out of not caring about other people's troubles. It was practically her trade's first commandment: *Thou shalt not ask what's in the crate.* But there was a second thing under the fear-smell tonight, something that had drifted through the Saltcellar's door with the stranger and stayed like woodsmoke in cloth. Something green. Something that smelled—gods help her—like the crossings used to smell, before the stars went out and the ways between worlds sealed up like scar tissue.

It smelled like home.

"Oi." She pushed off the doorframe and crossed the room, dropping into the chair across from the stranger without asking. Up close, the hood did a poor job. Young face, sharp-boned, exhausted, with a smudge of soot along one jaw and eyes that kept flicking to the window every time a lantern passed outside. "You're either the stupidest fugitive on this coast or you want to be found. Which is it?"

The stranger went very still. Then, quietly: "I was told you move people."

"I move *cargo*. Silk, brandy, unlicensed prayer-books, occasionally a person with more money than sense." Bramble turned the cup toward herself and sniffed it. Untouched, as suspected. "You've been nursing that beer since the tide turned. You flinched when the watch bell rang at moonrise, which everyone else has learned to ignore because it rings when it likes now, since nobody's minding it. And—" she leaned in, dropped her voice to almost nothing, "—you smell like a hedgerow in June. So. Stupidest fugitive, or something better?"

A long silence. Outside, the Saltcellar's common room roared and sang its usual songs, all of them slightly wrong since the Night Without Stars—the old verses about starlight sung flat, sung scared, sung like people trying to remember a dead friend's voice.

"I'm looking for passage east," the stranger said at last. "To Vellamere. Through the marshes, not the coast road. There are witch-finders on the coast road."

"There are witch-finders everywhere, these days. That's rather their whole thing." Bramble studied them. "Name?"

A hesitation. Then: "Xanthe."

"Just Xanthe?"

"Just Xanthe."

"Mm. And I'm just Bramble." She let that sit. Half-truths were currency in her trade; she recognized the minting. "Here's my problem, Just Xanthe. I don't ferry witches. Not because I've got anything against them personally, but because Captain Dune's people have burned two safehouses in Greyharbor this month, and my crew has families, and I am *not* dying so some hedge-charmer can run from a law that was always rotten anyway. You understand. Nothing personal."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face—not hurt exactly. Older than hurt. Tiredness with a spine in it.

"You're right," they said. "Nothing personal." They reached into their coat, and Bramble's hand went to the knife at her hip before she could stop it—but what came out wasn't a weapon. It was a luck-knot. Frayed, rain-damaged, one strand snapped clean through. A child's knot, cheap twine, the kind sold at market stalls for a copper.

"Do you know what this is?" Xanthe asked.

"It's a luck-knot."

"It's mine. Or—it was mine. A girl bought it from me two days ago, in Greyharbor market. Paid me with this." They set a coin on the table between them, and Bramble's breath stopped in her throat.

Old minting. Older than the kingdom, older than the usurper's ugly face on the new coppers. A circle broken by a living line—a sigil she'd seen once, long ago, in a place she'd spent eleven years telling herself she'd dreamed.

"That's the covenant mark," Bramble said. Her voice came out wrong. Flat. Careful. "That's the *old* seal. Where did a dockside child get—"

"She said her gran gave it to her for luck. Gran probably didn't know what it was. Most people don't anymore." Xanthe's fingers curled around the coin, and Bramble saw—actually saw, in the guttering lamplight—a faint green shimmer pass beneath the skin of their hand, like light through shallow water over moss. "But I know it. Because it's my family's mark. We cut the original seal. My blood is in that design."

The room seemed to tilt a degree. Bramble gripped the table's edge.

"You're a Voss," she said.

"I'm what's left of one."

* * *

They talked until the common room emptied and Wren started stacking chairs, shooting pointed looks that Bramble ignored with the ease of long practice. Xanthe told her about the wards shattering at moonrise five nights past. About the stars going out all at once, like candles pinched by a hand too big to see. About the letter from a dead mistress, hidden in a cache under a floor, speaking of Keepers and covenants and a place called the Hollow Hollow where everything had been sworn and everything had come undone.

"And you think if you reach the Hollow Hollow and do whatever blood-rite your mistress wrote down, the stars come back." Bramble turned her cup slowly on the table. "And the crossings open."

"That's the shape of it. I don't know if it'll work. I don't know much of anything, honestly. I know the free cities still have walls and councils and enough stubbornness to matter, and I need to get there before the next dark moon. After that—" they spread their hands. "After that the letter says the way closes for good. Whatever that means."

Bramble looked at them for a long moment. At the shimmer under their skin. At the frayed luck-knot they'd smoothed flat on the table like a talisman, which, she supposed, it was.

Here was the thing Bramble Ferrow never told anyone, the thing she kept locked below her ribs tighter than any cargo: she had kin on the other side of those crossings. Mother's people. The Glimmerfolk, her father had called them, drunk, before he called her worse. When the stars went out and the ways sealed, her mother had been on the far side—visiting, visiting *always*, that was the arrangement, back and forth like a ferrywoman herself—and Bramble had woken that starless morning knowing, with a certainty that lived in the marrow the fae half of her came from, that the door had shut with her mother standing behind it.

Eleven days ago. It felt like eleven years.

"So here's my price," Bramble said, and heard her own voice go hard, because softness would undo her. "Not silver. Silver I have. You want my boat, my marsh-paths, my crew risking Dune's pyres—you give me your word. Your actual word, sworn however you swears get sworn, that when you reforge this covenant of yours, the crossings open. All of them. For everyone trapped on the far side. Not traded away, not bargained off to some council, not held back for leverage. Open."

Xanthe went still again. "You have people there."

"My mother." The word cracked despite her. She hated that. "She was visiting. She was always *visiting*, that was the whole point of the crossings, that's what they were *for*—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "There are thousands. Half-bloods' kin, lost traders, folk who stepped through for an evening and woke to a sealed sky. Your mistress's covenant bound both realms together, and when it broke, it didn't just take the stars. It took *people*. So that's the toll. Swear it."

Xanthe picked up the coin. Turned it in the lamplight, the broken circle catching fire along its living line.

"I can't swear it works," they said. "I'd be lying if I promised the rite will open anything. I might die in a hollow tree in the middle of nowhere. I might fail. I'm not—I'm not the person everyone seems to need me to be. I sold luck-knots. I hid for five years. I'm not a Keeper, I'm a *forger's apprentice* who inherited a war."

"Good," Bramble said.

Xanthe blinked. "*Good?*"

"Good. Because the ones who think they're the person everyone needs are the ones who get everyone killed. I've carried a few. They all talked like prophets and steered like drunks." She leaned forward. "Swear what you can swear. Swear you'll try. Swear that if there's a choice in it—if the opening is ever yours to give—you give it. That's all I'm asking. That's the toll. A promise from someone honest enough to admit they might break it trying."

Xanthe looked at the coin a long time. Then they set it down between them, and pressed their thumb beside it, and Bramble watched—felt it, actually felt it, a warmth rising through the table like sun through floorboards—as they spoke.

"I swear it," Xanthe said. "By my blood and my family's mark: if the crossings can be opened, I will open them. If the choice is ever mine, I will choose the door. For your mother. For all of them."

The warmth flared. The sigil on the coin glowed green for one heartbeat—one impossible, aching heartbeat, the color of the Otherworld's edge at dusk, the color of Bramble's childhood—and then faded, leaving only old metal and a promise that Bramble felt settle somewhere behind her sternum like a swallowed coal.

"Well," she said, roughly. "That'll hold."

"Will it?"

"Oh, aye. Fae-sworn words always hold. Even quarter-fae ones." She stood, joints cracking, and jerked her head toward the back of the room. "Come on, then. You're sleeping in the cellar tonight, and tomorrow we're on the water before dawn. And Xanthe?" They paused at the cellar door, hood already up again, and something in Bramble's chest twisted at how practiced the motion was. How young. "The girl who bought your knot. Did she live?"

Xanthe's jaw tightened. "Sera Dune let us go. The witch-finder captain. I don't know why."

"Huh." Bramble filed that away in the drawer marked *things that will matter later*. "Down you go. First thread's spun; mind you don't snap it."

* * *

The marshes at dawn were gray silk shot with silver, mist lying on the black water like a second sky. Bramble poled the flat-bottomed skiff through channels she could have navigated blindfolded—which was fortunate, because the fog was thick enough to drink.

Xanthe sat amidships among the crates, hood down for once, watching the reeds slide past with an expression Bramble recognized and distrusted on principle: wonder. People who wondered got attached. People who got attached got you killed, or worse, got themselves killed, and then you had a promise-coal burning behind your sternum with no one left to honor it.

"Eel traps," Bramble said, nodding at the wicker cages bobbing in the shallows. "My crew checks them Tuesdays. You'll meet them at the second channel—Tansy, who does our maps, and Odo, who does our arguing. Fair warning: Odo will say this is a mad errand and then do it anyway. He always does."

"Why?" Xanthe asked. "Why do any of you do it? Smuggling people through a police state. There's no profit worth Dune's pyres."

Bramble poled in silence for a while. A heron lifted off ahead of them, unhurried, ancient.

"When I was nine," she said finally, "my mother took me through a crossing. Just for an evening—to the edge of the Otherworld, the safe edge, the meadow-lands. There was a festival. Lanterns on the water over *there* too, but the lanterns floated upside down, above the water instead of on it, and the fish jumped up to look at them." She smiled despite herself, at the memory, at the absurdity of describing it. "And there were people there from here. Mortals. Merchants, lovers, a whole wedding party that had crossed over for the dancing. Nobody checked papers. Nobody asked what you were. The crossing didn't care. It just... opened, for anyone who knocked politely."

The mist thinned ahead. Somewhere beyond it lay the coast road, and the witch-finders, and the world where knocking politely got you burned.

"That's what I smuggle," Bramble said. "Not people. *Doors.* Every run, every bribe, every forged seal—it's all just me holding doors open with my shoulder while the world tries to close them. So when a Voss walks into my tavern smelling like the meadow-lands and swearing by the old mark to open every door there is..." She shrugged, suddenly embarrassed, and stabbed the pole into the mud harder than necessary. "Well. I'd have been a fool to charge you silver."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. When Bramble glanced back, they were holding the frayed luck-knot, turning it over in their fingers.

"The girl who bought it," they said. "Her name was Merrit. She wanted luck because her father was taken to a harvest camp, and she thought a charm could bring him back." Their voice was steady, but their knuckles weren't. "I couldn't sell her that. So I taught her a ward instead. A real one. Small, practical—enough to keep nightmares off, maybe keep a finder's hound confused. It's not much."

"It's not nothing," Bramble said. "Nothing's what most people give."

Ahead, the fog began to tear, and through the gap Bramble saw the second channel, and two figures waiting on the shingle bank with a covered longboat and the unmistakable posture of people about to argue. Odo was already gesturing. Tansy was already ignoring him.

"Company," Bramble announced. "Last chance to turn back, Keeper. Once you're in that longboat, you're cargo, and I don't abandon cargo."

Xanthe looked at the figures on the bank—at strangers who would become, though none of them knew it yet, the web that would hold the sky up—and then back at Bramble, and something eased in their face. Not hope, exactly. Something more practical than hope. The look of someone deciding to put weight on a bridge.

"Cargo," they agreed. "Where are we headed?"

"Vellamere, if the fates are kind, which lately they haven't been." Bramble shipped the pole and let the current take them toward the bank, toward the voices, toward the future. "But first there's someone on the water road you should hear about. Word came down the marsh yesterday with the eel-boats. Witch-finder captain gone rogue. Killed her own Inquisitor on the ford at Mirren and ran with a satchel full of documents—harvest camp records, star-ledgers, the usurper's own seals." She grinned, sharp as a gutting knife. "They're calling her the Traitor of Mirren. They're calling her Sera Dune."

Xanthe went very still. "I know that name."

"Aye," Bramble said, watching the bank come closer, watching the first thread pull taut into a second. "I rather thought you might."
