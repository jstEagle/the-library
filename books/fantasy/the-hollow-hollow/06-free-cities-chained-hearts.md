## 6. Free Cities, Chained Hearts

Vellamere announced itself three days before we reached it: a smudge of chimney smoke on the horizon, then the glint of its famous glass spires catching a light that came from nowhere—because there were no stars left to catch, only the pale, sourceless glow the sky had worn since the night the wards broke. The city had turned that glow into commerce. Glassblowers along the wall sold *starcatchers*, little blown orbs that supposedly held the light of the vanished heavens, and pilgrims bought them by the fistful and hung them from every eave until the whole city glittered like a lie.

Bramble reined her mule beside mine at the top of the last hill and spat with great precision into the ditch.

"Pretty as a corpse at a wedding," she said. "You know what I smell from here? Money changing hands while the world ends. Free Cities." She said the words like a curse in two syllables. "Free for whom, I wonder."

"For people who can pay," I said. "That's always been the price of freedom here."

I should have known better than to hope anyway. But five years of hiding had taught me one thing well: how to want something quietly, so quietly even I couldn't hear myself wanting it. And I wanted this. I wanted to walk into that shining council chamber, lay my mistress's letter on the table, and watch nine powerful people do the right thing because it was the right thing. I wanted, just once, for the world to be run by someone other than fear.

The letter rode against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from my body. *If you are reading this, the covenant has broken, and you are the seal.* Mistress Odile's handwriting, cramped and urgent, written by candlelight in the last weeks of her life. *Go to the Hollow Hollow before the dark moon. Do not trust the councils—they will weigh your blood against their ledgers and find it wanting.*

I'd read those lines so many times I could recite them with my eyes closed. I'd told myself she was wrong about the councils. She was old; she was bitter; she had spent her whole life watching institutions fail and had stopped being able to imagine anything else.

We paid the road toll at the Westgate—three coppers apiece, plus a fourth for Bramble's mule, which she disputed at length on principle—and passed under the glass arch into streets that smelled of roasting chestnuts and river mud and, underneath it all, faintly, of fear. Refugees camped in the temple squares. I counted six different dialects in the first hundred paces. A woman was selling charms at a corner stall—not luck-knots like mine, but iron nails wrapped in red thread, warding trinkets, the kind of thing that worked only if you believed hard enough—and a city constable stood ten feet away, pointedly not seeing her, because Vellamere had no law against hedge-magic yet.

*Yet,* said the back of my neck.

* * *

The Council of Nine received petitioners on the third morning of each week, in a chamber at the top of the Spire of Deliberation, and the line began forming before dawn. We queued among fishwives and debtors and a delegation of canal-workers demanding wage protections, and I used the hours to rehearse.

"I am Xanthe Voss," I murmured, "and I come bearing proof that the fall of the border wards was no accident—"

"You're muttering again," Bramble said. She'd insisted on coming up with me, over my objections, on the grounds that "you look like a lost clerk without someone menacing standing behind you." Sera would have come too, but Sera Dune did not enter free cities anymore; her face was too well known, her desertion too recent, and the last thing our cause needed was a witch-finder's warrant pinned to its banner. She waited outside the walls with Cassian's men—or rather, with the men who had been shadowing us since the ford, whose allegiance we still hadn't tested.

"—proof that the fall of the wards was no accident," I continued, "that Aldric Vane is harvesting starlight from living captives, and that unless the covenant is reforged at the Hollow Hollow before the dark moon, both realms will go dark together. Nine votes. That's all I need. Nine."

"And if they say no?"

"Then I ask again. Louder."

Bramble looked at me with something that might have been pity, or might have been indigestion. "Sweetling, I've watched these councils for forty years. They don't say no loudly. They say no in beautiful rooms, with excellent wine, and by the time you're out the door you've thanked them for it."

The chamber, when we finally reached it, was exactly as beautiful as she promised. Nine chairs of pale Vellamere ash arranged in a crescent, each occupied by a Councilor in robes of office dyed the blue of the city's charter. Sunlight—real sunlight, hoarded through glass lenses—poured down from a skylight and made the floor gleam like water. It was a room built to make petitioners feel small and grateful, and it worked on me for approximately four seconds before anger burned the feeling off.

"Petitioner," said the First Councilor, a woman named Halvard with a voice like a ledger closing. "State your business briefly. There are forty souls behind you."

"Nine minutes' worth," I said, and I gave it to them.

I told them about Greyharbor, about the witch-finders sweeping the markets, about children burned for selling luck-knots. I told them about the night the stars went out, and the fae light bleeding through the cracks in the sky. I unwrapped the letter and read Odile's words aloud—the Keeper, the covenant, the seal of blood—and when I said *the Hollow Hollow* I saw a flicker pass between three of the nine chairs, quick as a signal lamp, and knew they recognized the name.

And then I told them about the camps. Sera's evidence, copied fair in her own precise hand: the star-harvesting stations along the Ash Road, the cages, the drained ones. I had expected horror. I had planned for horror. I had speeches ready for horror.

What I got instead was silence, and then Councilor Halvard folding her hands and saying, gently, the way one speaks to a child reporting a monster under the bed:

"Your evidence is secondhand. Your authority is—" she glanced at the letter, at the name Voss, and something moved behind her eyes that I couldn't read, "—complicated. And your request is that the Charter Cities declare war on Greyharbor's garrison, break a trade armistice that has held eleven years, and invite the Otherworld's attention to our borders. In exchange for what, precisely? A promise that a dead woman's letter is accurate?"

"In exchange for survival," I said. "The dark moon is nineteen days off. If the covenant isn't reforged—"

"Then the sky stays dark," said the Councilor for Trade, a soft man named Pellin whose rings clicked when he drummed his fingers. "And we adapt. We have lamps. We have glass. What we do not have, petitioner, is an army capable of fighting Vane's veterans, nor any guarantee that your—rite—at this hollow tree wouldn't simply invite the fae to walk back across our fields. You've met them, I take it. You've seen what they consider a fair bargain."

"They're starving," Bramble said from behind me, and her voice had gone low and strange. "Your rivers run thin, your orchards fruit half-crop, and you think that's weather. The Otherworld is dying because the covenant died, and when it finishes dying, what's left of it will come through the cracks looking for food. You don't get to adapt your way out of that."

"Madam, you are not the petitioner," Halvard said.

"No," Bramble said. "I'm the reminder."

I stepped forward before it could curdle further. "Councilors. I'm not asking you to love magic. I'm not asking you to fight for the fae. I'm asking you to fight for the sky over your own children's heads. Nineteen days. Give me soldiers, give me ships, give me grain for the road—I'll take anything you can spare and be grateful for scraps."

I heard my own voice crack on *grateful* and hated it. Five years of hiding, five years of smallness, and here I was begging a room full of ringed fingers for scraps.

The nine of them conferred. Not long. That was the worst part—how short the conference was, how smoothly the decision slid out of them, already made, perhaps made before I ever entered the room.

"The Council of Nine thanks the petitioner," Halvard said. "The Charter Cities will not commit forces beyond our walls. However, the Council votes to establish a commission to study the matter of the darkened sky, with a report due within the season—"

"A season," I repeated.

"—and to expand lamplighting subsidies in affected districts. Next petitioner, please."

The guards were already moving toward us, gentle and immovable, and I stood there holding my mistress's letter in a shaft of stolen sunlight while forty more desperate souls filed in behind me with their petitions about wages and fish quotas, and the great machine of the free world chewed us all with perfect courtesy.

Bramble had to take my elbow to steer me out. I let her. My legs weren't entirely convinced about walking.

Outside, on the Spire steps, the city glittered below us, starcatchers swaying on every eave, thousands of little glass lies pretending the heavens still existed. Somewhere a bell rang the noon hour, sweet and unhurried. The world's most beautiful cowardice, ringing the hour over refugees in the squares.

"They knew," I said. My voice came out flat. "Halvard recognized the name Hollow Hollow. Three of them flinched. They've had reports, Bramble. They've known since the night the wards fell, maybe longer, and they commissioned a *study*."

"They've known worse for longer," Bramble said. "That's what councils are. A way for ten people to share the blame for doing nothing." She squinted down at the glittering streets. "So. What now, Keeper? We've got eighteen days and no army."

"We go around them. To the guilds, maybe, or—"

"Or," said a voice behind us, smooth as poured cream, "you stop wasting your breath on men who count their gold in ledgers and start talking to a man who counts it in swords."

He descended the steps like he owned them—which, I would learn, he once nearly had. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed not in exile's drab but in a soldier's riding coat cut so well it insulted every beggar in the city, and he moved with the particular ease of a man who had never once needed to apologize for taking up space. Two retainers flanked him at a respectful distance, armed the way furniture is armed: beautifully, and obviously.

"Lord Cassian Roe," he said, and bowed—actually bowed, to me, in front of God and the fishwives. "And you are the Voss heir. Or the Voss whatever-it-is. Forgive me; the reports disagree, and I find I don't care."

"How did you—" I started.

"There are four people in this city who know the word 'Hollow Hollow,' and I bribe three of them." He smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated to the millimeter. "You went to the Council. That was noble. Noble things die in that room daily; you can visit the graves of a thousand good ideas on your way out. But you—" he tapped the air an inch from my shoulder, never touching, "—you are not an idea. You are an army's worth of legitimacy wearing a traveler's coat. Every soldier who won't march for a council's dithering will march for a Keeper. Every refugee who won't trust a lord will trust the person the stars chose. You simply need someone to put steel behind you."

"And that someone is you," Bramble said dryly.

"That someone is me." He spread his hands, candid as a merchant opening his scales. "Three hundred veteran lances, wintered and unpaid, sitting in my valley estates two days east. Exiled with me when the Greyharbor coup stripped my family's seat—though I notice the Council didn't mention that the same vote funded Vane's early campaigns. Ask them sometime. Watch them blink." His smile thinned by a degree, and underneath it, for half a heartbeat, I saw something older and colder, a man keeping a very long ledger of his own. Then it was gone, cream-smooth again. "Give me a reason to spend my fortune and my sword on your rite, Xanthe Voss, and I will put three hundred lances between you and Aldric Vane's Inquisitor General."

"And the price?" Bramble asked, because Bramble always asked.

"Modest," Cassian said. "When the covenant stands again—when the crossings open and the old order returns—there will be debts to settle and seats to fill. I ask only that the new covenant remember its friends." He looked at me, and his eyes were patient and kind and absolutely bottomless. "A word written into the oath here, a favor acknowledged there. Nothing you'd miss."

Nothing you'd miss. Later—much later—I would understand exactly how much those words weighed, and what a word written into an oath could build or break. But standing on those sunlit steps with eighteen days on the clock and the taste of the Council's courtesy still sour in my mouth, I felt only the enormous relief of a drowning woman thrown a rope, and I reached for it before I asked where it was anchored.

"You'll follow my lead at the Hollow Hollow," I said. "Whatever happens there, whatever the fae offer or demand—my word decides. Swear that, and you have your bargain."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, fast as the signal-lamp glance in the council chamber—and then he bowed again, deeper this time.

"On my family's name," said Lord Cassian Roe, "your word decides."

Behind him, far below, the starcatchers swayed on their strings, and the light they caught came from nowhere at all.
