## 7. The Usurper's Court

The starlight came to him in vials no thicker than a finger, sealed with wax and prayer, and Aldric Vane drank the first one of every evening standing at his window in the Tower of the Pale Sun, watching the sky that had betrayed him.

There were no stars now. That was the point. That was *his* point, his achievement, the single great work of his life: he had pulled them down. Not all at once—nothing worth doing was done all at once—but gutter by gutter, ward by ward, until the night over Greyharbor and Vellamere and every chained city between was a smooth black lacquer, unbroken, unlit, and entirely his.

He uncorked the vial. The light inside did not shine so much as *lean*, pressing against the glass like something alive and impatient, and when it touched his tongue it tasted the way he imagined forgiveness would taste—cold, clean, and gone too soon.

It spread through him the way ink spreads through water. His hands went luminous first, veins silvering at the wrists, then fading as his body drank the light down into whatever place inside him had been empty for so very long.

He had been drinking for eleven weeks. He had stopped counting the days after the third week, when the counting had started to feel like something a smaller man would do.

"Lord Protector." The voice from the doorway was Maren Colthe's, his chamber-secretary, a gray woman who had learned never to look directly at him after sundown. "The Council of Nine has sent its answer."

"They refused." He did not turn around. He could taste her flinch from across the room; fear had a flavor now, faintly sweet, like honey cut with iron. Another gift of the light. He had not asked for that one. He had not asked for any of them, precisely—he had simply taken what was offered by the harvest-camps, the distilleries, the long slow murder of the sky—and gifts freely seized were still gifts.

"They refused," Maren confirmed. "The Lord Speaker's letter uses the word *dialogue* four times."

Vane smiled. He knew he was smiling because his reflection in the black glass showed it to him—a white, narrow thing, like a scar opening. "Dialogue. How brave of them. Read me the rest."

"The rest is courtesies, my lord. Invocations of shared history. A request that you suspend the border levies while—"

"While they negotiate." He turned at last. Maren stood with the letter held out before her like a shield made of paper. She was perhaps fifty. She had served his predecessor, and the one before that, and she would serve whoever came after him if anyone ever did, which was a thought he found both irritating and oddly comforting. Continuity. The machine grinding on regardless of who fed it. "They think I am a man who can be negotiated with."

"Aren't you, my lord?"

That was the trouble with Maren. She was too old to flatter and too tired to lie well. He crossed the room, took the letter, and held it over the brazier until the courtesies curled and browned and let go of themselves. The ash smelled like burnt sugar. Everything smelled like sugar lately. He wondered, briefly and without much interest, whether that was the starlight changing him or whether he had always been this way and simply lacked the senses to notice.

"Maren," he said, watching the last corner of the letter catch, "do you know what I wanted, when I was young?"

"No, my lord."

"Neither do I." He said it lightly, almost fondly, and watched her decide—correctly—not to react. "I remember wanting to be warm. I remember wanting there to be enough. My mother sold charms, you know. Little knotted things, luck-strings, fever-braids. Illegal even then, under the old accords, but the magistrates looked away because she paid them. I used to hold the thread while she tied. I was very good at holding things still." He flexed his fingers. The light beneath his skin stirred, drowsy, obedient. "Do you know what I have learned since? Wanting is a hole. It doesn't fill. You pour warmth into it and it wants fire. You pour power into it and it wants the sky. There is no bottom to it. I checked."

Maren's throat moved. "My lord, shall I draft the siege orders?"

"There," Vane said warmly, "is a woman who listens. Yes. Vellamere first. Full investment, land and river. I want their granaries counted, their gates measured, their Council of Nine invited—I use the word loosely—to reconsider dialogue. And I want it done quietly. No proclamations yet. Let them hear the boots before they read the words. Fear travels faster than paper."

* * *

The Inquisitor General arrived at midnight, because Vane had ordered her to arrive at midnight and because Seraphine Dune was, so far, the only instrument he owned that never needed winding.

She came into the map room spattered with road-mud to the knee, which she did not apologize for, which was one of the thousand small reasons he tolerated her. Behind her, as always, hovered her adjutant, a soft young man named Pell whose eyes were too wet for the work. Vane had considered having Pell replaced six times and had never done it, because Dune performed marginally better when someone weak was watching her—some part of her straightened, some part of her *performed*—and Vane collected performances the way other men collected coins.

"You sent for me at midnight," Dune said. "So either the news is bad or you enjoy the theater."

"I enjoy efficiency. The theater is incidental." He gestured her to the map, the great painted sweep of the realm with its free cities marked in defiant blue along the coast. "Tell me what you know of the Voss family."

Her face did something small and complicated. He catalogued it. "Charm-sellers. Greyharbor quarter. The matriarch, Odile Voss, died last spring. There was an apprentice or ward—records disagree on the relationship. Sex unrecorded, name common. Xanthe."

"*Girl or boy or whatever it is,*" Vane said, savoring the phrasing he'd had drafted into the warrant, because precision was for people with something left to lose. "Yes. And what else?"

Dune was quiet a moment. Outside, the wind worried at the tower stones, a sound like slow chewing. "Since the wards fell, we've had forty-one reports of visible magic in Greyharbor alone. Most are panic—candleflames, shadows, drunk men seeing saints. But three reports describe the same figure. Young. Selling luck-knots in the fish market the morning the stars went out. When the wards broke, witnesses say the knots lit up like lanterns and the crowd around them didn't scatter. They *gathered*."

"Gathered," Vane repeated softly. "How tedious. And how telling." He put one fingertip on the map, on Greyharbor, and let a thread of stolen light run out of it into the paint, so that the city glowed faintly silver under his touch. Dune watched the light and did not step back, and he noted that too, filed it in the drawer where he kept everything he might someday need about her. "Here is what your witch-finders have not understood, Captain—or General, forgive me, the promotion papers are on my desk. Magic is not a crime. Crime requires victims. Magic is a *currency*. The old Keepers hoarded it, the councils taxed it, the hedge-witches bartered it in alleys, and everyone agreed to pretend it was wicked so that the pretending could be enforced by people like you. I am merely ending the pretense. All of it belongs to the state now. Every knot, every charm, every drop of blood that carries the old covenant in it."

"And the Voss child carries it."

"The Voss child *is* it." He let the light die from the map. "The Keeper line didn't end with Odile Voss. It hid. It has been hiding in a market stall for five years, selling luck to fishermen, and on the night my work came to fruition, it flared bright enough to be seen from the harbor wall. Do you understand what that means, Dune? It means the covenant isn't broken. It means it's *looking for a new anchor.* And I intend to be holding the leash when it finds one."

Something moved behind Dune's eyes—the same something that had been moving there for weeks, a hairline crack spreading through good porcelain. "The warrant says capture alive."

"The warrant says capture alive," Vane agreed pleasantly. "Dead is acceptable. Alive is *necessary*. Dead, the blood is a curiosity. Alive, it is a door. Bring me the door."

"And if the free cities shelter them?"

"Then the free cities will learn what my patience costs." He turned back to the window, dismissing her, and then—because the light made him honest in ways he despised, because some cold current in his chest insisted on saying the true thing aloud to see how it sounded—"Captain. One more matter."

"My lord."

"When you find this person. This Voss. Look at them closely, and tell me what you see. I'm told the Keeper blood glows now. I want a description. Precise. The color of it. Whether it moves." He heard himself and almost laughed. *Whether it moves.* As though he were commissioning a portrait. As though some starving boy still lived in his chest, holding thread taut while his mother tied her little illegal knots, wanting only to know what magic looked like when it wasn't being confiscated.

But that boy was a hole too, and holes did not get filled. They got *used.*

"You'll have your description," Dune said, in a voice carefully sanded flat, and left.

* * *

Alone, Vane drank the second vial.

This was the hour he did not schedule, the hour that did not exist in any ledger: the hour between the day's cruelty and the night's ambition, when the tower was quiet and the light was working through him and the hunger woke up and stretched.

It had a shape now. He had begun, in the last fortnight, to be able to feel its edges—a vast interior absence shaped vaguely like a person, like the outline of a man drawn in chalk on a slate and then wiped away, leaving only the ghost of the drawing. The starlight poured into the outline and vanished. Every vial. Eleven weeks of vials. Somewhere below him, in the vaults, lay the harvest of three hundred nights of murdered sky, casks and casks of distilled radiance, enough to make a god, and he drank and drank and remained exactly as hollow as before, only *faster* now, only *brighter*, a lantern with nothing inside it burning through its own walls.

He stood at the window and pressed his palm flat against the glass. Below, Greyharbor sprawled under his perfect darkness, ten thousand hearthfires the only lights left in the world, each one a small act of defiance or despair—he could no longer tell the difference, and suspected there wasn't one.

*What was it you wanted,* the hunger asked him, in a voice that was becoming less metaphorical by the week. *Warmth? Enough?*

"To never hold the thread again," he said aloud, to no one, to the black glass, to the boy-shaped absence wearing his face. "To be the hand that ties."

And the hunger, patient as a tide, said nothing, and took the words, and gave back nothing, and he reached for the third vial.

* * *

In the morning, the orders went out.

Three copies, sealed with the sun-and-chain, carried by riders who did not know what they carried. To General Ostrava at the Vellamere road: *invest and starve, but do not storm until the word.* To the harbor master at Greyharbor: *no ship leaves, no ship enters, the net closes.* And to the Inquisitor General, one line only, because he had learned that Dune obeyed brevity the way stone obeys gravity:

*Find the Voss. Before the dark moon. Bring me what the sky is missing.*

He signed it with a flourish, and the ink gleamed faintly silver as it dried, and if Maren noticed she was wise enough to say nothing, and Vane stood alone in the map room surrounded by the shapes of cities he intended to own and a sky he already did, and felt—for one unguarded moment, one crack in the lacquer—the truth underneath the ambition, which was simpler and colder than any of it:

He did not want to be a god.

He wanted to stop feeling like a man, and there was not enough starlight in heaven to manage it.

He corked the emptiness, so to speak, straightened his collar, and rang for Maren.

"Draft a proclamation," he said. "For when Vellamere falls. Begin with the words *for their own protection.*"

Outside, the wind went on chewing at the stones, and somewhere far to the south, a rider was already spurring toward Greyharbor with a warrant and a question she had not yet admitted she was asking herself—and Aldric Vane, Lord Protector, drinker of dead stars, turned his back on the window and began, patiently, hungrily, to wait.
