## 8. Road of Ash

**Bramble**

The border had been burned so thoroughly that even the ash had given up being ash. It lay in grey drifts against the broken fences like snow that had forgotten how to melt, and when the wind moved it, it moved in silence, without smell, without warmth—the way dead things move.

Bramble Ferrow rode at the head of the little column with her hood up and her fae-sight pulled tight behind her eyes like a shutter, because out here, on the edge of what had been the Marchlands, the world was thin enough to see through, and she did not want to see through it. Not yet. Not while she still had to count her people's faces among the living.

Behind her came the wagons—three of them, drawn by mules too stubborn to die—and behind the wagons came forty-odd refugees who had attached themselves somewhere past the Mirren ford and would not be shaken loose. And somewhere in the middle of all of it, walking because they refused to ride when others walked, was Xanthe Voss, Keeper of nothing, heir to everything, glowing faintly at the wrists like a lamp left burning in an empty house.

Bramble had noticed it first three nights ago. A soft gold light under the skin, visible only after dark, pulsing slow as a sleeper's breath. She'd said nothing. Half-fae learned early that pointing at strange things got them confiscated.

The refugees had noticed too.

"Mistress Ferrow." Old Tam, the wheelwright with the burned arm, trotted up beside her mule. He kept his voice low, the way men do when they're about to say something foolish and know it. "The Voss one. Last night I saw the light come right out of their hands. Right out, like water."

"Sunlight on a ring," Bramble said. "You were tired."

"I've worked metal fifty years, mistress. I know sunlight on a ring." He glanced back down the column, and his voice dropped further. "My granddaughter says it's the Saint of the Sundered Sky. Says the old stories tell of a light-bearer who walks the ashes before the stars come home."

"The old stories also say eating rowan berries cures cowardice," Bramble said. "Your granddaughter should eat some."

But Tam only smiled the patient smile of a man who has decided what he believes and will not be inconvenienced by facts, and dropped back down the line, and Bramble heard him telling it again two wagons back within the minute, the story growing already, sprouting details like mold: *the light-bearer*, *the ashes*, *the stars coming home*.

She looked over her shoulder. Xanthe walked with their sleeves pulled down over their hands, as they always did now, shoulders hunched, eyes fixed on the road. Trying to hide a bonfire under a coat.

*Oh, you fool,* Bramble thought, with the particular tenderness she reserved for people she'd known less than a month and would apparently die for. *They're going to build you a shrine out of wagon parts.*

**Cassian**

Lord Cassian Roe had commanded men through two sieges and one disastrous winter campaign, and he knew the anatomy of a mob the way he knew the anatomy of a pike square: where it would form, which way it would break, and how much pressure its spine could take before it bent.

What formed around Xanthe Voss on the third day of the ash road was not yet a mob. It was worse. It was a congregation.

He watched from the tail of the column as they gathered—not the desperate ones, not yet, but the ones with something left to give. A woman pressed a dried apple into Xanthe's hand. A boy walked backward for half a mile just to keep the light-bearer in view. When they made camp that night, someone had laid out a blanket for Xanthe near the fire, apart from the others, and no one sat on it, and no one asked Xanthe whether they wanted it there.

Cassian had seen this before. In the last years of the old king, before the throne his family had held for six generations was stolen out from under them by a Lord Protector with a talent for paperwork and poison, he had seen crowds look at his father exactly this way. His father had loved it. That love had been the first crack in the dynasty, though none of them had known it then—a man who enjoys being a symbol stops listening to the people making him one.

He found Xanthe at the fire's edge, turning the dried apple over in their fingers like a coin of doubtful currency.

"You're not eating it," Cassian observed.

"They gave up food for it." Xanthe's voice was flat with exhaustion. "The woman. Her kid has a cough and she gave me her food because I glowed at her wrong."

"She thinks you're a saint."

"I know what she thinks." The apple went into the fire. Xanthe watched it blacken. "Cassian, I sold luck-knots in Greyharbor for five years. Do you know what a luck-knot is worth? Whatever the buyer believes it's worth. Nothing more. I spent five years watching people hang their hopes on my knots and call it magic, and I hated every hour of it, because it was a lie I let them tell themselves so I could eat." They looked up, and the firelight and the wrist-light met somewhere in their face and made it hard to read. "This is that. Bigger. Worse. They're not buying anything. They're just... giving."

"So refuse it," Cassian said. "Stand up tonight and tell them you're not a saint. Tell them what you actually are."

"And what's that?" A bitter edge. "A hedge-witch with famous blood? A fugitive? Tell them their saint sells charms for copper and sleeps in ditches?"

"Yes," Cassian said. "All of it. Because here's what I know about symbols, Voss—I was raised to be one. My family's crest was stitched onto banners before I could walk. And I will tell you what a symbol costs: it costs you your voice. The moment they decide what you are, everything you say gets filtered through what they need. You can't correct them. Correction reads as humility, and humility reads as confirmation." He crouched by the fire, close enough that the heat pushed at his face. "I'm not telling you this to help you. Understand that. A saint leading this company serves me poorly. Saints don't make alliances; they collect worshippers, and worshippers can't be negotiated with. I need a person at the front of this column. A tired, stubborn, frightened person whom other tired, stubborn, frightened people can actually follow."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, they laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it.

"You're the second-worst flatterer I've ever met."

"Who's the first?"

"A fae queen I haven't met yet. Give me time." Xanthe stood, brushing ash from their knees. "Fine. Tomorrow. But not your way, Cassian. I'm not going to stand up and deny it. Denials feed these things—you know that as well as I do. Every denial becomes part of the story. *The saint who claimed to be merely mortal.*" They said it in a fair imitation of Tam's reverent murmur, and Cassian winced, because it was true, and they both knew it.

"Then what?"

Xanthe looked out across the camp—at the fires, the wagons, the forty-odd faces turned always, subtly, toward the place where the light-bearer sat.

"I'm going to teach them something," Xanthe said.

**Bramble**

What Xanthe taught them, starting the next morning and continuing for four days across the ash, was wards.

Not prayers. Not blessings. Wards.

Bramble stood at the edge of the circle the first morning and watched Xanthe kneel in the grey drifts with a piece of chalky bone—they called it grave-chalk, and Bramble's skin crawled at the name, because she knew what graves the Marchlands were full of now—and draw a simple closing-line in the dirt around a cluster of tents.

"This won't stop a witch-finder," Xanthe said, loud enough for the whole camp. Their voice had changed overnight; Bramble heard it, the way it had shed its apology. "Nothing you can draw in dirt stops a determined man with a warrant. But it will stop a stray hunger. It will stop the things that came through the cracks when the stars went out—the small ones, the stupid ones, the ones that follow warmth. You draw the line closed. You put a drop of your blood at the join, because the line needs to know whose ground it's guarding. And you renew it every seventh day, or it forgets you."

An old woman raised her hand. "Is it holy?" she asked. "Does it need a blessing?"

"It needs to be drawn straight," Xanthe said. "That's the whole holiness of it. Here—" They took the old woman's hand and guided her finger along the line, correcting a waver near the join. "There. Yours now. Not mine. I couldn't renew it for you if I tried. A ward belongs to the one who draws it. That's the law of it, older than any covenant."

Bramble felt something turn over in her chest.

Because that wasn't quite true—or rather, it was true, but it wasn't the whole truth, and Xanthe had to know it wasn't. The old crossings, the real wards, the covenant itself: those belonged to Keepers. To blood. To one line of one family, hoarded and consumed and finally, catastrophically, extinguished. What Xanthe was teaching on the ash road was a smaller, humbler, older thing—the hedge-magic, the hearth-magic, the magic that had belonged to everyone before anyone thought to make a priesthood of it. Magic as craft. Magic as chores.

*You clever, terrified creature,* Bramble thought. *You can't stop them worshipping you, so you're teaching them until there's nothing left to worship.*

It worked slowly, and then it worked utterly.

By the second day, half the camp was drawing closing-lines around their own bedrolls, arguing about technique. By the third, children were racing to renew the perimeter ward at dusk, and Tam the wheelwright—who had been the saint-story's chief evangelist—was discovered teaching a group of teenagers the proper angle for a threshold-scratch, bellowing *"Straight lines! The saint said straight lines!"* with the fervor of a man who has transferred his entire religion into geometry without noticing the difference.

And Xanthe—Bramble watched this happen on the fourth evening, and made sure to remember it—Xanthe sat outside the circle of learners, mending a strap, unwatched for nearly an hour. When a young mother brought her ward-drawing over for inspection, Xanthe corrected it, praised the join-work, and sent her off to fix it herself rather than doing it for her. The woman went away pleased. Not blessed. Pleased. There was a difference, and the whole camp was learning it.

"That was well done," Bramble said, dropping down beside them afterward.

"It was a distraction," Xanthe said. "Every hour they spend arguing about chalk-lines is an hour they don't spend deciding I'm divine."

"It was more than a distraction." Bramble pulled her hood back and let the fading light hit her face, let the faint silver of her own inheritance show at her temples, where her fae blood lived. She almost never showed it. She showed it now deliberately, and saw Xanthe notice. "My mother's people had a word for what you did today. *Tairthen.* It means: the making of a thing by giving it away. You can't do it alone. That's the whole point of the word—it doesn't exist in the singular. There's no way to say *I tairthen*. Only *we*."

Xanthe stared at her. Something moved behind their eyes—something that had been waiting, maybe, since a letter in a buried cache, since a sentence about anchors and unmaking.

"We," Xanthe repeated, testing the word.

"Don't make it weird," Bramble said, and threw a pebble at them, and the moment passed, but it didn't pass. Bramble could feel it settling into the ground under the camp like a seed into spring soil, patient, certain of itself.

**Cassian**

On the sixth night, the sky above the ash road did something new.

The stars had been gone since the Night Without Stars—everyone knew that, everyone had stopped looking up—but tonight the darkness overhead developed texture. A bruised, swirling depth, like ink stirred into milk, and far to the north a single point of light appeared that was not a star. It hung motionless for a full minute, cold and white and wrong, and then it began to move, tracing a slow arc eastward, deliberate as a finger drawn across glass.

The camp went silent. Forty people looked up, and Cassian watched their faces and understood that whatever the light was, they had already decided it meant something, and that what it meant depended entirely on who told them first.

Tam found Xanthe within thirty seconds. Of course he did.

"The light-bearer," he breathed. "What does it mean? Is it a sign? Is it—"

"It means we're being looked for," Xanthe said.

The words dropped through the camp like a stone through still water. Cassian moved forward, putting himself beside Xanthe, facing the crowd with them—shoulder to shoulder, deliberately, visibly, a lord and a hedge-witch standing in the same line of fire.

"Look at me," Xanthe said, and the camp looked. "All of you. That light isn't a sign. Signs don't have trajectories. It's a search-pattern—fae-lens, or something built on one, sweeping the roads for movement. Which means someone with resources knows people are crossing the ash, and wants to know where they're going." They paused, and Cassian saw them choose the next words with the care of a sapper choosing footing. "I'm not going to lie to you. If they find us, some of us die. That's the truth of the road we're on. But here's the other truth." They raised one hand, and the gold light at the wrist flared bright enough to throw shadows. "Every one of you drew a ward today. Every one of you renewed it at dusk. That light up there"—they pointed—"cannot see through a properly drawn closing-line. It's true. Ask Bramble Ferrow; her people invented half of what I taught you. So tonight, nobody in this camp is helpless. Nobody is waiting for a miracle. Tonight, this camp is defended by forty people who know what they're doing."

Silence. Then Tam, gruffly: "Forty-one. I counted the saint."

Laughter broke the tension like ice cracking, and the camp dissolved into motion—wards checked, watches doubled, fires banked low. Cassian lingered at the edge of it, watching Xanthe stand in the middle of the organized chaos they had created, and felt the old strategist in him take notes even as the better part of him felt something uncomfortably like awe.

*They did it,* he thought. *In six days they turned a congregation into a garrison. Not by denying the myth. By outgrowing it.*

Later, when the camp slept, he found Xanthe standing watch at the northern perimeter, arms crossed, staring at the point of light where it hung now, stationary, patient, at the exact center of the sky's darkness.

"It hasn't moved in an hour," Xanthe said quietly, without turning. "It's not searching anymore. It's waiting."

"For what?"

"For us to move. It's marking us." Xanthe's voice was steady, but their glow had gone strange—pulsing faster, brighter, answering something. "Cassian. My blood does this. It lights up when the Otherworld presses close. It's never done it like this before. Whatever that light is, it's not looking for refugees." They turned, and in the dark their eyes caught the wrist-glow and held it, and for the first time since the ash road began, Xanthe Voss looked genuinely afraid. "It's looking for me. And it's not Vane's. Vane's people burn things. This—" they nodded at the sky "—this is patient. This is court-magic."

Behind them, far to the south, at the edge of hearing, a single horse was moving fast along the old military road, riding through the night without a lantern, carrying a rider who had killed her superior officer at sundown and was following a rumor of a glowing saint north through the ash.

The light in the sky waited. The company broke camp in the dark, wards drawn, silent, forty-one strong.

And the horseman—horsewoman—drew closer.
