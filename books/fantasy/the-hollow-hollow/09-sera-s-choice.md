## 9. Sera's Choice

The order came at dawn, written in the Inquisitor General's own hand, and Sera Dune read it three times before the words stopped sliding away from her.

*By decree of His Excellency Aldric Vane, Protector of the Realm: the village of Mirren's Ford is declared a nest of illicit charm-craft. All practitioners, harbors, and abettors to be executed by fire, per Protocol Nine. No exemptions for minors. Captain Dune to oversee.*

No exemptions for minors.

She stood in the command tent with the paper trembling in her grip and forty witch-finders waiting outside for her voice, and she thought: *I have burned charm-sellers before.* She had. Three years of it. She had stood in market squares in her grey coat with its silver buttons and watched old women and hedge-wizards and one boy of perhaps fourteen go into the smoke, and every time she had told herself the same story: *the law is the wall between us and the dark, and walls are built from hard stones.*

But the wall had never before been ordered to crush children.

"Captain?" Lieutenant Hask poked his head through the tent flap. He was young, earnest, still polished his buttons every night. "The column's ready. Weather's clear for a burning."

"The weather," Sera said, "is not the concern."

She folded the order along its creases, once, twice, until it was a small hard square she could close her fist around. Outside, the camp was striking itself down with the efficiency she had drilled into them herself—tents collapsing, wagons groaning, torches being lit even though the sun was climbing, because Protocol Nine required torches and Protocol Nine did not care about the sun.

Mirren's Ford was half a day's march east. A river village, wool and barley, a stone bridge over brown water. She had passed through it twice on patrol. There had been a woman there who sold luck-knots from a cart—red thread, simple knots, the kind of thing that might be craft or might be magic or might be nothing at all but comfort sold for a copper. Sera had bought one once. She still had it, knotted around the strap of her saddlebag where no one could see it, and she had told herself that too was nothing.

Now she understood what the knot had been. A warning. Her own hand, reaching back through time to grab her sleeve.

"Captain Dune." The voice came from behind her, smooth as oil poured on water, and she turned to find Inquisitor General Corvane's aide standing at the tent's edge—a thin man named Pell whose smile never reached past his teeth. He carried a leather satchel she knew well. Reports. "A word before you march."

"You have words."

"His Grace wishes me to convey his confidence in you." Pell stepped inside, uninvited, and set the satchel on the map table. "And to deliver the supplementary directive." He drew out a single sheet. "Regarding the children."

Sera looked at him. "The order already covers the children."

"It covers their execution. This covers their *use*." Pell laid the sheet flat and turned it so she could read it, and he watched her read it, and she knew—he wanted her to read it. That was the point of Pell. He was the Inquisitor General's instrument for finding the cracks in officers and pressing on them until they widened or healed. "The younger ones, those under ten, are to be transported to the harvesting camps rather than burned. Their blood is fresher. His Grace has found that the starlight takes better root in young vessels."

Sera read the directive. It was written in the bland administrative language of evil—the passive voice doing its usual work, *vessels are to be conveyed*, *processing is to commence upon arrival*—and beneath the language, in her mind's eye, rose the image of the harvesting camps she had heard whispered about for a year and officially denied existing for that same year. The camps in the ash country north of the border where the wards had fallen. Where wagons went in heavy and came out light.

Where the stars' stolen light was wrung out of living blood, drop by drop, to fill the bottles Aldric Vane drank from.

"You've gone pale, Captain," Pell said gently.

"I'm considering logistics."

"Of course you are. You're the best officer the Order has. That's why you were chosen." He gathered his papers. "One more thing. The Voss creature—the one that slipped your net in Greyharbor. His Grace takes a personal interest. If your operation in Mirren's Ford yields intelligence on its whereabouts, you'll be commended beyond anything in your experience." He smiled his toothless smile. "And if it doesn't, well. There are always other captains."

He left. Sera stood alone in the tent with the two orders and the silence, and around her wrist, hidden under her glove, the skin prickled where a luck-knot's red thread had rested for three years like a question she'd refused to answer.

*The law is the wall,* she told herself one last time.

Then she thought about a child under ten, bound in a wagon, conveyed to processing, and the wall came down all at once—not crumbling but shattering, the way ice breaks, all along its length, everywhere, irreparably—and what was left standing in the rubble was just Sera Dune, thirty-one years old, holding a piece of paper that ordered her to feed children to a hungry man.

She picked up her sword belt. She buckled it on. And she began, very quietly, to plan a different kind of operation.

* * *

They reached Mirren's Ford at midafternoon, and the village looked exactly as villages look when they don't yet know they're going to die: smoke rising peaceably from chimneys, geese arguing by the bridge, a dog trotting the road with purposeless joy. Sera rode at the head of the column and felt every eye of her forty witch-finders on her back, waiting for the order, and she gave none of them the order, because the order she intended to give existed only inside her and had a different shape entirely.

She halted the column in the square. The villagers had begun to gather at doorways with the wary stillness of people who recognize grey coats. The charm-seller woman—Maren, her name was Maren, Sera had learned it on her first patrol and never let herself use it—stood by her cart, and her eyes found Sera's across the square, and she went very still, and her hand moved to the red thread at her wrist.

"Lieutenant Hask," Sera said. "Take twelve men. Sweep the eastern lane. Report any charm-craft, any sigils, any practitioners."

"Yes, Captain." He saluted and went.

"Sergeant Orla. Western lane. Same."

"On it."

She sent them off in pieces, six here, eight there, thinning her force around the square like a woman unraveling a sweater from the hem, until only nine remained with her in the square proper, and the villagers watched this strange choreography without understanding it, and neither, quite, did her own soldiers.

"Captain," said Corporal Vess, frowning at the emptying lanes. "Protocol Nine calls for full sweep and pyre construction. We should be raising the stake."

"We should," Sera agreed. "Corporal, ride back along the road a mile. There was a tinker's cart stalled near the milestone. See if he needs assistance."

"There wasn't any—"

"Now, Corporal."

He went, bewildered. Six left. The dog had come to sit near Sera's horse, panting up at her, and she looked down at it and thought absurdly: *even the animals know something's wrong today.*

Maren hadn't moved from her cart. Neither had the children clustered behind her skirts—four of them, the eldest maybe eleven, the youngest a girl of six sucking her thumb and staring at the witch-finders' torches with the frank curiosity of someone who has never seen a person burn.

Sera dismounted. She walked to the cart. She heard the six remaining soldiers shift behind her, hands drifting toward weapons, uncertain. She stopped an arm's length from Maren and spoke low, under the wind, under the creak of the cart.

"How many can you hide in the cellars under the mill?"

Maren's breath caught. "Captain—"

"*How many.*"

"All of us. The mill cellar runs to the riverbank. Old smuggler's tunnel, from my grandmother's day." Her eyes were wide and wet and fierce. "Why would you—"

"Because in one hour I give an order I cannot give, and in two hours men arrive who will search this village properly, and if they find anyone, I will not be able to stop them, because I will be dead or worse." Sera kept her voice flat, drill-ground flat, the voice that brooked no discussion. "Get everyone below. Take nothing that burns. Go now, while my people are scattered."

"You'll hang for this."

"I'll burn for it, most likely. They're traditionalists." She turned away, then stopped. "Maren. The luck-knots you sell. Do they work?"

The woman blinked. "A little. Not much. Enough."

"Good. Knot one for yourself and get underground."

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong: quickly, and from an unexpected quarter.

Sera had gotten the last of the villagers into the mill cellar and recalled her scattered squads with excuses fraying at every seam—Hask returning suspicious, Orla asking questions, the net of her lies visibly tearing—and she was buying time with a fabricated report of rebel activity to the south when Pell's riders came up the road.

Four of them, and Pell himself, arriving hours ahead of schedule, because of course he had. Because watching was his whole trade.

"Captain Dune." He sat his horse in the square and looked slowly around—at the empty doorways, the cold hearths, the abandoned carts, the total absence of anyone to burn—and his smile spread like frost. "How curious. I was given to understand there was a village here."

"There was a delay. Reconnaissance—"

"Don't." He dismounted, unhurried. Behind him, his four riders spread out with professional ease, cutting angles. Her own nine soldiers stood in a confused crescent, looking from Pell to Sera and back, understanding arriving in their faces at different speeds. "Don't insult both of us. I've read your file, Captain. Every commendation, every protocol followed to the letter—until Greyharbor. Until the Voss creature vanished out of your net like smoke. And now a village empties itself the moment you arrive." He drew a slim black baton from his coat—the suppression rod, the thing they used on captured witches, and her stomach clenched to see it pointed nowhere yet. "His Grace asked me to watch you. I confess I hoped I'd be wrong."

"Pell." Sera kept her hands loose at her sides, away from her sword, buying seconds. "You haven't seen what's in those camps. I have the reports. I've read what happens to the—"

"I've *written* some of those reports." He said it pleasantly. "That's the difference between us, Captain. You still think the Order serves the realm. The Order serves His Grace, and His Grace is becoming something that will not need realms at all. Now." He raised the rod. "You'll surrender your sword, and we'll proceed to the pyre question, and depending on your answers, you may yet die a soldier instead of a traitor."

Behind him, Hask's hand had gone to his own hilt, and his face was a war—loyalty against horror, training against the dawning knowledge of what Protocol Nine actually fed.

Sera made her choice the way the ice had broken: all at once, everywhere.

She moved on Pell before he finished raising the rod. Three steps, draw, and her blade took his wrist at the joint—the rod spinning away into the dust—and then the point was at his throat and her voice was carrying across the square, drill-ground flat, leaving no room for discussion.

"Stand down. All of you. That's an order."

Her nine witch-finders froze. Pell's four riders hesitated, blades half-drawn, suddenly unsure whose authority survived contact with this moment.

"He ordered children burned," Sera said. "Not arrested. Not questioned. Burned—or worse, sent to the camps, which is worse, and every one of you has heard the stories and every one of you has pretended not to. I'm done pretending. Vane is drinking starlight out of people. He's not protecting the realm; he's eating it. And I—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding it had been the whole disease— "*I am done.*"

Silence. Wind. The dog barked once, absurdly, and lay down.

Then Hask stepped forward—her earnest lieutenant, her button-polisher—and drew his sword fully, and turned, so that he stood facing Pell's riders beside her rather than behind her. "I never saw anything," he said hoarsely. "Whatever happens. I never saw."

Orla joined him. Then Vess, riding back into the square at the worst possible moment, taking it in with one glance, and swinging down from his horse to stand with them. One by one. Not all—two of the nine backed away toward Pell's riders, faces gray, and Sera let them go, because she would not start her rebellion by making prisoners of the frightened—but seven stood with her, and seven was more than she had dared hope and fewer than she needed, and it would have to be enough.

Pell, blood running down his cuff, smiled his toothless smile up at her blade. "You're dead, Captain. By morning your name will be on every post in the province. Traitor. Heretic-kin. They'll hunt you into the ash country and—"

"Yes," Sera said. "Probably." She struck him unconscious rather than kill him—not mercy exactly, but a refusal to begin this the way the Order would—and his riders, leaderless and outnumbered now, wheeled and fled west toward the Inquisitor General's lines.

She stood in the empty square of the saved village, sword bare, treason complete, and felt—beneath the terror, beneath the grief for the grey coat she had loved—something terrifyingly like relief.

"Hask. Get everyone above ground. We march within the hour—all of us, villagers included, anyone who wants to come. North and west, toward the free cities." She sheathed her sword. "And someone bring me that satchel Pell left in my tent. The reports. All of them."

"Why, Captain?"

"Because if we're going to be hunted as traitors," Sera said, "we're going to be hunted carrying proof of what Vane really is. Evidence is a weapon. I intend to arm every city between here and Vellamere."

* * *

They traveled by night for three days, forty-three souls and dwindling—some villagers peeled away to kin in other towns, two of her seven soldiers slipped off in the dark, unable to live as outlaws—and by the fourth dawn Sera rode at the head of a smaller, stranger company: Maren and her grandchildren, a miller, a shepherd, four witch-finders in coats whose silver buttons they had pried off and buried, and a satchel of documents that made Sera's hands shake every time she inventoried them. Camp manifests. Transport ledgers. Yield figures, written in tons, of refined starlight, with columns labeled *vessel losses* as casually as a grain merchant tallies spoilage.

On the fifth day, on a high moorland road where the grass grew pale around stones that hummed faintly when touched—old ward-stones, dead since the night the stars went out—they found the company they had been hearing rumors of for two days.

Sera saw them first from the ridge: a ragged line moving west, wagons and walkers, armed and unarmored, and at its center a figure on foot whom the refugees on the road had spoken of in the reverent hushed tones people usually saved for saints. *The glowing one. The Voss. Blood like lanternlight.* She had planned to avoid them—outlaws were outlaws, and her treason was fresh enough without compounding it—but the moor narrowed at the river crossing, and their scouts had clearly seen hers, and there was no avoiding a meeting that both parties had already decided must happen.

So she rode down alone, hands visible, sword left with Hask, the satchel strapped openly to her saddle, and halted twenty paces from the front of their column while archers' bows bent and a half-fae woman with mismatched eyes put an arrow to string with the lazy competence of someone who'd done it ten thousand times.

"I'm looking for Xanthe Voss," Sera called.

A stir ran through the column. A figure pushed forward—younger than Sera expected, travel-worn, wrapped in a patched cloak, with eyes that held a wariness she recognized because she'd spent five years putting it into other people's faces. Up close, even at twenty paces, Sera could see it: a faint luminance under the skin of their wrists, like coals under ash. The blood the refugees worshipped. The blood Vane wanted.

"You're witch-finder grey," Xanthe Voss said. Not a question. Their voice was level, but the bows stayed bent. "Coat's missing its buttons."

"I removed them myself. Along with my oath." Sera swung down slowly and let them see her empty hands. "My name is Sera Dune. Five days ago I was captain of the third witch-finder company. As of five days ago, I'm a traitor with a price on her head, thirty-eight refugees under my protection, and—" she lifted the satchel, held it out slightly from her body so they could see it— "every document I could carry out of Vane's headquarters. Star-harvest manifests. Camp locations. Names. Numbers." Her voice roughened despite her. "Children, some of them. He's bottling them."

The half-fae archer lowered her bow a fraction, eyes narrowing. An older man in exile's finery murmured something to Xanthe that Sera couldn't hear. And Xanthe Voss came forward alone, across the twenty paces of trampled grass, and stopped an arm's length away, and studied her face with an intensity that felt like being read by weather.

"The last witch-finder who stood where you're standing," Xanthe said quietly, "was hunting me through Greyharbor. I only got away because she hesitated. I've wondered ever since what it cost her to hesitate."

"It cost me everything," Sera said. "And it was the first honest act of my life." She held out the satchel. "This is evidence, not a gift. Use it however serves. But I'll tell you what I told my soldiers: Vane isn't building a tyranny. Tyrannies want the world intact. Whatever he's becoming wants it *consumed*, and these documents prove it, and every free city that sees them is one more city that won't stand behind him when it matters."

Xanthe took the satchel. Their fingers brushed the leather, and where they touched, Sera felt a warmth like sunlight through a window—faint, unmistakable, alive—and saw the same recognition flicker across Xanthe's face, and the wariness in those eyes shifted into something more complicated. Not trust. Not yet. But the beginning of the shape trust gets built in.

"A witch-finder captain," Xanthe said, "defecting with proof, chasing down a band of outlaws on the open road. Half my company will want to hang you and the other half will want to follow you."

"And you?"

Xanthe glanced back at their people—the archer, the exiled lord, the ragged line of them stretched down the moor—and then at the thirty-eight behind Sera, and something in their expression settled, decided.

"I want to know everything in that satchel," they said. "Every camp. Every name. Ride with us, Captain Dune. You can tell me on the way." A pause, and the ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "And you can start by telling me how a woman who spent her life enforcing the law decided, in one morning, to break it."

Sera looked back once at the road they'd come by—south, toward the smoke of the world Vane was making—and then forward, west, toward the pale horizon where the stars would someday need to return.

"It wasn't one morning," she said, and urged her horse alongside theirs. "It was three years of luck-knots. I just finally read one."
