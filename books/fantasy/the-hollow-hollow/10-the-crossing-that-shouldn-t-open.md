## 10. The Crossing That Shouldn't Open

Bramble Ferrow had crossed the veil two hundred and eleven times in her life, and she had never once done it standing still.

That was the first wrongness, and she hated it before she even understood it. Crossings were like rivers—you went at them moving, quick and low, trusting momentum to carry you through the thin place before it noticed you were there. You did not stand on a hillside of ash at dusk with six mortals and a defected witch-finder behind you and *wait* for a door that should not exist.

But there it was.

She could feel it the way you felt a tooth about to ache: a low, sweet pressure in her back teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the soles of her feet where they pressed the dead ground. The crossing sat in the hollow between two burned hills like water sitting in a cupped hand, and it was *open*. Not cracked, not leaking, not the furtive half-breath of a wild gate—it stood open the way a mouth stands open when the thing behind it has forgotten how to close.

"That's it?" Sera Dune asked. The witch-finder—former witch-finder, Bramble corrected herself, though the woman still wore the grey coat with its sigils scraped off, leaving pale ghosts of thread—came up beside her and squinted at what looked, to mortal eyes, like nothing at all. "It doesn't look like anything."

"It looks like nothing," Bramble said. "That's how you know it's bad."

Behind them, Cassian Roe's soldiers had made camp out of sight below the ridge, because Cassian had decided three days ago that his men would panic if they saw the actual crossing, and Bramble had decided not to argue, because she agreed with him. What came with them instead was worse: Xanthe Voss, hugging their own elbows against a cold that wasn't weather; Wren, the youngest of the outlaw charm-sellers, who hadn't stopped humming since the borderlands; old Tam with his ward-stones clicking in his pockets; Marisol and Dagny, twins in everything but temperament; and the refugee girl, Pell, who had followed them for eleven miles after Xanthe taught her a door-ward and refused to be sent home to a village that no longer existed.

"Everyone hold hands," Bramble said.

"I'm not holding hands," said Dagny.

"You're holding hands," Bramble said, "or you come back through without your shadow, and I'm not chasing your shadow. I've chased shadows. They're faster than they look."

Dagny took Tam's hand with the expression of someone performing a great sacrifice, and Tam, who had been holding hands with strangers since before any of them were born, patted her knuckles kindly.

Xanthe came last. They stopped beside Bramble at the lip of the hollow, and Bramble watched them look at the open door with an expression she couldn't read—hunger and dread braided together, the way you'd look at a letter from a dead person that you somehow knew was real.

"Your eyes," Xanthe said quietly. "They're doing something."

"They do that now." Bramble touched the corner of her own eye and her fingers came away faintly luminous, like moth dust. Her fae-sight had been fading since the night the stars died—every half-blood in the free cities said the same, the world going grainy and dim at its edges, colors draining out of things only fae blood could see—and then, three days ago, near this hill, it had come *back*, all at once, too bright, like a man deaf for years suddenly hearing every dog in the county scream. "It means we're close to something that wants to be seen. Or something that wants to eat us. With my family those are usually the same thing."

"Comforting."

"I'm a smuggler, Voss. Comfort costs extra."

* * *

Going through was like being swallowed by a memory.

Bramble had told them what to expect—the cold that came from inside rather than outside, the way sound arrived late, the way you must not answer if anything called your name in your mother's voice—but telling was different from knowing, and she heard Wren make a small wounded sound as the veil took them, and felt Tam's grip go rigid in hers.

Then they were through, and Bramble forgot, for one whole breath, that she was afraid.

The edge of the Otherworld opened around them like the inside of a cathedral built by something that loved beauty and understood nothing else. Trees the height of towers, silver-barked, hung with leaves like stained glass, and light coming from everywhere and nowhere—old light, honey-colored, pooling on ground soft as moss over sleepers. A river ran nearby without running, each drop hanging just slightly too long in the air, so the whole stream glittered like a chain of held breaths. Flowers turned to watch them pass—not following, just *watching*, with the patient interest of cats.

"Oh," said Pell, the refugee girl, and started to cry without seeming to notice she was doing it.

"Yeah," Bramble said. "I know."

This was the country her mother had sung about. This was the reason half-bloods spent their whole lives homesick for a place they'd never seen. And it was dying. That was the thing none of the songs mentioned—that beauty like this, unmoored from anything, curdles. Because Bramble's returning sight showed her what the mortals' eyes slid past: the stained-glass leaves were cracked along veins gone grey as ash. The river's held drops trembled, some of them falling upward. And the flowers that turned to watch—they weren't turning because they were curious. They were turning because they were starving, and anything that moved might be food.

"The stars," Xanthe said. It wasn't a question. They'd worked it out on the road, all of them, the way you work out a wound's shape from the way a man holds himself. "The Otherworld fed on starlight too. Same as the mortal side. When the covenant broke—"

"When the covenant broke, everything downstream of it started starving," Bramble finished. "Aye. This is downstream."

Something screamed, far off, in a voice like a violin bow drawn across a bridge cable. Nobody asked what it was. Bramble was grateful. She didn't have a lie ready.

* * *

They walked for what felt like hours and might have been minutes—time ran strange here even at the best of times, and these were not the best of times—and Bramble led them by the fading glow of her own eyes, following the pressure in her teeth toward the Hollow Hollow. The company had gone quiet in the way people go quiet in a sickroom. Even Dagny.

It was Wren who broke first, because Wren was fifteen and had been humming since Greyharbor and didn't have the sense to be afraid in the correct order.

"Bramble. Your kin. The ones trapped over here." The boy trotted up alongside her, keeping close the way children kept close to fires. "Is this where they are? Could we see them?"

"No." The word came out harder than she meant. She softened it, or tried. "My mother's people keep to the deep courts, days' walk from any crossing even when the crossings are behaving. And they're not—" She gestured at the cracked leaves, the trembling river, the watching flowers. "They're not having visitors."

"But they're alive?"

"For now." Bramble kicked a fallen leaf and watched it crumble into grey moths that dispersed without flying anywhere. "Everything over here is alive the way a candle is alive. Pretty, and burning down, and no way to make more of itself. That's what the covenant *was*, boy. Not a wall. A hearth. Mortal belief and mortal seasons fed across, starlight and fae-light fed back, and both sides stayed lit. Somebody cut the wick five years ago"—she glanced at Xanthe, and didn't finish the sentence, because she'd already said it once, angrily, on the second night, and once was enough—"and now everyone's burning their own furniture to stay warm."

"And the things screaming?"

"Those are what happens when furniture runs out."

Wren shut up. But he walked closer to her after that, and Bramble found she didn't mind.

* * *

The trouble came at the orchard.

There was no other word for it: an orchard of trees that bore fruit shaped like lanterns, glowing softly gold, arranged in rows too straight for anything wild. Bramble knew the type—cultivated groves tended by lesser fae, hedge-fae, the ones who traded rather than schemed—and her stomach lifted stupidly with hope before her sight caught up with the truth. The lantern-fruit was dim. The rows were crooked. And the keeper of the orchard sat slumped against the largest tree like a puppet with its strings cut.

It looked, at first glance, like a woman made of woven willow. At second glance, the willow was growing *through* her, roots threading her arms, her throat, one eye socket, patient as ivy. Her remaining eye tracked them as they approached, and it was bright—too bright, fever-bright, the brightness of something spending itself it didn't have.

"Mortals," the orchard-keeper rasped. Her voice sounded like wind through a keyhole. "Mortals walking free in the twilight. How long has it been? How long since the gates went quiet?"

"Five years," Bramble said, in the trade-tongue, careful and slow. "We're passing through, mistress. We'll take nothing."

"Take nothing." The eye rolled toward her, found the glow of her eyes, and fixed there. Something moved in the ruined face that might have been recognition, or hunger, or grief—all three wore the same expression out here, lately. "A ferrow-child. Half-and-half. Oh, little half-and-half, do you know what we ate when the light failed? Do you know what the beautiful ones ate first?" She laughed, and willow creaked. "They ate the small courts first. We were closest to the ground. Closest to the dark."

Tam shifted his weight, ward-stones whispering. Sera's hand drifted to the knife she'd taken off her dead superior, and Bramble saw the ex-witch-finder cataloguing threats with the same flat competence she'd probably used her whole career, and saw her hate herself for it a half-second later.

"We can help," Xanthe said.

Everyone looked at them. Bramble looked hardest of all, because Xanthe Voss had a habit of saying things in exactly that voice—quiet, level, absolutely certain—and every time, something got more complicated.

"We're going to restore the covenant," Xanthe said. "At the Hollow Hollow. Before the dark moon. If it holds—if the crossings open and the light comes back—" They knelt, slowly, in front of the rooted woman, the way you'd kneel to a child or a queen, and Bramble realized they weren't sure which the creature was either. "What's your name?"

The orchard-keeper stared. Then, in a voice gone sly and small: "Names cost. Names are food, out here, now. Why would you ask a hungry thing its name, mortal? So it owes you? So you own it?"

"So I can tell it the truth properly," Xanthe said. "You don't give your name away. I'm asking permission to use it. There's a difference. My mistress taught me that." Their voice caught, just barely, on *my mistress*, and smoothed itself over. "There's a difference. There was always supposed to be a difference."

The eye blinked. Willow creaked. Somewhere far off, the bridge-cable thing screamed again, closer.

"Lissel," the orchard-keeper said at last. "I was Lissel, of the hedgerow court, and I kept forty trees and a toll-road, and I have eaten three of my own cousins since the dark came, and I will eat you if you kneel much longer, little mortal, because you smell like blood and blood smells like morning."

Xanthe rose. Didn't step back. Bramble marked that, filed it away with all the other marks she'd been filing since Greyharbor: the Voss girl-or-boy-or-whatever-it-was kept kneeling in front of hungry things and did not flinch, and either that was courage or it was a death wish wearing courage's coat, and Bramble hadn't decided which yet.

"Hold on until the dark moon," Xanthe said. "Four days. If we fail, you'll be past caring, and if we don't—" They drew a knife across their own palm, quick, matter-of-fact, and let three drops fall onto the orchard soil. Where they struck, the ground *steamed*, and grass came up green, impossibly green, violently green, a hand's span of spring in a country of ash. Lissel made a sound like a hinge and a hymn. "A taste. Hold on with that."

They walked on. Behind them, Bramble heard the orchard-keeper weeping among her trees, and the trees, very faintly, beginning to grow.

"Well," said Dagny, finally. "That was either the bravest or stupidest—"

"Both," said Tam. "It's always both. Keep walking."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen tower—or what had been a tower; it lay on its side now, grown over with moss that glowed faintly blue, like a drowned ship's hull. No fire. Fire meant nothing here anyway; Bramble had tried a flame on her third crossing, decades ago, and watched it burn downward. They huddled instead under Wren's shared warmth-charms and Tam's ward-ring, and the blue moss gave enough light to see each other's faces, which was maybe a mistake, because nobody's face looked good.

Pell slept first, curled against Marisol. Then Wren, mid-sentence, the way children fall. The others took watches. Bramble didn't take one; she sat apart, on the tower's root-lifted stone, doing what her mother's people called *listening down*—letting her fading sight sink beneath the surface of the land to feel what moved underneath.

A lot moved underneath. That was the problem.

She heard Xanthe's footsteps before she saw them, soft on the moss. They sat down beside her without asking, which was another thing about them—no sense of rank, no sense of distance, as if the whole world were a kitchen and everyone in it belonged at the same table.

"It's worse than you told us," Xanthe said. "Out there. Past the edge. Isn't it."

"Aye."

"How much worse?"

Bramble considered lying, decided it was a waste of good lies. "When I was a girl, my mother brought me to a crossing at midsummer. Just to look. The Otherworld side was—there's no word. Every color you've ever felt. Music coming out of the stones. And the folk—" She shook her head. "Thousands of them, Voss. Crowding the crossings on our side, begging the gates to open wide enough for a hand, a letter, a song. Half-bloods like me were rich. People paid us to carry messages to relatives they'd been barred from for a century. Now the crossings are wounds and the folk behind them are—" She looked at her hands, at the moth-dust glow clinging to her fingertips, dimmer than it had been that morning. "Now I'm the last message anyone's going to get. Unless you're right about the Hollow Hollow."

"And if I'm wrong?"

"Then we're four days' walk from becoming furniture."

Xanthe laughed—a short, startled, genuine laugh, quickly strangled—and Bramble found herself smiling despite everything, because that was the other thing about the Voss girl-or-boy-or-whatever: they laughed at the worst possible moments, and it was always honest.

"My mistress used to say something like that," Xanthe said. "'If I'm wrong, we won't be around long enough for it to matter.' She thought gallows humor was a form of prayer." They were quiet a moment. The blue moss breathed light. Far off, the bridge-cable thing screamed again, and this time Bramble heard something in it that loosened her spine: it was screaming in *words*.

*Hungry,* it said, in Old Fae, in a voice like tearing silk. *So hungry. So bright, so near, so—*

"What is that?" Xanthe whispered. "Bramble. What is that?"

Bramble didn't answer. She was staring east, past the sleeping company, past the broken tower, to where the horizon of the Otherworld's edge should have faded into the deep country's glow.

Instead, it was black. A blackness with edges. A blackness moving.

And her fading sight, her dying, flickering, moth-dust sight, flared suddenly white-hot with warning, and every ward-stone in Tam's pockets began to ring at once.

"Up," Bramble said, standing, loud, no smuggler's caution left in her. "UP! Everyone up—we have company, and it hasn't eaten in five years!"

The stars were gone from both worlds. But something out there in the dark had learned to hunt by other lights—and the brightest light for fifty miles, the thing it had smelled across a dying country, was the blood of Xanthe Voss.
