## 11. Court of Thorns and Hunger

The court announced us with a sound like a knife being drawn very slowly from a sheath.

I felt it before I heard it—a vibration in the marrow of my teeth, in the hollow behind my sternum where my blood had been glowing brighter every day since we crossed. The Otherworld's edge had been beautiful the way a trap is beautiful, all silver moss and fruit that sang when you touched it, but here, deeper in, beauty had curdled into something with intent.

We came up out of the bramble-hollow onto a plain of black grass, and there it was: the court. Not a castle—fae didn't build, not really, they *grew* things and called it architecture. A hill like an overturned bowl, its flanks studded with doors at every angle, some upright, some sideways, one upside down above our heads with light spilling down from its threshold like spilled honey. Torches burned without smoke. Music played somewhere beneath the earth, a melody I recognized and could not name until I did: it was a lullaby my mistress used to hum, worn smooth by centuries, older than any mortal throat that had ever sung it.

"They know we're coming," Sera said quietly. She'd been quiet for two days, since the ford, since she'd started sleeping with her sword unsheathed beside her. "They've known since we crossed."

"Good," Bramble said. Her wings were folded tight under her cloak, and her skin had gone pale as birch bark. Being home was hurting her; I could see it in the way she kept touching the ground, checking it, the way you check a wound. "Let them waste their dread on us."

Cassian said nothing. He rode at the rear, immaculate even now, his exile's grey coat brushed clean, and he had spent the last two days asking small questions. How many doors does the court have. Who keeps the queen's calendar. What is the protocol for a mortal address. I'd thought he was being careful. Later, I would understand he'd been doing arithmetic.

The black grass parted for us. That was the first courtesy, and courtesies from the fae are never free.

* * *

We were received in a hall grown from living wood so old the grain had forgotten which direction trees grow upward. Pillars like petrified lightning. A ceiling lost in shadow, hung with lanterns that held no fire but rather captive slivers of night itself—and I understood, with a lurch, what those were. Fragments of the stolen stars, hoarded here, dimming year by year as the covenant starved. Even the fae were rationing their darkness.

The court assembled on tiers of root and stone: figures that were mostly human-shaped and mostly wrong. Some wore antlers like crowns. One had eyes set too far apart, kind and terrible at once. At the center, on a throne of white thorn, sat the Queen of the Autumn Court—not the High Queen, I would learn later, but autumn was who answered when the covenant broke, because autumn is the season of debts come due.

She was beautiful the way frost on a drowning man's lips is beautiful. She did not rise.

"Mortals," she said, and the word fell through the hall like a coin down a well. "You cross the veil uninvited, unwashed, and unpaid, and stand in the court of thorns as though you belong to it."

"We come invited by the covenant itself," I said. My voice held. Five years of hiding had taught me how to make a voice hold. "I am Xanthe Voss, heir of the last Keeper. The border wards are broken. The stars are gone. We've come to ask what must be done to mend them."

Silence. Then laughter—not loud, but everywhere, rising from every tier at once, the way wind rises through wheat.

"The Keeper," said a figure with a mouth where its eyes should be, "died alone and unmourned."

"The Keeper," said another, "hid among mortals like a rat in a granary."

"And this," said a third, leaning forward, and its face shifted as it moved the way faces do in dreams, "this little ember claims her title."

*Their* title. The pronoun landed strangely, deliberately wrong, and I felt Sera shift beside me, hand near her blade. But I'd been misnamed by experts for five years. Witch-finders had called me worse.

"I claim no title," I said. "I claim a debt. Your covenant bound both realms. When it broke, it broke for everyone. The Otherworld is starving—we saw it. Your crossings are closed, your wild places dying back. You need the mending as much as we do. So tell me the price, and let's stop performing for each other."

Something flickered across the Queen's face. Interest, maybe. Or appetite. In the fae courts, I suspected, they looked much the same.

"Bold," she said. "Boldness is cheap in the young." She gestured, and one of the captive-night lanterns swung closer, and by its borrowed darkness I saw her properly: hair like dead leaves, a crown of thorn grown through her skull, eyes the color of the moment after sunset. "You want the truth of the covenant, little ember? Here it is. Mortals broke it first."

The word went through the hall like a blade through wet cloth.

"Three hundred years ago your kings grew greedy," the Queen continued. "They wanted crossings they controlled, magic they owned, fae labor they could tax. They swore oaths of binding upon binding, each one a knot in the great weave, each one pulled tighter than the last. The Keepers warned them. The Keepers were ignored. And when the weave finally began to fray—" her smile showed teeth slightly too fine, "—your ancestors solved the problem the way mortals solve everything. They stopped feeding it and hoped it would die slowly enough to forget."

"That's not true," Bramble said sharply. "My grandmother remembered the old days. The crossings worked both ways. The tithe went both ways."

"Bramble Ferrow." The Queen said her name like tasting something spoiled. "Half-blood. Returned at last, are you? Tell me, child, how many of your kin crossed over in the last exodus and found the door shut behind them?" She turned back to me before Bramble could answer, and the question died in Bramble's throat like a snuffed candle. "Your bloodline made those doors, Voss. Your blood locked them. Whatever your mistress told you, whatever tender lies she fed you at her hearth—the covenant did not simply break. It was broken. Deliberately. By Keepers who decided one anchor was easier to manage than a shared oath."

I heard the words. I catalogued them. I refused, for the moment, to believe them—but I filed them away next to everything else my mistress had never quite explained: why she lived alone. Why she taught me wards and never history. Why, on the last night, she'd pressed her signet ring into my hand and said only, *it was supposed to be many, and then it wasn't.*

"You're telling me the truth badly," I said, "on purpose, to watch me bleed. Fine. I'll take it anyway. If mortals broke it, then mortals owe the mending. That's why I'm here. Name the price."

And the whole hall went still, the way a forest goes still when something large begins to move.

"The price," the Queen said softly, "is blood."

"I know. Mine. That's why—"

"Not a cupful, little ember. Not a scar, not a drop upon a stone. The anchor of a covenant is not a payment. It is a *position*." She rose from the throne of white thorn, and every torch in the hall bowed toward her. "Your mistress held it for sixty years and it ate her from the inside, thread by thread, year by year—that is why she hid, why she aged, why she died alone with her veins full of light and her name half-erased. The covenant requires an anchor. A living soul woven into its fabric, feeding it, holding it, until the weave consumes them entirely. That is the price. That has always been the price. Your blood opens the door. Your life pays the toll."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere above, in the upside-down door, honey-light dripped.

I had known. That was the worst of it—I had known since the letter, since *before the next dark moon*, since the way my mistress had written my name at the end of her last message like an apology. I had carried the knowledge across a burned country and through a starving crossing and I had not said it aloud to anyone, and now it stood in front of the whole court wearing a crown.

"How long?" Sera asked. Her voice was flat, captain-flat, the voice she used for casualty reports. "How long does the anchor last?"

"As long as it lasts," the Queen said. "Some burn fast. Your mistress was strong; she lasted decades. This one—" her gaze slid over me like a hand, "—is already half-lit. Perhaps less. Does it matter?"

"Yes," Sera said. "It matters."

"It doesn't," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Bramble's face had gone grey. Sera's jaw was working. And Cassian—

Cassian was watching the Queen. Not me. Watching her the way a merchant watches scales settle.

"If the price is agreed," I said, and my voice sounded far away to my own ears, calm and thin as ice over water, "then I agree to it. But not tonight, and not as a beggar. I'll hear the rite entire. I'll see the Hollow Hollow. And I'll choose the hour of my own—" I almost said *death*, and the hall leaned in hungrily to hear it, and I changed the word, "—my own weaving. No one takes an anchor. An anchor agrees."

The Queen studied me for a long moment. Then she inclined her head perhaps a finger's width, which from the Queen of Autumn was apparently a bow of profound respect.

"The dark moon is nine nights hence. Come to the Hollow Hollow prepared, little ember, or don't come at all." Her smile sharpened. "Oh—and do mind your nobleman. Courts talk. Thorns listen."

* * *

They gave us a chamber grown from a single enormous rosehip, hollowed and lit with foxfire, beds of moss soft as scandal. Hospitality, of a sort. Fae hospitality always had teeth in it somewhere, but we were too tired to search for them.

Bramble didn't sleep. She sat by the wall with her knees drawn up, and finally she said, into the dark, "What she said about your bloodline. About the Keepers locking the doors. Was that true?"

"I don't know."

"Xanthe."

"I don't *know*, Bramble. But she wanted it to hurt, and it did, which means it's at least half true, and half-truths are how the fae fight." I lay staring at the foxfire crawling the ceiling. "My mistress never told me the Keepers were consumed. Never told me what 'anchor' meant. Either she was protecting me or lying to me, and I keep finding new rooms in that house."

Sera stood at the chamber's entrance, arms crossed, watching the corridor. "Then we find another way," she said. "There's always another way."

"There isn't," I said. "That's the thing about prices, Sera. Sometimes they're real."

She didn't answer. After a while she went back to watching the dark, and I slept in fragments, dreaming of threads and hands and my mistress's voice saying *many, and then it wasn't.*

When I woke near dawn—which in the Otherworld was more of a suggestion than an event—Cassian's bed was empty, neatly made, and gone.

* * *

I found him by following the thorns.

Not literally—they weren't mine to command—but the court itself seemed to lean toward him as he walked, doors opening a crack as he passed, whispers running along the walls like water finding its level. He'd dressed with care. Of course he had. I caught up with him at the edge of a garden where the flowers were made of stained glass and rang faintly when the false wind moved, and he didn't startle, which told me he'd heard me coming and chosen to let me.

"Cassian."

"Lady Voss." He smiled. He'd taken to calling me *lady* lately, though he knew exactly what I was, boy and girl and neither and both depending on the hour. It was a weapon he liked to keep polished. "You're awake early. The foxfire disagrees with you?"

"What did the Queen mean? *Mind your nobleman.*"

"Ah." He turned a glass flower gently with one finger; it tolled a small, sad note. "Courts exaggerate."

"Courts lie. You negotiate. There's a difference, and I'm asking you to show me which one you are."

He was quiet for a moment. In the glass-garden light he looked younger, and older, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Do you know what happened to House Roe, Xanthe? Truly?"

"Vane took your seat. Stripped your titles. Exiled you."

"He took our *ward*. Three generations ago, House Roe held the ward-stone of Vellamere—the old covenant's local anchor, one thread of the greater weave. When the great covenant weakened, the local wards failed first, and Vane's people swooped in with witch-finders and ledgers and bought up every charter, every deed, every right-of-warding in the city. My family's power wasn't stolen by conquest. It was *purchased* from a corpse." He let the flower go; it swung, tolling. "I didn't come with you to save the world, Xanthe. I came because the covenant's death made my house poor, and its rebirth could make us rich again. I intend to sit in the negotiations. I intend House Roe to hold a charter-right in whatever new weave is spun. And yes—I spoke with the Queen's steward last night about terms."

The garden rang around us, small sad bells.

"You went behind our backs," I said.

"I went *ahead* of your backs," he corrected, mildly. "Someone has to. You walk into these rooms willing to give everything away for free. The Queen will take everything you offer and thank you for the privilege. Sera wants to fight everyone, and Bramble trusts anyone with pointed ears. None of you bargain. I bargain. That's what I'm *for*."

"And what did you bargain?"

He met my eyes then, and I will say this for Cassian Roe: he never lied when the truth would serve better. "A restoration. House Roe's charters, reinstated, guaranteed by the new covenant. In exchange, I've agreed to deliver the anchor to the rite—cooperative, consenting, punctual." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "The Queen's steward expressed concern that your companions might... interfere. Grief makes people unreasonable. I assured him I would manage you."

"*Manage* me."

"You're a figurehead whether you accept it or not, Xanthe. The refugees on the road already pray to your glowing blood. The council in Vellamere would have sold you if it profited them. Every cause needs a face, and yours happens to be the one the magic chose." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "I'm not betraying you. I'm making sure your sacrifice buys something. A throne restored can shield a hundred thousand people from men like Vane. Can your martyrdom say the same? Think of it as leverage purchased with the only coin you were ever going to spend anyway."

For a long moment I couldn't speak. Not because he was wrong about the world—he was right, horribly, in the way clever men are right—and that was precisely the poison of it. He had taken the shape of my sacrifice and tried to fit it inside his ambition, and the fit was close enough to pass, if you squinted, if you were tired, if you wanted someone else to carry the weight of deciding.

"No," I said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "No?"

"I'm not a charter. I'm not a face. And I'm sure as the dark moon not *yours* to deliver anywhere." My blood had begun to glow through my sleeves, I realized—anger did that now, grief did that, everything did that—and I let it. Let him see it. "If House Roe gets its charters back, it will be because the new covenant is written to mend what's broken for *everyone*, and your house lines up in the queue with everybody else. Behind the refugees. Behind Bramble's kin trapped on this side of the veil. Behind the charm-sellers Sera's order burned." I stepped back. "Go tell your Queen's steward the deal's off. And Cassian—if you speak for me again, in any room, to any court, we're finished. Not angry. Finished."

He regarded me for a long, measuring moment, and I watched him recalculate—I actually watched it happen behind his eyes, sums redone, columns shifted. Then he bowed, shallow and precise.

"As my lady wishes," he said, and the words were perfectly courteous and completely untrue, and he turned and walked away through the ringing glass flowers, and every door along the garden path opened for him a little wider than it had opened for me.

Sera was standing at the garden's edge. I didn't know how long she'd been there. Her hand rested on her sword, and her face was unreadable, and when I reached her she said only:

"So. Do we trust him?"

I watched Cassian's grey coat vanish into a doorway grown sideways in the hill.

"No," I said. "But we watch him. And we get to the Hollow Hollow before anyone's bargains finish maturing."

Behind us, deep in the court, a bell made of glass tolled once, twice, three times—and the black grass of the plain outside began, very quietly, to move against the wind, all of it pointing, like ten thousand fingers, toward the heart of the wood.
