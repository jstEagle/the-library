## 12. Cassian's Price

Cassian Roe had learned long ago that the most dangerous hour of any negotiation was not the bargaining itself but the silence after—when the other party believed everything was settled and stopped watching you.

He stood at the window of the guest chambers in the Court of Thorns, watching fae lanterns drift like drowned jellyfish through the perpetual violet dusk beyond the palace walls. Behind him, on a table of black glass, the queen's envoy had left the terms written in silver script that rearranged itself when no one looked directly at it. He had read them four times. They were generous. They were monstrous. They were exactly what he wanted.

*The covenant may be rewritten,* the script said, in letters that tasted of honey and iron when he traced them with one finger. *The bloodline anchor shall be bound to the covenant's purpose as defined by its architect. The architect shall be recognized by both courts.*

Architect. The word was a gift wrapped around a knife. The queen thought she was offering him influence over the new oath. She did not understand—or perhaps she understood perfectly well—that he intended to be more than an influence. He intended to write it.

And for that, he needed Xanthe Voss.

He heard footsteps in the corridor outside: light, uneven, the walk of someone exhausted down to the marrow. Xanthe had spent the day being stared at by the court like a specimen pinned to a board, and Cassian knew from experience how that wore at a person. Good. Tired people wanted simple answers. He could be very, very simple.

He turned from the window and arranged his face into something honest.

The door opened without a knock—it never occurred to anyone in this place to knock—and Xanthe came in looking like a candle burned down to the socket. Bramble followed, then Sera, then Wren and old Maddox bringing up the rear, the whole ragged company filing into the room with the wariness of soldiers entering ground they didn't trust. All except one seat at the table, which Cassian had made sure was comfortable, positioned near the fire, angled so its occupant would look toward him rather than away.

Small things. He had built his family's fortune back from nothing on small things.

"You met with the envoy," Xanthe said. Not a question. Their eyes—those strange eyes that had glowed gold in the burned borderland, that had made refugees fall to their knees—fixed on him now with a flatness that Cassian recognized and did not like. "Bramble says you were closeted with her for two hours."

"Bramble," Cassian said pleasantly, "has excellent hearing for someone who claims to have been drinking the whole time."

"I was drinking," Bramble said, dropping onto the windowsill, "and listening. Multitasking. It's a fae thing." Her smile had no warmth in it. "Tell them what you told her, lordling. Or shall I?"

"Or shall I?" echoed a voice from the doorway, and Cassian's stomach dropped before he even turned.

Sera Dune stood there with a scrap of silver script flattened in her palm—a torn corner of the agreement, stolen from the envoy's table or lifted from somewhere else entirely—and she wore the expression Cassian had seen her wear over the bodies of men she'd executed. He had thought, these weeks on the road, that he had seen all her faces. He had been wrong. This one was worse than any of them.

"Wren found this," she said, laying it on the black glass table where everyone could see. "In your saddlebag. You wrote a draft. Before you ever spoke to the envoy, you wrote a draft of the covenant you intend to see sworn."

The room went very quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere far off, fae music played in a key that made teeth ache.

Cassian considered his options in the space of three heartbeats, the way he had once considered battlefield dispositions across a map table in his father's hall, back before Vane's witch-finders burned that hall and hanged his father from its gate. Deny it—the draft was damning but deniable, parchment could lie. Blame the envoy—plausible; the fae were treacherous by reputation if not always by fact. Or—

Or tell the truth, because he had run the numbers already, days ago, lying awake in this violet dusk while the others slept, and the truth was the only hand he held that could still win the game.

"The throne of Roe was stolen," he said quietly. "Eleven years ago. My father was executed for the crime of sheltering charm-sellers during the first purges. My mother died in a camp that Captain Dune—" he nodded at Sera, who flinched, "—could describe better than I can, since she helped staff it. I am the last of my line. And the covenant we are about to rewrite will reshape both realms for a thousand years. So yes. I wrote a draft. I negotiated. I did what any man with a brain and a grievance would do."

Xanthe hadn't moved. "Read it," they said. "Aloud. All of it."

"There's no need—"

"*All of it.*"

So he did. He kept his voice level, his diction precise, the way he had been taught to deliver eulogies. *The restored covenant shall recognize the sovereign authority of the House of Roe over the Marchlands and the river territories.* *The anchor-blood shall serve as living seal and public figurehead of the renewed compact, its person held in sacred trust by the crown.* *The crown shall determine the disposition of anchor-magic in matters of state.*

When he finished, Sera laughed—one short, ugly bark of a sound.

"Held in sacred trust," she repeated. "Do you hear yourself? That's a leash with a coronation painted on it."

"It's *order*," Cassian snapped, and heard his own voice crack with something he refused to name. "You've all seen what happens without order. Greyharbor. The camps. The star-harvesting pits. Vane rose because the old world was soft and scattered and full of people like—" he gestured at the company, at all of them, their patched clothes and their smuggler's knives and their hedge-witch charms, "—like *this*. Well-meaning and powerless. I am offering structure. A crown that owes its legitimacy to the covenant itself, bound to protect the anchored magic forever. No witch-finder will ever rise under it because the law itself will forbid it. Xanthe would be safe. Everyone would be safe."

"And what does Xanthe get to decide?" Wren asked. The boy rarely spoke; when he did, it landed like a thrown stone. "In your draft. What do they decide?"

Cassian hesitated a half-second too long.

"That's what I thought," said Bramble from the windowsill. She swung her legs down and stood, and the lamplight caught the faint iridescence of her half-fae skin, the color it took on when her temper rose. "Let me translate the nobleman's poetry for the room. He wants a puppet with holy blood and a pretty glow to stand on balconies while he signs papers. He wants the one person among us who actually has power to have none at all. And he wants it dressed up as protection." She spat—a habit she claimed came from her mortal mother, deployed now with surgical precision. "I've dealt with lords like you on both sides of the crossing. You're all the same. You don't want belonging. You want ownership."

"That is unfair—" Cassian began.

"Is it?" Xanthe finally moved, crossing to the table, picking up the torn silver script Sera had brought. They read it again, though they must have heard every word twice already. When they looked up, their eyes were not glowing. They were just tired, and sad, and that was infinitely worse than anger. "Five years, Cassian. Five years I sold luck-knots in a market square and hid what I was so thoroughly that I almost convinced myself I was only the knot-seller. Do you know what that's like? Being a symbol against your will—being *hidden*, being *used*, being the thing other people need you to be?" They set the script down. "And then I find out my blood can save two worlds, and the first thing people start building around me is another box. Vane wants to devour me. The queen wants to own me. And you—" their voice caught, and steadied. "You want to crown me. All of you, reaching for the same person from different directions, and not one of you asking what I reached for."

"I asked," Cassian said. "That night on the road of ash. I asked why you taught wards to strangers instead of taking their worship and using it. You said—" he remembered precisely, because he had turned the memory over many times since, hunting for the flaw in it, the weakness he could use, "—you said worship is what people give when they've decided not to know you."

"Yes," Xanthe said softly. "And you listened, and you learned nothing. Because you don't want to know me, Cassian. You want a Voss-shaped thing standing behind your throne." They straightened. "No. Find another figurehead. Write your covenant with someone else's blood, if any blood but mine will answer it—I hope to the stars it won't. But I will not swear myself into your machine. I've been unmaking myself for other people's purposes since I was nineteen years old. I'm done."

The words fell into the room like stones into a well.

Cassian felt the moment tilt—the exact instant when a negotiation dies and becomes something else—and did what he had trained himself to do for eleven years in exile: he cut losses. "Then the queen gets her original terms," he said coldly, gathering the scripts, "and none of us get what we need, and Vellamere burns under Vane's black sky while we squabble about *feelings*—"

"It's not squabbling about feelings," Sera said, stepping between him and the door. Up close, she was shorter than him, broader, scarred along the jaw where a suspect had once gotten a knife through. "It's the difference between the order I served for fifteen years and whatever it is we're trying to build instead. I burned villages for order. I told myself it was protection too. I know exactly what it looks like from inside, and I know exactly what it costs the people underneath." Her hand rested—not on her sword, pointedly not on her sword—on the table beside Xanthe's. "You don't get to build Vane with a kinder face and call it salvation."

For one long moment Cassian looked at the door, and calculated. Three steps. Two of them armed. Bramble between him and the window. Maddox, silent as furniture, somehow already near the hearth. He was, he realized with distant clarity, not going to fight his way out of this room, and the fact that he had even measured it—that some part of him had gone to weighing blades while his allies' faces were still wet with disappointment—told him something about himself that he filed away in the locked room where he kept all the things he'd do anything not to feel.

"You'll come back to me," he said instead, quietly. "When Vane's vanguard reaches the ford. When the queen demands her pound of flesh. When it all comes apart the way it always comes apart—you'll remember that I was the only one with a plan." He pulled the door open. Violet light spilled in. "I'll be drafting."

He walked out through corridors of thorn-black wood and drifting lanterns, spine straight, boots silent on floors that pulsed faintly like living things, and only when he reached his own chamber and barred the door did he let himself lean against the wall and press the heels of his hands against his eyes.

His father had done this, he thought. Stood alone in rooms. Made the hard arithmetic alone, carried it alone, died for it alone at the end of a rope with the crowd cheering. *A ruler stands where others cannot,* his father used to say. *That is the whole of kingship.*

But Xanthe had looked at him tonight like he was a man standing in a hole he had dug and called it a mountain, and Bramble's voice was still in his ears—*you don't want belonging, you want ownership*—and worst of all, deepest, in the locked room behind his ribs, a smaller voice that sounded horribly like his own at nineteen, the year the halls burned:

*Was he wrong?*

Cassian Roe sat down at the black glass table, spread out the silver scripts, and did not touch them.

* *

He was still sitting there when the palace screamed.

Not a person screaming—though there was that too, rising a heartbeat later, voices in the corridors crying out in the liquid syllables of the fae tongue—but the palace itself, a groan that ran through the walls like a ship taking water. The lanterns in his chamber guttered from violet to a sickly grey. The floor's slow pulse stumbled, stuttered, raced.

Cassian was at the window before conscious thought caught up, and what he saw outside stopped his breath.

Beyond the palace walls, the edge of the Otherworld—the beautiful, lethal country of drifting light where Bramble had guided them across—was darkening. Not clouding. *Darkening*, a tide of absolute night rolling in from the east like ink poured into milk, swallowing the luminous forests hill by hill. And above it, impossibly, visible through the veil between worlds as if the veil itself had gone thin as gauze, hung the sky over the mortal realm.

Over Vellamere. Over the free cities.

There were no stars there at all anymore. There was only a single point of light, vast and white and hungry, moving slowly westward like an eye opening.

Behind him, running footsteps, and Xanthe burst into his chamber uninvited, unbarred, their blood-glow flaring gold beneath their skin bright enough to cast shadows, Bramble and Sera crowding in behind with weapons drawn against no visible enemy.

"What is that?" Cassian demanded, and hated how raw his voice came out.

Xanthe stood at the window, gold light pouring off them, staring at that single devouring point in the black sky, and when they answered, their voice was steady in the way of people standing at the edge of something enormous.

"That's not a star coming back," they said. "That's Vane. He's found enough of the hoarded light to burn through what's left of himself—and now he can see us." A pause. "Both realms. He can see both realms now. We have maybe two nights before he marches on the Hollow Hollow, and the queen knows it—listen."

Far below, horns were sounding throughout the Court of Thorns, deep and urgent, and the silver script on Cassian's table suddenly twisted itself, letter by letter, into a single line neither of them had asked for:

*THE HINGE OPENS AT DARK MOON. COME ARMED. ALL OF YOU.*

Cassian looked at the words. Then at Xanthe. Then at his own reflection in the black glass—alone, as always, framed in a window full of dying light.

"Two nights," he said. "Then we'd best decide quickly whether we're a company or a wreckage."

Nobody answered him. Outside, the white eye in the black sky blinked, and began, slowly, to move.
