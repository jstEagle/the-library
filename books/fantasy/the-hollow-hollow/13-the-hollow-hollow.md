## 13. The Hollow Hollow

The tree announced itself long before we saw it.

Bramble felt it first—she stopped dead on the ridge at dawn, her ears pricking toward a sound like a struck bell that had been ringing for so long it had become silence. "Do you hear that?" she whispered, and when I shook my head she frowned. "No. Of course you don't. You *are* it."

We crested the hill an hour later and there it was.

I had imagined the Hollow Hollow as a ruin—a broken tower, maybe, or a ring of stones like the ones in my mistress's old woodcuts. Instead it was a tree. A tree the size of a cathedral, or what had been one, because nothing remained of it but the shell: a vast grey cylinder of heartwood rising out of a valley of white grass, hollowed through its entire center as though some patient giant had bored out its living core with a spoon. The walls of it were smooth as river stone, silvered with age, and tall enough that clouds snagged and tore on the jagged crown of dead branches at its summit.

Through the hollow middle I could see the sky on the other side.

"Stars should show there," Bramble said quietly. "At night. The old crossings were all built under open sky." She glanced at me. "There haven't been stars for weeks."

"I know." My voice came out flat. "I'm the one who's going to fix it, remember?"

She didn't laugh. None of us did anymore. Cassian reined his horse up beside mine, his armor dull with road-dust, his smile worn down to something almost honest. "It's bigger than the maps suggested," he said. "The maps suggested a shrine. This is—" He searched for the word and didn't find it.

"It's a mouth," said Sera.

She had hardly spoken in three days—not since the ford, not since she'd stood over the body of the man whose orders she'd carried for eleven years and washed his blood from her hands in the Mirren. She spoke now staring at the great grey trunk, and her hand rested on her sword hilt without her seeming to notice.

"A mouth," Cassian repeated, amused. "It's a tree, Captain."

"It's a door," Sera said, "and doors have mouths. And things go in them." She kicked her horse forward before anyone could argue.

* * *

The entrance was a split in the western wall, tall as a city gate, its edges worn round by centuries of hands—or feet, or wings, or whatever else had once passed through. Inside, the light changed. It wasn't dark, exactly. It was *subtracted*, as if the air itself had been pressed flat and all color wrung out of it. Our voices fell dead a handspan from our lips. Even the horses refused to enter; Bramble left them hobbled outside among the white grass, and they stood trembling in a row like mourners at a graveside.

The floor of the great trunk was soft underfoot. Not earth. When I knelt and pushed my fingers into it, it gave like loam but came away pale, almost white, shot through with threads of silver that glimmered faintly when I touched them.

"What is this?" Cassian asked behind me.

I didn't answer, because I had just understood, and understanding sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

Roots. They were roots—the roots of the tree itself, grown up and across the hollow floor in a woven mat, silvered and dead. And threaded through them, following every channel the roots had carved, ran lines of that same faint shimmer, branching and rebranching, spreading outward through the whole vast wall of the tree like veins through a body.

Blood. Or what had once been blood. What had once been *someone*.

"Xanthe." Sera's voice, low. "Your arm."

I looked down. Through my sleeve, along the old scar-line where my magic lived just under my skin, a glow was rising to meet the glow in the roots. Faint. Answering.

"Oh," I said. Very small. Very calm. The calm you feel standing at the edge of something you've been falling toward your whole life. "Oh, I see."

"See what?" Bramble demanded. She caught my elbow. "Voss. See *what*?"

"The Keepers." I heard my own voice as though someone else were speaking it. "My mistress told me once—I was fifteen, I thought she was being poetic—she said the covenant wasn't kept. She said it was *carried*. I asked what she meant and she changed the subject." I got to my feet. My legs felt far away. "This is what she meant. Look at the walls. Look at the floor. Every vein in this tree is a person."

* * *

We walked inward, because there was nowhere else to walk, and because the glow under my skin pulled gently, the way a tide pulls.

The first of them we found forty paces in.

He stood against the curved wall—or hung in it, rather, sunk to the waist in the silvered heartwood as though the tree had taken him the way a riverbed takes rain. He had been dead a very long time. His robes had rotted to lace; his hair was still black; his face was peaceful, eyes closed, head bowed as if in sleep or prayer. From his chest, from the place where a heart would be, the silver veins ran outward, dozens of them, feeding into the greater channels that climbed the walls and vanished upward into darkness.

"He's not petrified," Bramble breathed. She hovered near him the way you hover near a sleeping animal. "He's not stone, Voss. The wood grew *through* him. Around him. Like—" She stopped.

"Like a graft," I said.

Cassian had gone very quiet. That frightened me more than anything else, because Cassian was never quiet unless he was calculating, and there was nothing here left to calculate. "How many," he said at last. Not really a question.

We counted as we went. We couldn't help it.

Eleven paces on, a woman in the eastern curve, her arms spread wide as if embracing the tree, her mouth open around words no one would ever hear again. Twenty paces past her, two together—a man and a woman back to back, their spines fused into the same panel of wood, their hands still touching. Further in, a child.

A child.

Sera stopped beside it for a long time. The little figure stood barely four feet high, sunken to the chest like the others, wearing the rotted remnants of something that might once have been a Keeper's sash, sized for small shoulders. Its eyes were closed. Its veins of silver were the brightest of any we'd seen, still faintly luminous after who knew how many centuries.

"They fed it children," Sera said. Her voice had gone rough as gravel. "The old orders. The witch-finders' founding charter says we guard against the fae stealing mortal children over the crossings." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "And the whole time, the *Keepers*—"

"We don't know that," I said. But I did know. I could feel it now, the closer I came to the center: the pull in my blood wasn't random. It was a summons. An inheritance passing from hand to hand down five hundred years, each Keeper walking here knowing—and coming anyway.

"My mistress knew," I said instead. "That's why the letter was sealed. That's why she waited until after she died to tell me. If she'd told me while she lived, I'd have had years to run."

"And you didn't run," Cassian observed softly.

"No," I said. "I gathered all of you instead. Make of that what you will."

* * *

The center of the Hollow Hollow was a round chamber where the trunk widened, its ceiling lost somewhere far above in a haze of grey light that came from no source. The floor here was bare heartwood, polished smooth, and carved into it in a spiral was the oath.

I knew it before I read it. I knew it the way you know your own name—except that until that moment I hadn't known my own name either, not truly. The letters were old script, deep-cut, and they spiraled inward toward a single word at the center, and I read them aloud because the room seemed to want them read:

*"One to hold, and one to hold, and one to hold, until the last holds alone, and the holding is done, and the holder is home."*

Silence.

"That's not a covenant," Sera said finally. "That's a funeral rite."

"It's both." The voice came from the doorway behind us, thin and dry, and we all turned with steel half-drawn—and there stood no one. Only the light bending, folding, and then unfolding into the shape of a woman made of birch-pale shadow and moth-wing memory. One of the court's heralds. It must have followed us since the crossing; Bramble swore softly in two languages.

"You are the Voss-blood," the herald said. Its eyes were holes full of distant weather. "You have come to the hinge. Good. It is time someone mortal saw it plainly." It drifted past us, its hem trailing roots, and gestured at the carved spiral with one long finger. "When the worlds were young and thin, the first oath was sworn here between the first Queen and the first mortal king, so that neither realm could devour the other. But oaths are hungry things. An unkept covenant curdles into famine; a kept one needs keeping. It needed a heart to beat inside it. A living anchor."

"So they bound someone," Cassian said.

"They *asked*," the herald said, "the first time. That is the tragedy your kind forgets. The first anchor knelt in this circle willingly and gave her blood and her years and at the end her self entire, and the covenant held, and the realms flourished, and everyone agreed it was a fair price—for her. And when she was consumed, another was asked. And then, in time, fewer asked and more were chosen. And then none were asked at all. The order of Keepers became a chain of sacrifices dressed in ceremony. Each one walked here believing they guarded the covenant." The herald's hollow eyes turned to me. "They *were* the covenant. Every one. Consumed one by one, root by root. Until your mistress, who was strong, and lasted longer than most, and died of it slowly in a house by the sea while her apprentice sold luck-knots downstairs."

The floor seemed to tilt. I put my hand against the carved spiral to steady myself and the silver in the roots flared beneath my palm, warm, recognizing me.

"She never said," I whispered.

"She could not. Anchors are forbidden to speak the truth of their burden—the covenant guards its own appetite. She spent her last free years trying to find another way. There is no other way. There has never been another way. The covenant must be refed or it dies entirely, and when it dies, the veil between the realms dies with it, and everything your world loves about being separate from ours dies too." The herald folded its hands. "You carry the last Voss blood. The seal is in you. Kneel in the circle, give yourself freely as the first did, and the stars return. Refuse, and watch the world go out by inches. There is no third door, Keeper."

"There's always a third door," said Sera.

"There has never been a third door," the herald repeated, with the patience of someone reciting a law older than laws.

I looked around the chamber—at Sera's white knuckles, at Bramble's ears flattened with grief she wouldn't name, at Cassian watching me with an expression I was only beginning to learn how to distrust. At the child in the wall behind us, four feet tall, eyes closed forever.

Five hundred years of Keepers. Every one of them told there was no other way. Every one of them believing it, because the alternative was unthinkable, and because hope is heavy and sacrifice is light—it flies so easily off the tongue of whoever isn't carrying it.

My mistress had lasted longer than most. Died of it slowly. In a house by the sea.

*I am the last Voss,* I thought. *The last name on the list. And lists can be rewritten.*

But I didn't say any of it aloud. Because the herald was still watching, and the tree was listening, and far away—beyond the white grass, beyond the hills, beyond the burning edge of the map—something else was moving toward this place with an appetite that made the herald look like a moth.

I felt it before any of them did: a cold thread through the silver veins under my palms, a wrongness flowing backward along the covenant's channels, like poison entering a wound. The glow in the roots guttered.

The herald's head snapped toward the entrance. For the first time, it looked afraid.

"What is that?" Bramble whispered.

The herald said, in a voice stripped of all its ancient patience: "Someone has been drinking the light. And now he has tasted where the rest of it lives."

Above us, through the hollow spine of the dead tree, the grey haze of light flickered—and went out.
