## 14. Vane Ascendant

The tent smelled of cold iron and burnt sugar.

Aldric Vane sat alone at a table of black oak, the map of Vellamere spread before him like a body prepared for dissection. Nine districts. Nine gates. Nine little republics of merchants and magistrates who believed their walls and their charters made them safe. He had studied them for years—had dined in them, charmed them, been *thanked* by them—back when he was merely Aldric Vane, patriot, reformer, the man who promised order after the border wards failed. Back when he still bothered to be loved.

He did not need to be loved anymore.

On the table beside the map stood a crystal decanter, and inside it swirled something that had no business existing in the mortal world: light that moved like liquid, pale gold shot through with veins of blue-white, turning over itself in slow spirals as though it were dreaming. Distilled starlight. Harvested from the camps—the places his Inquisitor General called *collection facilities* and everyone else, when they thought no one could hear, called the star-farms. Rows of the bound and the desperate, each one hooked to a thin silver catheter threaded into the marrow, each one giving up the small light that lived in every human soul, the light that had once been only theirs to spend.

Vane lifted the decanter. The light inside pressed against the glass like a hand against a window.

He drank.

It went down like swallowing winter. His teeth ached. His spine lit up from tailbone to skull, a ladder of cold fire, and for three heartbeats he saw everything at once—the whole siege line around Vellamere, the nine gates, the river, the refugee columns clogging the southern roads, the fae crossings flickering dead along the old borders like snuffed candles—and then it settled, and he was himself again, except that *himself* was larger now, and colder, and the tent's single candle looked dimmer than it had a moment ago. Everything looked dimmer. That was the price and the reward both: the more light he took in, the less light anything else seemed to hold.

He set down the decanter with a steady hand. His hand was always steady now. It had trembled, once, in the early days—after the first vial, after the first night he'd lain awake feeling his heartbeat slow and widen until it sounded less like a pulse and more like a tide. He had wondered then if he was dying. He knew better now. He was being *edited*. The trembling parts were going first. The parts that flinched. The parts that remembered his mother's kitchen, or the smell of rain on Greyharbor slate, or the face of the woman who had taught him his letters and died owing money to a charm-seller. Those memories were not gone. They were simply very far away, like rooms in a house he no longer lived in.

"Your Grace." The voice came through the canvas, deferential and dry. "The Council's envoys are asking to parley."

"Send them away."

"They've offered terms. Full trade concessions, grain, the lifting of the charter assemblies—"

"I know what they've offered. I wrote half of it for them, through intermediaries, so I would know exactly what they think I want." Vane rose. The candle flame bent toward him, faintly, as if leaning in to listen. "They think this is a negotiation. Tell them the next envoy who crosses my picket line will be sent back in pieces, and the pieces will be arranged alphabetically."

A pause outside. Then, carefully: "Alphabetically, Your Grace?"

"By name," Vane said, "spelled backward."

He heard the man swallow. Good. Fear was a tool, but terror was an economy, and Vane had learned to run surpluses.

* * *

From the ridge above the siege lines, Vellamere looked almost gentle in the dusk. A city of pale stone and copper roofs, its watchtowers crowned with warning-beacons that had not been lit in two hundred years because no one had believed they'd ever be needed again. Its walls were old but honest, built by masons who had never imagined artillery, much less what Vane intended to bring against them.

Because he did not intend to breach those walls with engines. Engines were for men with limits.

In the hollows behind the ridge, under tarps and earthworks, his artificers had assembled the Loom—a cathedral-sized engine of silver rings and black glass, built from schematics recovered from the last Keeper's confiscated papers. It did not throw stones or fire. It threw *hunger*. It reached out along threads no eye could see and pulled at the light inside living things, the way a spider pulls at silk, gathering it, winding it, feeding it home through conduits of enchanted lead into the tanks where Vane's harvest waited to be decanted.

The Keeper's papers had described all of this in the language of maintenance. Anchoring. Tending. *Feeding* the covenant so it would feed the world. The old Keepers had understood that the light had to circulate—that stars burned, that wards drew on mortal hearts, that everything given returned. They had spent themselves keeping the circle closed.

Vane had read those same papers and seen something else entirely. Why should the light circulate? Why should it return? Why should it belong to anyone but the one strong enough to hold it?

That was the whole of his heresy, and it had carried him further than any faith ever had. The covenant was a leash. He meant to melt it down and wear it as a crown.

Tonight, he would take Vellamere. Not with storming parties—not yet. Tonight he would simply stand on the ridge, open the Loom, and drink.

* * *

The command tent at the ridge's crest had no back wall. Vane had ordered it removed. Let the wind come through; let the men see him standing at the edge of the dark with the city laid out below like a feast.

Behind him, the Loom began to sing.

It was not a sound so much as a pressure—a note below hearing, felt in the fillings of teeth and the water of the eyes. Soldiers nearer the engine dropped their pikes and covered their ears; some wept without knowing why; one man knelt and began to pray to gods whose names he'd forgotten, because the body reaches for old words when the mind has none. The silver rings turned. The black glass drank the torchlight around it and gave nothing back. And along the conduits, visible now even to ordinary sight, the harvested light came running home—rivers of it, torrents, decades of hoarded harvest drawn up from the deep tanks and hurled toward the figure standing at the ridge's edge with his arms slightly away from his body, palms open, like a man greeting rain.

It struck him.

There was no other word for it. It *struck* him, and he did not fall, and he did not burn, and he did not scream, though every man watching expected one of those things. What happened instead was worse. He *opened*. The light poured into him the way flood pours into a channel cut for it long in advance, and where it passed, it remade him. His eyes went first—pupils dilating until the grey of his irises was a thin ring, then vanishing altogether, until his gaze was two wells of white fire. The veins of his throat and temples rose beneath the skin and glowed, blue-white, branching, a map of rivers drawn in light. His shadow fell behind him across the tent floor and it was wrong—too large, too many-jointed, edged in a shimmer that moved when he did not.

And still he stood there, drinking, while the drums of Vellamere beat alarm and the beacon-fires bloomed one by one along the walls like a rash of orange flowers.

Below, in the city, people were coming out of their houses. They always did, the first time. They crowded the battlements and the bell towers and the steps of the Council Hall, thousands of them, looking north at the ridge where a man made of light stood above a machine made of hunger, and they pointed their children toward him, and some of them cheered, because they still believed he was theirs—the reformer, the strong hand, the father of the new order. Vane could hear the cheering from the ridge. It sounded very small. It sounded like insects.

He raised one hand.

The Loom's note climbed. The second wave of light hit him harder than the first—he felt his ribs flex outward like a cage being refitted, felt his heart slow to four beats a minute, each beat rolling through the ground under the ridge like a muffled drum—and somewhere in the middle of it a voice that might have been memory or might have been conscience said, distantly, *this is too much, no vessel holds this, you will crack*—

He cracked.

Not broke. Cracked. Like glaze on old porcelain: fine lines spreading across the backs of his hands, across his cheeks, hairline fractures glowing at the edges, and through the cracks the light shone out of him instead of merely sitting in him. He looked down at himself with detached interest. He had expected pain. There was none. There was only expansion—the sensation of a thing built for one purpose finally being used at full capacity, which is either glory or ruin depending entirely on whether the maker knew what they were doing.

The old Keepers had known. That was the joke of it, the bitter punchline he kept finding at the bottom of every stolen text: they had *always* known what he was attempting, because they had attempted it too, once, ages ago, and the ones who survived had stopped, and written warnings in the margins, and buried the warnings under doctrine. *The vessel is not the treasure,* one marginal hand had scrawled, in ink gone brown. *The vessel is the wound through which the treasure leaves.*

Vane had read that line a hundred times. He had decided it was fear talking. Fear of greatness. Fear of the singular man.

He decided that again now, standing full of a city's worth of stolen dawn, cracking gently at the seams, and the decision held—for tonight.

* * *

The sky went out at moonrise.

It did not cloud over. Clouds have edges, texture, the grey belly-light of reflected towns. This was different. The darkness came down from directly overhead, a perfect circle of absolute black centered on the ridge, expanding outward with terrible calm, and where it passed, the stars did not dim—they *ceased*. Not hidden. Erased. Sailors on the river swore afterward that they had watched constellations go out like windows in a burning house, one after another, the Wagon-Wright, the Sisters, the Old Wolf, until the whole vault overhead was a smooth black lid and the only lights left in the world were the fires of Vellamere and the glowing seams of Aldric Vane.

The cheering stopped.

In its place came the sound a city makes when it understands, all at once, that the story it has been telling about itself is over. A low moan, collective, rising off the walls like heat-shimmer. Bells ringing wrong—panic rhythms, unsanctified, clappers swung by hands that had forgotten how. Somewhere a herald's voice cracking as he shouted that the Council was in emergency session, that terms would be offered, that someone must know what to do—and the shout dissolving into the moan, because everyone below had already grasped the truth that the Council had not yet admitted: you do not negotiate with weather. You do not send envoys to the end of the world.

Vane listened to all of it from the ridge, and found, examining himself carefully, that he felt very little. That frightened him more than anything else had. Not the cracking. Not the dark. But the *distance*—the way the city's terror reached him now as information rather than sensation, the way the screaming below arrived at his ears already translated, already filed, already smaller than it was. He remembered fearing things. He had a record of it, like a ledger: here, fear of poverty; here, fear of exposure; here, fear of the witch-finders' cells back when he'd been a boy in Greyharbor hiding a hedge-charm in his boot. He could recite the fears. He could no longer perform them.

Was this godhood, then? He had assumed it would feel like power. It felt like altitude. Thin air. The world below growing detailed and distant at once, the way a map is detailed and distant, until caring about it became a kind of charity.

He looked at his hands. The cracks had spread to his wrists. Through them, his blood showed as light—not red, not anymore, but a slow white-gold current, and where a drop fell to the trampled grass of the ridge, the grass did not wither. It *stood*. Straightened. Turned toward him like a congregation. Even the weeds worshipped him now, or fed on him, or both; he could no longer tell the difference, and suspected there wasn't one.

"Your Grace." The Inquisitor General had come up the ridge path at a run, and now stood at the edge of the light pouring off his master, shading eyes that watered and streamed. Behind her, officers hung back at the boundary of the glow like men at the lip of a furnace. "The city—the Council—their envoys—"

"Burned," Vane said. "I told you what would happen to envoys."

"You said pieces, Your Grace. Arranged—" She faltered. Looking up at him was difficult; his face had become a problem of brightness, features swimming behind glare. "There is another matter. A report from the western road. The Voss creature. It escaped Captain Dune's net. It's moving east with a company—smugglers, a defector, exiled Roe. Toward the Hollow Hollow."

Something stirred in him at the name. Not memory—memory was a house he no longer lived in—but something older than memory, something the light had not managed to edit out because it had never been *his* to begin with. The Hollow Hollow. The dead tree where the first oath was sworn. The hinge of the world. His texts described it as a ruin, a relic, a place where nothing lived because everything had been spent. But the texts were written by Keepers, and Keepers lied the way dams lie about rivers.

If the vessel was a wound through which the treasure left, then the Hollow Hollow was the oldest wound in the world. And wounds could be widened.

"Let them come," Vane said.

"Your Grace?"

"The Voss whelp carries the seal in its blood. Let it carry the seal to the hinge. Let it open the door." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because the light behind his teeth made it look like a lantern being lit inside a skull. "I don't need to break the covenant, Inquisitor. I need it *open*. Wide. Once it stands open, everything that flows through it flows past me—and I am the largest thing in the channel." He spread his arms. The cracks along them blazed. Below, the moaning city saw their enemy transfigured against a starless sky, and the moan became silence, and the silence was the loudest sound Vane had ever heard. "Send a rider. Double the pace. I want the company watched, not touched. Every mile it travels toward the tree, it digs my well deeper."

"And if it reaches the tree before us?"

"Then we arrive," Vane said, "to a door already open. And I walk through it last, and I close it behind me—from the inside—with the whole of the light of both worlds in my chest." He lowered his arms. The glow settled, banked, patient. "There is no version of the next dark moon in which I am not a god. The only question the universe gets to answer is how much of it survives the coronation."

The Inquisitor General stared at him a moment longer—at the white eyes, the glowing seams, the shadow with too many joints—and then she bowed, lower than protocol required, the way one bows to a storm front, and went down the ridge at a run.

Vane remained alone at the edge of the dark.

Overhead, where the Sisters had hung since before the first wall of Vellamere was laid, there was nothing. He searched the black for the stars he had eaten and found no guilt there, no grief, only inventory. So much taken. So much more to take. The Loom hummed behind him, patient, insatiable, and far to the west—beyond the burned borderland, beyond the ford at Mirren, beyond the reach of anything he considered a peer—a ragged company of outlaws was marching toward a dead tree carrying a secret he believed he had already priced.

He believed wrong. But belief, he had learned, was just another fuel, and he had plenty.

The last thing he did before descending was look once more at the black sky, and speak—to the dark, to the cracks in his own skin, to whatever marginal ghost had written *the vessel is the wound*—three words that the wind carried south toward Vellamere, and west toward the road, and that no one heard, and that were true anyway:

"Soon," he said. "Kneel."

* * *

In the city below, in a shuttered house on Tanner's Row, an old woman who sold luck-knots in better days lit a stub of candle and set it in her window, alone, defiant, a single point of light under the murdered stars. Across Vellamere, one by one, other windows answered—dozens, then hundreds, then thousands, until the whole dark bowl of the besieged city glittered upward like a false sky, a constellation built of stubbornness and tallow.

And far to the west, on a road of ash, a traveler with light in their blood looked north at the place where the stars used to be, and did not kneel.
