## 15. What We Owe Each Other

The fire was dying, and no one moved to feed it.

They had made camp in the ribs of a ruined waystation, three days' hard march from the ford of Mirren, and the wood they'd gathered was wet and sullen, smoking more than it burned. Above them the sky was a lid. No stars, no moon worth the name—the dark moon was four nights off and coming for them like a debt collector—and the blackness pressed down on the company like a hand.

Xanthe Voss sat with their back against a fallen beam, their blood's faint glow banked beneath their sleeves out of old habit, though there was no one left to hide from. The others were scattered around the coals in the postures of exhausted people: Sera cleaning a sword she had already cleaned, Bramble braiding and unbraiding the same strand of her hair, Cassian staring into the smoke as if it owed him money. Old Wren dozed sitting up, the way soldiers do. The twins—Pell and Perrin, refugee militia boys who had joined at Mirren and not yet learned to be quiet—took turns feeding sticks to the fire and watching each one die.

It was Bramble who broke the silence, because Bramble always did.

"Right," she said, in the tone of someone opening negotiations. "I want to know something, and I want it honest, because tomorrow we might all die at a tree."

"Cheerful," said Pell.

"Tomorrow we might die at a tree," Bramble agreed. "So. Why are you here? Not the pretty version. The real one." She pointed a finger around the circle. "Because I'll go first, since I asked. I came for the crossings. My kin are locked behind a door that doesn't open, starving in a beautiful country, and I am the only one of my line who got out before the wards fell, and every night I hear them in my sleep asking me why I'm eating warm bread while they eat light that tastes like nothing." She shrugged, and the shrug didn't quite work. "That's why. Not adventure. Bread-guilt."

Silence again, but a different kind now—a listening silence.

Sera laid her sword across her knees. For a long moment Xanthe thought she wouldn't answer. She had been with them only weeks, defected only weeks, and trust sat on her like armor that hadn't been fitted yet.

"I burned a village," she said finally.

The twins went still.

"Not this one. A different one. Charm-sellers, the order said. Traitors to the natural law. I rode in with thirty men and a writ signed by the Inquisitor General, and I stood there while they—" Her jaw worked. "There was a woman who sold weather-charms. Little ones. Rain for the barley, that sort of thing. She looked at me when they put the rope on her and she wasn't afraid, and that was worse than fear, because it meant she'd already decided what I was, and she was right." Sera turned the sword over. "I came because I can't unburn it. Because the law I served told me children were fuel, and I obeyed right up until the moment obedience became impossible, and then I ran, and running isn't enough. So I'm walking toward the thing that might make it enough. That's all. That's the whole confession. Don't look at me like saints look at people. I hate that."

"We don't," said Perrin quietly. "Look like saints, I mean."

"Why did *you* come?" Sera asked, almost gently.

Pell and Perrin exchanged the glance twins exchange, the one where half a conversation happens invisibly.

"Our village is under the star-harvest camps' shadow," Pell said. "You know what they take. Not just light. They take people who glow. People like—" He nodded at Xanthe, apologetic. "People like you. Our cousin glowed. Faintly. You'd never see it except at night. They took him in a cart with the windows painted over, and we never saw him again, and our mother told us to forget his face so we wouldn't be next." He fed a stick to the fire. "We came because forgetting is a kind of dying too, and we're done doing it."

Cassian laughed then—a short, bitter sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who has audited his own soul and found the ledger wanting.

"You want mine? Fine. Mine is the ugliest." He didn't look at any of them; he looked at the fire as if addressing it directly. "I came for a throne. There. Spoken aloud, in front of witnesses, may it rot me. My family ruled one of these cities before Vane's creatures engineered our fall, and I have spent eleven years polishing my grievance like a relic, and when a glowing foundling walked into the Council chamber needing an army, I thought: *there is my instrument.*" His voice caught, and he pushed through it. "I negotiated with the fae court behind your backs. I planned to set you up as a figurehead, a saint on a banner, and rule through your light. And then—" He stopped.

"And then?" Bramble prompted, merciless.

"And then the ford of Mirren." He said it like a man naming a wound. "I watched a smuggler take a crossbow bolt meant for a witch-finder. I watched twin farm boys hold a bridge with pitchforks. I watched *you*"—he pointed at Xanthe—"give away wards to people who could pay you nothing, when everything in my education says power is hoarded or it is nothing. And I understood that I had spent eleven years wanting back a throne that was built on the same lie Vane's is built on. That some people are anchors and some people are sails." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I don't know what I am now. But I'm here. That's my confession. Use it how you like."

Old Wren opened one eye. She had been a camp follower, a cook, a grandmother, and—though she rarely said it outright—for two decades a hedge-witch of the small magics, the kind the order called vermin.

"I came because I'm old," she said simply. "And because when I was young, the magic wasn't outlawed yet. I remember the crossings open. I remember my mother trading songs with a fae woman at the spring fair, actual trading, coin for charm, neighbor for neighbor. Then the Keepers started dying—or being used up, which is worse—and the doors shut, and the order needed someone to blame, and it's always easier to burn the hedge-witch than to ask why the sky went dark." She poked the coals with a stick. "I came because I want to die in a world with doors in it. That's all. Old women are allowed simple wants."

Then they all looked at Xanthe.

Xanthe had been dreading this. Five years of hiding, of luck-knots sold as mere craft, of flinching from their own reflection when it glowed—and now a circle of tired, damaged, honest people waiting for the truth.

"I came because I was told to," they said at last. "My mistress wrote me a letter after she was dead, which is a very mistress thing to do, and it said: *your blood can reforge the covenant, if you reach the Hollow Hollow before the dark moon.* Your blood. Singular. One anchor, one sacrifice, one person who gets to be unmade so everyone else stays safe." They pulled back their sleeve, and the light came up along their veins, soft gold, the color the refugees at Mirren had knelt to. "I've been carrying that sentence for months like a stone. One anchor. Me. And I've been so busy being terrified of dying that I never once asked the obvious question."

"Which is?" Sera said.

"*Why one?*" Xanthe stood, and the firelight and the bloodlight mixed on their face. "Think about it. Think about what we just heard. Bramble crossed the wards alone and got out—that cost her everything. Sera served the law alone and it took her soul a piece at a time. Cassian plotted alone and it curdled into this—" they gestured at his confession, still hanging in the air. "The Keepers were consumed one by one because the covenant was built *one by one*. One anchor at a time, one life poured into the seal, and everyone else got to live free and forget. That's not a covenant. That's a tax paid by whoever's unlucky enough to be born glowing."

Wren's stick stopped moving. "Go on," she said, in the voice of a woman catching a hedge-spell mid-fall.

"The first oath was sworn at a tree by—I don't know, by however many there were. But somewhere along the centuries it got narrowed. Professionalized. Handed to Keepers, to specialists, to *sacrifices*, because it was convenient for everyone else." Xanthe's voice shook, not with fear now but with something faster. "What if that's the corruption? Not Vane. Vane's just the newest thief. What if the original sin was letting one person carry what was meant for many hands?"

Bramble sat forward. "A shared anchor."

"A web, not a pillar. Not one life holding the roof up—an oath with a thousand hands in it, so no hand can be cut off, no Keeper consumed, no order of witch-finders needed to guard a secret that belongs to everybody." Xanthe looked around the circle, at each face in turn. "I can't rewrite the covenant alone. I don't think anyone can. But maybe that was never the requirement. Maybe the requirement was always *willing hands*, plural, and we lost the plural somewhere in a thousand years of kings and orders and councils deciding other people should pay."

The fire chose that moment to gutter low, and Wren, without ceremony, fed it the good dry wood she'd been hoarding, and the flames climbed.

"It would need consent," Sera said slowly. "Real consent. Not conscription. If it's a web, every thread has to be tied willingly or the whole thing frays."

"Which is why Vane can't do it," Bramble said, grinning suddenly, sharp-toothed. "He's drinking starlight *alone*. Hoarding it. A covenant of one. It's eating him hollow—you saw what's left of him. He's building a cathedral out of stolen candles and calling himself the sun."

"And the fae queen will hate it," Cassian said. "She wants custody. Sole custody. A web of mortal keepers across both realms means no one owns the door—including her."

"Everyone powerful will hate it," Xanthe agreed. "That's how we'll know it's right."

They sat with it. The idea moved around the fire like a passed cup, each of them drinking, each of them testing its weight. Pell reached over and took Perrin's wrist—twins, sharing a pulse—and said, "Two hands here." Wren raised hers, spotted and steady. Bramble laced her fingers together, fae-blood and mortal both. Sera, last of all, laid her sword flat on the ground between them all, hilt outward, which among witch-finders meant surrender of authority, and among soldiers meant something older: *count me.*

Cassian hesitated longest. Then he put his hand—not on the sword, beside it, an honest distance away. "I'll tie my thread," he said. "Not for a throne. For the ford of Mirren. That's the man I'd rather be."

Xanthe looked down at their own glowing veins, then at the circle of hands, and felt the stone they'd carried since the letter crack straight down the middle. Not gone—heavy things don't vanish—but cracked, and light coming through the crack.

"Four nights," they said. "The dark moon. We march at dawn."

They slept, all of them, deeper than they had in weeks.

* * *

In the last hour before dawn, Xanthe woke to find Wren awake, mending a sock by feel in the dark.

"You knew," Xanthe whispered. "About the plural. Didn't you? Some old hedge-lore."

Wren snorted. "Child, my mother sang a skipping rhyme about it. *One hand holds, one hand breaks, many hands the covenant makes.* We thought it was nonsense. Children's rhyme." She bit her thread. "Turns out the old ones hide the important things in nursery songs, because nobody burns you for a rhyme."

Xanthe lay back and looked at the black sky, and for the first time since the night the stars died, the darkness felt less like a lid and more like a held breath.

Somewhere east, at the Hollow Hollow, something vast and dead was waiting to be sworn alive again.

And it would not be sworn by one.
