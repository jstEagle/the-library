## 16. The Dark Moon March

Sera Dune had burned villages for less than what the refugees at the ford of Mirren were guilty of.

She stood at the reed line with her old badge still sewn to her collar—she hadn't been able to bring herself to tear it off, not yet—and watched the fires on the far bank multiply like spilled embers. Three hundred souls, maybe more. Farmers with billhooks and boar spears, a fisher-militia from the delta towns in mismatched lacquered river-armor, grandmothers who had stitched ward-sigils into their shawls the way their great-grandmothers had before the witch-finders made such stitching a hanging crime. Charm-sellers, every one of them now, by Vane's law. By her old law.

A year ago she would have read them their sentence and lit the pyre without flinching. She had done exactly that, twice, and slept afterward.

"You're doing the face again," Bramble said beside her. The half-fae smuggler crouched low among the reeds, her fox-colored ears flat against her hair, her crossbow cradled like something sleeping. "The one where you look like you're chewing on your own conscience."

"It's a large conscience."

"Mm." Bramble's eyes never left the far bank. "It's going to be a long night either way. Vane's vanguard is maybe two hours behind us—we heard the horns at the last ridge. And these good people are camped directly across the only ford within thirty miles."

"They're not moving because they think we're here to help them," Sera said. "Word's gone up and down the river. The Saint of the Glowing Blood is coming to Mirren."

Xanthe hated that name. Sera had watched them try to scrub it off themselves for weeks—the flinches when refugees knelt, the awkward lessons that followed, *here, no, don't pray, do this instead: chalk, salt, iron nail, three knots and a spoken word.* It had never once worked. People wanted saints. Saints asked nothing of them except belief, and belief was easier than learning.

"Then we'd best not disappoint them," Bramble said, and grinned with too many teeth.

* * *

The war council was held in the hull of an overturned ferry boat, by lamplight filtered through a red cloth so it wouldn't carry. Xanthe sat at the center with their hands wrapped around a mug they weren't drinking, and Sera watched them the way she'd once watched suspects—not hunting guilt anymore, but reading exhaustion.

"The vanguard will reach the ridge by midnight," Cassian Roe said. He'd spread his maps across the bilge boards, weighted with dagger hilts, and his voice had the polished calm he wore like court dress. "Four hundred picked men, star-lanterns, at least two witch-finder interrogators attached. They'll want the ford intact—they need this crossing as much as we do. Which means they won't simply burn us out. They'll want to take the bridgehead clean."

"Clean," said Old Maren, matriarch of the delta militia, and spat. Her arms were bare and corded, her shawl sigils glowing faintly now that the dark moon was rising and every scrap of hedge-craft woke hungry. "They took my village clean, lordling. Clean as a butcher's block."

"I'm not defending them," Cassian said, mild as milk. "I'm predicting them. There's a difference, and it wins battles."

Xanthe looked up. "There's another way across the river. You know there is. We could go around—lose a day, maybe two—"

"And let the vanguard fall on three hundred unarmed civilians alone?" Sera didn't mean to say it loudly. It came out loudly anyway. Every head turned. She kept her eyes on Xanthe. "I did that math for six years. *Move the villagers along, let the law take who the law takes.* I'm not doing it again. Not tonight. Not ever."

The lamplight moved. Somewhere outside, a child was singing a warding-song under its breath, the old kind, the kind Sera's mother had hummed before the purges reached their coast.

"We fight, then," Xanthe said quietly. "But we fight like charm-sellers, not soldiers. No pitched line. No honor." A ghost of a smile. "Bramble?"

"Oh, I've been waiting all week." Bramble's grin went feral. "River mud, false lights, and every trick my grandmother taught me before the crossings closed. Give me twenty of Maren's fishers who aren't afraid of the water and an hour of darkness."

Cassian's jaw tightened—Sera saw it, the aristocrat's distaste for fighting dirty—but he inclined his head a precise inch. "And the ford itself?"

"Holds or doesn't," Sera said. "That part's mine."

* * *

She walked the bank before midnight with her old witch-finder's coat buttoned to the throat, and the refugees watched her pass with eyes she recognized. She had worn that look herself, standing in market squares while her superiors read sentences aloud: the flat, braced stare of people deciding whether running would save them.

An old man stepped into her path near the waterline. He held a boar spear with a charm knotted below its head—a luck-knot, crude but genuine. Xanthe's craft, taught and passed hand to hand down the refugee columns like seed grain.

"Witch-finder," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"My daughter. Two years back. Marrowmere market." His voice was level in the way that cost everything. "You were there. I've seen your face in my dreams enough nights to be sure."

Sera did not look away. "I was there. If I gave the order, I'm sorry. If I stood watch, I'm sorrier. Neither one brings her back."

"No," the old man agreed. "Nothing does." He looked at her coat, at the badge she still hadn't torn away. "Why are you wearing that?"

"Because taking it off would be easy." She heard her own voice go quiet. "Tonight has to be hard, or it isn't worth anything. When this is over—if it's over—I'll burn it myself. But I want Vane's men to see it coming toward them first. Let the order's own colors break the order's own lines."

The old man studied her for a long moment. Then he unknotted the luck-charm from his spear and pressed it into her gauntlet. "For hardness," he said. "My wife made them better. But it holds."

She tied it where her badge should have been.

* * *

Vane's vanguard came down to the river at the hour the old texts called the wolf-dark, and they came beautiful and wrong. Star-lanterns bobbed above their column like captive constellations, hoarded light bleeding white through pierced iron cages, and under that light the soldiers' faces were smooth and faintly luminous, fed for months on distilled sky. Sera knew that glow. She had seen it first on Inquisitor General Hale's hands, then on the prisoners from the harvest camps, drained to gray. Light taken from other people always sat strangely on the thief.

"Positions," she breathed.

The trap unfolded the way Bramble had promised it would, which is to say it unfolded like weather.

First the false fires: a dozen camouflaged blazes on the northern bank, tended by fishers who could build a signal fire that lied convincingly at fifty paces. The vanguard's scouts took the bait exactly as predicted and wheeled their column north, toward a stretch of bank that was deep silt under a skin of reeds.

Then the mud did its work.

The sound the column made when the front rank went down into the silt was not a battle sound at all—it was the sound of a giant swallowing. Horses screamed. Lantern-bearers toppled, and their precious starlight rolled hissing into the shallows, and the moment the light touched open water Bramble's prepared charms woke: sigils painted on river-stones flared green, and the whole drowned stretch of the ford lit up like a festival, showing every soldier in the muck exactly how badly positioned he was.

"Now," Sera said.

They hit the flanks from the reeds—Maren's fishers in the water where no trained soldier wants to meet an enemy, hauling men down by sword-belts, hamstringing horses gently, *gently*, because Maren had given one order about horses and meant it. Cassian's exiles formed the visible line at the true ford, pike wall gleaming, giving the vanguard somewhere respectable to die. And through the middle of it all moved the thing no doctrine had prepared the usurper's army for: hedge-witches in the open, casting without shame, ward-shields blooming blue over charging militiamen, hexes unraveling officers' commands mid-word.

Sera fought at the point of it, and it was the strangest battle of her life, because she was killing men she had trained.

She knew their footwork. She knew the interrogators' paired-blade drill, knew the counter to it, knew the little tell before a star-fed soldier lunged—the half-breath they'd all learned in the new academies. She parried a young witch-finder's opening sequence move for move and saw his eyes go wide behind his visor as he recognized the forms.

"Sergeant Dune?" he gasped. Young. Sixteen, maybe. Someone had put her old academy insignia on a child.

"Not anymore." She broke his guard and laid him flat with the pommel, not the edge. "Stay down, boy. This river's full of people kinder than your masters."

He stayed down. Some of them did. Most didn't get the choice.

* * *

It nearly broke at the hour before dawn.

A second column—scouts returning, or a reserve nobody had mapped—came down the ridge with torches, and suddenly the arithmetic reversed. The false-fire ruse was spent; the mud traps were full; Maren's fishers were exhausted and bleeding; and the fresh troops hit the true ford in ranks four abreast with star-lanterns blazing, driving Cassian's line back a yard, then two.

Sera found herself shoulder to shoulder with Xanthe at the water's edge, the two of them holding a gap in the shield wall while militiamen scrambled to close it. Blood ran down Xanthe's forearm—not theirs, someone else's—and where it struck the wet stones the old light answered. The covenant-light, the bloodlight, gold-white and alive, spilling up out of the ground like springwater.

The enemy hesitated. Superstition runs both ways.

"Don't," Xanthe gritted, seeing the soldiers flinch. "Don't kneel, don't gawk—" But they were already raising their free hand, and Sera felt the air change pressure the way it did before every working now, a held breath over the whole river.

What Xanthe cast wasn't a weapon. Sera understood that even as it happened. They drove their bloodied palm against the ford's oldest boundary stone—the ferry-stone, older than any kingdom—and spoke three words in a language that made Sera's fillings ache, and the *river remembered itself.*

The Mirren rose. Not a flood, nothing so crude—a single traveling swell, hip-high, rolling upstream against current and sense, and it went through the fresh column like a broom through a knocked-over shelf. Torches died. Ranks dissolved. Men who had been winning a battle found themselves losing an argument with the water, and the water was not interested.

When it passed, the ford was silent except for groaning, and the surrendering had already begun—soldiers dropping weapons in ones and twos, then in bunches, kneeling in the shallows with their hands laced behind their heads because some animal wisdom told them that was what the victors would want.

Old Maren stalked down the bank, spear leveled at a cluster of them, and stopped. Looked at their young faces. Looked at Sera.

"Well, witch-finder," she called. "These are yours. What does the law say we do with them?"

Every eye on the riverbank turned. Sera stood in the wreck of the battle with her old badge at her throat and a luck-charm beside it, and understood that whatever she said next would be repeated in every refugee camp from the delta to the mountains by morning.

"The law says burn them," she said. Her voice carried clear across the water. "So we're done with that law. Feed them. Take their lanterns—those are stolen light and we'll return every drop to the sky where it belongs. Bind the officers and ask them questions. The rest—" she looked at the sixteen-year-old sergeant, gray-faced and kneeling, "—the rest we send home with a story to tell. Let Vane's own army learn what mercy looks like when it comes from the people he ordered you to fear. Fear keeps his throne. Let's see it stand against this."

For a heartbeat nobody moved.

Then the old man with the boar spear—the one whose daughter she might have burned in Marrowmere—began, slowly, to unbuckle a prisoner's armor so he could give him soup. And the bank followed him, all down the line, the outlawed and the lawful changing places in the grey pre-dawn light like dancers trading partners at the end of a song.

* * *

They buried fourteen of their own on the southern rise as the sky went from ash to pearl. Cassian spoke the rites beautifully, of course; exiled nobles are raised on funerals. Maren sang something delta-raw that undid everyone. Xanthe stood at the edge of the grave-circle with their sleeves rolled down over their forearms, hiding the bloodlight out of respect, and Sera came to stand beside them.

"You could have gone around," Xanthe said quietly, when the songs were done. "I want you to know I know that. You chose the ford. You chose them." A nod toward the militiamen striking camp below. "Toward me."

Sera pulled the old badge off her collar at last. It came away with threads. She turned it over in her fingers—silver, stamped with the order's eye-and-flame, dented from a blade deflected years ago in some other unjust cause.

"I told a man I'd burn it when it was over," she said, and fed it into the grave-side lamp until the silver ran soft. "Turns out I wasn't burning it. I was handing it back."

Below them, Bramble whistled sharply from the picket line. Sera turned, and her stomach dropped, because the smuggler wasn't pointing south, toward Vane's broken vanguard.

She was pointing east. Toward the Hollow Hollow.

The horizon there was wrong. Not dark—dark they had learned to live with, five years of guttering stars—but *wrong*: a blackness with texture, a hole in the world shaped like an absence of everything, spreading along the treetops like ink through water. And threaded through it, thin as harp strings, lines of pale light being drawn inward, mile by mile, toward something drinking at the center of the map.

"He's not sending an army anymore," Bramble called down, and for once there was no grin anywhere on her. "Whatever that is—he's *going himself*. And the sky between here and the hollow tree is coming down behind him."

Xanthe's hand found the luck-charm at Sera's wrist, gripping hard.

"Then we march now," they said. "Dark moon or no dark moon. Before he drinks the last of the light, we make him drink us instead."

They broke camp before full sunrise, three hundred strong, and marched east into the failing morning.
