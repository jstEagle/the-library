## 17. Blood and Bargain

The Hollow Hollow announced itself long before we saw it.

We felt it first as a silence—not the absence of sound but the absence of *echo*, as though the world had stopped bothering to answer itself. Then came the smell: loam and lightning, the green rot of a forest that had died standing up. Bramble walked ahead of us with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, the way she'd done for two days now, as if holding something in place inside her chest.

"It's pulling," she said quietly. "The crossing-sense. It's not pointing anymore, it's just—dragging."

"Can you fight it?" Sera asked from the rear, where she always walked, sword loose in its sheath because she no longer trusted herself to keep it sheathed otherwise.

"Don't want to," Bramble said. And that was somehow worse.

We crested the last ridge at dusk—or what passed for dusk now, under a sky that had held no stars since the night everything broke. The valley below was full of dead trees, thousands of them, silver-grey and leafless, all leaning inward like courtiers bowing toward a throne. At their center stood the tree.

I say *stood*, but that's too small a word. The Hollow Hollow was a tree the size of a cathedral, hollowed out ages ago into a vast chamber open to the sky through its broken crown, its inner walls carved with spirals of script older than any alphabet I knew. Its roots rose from the ground like the ribs of some buried giant. It was dead, and it had clearly been dead for centuries—and yet when I looked at it, my blood answered. Not flared. *Answered.* A slow pulse behind my ribs, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"There," Cassian said, and his voice did the thing it always did, smoothing itself into statesmanship even here at the end of the world. "The hinge of both realms."

"The grave of every Keeper who ever kept it," I said.

No one argued. They'd all seen what the rite-texts showed, what Mistress Odile's letter had confessed in her careful, dying hand: *the Keepers were not guardians of the covenant, child. They were its food.*

* * *

We made camp inside the tree's shadow rather than inside it. Bramble insisted—the walls were still listening, she said, and we weren't ready to be overheard. Sera set watches with the crisp economy of a woman who had once commanded forty witch-finders and now commanded eleven ragged souls who followed her because she'd chosen them over the law. Cassian's soldiers ringed the camp with a discipline that reminded everyone, constantly, that they were his before they were ours.

I didn't sleep. I climbed instead, up the great root-ribs to a shelf of bark wide enough to sit on, and unbound the wrappings from my left forearm.

My blood glowed in the dark now. It had been doing that since the ford of Mirren, faintly, like embers under ash—a soft gold light seeping through my skin along the old covenant lines that traced my veins. The refugees at the ford had knelt in the mud to me. I had taught them salt-and-iron wards until my voice cracked, trying to give them back their dignity one practical charm at a time, but I could still feel the weight of their eyes. Saints are easy to worship and terrible to be.

Below me, the fire was small. Sera sat apart from it, sharpening a blade that needed no sharpening. Bramble lay curled against a root, humming a fae lullaby so old it had no words left, just melody worn smooth. Cassian stood at the edge of the light, looking north toward Vellamere, which we could no longer see and which Vane's darkness had swallowed whole days ago.

"You should rest," Sera called softly, without looking up. She always knew where I was. Witch-finder training, she said. You learn to count heads.

"So should you."

"I'll sleep when there's a world worth dreaming in." She sheathed the blade and climbed up beside me anyway, settling onto the root with the gracelessness of someone built for armor. For a while neither of us spoke. The dark sky pressed down like a lid.

"Tomorrow," she said finally. "You go in there and offer them your—" She gestured at my arm, at the glow. "And then what? We've read the same texts, Xanthe. The anchor doesn't survive. The anchor doesn't even *remain*. You dissolve into the covenant. No body, no ghost. Just... structure."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it—I watched her hate it—and she pushed on anyway. "Because I burned people for less magic than what's in your little finger, and I told myself it was law, it was order, it was protection, and it took me twenty years to understand I'd been feeding a furnace. Don't you dare climb onto that altar because it's tidy. Because it's *one life against the world* and that arithmetic is exactly how men like Vane are made."

I looked at her. Firelight caught the scar across her jaw—the one she'd earned the day she killed Inquisitor General Halloway and became a traitor instead of an instrument.

"What would you have me do?" I asked. "There's no other anchor. Odile searched for thirty years."

"I don't know." She said it flatly, honestly. "But I've learned that 'I don't know' is where the living answers hide. The ones who always know are the ones building altars." She touched my glowing arm once, briefly, almost gently. "Whatever happens tomorrow—you walk out. That's an order. I'm still technically your superior officer in something or other; I've decided it counts."

A laugh escaped me, thin and surprised. Below, Bramble's lullaby drifted up, and somewhere far away, thunder rolled without clouds.

Neither of us knew yet that we were already being watched.

* * *

Dawn came grey and starless, and we went down into the Hollow Hollow together.

Inside, the tree was vast enough to hold weather. Mist moved between the roots in slow currents. The carved spirals ran up the walls in bands, and as I stepped onto the floor of the chamber—packed earth worn glass-smooth by centuries of bare feet—the carvings began to glow. Faint gold, answering mine. Lines lit in sequence, spiral by spiral, like breath drawn after a very long holding.

At the center of the floor was a shallow basin of stone, and in the basin, a circle of interlocking grooves: hands. Dozens of hands, carved reaching toward one another, forming a ring. The first oath, made visible. I had expected an altar. I found a table instead—a table set for many.

"Well," whispered Bramble, her fae-sight flaring silver in her eyes. "That's not what the queen thinks is here."

The air tore.

It didn't open—it *tore*, a seam splitting the mist, and through it came the Court. Not the whole court, but enough: twelve figures of thorned beauty, tall as doorframes, their skins shifting between bark and moonlight. At their center walked the Queen of the Autumn Throne, and she wore her hunger like perfume.

"Mortal," she said, and her voice made the mist kneel. "Daughter-of-nothing, blood-of-the-Voss. You stand on our stone, in the hinge of worlds, and you mean to do *our* work. So we have come to supervise."

"The covenant binds both realms," I said, and was proud that my voice held. "Odile's letters name your court as signatory. You get a voice. Not custody."

"We get what survives," the Queen said, drifting closer, and the thorned lords spread around the chamber's rim like a closing fist. "When your blood fills that basin, the new covenant must have a keeper. A single will to hold the weave, or it frays—we have seen this across ten thousand years. Give us the anchor. Bind it to the throne. We will keep the crossings open, the stars kindled, and your fragile cities untouched." Her smile was an orchid blooming. "Refuse, and we let the usurper take you instead, and make our own arrangements with whatever he becomes."

"Custody," Sera said, stepping forward with her hand on her hilt, "meaning slavery with better paperwork."

"Human," the Queen said, without looking at her, the way one dismisses weather.

And that was when the sky above the broken crown of the tree went wrong.

Not darker—it was already dark—but *thicker*, as if the night itself congealed, poured downward like tar. The mist in the chamber froze mid-current. Bramble screamed and dropped to her knees, clutching her head, and I understood dimly that her fae-sight was being drowned, smothered by something vast approaching. The Queen's thorned lords turned as one, and for the first time since I'd met her, the Queen of the Autumn Throne looked afraid.

He came down through the crown of the tree like smoke learning to be a man.

Aldric Vane had been handsome once, in the cold way of statues. What descended into the Hollow Hollow had kept his outline and lost everything else. His skin held a faint luminance, stolen light showing through him like a lamp through parchment. His eyes had no whites anymore. When he landed on the chamber floor without sound, the carvings around him guttered and died.

"Keeper," he said. His voice was quiet, reasonable, and utterly empty—the voice of a well. "Do you know what I discovered, drinking my way through the harvest camps? Starlight remembers being *whole*. Every stolen fragment screams for the rest. And every fragment whispers of the place where wholeness lives." He turned his lamp-pale gaze to the basin, to the ring of carved hands, and something moved behind his face that might have been longing in a starving man. "The anchor-stone. The original seal. All that gathered light, still woven into this place."

"You want to eat the covenant," I said.

"I want to *become* it." He smiled, and the smile was worse than the emptiness. "A god needs no wards, no borders, no bargains. A god simply *is*, and the world arranges itself. Your mistress hoarded this secret her whole life and died before using it. Wasteful woman. I intend to be thorough."

The Queen's thorned lords drew weapons of black wood and colder moonlight. "Usurper," she said, "this hinge belongs to the Court."

"This hinge," Vane said, raising one hand, and the darkness around him thickened into shapes—harvest-camp shapes, cages of compressed night, things that had been his Inquisitors once and were something else now, "belongs to whoever can hold it. Shall we discover who that is?"

Everything happened at once.

The thorned lords charged. Vane's shadow-things met them with a sound like glaciers colliding. The Queen raised both arms and the mist itself became blades, and the ancient carvings blazed and shattered underfoot as powers that hadn't contended since the first oath tore the heart-chamber apart. Bramble dragged Sera behind a root the width of a house. Cassian shouted orders to soldiers who couldn't hear him. The world's hinge had become a battlefield, and I stood alone at the center of it, beside the basin, with a knife in my belt and my blood singing so loudly I could taste copper and gold.

I could cut my palm now. Right now, while they fought. Fill the basin, trigger the rite, become the anchor before either monster could stop me. One life against the world. Tidy arithmetic. Sera's voice in my memory: *that's exactly how men like Vane are made.*

Or—

Vane saw me. Across the chaos, through falling splinters of moonlight, his empty eyes found me and narrowed, and he abandoned the Queen's lords mid-battle, flowing toward me faster than anything human should move, darkness streaming behind him like a comet's tail.

The Queen saw him move and screamed, *"Stop him, he'll unmake the seal!"*

And I did the only thing no one at the hinge of the world expected.

I stepped *into* the basin and put my bleeding-free hand flat on the ring of carved hands, and I spoke—not the old oath, not any rite I'd been taught, but the truth, loud enough for all three armies of the impossible to hear:

"This covenant was never meant for one! Look at the carving—it's *hands*, plural, a circle, a table set for many! Odile lied to herself, the Court lied to its people, Vane lies to himself right now—and I refuse all of it!"

Gold light erupted from the carvings, racing up the walls, and beneath my palm the ring of stone hands began, impossibly, to *warm*—as if the first oath-takers were reaching back across ten thousand years to grip mine.

Vane stopped three paces away, darkness coiling, hunger and confusion warring in his ruined face. The Queen hovered at the chamber's rim, thorns bristling, calculation replacing fear. Behind me, boots on stone—Sera, Bramble, Cassian, running toward the light without knowing yet what it asked of them.

"Little Keeper," Vane said softly. "You cannot rewrite what is about to belong to me."

"Watch me," I said, as the first of my friends reached the basin's edge, and the hollow tree began, for the first time in a thousand years, to hum.
