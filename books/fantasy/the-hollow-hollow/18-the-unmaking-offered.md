## 18. The Unmaking Offered

The hollow tree breathed.

That was the only word for it. The vast trunk rose out of the earth like a cathedral turned inside out, its heartwood scooped away by centuries of wind and oath and time, and when the wind moved through it, the whole structure sighed—a long, low sound that Xanthe felt in their teeth, in the marrow of their arms, in the scar-tissue of every secret they had ever kept. Inside, the walls were carved floor to impossible height with names. Millions of them. Some so old the letters had softened back into wood grain, some fresh enough to still hold the ghost of a knife's edge.

The names of the Keepers. All of them. Every one who had ever been consumed.

Xanthe stood at the center of the hollow with their palm pressed to a shallow basin of living stone, and they did not look up at the names, because they had already found the ones they were looking for: *Voss*, repeated down through the generations like a refrain, each entry ending in the same small carved symbol—a circle with a thread passing through it. A knot. An anchor point.

A noose, if you looked at it honestly.

"Say the words," the fae queen said from the rim of the hollow above, her voice falling down through the dark like cold water. "And be done with it."

* * *

They had come to this moment the way all terrible things come—slowly, then all at once.

The rite itself was simple. That was the cruelest part. Five years of hiding, weeks of road and river and ash, a battle at the ford of Mirren where Sera had stood in the water beside people she'd once been ordered to burn—and all of it funneling down to this: blood in a stone bowl, an oath spoken aloud, and the covenant's fabric reaching up to take what it was owed.

Bramble had explained it on the last mile of the march, her fae-sight flickering in and out like a guttering lamp as they crossed into the deep wilds where the Otherworld's edge bled through. "It's not death," she'd said, not looking at Xanthe, which was how Bramble said the hardest things. "Death would be kinder, maybe. It's—you get woven in. Every thought, every memory, every stubborn little piece of what makes you *you*, unpicked and spread through the pattern. You don't stop existing. You stop being *anyone*. Like a drop of dye in a river."

"And the old Keepers knew?" Xanthe had asked.

"The first one did. After that—" Bramble's shrug had been very small. "After that there wasn't anyone left to ask."

Now the basin waited, and the queen waited, and somewhere beyond the tree's far wall—beyond the world's hinge—the air trembled with the approach of something that drank starlight and called itself Aldric Vane.

Xanthe drew the knife across their palm.

The blood came bright and strange, glowing faintly gold the way it had since the night the stars went out, since the wards shattered and their hidden magic flared visible for the first time in five years. It fell into the basin and the basin *drank*, and the hollow tree woke around them like a beast remembering hunger.

Then the rite showed them.

* * *

It was not a vision, exactly. Visions had edges. This had none.

One moment Xanthe was standing; the next they were *unstanding*, poured out along threads of light that ran in every direction—through the roots of the tree, under the sea, up into the wound in the sky where the stars should have been. They could feel the covenant's fabric the way a hand feels cloth: every ward on every door in Greyharbor, every crossing Bramble's kin were trapped behind, every hedge-charm and luck-knot and whispered protection, all of it one great weaving with a hole torn in its center. And the hole was shaped like them.

*This is what you are for,* the weaving seemed to say, without words, without cruelty. *This is what you have always been for.*

And Xanthe understood—finally, completely, the way you understand a thing only when it is too late to matter—that their mistress had known. Old Maren, who had taught them knots and charms and how to laugh quietly, had known exactly what her apprentice's blood was for, and had died keeping the secret anyway, because she had wanted Xanthe to have five years of being a person before becoming a pattern.

*I'm sorry, child,* the memory of her voice said, or Xanthe imagined it said. *I couldn't carry it and I wouldn't hand it to you until your hands were strong.*

The dissolution came next, shown honestly, the way the covenant showed everything to those who fed it. Xanthe felt themselves begin to unpick. Their name going soft at the edges. The smell of rain on Greyharbor cobblestones blurring into the smell of rain everywhere, nowhere, everywhere. Bramble's crooked grin dissolving into the general warmth of *people I have known*. Sera's treason, Cassian's ambition, the child with the sigil-stamped coin—all of it flattening, averaging, becoming texture instead of memory.

They would not suffer. That was the horror of it. They would simply *stop being someone*, gently, thoroughly, forever, and the stars would come back, and no one would mourn them because no one would remember there was anything to mourn.

*It isn't so bad,* the weaving offered, and the worst part was that it wasn't lying.

Xanthe stood inside their own unmaking and considered it.

They were tired. Gods, they were tired. Tired of hiding magic behind mundane craft, tired of running, tired of being hunted by witch-finders and distrusted by courts and bargained over by exiled lords. The offer was peace. Not death—peace. To lay down the unbearable weight of being Xanthe Voss and become instead the thing that held up the sky.

Their knees found the stone. Their blood kept falling, patient, golden.

*Let go,* said the hollow tree, in the voice of every Keeper whose name was carved on its walls. *We did. It doesn't hurt.*

"I know," Xanthe whispered. "I know it doesn't hurt. That's what frightens me."

Because they had learned something on the road of ash, among refugees who mistook them for a saint: that identity was not a possession but a practice, something given by community, built hand to hand. And they had learned something worse at the court of thorns: that belonging could be counterfeited, that being used could wear belonging's face. If they let themselves dissolve now—if they chose erasure because erasure was easy—then everyone who had ever told them *you are one of us* would have been speaking to a shadow.

Maren hadn't died so Xanthe could stop existing. Maren had died so Xanthe could *choose*.

But oh, the choice was heavy. The choice was a mountain. Xanthe knelt in the basin of their own blood and felt their edges thinning and thought, with a calm that terrified them: *I could just stop fighting. I could just let the river take the dye.*

The stars would return. Vane would be stopped—or he wouldn't, and it wouldn't be Xanthe's grief anymore. Either way, the ache would end.

Their eyes closed.

* * *

What broke the spell was not a shout. It was boots.

Xanthe heard them coming down the spiral of the hollow—more than one set, uneven, human, *refusing to be quiet about it*—and then the circle of stone around the basin filled with shadows, and Xanthe opened their eyes.

Sera Dune stood at the northern point, still in the scorched half-armor she'd worn at the ford, her witch-finder's badge torn off and left somewhere on a battlefield. She didn't kneel. She planted herself like a pike in the ground and said, "Whatever it's asking, the answer is no."

"It isn't asking," Xanthe managed. "It's showing."

"Then show it mine." Sera dragged her own knife across her palm before anyone could stop her and pressed her bleeding hand flat against the stone. "I spent ten years burning charm-sellers. If this thing needs an anchor, it can take my guilt. There's plenty. It'll hold."

The covenant shuddered. Threads of light whipped toward her hand—and recoiled, confused, finding a soul it had no claim on.

"That's not how it works," Xanthe said, and their voice cracked in the middle. "It has to be willing. It has to be *me*. My blood is the seal."

"Your blood is *blood*," said Bramble, dropping to the eastern point, her fae-sight blazing suddenly bright, both her inheritances awake at once. She cut her palm—her fae blood ran silver-gold, mortal blood ran red, and where they mixed on the stone the glow turned white. "Half of me is from the other side of the veil. If the covenant binds both realms, it can bind both halves of me. Take the fae half. I've been carrying it like luggage my whole life."

"Bramble—"

"Don't you *dare* tell me it doesn't work that way." Her grin was crooked and wet-eyed. "You think I followed you across a burned country to watch you dissolve politely? I made you promise me the crossings, Xanthe. Promises are *my* currency. Let me pay."

Cassian Roe took the southern point, and for once there was nothing polished in his face—no negotiation, no angle, just a man who had watched his family's throne stolen and his name made mud and had nearly made Xanthe a figurehead to fix it, and knew it. He opened his palm without a knife. A thin line already marked it—an old scar, self-inflicted, from the night of his exile.

"No more blood tonight," he said. "Take the oath instead. I swear my house's claim—every right, every title, every debt owed to the name Roe—into the covenant's fabric. Let the weaving hold my legacy instead of your self. It's worth less than a life. It's also mine to give, which is more than I can say for what I tried to spend of yours." His jaw tightened. "I'm sorry. For the side deal. For all of it."

The fourth shadow hesitated at the western point. Wren—the refugee girl from the road of ash, fifteen years old, who had pressed a sprig of rowan into Xanthe's hand outside a burned village and called them saint until Xanthe sat her down and taught her wards instead. She was crying, and she was not ashamed of it.

"You said magic belongs to communities," Wren said. "You said it on the road. You said nobody keeps the fire alone." She didn't cut herself. She simply put both hands on the stone, palms open, the way you offer bread. "So I'm putting myself in. Not my blood. My *keeping*. Somebody has to remember you were a person. If the covenant takes you, it has to take the remembering too, and I won't let it forget."

The hollow tree groaned—a sound like a forest arguing with itself. The threads of light spun wildly now, darting from Sera to Bramble to Cassian to Wren and back to Xanthe, unable to settle, a loom with too many hands on it.

And then, from the top of the spiral, came a shuffling, muttering, unmistakable sound: Old Tam the tinker, who had joined at Mirren because Xanthe had re-strung his cart axle and asked nothing for it, hauling himself down step by step with his bad hip, swearing cheerfully at every stair. He reached the circle, looked at the four bleeding hands and one pair of open ones, and snorted.

"Well, I'm not cutting myself, I've got work tomorrow." He dug in his coat and produced a luck-knot—one of Xanthe's own, bought in Greyharbor market a lifetime ago, frayed almost to nothing. He dropped it into the basin atop the blood. "There. Knot's paid for. So am I. Add me to whatever this is."

Xanthe knelt in the middle of them, undone.

They had walked into the hollow ready to be unmade alone, and the truth of it—the simple, staggering truth—was that they had never once been alone, and had been too busy carrying everything to notice that other hands kept reaching for the load.

"It won't balance," Xanthe whispered. "The rite needs a single anchor. One seal. That's how it was written—"

"Then we're not doing the rite as it was written," Sera said flatly. "We never have. Look at us."

And Xanthe looked. At the witch-finder who chose justice over law. At the smuggler paying in inheritance. At the nobleman surrendering his throne. At the child keeping memory. At the tinker with his frayed knot. At the covenant's fabric swirling overhead, confused and hungry and—for the first time in three thousand years—*listening*.

The hole in the weaving was shaped like Xanthe. But holes could be patched by many hands. Holes could be *rewoven*.

Xanthe raised their head, and their blood glowed in the basin, and the fear was still there—it would always be there—but it was no longer alone either.

"All right," they said, and their voice carried up through the hollow, past the millions of names, to the wounded sky. "All right. Then we write a new oath. Everyone who steps into this circle gives a piece—not their whole self, a *piece*, freely, and no one carries the weight alone. Not me. Not any single Keeper ever again. A web, not a chain. Do you all understand what you're agreeing to? Once spoken, the covenant will hold us to it. All of us. Forever."

Silence, in the breathing dark.

Then Bramble laughed—that wild, silver-bell laugh—and slapped her bleeding palm against the stone again. "Forever's the first honest price anyone's offered me."

"One condition," Sera added, and the corner of her mouth lifted. "When the stars come back, somebody owes me a drink in Greyharbor. Write that into the covenant if you have to."

Xanthe stood. Around them the circle tightened, hands joining hands across the basin, and high above—far beyond the tree, beyond the hills, beyond the black horizon—the air began to scream.

Vane had reached the world's hinge. The stolen starlight came ahead of him like a tide of inverted night, pouring down the slopes toward the hollow, and in its heart something barely human walked wearing Aldric Vane's hunger like a crown.

He had come for the anchor.

He was about to find six.

Xanthe gripped the nearest hands—Sera's calloused fingers, Wren's small ones—and began to speak the first words of an oath that had never existed before, while the darkness came down the hill like the end of the world, hungry to swallow a light that had just decided, all together, to burn.
