## 19. A Covenant of Many Hands

### Xanthe

The hollow tree breathed.

Xanthe felt it in the soles of their boots and in the marrow of their arms—the slow, arrhythmic pulse of a heart that had been dying for three hundred years, kept alive by Keepers who had fed it piece after piece of themselves until nothing remained but Xanthe's blood in a silver bowl and the smell of old sap gone to rot. The chamber inside the trunk was vast as a cathedral, ribbed with wood pale as bone. Light came from nowhere. Or rather, from the walls themselves, where the names of every Keeper were carved in spirals going down and down and down, and each name still glimmered faintly, the way embers hold light long after the fire is ash.

Xanthe's own name was already there. They had not carved it. It had appeared when they first bled on the root-threshold, letters rising through the grain like bruises surfacing on skin.

*Voss*, it said. Nothing else. No first name. Even the covenant didn't know what to call them.

"Say it again," Sera said behind them. She stood at the circle's edge with her sword drawn—not pointed at anyone, just drawn, the way she held it when the world required honesty of her. "The oath. All of it. Before we do anything we can't undo."

"There's nothing left to do that can be undone," Bramble muttered from the other side of the bowl. Her wings—torn, translucent things that had carried her across the wild crossing—folded tight against her back. "The tree's awake. I can hear it chewing."

"It isn't chewing," Xanthe said. "It's *listening*." They looked down at their hands. The glow beneath the skin had spread past the wrists now, up the forearms, veins mapped in soft white-gold like rivers seen from very high above. When they spoke, the light pulsed with each word, and they had stopped being afraid of it somewhere on the road from Mirren ford, or maybe in the moment Sera had knelt beside them in the mud and said *I burned villages for less than what you are, and I'm sorry*. Fear needed a future to live in. Xanthe was no longer sure they had one.

So they said it again. The oath as the first Keeper had spoken it, the words rising out of the tree itself, humming up through the floor:

*"One heart to hold the hinge. One life to bind the worlds. One anchor, willing and alone, until the stars burn out."*

The chamber swallowed the echo and gave back silence, and in that silence everyone understood what the tree wanted. What it had always wanted. It did not want worship. It wanted *consumption*. It wanted Xanthe poured out into its roots, self dissolved, name smoothed away, an anchor set into the world's fabric forever—and it would take them gladly, and the stars would come home, and Xanthe Voss would never have existed at all except as one more glimmering spiral of carved letters going down into the dark.

"No," Xanthe said aloud.

They hadn't meant to say it yet. But the word was true, and true words had weight here.

The whole tree flinched. The light in the walls guttered.

"The covenant doesn't get to eat me," Xanthe said, louder, and their voice rang against the ribs of the hollow world. "And it doesn't get to eat anyone else either. It was written by frightened people who thought love meant sacrifice. Well—I've been loved recently. Properly. And I know better."

Bramble snorted. "Here comes the speech."

"Bramble," Sera warned.

"No, let her—let *them* talk." Bramble's grin was crooked and fierce and terrified all at once. "I crossed the sea on a hull full of contraband and lies because I believed this exact speech existed somewhere. Go on, Voss. Say the impossible thing."

Xanthe turned to face them—all of them. Sera with her traitor's sword. Bramble with her fading sight and her kin trapped behind a sealed border. Old Wren, the refugee stonemason who had taught half the company practical wards on the road, standing with his hands folded like a man in prayer. Cassian Roe, exiled lord, would-be kingmaker, who had sold them out to the fae court and then, when the bargain soured, had thrown himself between Xanthe and a thorn-knight's blade without hesitation—and who now stood apart from the others, arms crossed, waiting to learn whether he was forgiven.

And beyond them, outside the tree, coming up the hill through the starving dark of the Otherworld's edge: the sound of an army that should not exist. Drums. Hooves. And underneath it all, a cold bright singing, like glass being sharpened.

Vane.

"He's here," Wren said quietly. "Moon's almost dark. He'll want to take the anchor while the hinge is open."

"Then we're out of time for speeches." Xanthe crouched over the silver bowl where their blood pooled, catching the light of their own glowing arms. "I'm not giving the tree my life. But I watched it show me what an anchor is, and I learned something it didn't expect me to learn." They looked around the circle, meeting each pair of eyes. "An anchor holds because of everything attached to it. Not because of what it is. A ship doesn't stay put because the iron is strong. It stays put because of the chain. Because of the rope. Because of every hand that ever hauled it up and dropped it down."

"You want to chain us to a tree," Cassian said. Flat. Not mocking—calculating, which was worse.

"I want to rewrite the oath."

### Bramble

Bramble Ferrow had heard a great many oaths in her life, most of them sworn by people lying.

Sailors swore they'd come back. Clients swore the cargo wasn't cursed. Fae lords swore by their names, which was the worst kind of lie because they could always grow new ones. So when the mortal started talking about rewriting a covenant older than the sea, Bramble's first instinct was to check the exits.

But the tree was listening. She could feel it listening, the way she'd once felt her grandmother listening through a closed door—hungry, hopeful, ashamed of both. And the mortal's blood-light filled the chamber like dawn filling a well, and Bramble thought: *this ridiculous, stubborn, half-starved creature is actually going to try.*

"Rewrite it how?" she asked. "You can't just cross out the words. Oaths aren't ink."

"They're not," Xanthe agreed. "They're *structure*. Load-bearing. You don't demolish the wall—you put a new beam beside it, and another, until the roof stands on many instead of one." Their hands moved as they talked, sketching shapes in the air, and the light followed the motion, drawing lines that hung there, glowing, a diagram made of dawn. "One heart to hold the hinge. Fine. Then let there be many hearts. Let every hand that swears carry a thread of the anchoring. Not all of it—a *piece*. Small enough to bear. Shared enough that if one fails, the rest hold."

"And the tree?" Sera asked. "It's built to consume a single life. Will it accept crumbs?"

"It'll have to." Xanthe smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile; it was the smile of someone who had finally stopped asking permission. "Because I'm not offering anything else."

The tree groaned. Deep in its roots, something shifted—protest or hunger, hard to tell.

"It needs blood to seal it," Wren rumbled. He was the oldest of them, a mason with forearms like bridge cables and a granddaughter back in Greyharbor he'd walked two hundred miles to reach. "Whose?"

"Everyone's," Bramble said before she could stop herself.

Every head turned toward her. She shrugged, wings buzzing with nerves. "What? I'm half fae. My blood's already half-oath. Might as well spend it on something that pays out." She stepped forward, drew her belt knife, and held her palm over the bowl before her courage failed. "For the crossings. For my kin locked behind the wards. For every smuggler who ever ran the dark because the legal road didn't exist." She cut, quick and clean, and let three drops fall into the silver.

Her blood bloomed in the bowl like ink in water—and then, impossibly, it *rose*, threading upward into the glowing lines Xanthe had drawn in the air, finding a place in the structure, becoming part of it. Bramble gasped. It didn't hurt. It felt like being *counted*.

"Well," she said, voice unsteady. "That's either wonderful or a trap."

"Both, probably," Sera said, and stepped up next, laying her sword flat on the roots and opening her palm with a soldier's economy. "For the law I served and the law I should have served. For the ones I couldn't save and the ones I still might." Three drops. The threads took them.

Wren went next, muttering a mason's blessing, thinking of his granddaughter. Then Cassian—who hesitated, and whose hesitation cost him more visibly than any wound.

"This changes nothing of what I owe you," he said to Xanthe, quietly.

"It changes everything," Xanthe said. "That's rather the point. Swear or don't. Both are honest."

Cassian Roe, who had schemed for a throne, who had traded his honor for a crown that turned to thorns in his hands, cut his palm and said, "For the house my family broke trying to keep. Let me build instead of take." And the light accepted him too, and Bramble watched something crack open in his face that looked, for the first time since Vellamere, like relief.

Outside, the drums reached the foot of the hill.

### Sera

Sera Dune had killed her superior officer eleven days ago, and she had not regretted it for a single hour since, but she thought about it constantly—in the way you think about a door you've walked through and cannot walk back through.

She stood at the mouth of the hollow tree with her sword bare, watching the treeline, while behind her the strangest rite in the history of either world gathered strength. She could feel it building against her back like heat from a forge: Xanthe's blood-light woven now with six other strands—herself, Bramble, Wren, Cassian, the twins Pell and Odile from the refugee camp at Mirren, even quiet Tammas the horse-boy, who had sworn his three drops with tears running down his face and had not explained why, and had not needed to.

Seven anchors. Seven small pieces of a burden that had been designed to crush one.

The witch-finder order had taught her that power hoarded at the top kept the bottom safe. She had climbed that ladder believing it. She had burned a village of charm-sellers believing it. And then she had read the ledgers from the star-harvest camps—her evidence, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs right now—and understood that the ladder only went up. There had never been a bottom. Just people like her, holding it steady with their shoulders.

"They're forming up," Tammas whispered from the threshold. He had eyes like a hawk and the nerve of one. "Cavalry on the east slope. And—" His voice caught. "There's something walking ahead of them. It used to be a man."

Sera looked. The thing coming up through the dead grass was tall and wrong, a silhouette wearing starlight the way a corpse wears clothes. Aldric Vane had drunk deep of his stolen sky, and it had drunk him back. Where his eyes had been there were two points of white fire, and where he stepped, the grass did not bend—it *greyed*, color draining out of the world around him like water into sand. Behind him marched witch-finders in black, and behind *them*, pressed into service with chains and promises, a column of soldiers who did not look at the tree, because looking meant hoping, and hoping hurt.

"Sera." Xanthe's voice came from inside, resonant now, doubled with the tree's own deep tone. "If this works—if the oath takes—it will pull at him. Everything he stole will want to go *home*. I need time. Minutes. Maybe less than that."

"How many of them?" Sera asked.

"More than seven," Tammas said miserably.

"Then we do what we always do." She rolled her shoulders, settled her grip, and found to her own surprise that she was smiling. "We make the numbers lie." She raised her voice. "Wren! Those wards you taught the twins—give me the wide one, the one that turns a charge!"

"On your word, Captain!" The old man came limping to the threshold, hands already moving, chalk-lines of borrowed magic waking under his fingers.

"Not captain," Sera said. "No more captains. Just Sera."

Wren grinned through his beard. "Just Sera, then. Say when."

When came fast. The grey thing that had been Aldric Vane raised one burning hand, and the charge began.

### Cassian

Cassian Roe had fought in three battles before this one, and all of them had been lies—battles fought for territory, for succession, for the vanity of men who called ambition duty. This one was honest. That was the only reason he was any good at it.

He held the western approach with Pell and Odile, the refugee twins who fought the way water fights—everywhere at once, impossible to grip. The wide ward Wren had laid sang around them in a shimmering arc, and when the first rank of Vane's cavalry hit it, horses screamed and reared and men tumbled into the grass, and Cassian stepped through the shimmer and did what he had been trained to do since boyhood, which was kill efficiently and hate himself cleanly afterward.

But the stolen starlight fought strangely. Every man Vane had touched carried a little of it—a coldness in the chest, a grey cast to the skin. When Cassian's blade opened a soldier's arm, the wound wept light instead of red, and the light tried to *crawl up the blade toward him*, whispering.

*Come away,* it whispered. *Be a king. Be a symbol. Be adored.*

"That offer expired at the fae court," Cassian told it, and cut the connection, and fell back inside the ward.

Through the trees he could see the center, where it mattered: Sera and Tammas and a knot of refugee militia—people Sera would once have burned—holding the road against the grey thing itself. Vane did not fight like a man anymore. He reached out with handfuls of night, and where the night touched, courage guttered like a candle. Sera's line bent. Bent again.

Inside the tree, the chanting had changed.

Cassian didn't know the old words, but he knew architecture when he heard it—load-bearing sounds, a structure rising. The light spilling from the hollow tree's crown had stopped being Xanthe's gold and become something braided, seven-stranded, moving like aurora. And Vane had noticed too. The grey thing turned its burning face toward the tree, and hunger rolled off it in waves, and it abandoned the road entirely and simply *walked through* Sera's line, scattering soldiers like leaves, coming for the anchor it intended to devour.

"No," Cassian said—to himself, to the light in his own veins now, to whatever was listening. "Not this time. We are *not* furniture in someone else's coronation."

He ran for the tree.

### Xanthe

The oath finished speaking itself at the moment Vane reached the threshold.

*"Many hearts to hold the hinge. Many lives to bind the worlds. No anchor alone; all keepers, willing, until the stars burn out and kindle again."*

Xanthe felt it close around them like a ring of hands. Seven threads of selfhood, none of them severed, all of them *shared*—and the tree, which had waited three hundred years to consume a single soul, received instead a feast of fractions and found, to its ancient surprise, that it was satisfied. The hollow heart filled with light. The carved names spiraling down the walls blazed. And far away, though Xanthe could not see it, they knew: wards waking along borders that had been dust. Crossings yawning open. Stars beginning, faintly, to itch back into the sky.

Then Vane filled the doorway, and the light recoiled from him like a tide from a cliff.

"You would give it away," the grey thing said. Its voice was Vane's voice still, barely—ambition stretched over a hollow so deep the words echoed. "I drank a *sky*, little anchor. I am owed a godhood. And you hand divinity out like *bread*—"

"It was never bread for one table," Xanthe said. They rose. The light in them was not theirs alone anymore and had never been, and that was precisely why they did not tremble. "You misunderstand what you stole. Starlight was never power. It was *belonging*. Every person who ever navigated by it, wished on it, told their children its stories—they held pieces of it too. You gathered what was never yours, and it's been eating you alive, and you came here to make the ache stop by swallowing the last of it."

Vane lunged.

What happened next, no chronicle got quite right, because it happened everywhere at once. The seven threads *pulled*—not Xanthe commanding them, but each keeper choosing, in the same breath, to haul on the chain. Sera, bleeding at the threshold, drove her blade into the earth and swore her piece into it. Bramble's torn wings blazed with fae-fire as she threw herself into the air. Wren slammed his palms to the roots and gave his stone-mason's patience. Cassian gave his ambition, turned inside out. The twins, Tammas, and Xanthe at the center, arms spread, pouring everything into the structure they had built together—

—and the stolen starlight *heard them*. All of it. Every hoarded vial, every harvested camp, every swallowed constellation in the grey thing's flesh recognized, suddenly, where it belonged: not in one man's hunger, but in the web of many hands, in the shared and ordinary miracle of a sky everyone could see.

It tore free.

Aldric Vane screamed with a voice like a universe being unpacked, and came apart—not into blood, not into ash, but into *light*: a torrent of it bursting outward through the tree's crown, up through the wounded sky of two worlds at once, streaming home to ten thousand vacancies. The witch-finders on the hillside threw down their weapons and covered their eyes. The chained soldiers wept. And where the grey thing had stood, there was nothing at all—no body, no relic, no throne to inherit. Just scorch marks in the shape of a man who had tried to be a sky by himself.

Silence. Then, overhead, through the gap in the hollow canopy, the first returned star pricked through the dark like a needle through cloth.

Then a second. A third.

By the time Xanthe found the strength to stand, the Otherside sky was filling, and through the newly reopened crossing at the tree's root—air rippling like water, smelling of rain and green growing things—came voices. Voices in the old tongue. Wings. Lanterns.

Bramble landed hard in the grass, staring at the opening crossing, and made a sound Xanthe had never heard from her: small and broken and joyful.

"They're coming through," she whispered. "My kin. They're *coming home*."

Sera leaned on her sword, gray-faced, laughing shakily. "Someone should probably tell them the war's over."

"Someone should," Xanthe agreed. And then, looking east, where the horizon held the faint smudge of the free cities' watchfires—and the colder lights of council towers where men were surely already asking who controlled the new stars, who regulated the reopened crossings, who owned the miracle—they added, quieter: "But I suspect it's only half over."

Above them, the stars kept arriving, one by one, like answers to a question the world had finally learned to ask together.
