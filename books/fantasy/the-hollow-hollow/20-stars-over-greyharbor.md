## 20. Stars Over Greyharbor

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

That was the strange part, Xanthe thought later—not the light itself, not the sky tearing open like a seam unstitched by careful hands, but the ordinariness of the hour. No thunder. No trumpets. Just dusk settling over Greyharbor's rooftops, gulls wheeling home to their roosts in the old lighthouse, and then—starlight. First one star, trembling above the harbor mouth like a held breath finally released. Then ten. Then a thousand, pouring back into the dark as if they had never left, as if the night without them had been only a bad dream the whole world had agreed to share.

Xanthe stood on the widow's walk of the Saltglass House with Bramble Ferrow beside them and watched the city below come apart with joy.

Doors banged open all down Lantern Row. People poured into the streets in their nightclothes, shouting, weeping, holding candles up to the sky as though to compare notes. Somewhere a fiddler started playing and could not be stopped. An old fisherman knelt in the mud of the fish-market with his cap pressed to his chest, and around him children spun in circles with their heads tipped back, trying to count what could not be counted.

"They're really there," Bramble said. Her voice was rough. She had not slept since the rite at the Hollow Hollow—none of them had—and her fae-sight, restored now that the crossings breathed again, made her eyes shine like coins at the bottom of a well. "All this time I told myself I remembered them right. I didn't. They're better."

Xanthe looked up. The stars hung thick as herring shoals, and beneath them, faint as breath on glass, the crossings shimmered—the old doors between worlds, unbarred for the first time in a season and a half. They could see one from here: a standing stone on Gullwrack Isle, its shadow no longer just shadow but a soft silver doorway, humming.

"Your kin," Xanthe said quietly. "They'll feel it tonight."

Bramble made a sound that was half laugh, half something else entirely. "They felt it the moment you rewrote the oath. Every fae soul trapped behind the veil felt the covenant change shape." She turned to Xanthen, and in the new starlight her face was wet and she did not bother hiding it. "You understand what you did? You didn't just give the mortals back their sky. You opened every door."

"I didn't do it alone."

"No," Bramble agreed. "But someone has to be first through the door. That's you."

* * *

The kin came home three days after, and Greyharbor wept openly.

They came through the Gullwrack crossing at dawn—a procession of the fae-born and half-fae who had been stranded on the Otherworld's edge when the wards fell, gaunt and starved and blinking at the mortal sun like creatures from an older, kinder story. Bramble stood at the head of the pier with her arms open, and when the first of them—a stooped old woman with moth-wing ears and Bramble's same crooked smile—reached her, the two of them went down together onto the salt-rotted planks and stayed there a long while, holding each other while the tide came in around their knees.

Xanthe kept back. This was not theirs to stand inside of. But they could not look away either, and so they stood at the pier's landward end with Sera Dune at their shoulder, watching families find each other across the impossible distance of two worlds.

"There," Sera said softly, nodding toward a boy of perhaps twelve, half-fae, his hair the green of deep water. He was pressing his face into the coat of a tall figure whose skin shifted like oil over pearl. "That's how it's supposed to work. Doors open, people go home."

"Yes."

"So why does your face look like that?"

Xanthe laughed despite themselves—a short, tired sound. "Because it's been four days, and the Council of Nine has already sent three letters asking me to appear before them. And because I know what councils do with things that frighten them."

Sera was quiet a moment. Below them, the reunion on the pier had become a crowd; dockworkers and fishwives had gathered to watch, and instead of fear Xanthe saw wonder on most faces, and something warmer than wonder on some. A baker's wife was pressing rolls into the hands of a starving fae child. An old woman was touching the hem of a stranger's cloak as though checking whether miracles were solid.

"You think they'll try to shut the doors again," Sera said.

"I think they'll try to do something worse than shutting them. Shutting is honest. What I think they'll do is *manage*." Xanthe watched the baker's wife laugh and cry at once. "Vane hoarded starlight because he wanted to own the sky. The councils will never say it aloud, but they want a smaller version of the same thing. Not to own the magic—to own the people who carry it. Licenses. Registries. Wards with paperwork."

"That's not tyranny," Sera said slowly. "That's just—" She stopped. Xanthe watched her hear her own words and flinch from them. "That's what I used to call it. Procedure. Order. I burned a village over procedure, Xanthe. I know exactly what procedure looks like when it takes off its coat."

Xanthe put a hand on her arm. "Then help me make sure it doesn't get to put it back on."

* * *

The summons, when it finally came in person rather than by letter, arrived wearing velvet.

A herald of the Council of Nine stood in the Saltglass House's front room, looking deeply uncomfortable among the charm-knots drying on strings and the hedge-school primers stacked on every surface, and read out in a voice like oiled wood that Xanthe Voss, Keeper of the Restored Covenant, was requested—*requested*, he emphasized twice—to attend the Council's session in Vellamere, there to give account of the events at the Hollow Hollow and to advise on "the orderly integration of thaumaturgic practice within free civic life."

"Advise," Xanthe repeated.

"The Council values your unique perspective," said the herald.

"The Council refused me aid when I begged for it," Xanthe said. "The Council would not even lend me a boat. Now the sky is fixed and they value my perspective."

The herald's composure flickered. Behind him, in the doorway, Cassian Roe leaned against the frame with his arms crossed, watching with an expression Xanthe had learned to read carefully in the weeks since his betrayal and grudging, partial redemption. Exile had taught him to smell power shifts the way sharks smell blood, and he smelled this one coming from a mile off.

"You should go," Cassian said when the herald had gone. "Refusing makes you a symbol of defiance. Attending lets you set terms."

"I'm done being anyone's symbol."

"Then attend as a nuisance. Those are harder to dispose of." He pushed off from the doorframe. "I know these people, Voss. I sat in that chamber before my family lost everything. They are not evil. That's the danger of them. Evil can be fought. Caution dressed up as wisdom just gets nodded at until the chains are on."

"And whose side are you on in this?" Xanthe asked. It was not cruel; it was simply necessary. Cassian had taught them that much.

He met their eyes without flinching, which was new, and which cost him visibly. "Yours. For whatever my word is worth now. Which I grant is very little." A pause. "But I'd like to spend what's left of it well."

* * *

Vellamere had changed since Xanthe last stood within its walls.

Then, it had been a city of shuttered windows and whispered bargains, crouched under the black sky like a animal under a boot. Now the blackness was gone, the stars were back, and the city had bloomed into a nervous, glittering festival of itself. Banners hung from every balcony. Street singers had already composed eleven different ballads about the Battle of the Hollow Hollow, nine of which contained facts. Children wore paper crowns painted with the many-hands sigil—the new mark, the web of willing keepers, already spreading through the free cities faster than any decree could follow it.

And everywhere, threaded through the celebration like wire through a cake, the soldiers.

Not Vane's witch-finders—those were ash and scandal now, their order broken, their Inquisitor General awaiting trial thanks to Sera's evidence. These were free-city troops, Vellamere's own, patrolling in matched pairs with polished halberds and expressions of profound embarrassment whenever crowds cheered them. But they were posted outside every shop that sold charms. Outside the temple where a hedge-priest was teaching wardwork to anyone who asked. Outside the inn where Bramble's kin had taken rooms.

Watching. Counting. Writing things down.

"They're making lists," Bramble muttered as the company walked toward the Council hall. She had insisted on coming, along with Sera and, over his own objections, Cassian. "Lists of who does magic. You know what happens to lists. Lists outlive the governments that make them."

"Then we'll burn the lists," Sera said.

"With respect, Captain, that's how you ended up a fugitive the first time."

Sera grinned, sharp and unrepentant. "Best decision I ever made."

The Council hall had not changed: the same ring of nine high seats, the same mosaic floor depicting the free cities as nine linked hands, the same smell of beeswax and old money. But the nine faces above them had rearranged themselves since Xanthe's last visit. Two seats were empty—their occupants had died in the siege of Vellamere, defending walls they had voted not to reinforce—and the woman who now spoke first from the center seat was one Xanthe did not know: Chancellor Odile Marchetti, gray-haired, sharp-eyed, with the particular stillness of someone who had survived a siege by being more stubborn than the siege.

"Keeper Voss," she said. "The free cities owe you a debt beyond reckoning. We acknowledge it freely, and we intend to honor it."

Xanthe, standing alone on the mosaic floor—they had been offered a chair and declined it—waited. Experience had taught them that sentences beginning with acknowledgment usually ended somewhere expensive.

"We intend to honor it," Marchetti continued, "with gratitude, with reparations to your company, and with structure. The events of this season have revealed that the practice of magic within our walls cannot remain... informal. Unregulated craft bred the conditions that allowed Aldric Vane to rise. Fear, secrecy, rumor. We propose a Registry of Practitioners. Licensing of charms and workings. Inspection of hedge-schools by qualified civic officers. Nothing punitive, Keeper. Simply the ordinary machinery of a free society protecting itself."

The words were reasonable. That was the horror of them. Xanthe heard the hall breathe, heard the galleries murmur approval, heard in that murmur the exhausted longing of people who had lived through a season of terror and wanted, desperately, for someone to tell them it could never happen again.

"Chancellor," Xanthe said, "may I ask a question?"

"You may speak freely."

"When Vane's witch-finders came to Greyharbor, they had registries too. Very thorough ones. They knew every charm-seller in the city by name, because the guilds had licensed us for generations. The lists you propose would have saved them years of work." A ripple of discomfort moved through the benches. "I am not comparing your council to Vane. I am saying that the tool does not care whose hand holds it. A registry is a list of people to protect—or a list of people to find. You cannot promise me which it will be, because neither can you."

Marchetti's jaw tightened. "Orderly society requires—"

"Requires trust, yes. I agree." Xanthe let the word sit in the air. "So let me offer you something better than a registry. Not a petition—I learned that lesson in this room, the first time. I stand here as a witness. I was there when the covenant broke. I was there when it was remade. And I will tell you what I witnessed: the magic did not fail because it was unregulated. It failed because it was *hidden*. Because frightened people practiced in cellars and lied to their neighbors and had nowhere to turn when something went wrong. Secrecy is what Vane harvested. Don't replant his field."

From the gallery, someone began—quietly, then less quietly—to applaud. Marchetti's eyes flicked upward, irritated.

"Then what do you propose, Keeper? Anarchy? Every hedge-witch a law unto herself?"

"I propose what already worked." Xanthe drew a breath. "Communities keep their own. Teach magic openly—in schools, in temples, in market squares—so that everyone knows what it looks like and no one fears it blindly. Let neighborhoods swear their own small covenants, bound to the great one, answerable to each other rather than to a clerk with a ledger. Where a practitioner harms, their community answers first, and the cities' courts after. Consent, not decree. That is the shape of the new covenant itself—it holds because every hand in it chose to hold it. Build your law the same way, or build a cage and wait for someone strong enough to pick the lock."

Silence. Long enough that Xanthe heard the gulls outside, wheeling over Vellamere's towers, crying their harsh ordinary cries under a sky full of returned stars.

It was Marchetti who broke it, and to her credit she did not break it with dismissal. She leaned forward, elbows on the armrests of the high seat, and studied Xanthe the way one studies a weather system.

"You ask us to legislate trust," she said. "Trust cannot be legislated."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "It can only be practiced. Start small. Charter one school. One neighborhood. Watch what grows."

Marchetti was silent another long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she almost smiled. "You are either the wisest person in my city or the most dangerous, Keeper Voss, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Neither can I," Xanthe said. "Ask me in a year."

Laughter ran through the galleries—real laughter, tired and relieved—and Xanthe felt something in their chest loosen that had been tight since the night the stars went out. Not victory. Nothing so clean. But a door, cracked open, in a wall that had seemed solid.

The Council deliberated for three hours behind closed doors. When they emerged, Marchetti announced that a pilot charter would be drafted—one district of Vellamere, one school, one year—and that the Keeper of the Covenant was invited to help draft it.

Invited. As a partner. Xanthe accepted, and tried not to notice how good the word tasted, or how carefully they kept from letting it taste like triumph. Triumph was Vane's disease. They meant to stay immune.

* * *

They walked back through the celebrating city as evening fell, and the stars came out one by one overhead, and Xanthe found they could not stop looking at them.

"You realize," Sera said, falling into step beside them, "that in a year you'll be testifying again. Charter renewal. Budget disputes. Some alderman will claim hedge-magic caused his gout."

"I know."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I know that too." Xanthe smiled up at the sky. "Belonging isn't granted, Sera. I learned that the hard way. It's built. And building means showing up to meetings."

Ahead of them, at the corner of Starfall Square—renamed last week by popular acclamation, over the objections of two cartographers—a crowd had gathered around a fountain, and Bramble stood on its rim with her kin around her, teaching a circle of mortal children to fold luck-knots the fae way, their fingers moving in patterns older than any city. She caught Xanthe's eye across the square and grinned, and tossed them a knot, and Xanthe caught it without thinking, the way you catch something that has always belonged to you.

It was then that the rider came through the north gate at a gallop.

Xanthe saw him first as a commotion—a horse foam-flecked, a rider half-falling from the saddle, a city guard catching him as he slid down. Then the rider found his feet, saw the crowd, saw Xanthe—there was no mistaking whom he was looking for, the many-hands sigil glinting on Xanthe's collar—and came stumbling toward them across the square, shouting words that the festive noise swallowed whole.

Sera's hand went to her sword. The crowd quieted in patches, like rain starting.

The man reached them, doubled over, gasping. He wore the mud-gray cloak of a border courier, and his face was the color of tallow, and when he finally got the words out, the whole square heard them:

"The eastern crossings—" he rasped. "Something's coming through. Something that isn't fae. The wardens at Mirren Ford say the doors won't close, and whatever it is—" he swallowed, staring up at Xanthe with the naked terror of a man who had ridden three days without sleeping, "—whatever it is, it's asking for you. By name. It says the old covenant had *two* signatures, Keeper. And it says the other one never died."

The luck-knot went cold in Xanthe's hand.
