## 21. The Charter of Small Magics

The reckoning tribunal met in the salt court of Greyharbor, which had been a fish market before it was a witch-finder barracks and was now, briefly, neither. Sera Dune walked its length on the first morning with her boots leaving wet prints on stone that still smelled of brine and old blood, carrying her evidence in an iron-strapped chest that took two clerks to lift.

She had imagined this moment for months — on the road, in the camps she'd burned rather than burn, in the long gray hours when sleep refused her. She had imagined herself shaking. She was not shaking. That frightened her more than anything.

"Captain Dune." The presiding magistrate was a thin woman named Odile Varr, formerly of Vellamere's judiciary, with a voice like dry paper. "You understand what you're offering here. Not testimony. *Condemnation.* Of your own order. Your own superiors. Yourself."

"I understand," Sera said.

"You could still claim coercion. Fear. Many of your rank have."

"I don't want many of my rank's defense." She set her hand flat on the chest. "I want what's inside it read aloud where everyone can hear."

* * *

It took three days to read aloud.

The ledgers of the star-harvesting camps came first: columns of names, ages, the yield of light drawn from each prisoner before they were spent. Sera had carried those ledgers out of Camp Merren with her own hands, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain, and had not opened them since. Hearing them recited by a clerk with a bored, careful voice was worse than any memory. Numbers did not flinch. Numbers made it real.

Then the orders themselves — signed, countersigned, sealed in black wax with the inverted eye that had been the witch-finders' mark for a century. Orders to burn charm-sellers' stalls. Orders to burn charm-sellers. Orders, in Inquisitor General Halloway's own hand, specifying that *children be included, as their craft takes root earliest.*

Sera stood through all of it without sitting. When they came to Halloway's name, someone in the gallery wept loudly, and no one told them to stop.

On the third day, Magistrate Varr looked up from the last page. "And you executed Inquisitor General Halloway yourself. On the road. Without trial."

"Yes."

"That is also a crime, Captain."

"It is," Sera said. "Add it to the list. I'll answer for it however this tribunal decides. But I'll tell you why, and I want it written down." Her voice held steady, though her hands had finally begun to tremble, low, where the table hid them. "Because I had spent ten years believing that if I just followed the law far enough, faithfully enough, I would arrive somewhere just. And then I was handed a child and a pyre and told the law required both, and I understood there was no *somewhere*. There was only the next order. So I stopped taking orders. Everything I've done since — everything in that chest and everything outside it — began the day I decided that some things are wrong even when they're legal, and that I'd rather be judged by strangers than live with what I knew."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere above them, gulls cried against a sky that had stars again — real ones, returned four days past, and half the city still went out at night just to stand and look up.

Magistrate Varr folded her hands. "This tribunal will recess to deliberate. Captain Dune — you are remanded to the custody of this court, under honor, until we reconvene."

Under honor. It was almost funny. A year ago she'd have been chained to a wall in a cellar that smelled like the one she'd grown up in. Now they trusted her word because her evidence had been too heavy to doubt.

Honor, she thought, walking out between the clerks, was a strange currency. You couldn't buy it back once you'd spent it all. You could only spend more.

* * *

The verdict came at dusk on the fifth day.

Sera stood alone in the center of the salt court while Magistrate Varr read it out — the dissolution of the Order of Witch-Finders entire, its lands forfeit to the communities it had terrorized, its archives opened to public record, its name struck from every roll and registry in the free cities. Then the second part, the part Sera had argued for behind closed doors until her throat was raw:

"The office of Warden is hereby established," Varr read, "bound not by decree but by consent. Wardens shall be nominated by the communities they serve, confirmed by open vote, subject to recall at any time by the same. Their charge is not the hunting of magic but the protection of the people who hold it and the people who live beside it alike. No warden shall act without witness. No warden shall act for profit. No warden shall ever again be asked to do what Sera Dune was asked to do."

Varr lowered the page and looked at Sera across the length of the hall.

"The tribunal further rules that Captain Dune's killing of Halloway was an act committed in defense of life under circumstances the law itself had corrupted, and that punishment for it would be justice inverted. We decline to punish you. Instead—" and here the old woman's dry voice cracked, just slightly, the first human sound Sera had heard from her in five days, "—we ask you to build the thing that replaces what you were."

The gallery rose. Not cheering — not at first. Just standing, hundreds of them, the mended and the mourning, and then the sound came anyway, rolling up from the benches like a tide finding its channel, and Sera Dune, who had faced down witch-finder generals and fae knights and the black sky itself without blinking, put her face in her hands and let them see her cry.

* * *

Xanthe found her that evening on the seawall, where the harbor lights were coming up one lamp at a time and the water caught them all and shook them into gold.

"They gave you a whole new order," Xanthe said, settling onto the wall beside her. "That's either a promotion or a curse. Haven't decided which."

"Ask me in a year." Sera watched a fishing boat come home through the gap in the breakwater. "You heard the rest?"

"That they ratified your friend's charter?" Xanthe grinned. "I heard. Hard to miss. Someone chalked it on my door in letters a foot high. *Charter of Small Magics, approved.* My landlady wants me to know she always believed in me, which is a lie, because she reported me twice."

"She what?"

"Once in the first year, once in the third. I've forgiven her. She brought me honey cakes yesterday and cried on my shoulder, so I think we're even." Xanthe's smile softened. "They're going to teach it, Sera. Out loud. In schoolrooms. Luck-knots and threshold-wards and hearth-spells, taught by anyone who knows them, kept by whoever needs them. No licenses. no registries. No lists of names for the next Vane to find."

"No lists," Sera said quietly. That had been her amendment, argued at midnight over cold tea: *the charter must forbid the keeping of rosters.* Every horror she'd ever enforced had begun with a list.

"No lists," Xanthe agreed. "Bramble says the fae are already sending teachers through the reopened crossings. Apparently hedge-craft was originally theirs anyway, before we borrowed it and got precious about hiding it. She's insufferable about it."

"And Cassian?"

"Being a lord about it. He's offered to fund schools in three cities, which is genuine, and to have his crest on the charters, which is not, and he's sulking beautifully because the council told him the documents would bear no crest but the city's." Xanthe laughed, and the sound of it — easy, unguarded, the laugh of someone whose name was safe to say aloud — still caught in Sera's chest sometimes, the way sunlight catches after a long winter. "He'll survive. He always does. Being thwarted is practically his hobby."

They sat in silence a while. The boat reached its berth below; lanterns swung; voices called numbers and prices into the dark, ordinary as bread.

"Sera." Xanthe's voice had changed. Quieter. "There's something I should tell you. Before tomorrow, when it's everywhere anyway."

Sera turned. "Tell me now, then."

"The courier. The one who collapsed in the square, four nights ago." Xanthe was looking out at the water, not at her. "The crossings won't close at Mirren Ford. Something's coming through — not fae, the wardens say, not anything they know. And it asked for me by name. Said the old covenant had two signatures." A pause. "Said the other one never died."

The words landed in Sera like stones dropped down a well. She counted the fall. "Your mistress was the Keeper. The last. She's dead — you buried her."

"My blood was the seal. Hers was the hand that wrote it. Two signatures — that's how covenants work, Bramble says. One to bind, one to anchor. The mistress bound. I anchored." Xanthe's jaw tightened. "But binding and anchoring aren't the same as *signing*. And whatever wrote that second signature — whatever has been waiting on the other side of the doors all this time —"

"— it's been patient," Sera finished. Ten years of instinct moved through her, old reflexes firing in new directions: threat, distance, terrain. Except there was no terrain for this. No door to kick, no camp to burn. Only the horizon, and whatever was walking toward it. "When does it arrive?"

"The wardens at Mirren say the doors started opening wider each night. Whatever it is, it isn't rushing. It said—" Xanthe's voice went carefully flat, the way it did when they were holding something by the edges so it wouldn't cut them. "It said it had waited one full turning of the dark moon, as courtesy. Courtesy, Sera. It's being *polite*. Which means it thinks it has nothing to fear from us."

Below them, out past the breakwater, where the harbor lights thinned to nothing and the open sea began, the water went suddenly, perfectly still — flat as poured glass from shore to horizon, in a wind that hadn't stopped blowing all evening.

Both of them saw it. Neither spoke.

Then, far out, at the exact line where sea became sky, a light kindled. Not starlight. Not lamplight. Something older, and slower, and deliberate — rising like a lamp being carried up a very long stairway by someone who knew exactly which door they were knocking on.

Sera stood slowly, her hand going to the sword she no longer had orders to draw.

"Well," Xanthe said softly, beside her, watching the light climb. "So much for the quiet year."

* * *

By morning, the whole city knew. By noon, so did the tribunal, the council, and every crossing-warden from Mirren to the Ash Road. And by dusk, when the light had crossed halfway to the harbor and still showed no sign of hurrying, Sera Dune stood on the same seawall with the charter's ink barely dry on her commission, reading the newest signature on the document in her hands — the one the council had added that afternoon, in a clerk's careful hand, beneath the seals of nine cities:

*Provisional authority granted to Warden-Commander Sera Dune and Keeper Xanthe Voss, jointly, until the matter of the second signature is resolved.*

Jointly. She looked at Xanthe, who was already tightening the straps on a travel pack that had clearly never fully been unpacked.

"You know," Sera said, "most people get a plaque."

Xanthe shouldered the pack and smiled — tired, terrified, and utterly unbowed. "Most people don't get to find out what's been waiting for them since before they were born. Come on, Captain. Let's go ruin its courtesy."
