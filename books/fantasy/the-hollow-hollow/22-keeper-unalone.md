## 22. Keeper, Unalone

The hedge-school smelled of chalk and beeswax and, faintly, of yarrow—the way all honest places did now, Xanthe thought, though they admitted that might be wishful thinking dressed up as wisdom.

Morning light came through the east windows in long gold bars, catching the dust motes above the worktables where seven apprentices sat bent over their knots. Not luck-knots. Not anymore. Ward-knots, the real kind, the kind Xanthe had spent five years hiding inside harmless trinkets because the world had made it a death sentence to do otherwise. Now the trinkets hung openly from the rafters on display pegs: the first charm Xanthe had ever sold in Greyharbor market, bought back last spring for an absurd sum from a woman who swore it had saved her son from a fever. It hadn't, probably. But it had given her hope, and hope was its own kind of working.

"Master Voss." Pip, smallest of the seven, tugged Xanthe's sleeve. She was nine and missing two teeth and had the particular ferocity of children who had learned early that the world could be cruel. "Mine keeps fraying."

"Show me."

She held up her knot—a three-strand ward meant for a doorframe—and sure enough, the binding strand had slipped loose at the third pass. Xanthe didn't fix it for her. That was the whole point of this place; that was the whole point of everything, if Xanthe was honest, which they were trying very hard to be these days.

"Where does it go wrong?"

"Third crossing. Every time."

"And what does the thread want?"

Pip frowned at it, lips moving. Then her face cleared. "It wants to cross *under*, not over. Because the left strand's thicker."

"There you are." Xanthe crouched so they were eye to eye. "Magic isn't forcing the world to obey you. It's listening until you understand what it was already going to do, and then standing beside it while it does that thing. The old Keepers forgot that. Or maybe no one ever taught them."

"The ones who got eaten," Pip said, with the cheerful brutality of children.

"The ones who got eaten," Xanthe agreed. "So we don't do it alone, and we don't do it by force, and we *always* sign our work, because a spell without a name is a promise nobody has to keep."

Pip went back to her knot, tongue between her teeth. Around the table, the others worked: Tam, fourteen and sullen and gifted with growing things, coaxing a sprig of rowan into rooting in a bowl of river sand; Marisol, who had come out of the witch-finder orphanages with burns on her hands and an instinctive grasp of fire-wards that made Xanthe's own look like a child's scribble; the twins from the ford at Mirren, whose family had fought beside Sera and who spoke to each other in a private grammar of half-finished charms. Seven children. In another year there would be twelve, and the year after that more still, because the charter had gone out from Greyharbor to every free city like seed on wind.

Xanthe stood in the doorway a moment longer than they needed to, watching.

Five years they had hidden in this city. Five years selling lies wrapped in string, magic disguised as craft, self disguised as nobody. They had been good at being nobody. They had been so good at it that when the stars went out and their power flared visible in the market square, the first feeling—not terror, not even wonder, but the first feeling, hot and shameful—had been *relief*. The mask burning off their face like frost off glass.

They touched the scar at their wrist where the covenant's first mark had seared itself, a year ago tonight.

Outside, someone was calling their name.

* * *

Sera Dune leaned against the school's gatepost with the particular patience of a woman who had once kicked doors down for a living and had since learned that waiting was also a weapon. Her warden's badge—new-made, grey steel instead of the witch-finders' black iron—caught the light. She wore it the way she wore everything now: loosely, like something she'd chosen rather than something that had chosen her.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm teaching."

"You're dawdling. There's a difference." She pushed off the post and fell into step beside them as Xanthe locked the gate. "Bramble's already loading the boat. Cassian sent his carriage, which I told him to send away, which he ignored, because he is Cassian."

"He means well."

"Cassian Roe has never once meant well. He means *strategically*." But Sera said it with the corner of her mouth twitching, and Xanthe knew that whatever else the exiled lord was, he had bled at Mirren beside refugee militias he'd once considered beneath notice, and some things, once bled together, could not be unbled.

The road down to the harbor wound past the rebuilt market stalls, past the fountain where someone had chiseled out the old covenant sigil's prohibition and carved instead the new mark—the circle of many hands, open at the top, never closed around any one throat. Children had tied ribbons to the fountain's rim. Red, mostly. Xanthe's color, though they had never asked for it and had quietly discouraged it, and had failed utterly, because people will make saints out of anyone who survives, and the only cure they'd found was to keep showing up with chalk dust on their sleeves and thread under their fingernails until the legend starved of dignity.

At the dock, Bramble Ferrow stood on the deck of her boat arguing with the tide, which was low, and losing, which was unusual, because the tide usually listened to her. Half-fae, sharp-eared, sharp-tongued, her kin's blood running green-gold under skin weathered by salt and smuggling. A year ago she had been trapped on this side of sealed crossings, guiding desperate travelers toward doors that wouldn't open, carrying letters to relatives who might as well have been dead.

Now the crossings stood open. And today—

"About time," Bramble called. "The boat won't wait on your pedagogy."

"It's one day a year," Xanthe said, climbing aboard. "You begrudge me one day?"

"I begrudge you nothing." Bramble's voice softened in the way it only ever did about this. She looked east, toward the water-road that led to the wild crossings, to the Otherworld's edge, where her mother and forty-one cousins and an entire displaced court had come home eight months ago, weeping, to a land that had starved for starlight and was learning to be green again. "I just don't like keeping them waiting. You know how they get. My aunt once held a grudge across four generations over a borrowed comb."

"We're not borrowing anything today."

"No." Bramble turned the tiller. "We're paying."

* * *

The hollow tree had been dying for a thousand years before the covenant broke, and dead for one long terrible year after, and now—

Now it was green.

Xanthe felt it before they saw it, the way you feel a hearth-room before you round the stair: warmth radiating outward through the air of the Otherworld's border-marches, where the grass grew silver and the light came up out of the ground instead of down from the sky. Then the hill crested, and there it stood.

Vast beyond sense, the Hollow Hollow, the first tree, the place where the first oath had been sworn by hands that had not understood what they were promising—that the Keeper would hold the covenant's weight alone, body and soul and name, until the anchor dissolved into the binding and another took her place and hers and hers, generation upon generation, women and men and others unmade one by one so that the worlds could keep touching. Xanthe's mistress, Maren Voss, had died holding a fraction of that weight, and had hidden the truth of it even from the child she trained, because that was the cruelty of the old covenant: it demanded silence along with sacrifice.

But its bark, grey as ash for a year, had begun to blush green at the crown. New leaves unfurled along branches wide enough to walk. And in the great hollow at its heart—the chamber where the rite had nearly unmade Xanthe, where seven friends had stepped into the circle unbidden and offered pieces of themselves, where Aldric Vane had shattered like a struck bell as stolen starlight tore free of him—saplings grew in a ring. One for each hand on the oath. Thirteen of them now, counting Sera and the twins' grandmother and the two fae envoys who had sworn alongside mortals that night, because the rewritten covenant bound no one alone and no realm apart.

The fae queen was already there.

She stood beneath the tree in a gown the color of deep water, and she was talking to Pip.

Xanthe stopped dead. "Is that—is that my *apprentice*?"

"Ah." Bramble winced. "Small matter. My aunt may have brought a few visitors through the crossing this morning. Word travels, among the kin, about the anniversary. And the little one stowed away rather than be left behind, and when the queen found her, instead of eating her—which was honestly on the table for a moment—she asked her about the knots in her hair, and apparently Pip has been *teaching her*."

"Teaching the queen of the Otherworld hair charms."

"She says the queen's braids were 'structurally unsound.'" Bramble shrugged. "Honestly? They were."

Xanthe crossed the grass slowly. Up close, the queen looked different than she had at the rite—softer at the edges, or perhaps just less hungry. A year of returning starlight had done for the fae courts what a meal does for the starving: restored not just flesh but manners. When she saw Xanthe, she inclined her head. Not deeply. The queen did not bow to anyone, and everyone had agreed, in the weary diplomacy of the aftermath, that this was acceptable.

"Keeper Voss." Her voice was autumn leaves. "Your child tells me the old bindings were done wrong. Over instead of under. That the thick strand must lead."

"Pip," Xanthe said faintly, "we're going to discuss stowing away later."

"Yes, Master Voss," said Pip, entirely unrepentant, braiding moonlight into royal hair.

"But she is not wrong," the queen said. "My people kept the covenant as a chain. Yours kept it as a cage. Neither of us understood it as—" she searched, and found the word in mortal trade-tongue, adopted it with visible reluctance— "*craft*. Listening-work. Your hedge-schools spread faster than we expected. Last month a warden in Vellamere—a former witch-finder, if you can credit it—taught a fae smith to sing iron without pain. The smith wept. Iron, Voss. We wept over *iron*."

"That's the whole idea," Xanthe said. "Nobody holds it alone. Nobody holds anybody else down to hold it up."

"Mm." The queen examined a leaf on the lowest sapling—the one that was Xanthe's, planted in the circle's heart, small and stubborn and greener than any of the rest. "A year ago I came to this tree demanding sole custody of the new covenant, and your company stood in my way, and I thought then that I was witnessing obstinance. I understand now I was witnessing the actual covenant. The thing the first oath was reaching for and missed." She straightened. "The courts have drafted terms for the renewal. We will bring them to the moot at midsummer. Bring your council. Bring your *children*, apparently, if they can be trusted near royalty."

"They can't," said Sera.

"They cannot," the queen agreed, and there was something at the corner of her mouth that on a human face Xanthe would have called delight.

* * *

They stayed until dusk, which in the border-marches came slow and violet, the light rising off the grass like breath off a pond. Cassian laid flowers at the sapling ring—he'd brought an absurd carriage-load of them, white and gold, and Sera pretended to be embarrassed and arranged them anyway, with a soldier's precision, into patterns that were secretly wards. Bramble sat with her back against the great trunk and told stories to the gathering crowd of fae kin and mortal pilgrims both, and for once nobody asked Xanthe to speak, and Xanthe let the silence hold them like water.

That was the hour when they took out the book.

It was a plain thing: leather cracked, pages soft at the edges, Maren Voss's handwriting filling the first half and Xanthe's the rest. Every spell Xanthe had ever written was signed the same way—a single initial, *X.*, pressed small and quick, the signature of someone perpetually ready to deny authorship. Ready to run. Ready to be nobody.

Maren had signed hers fully. *Maren Voss, Keeper.* Xanthe had found the letter in the cache a year ago, and read it a hundred times since, and every time had noticed the same thing: how steady the name was written. How much it must have cost, and how worth the cost it must have seemed, to put your whole self into the record of what you'd made.

Tonight Xanthe turned to the last page, where they'd copied out the rewritten covenant—the web-oath, the many-hands binding, the charter's root-spell—and dipped a pen in the ink Sera carried for requisition forms, because it was the only ink Sera trusted.

*Xanthe Voss.*

Just that. No title. The title had eaten people; the title could stay in the ground with the old covenant. Just the name, written slow, letter by letter, the way Pip wrote hers on her finished knots—proudly, carefully, like a promise.

Underneath, after a moment, they added: *and all who swear beside me.*

The page drank the ink and seemed, though Xanthe would never claim this under questioning, to settle. Like a knot finding its true crossing. Under, not over.

"Done?" Sera asked, beside them, having materialized the way she did.

"Done."

"Reads better with the whole name."

"It reads terrifying with the whole name."

Sera snorted. She looked out at the tree, at the crowd, at Bramble laughing with her mother, at Cassian asleep sitting up with flower petals in his hair like a fop struck down mid-battle. "A year ago I burned a list of names because I was ordered to. Tonight I watched a child teach a queen to braid. I still don't know what to call that. Progress seems too small a word."

"It's built," Xanthe said. "That's the word. None of it was granted. Not the school, not the crossings, not the peace, not—" they touched the page, the drying ink, their own name—"this. Somebody had to build every piece of it, and mostly it was everybody, and mostly it was slow, and mostly it hurt. Belonging isn't a gift anyone gives you. You build it, plank by plank, person by person, and then one day you're standing on a floor and you realize it holds."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then: "The apprentices will riot if you're late tomorrow."

"Then we'd best start home."

They walked down out of the border-marches side by side, past the sapling ring, past the green-blushing giant that had been a grave and was now a classroom of a kind, its leaves whispering overhead in a language older than either realm. Behind them the first true stars of evening pricked through, each one accounted for, each one free, each one shining equally on Greyharbor's market stalls and the Otherworld's silver grass and the small stubborn hedge-school where, in the morning, a girl with two missing teeth would tie her first perfect knot, and sign it, and belong.
