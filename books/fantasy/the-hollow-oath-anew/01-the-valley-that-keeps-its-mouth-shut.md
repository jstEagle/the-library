## 1. The Valley That Keeps Its Mouth Shut

The mare was dying, and she was frightened, and Bram was the only one in Hollowmere who could tell her so.

She knelt in the straw at the back of Doss Harrow's stable with her palms flat against the mare's sweat-dark neck, and she listened—not to the wet rattle of the breathing, not to the hammering heart, but underneath that, to the thing the horse was actually saying. *Cold. Cold where the light doesn't reach. Where is the herd. I don't want to be alone when it happens.*

"I know," Bram murmured, so quietly the words barely stirred the air. "I know. You're not alone. I'm here."

The mare's eye rolled toward her, white-rimmed, then steadied. Horses didn't understand words, not really. What they understood was the space beneath them, the current that ran under speech the way water ran under ice—and Hollowmere's ward pressed down on that current like a hand on a bell rope, muffling everything, flattening every voice in the valley into silence. But a muffled bell still rang, if you put your ear close enough.

Bram had spent nineteen years learning how close an ear could get.

Outside the stall, evening was coming down on Hollowmere the way it always did: soft and total, like a lid settling onto a pot. Through the gaps in the boards she could see the last light going amber over the terraced fields, the smoke from forty chimneys rising perfectly straight because there was no wind—there was never any wind in Hollowmere, or hardly any, the valley walls cupping the air the way they cupped everything else. The millwheel turned with its patient groan. Somewhere a mother called a child in to supper. The peace of the valley was famous three mountains over. People said you could sleep with your door unlocked. People said nothing bad had happened in Hollowmere in a hundred years.

People were right, mostly. Nothing bad ever happened here. Nothing much of anything did.

*Hurts,* the mare said. Not the leg—the leg had gone past hurting—but somewhere deeper, some old ache surfacing now that the body was too tired to hold it down. *Hurts, and nobody knows.*

Bram swallowed. She let warmth flow through her palms—not magic exactly, or not the kind that got people burned; nothing the Wardens would call working, just attention, just listening with her whole self, which was its own kind of crime in a valley built on not-listening. The mare's breathing slowed. Her fear thinned out like fog in sun.

"There you go," Bram whispered. "That's it. Just rest."

*Who are you?* the mare asked. It wasn't confusion, exactly. It was the question animals had been asking her all her life, the one she'd never once been able to answer honestly. *You smell like us but you talk like them.*

Bram laughed, low and broken, and pressed her forehead against the warm neck. "That," she said, "is a very good question."

Footsteps on the gravel outside. She pulled back so fast she cracked her knee on the stall rail and bit down on the sound. The mare snorted.

Doss Harrow's shape filled the stable door, lantern swinging. He looked at his dying horse, then at Bram kneeling in the straw with hay in her hair, and his weathered face did something complicated—gratitude and embarrassment fighting for space.

"She went quiet," he said. "Went quiet all of a sudden. Thought maybe—" He didn't finish it. "Your aunt said you had a way with her."

"I sat with her," Bram said. "That's all anybody can do."

"Well." Doss shifted. His eyes were wet and he pretended they weren't, the way men in the valley did. "Maren sent me. Says you're wanted home. Says supper's getting cold."

*Tell her thank you,* the mare said drowsily. *Tell her the girl stayed.*

"I'll come by again tomorrow," Bram told Doss, and stood, and brushed off straw that hadn't been there before she arrived, and walked out into the blue dark with her gift folded up small inside her chest where it lived, where it had always lived, small as a stolen coin.

* * *

Maren's house sat at the crook of the millstream, stone-walled and low-roofed, with rosemary going woody along the path. Light leaked gold from the kitchen window. Bram stood outside it for a moment the way she sometimes did—just a moment, one breath—looking at the window like a person checking whether a fire was still burning.

Inside, Maren had the table set for two, because Maren had set it for two every night for eleven years, since the night she'd taken in a silent eight-year-old whose parents had drowned in the reservoir and who had not spoken for a month afterward, and who had never, in all the years since, told her why.

"You fed Doss's mare something," Maren said, ladling barley soup without looking up.

"I sat with her."

"Hm." Maren set the bowl down. She was a broad woman, gray-streaked, hands like root vegetables, a face that gave away nothing because it had decided decades ago that giving things away was wasteful. "Harrow says she calmed right down. Says whatever you did, it worked better than Vetch's tinctures."

"Bodies calm down when someone stays with them. It's not a mystery."

"No," Maren said. "It isn't."

They ate. The clock ticked. Outside, the ward hummed its note below hearing—the valley's own heartbeat, the sound Bram had slept inside of her whole life, the sound she only noticed when she left the valley twice as a child and had come back feeling deaf both times, unable to say which state was the silence and which the noise.

"Maren." Bram turned her spoon in circles. "Did you ever want to leave? Hollowmere, I mean. Even once?"

Maren chewed. Swallowed. Considered her with those flat gray eyes that missed more than the village believed.

"Wanting's cheap," she said finally. "What would I leave for?"

"I don't know. Anything. Somewhere the sky isn't a lid."

"The sky's a lid everywhere. Here it's just a closer fit." Maren took her bowl to the basin. Then, without turning around: "You've got your father's look when you ask questions like that. Same hungry face."

Bram went still. "You knew my parents well?"

"I knew them enough." Maren scrubbed the bowl. Water ran. Eleven years, and this was the whole of what Maren had ever offered about the couple who'd left their daughter behind like a coat outgrown. *They drowned. They were travelers passing through. Be grateful for what you have.* Once, drunk on plum brandy at midwinter, Maren had said, *Some people aren't made for valleys,* and then refused to explain, and the next day remembered nothing, or claimed to.

"They weren't from here," Bram said. It wasn't a question anymore; it hadn't been for years. "Nobody will even say their names."

"Wash up. It's late."

"Maren—"

"I said it's late." But Maren's shoulders had gone stiff under her shawl, and her hands in the washwater had stopped moving, and Bram understood suddenly, with a clarity that hurt, that Maren knew things. Had always known things. Was keeping them the way the valley kept everything—pressed flat, sealed tight, safe.

And Bram thought, not for the first time: *even here, even with the one person who chose me, I am a locked room.*

* * *

She couldn't sleep. She climbed through her window instead, out onto the roof tiles, the way she'd done since she was twelve—a habit Maren officially disapproved of and unofficially had never once mentioned, which in Maren's language meant permission.

From the roof, Hollowmere lay below like a held breath. Forty chimneys, all smoke rising straight. Lamps going out one by one, household by household, obedient as a litany. The valley walls rose black against the stars, and above the northern pass—if you knew where to look, and Bram had always known where to look—there was a place where the stars seemed slightly wrong. Slightly smudged. As a child she'd thought it a cloud that never moved. She was old enough now to suspect clouds that never moved, wards that never slept, valleys that kept their mouths shut about something.

Her grandmother—no, not grandmother, she'd been told there was no such relation, but the old woman had come to the edge of Bram's dreams anyway, in the way of stories half-remembered—her not-grandmother had sung to horses. That was the family story Maren had let slip once, years ago, before catching herself. *Sang to horses, your great-aunt did. Strange lot. Good riddance.* And Bram, eight years later, still lay awake wondering: sang *what* to them? Sang *why*?

Because Bram didn't sing. But she listened. And lately, more and more, when she pressed her ear to the world beneath the ward's muffling hush, she heard things that didn't belong to a peaceful valley. Fragments. A word in no language she knew riding the crows' chatter down from the pass. A rhythm in the stream's mutter that was almost—almost—a lullaby, in a meter no lullaby of the valley used. The land itself, humming something old underneath the ward, the way a prisoner hums under a hood.

*Hurts,* the dying mare had said, *and nobody knows.*

"That's the whole valley," Bram said aloud to the stars. "That's the whole damn valley."

Below her, a dog began to bark—not the lazy territorial barking of a Hollowmere evening, but sharp, alarmed, at something up-valley. Then a second dog. Then a horn, far off near the ford, one long note that bent wrong in the middle, a note Bram had never heard in nineteen years of never-hearing-anything-happen.

Lamps relit, window after window. Voices rose, muffled by walls and by the ward itself, which seemed suddenly less like a blanket and more like a gag.

On the dark road below, a figure staggered out of the shadow of the bridge and fell to its knees in the middle of the lane, and even from the roof, even through the ward's thick hush, Bram heard what it cried out—heard it clear as a bell rung against ice, in a language she had never learned and somehow knew:

*A door. There is a door—*

The crow that sometimes watched her from the millroof—one-eyed, ragged, wrong—lifted off into the night, heading for the ford, and Bram was already climbing back through the window.
