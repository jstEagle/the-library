## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The ford was the first place in Hollowmere where the water forgot to be quiet.

Bram liked that about it. The river came down off the western ridge in a hurry, all white noise and argument, and it drowned out everything else—the creak of cart wheels on the mill road, the gossip of women washing linens downstream, even, sometimes, her own thoughts. She came here to think because thinking was dangerous anywhere else. In Hollowmere you learned to keep your skull shut like a well-kept house: curtains drawn, fire banked, nothing left out where a passing Warden might see it and ask questions.

She was crouched at the water's edge with her skirts pinned up, ostensibly gathering river-stones for Maren's garden path, when she felt him before she saw him.

It came through her feet first, the way it always did. The valley's animals spoke to her through the ground as much as the air—a tremor of meaning, a pressure behind the ears. This was not one of the valley's voices. This was wrong. Ragged. A mind flickering like a lamp running dry, somewhere upstream past the alder stand, and threaded through it was something Bram had never felt in twenty-two years of living inside the ward:

Fear shaped like words she almost understood.

She stood too fast. The stones spilled from her apron. *Don't*, she told herself, the way she'd told herself a thousand times. *Whatever it is, it isn't yours. Walk home. Boil water. Be nobody.*

Bram Joubert was very good at being nobody. She'd been practicing since she was six years old, the day she'd answered a neighbor's barking dog aloud—*I'm sorry, I know, your hip hurts*—and her mother had gone white as flour and gripped her shoulders hard enough to bruise and said, *Never again. Not once. Not ever.* Her mother was four years buried now, and Bram still heard the bruise in her voice every time something wild looked at her with meaning.

She walked toward the alders anyway.

That was the trouble with being what she was. She could refuse to speak, but she couldn't refuse to listen.

He was half in the water when she found him, wedged against a stone like driftwood, and he was dying. She knew it the way she knew weather—not from any single sign but from the whole of him, the gray lips, the smell of blood gone cold and coppery under the river-smell, the shallow hitching rhythm of his breathing. His coat had been fine once. Traveler's wool, good stitching, now stiff with dried mud and darker stains she chose not to name. One hand was pressed flat against his chest as if holding something in—or keeping something safe.

And around him, faint as heat off a summer road, his mind was open. Dying men couldn't close their doors. What leaked out of Sef—and she would learn his name later, gasped between one breath and the next—was not fear exactly. It was *urgency*. A single thought worn smooth as a river pebble from repetition, turning over and over in the wreck of him:

*The door. The door. Someone has to reach the door.*

"Hey." She knelt in the shallows, water soaking through her pinned skirts, and put two fingers to his throat. His pulse fluttered like a moth in a jar. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His eye found her—one eye; the other was swollen shut, crusted black—and fixed on her face with an intensity that made her flinch. His mouth worked. What came out was barely sound.

"You—" A wet rattle. "You're not—warded."

"I don't know what that means," Bram lied, because lying was also a thing she was very good at.

But he laughed then, or tried to, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up blood, bright and shocking against the gray stones. And through the leak and flicker of his failing mind she caught images, unbidden, unwanted: a country she had no name for, green and vast, roads like veins; a great door standing alone in a field; fire taking a library shelf by shelf while a man wept over the burning spines; and underneath it all, always, the door, the door, the door.

Her hands were shaking. She made them stop.

"Listen to me." She hauled his arm across her shoulders. He weighed less than he should have, which meant he'd been starving longer than he'd been bleeding. "There's a village a mile east. There are people called Wardens there, and if they find you they'll ask you questions, and I don't think you want their questions. So you're going to be quiet, and you're going to walk, or I'm going to make you walk. Do you understand?"

Something moved behind his ruined face that might have been a smile. "*Speaker,*" he breathed, and Bram's blood went cold enough to ache.

"Not a speaker. Nothing. Just a girl gathering stones."

"Mm." His head lolled against her shoulder. "Gathered me, though."

* * *

She hid him in the root cellar.

It wasn't cleverness so much as desperation—Maren was at market until dusk, and the cellar was cool and dark and smelled of turnips and earth, and the trapdoor sat beneath the kitchen rug where no Warden had reason to look. Bram got him down the ladder by main strength and stubbornness, stripped his ruined coat, cleaned and bound the wound in his side as best she could with boiled linen and the willow-bark tincture Maren kept for fevers. The cut was long and ugly, a blade's work, deliberate. Someone had wanted him slowed, not dead. Someone had expected the Wardens to finish the job.

He drifted in and out. When he surfaced, he talked, low and cracked, and Bram knelt in the dark listening with her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out.

"They unmade it," he whispered. "A hundred years ago. Not conquered—unmade. You can't burn a country out of every book and every head unless the country lets you believe it was never there." His fingers found her wrist, grip shockingly strong. "Vael. Say it."

"Don't—"

"*Say it.*"

"Vael," she whispered, and the word went through her like cold water through cloth. It fit somewhere. That was the horrible part. It fit some hollow in her she hadn't known was hollow, the way a key fits a lock no one remembers installing.

"There were speakers," Sef murmured. "Beast-speakers. The land listened to them, and they listened back, and that was Vael's true wall—not stone, not ward. Understanding." His breath caught. "Hollowmere was built on top of it. The ward doesn't keep magic out, girl. It keeps memory in. Pressed flat. Buried alive."

"You're feverish."

"I'm a cartographer." Almost a laugh. "Maps are how I pray. And I've seen the borders. Underneath your pretty valley, underneath the silence—there's a door, and it's still standing, and it's waiting for someone who can hear it." His grip tightened. "They followed me across three ranges. They'll sweep this village within a day. You have to understand what you've done, touching me. You're marked now. The moment you helped me, you—"

Above them, muffled by floorboards, a bell began to ring.

Not the market bell. The other one. The one that rang twice a year, ceremonially, when the Wardens rode the circuit—and which had not, in Bram's living memory, rung at any other time.

Sef's eyes closed. "Too late," he said, almost gently. "I'm sorry. I looked for anyone else. There was no one else."

* * *

They came up the mill road at dusk, six riders in slate-gray cloaks, and the village of Hollowmere did what Hollowmere always did: it became very quiet, very quickly. Doors closed. Shutters drew. Children were pulled indoors mid-game. The whole valley performed its suffocating peace like a play everyone had memorized, and Bram stood at her own window watching lantern-light swing up her aunt's garden path and understood, with terrible clarity, that the play was ending.

Three knocks. Measured. Patient.

She was in the kitchen when Maren opened the door, and Bram heard the whole exchange from the shadows of the stairwell, her hand pressed over her own mouth.

"Warden-Sergeant Corvane. We're searching for a fugitive. Injured man, traveler's dress. He'll be headed for shelter. Has anything come to your door?"

"Nothing but the wind," Maren said, in her flat market voice, the voice that had haggled down fishwives and out-waited tax collectors for thirty years. "Though if he's injured, sergeant, I hope someone finds him soon. Cold night coming."

"We'll need to look."

"Will you." A pause with weight in it. "Then look. But you'll wipe your boots, and you'll put everything back where you found it, and you'll do it quick, because my niece is abed and she's got market stall duty at dawn."

Boots on floorboards. Lantern-light sweeping the parlor, the pantry, the stairs—Bram pressed herself into the dark beneath the staircase, small and silent, listening to the leak and mutter of strange minds just overhead. One of them was bored. One was hungry. One carried a picture of Sef's face rendered in ink, a warrant-thought, and beneath it a colder picture: a door, and orders concerning it, and a name for people like Bram that she didn't let herself finish hearing.

*Speaker. Last speaker. Find the last—*

The boots went down to the cellar door. Stopped.

"Locked," said a voice above her.

"Mice," said Maren. "We keep it locked. You can break it if you like, sergeant, and explain to Captain Vey why six armed men spent an hour tearing apart a widow's turnip cellar on a rumor."

Silence. Then, reluctantly, the retreat of boots, the click of the front door, hoofbeats fading up the mill road into the dark.

Bram didn't move for a long time. When she finally crept into the kitchen, Maren stood with both hands flat on the table, head bowed, and when she lifted her face there was no surprise in it at all. Only exhaustion, old and deep, like a debt long carried.

"How long," Bram whispered, "have you known?"

Maren looked at her for a moment. Then she crossed the room, took Bram's face in her rough hands, and kissed her forehead the way she had when Bram was small.

"Since you were six," she said. "Go down to him. And Bram—" her voice caught, steadied, "—whatever he gives you, take it. And whatever he asks of you—" She stopped. Started again. "Come say goodbye properly. That's all I ask."

* * *

He was worse. The climb down the ladder and the ringing bell and the hours had spent whatever was left of him, and Bram knew it the moment she knelt beside him in the dark, the way you know a storm has passed even before the rain stops.

"Sef."

"Mm. Still here." His one eye found her. "They'll come back. Corvane's thorough. Tomorrow, maybe the day after. You can't be here when they do."

"Neither can you."

"No." He smiled with bloody teeth. "No, I'm done traveling." His hand crawled across the dirt floor, found hers, and pressed something into it—rolled oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, stained dark at one end. It was warm from his body. It was heavier than it looked. "The map. It's not paper, don't ask me what it is, I don't know anymore. It knows where it's going. It's been trying to go home since the day I bought it off a corpse." His breathing hitched, steadied. "Follow the water west. Past the ghost orchards. Don't trust the crow."

"The—what?"

"And girl." His grip tightened, sudden, desperate, the last of him funneled into five words. "When they ask who you are—and they will ask—you tell them the truth. Tell them you're the last speaker of Vael."

The words landed in her like stones dropped down a well.

"No," Bram said. "No, I'm not—I'm nobody, I calm horses, I talk to Maren's hens, that's—that's nothing, that's a trick, I never asked for—"

"Nobody asks," Sef whispered. His eye was losing its focus, going soft and far away. "That's how they choose. The ones who never asked." His fingers loosened. "Door's... waiting. Been waiting a hundred years for... someone who'd listen..."

And then, so quietly she nearly missed it, in a language she had never been taught and understood perfectly, the way you understand your own heartbeat:

"*Thank you for hearing me.*"

He died between one breath and the next, in a turnip cellar in a valley that kept its mouth shut, and Bram Joubert knelt in the dark holding a stranger's map against her chest, shaking, marked, and utterly alone with a burden she had never asked for and could not put down.

Above her, faint through the floorboards, she heard Maren moving about the kitchen. Drawing the bolt on the cellar door. Dragging something heavy across it.

Keeping her in—or keeping the world out.

By dawn, Bram knew, the Wardens would return. By dawn she would have to choose: stay and be found, or run into the erased country with a dead man's map and a word—*Vael*—that fit a hollow in her she was suddenly terrified to examine.

The map, rolled warm against her heart, seemed to shift very slightly.

Like something waking up.
