## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The dogs started barking before dawn, and Bram knew the sound was wrong.

Not the loose, conversational barking of Hollowmere's yard-dogs trading complaints across fences. This was sharp, rhythmic, driven—the sound of animals being made afraid on purpose. She lay in her narrow bed under the eaves and listened, and beneath the barking she heard boots. Many boots, moving in step.

She was out of bed before her mind finished the sentence *Wardens*.

The map was where she'd hidden it three days ago, wrapped in oilcloth inside the flour crock, because no Warden in history had ever searched a flour crock and found anything but flour. She'd told herself that every night as she lay awake listening to the house settle. It had almost become true through repetition.

Now she pulled it out, and the oilcloth felt warm in her hands, which it should not have, and she did not let herself think about why.

Downstairs, Maren was already at the stove, hands steady on the kettle, as if this were any morning. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, with forearms like a blacksmith's from forty years of kneading and hauling and holding the farm together alone. She didn't look up when Bram came down with her coat already on.

"You'll want the back gate," Maren said. "It doesn't squeak."

"Maren—"

"Boots are coming up the lane." She poured water over the leaves, unhurried. "Two minutes, maybe three. They knock first. Wardens love their rules—it's the only thing they love. So you have time for tea you won't drink, or you have time to be gone. Choose fast."

Bram stood there with the map pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Every plan she'd made in the last three sleepless nights had ended at this kitchen door: she would tell Maren nothing, she would leave quietly, Maren would never be implicated because Maren would never know. It had been a coward's arithmetic and she'd known it even as she'd done it.

"I'm sorry," she said.

Maren finally looked at her. Her eyes were brown and ordinary and they had looked at Bram across this table for nineteen years, and Bram realized, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that Maren wasn't surprised. Not by the map-shaped bulge under her niece's coat, not by the hour, not by any of it.

"Your mother used to talk to the geese," Maren said. "Before the fever took her and your father both. I'd come out of an evening and she'd be standing in the pen having what looked for all the world like an argument with the gander, and he'd be losing." A ghost of a smile crossed her face and was gone. "I never said a word. Not to her, not to anyone. Twenty-two years of not saying a word. You don't get to apologize for that now, girl. It's mine. I chose it."

The knocking came—three blows, formal, patient.

Bram's eyes were burning. "They'll burn the house."

"They'll burn records, mostly. I keep my accounts boring on purpose." Maren crossed the kitchen and gripped Bram's shoulders, hard, her hands strong enough to bruise. "Listen. The back path goes to the alder stand, then down along the millrace. Don't take the high trail—they watch it. Get past the ford and keep walking until the valley stops making sense, and then keep walking anyway." She shook her once, hard. "And whatever happens, whoever finds you—you are my brother's daughter, and you were never a burden, and if you die out there I will haunt you so thoroughly you'll beg for the Wardens instead. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Bram whispered.

Another knock, louder. A voice: *"Warden's office. Open in the name of the valley's peace."*

Maren turned her toward the door and pushed. At the threshold Bram looked back one last time—at the stove, the kettle steaming, the two cups Maren had set out without thinking after all these years—and then she went into the dark.

Behind her, calm as Sunday, she heard Maren unbolt the front door and say, "Good morning, Warden. You're up early. Will you take tea? It's just poured."

* * *

The valley did not want her to leave.

That was the thing Bram understood only as she ran, hunched and breathless, along the millrace in the gray half-light: Hollowmere had always been easy to move through and hard to move out of. The paths curved gently away from the edges. The hills folded in around the villages like cupped hands. For nineteen years she'd read that shape as safety. Now she could feel it as pressure—a thumb pressing down on a lid.

The horses knew. That was how she first noticed the pursuit behind her: not by hearing boots over the millrace's chatter, but by feeling the fear of the Wardens' mounts bleeding up the slope toward her like cold water finding cracks. Animals downstream of her gift had always leaked into her awareness whether she wanted them to or not. These horses were terrified, and their riders' hands were hard on the reins, and there were six of them, and they were cutting upslope to flank the alder stand.

She left the path and went straight up through the bracken, which no sane person did, because the hillside above the millrace was scree and old deadfall and the kind of ground that broke ankles. But the horses couldn't follow there, and the fog off the river was climbing, and Bram Joubert had been scrambling over this valley's bones since she could walk.

By full light she'd gained the ridge below the standing stone, lungs full of knives, and stopped to look back.

Smoke was rising from the village. Not from Maren's house—she checked twice, heart hammering, and Maren's chimneys smoked their ordinary smoke—but from the meetinghouse and the record-hall, two thin grey threads standing up out of the valley's neat pattern. Even at this distance she could see figures moving through the lanes in the Wardens' dove-grey coats, going door to door. Somewhere down there, Maren was pouring tea for men who burned books, keeping her voice level, buying minutes with cups and saucers.

Bram put her hand against the standing stone to steady herself and nearly fell over backward when something answered.

Not a voice. Nothing so kind. It was more like pressing her palm against a frozen pond and feeling, deep underneath, something vast turn over in its sleep. The stone was old—older than Hollowmere, older than the ward, she understood suddenly—and it remembered being part of something larger. A wall. A boundary of a different kind. Under her hand, faint as a pulse in the wrist, it hummed in a language she had never learned and somehow knew:

*Vaelish.*

"Later," she said aloud, to a rock, like a madwoman. "Help me later. Right now help me get out."

The hum faded, but the cold in the air eased, and the fog thickened behind her as though drawn like a curtain. She didn't stop to wonder about it. She ran.

* * *

She met Tam Vasseur at the valley's edge, and he very nearly killed her.

Later she would learn the details—that he'd deserted the night before, that his kit was still packed in Warden grey, that he'd been sitting in the rocks above the last pass for hours working up the nerve to do a thing that got deserters hung. What she knew at the time was simpler: she came over the lip of the pass, half-dead on her feet with the map against her ribs and the valley smoking behind her, and a man stepped out of the rocks with a crossbow leveled at her chest.

"Don't," he said. His voice cracked on it. He was young—younger than her, maybe—with a rawboned face and a week of beard and the flat, exhausted eyes of someone who hadn't slept since doing something unforgivable.

Bram froze. Below them, the land fell away out of the valley's cupped hands into country she had never seen: moorland running silver and green to a horizon with actual distance in it. The wind here smelled like nothing she knew. Freedom, she'd imagined, would smell like something. It smelled like wet grass and far-off rain.

"Turn out your pockets," the young Warden said. "Slowly. If you've got contraband—if you've got *records*—" His bow arm wavered. He was looking past her shoulder, down at the smoke rising from Hollowmere, and his face was doing something complicated and awful.

"You're one of them," Bram said.

"I'm not—" He swallowed. "Turn out your pockets."

She thought about running. Her legs had nothing left; they both knew it. And she thought about Maren, saying *keep walking until the valley stops making sense*, and about the flour crock and the oilcloth and the warmth that shouldn't have been there, and about Sef dying in her arms with blood on his teeth, whispering *the last speaker*. She thought: if this boy shoots me, everything dies with me. Sef. The map. Whatever the humming stone remembered. All of it, into a ditch at the edge of the world, because a frightened child with a crossbow had orders.

So she did the stupidest thing available.

She reached slowly—not to her pockets, but under her collar—and pulled out the oilcloth bundle, and held it up between them in the wind.

"This is what you want," she said. "Or what your Captain wants. I don't know what it is, exactly. A dead man gave it to me and died saying I was the last speaker of a country that isn't supposed to exist anymore." Her voice shook and she let it. "So either shoot me, or tell me what a Warden deserter is doing guarding the only way out."

The silence stretched. Wind moved the moor grass below them in long silver strokes. Somewhere a curlew cried, and—faintly, impossibly—cried again in a pattern that sounded almost like words, and she saw the young man flinch as if he'd heard it too.

"I burned the Kellwick records," he said abruptly, as if the sentence had been fighting its way out of him for days. "Last autumn. Whole village's memory-books. Birth-rolls, song-cycles, a woman's letters to her son. I carried the lantern myself." His eyes were wet and furious. "Captain Vey says it keeps the peace. I keep telling myself it keeps the peace. And now they're burning Hollowmere too, and I sat in those rocks all night trying to make the arithmetic work, and it doesn't. It never did."

He lowered the crossbow. Then, deliberately, watching her the whole time, he unslung it and dropped it in the heather, along with the quiver, along with the grey officer's sash he wore knotted at his waist.

"Tam," he said. "Tam Vasseur. Formerly nothing. I can't go back down there and I can't go forward without knowing what I'm walking into, and you're the only person in the world who knows less than I do, which makes us practically family." A wild, exhausted almost-laugh escaped him. "Where are we going?"

Bram looked down at the oilcloth bundle in her hands. The fog was lifting off the moor below, revealing a road none of her maps of the valley had ever shown—old, sunken, running straight out into the unmarked country. As she watched, the wind shifted the grass aside along its length, and for one clear moment the road seemed to gleam, as if something under the earth had recognized footsteps it had waited a hundred years to feel.

"That way," she said. "Toward a door. And I think we're already expected."

Behind them, far down in the cupped hands of the valley, the smoke thinned. Ahead, the crow arrived—one-eyed, ragged, dropping out of nowhere onto a boundary stone to watch them with its single gold eye—and Tam stared at it and said, weakly, "Please tell me that bird isn't following us."

Bram, who could feel the crow's attention settling on her like a hand on her shoulder, said nothing at all.
