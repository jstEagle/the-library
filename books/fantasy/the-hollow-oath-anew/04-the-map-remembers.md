## 4. The Map Remembers

Tam had been a Warden for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one thing worth carrying out of the valley: the fastest way to tell if someone is lying is to watch their hands. Liars guard their faces. Their hands forget.

Bram Joubert's hands never stopped moving. She tucked the map into her coat, then took it out again. Folded it along creases that were already wearing soft. Unfolded it to check the road, though they'd walked this stretch an hour ago and nothing had changed.

Except everything had changed, because the map was changing with them.

"Stop doing that," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Checking it like it's going to bite you."

"It might." She held it up between two fingers, like something pulled from a drain. "Look at the ford. We crossed at the ford yesterday morning. It's not there anymore."

He looked. She was right. Where the ford had been inked—two banks, a scatter of reeds, Sef's careful little notation *shallow even in thaw*—there was now just river, unbroken, running bank to bank across the parchment. And downstream, maybe half a mile by the map's scale, a new crossing had appeared, drawn in the same hand, the same brown-black ink, as if it had always been there.

"That's not possible," Tam said.

"You keep saying that." Bram folded the map away, hands still moving. "You said it about the orchard too."

The orchard. He didn't want to think about the orchard. They'd come through it at dusk, a field of apple trees in full blossom standing in a hollow where no trees should grow this far north, this late in the year. Blossom drifting down like snow in the wrong season. And when Bram had touched one of the trunks and gone quiet—that listening look she got, the one that made his Warden training itch to reach for iron—the trees had been *wrong* under her hands. Old. Grieving. Rooted in ground that remembered being something else.

By morning the hollow was empty grass. No stumps, no leaf litter, no sign anything had ever grown there. Just blossom dust on their boots that wouldn't brush off.

"The map isn't redrawing itself," Bram said now. "I've been watching. It redraws when *we* change. When we cross something. Like it's—" She struggled for the word. "Like it's remembering ahead of us. Or we're remembering behind it."

"That's not a sentence that means anything."

"It means," she said, with the flat patience of someone talking to a very large, very stupid animal, "that this country is coming back. Piece by piece. And the map knows which pieces."

*This country.* Vael. The word sat between them all morning, neither of them willing to pick it up. Tam had grown up on the edge of Hollowmere hearing it only as a warning: *the borders are warded, the borders are holy, what lies past the borders is dead and must stay dead.* Eleven years of service and he'd never once been told what the warding was *for*. You didn't ask. Asking questions about the borders was how you ended up reassigned to the salt marshes counting gulls until your mind went soft.

He'd deserted yesterday. It still didn't feel real. His uniform was bundled at the bottom of his pack; he kept expecting to feel the weight of the badge against his chest and finding nothing there, over and over, like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen boundary stone—a real one, granite, older than the valley wards, carved with letters worn to ghosts. Bram slept first, curled against her pack with the map under her coat, and Tam sat up with the fire low and did the thing he'd sworn he wouldn't do: he read the map by himself while she slept.

It wasn't hard. She slept like someone who'd spent years being careful in a house full of people who could destroy her—light, one ear open. He eased the parchment out and spread it across his knees.

In the firelight, the ink moved.

Not dramatically. Not the way stories said enchanted things moved, writhing and glowing. The lines simply *settled*, the way water settles, the way a sleeping person turns toward warmth. A road here thickened into a highway. A highway thinned into a footpath. Small annotations surfaced and sank: *well, sweet* — *bridge, burned twice* — *here the singing starts, do not stop walking*. In the corner, where a cartographer would put his name and seal, there was instead a line of text in a script Tam almost recognized from the oldest Warden records, and beneath it, in plain trade-tongue, smaller:

*If you are reading this alone, put it down. It is not meant for one pair of eyes.*

His skin crawled. He put it down.

"You read the part at the bottom?" Bram's voice, quiet, from the dark. She hadn't been asleep at all. Of course she hadn't.

"I did."

"And?"

"And I put it down."

She sat up, took the map, and looked at him for a long moment—not angry, which somehow was worse. Assessing. The way you'd assess a dog that had bitten once. Then she lay back down and said, "Good," and closed her eyes, and that was the whole conversation, and Tam lay awake most of the night understanding that he had just been tested and passed, and hating how much it mattered to him that he had.

* * *

The third day, the road began to hum.

It was a good road, better than any road had a right to be out here—flagstones fitted tight, drainage cut clean, the work of a country that had expected to last forever. And somewhere under the stones, or in them, there was a sound. Low at first. Tam thought it was wind in the wire-grass until the wind died and the sound didn't.

By midday he could make out a melody. Simple. Rising and falling in a pattern that snagged at something in his chest he couldn't name, because he'd never heard it before and yet his body seemed to know what came next, leaning into each phrase like a listener leaning toward a storyteller.

Bram had stopped walking. Her eyes were closed. Her lips were moving.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Listening." Her voice came out strange. Thick. "It's a lullaby. It's a lullaby, Tam, it's a *Vaelish* lullaby, the words are—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth. When she lowered it, her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "There are words in the stone. Someone sang children to sleep on this road for hundreds of years and the road learned it, and when they erased the country they couldn't erase the road, because you can't burn a song out of granite."

"We should stop walking," Tam heard himself say.

"No." She wiped her face briskly, angrily. "No. Sef's notes said—there was a note, *do not linger where the land sings, the land will ask you to stay.*" She started forward again, fast, arms wrapped around herself. "Come on."

But Tam lingered. One step, two. The melody rose, and underneath it now—was it underneath, or inside his own skull?—he could hear a woman's voice, warm and tired, singing to someone small. *Sleep, my heart, the walls hold, the walls have always held—*

"Bram." His voice cracked on the second syllable.

She turned. Whatever she saw in his face made her come back for him, take his sleeve, and haul him bodily down the road, out of the phrase, out of the hum, until the flagstones gave way to packed earth and the melody frayed into silence behind them. He stood shaking. He had helped burn a granary once. He had stood watch outside a records-house in Lowmarsh while his captain fed ledgers into a stove, birth-rolls and tithe-lists and a book of children's names, and he had told himself it was necessary, it was order, it was mercy compared to what the Wardens did to *people*—

"I burned a village's records," he said.

Bram went still.

"Not Lowmarsh. Before that. Ellerby, it was called. Little place, sheep country, right up against the inner border. There was a schoolmistress who kept writing things down. Local histories. Songs. Names of places that weren't supposed to have names anymore." His mouth tasted of ash, and he realized the ash was memory, real ash, ten years old. "We got orders. I carried the crates out of the schoolhouse. I stacked them. Captain Vey lit the fire herself—Ilsa Vey, the one hunting us now—and I stood there and watched twenty years of a village's memory go up, and the schoolmistress screamed at me, and I didn't move, because the oath says the past is a wound and wounds have to be cauterized, and I *believed* it, Bram. That's the worst part. I believed every word."

The silence stretched. Somewhere off in the grey grass a bird called, three notes, and fell silent as if embarrassed.

"Why are you telling me this?" Bram asked finally. Not cruelly. Genuinely asking.

"Because the map knew." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "When I read it last night, before I put it down—for a second, just a second, the ink shifted. It showed me Ellerby. The schoolhouse. The burn scar, still there after ten years, drawn in fresh. It *knows* what I did. It's not a map, Bram. Maps don't grieve."

"No," she said softly. "They don't." She looked back down the road they'd come, toward the humming that no longer reached them but that she clearly still heard, the way he suspected she always heard things no one else could. "Sef called me the last speaker of Vael. But I think—" She hesitated, choosing. "I think the map isn't looking for a speaker. I think it's looking for someone worth telling. And it's deciding whether we're that. Whether either of us is."

"Are we?"

"I don't know yet." She met his eyes, and for the first time since the ford there was no fear in her face, only a kind of fierce, exhausted honesty. "But you put it down when it told you to. And you told me the truth without me asking. So maybe."

*Maybe.* From her, it felt like a decoration for valor.

They walked on. Behind them, faint as breath, the road hummed its lullaby to no one, patient as stone, certain as a mother, sure that someday—someday soon—someone would come back and lie down and sleep.

* * *

Near dusk, Bram stopped so abruptly that Tam walked into her.

"Crow," she said.

He followed her finger. On a fencepost ahead—or what remained of a fencepost, blackened, ancient—sat a crow. Black as spilled ink, ragged, and wrong in a way Tam's eye caught before his mind could explain: it held its head tilted too still, watching them with a single eye that caught the dying light and held it, amber and old, old, old.

"Don't," Bram breathed, and he realized she'd grabbed his arm. "Don't reach for your knife. It's not—it's not a bird, Tam. It's *speaking*. Underneath. It's speaking Vaelish."

The crow opened its beak.

What came out was a croak, harsh and ordinary. But underneath it—Tam couldn't hear it, wouldn't ever hear it, but he saw it land on Bram like a physical blow—something else. Something that made her stagger forward off the road, off the safe packed earth, into the darkening grass, staring up at the bird with her hands half-raised as if approaching an altar or a cliff edge.

The crow cocked its head. Its one eye fixed on her.

And it said, clear as a bell, in the trade-tongue, in a voice like dry leaves:

"*Little speaker. You're late. Your grandmother has been waiting a hundred years.*"
