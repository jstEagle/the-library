## 5. The Crow with One Eye

The crow found them on the third morning out of the valley, sitting on a fencepost that had no fence attached to it anymore—just the post, standing in a field of wet grass like a man waiting for a cart that would never come.

Bram saw it first. She always saw them first; it was the one part of her gift she'd never managed to switch off, the way birds noticed her noticing them. But this crow was different. It didn't startle when her eyes met its eye—the one good one, amber-bright in a face otherwise black as a burned page. It looked back at her with an attention that had weight to it, and then it said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly through a sieve:

*"Vael."*

One word. One syllable of a language Bram had never learned and yet understood the way you understand your own name shouted from a burning house.

She stopped walking. Tam, three paces ahead with his hood up against the drizzle, took two more steps before he realized she wasn't beside him and turned.

"What?"

"That bird just spoke."

Tam looked at the crow. The crow looked at Tam with the flat contempt only crows can manage. "It's a crow," he said. "They make noises."

"It said *Vael*." Her mouth had gone dry. "In Vaelish."

"You don't speak Vaelish."

"No," Bram agreed. "I don't." And that was the whole horror of it, sitting there between them in the rain: she didn't speak Vaelish, and she had understood it anyway, the word arriving not through her ears but through some older door inside her, the same door the mare's pain had come through, the same door every animal voice had ever used. Except this wasn't an animal voice. Not entirely. This was something wearing an animal like a coat worn inside out, seams showing.

The crow hopped down from the post. It landed in the mud a yard away and regarded her, head tilted, and Bram felt—there was no other way to say it—felt *recognized*. The way you feel when a stranger on the road has your aunt's walk, your grandmother's hands.

"Tell me I'm imagining this," she said to Tam.

"I've been telling myself that about the map for three days." He glanced at the sky, at the low gray weight of it. "We should keep moving. If it follows, it follows."

It followed.

* * *

For two days the crow shadowed them along the old road as it climbed out of the river country into hills where the erasure got strange. They passed a ghost orchard in full bloom though it was weeks past blossom-season—trees that cast no shade, apples that turned to mist if you reached for them—and the crow flew the rows like a farmer inspecting his land, croaking soft fragments that were half bird-noise and half language. *Vael. Senneth. Keep the count. Keep the count.* Bram walked with her arms wrapped around herself, listening, and each fragment settled into her like a stone dropped into still water, rings spreading outward through her blood.

On the second night, camped under a leaning stone wall, she finally asked it directly.

"Who are you?"

The crow sat on Tam's saddlebag, which it had claimed as territory, and worked at something in its feathers. When it answered, the words came slow, dragged up from somewhere deep, like a man speaking around a mouthful of ash.

*"Last. Speaker. Kept the count."*

"Speaker of what? Beasts? Like me?"

The amber eye fixed on her. And then the memory came—not words, but a flood, the gift opening wide without her permission, and Bram gasped and gripped the wall because suddenly she was standing in a burning orchard a hundred years ago, holding a dying dog while soldiers in Warden gray moved down the rows, and there was a girl's voice screaming names into the smoke, a list, a count, *keep the count, someone has to keep the count*, and the girl's hands were her own hands, narrow and brown and scarred across the knuckles the way—

The way Bram's were.

She came back to herself on her knees in the wet grass, Tam's hand on her shoulder, the crow watching her with its one eye and its ruined face, and she understood. Not all of it—maybe never all of it—but enough.

"It's her," Bram said. Her voice came out cracked. "Or what's left of her. The last beast-speaker of Vael put what she was into a bird so the Wardens couldn't burn it out of her, and it's been carrying her ever since. A hundred years of keeping the count." She looked at the crow, and the crow looked back, and something in Bram's chest broke open and bled. "She might be my blood. Tam, she might be my *family*."

Tam was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked on the stones. "Family-shaped things have bitten me before," he said finally. "I'm not saying don't trust it. I'm saying trust it with your eyes open."

But Bram was already reaching out her hand, palm up, the way you offer it to any wild thing, and after a long moment the crow stepped onto her wrist, light as a lie, and pressed its scarred face against her fingers.

That night she slept better than she had since the ford.

She would remember that, later. How good she'd slept.

* * *

The waystation appeared on the fourth day, exactly where the map said it wouldn't be.

They'd been following a ridge trail through country gone quiet and wrong—no birdsong except the crow, no insects, the trees standing too straight, like mourners who'd been told to stand up straight at the funeral—and the map in Bram's satchel had begun drawing a spur line off the ridge, a dotted thread leading down into a fold of the hills. The crow led. It flew ahead and doubled back and flew ahead again, insistent, croaking *"Shelter. Shelter. Storm coming,"* and it was right about the storm; they could see it building over the western hills, a bruise-colored wall dragging curtains of rain behind it.

The waystation was built into the hillside itself, a door of gray wood set into a slope so overgrown you'd never find it unless something showed you. Inside: dry straw, a cold hearth stacked with old wood, shelves holding jars of honey gone dark with age, blankets folded with a care that hurt to look at. Someone had maintained this place once. Someone had expected travelers.

"Vaeli safehouse," Tam said, running his thumb along a carved mark above the lintel—a door symbol, the same one inked on Sef's map. "Pre-erasure. Which means the Wardens either never found it or—" He stopped. He didn't finish, and Bram didn't ask him to. She knew how sentences about the Wardens ended.

They made a fire. The storm arrived and hammered the hillside and they sat in the small gold circle of the hearthlight, and Bram fed the crow scraps of dried apple from her pack, and for a few hours it felt almost like safety. Tam told a story about his first year as a Warden recruit, before the disillusionment, something absurd involving a mule and a lieutenant's ceremonial hat, and Bram laughed until her ribs ached, and the crow made a sound that might have been laughter too, a dry clicking rasp, and outside the storm raged and the world forgot them.

She fell asleep warm. That was her second mistake.

* * *

She woke to the crow's talons on her shoulder and the fire dead and Tam already on his feet with his hand raised for silence.

Hoofbeats. Many. Below the ridge, moving fast despite the storm.

"How long?" Tam breathed.

"I don't know. An hour? Less." Bram's stomach went cold. "They couldn't have tracked us through this rain. Nobody could track us through this rain."

"They didn't have to." Tam was already kicking dirt over the hearth's embers, his face hollow in the last red glow. "Something told them where we were."

The crow sat on her shoulder, feathered tight, silent. And Bram felt it then, through the gift—a wrongness in the bird, a splintered quality, like a house with two families living in it who refuse to acknowledge each other. The speaker-memory, the hundred years of keeping the count. And underneath it, threaded through it, something older and more frightened and more loyal to a different oath entirely.

"The wardens of the door," Bram whispered. "Before there were Wardens in gray, there were keepers of Vael's door. Sworn to guard it. Sworn that no one should reach it." She lifted her hand and the crow stepped onto it, and up close its amber eye held a light that flickered between two flames. "You brought us here because she wanted shelter for us. And you're calling them here because *you* want the door kept shut. Both of you. In the same body. In the same *eye*."

The crow made a sound then that Bram would hear in her dreams for years afterward—a sound like grief arguing with duty, like a hinge rusting shut. Then it launched off her wrist, burst through a gap under the eaves, and was gone into the storm.

"Bram." Tam's voice, very level. "Back door. Now."

There was a back door—of course there was, the Vaeli had thought of everything—and they went through it into the lashing rain with the hoofbeats cresting the ridge above the waystation, torchlight smearing yellow through the downpour, and a voice Bram didn't know yet but would learn to dread calling out orders in the Warden cadence, crisp and cold:

"Sweep the structure. Two fugitives. The woman is to be taken alive."

They ran downhill through the dark, Bram's lungs burning, the map a hard rectangle against her ribs, and behind them the waystation door banged open and light spilled out across the wet grass.

Ahead, on the trail, a shape dropped out of the storm and landed on a branch at eye level. One amber eye caught the distant torchlight and held it.

*"This way,"* the crow rasped. *"Quickly. Before I change my mind again."*

And Bram, soaked and betrayed and unable to stop herself, followed her family into the dark.
