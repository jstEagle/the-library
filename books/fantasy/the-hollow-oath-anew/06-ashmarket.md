## 6. Ashmarket

The town announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and tallow and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a cellar you'd promised yourself you'd clean. Tam smelled it from the ridge and felt his shoulders drop an inch for the first time in three days.

"Ashmarket," he said.

Bram squinted down at the sprawl of rooftops wedged into the river bend, crooked as teeth after a bar fight. "It looks like it's falling into the water."

"It is. Slowly. That's the arrangement." He started down the slope. "Nobody official owns Ashmarket. It sits on the old border of Vael, or where the border was, and the Wardens can't burn it because half the people in it know things about Hollowmere that would be inconvenient in daylight. Exiles, deserters, smugglers." He glanced back at her. "People like us."

"People like us," Bram repeated, flat. She had a way of saying things that made them sound like accusations. The crow rode her shoulder with its one eye fixed on the town below, and Tam still hadn't decided whether that was comforting or the opposite.

"Rules," he said as they walked. "Don't give your real name. Don't say *Vael* out loud indoors. And if anyone offers to buy something from you—a memory, a word, a dream—walk away."

"Who'd want to buy anything from me?"

Tam looked at her. The crow looked at her. Neither of them answered, and she flushed and didn't ask again.

* * *

Ashmarket took them in the way a tide takes in driftwood: without ceremony, without letting go. The gate was a gap in a wall that had given up being a wall, watched by a boy of maybe twelve who charged them two coppers each and didn't blink at the crow. Inside, the streets were canals of mud and lamplight, and every third doorway sold something that couldn't be weighed. A woman whispered through a shutter crack that she could sell him back the smell of his mother's kitchen. A man with no shadow—he checked twice; the man genuinely had none—offered Bram a lantern that burned names instead of oil.

"Keep walking," Tam murmured.

They found lodging above a tavern called the Drowned Bell, run by a broad woman named Osk who looked at Tam for a long moment and said, "You're Vey's, aren't you. Were." Then she gave them a room with one bed, a floor pallet, and a window that overlooked the privies, and charged them double for the view.

That night, in the tavern's common room, Bram learned what kind of town she'd walked into.

The storyteller was a rail-thin old man with a voice like wind through reeds, and he wasn't telling a story so much as selling one—a sample, free of charge, to hook the room. He spoke of a door in an unmade country, of a map written in blood that only opened for its heir, of seekers who had crossed the erased lands and come back wrong, or hollow, or not at all.

Bram's hand went white-knuckled around her cup. Tam saw it happen—the moment the story stopped being a stranger's tale and became hers.

"That's not true," she said, too loud, when the man finished. "The door isn't—it doesn't open for anyone. They just failed."

The room turned. The storyteller smiled with all the warmth of a fishhook. "And how would a farm girl from the warded valley know what the door does, I wonder?"

Tam stood up so fast his stool went over. "She doesn't. She's simple. Forgive her." He steered Bram toward the stairs while the room laughed, and behind them the storyteller's voice followed, silk over wire: *"Every seeker says the same thing, friend. Every seeker thinks they'll be the last."*

Upstairs, Bram shook him off. "They've tried. Others have tried to reach it."

"Nine that Osk knows of. Maybe more." Tam sat on the bed's edge. "None came back whole. One came back and couldn't remember her own name, so she bought a new one off a broker and started over. One came back and wouldn't stop talking in a language nobody knew, until the Wardens took him." He met her eyes. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from someone who won't sell it to you afterward."

Bram sat down slowly on the pallet. The crow hopped from her shoulder to the windowsill and stared out at the dark.

"They failed," she said. "But they tried."

"Yes."

"And I'm going to try anyway."

"I know," Tam said. "I just needed you to walk in knowing the price list."

* * *

The memory-broker found them the next morning, which meant he'd been looking since the night before.

He was small and neat and gray as ash, with kind eyes that were the most frightening thing about him, and he set three cups on their table in the Drowned Bell's empty morning room and poured tea like they were old friends.

"Tamsin Vey's deserter," he said. "And the last speaker of Vael. You'll forgive me—I deal in memories, and yours arrived in this town ahead of you. Rumor travels faster than horses."

"You have us confused," Tam said.

"I really don't." The broker pushed a cup toward Bram. "Drink or don't. My name is Corvel. I'm going to make you an offer, girl, and I want you to understand that I make it out of respect. I've seen nine people walk toward that door. I sold passage to four of them. I remember all of them"—he tapped his own temple—"because remembering is my trade. Would you like to know what they had in common?"

"No," Tam said.

"They were all carrying something too heavy," Corvel continued, as if Tam hadn't spoken. "A gift. A grief. A name. The door doesn't kill seekers, girl. What kills them is arriving at it still full. Full people break. Empty people pass through." He leaned forward. "So here is my offer. I will take your gift—the beast-speaking, the hearing, all of it. Strip it clean, store it, sell it to some noble's daughter who wants to chat with her hunting hounds. In exchange: safe passage across the erased country, guides who know the ghost roads, protection from the Wardens. You arrive at the door light. You arrive at the door *empty*. And you live."

The silence stretched. Tam realized he was holding his breath.

Because here was the thing, the ugly thing, the thing he'd been turning over all night in the dark: it was a good offer. It was a *kind* offer. He thought of Bram on the road, flinching at every birdcall, carrying voices in her head that weren't hers, carrying a dead man's map and a dead country's hope and a living crow's grief—and he thought, *she's twenty years old and she's carrying a nation.* He thought of the village whose records he'd helped burn, and how he'd have given anything, that night, to stop remembering it.

"Listen to him," Tam heard himself say.

Bram turned. Her face did something complicated.

"He's right about one thing," Tam pressed on, hating himself and meaning it anyway. "You can't carry all of it. The map, the crow, the names, the war nobody finished—you're one person. If emptying you out is what gets you to that door alive, then—"

"Then I walk up to it as nobody," Bram said quietly. "No gift. No past. Just a body that arrives on time."

"The door needs an heir, not a person," Tam said. "You said it yourself—the oath, the inheritance. It doesn't need your *memories*, Bram. It needs your blood and your choice. You could still choose. You'd just get to choose without—" he gestured helplessly at everything, at her, at the weight he could see sitting on her like weather, "—without all of it crushing you first."

Corvel sipped his tea and said nothing, which was its own kind of argument.

The crow made a sound. Not a caw—something lower, older, a syllable of Vaelish that Bram alone understood, and whatever it said made her close her eyes.

"My aunt knew," Bram said at last, eyes still shut. "About my gift. All those years, and she never said, never turned me in, just left food where the beasts wouldn't see me feed them and lied to the Wardens' faces. She carried that secret for me without ever once asking me to put it down." She opened her eyes. "If I sell it, I don't just lose the hearing. I lose the version of me that Maren loved enough to lie for. And then what walks to that door? Something wearing my shape that owes her nothing?"

"Bram—"

"No." She stood. She wasn't loud. She was worse than loud; she was certain. "I've spent my whole life trying to be less so the valley would keep me. I'm done paying to be allowed to exist." She looked at Corvel. "Thank you for the tea. The answer is no."

Corvel inclined his head, unoffended, gathering his cups. "Nine seekers," he said. "All of them refused my colleagues' offers too, in the end. Pride, mostly. Love, sometimes." At the doorway he paused. "For what it's worth, girl—the ones who kept their burdens? They broke at the door. But the ones who sold theirs?" His kind eyes were suddenly very old. "They broke *before* it, and never knew why."

He left.

Tam stared at the table. "I was trying to keep you alive."

"I know." Bram sat back down. After a moment her hand covered his, brief and hard. "That's the worst part. So was he."

* * *

They were packing at dusk when Osk knocked—two sharp raps, then one soft, and Tam's spine went cold because that was a Warden signal, old code, and Osk had never used it before.

"Warden-Captain Ilsa Vey rode through the south gate an hour ago," Oks said through the door, low and fast. "With six blades and a hound that smells blood-maps. She's asking after a deserter and a valley girl." A pause. "There's a smuggler's tunnel out the back. Costs you everything you're carrying."

Tam looked at Bram. Bram looked at the map against her ribs, and the crow lifted its one eye from the windowsill.

"We were leaving anyway," Bram said.
