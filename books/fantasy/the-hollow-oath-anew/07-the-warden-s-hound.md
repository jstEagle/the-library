## 7. The Warden's Hound

The hounds found them at dawn, or something shaped like hounds.

Bram heard them first through the horses—not the animals themselves but the fear coming off them in waves, sour and electric. She'd been dozing against Tam's shoulder in the cart when the mule stiffened mid-step and refused to go on, ears flat, breath steaming fast.

"Off the road," she said. "Now."

Tam didn't argue. He'd learned that much about her in three weeks of running: when Bram went quiet like that, the world was telling her something, and it was never wrong. He hauled the mule into the alder scrub while she pulled the map from its oilcloth sleeve. It had been restless all night, ink crawling like insects under lamplight, redrawing itself toward a ridge line to the north that no honest map should show.

The hounds came up the road a minute later. They were wrong in a way her gift couldn't name—too silent for their size, moving with a smoothness no four legs owned, and where they passed, the frost on the grass blackened. Warden work. Ward-bound things, hollowed out and filled with purpose.

Behind them walked a woman in grey.

Bram knew her without being told. Ilsa Vey. The name had been in Sef's dying ramblings, in the memory-broker's warnings in Ashmarket, in Tam's nightmares. The Warden-Captain who had spent nine years hunting a piece of bloodstained paper across half the borderlands. She was smaller than Bram had imagined, grey-eyed, unhurried, and she stopped at the exact place their cart tracks left the road as though she could read them like handwriting.

"She's not tracking us," Tam breathed beside her. "She's tracking the map. It pulls. I've felt it pull."

"Then we let it pull somewhere else." Bram pressed her palm flat against the paper, the way she'd learned to, and whispered to it the way she whispered to frightened animals: *not this way. Quiet now. North, into the rocks.*

The map shivered. Somewhere down the road, Ilsa Vey turned her head.

* * *

They ran. There was no dignity in it—three weeks of careful travel undone in an hour of crashing through scree and frozen bracken, the crow streaking ahead of them like a thrown knife, Bram clutching the map so hard the edges cut her palms. Behind them the hollow hounds made no sound at all, which was worse than baying. Sound you could measure. Silence just came.

Tam fought rearguard the whole way. He knew Warden craft from the inside—their spacing, their signals, the way they folded around fleeing prey—and he used all of it against them, dragging Bram down game trails no patrol would bother with, doubling back through a stream twice, once shoving her into a hollow under a deadfall and lying flat over her with his hand clamped over her mouth while two grey shapes flowed past close enough to touch.

It bought them until midafternoon. Then the trees ended.

The ridge dropped away into a bowl of bare moorland, brown and wind-scoured, with nowhere to hide for a mile in any direction. Bram stood at the treeline gasping, and below, perhaps half a mile out, Ilsa Vey stepped out of nothing onto open ground—no hounds visible now, just the woman, walking toward them at an easy pace, as if she had all the time the world contained.

"Go," Tam said.

"No."

"Bram." He wasn't looking at her. He was pulling off his Warden-issue coat, the one he still wore because it was warm, folding it over his arm. Underneath he had on a shepherd's smock he'd stolen in Ashmarket, and with the coat gone he looked like nobody at all. "She wants the map and the speaker. If she finds one of us alone, she finds both eventually. But if she finds me—" he shrugged, "—she finds a deserter. A loose end. Loose ends get tied quick and clean, and then she stops looking hard."

"You don't know that."

"I was a Warden for eleven years, Bram. I know exactly how it goes." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, and she saw him hate it. "Give me the map."

"No."

"*Give me the map.*" And when she still held it: "You're the last speaker of Vael. I'm the man who burned a village's records because they told him to. One of us is worth saving. It isn't me."

He took it from her hands before she could decide—that was the trick of it, later she understood; he'd asked loudly so she'd refuse, and taken it gently so she couldn't stop him—and then he walked out onto the moor with his empty hands raised, calling out in a clear voice: *"Warden-Captain! Deserter Tam Voss, garrison Hollowmere, self-reported!"*

Bram watched from the treeline with her nails dug into bark.

Ilsa Vey stopped ten paces from him. Even at this distance Bram could see the conversation happen—Tam talking, gesturing, playing the broken soldier, pointing back the way they'd come, feeding her a false trail south with the fluency of a man confessing. And Ilsa Vey listened with her head tilted, and then she said something short, and two shapes rose out of the brown grass behind her like smoke given bones.

They didn't kill him. That was the worst part. They took his arms and walked him north along the ridge, and Ilsa Vey paused once, turned her head, and looked directly at the treeline where Bram stood hidden—as if the whole performance had been for her benefit, as if to say: *I know exactly what you gave up to keep yourself. Now carry it.*

Then they were gone, and the moor was empty, and Bram was alone with a crow and a heartbeat.

* * *

The crow landed on a stone near her feet as the light went copper and low. It regarded her with its single eye, and in that eye, as always, there was too much—a depth of looking no bird should own.

"They have him," Bram said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Because of me. Because of the map. Because of a country that died before I was born."

The crow croaked—one syllable of Vaelish, soft, almost gentle. *Stay.*

"I can't stay. If I run south she'll catch me by tomorrow. If I run north she's already there." Bram slid down against the stone, and for the first time since the ford, since Sef's blood going cold on her hands, she let the fear finish its sentence. She shook. She put her face in her filthy hands. "I'm one person. I'm not a nation. I never asked to be anybody's last anything."

The crow hopped closer. Then it did something it had never done: it pressed its head against her knee, the way a horse leans, the way a dog leans, and let her feel the small warm weight of it.

And the land spoke.

Not in words at first. The moor simply *noticed* her—the way a stable of sleeping animals notices a stranger at the door, a ripple of attention passing through soil and stone and the roots of the alders behind her. Bram had spoken with beasts her whole life, but beasts were creatures; this was the ground itself, old and slow and vast, turning its regard on her like an eye opening underground.

Her gift reached for it before her courage did. *Hello,* she thought, wildly, absurdly, the way she'd greet a nervous mare. *I'm here. I won't hurt you.*

The land answered in Vaelish.

It came up through her knees, through her palms against the earth—words in a language she had never been taught and somehow knew, the same language the crow spoke, the language Sef had died muttering. Not speech exactly. More like the memory of speech, pressed into the soil the way flowers are pressed into books, a century deep.

*Who keeps the names?*

Bram's breath stopped. Because she knew—knew in her marrow—that this was the question the door would ask, the question the whole unmade country had been asking since it was erased, and it was asking *her*, here, on a nameless moor, because she was the only thing left alive that could hear it.

"I do," she said aloud, her lips cracking, tears freezing at her jaw. "I don't want to. I didn't volunteer. But nobody else is carrying them, so—I do."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of something enormous considering.

Then, beneath her hands, the ground shifted—not earthquake-slow but deliberate, and a seam of stone parted near the alders, revealing a hollow like a cupped palm, and inside it lay things that had no business surviving a century of erasure: a clay lamp still smelling of oil. A child's shoe. A strip of cloth embroidered with names in Vaelish thread, dozens of them, stitched by someone who had refused to let the forgetting be total.

*Keep,* said the land, in the voice of pressed flowers. *Carry. You said you would.*

Bram gathered the objects into her satchel with shaking hands. The embroidered names she wrapped around the map like a bandage. When she looked up, the crow was watching her with its one eye, and she saw its decision arrive before it moved—the way you see it in animals, the stillness before sacrifice.

"What are you doing," she whispered. "No. Whatever you're thinking—no."

The crow tipped its head back and opened its beak, and began to pour.

Memory came out of it like light out of a shattered lantern—Vaelish pouring into the twilight, a flood of it, names and faces and orchards and songs, and every Warden hound within miles staggered as though struck, every ward-glass in Ilsa Vey's kit clouding white, every enchanted thing on the moor suddenly blind and screaming. The crow's eye—weaving, layered, holding a century of a murdered country—burned like a coal, and emptied.

When it was done, the bird swayed on the stone. Its eye was still an eye, but the depth was gone; what looked out of it now was smaller, older, tired—a crow's eye, finally and only a crow's eye. It knew Bram. It remembered loving her. But the country behind that love had been spent, all of it, poured out into the dark to buy her one night of blindness among her enemies.

"Oh," Bram said. "Oh, you foolish, brave thing."

She gathered it against her chest, and it let her, and far to the north a light that might have been a signal fire stuttered and died.

They had one night. Maybe less. Somewhere out there, Ilsa Vey was standing in a darkened camp, touching instruments that no longer worked, and recalculating—and Bram understood, with a clarity that felt like the land itself speaking again, that the Captain would not need wards to find her next time. Ilsa Vey had been Vaeli once too. Grief needed no magic to track its own.

Bram stood, slung the satchel, tucked the exhausted crow inside her coat, and faced north—toward the ridge, toward the unmade interior, toward the door and the man they'd taken from her.

"Keep the names," she told the moor, in its own tongue, badly but truly. "I'm going to go make sure there's someone left to say them."

The land hummed beneath her boots, faint and steady, like a lullaby remembering itself.

She walked north, alone, and did not look back.
