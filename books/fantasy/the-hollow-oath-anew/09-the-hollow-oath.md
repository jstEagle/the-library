## 9. The Hollow Oath

**BRAM**

The door had no wood and no stone. It stood alone in a field of unburied names—she understood that now, standing before it—a frame of black iron taller than the pines of Hollowmere, filled with nothing but the light of a country that had been erased so thoroughly that even its sky remembered wrong.

Bram's boots sank into soil that was soft as flesh. Around her, in a rough ring half a mile wide, small white stones lay scattered in the grass. She'd thought them river pebbles when they first crossed the ridge. They were not pebbles. Each one was carved with a name in Vaelish script, and there were thousands, tens of thousands, laid out like seed grain.

*No one buried us,* said the land, in her bones, in Vaelish. *There was no one left to dig.*

Behind her, Ilsa Vey came down the slope with her sword still sheathed and her face wet, which was the most frightening thing Bram had seen in all these weeks of flight.

"You should have run," Ilsa said. "I gave you every chance to run."

"I know." Bram didn't turn. "Why would you want it shut? You're *Vaeli*. I felt it at the ford, when your blood touched the grass. The land knows you."

"My mother carried me out through this door when I was three years old," Ilsa said. "She set me down on the far side, went back for my father, and the door closed between them. Forever. Do you understand? The door doesn't open, girl. It only *takes*, or it doesn't. And every person who has ever tried to force it has tried to take something too large." Her voice frayed. "A kingdom is too large."

"Then we don't ask it for a kingdom."

"There is no other asking." Ilsa drew her sword—not at Bram, but held flat across both palms, an offering or a surrender, Bram couldn't tell. "The oath is written into the iron. Inherit and reopen the war, or seal it and forget. Those are the two hands on the scale. There has never been a third."

Bram looked at the door, and the door looked back.

* * *

**ILSA**

Twenty-six years Ilsa had kept the seal. She knew the door's grammar the way other soldiers knew drill: the way the iron warmed before it spoke, the way the field of names brightened when someone with old blood stood near it. It was warming now. Brightening now. The last speaker of Vael had finally come home, and the door was *hungry*.

*Ilsa,* said the iron, in the voice of her mother.

She did not flinch. She had learned not to flinch. "You are not her."

*We keep what was given. She gave herself. Come inside.*

"No." But her hands shook, and she hated that the crow saw it—the one-eyed bird perched on the doorframe like a gargoyle, head tilted, its single eye reflecting the field of names back at itself.

The girl knelt among the stones. Running her fingers over the carvings. Reading them aloud under her breath, badly, stumbling over the grammar, and every mispronounced name made the grass shiver anyway, because the land was starved for its own tongue even mangled.

"She's going to refuse," the crow said, in Vaelish, in a voice like dry leaves. "Watch. She always refuses. That's the whole of her."

"And when she refuses?" Ilsa asked.

"Then the door chooses for her." The crow ruffled its feathers. "It has been choosing for people for a hundred years, Warden-Captain. Why stop now?"

* * *

**BRAM**

The door spoke to her in her aunt's voice first, and Bram nearly broke right there.

*Bramlin,* it said, warm as Maren's kitchen, as bread and lamp-oil and the smell of the mare's coat under her palms. *You have carried so much alone. Put it down. Take the crown instead. A crown weighs less than grief—you know it does.*

"That's not true," Bram said aloud. "That's the first lie you've told me and I want you to know I noticed."

The warmth guttered. Behind her, Ilsa made a small sound—not quite a laugh.

Then the door showed her the second path, and this one it didn't dress up. It simply opened the memory like a drawer: the sealing, a century ago, Vaeli hands setting the wards, the forgetting spreading outward like frost across a window. Peace. Quiet. Hollowmere's suffocating, perfect silence. No war. No throne. No weight. And underneath everything, the fine print written in iron: *and no memory either. You will walk away from this field and by the time you reach the ridge you will not know why you came. You will be whole, and empty, and free.*

Bram thought of Maren's face in the doorway as the Wardens burned the house—*I always knew about you, child, go* —and thought about how love without knowing isn't love at all, just habit wearing its clothes.

"No," she said.

*Then inherit,* said the door, and all its patience was gone, and the voice was ten thousand voices, the field of names speaking at once. *Or be forgotten. Choose. CHOOSE.*

"No."

The iron began to glow.

* * *

**ILSA**

Ilsa had seen the door kill a man once. A memory-broker's hired blade, twenty years ago, who'd thought to pry loose one of the name-stones and sell it. The door hadn't burned him. It had simply stopped remembering he existed, mid-stride, and his body had fallen over like clothing nobody wore anymore.

Now the glow climbed the frame and the girl still wouldn't choose, and Ilsa understood with terrible clarity that she was watching the door decide to take both options at once—to make the girl a sealed thing, a forgotten thing, another white stone in the grass.

Her mother's voice was in that iron. Part of her wanted to kneel down beside it forever.

She sheathed her sword. Walked forward. Stood next to the girl who had ruined her career and her certainty, and said, "If you have a third answer, say it now."

"The land remembers what's spoken aloud," Bram said. Her voice cracked down the middle and kept going. "It told me so. It's been waiting a hundred years for someone to speak. It doesn't need a ruler. It needs a *reader*."

"It needs—" Ilsa stared at her. "You can't read ten thousand names."

"Not alone."

* * *

**BRAM**

Bram knelt and picked up the nearest stone.

"Adele Voss-Kettering," she read, and her gift went out of her like water through open fingers—not into a beast, not into a hiding place, but into the ground, into the grass, into the iron. Somewhere behind her eyes she felt the map dissolve entirely, its work done, its ink becoming ordinary ink.

The grass around the stone turned green. Just that one patch. Just that much.

She picked up the next. "Oskar Lindt-Halloway."

Another patch. The door screamed—or rather, the *oath* screamed, the old binary tearing at its hinges, because it had never once in a century been offered neither hand of the scale. It demanded again, louder, offering her Maren's kitchen, then her mother's face, then Tam bleeding in a Warden cell, then silence, sweet silence—

"I said no." Bram's voice was raw. "I'm not inheriting you and I'm not forgetting you. I'm going to *say* you. All of you. Until the land knows itself without needing anyone to rule it."

She reached for the third stone.

And the crow dropped from the doorframe onto her shoulder.

* * *

**THE CROW**

It had been many things before it was a crow. A woman, mostly. A beast-speaker of Vael, the last before this child, and kin to her besides—grandmother twice removed, though such arithmetic meant little to the dead.

It had spent a hundred years carrying what it could hold in one eye and then, tonight, in none.

*Let me help,* it said, and pressed its beak gently against her ear, and began to pour.

Not memories—it had given those already, eye by eye, buying Tam's freedom, blinding the patrol, spending itself down to the wick. What it poured now was older. Language. Grammar. Ten thousand names, each one attached to a life: Adele Voss-Kettering, who taught children to whistle to bees. Oskar Lindt-Halloway, who died holding a bridge no one else remembers existed. On and on, the whole census of a murdered country, and the crow gave the reading of it, the pronunciation, the music, until its single eye dimmed like a lamp running dry.

*Say them well,* it whispered. *Say them all. I'll listen from wherever listening happens next.*

"Bram—" Ilsa's voice, sharp with alarm. "Its eye—"

But the crow was already folding down onto the girl's shoulder like a spent glove, weightless, hollow, a shape where a bird used to be. And Bram Joubert stood in a field of unburied names with a dead woman's language in her mouth, and began, stone by stone, to speak.

* * *

**ILSA**

It took until dawn.

Ilsa counted, because counting was the only soldier's discipline left to her. Four hours in, the iron stopped glowing. Five hours in, the grass had gone green to the tree line. Six hours in, the girl's voice failed completely and Ilsa—who had sworn never to speak Vaelish aloud, whose throat had been locked around her mother's tongue for twenty-six years—knelt in the wet grass, picked up a stone, and read.

*"Mira Vey,"* she said, and her own surname, her mother's name, and the ground beneath her knees went warm as a hearthstone.

At some point the door changed. Not opened—there was nothing behind it, no hall, no kingdom, no army sleeping in wait. The black iron simply thinned, and thinned, and became a frame full of morning fog, and in the fog moved shapes: a market street, a harbor, a woman laughing while bees whistled around her head. Memory itself, unguarded, unruled, open to any wind.

The oath shattered with a sound like ice leaving a lake in spring.

When it was done, Bram sat down hard in the grass. The crow lay in her cupped hands, feathered husk, one empty socket and one eye clouded white—and then, as they watched, the white cleared, and the eye blinked, and blinked again, and what looked out of it was new. Young. Blank as fresh snow.

"What do I call you?" Bram whispered.

The crow opened its beak. What came out was not Vaelish. It wasn't anything yet. Just a sound, curious and unformed, the first word of a creature with no past at all.

Ilsa looked at the fog-filled doorway, and at the girl, and at the green field where ten thousand names had finally been spoken home.

"So," she said, and her voice shook, and she let it. "What does a country do when it doesn't need a queen?"

Bram looked up at her, exhausted and lit from within, the fledgling crow gripping her thumb like a lifeline.

"It gets told," she said. "Starting tomorrow. Starting with my aunt." She rose, swaying, and slung the dead map's blank leather over her shoulder. "Come on, Warden-Captain. We've got a valley to unwall—and Maren Joubert has never once let anyone arrive hungry."

They walked out of Vael together as the sun cleared the ridge, and behind them, in the doorway full of fog, something like a city exhaled.
