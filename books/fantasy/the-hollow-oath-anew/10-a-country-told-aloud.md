## 10. A Country Told Aloud

The road into Hollowmere smelled the same, and that was the strangest thing.

Bram had expected the valley to feel different now—thinner, or louder, or changed in some way she could point to. But it was just the valley. Wet stone and pine and the cold mineral breath of the ford. The same road she'd walked her whole life, except this time she came up it with a Warden deserter at her left shoulder and a Warden captain at her right, and a crow riding the air above them like a small dark thought.

The crow had one eye again.

That was how Bram knew what it had cost. In the unmade country, when the oath shattered and the door opened into memory instead of a kingdom, the crow had poured itself out—every stolen year, every fragment of Vael's last beast-speaker—and what was left was smaller. Not empty. But lighter. It flew differently now, a little crooked, like a word someone had learned late in life.

It had chosen to come back anyway.

"You're sure about this," Tam said. Not a question. He'd been saying it since Ashmarket, since Ilsa Vey had traded her captain's seal for a satchel of stories and started walking beside them like it was the most natural thing in the world. "Maren's house. Wardens' eyes on every lane."

"The wardens are done," Ilsa said quietly. She hadn't raised her voice once since the door. It made people lean in to hear her, which Bram suspected was a habit she'd have to break. "There's nothing left to guard. Hollowmere's purpose died the night the oath broke. Whatever's left of the Order will scatter by winter."

"And if they don't?"

"Then they'll find an old woman, three travelers, and a valley full of beasts who've stopped pretending not to listen." Ilsa almost smiled. Almost. Her face was still learning the shape of it. "I wouldn't advise it."

They crested the last hill as the sun went down behind the western ridge, and there it was: Maren's house, slate roof gone green with moss, the goat paddock, the woodpile stacked with the particular obsessiveness of a woman who expressed love through labor. Smoke from the chimney. Light in the window.

Bram stopped walking.

She hadn't let herself imagine this part. All through the erased country, all through the long walk home, she'd kept moving because stopping meant thinking about Maren—the look on her face when the Wardens came, the lie she'd told with her chin up, *I always knew about you, girl, go*, and then the door closing between them with Bram on the wrong side of it. Weeks. Months. Long enough for anything to have happened.

"She'll be angry," Bram said.

"Probably," said Tam.

"She has a right to be."

"She does," said Ilsa. "Anger is just grief that found somewhere to stand. Let her have it."

The crow croaked—a sound that was almost a laugh—and dropped out of the sky to land on Bram's shoulder, its claws finding the old familiar grip. Together, they walked down toward the light.

*

Maren opened the door before Bram knocked.

Later, Bram would learn that the goats had told her—told her days ago, actually, in their flat, complaining way, that three two-legs and a bird were coming up the valley road, and that one of them smelled like Bram. Maren had been setting a fourth place at the table ever since, and then a fifth, and had argued with herself nightly about whether crows ate at tables.

But in the moment, all Bram saw was her aunt standing in the doorway with flour on her hands and an expression that moved through fury and relief and fury again so fast it blurred.

"You," Maren said.

"Aunt Maren—"

"You absolute—" Maren's voice cracked. She grabbed Bram by the collar with both floury hands and pulled her into a hug so fierce it hurt, and Bram felt something in her chest that had been clenched since the ford finally, finally let go. "Weeks," Maren said into her hair. "Weeks, and no word, and I tell them you ran off alone, and I didn't know if I'd buried a lie or a niece."

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I came back."

"You'd better have." Maren pulled back, held her at arm's length, and looked her over with the ruthless inventory of a woman who had raised her: thinner, scarred at the temple, eyes older. She catalogued it all and filed it away to grieve later. Then she looked past Bram at the others. "You're the deserter. You're the one who burned the mill records." A pause. "And you're Ilsa Vey, who put a knife to my niece's throat on the high road."

"That was a negotiation," Ilsa said.

"It was a threat."

"It was both. I'm told I'm carrying stories now instead of a seal. I'm told I'm learning." Ilsa's voice was even, but her hands weren't quite steady, and Bram understood: this was Ilsa asking. The way she knew how. Formally. Like surrendering a weapon.

Maren looked at her a long moment. Then she stepped back from the doorway and held it open.

"There's stew," she said. "Wash your hands. All of you. And the bird eats outside or it eats off my plate, I haven't decided."

The crow hopped onto the table and ate off her plate.

*

The ward began to die gently, the way old things do.

Bram noticed it first in the mornings. For her whole life, Hollowmere's silence had pressed against her gift like a hand over her mouth—not pain, exactly, but pressure, a constant low hum of *no*. Now the hum was fading. The valley had been built to hold a secret, and the secret was out; the ward was a lock with nothing left to protect, rusting open.

The animals noticed too.

On the fourth morning, Bram went out to milk the goat and the goat looked at her—really looked, head tilted, amber eyes level—and said, in the way of goats, which is less words than weather: *You're back. The valley tastes different. Tell me why.*

So Bram sat down in the straw and told her.

About Vael. About the door. About a country unmade and a hundred years of silence and a field of unburied names. The goat chewed and listened and asked blunt questions, and when Bram finished, the goat said, *Good. Silence never agreed with me either,* and presented her flank to be milked, conversation concluded.

Bram laughed until she cried, right there in the straw, and didn't try to explain it to anyone.

By the second week, she wasn't hiding anymore. She spoke with Maren's hens about the fox (the hens were dramatic; the fox was innocent). She negotiated with the mule about fence lines. When the neighbor's dog came trotting over, tail high, and asked her the question every animal in the valley seemed to be asking—*is it true, are you allowed now*—she scratched his ears and said yes, and the news went through Hollowmere faster than any Warden's writ ever had.

Some neighbors were frightened. Old Doss wouldn't meet her eye at the well, and the miller's wife crossed herself when Bram murmured thanks to her carthorse. But others came. Tentatively, at first, leading lame ewes and sour-tempered plow horses, asking, in the embarrassed tone of people requesting a miracle they don't believe in: *Could you ask it what hurts?*

Bram could. Bram did.

And in the evenings, when the work was done, they came for the other thing too.

*

It started with the children, the way true things usually do.

Tilde Fenner, eight years old and fearless, planted herself in front of Bram at the well and demanded to know if it was true she'd walked into a country that didn't exist. Bram said it existed; it just hadn't been allowed to. Tilde said that was stupid. Bram said yes, it was, and sat down on the well's edge, and told her about the ghost orchards where the trees still bloomed with fruit no one could pick, and the road that hummed a lullaby no living person remembered writing.

By the third evening there were six children. By the seventh, their parents stood at the edges of Maren's yard, arms crossed, pretending they'd only stopped by. By the tenth, Bram had to move the telling to the barn, and Maren set out benches, grumbling about straw in everything, arranging the lanterns with the care of a woman staging a theater.

Tam sat by the door those nights, quiet, sharpening a knife he no longer needed for anything. Once, when Bram told the part about the village whose records he'd helped burn—the part she'd made him promise she could tell, all of it, his name included—he stood up and said it himself. His voice shook. The barn was silent. Afterward, old Doss, who still wouldn't take Bram's eye, nodded once at Tam across the room, and that nod was the beginning of something neither of them named.

Ilsa never sat on the benches. She stood at the back like a soldier at a funeral, and she corrected nothing, and slowly Bram noticed that Ilsa had begun to add things—small details, the color of a Vaeli banner, the taste of a festival bread, the way her grandmother had cursed in three dialects. The stories in her satchel were finding their way out of her mouth, one evening at a time, and each one she gave away seemed to make her stand a little straighter, as if she'd been carrying them as weight and was learning to carry them as wings.

One night, after the children had gone and the lanterns were guttering, Ilsa lingered.

"They'll want it written down eventually," she said. "What happened. What Vael was. Someone should start."

"I'm not a scholar."

"No." Ilsa looked at her, and for a moment Bram saw the woman who had hunted a map across half the world, and beneath that, the girl from a country that no longer appeared on any map at all. "You're something rarer. You're a teller. Scholars keep the past in boxes. Tellers let it breathe." She paused. "Start with the names. The ones in the field. Say them first, always. Everything else is just the story of why we stopped saying them."

Bram wrote the first name that night, at Maren's kitchen table, in a blank book Maren produced from the chest under the stairs without being asked. *Sef,* she wrote. Cartographer. Who carried a country in his blood and gave it to a stranger.

The crow watched from the windowsill with its single eye, and when she finished the page, it dipped its head—an old gesture, formal, Vaeli—and Bram understood that somewhere in whatever remained of it, the last speaker of Vael approved.

*

Winter came early that year, and the valley wore it well.

The ward was nearly gone now. Travelers passed through Hollowmere without feeling the strange pressure at the ridgeline, the sense of being watched and turned aside, and some of them stayed. A tinker. Two sisters fleeing a debt. A young man who claimed to be a memory-smuggler out of Ashmarket and turned out to be simply lonely. Maren fed all of them, complained about all of them, and quietly added chairs.

Bram's gift was no longer a death sentence. It was a chore, mostly—there were a lot of animals in Hollowmere, and they all had opinions—but it was also, more and more, simply the way she moved through the world. Unhidden. Seen.

Seen, she discovered, was not the same as known. Being known took longer. It took Maren learning to say the word *gift* without flinching. It took Tam teaching the neighbor boys to whistle bird-calls, badly, while the birds critiqued. It took Ilsa laughing—one real, startled laugh, at the goat's opinion of Warden discipline—and looking as surprised as everyone else.

It took evenings, and stories, and time.

On the first hard frost, Bram climbed the ridge above the house with the crow on her shoulder, and looked down at the valley where she'd spent her whole life holding her breath. Lights in the windows. Smoke from chimneys. Somewhere below, faintly, Tilde Fenner's voice rising in the barn, already retelling tonight's chapter to anyone who'd listen, getting half of it wrong, which was fine—that was how stories stayed alive, they had to be mishandled, passed around, worn soft at the edges like good coins.

"We did it," Bram said to the crow. "We got it out."

The crow preened, insufferably modest.

Below, the barn door opened, spilling gold light across the snow, and Maren's voice carried up the hill, sharp and warm at once: *"Bram! If you're going to talk to birds all night, do it where the stew can reach you!"*

Bram smiled, and turned back down toward the light, toward the house that had never been able to keep her secret and had finally, at last, become the kind of home that didn't need one—

—and stopped.

At the treeline, at the very edge of the lantern-light, the snow lay unbroken.

Except for one line of prints. Small. Barefoot. Coming out of the forest, walking straight toward the barn, and ending—simply ending—ten paces from the door, as if whoever made them had been there and then, all at once, had never been anywhere at all.

The crow went very still on her shoulder.

From the treeline, faint and clear, in a language no one in Hollowmere had spoken aloud in a hundred years, something called her name.
