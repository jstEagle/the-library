## 1. The Keeper's Round

The valley woke the way it always did: slowly, and without magic.

Freya Hartwell had lived her whole life inside that fact. Other lands—so the traders said, when traders still came, before the road south silted over with bramble—hummed with things that bent and burned and whispered. Here there was only weather, stone, wheat, and the long patient work of hands. She had never seen a spell cast. She had never wanted to, particularly. Wanting was for people whose lives contained room for it.

She rose before dawn because her mentor had risen before dawn, and because the gate did not care whether she was tired.

The cottage was small enough to heat with one armful of pine. She lit the stove, set the kettle on, and dressed in the dark by feel—the grey wool coat with its brass keeper's clasps, worn smooth at the cuffs where she gripped them each morning like a woman steadying herself on a rail. On the wall above the hook hung the round-bell of her office, a hand-span of dented bronze that had not rung in warning in sixty years. She touched it as she passed, two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't fully trust.

Outside, the frost had come down hard in the night. The whole valley lay silvered and silent under it, every fence post furred white, the terraced fields below her door stepped down into mist like the pages of some enormous book half-sunk in water. Far off, the mountains held their line against the sky, black on grey. And between her and the mountains, at the head of the valley where the land pinched itself narrow, stood the Hollow Gate.

It was not, despite its name, hollow. That had been the first thing Maren had taught her, when Freya was seven years old and small enough to stand beneath the arch without stooping. *Hollow is what they called the country behind it,* the old woman had said, tapping the stone with her stick. *Not the door itself. The door is very full.* Freya had asked full of what, and Maren had looked at her for a long moment with those river-grey eyes, and had said, *That's tomorrow's lesson,* and then there had never been a tomorrow that answered it. There never was, with Maren. Forty years of tomorrows, and the old keeper had carried every answer out of the world in her own skull last winter, quietly, in her sleep, leaving Freya nothing but the questions and the keys.

The keys were iron, seven of them, and they hung on her belt now as she climbed the switchback path. Her breath came out in white flags. The path was hers alone; no other feet wore it. Once there had been nine keepers, then four, then two, and now one, and the order's decline had been so gradual and so unremarked that it felt less like a death and more like a tide going out—each year a little further, until one morning you looked up and the sea was simply gone.

* * *

The gate stood in a cleft of the mountain like a tooth in a jaw: an archway of grey-green stone thirty feet high, set into a wall that ran from cliff to cliff as if the mountain itself had grown a mouth and then thought better of it. Behind the arch, instead of a road or a country, was—nothing. Not darkness. Not fog. A kind of smooth absence, like the surface of deep water seen from underneath, faintly silvered, giving back nothing. You could put your hand toward it and feel a pressure, gentle as a held breath. You could lean your whole weight against it and it would hold you the way the earth holds a buried seed: kindly, and absolutely.

The seals were carved across the face of the arch in three tiers, and this morning, as every morning, Freya began at the bottom left and worked her way up.

The first tier was script. Long lines of it, dense as stitching, running around both pillars and along the lintel—a language of hooked letters and doubled strokes that no scholar in the valley could read, though scholars had tried. There had been a man once, a linguist from the lowlands with ink-stained fingers and a burning certainty that all writing was kin to all other writing. He had spent a summer copying the gate into his notebooks and gone home thinner and quieter than he'd arrived. At the end he'd told Maren, in a voice like a man describing a dream he was embarrassed by, that the letters were *native*. That they hadn't been borrowed from anywhere. That they belonged to the stone the way a voice belongs to a throat. Then he'd thanked her stiffly and never written again.

Freya ran her gloved thumb along the first line now, the way Maren had taught her—not reading, but listening with her skin. The carving was warm. It was always warm, even in frost, even in snow, and this warmth was the first check of the round. Cold script meant a weakening seal. Warm meant the oath held. She moved down the pillar, thumb trailing, and the stone gave back its faint stubborn heat like a body under a blanket, and she nodded to herself, and went on.

The second tier was the sigils: seven of them, one above another on the keystone, each the size of a dinner plate, each bound to one of her iron keys. She took out the first key—it fit a slot beside the lowest sigil, a slot that seemed shaped for it the way a lock is shaped for its key, except that turning it accomplished nothing visible—and she pressed the key's bow flat to the sigil and waited. This was the part outsiders would have found maddest, if any outsider had ever stood here: the ritual was to do nothing, and to notice everything. If the sigil warmed past blood-heat, or shivered, or—if it ever happened—went dark, then something had changed on the other side of the water-smooth absence, and she would need to know it before she knew anything else.

Sigil one: cool. Sigil two: cool. She worked upward through the morning cold, her breath smoking, her fingers aching, and all seven kept their temper like well-trained dogs. By the fourth she had stopped thinking about them at all, which was itself part of the training. *A keeper who watches too hard sees things that aren't moving,* Maren used to say. *Watch the way you watch bread rise. With your whole day, not your eyes.*

On the seventh sigil her hand paused, as it did every morning, because the seventh sigil was different. The others were abstract—knots and wheels and interlocking rings—but the seventh was a figure: a standing person, arms open, palms out, rendered in the same hooked native script flowing through and around the body as though the person were made of sentences. Freya had asked Maren, once, age eleven, who the person was.

*A keeper,* Maren had said.

*Which keeper?*

Maren had been quiet for so long that Freya had turned to look at her, and found the old woman staring at the sigil with an expression Freya had no name for yet—she had a name for it now, forty years later. It was grief wearing the mask of patience.

*The next one,* Maren had said finally. And had walked down the path, and had not spoken for the rest of the day.

Freya looked at the open-palmed figure now, as she did every morning, and felt the small familiar vertigo of looking at her own future carved in a dead alphabet. Then she let it go, the way she let go of everything, and climbed the ladder to the third tier.

The third tier was a single inscription, centered above the arch, larger than the rest, and this was the oath. She knew its sounds. She had known its sounds since before she could read the valley tongue, because Maren had made her recite it phonetically, syllable by syllable, until the shapes of the words lived in her mouth like pebbles from a riverbed—smooth, meaningless, hers. Every keeper for generations had learned it the same way. Sound without sense. An inheritance of noise.

She planted her boots on the lintel ledge, faced the sealed absence, and spoke.

*"Vessa-oran, keth-immaru soll. Vessa-oran, del-athuin korr."*

Her voice came out rough with cold and cracked on the second phrase, as it always did, and she corrected herself, and said it again, cleaner. Twice through, morning and evening. The words fell into the silvered surface and did not echo. They never echoed. It was like speaking into a room that absorbed sound the way sand absorbs rain.

*Vessa-oran.* She had built a whole private mythology around that first word across the lonely decades, in the way that solitary people build cathedrals out of matchsticks. Perhaps it meant *hold fast*. Perhaps it was a name—someone's name, some founder's name, sworn into the stone. *Keth-immaru* might be *shut the door*, or *keep the door*, or *the door keeps us*. *Del-athuin korr* had a falling, final cadence; she'd decided long ago it was the *amen*, the seal upon the seal, whatever the seal was for.

She didn't know. No one knew. That was the shape of her life, and she had made her peace with its shape the way you make peace with a limp: completely, and never once forgetting it.

* * *

Midmorning she made her rounds of the valley proper, and this part of the duty had no ritual attached, which was how she knew it mattered more. Rituals, Maren said, were for things you couldn't fix. Everything else just needed doing.

She walked the boundary stones first—twelve of them, spaced along the ridgelines, each a waist-high pillar marked with a single character of the same hooked native script. She cleared snow from their bases and laid her palm against each in turn. All quiet. Then down through the terraces, where the winter wheat lay gold-green under its frost blanket, checking the sluice gates on the high channel, freeing a jam of ice at the third one with a boot and a muttered word. Then the village square, where she traded her surplus butter for salt and heard the news, such as it was: the Aldermans' daughter betrothed to a miller's son from the east fork; the roof of the meetinghouse sagging and a working bee called for the thaw; Wren the stonemason complaining cheerfully that everyone wanted hearthstones repaired before midwinter and nobody wanted to pay in anything but gratitude.

"Keeper," they called her, and touched their caps, and were kind to her in the diffuse, distant way people are kind to a function. She was the valley's hinge and its hedge, and they loved her the way they loved the boundary stones: sincerely, and without ever once wondering what it was like to be one.

She stayed for the midday meal at the common table because Maren had insisted keepers must never become strangers to the people they served, and because otherwise she would eat alone, and she had eaten alone for fourteen months and knew precisely what that tasted like. But conversation slid off her these days. They talked of crops and marriages and the price of wool, and when talk turned—as it sometimes did—to the old days, the elders' faces developed a curious blankness, a polite fog rolling in, and someone would say *the keepers mind the gate, that's all anyone needs to know*, and the subject would die with remarkable efficiency, like a fire kicked apart.

Fourteen months since Maren died, and Freya had opened the mentor's cedar chest exactly twice. Both times she'd taken out the ledger of rounds, copied the day's entries forward in her own hand, and put it back. The chest held more than ledgers. She knew it held more. There was a bundle of letters at the bottom, tied with cord, sealed with wax, addressed in Maren's cramped hand: *For the keeper who follows me, when the gate asks its question.*

She had read that superscription a hundred times. *When the gate asks its question.* Gates didn't ask questions. Gates were stone and silence and a surface like held breath. In fourteen months the gate had asked her nothing at all, and so the letters had stayed tied, and stayed sealed, and stayed at the bottom of the chest under the winter cloaks, and Freya had told herself that this was patience, and knew in her bones it was fear.

* * *

Evening came early in the short days, and she climbed the switchback path a second time with a lantern swinging from her staff, and performed the round again: thumb along the warm script, key to each of the seven sigils, boots on the lintel ledge, the oath twice through into the listening nothing.

*"Vessa-oran, keth-immaru soll. Vessa-oran, del-athuin korr."*

And then, because the light was failing and no one in the world could see her, she did the thing she always did at the end of the evening round, the thing that was not in any ledger and had never been taught. She leaned close to the silvered surface, close enough that its strange pressure pushed gently against her cheekbones like the memory of a mother's hands, and she spoke to it in plain valley speech.

"Another day," she said. "All quiet. Nothing's changed."

The surface gave back nothing, as it had given back nothing for as long as there had been keepers, as far back as the oldest ledger went, and further, into the region where the ledgers became legend and the legend became stone.

"I don't know what I'm keeping," she told it, in the voice she used for confessions. "I don't know what you are. I say the words and I don't know what they mean and neither does anyone alive. Somewhere in that chest downstairs there's a letter that probably explains everything, and I can't make myself break the seal, because—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Breathed out a long flag of white.

Because as long as she didn't read it, Maren wasn't entirely dead. As long as the answers stayed locked away, the questions stayed alive, and the questions were all she had left of her. It was the loneliest arithmetic she knew, and she did it every night, and it never once came out different.

She straightened, touched the seventh sigil—the open-handed keeper, the next one, herself—and turned for home.

That was when she saw it. Or rather, that was when she saw that it was gone: the smoke.

From the gate's ledge you could see every chimney in the valley, a dozen threads of woodsmoke rising straight up into the windless dusk, and she counted them out of habit, the way she counted sigils. Ten, eleven—her own chimney off to the right, unlit, she hadn't banked the stove before climbing—and then the twelfth thread, the one that should rise from the shepherd's hut at the valley's eastern mouth, was missing. And in its place, down on the old drover's road where the mist pooled thickest, there was a light. Small, orange, moving. Not a lantern's steady glow. Something flickering, stumbling, coming toward her valley out of the dark—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable on the frozen air, the sound of footsteps, small and uneven, and a child's voice calling out words she did not know.

Except that some treacherous chamber of her ear, tuned by forty years of sound-without-sense, knew that she almost did.
