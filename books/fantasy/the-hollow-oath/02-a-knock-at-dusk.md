## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The rain had been coming down since noon, a steady grey insistence that turned the valley into something soft and half-dissolved, as though the whole world were being slowly rinsed of its edges. Freya Hartwell stood at her kitchen window with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands, watching the water sheet off the cottage roof and pool in the ruts of the path, and she thought, not for the first time, that this was what her life would look like from the outside: a woman alone in a stone house at the end of a valley, waiting for weather to pass.

She had learned, over eleven years of keeping, that the trick was not to mind it. The trick was to make the minding unnecessary. So she had filled her days until they brimmed: the morning round of the gate's seals before the mist burned off, when the stones held the night's chill and answered her touch most honestly; the tending of the herb rows behind the cottage; the mending, the preserving, the endless small repairs that an old house demanded like a petulant child. In the evenings she read—her mentor Maera's books, mostly, their margins crowded with a handwriting so cramped and impatient it seemed the words had been chased onto the page rather than set there—and she drank her tea, and she slept without dreams, or nearly.

It was a good life. She said that to herself sometimes, in Maera's voice, because Maera had said it first: *You have the best post in the order, girl, and the last one, which is better.* Eleven years, and she almost believed it on the good days. On the bad days she counted the silence the way other people counted coins, and found herself poor.

The knock came at dusk, just as the rain thickened from grey to black and the light failed all at once, the way it did in autumn, without apology.

Three knocks. Small ones. A fist too little to make much argument against oak.

Freya set down her cup and stood very still, listening. Visitors did not come to the keeper's cottage. That was not a rule anyone had written down; it was simply a fact of the valley's shape, of its single road winding up from the lowlands and the way travelers always seemed to find reasons to hurry through rather than linger. The elders in Millbrook traded with the outside world twice a year and spoke of it the way one speaks of a distant relative—fondly, formally, without any wish for proximity. And no one, ever, came knocking at the Hollow Gate's cottage after dark.

Three knocks again, weaker now, and then a sound that undid her faster than any alarm could have: a cough, thin and wet, the cough of someone small who has been breathing cold rain for hours.

She took down the lantern, checked the shutter bar out of habit, and opened the door on a wall of rain and, standing in it, a boy.

He could not have been more than eight. He was soaked past any meaning of the word—his hair plastered flat to his skull, his clothes clinging to him like a second skin gone wrong, his bare feet blue-white and cut, standing in puddles that spread outward from him as if he had brought the storm up the path himself. His lips were the color of slate. But it was his eyes that stopped Freya's hand where it hovered, halfway to hauling him inside: they were wide open and utterly calm, fixed on her face with the terrible steadiness of a child who has already seen whatever the world can do to him and is merely waiting to learn whether tonight will be worse.

"Inside," she said. "Now, before you drown standing up."

He didn't move. He looked at her mouth when she spoke, tracking the words, and then shook his head once—a small, deliberate motion, not refusal exactly, but assessment. *I hear you. I do not understand you.*

"You don't understand me," Freya said, and felt the strangeness of saying it aloud. Everyone in the valley spoke the same tongue; the lowland traders' accents were the only variation she'd heard in years. "That's all right. Come in anyway."

This time she reached out and drew him in by the shoulder, and he let himself be drawn, stiff and shivering, leaving a trail of water across her clean floor that she found she did not care about at all.

* * *

She worked the way Maera had taught her: fire first, then warmth, then questions. She stripped off his ruined clothes—he flinched at that, guarded some small bundle he'd carried against his chest with both hands, and she let him keep it, wrapped in dry wool—and put him in front of the hearth wrapped in her own spare blanket, rubbing heat back into feet that had gone the texture of old candle wax. She cut the blisters on his heels and salved them. She made broth from bones and barley and watched, with a keeper's attention, as he drank it in slow, disciplined swallows, as though he knew better than to trust his stomach with abundance.

Only then, with the boy pinkening by degrees and the fire crackling its ordinary domestic music, did she sit back on her heels and really look at him.

He was not valley-born. That much she knew the way she knew weather. Valley children had a certain look—the same broad cheekbones, the same dark hair, generations folded into one another until the families blurred. This boy's face belonged nowhere she recognized. His hair, drying now, curled in tight black ringlets; his skin was a deep warm brown; and there was something in the set of his features, some architecture of jaw and brow, that made her think of the carvings.

Of the gate's carvings.

The thought arrived uninvited and sat down in her chest like a guest who intended to stay. She pushed it away. She had spent eleven years learning not to see patterns in the gate beyond the ones Maera had shown her—the seal-points, the maintenance lines, the old script worn smooth along the lintel. Patterns were how keepers went strange. Maera had been clear about that too.

But the boy chose that moment to speak, and the pattern walked into the room wearing a child's voice.

He said something soft, two syllables, addressed to the bundle in his arms. Comforting it, maybe. Or comforting himself. And the sound of it went through Freya like a bell struck against her spine.

Not because she understood it. She understood nothing. Because she *recognized* it—the way you recognize, in a crowd of strangers, the cadence of your childhood street. The vowels of it had a shape she knew. The way the second word fell away at the end, unfinished, trailing, matched a line of inscription above the gate's third seal that she had traced with her finger a thousand times on her morning rounds, tracing it without ever once hearing it aloud, because the inscriptions had never been meant for voices. They were meant for stone.

And here was a boy of eight, wrapped in her blanket, dripping her broth, speaking stone.

"What did you say?" she asked, and immediately felt the foolishness of it. She tried again, slower, pointing at herself. "Freya. I'm Freya."

His eyes flicked to her finger, to her face. After a moment he touched his own chest. "Tam," he said. The word had a weight to it, a finality, like a stone set on a cairn.

"Tam." She nodded. Then, because the question had been building since the door opened, she pointed out the window, toward the dark and the rain and the direction of the low road. "*Where?*" She widened her hands, helpless. "Where did you come from?"

Tam looked at the window for a long time. Something moved behind his face—not confusion, exactly. It was more like the expression of someone being asked to point at the far side of the moon.

Then he began to talk.

He talked fast, low, urgent, his hands coming up to help him, sketching shapes in the air: something tall, something falling, light—he cupped his hands and made them pour, made them blaze, made them break apart—and all of it in that language that should have been noise to her and was instead a half-remembered song, every phrase landing somewhere under her ribs and echoing against a carving she couldn't quite bring to mind. She caught herself leaning forward. She caught herself mouthing the shapes of his sounds. Once—she would have sworn it later, and doubt it, and swear it again—her own lips formed the end of one of his sentences a heartbeat before he did, as though the language were running ahead of him through her like water finding an old channel.

She made herself sit back. She made herself breathe.

"Slowly," she said, uselessly, in her own tongue. "Slowly. I'm trying."

Tam stopped. He studied her face with that unnerving adult patience, and then he did something that made the fire seem suddenly less warm: he reached out and touched the blanket at his knee, and then the hearthstone, and then pointed at her, and said one word.

One word, two syllables, rising at the end like a question.

And Freya knew—knew with the cold certainty of the keeper's round, of the seals answering her hand at dawn—exactly which inscription that word belonged to. It was carved into the gate's central stone, the oldest line, the line even Maera had refused to gloss. She had rubbed charcoal over it as an apprentice and copied its shapes into her workbook, letter by letter, twenty years ago. She had never known its sound.

Now she did. It was the sound this boy had just made, asking *who are you*, or perhaps *what are you*, or perhaps something older and larger that no living mouth had spoken in a hundred years until tonight, in her kitchen, in the rain.

The seal-stone above the third arch, forty paces away across the dark yard, hummed.

Freya heard it through the walls—felt it, rather, a vibration in her back teeth, in the bones of her feet, the way she always felt the gate when it was troubled. It lasted three heartbeats. Then it stopped, and the night was only night again, and the rain came down, and Tam watched her with his calm dark eyes, waiting, as though he had asked his question and fully expected the world to answer.

Freya got up, because her legs wanted to do something, anything, and fed the fire logs that did not need feeding.

A lost boy. That was the sane explanation. A refugee from the lowlands, from farther than the lowlands, from some war or famine the trade roads hadn't yet carried word of, washed up the valley road in a storm. Children turned up displaced after hard winters; the elders would know what to do; the Compact's survey offices in the low country kept records, kept languages, kept answers. Tomorrow she would walk him down to Millbrook and hand the problem to people whose job was problems.

Except that lost children did not speak the language written on a gate that had stood sealed for a hundred years. Lost children did not make seals hum. Lost children did not look at a stranger's face and ask, in the voice of the gate's oldest stone, *what are you.*

She turned back. Tam had not moved from his place by the fire. The bundle he'd carried was open now on his knees, and in the lamplight she saw what it held: a scrap of cloth, embroidered densely, and beneath the embroidery—a strip of pale bark, peeled and dried, covered edge to edge in fine, careful writing.

In the same letters as the gate.

He saw her looking. He lifted the strip of bark and held it out to her, offering it with both hands, the way one offers something that matters, and said—in her language, his first words of it, accented but unmistakable, a sentence he must have carried a long way and saved:

"It is my name. All of it. Please—I need someone who reads."

Outside, the rain went on falling, and beneath the rain, faint and patient, the Hollow Gate hummed like something waking.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat across the hearth from the sleeping boy—sleep had taken him mid-sentence finally, exhaustion winning its long siege—and turned the strip of bark in the lamplight, matching letter to letter against the workbook pages of her apprenticeship. The correspondence was exact. Not similar. Exact. Every character on the bark had its twin on the gate, and more: the bark's script flowed with a fluency the gate's carvings lacked, the way a native hand outstrips a monument's mason. Whoever had written this had not been copying the gate's language. They had been thinking in it.

And the boy who owned it lay breathing by her fire, eight years old, carrying a dead country's name against his chest through a storm.

Freya looked at the dark window, where the gate stood invisible in the yard, humming its low note to itself, and felt the ground of her eleven careful years tilt gently beneath her, like a floor becoming a deck.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow we go to Millbrook. Tomorrow there are elders, and records, and sense.

But some part of her, the part that had traced those inscriptions with one finger for twenty years without knowing why, already understood that tomorrow the gate would still be humming—and that it was humming, softly, steadily, unmistakably, for the sleeping child wrapped in her blanket.

As if it knew his name.
