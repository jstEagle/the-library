## 3. The Word That Fits the Stone

Freya did not sleep.

She told herself it was the storm still working itself out over the ridge, thunder grumbling eastward like an old man grumbling about his knees, but that was a lie she let stand because the truth was less comfortable. The truth was sitting at her kitchen table with a blanket around his shoulders, eating bread and honey with the solemn concentration of a child who had learned not to trust the supply of either, and speaking — when he spoke at all, which was often, in a low running murmur like water under ice — in a language that should not exist inside her valley.

She had put him to bed twice. Both times he had climbed back down from the loft without a word of protest, as though he understood she needed him where she could see him, and settled at the table again, and gone on murmuring. So eventually she gave up on the pretense of bedtime for both of them and lit her second lamp, and got out the thing she had been avoiding getting out since dusk.

The rubbing.

Every keeper made them. It was the first lesson old Maren had taught her, before arithmetic, before the herbs, before anything: *the gate speaks in stone, child, and stone is patient, but we are not. Copy it while you can.* You pressed good linen paper against the carved band that ringed the Hollow Gate's arch, and you worked a charcoal stick sideways across it until the letters rose up pale against the black, ghost-writing, the negative of a voice. Freya had made her first rubbing at seven years old, standing on Maren's crossed hands, and she had made hundreds since. She knew those curves the way she knew the lines of her own palm. Better, maybe. Her palm had never asked anything of her.

The boy's language kept asking.

She spread three rubbings across the table — the oldest one Maren's own, browned and soft-cornered; one of hers from ten years back; one fresh, taken only last spring — and weighted their corners with the salt cellar, the candle-snuffer, a river stone worn smooth as a knuckle. Then she sat across from Tam with a sheet of scrap paper and a pencil stub, and said, in the tone she used for nervous livestock and frightened children alike, which was to say gently and without looking directly at him:

"I'm going to ask you to say some things. And I'd like you to say them slowly."

He looked up. Honey at the corner of his mouth. His eyes were dark and very steady for eight years old, or whatever age he was — she was no longer certain *eight* was anything but her own guess, a number she'd assigned him the way you assign a weight to something you have to carry.

"You understand me," she said. "Don't you. Even though you don't speak my words."

He nodded once. Then, after a moment, he added a small shrug, which she chose to read as: *mostly.*

"Good. That's — all right. That's something." She turned one of the rubbings so it faced him and set her pencil against the first character. "This mark here. Can you say what this says? Just the sound. Don't try to tell me what it means yet. Just the sound."

Tam leaned over the paper. He studied the character with the same seriousness he'd given the bread, and then he opened his mouth and made a sound.

The lamp flames all bent sideways at once.

Not guttered — there was no wind, the cottage was shut tight against the storm's last edges — they *bent*, both of them, toward the door, toward the gate, the way grass bends toward a thing walking through it, and then straightened. Freya sat very still. The sound the boy had made was small, a rounded syllable, nothing more than a pebble dropped in a well. But the well had answered. Somewhere down the lane, through two walls and a shuttered window, she felt — not heard, *felt*, in the bones of her forearms, in the place behind her sternum where twenty years of keeper's rounds had taught her to feel the gate the way a sailor feels weather — a faint, slow pulse. Like a heart remembering it had one.

"Again," she whispered, and hated how her voice shook.

He said it again. The lamps leaned. The pulse came, a little stronger, and beneath it, faint as a word spoken in another room, something that might have been a hum, or might have been a held breath.

Freya looked down at her scrap paper, on which she had written nothing at all, and understood that her plan — hear the sounds, compare them, be methodical, be a keeper — had just run up against the fact that the sounds were not sounds. They were keys. And she had spent her whole life living in a house full of locks without ever once being told what the doors were for.

* * *

She worked until the candles burned down to their collars.

It went like this: she would point to a character on the rubbing, and Tam would give her its sound, and she would write the sound out in her own letters, phonetically, clumsily, the way Maren had taught her to transcribe birdsong for the almanac. Then she would find the same character elsewhere — it recurred, she realized, the way certain words recur in any speech, the way *and* recurs, the way *of* recurs — and match the contexts, and build, letter by letter, a bridge between the boy's mouth and the stone.

By midnight she had forty characters mapped. By the second candle she had eighty. And by the time the sky outside the east window went from black to the grey of wet slate, she had stopped mapping and started reading, haltingly, the way a child reads, sounding out each assembled fragment aloud to herself in a whisper, and every fragment she read made the whisper smaller.

The band around the arch was not a warning. She had always assumed it was a warning — Maren had assumed it, and Maren's teacher before her, going back through the ledger of keepers into the fog. *Here ends the world's permission,* the keepers said the stones meant. *Turn back. Be content.* Generations of keepers had walked their rounds beneath those curves and read them as a fence reads: a boundary, a prohibition, a *no*.

But the boy's tongue fit the stone the way a key fits a lock, and locks do not say *no*. Locks say *here is how*.

The first line, as far as she could assemble it, ran something like: *We who were set to watch the door of Vessia—*

Vessia.

She had read that name a hundred times in the carvings and never known it was a name. She had taken it for a formula, a ritual term, one of the nonsense-syllables keepers memorized without understanding because understanding was not part of the duty. Duty, in her order, had always meant doing the thing correctly and asking nothing about why. Maren had been gentle about it, but firm: *Some doors are best kept by people who don't know what's behind them, Freya-girl. Curiosity is how keepers become casualties.* And Freya, orphaned at nine, raised by the order and the valley in equal measure, had built her whole self on that foundation: do the round, tend the seals, copy the stones, ask nothing.

Now a soaked eight-year-old was sitting at her table saying the stones' own words with bread-honey on his face, and the stones were answering him, and the word *Vessia* was not a formula. It was a country. She knew it was a country the way you know a face belongs to a person — the grammar around it was the grammar of places, of *we who dwell in*, of *from the mountains of*, of *the bells of*. A country with mountains and bells and people set to watch a door.

A country she had never heard of. Not in Maren's books, not in the peddler's maps, not in any of the almanacs and histories the valley traded for with the lowland towns. She cast her mind through every atlas she'd ever paged through and found the space where such a country would sit — north of the Compact states, west of the old imperial roads — and found the space occupied by nothing. Not blank, even. Blank would have been something. The maps simply showed other countries continuing, unbroken, over the ground where Vessia's mountains should have stood, the way water closes over a drowned thing.

The second line of the arch-band assembled itself under her pencil, and her hand slowed:

*—do swear the hollow oath: that the door shall open for none, that the name shall pass to none, that the border shall hold until—*

Until. The clause broke off there, or rather the carving did, worn away by centuries of weather, and the next legible stretch picked up mid-sentence generations later in a different, shallower hand — keeper's additions, vows renewed, the same phrases repeated like stitches closing a wound: *we renew, we renew, we do not ask, we renew.*

*That the name shall pass to none.*

She looked at the boy.

Tam had fallen asleep sitting up somewhere past the third candle, his cheek on his folded arms, his breath long and even, the blanket sliding off one shoulder. Asleep, he looked younger than eight. Asleep, he looked like any child in any village in the valley, except that when the wind gusted against the shutters he murmured in his sleep — and the murmuring was in the gate's own tongue, soft and looping, a dream talking to itself in a language that had, until tonight, existed in her valley only as stone.

*That the name shall pass to none.* And a child had arrived carrying the name in his mouth like a bird carrying a seed across a wall.

Freya sat with her pencil motionless and felt the dread arrive — not the sharp dread of danger, which she knew well and could act on, but the slow cold dread of comprehension, the kind that comes when a shape you have lived inside your whole life suddenly shows you its outline from the outside. All those rounds. All those rubbings. Twenty years of tending seals whose purpose she had recited every morning in words she did not understand, and the words had been right there under her charcoal, under her fingertips, readable all along by anyone who spoke — 

Anyone who spoke Vessian.

And the order had made sure, hadn't it, that no one spoke Vessian. Had made sure the tongue stayed dead in the valley, dead everywhere, if it had ever lived anywhere. *That the name shall pass to none.* Not a ward against magic. A ward against memory. Against a word. She thought of Maren teaching her the ritual phrases, the beautiful meaningless syllables, and felt something turn over in her chest that was half grief and half anger and entirely new.

The seals. She had to check the seals.

It was not yet dawn, hours early for the round, but her body was already moving, taking down the oilskin coat, the lantern, the iron rod with its ring of tuning-keys that every keeper carried and no keeper she had ever met had needed. At the door she paused and looked back at the sleeping boy, and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do all night: the gate had pulsed for him. Twice, from two syllables, across a quarter mile of lane. What would it do if he stood in front of it? What would it do if he stood in front of it and *read*?

She should leave him here. Lock the cottage, take the key, walk the round alone, come back, and think — think properly, in daylight, with tea, with the ledgers, with the calm that distance buys.

She looked at his dark head on his folded arms, at the rise and fall of his breathing, at the blanket finally slipping all the way off and her own hands moving almost of themselves to pull it back up over his shoulder before she left.

Then she took the boy with her anyway. Later she would examine why. In the moment it was simple: the gate had called to him all night, faint and patient, and she did not intend to leave anything the gate wanted alone in her house unsupervised. That was the reason she gave herself, and it was even mostly true.

* * *

The storm had washed the valley clean. Dawn came up thin and gold along the eastern ridgeline, and the birches on the lower slopes dripped light, and the lane to the gate was a ribbon of mud printed with her boots and, beside them, small bare feet she'd failed to find boots for. Tam walked close to her, not touching, the way a creature walks near a fire it has learned to trust. He had woken easily when she touched his shoulder, had looked at her lantern, at the coat, at the rod, and had simply gotten down from the chair, as if he'd been waiting to be invited to exactly this.

The Hollow Gate stood at the valley's throat where the two ridges drew together, and it was not, despite its name, hollow-looking at all. It was a wall dressed as a doorway: a great arch of grey stone fitted without mortar, filled to the crown with fitted blocks, and around the arch ran the carved band, and set into the blocking stones at five points — crown, springline, threshold, and the two haunches — were the seals. Each seal was a disc of paler stone, wide as a cartwheel, carved in concentric rings of the same characters, and each disc was keyed to one of the tuning-rods on her iron ring. Once a day, every day, for twenty years, Freya had touched rod to seal and felt the faint harmonic shiver that meant *holding*, and written *all well* in the ledger, and walked home.

"All well," she said now, out loud, to the boy, because saying it aloud steadied her. "Five seals. We check them in order. Crown first." She was aware she was narrating for a child who couldn't follow the words, and aware that she was doing it anyway, the way you talk to a horse on a bad day — for your own sake as much as its.

They climbed the keeper's steps to the top of the arch, where the maintenance ledge ran narrow behind the parapet, and Freya knelt at the crown seal and fitted the first rod into its socket. The old ritual: press, turn a quarter, listen. The seal hummed its low note. Holding. She exhaled.

"First one's true," she said. "See? Nothing to—"

Tam had gone rigid.

He was standing at the parapet, looking down at the carved band below the ledge, at the stretch of it nearest the crown, and his lips were moving. Reading. An eight-year-old, reading fluently, effortlessly, text that Freya — with her eighty mapped characters and a night's work — could only pick apart word by word like a woman wading a river. He read the way she breathed. Of course he did. It was his. Whatever it was, whatever the stone was written in, it was the air he'd been born breathing, and she was the foreigner here, on her own ledge, at her own gate, in her own valley.

"What does it say?" she asked quietly. "There. Where you're looking."

Tam's finger traced the worn groove of the broken clause — the *until* that trailed into weather. He looked at her, and she saw him decide something, saw the child weigh the adult's question and choose to answer it honestly, and that choice frightened her more than everything else combined, because honest children are the most accurate instruments in the world.

"It says *until the speaker comes*," he said — in her language, carefully, the words picked up and placed like eggs. "The rest is broken. Rain ate it." His finger moved further along the band, to a later section, the deep-cut renewal vows. "This part says the same thing many times. Like people arguing with the rain." A pause. "Like people hoping the rain isn't listening."

"The speaker," Freya said. Her mouth was dry. "It means — someone who speaks this. Your language."

Tam nodded. Then, with the terrible literalness of children, he added: "Me."

The word had barely left him — one syllable, his own name for himself, *me*, in the gate's tongue, the simplest word in any language, the first word, the word that costs nothing —

The crown seal flickered.

There was no better word for it. The pale disc did not glow, exactly; it was more that its color *stuttered*, the way a candle flame stutters when a door opens somewhere in the house, one heartbeat of a different light underneath, a light like the sky at the bottom of deep water — and the hum changed key. Freya had listened to that hum for twenty years. It had never changed key. The rod trembled in the socket, wanting to turn further, wanting, and she snatched her hand back and the rod rang against the stone and lay still, and the seal settled back into its old grey patience as though nothing had happened.

But she had seen it. And the gate had felt it. Down the whole length of the arch she felt the shiver pass, seal to seal, like a word passed down a row of seated people, crown to haunch to springline to threshold, and at the threshold — the lowest seal, the one set in the ground before the blocked doorway, the one no keeper was permitted to tune alone — the shiver stopped, and stayed.

Tam was looking at the crown seal with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone who has knocked on a door without meaning to and heard footsteps coming.

"I'm sorry," he said, in her language, and then, helplessly, in his own, and the threshold seal, a hundred feet below them, hummed one clear note like a struck glass.

Freya stood on the ledge in the gold morning light with her ledger flapping in the wind, and understood, with the full dreadful clarity of comprehension, three things at once.

That the gate had been waiting for this boy — had perhaps been waiting, patient as stone, for longer than her order had kept its records.

That her order's whole edifice — the rounds, the rubbings, the sacred ignorance, the beautiful meaningless syllables — had been built for exactly this contingency, and that whoever built it had known the speaker would come someday, and had armed her with nothing but a broom and a blindfold.

And that she had just written *all well* in her mind out of pure habit, and it was the last time in her life she would ever be able to write it.

Below them, at the threshold, the seal's hum faded — but slowly, reluctantly, the way a bell fades, and the silence it left behind was not the old silence. It was a listening one.

"Come away from the edge," Freya said, and reached for the boy's hand, and found that her own was shaking.
