## 4. The Order's Last Letter

The letter had been in the cottage for as long as Freya could remember, and she had never once been allowed to open it.

It sat in the third drawer of the writing desk, beneath a false bottom her mentor, Aldous Wren—no relation to the stonemason, though the valley joked otherwise—had built himself from blackthorn wood. It was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a cord that had gone stiff with age, and across its face, in Aldous's cramped leftward-slanting hand, were the words: *For the keeper who comes after me. Not before. After.*

She had found it when she was nineteen, three days after they buried him. She had held it in both hands, felt the weight of it, read the instruction twice—and then she had put it back, because the words said *after*, and she had not understood what *after* meant until now.

Now there was a boy asleep in her bed who spoke the language of the gate.

* * *

Freya did not sleep that night. She sat at the desk with a single lamp lit, the letter before her, and Tam's breathing soft and even in the next room—the small room that had been hers as an apprentice, that had stood empty for eleven years until last night, when a soaked and shivering child had appeared at her door speaking words that fit the stones of the Hollow Gate like water fits a channel.

She had checked on him four times already. Each time he was the same: curled on his side, one hand fisted in the blanket, his face slack with the particular exhaustion of someone who has walked much farther than any eight-year-old should. In his sleep he murmured sometimes—not words she knew, but sounds that made the lamp flame lean, very slightly, toward the door. Toward the gate. Toward the valley's edge, where the Hollow Gate stood in its frame of grey stone, sealed by an oath no living keeper fully understood.

She turned back to the letter. The cord resisted her fingers, stiff as root. She worked it loose with the patience she used on the gate's seals—slow pressure, no force, respect for the mechanism—and unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside were letters. Seven of them, tied in a second bundle, each addressed in different hands, each bearing seals she did not recognize except one: the sigil of her own order, the circle broken by a vertical line, pressed into wax so old it had gone the colour of dried blood. And beneath the bundle, folded separately, a single sheet in Aldous's own writing.

She started with his.

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or I have failed, and in my experience the two arrive together.*

*I have kept this correspondence for forty years because I was told to keep it and not to understand it, and I obeyed, and I want you to know that I am sorry for what obedience cost me, because it will cost you more. You will have noticed by now—I hope you noticed long before this—that the oath we recite every morning is nonsense. Beautiful nonsense, structured nonsense, but nonsense. "That which enters shall be turned aside. That which sleeps shall not be woken." We say it in Old Common, or something wearing Old Common's clothes, and we have never once asked why the words do not parse.*

*They do not parse because they are not Old Common. They are a translation of a translation of a translation, and somewhere back along that chain, someone wanted us to stop understanding what we were saying. Ask yourself, as I finally asked myself too late: who benefits from keepers who guard a door they cannot describe?*

*Read the others. Read them in order. And then go to the gate and look at it—not at the seals, look at the stone beneath the seals—and forgive me, because I did not have the courage to tell you while I lived, and the dead are cowards by definition.*

Freya set the page down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake. Outside, the wind moved through the valley's pines with the sound of a long exhalation, and somewhere far off, the gate hummed its nightly hum, the note it had held for as long as anyone could remember, low and constant, like a struck bell that never finished ringing.

She untied the second bundle and began, as instructed, in order.

* * *

The first letter was the oldest, its paper browned and brittle, written in a flowing antique script:

*To the Keeper of the Hollow Gate, greetings, and to all who come after, vigilance.*

*You will have been taught that your order was founded to protect the valley from magic. This is a kindness told to children and to new keepers, and you should continue telling it, because it is simpler than the truth and the truth has killed better people than either of us.*

*Your order was founded to protect a border. Not against magic—from magic, yes, but that is a side effect, not a purpose. The valley sits upon a line drawn in treaty two hundred years ago, a line between what the world agreed to remember and what it agreed to forget. On one side of the line: the kingdoms of the Compact, their maps, their histories, their names. On the other side: Vessia.*

*You will not find Vessia on any map. That is the point. That was the price. When the last war ended, the victors did not merely defeat Vessia—they unmade it, name by name, road by road, from every ledger and chronicle and memory they could reach. But a country is not only its records. A country is also its ground, and ground remembers. So the Compact bought itself a valley where forgetting would hold—a valley warded against magic, because magic is how countries refuse to die—and they hired our order to stand at the seam and keep the ward intact.*

*We are not heroes of the valley. We are gaolers of a grave. Guard the seal. Speak the oath. Ask nothing.*

Freya read it twice. Then a third time, moving her lips over the word *Vessia*, tasting it, finding—to her horror—that it fit. It fit the way Tam's words had fit the gate carvings. Somewhere in her, in the deep sediment of a lifetime's recitation, the name had been waiting, worn smooth by two hundred years of oaths spoken without comprehension.

She thought of the inscription above the gate's arch, the one every keeper learned to trace with a fingertip but never translate: *Vessia duran keth.* She had always assumed it was a blessing. A warding formula. Something protective.

It was a name. It had always been a name.

* * *

The second letter was newer, angrier, written in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places:

*Brother-keeper,*

*I write to you from the archive at Corvane, where I went seeking the founding charters of our order, and I write to you shaking. There is no charter. Do you understand me? There is no founding document, no first oath, no record of any keeper before two hundred years ago. Our order simply appears in the record, fully formed, funded by a consortium of three Compact crowns, with instructions and a salary.*

*We were not founded. We were employed.*

*And here is the thing I cannot put down, the thing that followed me on the whole road home: the payment never stopped. There is a ledger, still active, still current, in a counting-house in the Compact capital, listing disbursements to "the custodial trust of the Hollow Valley" paid quarterly for two centuries. Someone, today, in a warm office, signed a paper that pays for our bread. We are not an ancient holy order. We are a maintenance contract.*

*I looked for the names of those who signed the original treaty. They are redacted—not lost, redacted, deliberately, with a precision that frightens me more than any spell I have ever heard described. Whatever was done to Vessia was done carefully, by competent people, on purpose, and then the doing was hidden from the doers' own descendants.*

*Burn this if you must. I could not burn mine. I am going to keep reading. God forgive us both.*

There was no signature, only the broken-circle sigil stamped at the bottom, and beneath it, smaller, almost invisible: *Keeper Ede Marrow, 141st of the line.*

Freya counted backward through the list of keepers she had memorized as an apprentice, the roll call recited at every investiture. The 141st. Ede Marrow. A name attached to nothing else in the order's records—no deeds, no dates of service, just a name in a list, like a brick in a wall.

Like everyone in the list. Like her own name would be, someday, if she let it.

* * *

The middle letters blurred together, decades passing between them, and the story they told was always the same story wearing different clothes. One keeper wrote of finding a child at the gate—a girl, perhaps ten, half-starved, speaking a language no one knew—and of how the keeper's superior had arrived within a day, unasked, from outside the valley, and taken the child away, and how the keeper had been instructed afterward to report any such arrival immediately and to touch nothing. One wrote, in a hand gone shaky with age, of standing at the gate during a storm and hearing, distinctly, on the far side, bells. Bells rung in patterns, rung by hands, rung by someone. One wrote only a single line: *I looked through the keyhole of the world tonight and I saw a street, and there were people on it, and one of them waved at me, and I have not slept since.*

Freya read them all by lamplight while the night deepened around the cottage and the pine wind rose and fell. Twice she stopped to check on Tam, and the second time she stood in the doorway longer than she meant to, watching him sleep, thinking: *they took a child once before. They will come for him.*

Because that was the shape of it now, wasn't it? The letters laid it out plain as a ledger. Her order existed to maintain a forgetting. Anything that remembered—anything that crossed the border carrying memory, carrying language, carrying *names*—was a breach. Was a threat to the treaty. Was to be reported, removed, erased.

And a boy who spoke the dead tongue of a country that officially never existed had walked out of the rain to her door.

* * *

The last letter in the bundle was the newest, maybe thirty years old, written in a careful, formal hand:

*To the current keeper, from the Office of Boundary Affairs, Compact of Nations:*

*This office notes with satisfaction the continued stability of the Hollow Valley ward. Be advised that periodic inspections will resume following the recent irregularity. Should any person or phenomenon exhibiting pre-Treaty characteristics present itself within the valley, the keeper is reminded of Article Nine of the Treaty of Severance: such persons are to be secured and surrendered to Compact custody without delay, and the keeper is further reminded that failure to comply constitutes breach of contract, with all consequences thereof.*

*The Compact thanks the keeper for two centuries of faithful service.*

Freya read *Article Nine* three times. *Secured and surrendered.* She looked up from the page, through the window, into the dark, toward the gate, and imagined the inspection that must have followed that letter—the grey coaches, the strangers with instruments, the child from the earlier letter already gone wherever such children went.

*A maintenance contract.* Aldous had been right. They were not an order. They were employees, and the boy in her bed was a defect in the product.

She picked up Aldous's final page again, the one she'd read first, and read the lines beneath the ones she'd memorized, the ones she'd stopped at because her eyes had blurred:

*I went to the gate the winter before last, when the ice made the road treacherous and no one walks it. I took a lamp and I looked at the stone beneath the seals, the old stone, the foundation stone, and I cleaned two hundred years of moss off it, and I read what is carved there. It is not a warding formula. It is not a prayer. It is a boundary marker, the kind surveyors set, and it reads, in the tongue they tried to bury: HERE ENDS VESSIA. HERE BEGINS THE WORLD THAT FORGOT HER.*

*I recited the oath the next morning as always. I said the false words in my false language and I kept the false peace, because I am an old man and afraid, and because the valley is full of people who did not choose this and do not deserve to pay for it. But you—you are young, and stubborn, and you ask questions I stopped asking years ago. So I will leave you the truth and no instructions, because I cannot decide for you, and because deciding is the one thing they never trained us to do.*

*Forgive the dead, Freya. We were cowards, but we were paid cowards, and there is a difference, and the difference is the shame of it.*

*—Aldous*

Freya sat for a long time without moving. The lamp burned low. In the other room, Tam murmured in his sleep, and the sound of it—those vowels, those soft consonants, the grammar of a country erased from every map—drifted under the door and settled over her like snow.

Betrayal, she thought, was supposed to feel like anger. This felt like arithmetic. Two hundred years of dawns, two hundred years of keepers rising to recite an oath in a language chosen precisely because it meant nothing to them, two hundred years of honest men and women standing guard over a crime and calling it holiness. Every keeper in the roll call. Aldous. Herself. All of them paid, all of them deceived, all of them complicit in a silence so old it had fossilized into ritual.

And the dead had known. Some of them had known and written it down and tied it in oilcloth and left it in a drawer, because knowing and telling were different duties, and they had chosen the easier one.

"I'm not angry," she said aloud to the empty room, and found it was true, and found that worse.

She gathered the letters, rewrapped them, and then—instead of returning them to the drawer—carried them to the hearth and set them on the stone beside it, where she could reach them. Where they would stay.

Then she took the lamp and went out into the blue hour before dawn, down the path to the gate, because there was one thing left to check, and she was done being a woman who didn't look.

The Hollow Gate stood grey and patient in the mist, its arch carved with words she had traced a thousand times and never read. Freya climbed the keeper's step, wet her thumb, and scrubbed at the moss at the base of the eastern pillar, working fast, working hard, the way Aldous must have, until the stone showed clean and pale beneath her hand.

She knelt. She read.

*HERE ENDS VESSIA.*

Behind her, inside the cottage, faint through the open door, the boy cried out in his sleep—one short, sharp syllable in a language two hundred years buried—and every seal on the Hollow Gate flickered, once, like a candle in wind.

And down the valley road, far off but coming, came the creak of wheels.
