## 5. What the Valley Cannot Hold

Tam woke on the third morning in the keeper's cottage and did not know, for a long moment, where the ceiling had come from.

It was a low ceiling, whitewashed, with a beam that ran crooked across it like a river drawn by someone who had only heard about rivers. In the house he remembered—no. Not a house. A flat. Rooms stacked one atop another like stones in a wall, and out the window a tower with bells, and the bells rang at hours that meant things: wake, eat, remember, sleep. There were no bells here. There was a rooster instead, somewhere down the lane, shouting the same word over and over as if it had forgotten all its other words.

He lay still under the wool blanket and listened to the cottage breathe. Old houses breathed differently than new ones. This one creaked at the joints the way his grandmother's chair had creaked, the way old people's knees creaked, and there was a smell of woodsmoke and dried herbs and something underneath it all like cold stone, like the inside of a hill.

The woman was up already. He could hear her moving in the other room, setting iron against iron, banking a fire. She had carried him here three nights ago out of the rain. She had wrapped him in this blanket and fed him soup that tasted mostly of salt and leeks, and she had not once asked him the questions he could see sitting behind her eyes like birds on a wire.

Her name was Freya. He knew that because she had said it slowly, pointing at herself, the way you talk to someone you think is simple. He wasn't simple. He was eight years old and he knew more words than most grown-ups he had ever met, just not these words, not the ones she spoke, which sat in his ears like coins from a country he'd never been to—and yet some of them fit in his mouth anyway, round and worn smooth, *bread*, *water*, *sleep*, as if he'd always known them and had only just now found a use for them.

That was the strange thing. That was one of the strange things.

Tam got up and dressed in the shirt she had left for him, which smelled of cedar and was too big in the shoulders, and went to the window.

* * *

The valley did not look real.

He had thought this every morning since he arrived, and every morning it looked less real and more beautiful, the way a drawing of an apple can be prettier than an apple and also wrong. The hills rose on every side, steep and green, stitched with stone walls older than any wall should be, and above them the sky sat low and pale like a lid on a pot. There was no wind. He had noticed that the first day. The trees stood perfectly still, even the tall ones on the ridgeline, even when clouds moved overhead—which they did, but slowly, politely, like guests who didn't want to be noticed leaving.

And everything was quiet in a way that wasn't silence. It was more like deafness. Like the valley had cupped its hands over its own ears.

Tam pressed his palm flat against the cool glass of the window and tried to do the small thing. The light-thing. The thing he could do, had always been able to do, the way other children could whistle or wiggle their ears.

Nothing happened.

It wasn't that it failed. It was that it fell. He would reach for the feeling—the warm thread behind his breastbone, the one that answered when he called—and it would rise up in him like a spark off a fire, and then it would simply drop, dead, before it got anywhere, as if the air itself were wet and every spark drowned on the way out of him.

He tried again, harder, screwing his eyes shut, holding the shape of it in his mind the way his mother had taught him—

His mother.

The thought arrived the way it always arrived, without knocking, and took the floor out from under the morning.

Mama. Standing in the kitchen with flour on her hands, laughing at something Papa said, the light through the window catching the grey in her hair that she pretended wasn't there. Mama humming while she kneaded. Mama's voice saying his name—not *Tam* the way everyone said it, but the long way, the whole way, *Tamriel Vessin Ondra*, three names like three steps down into water, and the third name always made her voice go soft and careful, because that name was a gift and gifts are fragile—

He hadn't seen her fall.

That was the thing he kept circling and circling, a dog worrying a bone it couldn't crack. He remembered the light. He remembered the light coming down through the ceiling of the world like a spear through cloth, and he remembered reaching for it—or being reached for, he couldn't make those two things sit together—and then he remembered falling, falling for much longer than any fall should last, through a place that was all color and no ground, and then rain, and mud, and a road, and a woman with tired eyes lifting him out of the dark.

But between the light and the fall there should have been her hands. There should have been her voice. There should have been *something*, and there was nothing, and the nothing was shaped exactly like his mother, and it hurt to look at it directly so he looked at it sideways, all day, every day, the way you look at the sun.

"Tam?"

He turned. Freya stood in the doorway with two bowls, steam rising off them. Up close her face was a map of a country with hard weather—all lines going the same direction, all carved by the same wind. But her eyes weren't hard. Her eyes were the softest thing about her, brown and watchful, and they looked at him the way he imagined a person looks at a door they're afraid to open.

She set a bowl on the table and pointed at it, then at him. "Eat," she said, in her coin-smooth word.

"I know," Tam said—in her language, or almost; his tongue stumbled on the sounds sometimes, they came out too round, too musical, and he saw her flinch slightly every time, though she tried to hide it. "I know *eat*. I'm not a baby."

Something flickered across her face. Not quite a smile. "No," she agreed. "Not a baby."

They ate. The porridge was good, heavy with honey. Outside the window the rooster shouted its one word. Tam ate and watched the still trees and felt the dead place behind his breastbone where his spark used to live, and thought: *the valley has taken it.* He didn't know how he knew. He just knew, the way you know a room is empty before you turn on the lamp. Whatever this valley was, whatever walls it had, they were walls against him specifically. Against the thing he was.

And yet his memories worked fine. Better than fine. They were the loudest things in the whole quiet valley.

* * *

After breakfast Freya went out to her rounds—she had shown him yesterday, walking the wall of the gate, touching the stones, murmuring words in that half-familiar language—and Tam was left alone with instructions he half understood and a cottage full of questions.

He explored instead.

There wasn't much to explore. Two rooms, a loft, a garden gone slightly wild at the edges. But under the loft ladder there was a chest, and the chest was unlocked, and eight-year-olds have never in the history of the world respected a locked or unlocked chest when left alone with one.

Inside: books. Old ones, fat ones, their covers cracked like dry mud. And loose papers. And—a folded cloth, and inside the cloth, a rubbing. Charcoal on paper, taken from stone. Tam knew rubbings; every schoolchild in—every schoolchild *at home* learned to take rubbings of the memorial walls, pressing paper against carved names and dragging charcoal until the names appeared ghost-grey, so that the dead could travel out of the wall and into your pocket.

This rubbing showed a line of carving. And Tam went very still, kneeling on the cold flagstones, because he could read it.

Not guess. *Read.*

*Vessia endures. Vessia remembers. Let the gate stand between what was and what must not return.*

The words were in his own tongue. His mother's tongue. The tongue he'd spoken before he could walk, the tongue of the bell tower and the market stalls and the lullaby about the moon who lost her shoe. Here, in this stranger's cottage, in this stranger's valley, written in stone by hands long dead.

*Vessia.*

He said it aloud, just to hear it, just to prove the cottage was real enough to hold it. The syllables came out clean and bright and they seemed to hang in the air longer than words should, and for half a heartbeat—one heartbeat, no more—he felt the warm thread behind his breastbone twitch, like a sleeper turning over.

Then it died again, drowned in the wet air.

But it had moved. It had *moved*, and Tam knelt there with his heart hammering, staring at the charcoal ghosts of letters, and understood two things at once, the way children understand things, suddenly and completely and without any of the cushioning adults wrap around knowledge.

First: this gate, this valley, this whole green deaf world—he was from the same place it was from. The words fit the stone because the stone and he were kin.

Second: *what must not return.* The carving said it plainly, in his mother's language, in the language she'd sung him to sleep in. Something had been shut away here. Something had been told *never come back.*

Was it him? Had he been sent? Had the light that fell through the ceiling of the world been a door opening just long enough to throw one small boy through it, out of the burning—no, nothing burned, why did he keep thinking *burning*, nothing in his memory burned, the city was grey, the city was grey and the bells rang and his mother had flour on her hands—

He put the rubbing back. He folded the cloth the same way, corner over corner, because his mother had taught him that too: *leave a thing as you found it, unless you mean to change it.* His hands shook and the fold came out crooked and he hated himself for the crookedness, stupidly, enormously, the way grief makes you hate small failures because the large ones are unholdable.

He climbed the ladder to the loft and lay on the straw pallet and stared at the underside of the roof and let it come.

It came like weather. It always came like weather.

The city first: Vessa-Coronne, the grey city, the city of ninety-nine bells, where the streets ran downhill toward the river in long straight lines like water learning to flow. The market with its awnings the color of eggplant and mustard. The tram-bells. The smell of hot oil and cinnamon from the fritter carts. His schoolroom with its high windows. Papa's coat on its hook, smelling of pipe smoke and rain. Mama's hands. Mama's laugh. Mama saying his whole name, all three steps down into the water.

*Tamriel. Vessin. Ondra.*

And under all of it, everywhere, the greyness. That was the part he couldn't explain, couldn't find a shelf for. The city was alive—he could hear it living, bells and carts and voices—but everything in it was faint, like a painting left out in the rain, colors running, edges softening. People walked through the market and their faces wouldn't stay. He remembered trying to hold onto the face of the fritter-seller, a man who'd winked at him every morning for years, and the face slid through his memory like fish through fingers. He remembered his teacher writing on the slate and the letters fading even as she wrote them. He remembered—this was the worst one, this was the one he kept in the deepest drawer—he remembered standing in the square and realizing he could not remember the name of his own street. He lived there. He had lived there all his life. And the name of the street was *gone*, a smooth blank place in his mind like a tooth knocked out.

A country forgetting itself. That was what it had felt like, before the light came. A whole country quietly forgetting its own name, and everyone pretending not to notice, the way a family pretends not to notice a father drinking.

And now he was here, in a valley that had forgotten how to blow wind, lying in a stranger's loft, and the forgetting had followed him. Or he had run ahead of it. He didn't know. He was eight. There were words for what was happening to him, he was sure of it—there were probably whole books of words, fat scholarly words with footnotes—but none of them belonged to him. Grown-ups had words. Children had only the weather.

So he did what he always did when the weather came. He said the names.

Quietly, into the straw, into the crooked beam, into the deaf air of the valley: the names he could still hold. His mother's name—her true name, not *Mama*, but the name her own mother had given her, *Ilsette Vessin Ondra*, which meant *lily-of-the-border* in the old formal way, though his father had always joked that she was less a lily than a nettle. His father's name. The fritter-seller's name, which he had caught and held like a frog in both hands, *Master Pell*. His street—no. The street was still gone. He skipped it. You learned to skip the holes.

He said the names and the names were small warm stones in his mouth and he arranged them carefully, one by one, because as long as he could say them, they existed, and as long as they existed, he was not the only one of himself in the world.

Because that was the other thing he knew, the second-worst thing, the thing under the worst thing. Somewhere between the light and the fall, between the grey city and the green valley, everyone else had stopped coming along. He was alone here. Utterly, completely, in a way he had no word for. The woman downstairs was kind, but kind was not the same as *from*. There was no one else in this whole still valley who knew what cinnamon smelled like frying in oil, who knew the tram-bells' schedule, who knew that you crossed yourself with your thumb before eating fritters because Master Pell said it kept the grease off your soul.

He was the last one. He didn't know how he knew that either. He just knew it, the way you know the depth of water by the pressure in your ears.

Tam lay in the loft and cried the way children cry when no one teaches them they're allowed to: silently, efficiently, all of it at once, wrung out like a cloth, done in four minutes, leaving him hollow and clear-eyed and terribly calm.

Then he heard the hoofbeats.

* * *

They came up the valley road at a trot—three riders, maybe four, grey shapes against the green. Tam leaned out of the loft window and watched them, and something in his chest went tight and cold, tighter than grief, colder.

Their coats were grey. Their banners—if you could call those narrow pennants banners—were grey. And on the pennants was a device he knew, the way he knew his own hands: a circle broken by a straight line. The sign of the Compact. The men who came to the city twice a year in carriages with covered windows, the men the grown-ups stopped talking about when children entered the room, the men whose arrival always coincided with another name going missing from the memorial wall.

The valley had cupped its hands over its ears. But the world outside had not forgotten the valley. The world outside was riding up the road toward the cottage, patient as weather, and Tam knew—with that sudden complete child-knowledge, that terrible gift—exactly what they had come looking for.

They had come looking for a speaker.

They had come looking for *him*.

Downstairs, the door opened. Freya stood in the lane, one hand shading her eyes, watching the riders come, and Tam saw her go very still in a way that had nothing calm in it at all.

He gripped the windowsill. Behind his breastbone, in the drowned dark where his spark lay, something turned over in its sleep—and this time, faint as a match struck underwater, it did not go out.
