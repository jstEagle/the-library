## 6. Riders on the Ridge

The first sign was the birds.

Freya noticed it on her morning round, three days after Tam had begun to settle into the cottage like a stone settling into a streambed—slowly, and with a great deal of noise at first, then all at once. The swallows that nested under the eaves of the gatehouse had gone quiet. Not flown; quiet. They sat in their rows along the lintel with their heads turned all in one direction, every one of them facing north, toward the ridge where the valley's walls rose highest and the old road came down from a world Freya had never been expected to think about.

She had learned to read the valley the way her mentor, Aldous Crane, had taught her to read the gate: not by what it said but by what it stopped saying. A silence in the wrong place was worth ten warnings carved in stone.

So when she crested the rise behind the keeper's cottage and saw, against the grey-green flank of the ridge, a line of movement no larger than a stitch in cloth, she did not need her spyglass to know what it meant. Something had come over the pass. Something that made birds hold their tongues.

* * *

She had lived in the Hollow Valley for nineteen years, and in all that time she could count the outsiders who had entered it on one hand, without using all the fingers. There had been a lost shepherd once, before Aldous died, who had wandered in through the southern sheep-paths and been fed, redirected, and sent on his way within a day. There had been a pair of cartographers' assistants who had come as far as the valley mouth, looked at the fog that pooled there like milk in a bowl, and turned back without ever learning why their instruments spun. And there had been the tax assessor from the Marchlands, years ago now, who had ridden up to the cottage, squinted at the gate, written *unproductive land, exempt* in his ledger, and left looking vaguely disappointed, as though he had hoped to find something worth taxing and been cheated by the emptiness.

That was the whole of it. The valley kept itself closed the way a shell keeps its pearl—not by force, but by being so thoroughly uninteresting to everything outside that nothing bothered to look in. The Compact maps marked it as a blank. The roads went around it. Even the weather, sometimes, seemed to part around its borders like water around a standing stone, and Freya had spent enough years walking the ridgeline to stop finding that strange and start finding it comforting.

Which was why the riders frightened her before she could name a single reason why.

There were six of them. They came down the old north road in single file, and even at half a mile's distance Freya could see that they were wrong for the road they rode. The north road had been built for carts and pilgrims, wide-shouldered and slow, and these riders took it like men taking a throat. They sat tall in the saddle. They did not pick their way. They did not pause at the boundary stones where every traveler before them had paused, whether to rest or to wonder or simply because the stones invited pausing, worn smooth on top by generations of idle hands.

They rode straight through.

Their livery was grey. Not the grey of homespun or the grey of rain coming, but a deliberate grey, a uniform grey, the same shade on all six of them, cut close and buttoned high, with a device on the breast that Freya could not resolve at distance—a circle, perhaps, or a seal, some emblem pressed flat and formal as a wax stamp. Their horses were the same iron colour, or near enough, and moved with the drilled precision of animals trained to keep formation. Everything about them matched. Nothing about them was individual. From a distance they might have been six pieces of a single machine, unfolding down the mountainside.

Freya stood on the rise above the cottage with the wind pulling at her braid and watched them come, and felt something she had not felt since Aldous died: the specific loneliness of being the only person awake in a house while something moves toward it in the dark.

Except it was morning. The light was clean and gold and lay across the valley floor like spilled honey. Whatever was coming was coming in full daylight, openly, at a walking pace, and somehow that was worse. Weather arrived like this. You saw the cloud building over the ridge hours before it reached you, and you knew there was nothing to be done about it except bring in what you could carry and wait to find out how much of your life would still be standing afterward.

* * *

She went down to the cottage and found Tam in the kitchen, sitting cross-legged on the hearthstone, sorting through the small hoard of things he had gathered in his two weeks among them: a river-smoothed pebble, a bent nail, a wooden button off one of Aldous's old coats, a moth's wing he had asked her to help him press between the pages of the least useful book on the shelf. He looked up when she came in. He had gotten better at looking up when she came in—at expecting her, at orienting himself to her presence the way a plant orients to a window—and the sight of it still caught somewhere behind her ribs, tender and strange.

"Riders," she said. "On the north road. Six of them. Grey."

Tam went very still. It was a stillness she had learned to recognize: not the stillness of a child listening, but the stillness of a child deciding whether to be afraid. His hands stopped moving over his treasures. His eyes went somewhere else entirely—to a place behind his own face that Freya had glimpsed only in fragments, in the words he spoke that fit the gate's carvings, in the way he flinched from certain shapes of cloud.

"Grey," he repeated. His voice came out flat. "With a circle on the chest?"

Freya felt the floor tilt slightly under her certainty. "I couldn't see the device. Why would you ask that?"

"I've seen them." He said it simply, the way children say terrible things, without any of the architecture adults build around terrible things to make them bearable. "Before. In—" He stopped. His mouth worked around a word that wasn't in the valley's language, wasn't quite in his own, and finally he gave up and said, "Before here. Before I fell." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were the brown of deep water. "They were looking for people who talk like me."

Freya stood in her own kitchen and felt the temperature of the morning change.

She thought of the gate. She thought of the seals she checked twice daily, the seven rings of inscription that had hummed faintly under her palm for nineteen years, steady as a sleeping pulse—and of the night, eleven days ago now, when Tam had spoken a word in the dead tongue and one of those rings had flickered like a lamp in wind. She had told herself it was coincidence. She had told herself a great many convenient things in eleven days, the way a woman tells herself things when the alternative is to admit that the ground under her feet has been hollow all along.

Now six riders in grey were coming down the north road, and the boy in her kitchen said they hunted speakers of his language, and the birds on the gatehouse had all turned to watch them come.

"Aldous," she said aloud, mostly to herself, and went to the back room.

* * *

Aldous Crane had been dead four years, and in four years Freya had opened his study perhaps a dozen times, always with the same careful grief, always putting everything back exactly as he'd left it—the inkwell with its skin of dried black, the spectacles folded on the open ledger, the chair pushed in at the angle of a man who intended to return. She had treated the room as a shrine. Shrines are for the comfort of the living, and she had needed the comfort.

Today she opened it the way you open a strongbox.

The ledger lay where it always lay, and she forced herself past it, past the comfortable familiar record of decades of rounds and repairs, to the shelf behind the door where Aldous kept the correspondence that mattered. Letters came to the valley rarely—perhaps twice a year, borne by circuit riders who never dismounted—and Aldous had filed them with an archivist's precision in bundles tied with twine, each bundle labeled in his cramped, upright hand. She had read none of them. It had not occurred to her that she should. The letters were his; the duty was hers; the two had passed each other in the dark without touching.

She untied the most recent bundle and stood in the cold light of the study window reading letters from men whose names she barely knew, and the letters said almost nothing. *Rounds proceeding normally. No irregularities reported. The Compact extends its regards.* Year after year, decade after decade, the same polite blankness, the same formal nothing, correspondence conducted between two parties who appeared to have agreed long ago that the subject of their correspondence must never be mentioned.

It was the agreement itself that chilled her. You do not maintain forty years of perfectly empty letters unless both sides know exactly what is being not-said.

At the bottom of the bundle, separate, unfiled, was a single sheet in Aldous's hand, undated, unfinished—a draft of a letter he had apparently never sent. It began mid-thought, the way drafts do, as if the beginning had been too dangerous to write down:

*—and so I ask again, plainly, as I have asked in every letter these twenty years: what is it the Compact believes we are keeping? You say the border is settled. You send surveyors' fees and call it maintenance. But maintenance of what? A gate does not need funding. A wall does not need watching this closely. Someone, somewhere, still cares what happens inside this valley, and after thirty years I am no closer to knowing who, or why, or what it is they fear we will find—*

The draft ended there. Below it, in different ink, older, smaller, someone else had written a single line—perhaps a note passed to him, perhaps copied from something:

*Ask no questions you are not prepared to answer with your life. —M.*

Freya read it three times. Then she put it in her apron pocket, against her body, where it lay like a coal.

* * *

By the time she came out again, the riders had reached the valley floor.

She watched them from the cottage door, and watching was its own kind of education. They did not behave like lost men. Lost men scatter; these held their line. Lost men look at the ground; these looked at everything—the boundary stones, the orchards, the rooftops of the village below, and most of all, longest, the gate. One of them, riding second, carried something slung across his saddle that Freya at first took for a rifle case and then, as they drew nearer, understood to be something else entirely: a long instrument in a padded sleeve, brass fittings glinting, shaped like nothing so much as a very large tuning fork wrapped in velvet and menace.

They reined up at the foot of the keeper's hill, precisely where the road forked—one branch to the village, one to the gate—and the leader raised a hand, and all six horses stopped as if cut from the same cloth.

He was a lean man, forty perhaps, with the kind of face that seems assembled rather than born: regular features, no scars, no laughter lines, sunburned in a way that suggested travel without suggesting hardship. When he dismounted, he did it without wasted motion, and he stood at the bottom of her path and looked up at the cottage for a long moment before speaking, the way a man looks at a property he is considering buying.

"Keeper Hartwell?" His voice carried easily up the hill, trained, projected, pitched to be pleasant. "My name is Corvan. These are my colleagues. We're with the Border Survey Commission—we're mapping the northern approaches." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was also, Freya thought, a smile that had been practiced in front of a mirror until it stopped belonging to anyone. "We understand the valley hasn't been surveyed in some years. We're here to correct that. Nothing more."

Nothing more. The phrase hung in the air like smoke.

Freya descended the path slowly, and as she did, she watched them the way Aldous had taught her to watch everything in the valley: for what they touched, what they avoided, what they looked at twice. The five subordinates remained mounted. They sat in a loose arc, faces forward, and not one of them looked at her as she approached. They looked past her. Past the cottage. Past the village smoke rising thin and blue beyond it. Every gaze in that grey arc rested, with the patience of men being paid by the day, on the Hollow Gate.

"Surveyors," Freya said, when she reached level ground. She kept her voice as flat and courteous as a frozen pond. "We haven't had surveyors in the valley since before I was keeper. What brings the Commission out now?"

Corvan spread his hands—an easy gesture, an innocent gesture. "New administration in the Marchlands. New priorities. Borders drift, Keeper, even when the land doesn't. Rivers move. Roads decay. Treaties get renegotiated." He said the word *treaties* the way a man says a word he owns several of. "Our mandate is simply accuracy. Maps go stale. Stale maps cause disputes. Disputes cause wars. We prevent wars." Another smile, smaller, warmer, more insulting. "You'll hardly notice us."

But she already had noticed them, and what she noticed was this: they had not brought camp gear. Six men on a surveying expedition into remote country, and not a tent between them, not a cook pot, not a single item that suggested they expected to stay the night. They had brought instruments instead. She counted them now as the horses shifted: the long cased thing on the second rider's saddle; a box of dark wood strapped behind the third, humming—actually humming, a low tone she could feel in her back teeth from twenty feet away; and on the leader's own pommel, a flat leather wallet thick as a brick, tied shut with cord stamped with the same circular seal as their breasts.

Instruments that hummed. In a valley where magic could not enter. Humming at what, then?

"The gate," Freya said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she'd intended. "You'll want to note it on your map. It's the only landmark of consequence. It's also sealed, warded, and under the keeping of my order, and no instrument of yours goes within fifty paces of it. Those are the terms. They're old terms. Older than your Commission."

Something flickered across Corvan's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. Not anger. Recognition, maybe. Or recalculation. "Of course," he said smoothly. "We respect local custodianship. We'll sketch it from the road, nothing closer." He turned to give orders to his men, and the orders were in a language Freya didn't know—but Tam would have, she realized, with a lurch. The orders were in a language with the sound of the gate's inscriptions in it, the same liquid consonants, the same falling cadences, spoken casually, administratively, by men in grey livery on a Tuesday morning.

Then the second rider swung down from his horse and began unpacking the long case, right there at the fork in the road, fifty-one paces from the gate if he was an honest man and fifty if he wasn't.

And from the direction of the gate—faint, steady, unmistakable, a sound Freya had lived beside for nineteen years and never once heard change—came the sound of the seals changing pitch.

* * *

She left them with a promise of bread and water and the valley's grudging hospitality, which was to say she left them at the fork with a curt nod and walked fast up the hill, and she did not let herself run until the bend of the path hid her from their sightline. Then she ran.

The gate stood in its cutting of grey stone as it had always stood, and yet it did not stand as it had always stood. She felt the difference before she could see it, the way you feel a storm in an old joint. The air around the arch was thicker. The light fell into the gateway and did not quite come out the other side. And the seals—the seven rings of script she had traced with her palm every morning and evening since she was a girl—were warm. Not flickering, not yet. Warm, the way a banked fire is warm, the way a thing is warm when it is not asleep anymore but merely waiting.

She laid her hand flat against the lowest ring and listened with her skin.

Under the familiar hum, deeper, slower, like a second heartbeat beneath the first, something was answering. Something was answering the riders' instruments. Call and response. Question and echo. The valley had been silent for a hundred years, and now, in a single morning, two voices had begun speaking to each other across it, and Freya Hartwell was the only soul in either country who could hear both.

Behind her, down at the fork in the road, the second rider finished assembling his instrument. It was a fork indeed—brass, taller than a man, its two tines aimed at the gate like a diviner's rod. When he struck it, softly, with a padded mallet, the tone it gave was almost inaudible, a frequency more felt than heard.

And the third ring of the gate's seals shivered, visibly, like water struck by a dropped stone.

Freya pulled her hand back. She stood in the gateway's shadow with the coal of Aldous's unsent letter burning in her pocket and the taste of copper rising in her throat, and she understood, with the calm of complete catastrophe, that the outside world had not come to map the valley.

It had come to take its measure. To learn its weight. To find out, gently, politely, with fees and forms and fifty-one paces of legal distance, whether the door still held—and if it didn't, to be standing close by when it opened.

Down the hill, Tam was standing in the cottage doorway. She hadn't seen him follow her; he had simply appeared, small and watchful against the light, the way he always seemed to appear when the valley shifted underfoot. He looked at the gate. He looked at the riders. He looked at her.

"They're counting," he said quietly, in the valley tongue, and then, in his own, in words that fitted the stone: *"They count the locks before they break them."*

Freya looked at the boy, and at the gate, and at the six grey figures waiting at the fork like weather about to break, and she knew that whatever the next days brought, they would bring it soon.
