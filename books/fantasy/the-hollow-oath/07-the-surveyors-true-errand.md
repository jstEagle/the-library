## 7. The Surveyors' True Errand

Tam knew the grey coats.

He didn't know how he knew them. That was the trouble with everything since the fall through the light—his memory came to him sideways, in pieces, like rain through a broken roof. He would be doing one thing, an ordinary thing, watching Freya stir the pot or stack wood for the evening fire, and then a whole other thing would arrive in his head without knocking, and he would have to stand very still until it finished happening.

That was what happened when he saw the riders at the fork of the road.

He was standing in the cottage doorway, half-hidden by the door itself, the way he'd learned to stand in the first days—small enough to be missed, positioned so he could run. He hadn't needed to run in the valley. The valley was soft at the edges. His magic went quiet here, muffled like a shout into wool, but so did everything else that wanted him, and for a while he had believed—because Freya was kind and the bread was good and no one shouted—that quiet meant safe.

Then six riders had come over the ridge, and his chest had gone tight and cold before his head understood why.

Grey coats. Grey cloaks, grey gloves, even their horses dappled grey as if the colour had been ordered along with everything else. And on the left breast of each coat, stitched small and neat and deliberate: a circle, quartered by four lines, with a keyhole where the heart should be.

*The Compact,* said something inside him, in Vessian, in his mother's voice, low and urgent the way she spoke when she pulled the shutters closed at dusk. *The Compact is measuring.*

He pressed his back against the doorframe and let the memory come, because fighting it only made it worse, made it arrive in jagged shards that cut on the way down. Better to stand still and let it pour.

---

A kitchen. Not this kitchen—a bigger one, with a copper kettle and a window that looked out on a street where bells hung in a tower taller than any tree. His mother was kneading dough, flour to her elbows, and she was talking fast and quiet, not to him exactly, more to the air, the way people talk when they are afraid and don't want their children to hear but want them to hear anyway.

"—the surveyors came through Marrowgate in spring," she was saying. "Grey coats. They asked old Pell about the well-water, about the roads, about how many families. He told them, because they showed papers, and papers are papers." Her hands worked the dough harder. "And in autumn Marrowgate wasn't on anyone's map anymore. Not burned, Tam. *Unlisted.* You can't burn a town out of every ledger in the world, but you can forget it out, if you have enough pens."

"Who has enough pens?" he had asked, or some smaller version of himself had asked, standing on a stool to see over the table.

"The Compact." She said it the way you say a word you've decided not to be frightened of, which is to say she said it too carefully. "Four kingdoms who agreed on one thing only: that borders are better drawn than remembered. They survey. They measure. They write things down in books that make other books untrue." She wiped her hands and crouched to his height, and there was flour on her cheekbone, white against brown, and her eyes were the exact colour of the gate-stone's shadow. "If grey coats ever come to Vessia, Tam—if they ever come—you go to the bell tower and you find Ondrey, and you say the word I taught you, and you don't say anything else. Not your name. Especially not your name."

"Why not my name?"

"Because names are what they count." She kissed his forehead and left flour in his hair. "They count the locks before they break them, and they count the names before they take them."

---

The memory ended, the way memories did, mid-breath, and Tam found himself still in the doorway, still gripping the frame, and the six riders were still at the fork of the road below, and one of them had dismounted and was walking toward the gate with a long instrument case slung across his back.

*They count the locks before they break them.*

Freya was coming up the hill toward him, moving fast, her keeper's staff striking sparks off the frost-hard ground. He watched her face and saw that she had not yet understood—not really, not the way he did. She thought the riders were weather. She thought weather could be waited out.

"They're not surveyors," Tam said when she reached the doorway. He said it in the valley tongue, because the valley tongue was for ordinary dangers, wolves and rockslides and bad harvests. Then he switched, because this danger was not ordinary, and some things could only be said properly in the language they belonged to. "They're Compact. They hunt speakers."

Freya stopped on the top step. Wind moved her hair; she didn't seem to notice it. "Speakers of what?"

"Of the dead tongue." His own voice sounded far away to him, flat and small. "Of Vessia. Of me."

* * *

They sat at the table, which was where Freya put things when she wanted them to feel settled—the table, the fire, the pot of tea she brewed for every crisis large or small, as if steam could hold the world together. Tam wrapped both hands around his cup though he didn't drink. The heat felt good against the cold that had lived in his chest since the riders crested the ridge.

"Tell me from the beginning," Freya said. "Everything you remember about them. Slowly."

So he did. It took a long time, because remembering was slow work, and because he kept stopping to check each piece against the stone of truth, the way you test ice before you step. He told her about Marrowgate, the town unlisted. He told her about the papers the surveyors carried, documents that made other documents lies. He told her about the livery—the quartered circle, the keyhole—and watched Freya go still when he described it, watched her lips move silently, matching it against something carved above the gate.

"You've seen it," Tam said. "On the gate."

"On the *older* stones." Freya's voice had a strange texture, like cloth turned inside out. "Under the inscription. I always took it for a maker's mark."

"It's not a maker's mark." He was sure of this the way he was sure of his own hands. "It's a warning. My mother—" the word caught, the way it always caught, a fishhook behind his ribs— "my mother said the Compact marks the things it means to erase. So everyone knows whose work it is. So no one else claims it. It's not pride. It's bookkeeping."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing, and the last of it came through the window and lay across the table between them like something spilled.

"And they hunt speakers," she said. Not a question, but he answered anyway.

"They hunt words. People are just where the words live." He turned the cup in his hands. "There were others. Before me. In Vessia, near the end—" he stopped, because the end was a place his memory refused to go, a door in his mind that stayed shut no matter how he pushed. "I remember being taught to hide it. All the children were. We learned the valley tongue first and Vessian second, and we learned Vessian quietly, indoors, with the shutters closed. Like a sickness. Like something you could catch from being heard."

"That's monstrous," Freya said simply, and Tam blinked at her, because he had never once thought of it as monstrous. It had just been the shape of life, the way weather was the shape of life. Hearing it named—hearing a grown woman, a stranger, a keeper of a door at the edge of the world say *that's monstrous* in a plain voice over tea—made something crack a little in his chest, and he had to look at the fire until his eyes behaved.

"When they find a speaker," he went on, steadier now, "they don't hurt you. That's the worst part. Everyone always thinks there will be hurting, and there isn't. They sit you down at a table like this one, and they open a book, and they ask you questions, and they write down your answers. What words you know. Who taught you. Who else speaks. And then they thank you, and give you bread—" his mouth twisted— "and after, the words you told them stop working. Not for you. For everyone. You say *good morning* in Vessian and it comes out as nothing, as air shaped like nothing, and the person you said it to hears nothing, and slowly, slowly, nobody remembers that anything was ever said in it at all."

The fire popped. Neither of them moved.

"They wrote my mother's name in a book," Tam said. "I know because she stopped answering to it. Not right away. First she'd pause, like the name was at the bottom of a deep bag and she had to dig for it. Then the pause got longer. Then one day I called her and she looked around the room to see who was speaking." His voice did something he didn't authorize, thinned and wobbled, and he pressed it flat again. "She never forgot *me*. I want that written down somewhere, wherever things get written. She never forgot me. But she forgot her own name, and she forgot the songs, and she forgot the word for the little shelf above the sink where we kept the cups, and a country is made of words like that, thousands of them, millions, and they took them one page at a time."

Freya reached across the table. She didn't grab his hand; she laid hers palm-up beside his cup, an offer, and after a moment he put his hand in it, and her grip was warm and rough and steady, and they stayed like that while the light died completely and the room went blue with dusk.

"I won't let them write your name in a book," Freya said at last.

"You can't stop them writing it," Tam said. He was surprised at how calm he sounded, how much he sounded like his mother. "You can only stop them keeping it."

* * *

That night he couldn't sleep. The cottage was small and warm and smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs hung from the beams, and it should have been the safest place in the world, but the wind had changed direction in the afternoon and now it came down off the ridge carrying sounds from the camp at the fork of the road—the chink of harness, a man's laugh, once, horribly ordinary, the scrape of metal instruments being unpacked.

Near midnight he gave up and went to the window.

The valley at night was a bowl of darkness with stars poured into it, and down at the fork of the road the Compact camp glowed with a different light—not firelight, though they had fires too, but a thin cold gleam from the instruments themselves, set upright in the earth in a row like strange pale flowers. Even at this distance Tam could feel them working. It was like hearing a tune through a wall: he couldn't make out the notes, but he knew the song. Measuring. Counting. The locks, one by one, and under the locks—

Under the locks, him.

One of the figures detached from the camp and walked a slow circuit around the gate, and where the figure passed, the thin gleam followed, and Tam pressed his fingers against the cold glass and did the thing his mother had taught him, the last lesson, the one for emergencies: he took his own name and hid it. Not spoke it backward, not buried it—simply set it down somewhere deep inside, beneath the memory of the copper kettle and the flour on her cheek and the bells ringing in the tower, and left it there, quiet, uncounted.

*Let them measure the locks,* he thought, in Vessian, in the dark, in the language that fit the stone. *I am not a lock.*

But even as he thought it, he saw the figure at the gate pause. Saw the instrument in its hands tilt, gently, like a compass needle finding north. Saw the pale gleam swing toward the cottage on the hill, toward the small dark window, toward the boy standing in it.

The figure stood there a long time. Then it raised one grey-gloved hand—not a wave, nothing so human—and tapped twice on the air, as if marking a point on a map only it could see.

Tam stepped back from the window, heart hammering, and in the morning he would tell Freya, he would tell her everything, they would run or hide or fight—but tonight, in the dark, he lay awake listening to the wind off the ridge, and for the first time since he fell through the light into this soft green valley, he understood that the quiet had never been safety at all.

It had been the sound of being found.
