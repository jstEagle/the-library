## 9. Running the Hollow Road

The seal shattered at moonset, and the sound it made was not a sound Freya had ever heard before, though some buried part of her had been waiting for it all her life.

It was not the crack of stone or the snap of iron. It was more like a word being spoken too loudly—a syllable the size of a cathedral, forced through a gap meant for whispers. The second seal from the top, the one shaped like a closed eye, burst from the gate's face and spun away into the grass, still glowing, still *singing*, and where it landed the frost melted in a perfect circle and the grass began, impossibly, to grow.

"Tam." Her hand found his shoulder in the dark and closed on it hard enough to hurt. "Run."

* * *

Tam ran.

He had never run like this—not from Aldric's dogs in the village below the ridge, not from the storm that had chased him up the mountain in the first terrible hours of his arrival. This was running with the ground itself uncertain beneath his feet, the keeper's road unspooling ahead of them in the moonlight like a grey ribbon someone had dropped, and behind them—

Behind them the gate was *opening*.

He didn't look. He made himself not look, though every nerve in his body screamed to turn his head, because he had seen it begin. He had seen the great door—taller than the keeper's cottage, older than the oldest story—shudder in its frame like a thing waking from a long sleep, and he had seen what came through the widening gap before Freya had hauled him away: not fire, not darkness, but *light*. A road. A paved road going down and down toward a city with a hundred thousand windows, and above the city a sky that was the color of a bruise healing, violet shading into green, and stars in it that did not match any stars Tam knew.

He knew that sky.

He had fallen out of it. He remembered that now, remembered it the way you remember a wound—the fall through light, the long screaming dark, and then cold water and a valley where everything felt muffled, wrapped in wool, *quiet* in a way that had made him weep for three days without knowing why. The valley had been holding him the way a hand holds a moth. Gentle. And keeping him from something.

"Keep to the stones!" Freya's voice, ahead of him, ragged. "The old road holds the warding—the grass doesn't!"

He didn't understand what she meant until he stumbled, one foot sliding off the flagstone edge into the verge, and the moment his boot touched the living earth the whole world *tilted*. Not the ground—the ground was fine. Something else tilted. Something in him. The muted feeling he had lived inside for weeks suddenly thinned, and he could hear—God, he could *hear*—the gate behind them, a vast low note like a bell the size of a mountain being struck once and never allowed to finish ringing. And under it, fainter, a second sound. Voices. Hundreds of voices, far away, speaking Vessian.

His language. His mother's language. The language that had gotten the grey men with their humming instruments to turn and look at him with eyes like coins.

He wrenched his foot back onto the stone and the wool came down again, the muffling, the quiet, and he wanted to cry because the quiet had never felt like a cage before tonight and now he could not unfeel it.

* * *

Freya ran the way she had run the keeper's round for twenty years: knowing every stone, every root, every place where the old road dipped toward the stream and climbed again. But she had never run it *away* from the gate. The road had only ever been a circle to her—cottage to gate, gate to cottage, dawn and dusk, the same eleven hundred paces each way. Tonight it was a line, and the line went down the valley's throat toward the lowlands, toward Wren's quarry, toward the village, toward people who were asleep and did not know that the thing their grandparents had walled up was waking behind her.

The gate groaned again. She felt it through her boots, through her teeth, through the old oath scar on her palm that had ached every day since her mentor died and had never once told her *why*.

*What did you seal in there?* she had asked Maren once, when she was young enough to ask questions. *What's behind the Hollow Gate?*

*Nothing, child,* Maren had said, in the voice keepers used for children and for lies. *The gate holds back nothing. That's the point of it. It holds a nothing in place so the nothing stays nothing.*

Freya had repeated that answer to herself for two decades like a prayer, and it had taken a soaked, starving boy speaking a dead tongue to show her the shape of the lie. *Nothing* had a road in it. *Nothing* had a city with lit windows. *Nothing* had a sky the wrong color, and a sound like a bell, and—she had seen it, one heartbeat, one terrible heartbeat when the second seal burst—*nothing* had people. Figures on the road. Standing very still, the way the faded things in old stories stand, all of them turned toward the gate, all of them watching.

Watching her.

"Freya." Tam's voice, small and cracking. He had fallen three paces back and she hadn't noticed, and guilt went through her like a blade. He was eight years old. He was eight years old and she was asking him to run through the night away from the only thing in the world that might be his home. "Freya, they're *calling*."

She stopped. She hated herself for stopping. The keeper in her—the twenty years of rounds and recitations, the woman who had recited an oath every morning without understanding a word of it—said *keep moving, keep the boy moving, duty is distance.* But the oath was ash in her mouth now. She had watched a seal shatter tonight and understood, in the marrow of her, that every word she had ever spoken over the gate had been a lock, and she had been the key's hand, turning it faithfully, faithfully, faithfully, for twenty years.

She went back for him.

He was bent double, hands on his knees, and in the moonlight she could see that his breath came out faintly luminous—silver, like breath on the coldest morning of winter, though the night was only cool. The gate's opening was pulling at him. She could see that too, now that she knew what to look for. The light spilling down the old road from behind them wasn't light exactly; it was *attention*, and it had found the last Vessian in the world and was calling him the way a tide calls everything back to itself.

"Tam. Look at me." She crouched, took his face in her two hands. His cheeks were wet. "I know you can hear it. I know what it's saying."

"You *don't*—" His voice broke. "It's my *name*, Freya. It's saying my name the way my mother said it. It knows the—the little part, the part nobody here could ever say right—"

*Vessia,* the letters had read. *Herein is sealed the border of Vessia, and the oath of those who sealed it.* A country erased from every map. A people erased from every ledger. And one small boy, fallen through the crack, carrying the whole language of them in his mouth like a coal that wouldn't go out.

"Then we'll go and answer it," Freya said, and heard the words leave her mouth and felt the ground shift under her life. "But not tonight. Not running backward, blind, into whatever's calling. We go down to the valley. We go to Wren. And then—" She stopped. Swallowed. The next words were the truest and most terrible she had ever spoken, and they came out steady. "And then we go through. Together. On our feet. When *we* choose."

Tam stared at her. Above his head, far up the valley, the gate's light pulsed once, patient, like a slow heartbeat. Like something that had waited a hundred years and could wait out one child.

"You'd go *in*?" he whispered. "You're a keeper. Keepers don't—"

"Keepers sealed your country out of the world, Tam." The words tasted like iron. "I think it's past time one of them went in."

* * *

They ran.

The road took them down through the birch stands where the keeper's path crossed the stream at the stepping stones, and here Freya made her first mistake of the night, or perhaps her first honest act—she stopped, knelt, and pulled up three of the warding stones that marked the old boundary, the ones every keeper before her had set and every keeper before her had sworn to maintain. They came up easier than they should have. Easier than stone had any right to move, as if the ground itself had been waiting for someone to ask.

"What are you doing?" Tam whispered.

"Buying the valley time." She heaved the last stone out and dropped it into the stream, where the water hissed and steamed faintly, unhappy. "The warding runs through these. Without them the gate can't pull the valley's magic through the lowlands. It'll slow the opening." She stood, wiping her hands on her coat, and looked back up the valley one time—one time, she allowed herself—and what she saw made her stomach drop through the world.

The gate stood open the width of a cart and a horse. Not bursting, not broken—*opening*, the way a flower opens, deliberate and unhurried, and through the gap the violet-green sky poured its strange light down the keeper's road like spilled ink, and in that light the paved road of Vessia ran right up to the threshold and stopped, as if waiting for permission.

And on the threshold stood a figure. Tall. Thin. Wearing grey that was almost the grey of the surveyors' livery and almost the grey of fading, and Freya could not tell which, and the not-knowing frightened her more than either.

The figure raised one hand. Not a threat. An invitation.

Freya took Tam's hand and ran.

* * *

They came down off the high road as the sky went from black to the bruised grey before dawn, and below them the valley lay in its morning smoke—Wren's quarry, the mill, the crooked rooftops of the village, the elders' hall with its ancient weathervane. Everything ordinary. Everything asleep. Freya looked at it and felt something in her chest crack quietly, the way the seal had cracked, because she understood that she was looking at her home for what might be the last time as a keeper looks at it—as the thing she guarded.

*The valley cannot hold magic,* the old teaching went. *That is its blessing and its purpose.*

*The valley cannot hold magic because it was bought and paid for,* the letters had said. *It is the one place in the world where the price of Vessia's erasure is still being collected.*

Tam tugged her sleeve. He was grey-lipped, exhausted, luminous-breathed, and eight years old, and he pointed down at the village with a hand that shook.

"Freya. The smoke." She followed his finger. The mill chimney, the cottage chimneys, the hall—and one more smoke, thin and wrong, rising from the ridge path on the far side. The path the surveyors had come by. And on the road below it, catching the first light, a line of grey figures moving fast, moving down, moving toward the valley with their instruments humming.

They had heard the gate open too. Everyone in the world had heard the gate open, Freya thought wildly. It was the loudest nothing in history.

"Which way?" Tam asked. And there it was—the whole night folded into a child's two words. Which way. Back up to the gate and the calling and the violet sky, or down to the village and the grey riders and the elders who had known, who had all *known*, for a hundred years.

Freya Hartwell had recited the oath every morning of her adult life. *I hold the door. I keep the nothing. I ask no questions of the stone.* She had meant it. She had believed meaning it was the same as being good.

She turned her back on the village.

"Up," she said. "We're going to the gate. We're going through. And Tam—" She knelt, so their eyes were level, so he could see she wasn't lying, because he deserved, at last, one adult in his short ruined life who did not lie to him. "Whatever's on the other side—your country, your queen, your mother if she's there, all of it—whatever they ask of you, you remember this: I chose you. Not the oath. Not the valley. Not the door. *You.* Whatever happens on the other side of that gate, that doesn't change. Say it back to me."

Tam's chin wobbled. His luminous breath came fast. "You chose me."

"Say the rest."

"Not the oath." A wet, hiccupping almost-laugh. "Not the valley. Not the door. *Me.*"

"Good boy." She stood, and took his hand, and turned them both toward the light pouring down the valley like a tide coming in.

Behind them, far below, a bell began to ring in the village—alarm, alarm, the elders waking, the valley waking, everything her choice would cost beginning to come due. Ahead, at the top of the world, the Hollow Gate stood open the width of a cart and a horse, and the figure on the threshold had not moved, and had not stopped waiting.

Freya ran toward it, holding on to the last Vessian in the world, and did not look back again.
