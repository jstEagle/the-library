## 10. The Door Out of a Dead Country

The last hundred yards of the keeper's road were not a road at all anymore. They were a stair of raw stone, each step worn hollow in the middle by feet that had stopped climbing them two centuries before Freya was born, and she took them at a half-run with Tam's hand crushed in hers, his small fingers slick and cold, the bell behind them ringing its alarm into the bones of the valley.

She had climbed this stair every day of her adult life. She had never once climbed it with the gate open.

That was the thing her body could not stop noticing, the wrongness that made her breath come shallow: the light. The Hollow Gate had always stood at the top of the world like a closed eye, and the light that fell down the mountain from it had been valley-light, grey-gold, familiar as her own hands. This light was *other*. It came through the gap in the stone the width of a cart and a horse, and it fell across the steps in a long slanted bar, and it was the color of nothing Freya had a name for—pale gold shot through with green, like sunlight through river water, like the underside of a leaf held up to the sun.

Tam stopped three steps from the top.

Freya felt him stop more than saw it; his hand went rigid in hers, and when she turned, he was staring up at the threshold with an expression she had no word for either—not fear, not longing, something older than both, the look of a child standing at the door of a house he has dreamed about every night without knowing the house existed.

"That's the gate," he said. In Vessian. Then, remembering himself, in the valley tongue, halting and careful: "It's *big*."

"It's smaller than I always thought," Freya said, because it was true, and because saying something true and small might hold the moment still a little longer. All her life the gate had been vast in her mind—the whole weight of the oath, the whole height of her duty. Seen plainly, it was a door. A very old door in a very old wall, its arch carved over completely with the script she now knew was Vessian, its seals—the ones left unbroken—glowing along the jambs like embers banked for the night.

And in the opening, the figure.

She had seen it from below as a smudge, a suggestion. Up close it resolved into a woman, or what had been a woman once, standing just inside the arch where the other-light met the valley-stone. She was dressed in grey that might have been fine once—there were ghosts of embroidery at the cuffs, silver thread gone dull as ash—and her face was the problem. Freya's eyes kept trying to find it and kept sliding off, the way a finger slides off wet glass. She was there. She was unmistakably there. And she was also, in some way Freya's mind refused to file, *thinning*, like ink washed one time too many.

"You are the keeper," the woman said. Her voice was strange too—present, but quiet, as if heard through a wall. "You came running. The others always walked."

"The others?" Freya kept Tam behind her hip without quite meaning to. "There haven't been others. I'm the last."

"Yes." Something moved across the unfixed face—sorrow, or the memory of sorrow, worn smooth by handling. "That is what we hoped you would say, and feared."

"Who are you?"

"I am called Serevan. I keep the far side of the door, as you keep the near." She looked past Freya then, down the stair, and her gaze went soft and terrible. "Ah. There he is."

Tam stepped out from behind Freya. He did it slowly, the way he did everything that mattered, and he looked up at the fading woman and said something in Vessian—three words, four—and Freya watched the woman's whole insubstantial body tremble like a struck bell.

"He says—" Serevan pressed her hand flat against her chest, as if holding something in. "He says I look like the ladies who came to his mother's house. He is right. I taught some of their daughters my letters, long ago." She knelt. It took her a long time to kneel, as if gravity argued with her, and when she was level with the boy she said, in slow, careful valley-tongue, so that Freya would understand too: "Welcome home, Tam of the Line of Vess. Your country has been waiting to be able to see you."

"I can't stay on the step forever," Freya said. Her voice came out harder than she intended. "The elders will have riders on this road within the hour, and the Compact's surveyors know exactly where this gate is. If we're crossing, we cross now."

Serevan rose. "Then hear the only thing a door-keeper owes another door-keeper." She laid her thinning hand against the stone of the arch, gently, the way one touches a sleeping animal. "What waits beyond is real. Whatever else fails in there—names, faces, bread, memory—it is all *real*, and it will try to make itself ordinary to you, because that is how a forgotten country survives: it becomes furniture. Do not let it become furniture. Look at everything twice."

"Is that a warning or a blessing?"

"It is what I was told, when I crossed the other way, sixty years ago." For a moment her face almost coalesced—a jaw, a mouth, eyes the color of the light coming through the arch. "I was a keeper once too. Of the far side. We thought someone should be watching from both directions." She smiled, and the smile was the most solid thing about her. "Go. Both of you. And keeper—if you meet the Queen, remember that grief makes excellent arguments. That is not a warning against her. It is a warning about yourself."

Then she stepped aside, out of the threshold, and the way was open, and there was nothing left between Freya Hartwell and the end of everything she understood except one stride of mountain stone.

Tam's hand found hers again. He didn't pull. He just held on, and said, in the valley tongue, "Together?"

"Together."

They stepped through the Hollow Gate.

* * *

The first thing was the smell.

Not incense, not smoke, not any of the things Freya had braced herself for after a lifetime of stories about what lay outside the world. It was bread. Bread baking somewhere out of sight, yeast and wheat and woodsmoke, the plainest smell in creation, and it hit her so hard and so suddenly homesick-for-a-home-that-was-never-hers that she stopped dead on the far threshold and had to breathe through it.

The second thing was the road.

It ran away from the gate downhill through country that was, feature by feature, ordinary. A cobbled way, well-laid, its edges tufted with grass that needed cutting. Stone walls on either side, dry-built, mended and remended. An orchard—apples, she thought, though the fruit hung pale as moonstone—and beyond the orchard a field of barley so uniformly silver-grey it looked like a photograph of a field rather than a field itself. A cart track branched off toward a farmhouse whose windows were dark. Laundry hung on a line, motionless, as if the wind had forgotten it mid-flap.

Everything was *there*. Everything was also, somehow, waiting.

"Do you feel that?" Tam whispered. He had gone very still, his head tipped like a bird's. "It's like—the valley. When you walk through the valley and nobody's home? Like that. But bigger. Like the whole country is a house where everyone stepped out a minute ago and never came back."

Freya felt it too, and worse than he did, because she had a lifetime of comparisons to fail against. The air was breathable and tasted of nothing. The light was daylight and cast shadows that seemed reluctant. Sound carried strangely—her own footsteps on the cobbles arrived at her ears a half-beat late, as if the country had to be reminded to echo.

"Serevan said to look at everything twice," she murmured. "Come on. Twice, then."

They walked. The road descended through the orchard, and passing under the first tree Freya saw that the pale fruit was apples after all, just apples grown in a place where color had begun to be optional. A windmill stood on the next hill, its sails frozen at three-quarters turn. A dog lay in a farmyard and lifted its head as they passed, and its eyes followed them with an intelligence that made the hair rise on Freya's neck, and it did not bark, and it did not stand, and when she looked back it had lowered its head again as though even curiosity cost too much.

"How far does it go?" she asked. "The—the country. How big?"

"Big." Tam said it simply, the way children say enormous things. "Bigger than the valley. Bigger than—there were nine cities. I lived in the third-biggest. Ilvaren." He stumbled on the name and Freya realized he hadn't said it aloud since the night he'd arrived at her cottage, soaked and speaking the dead tongue. "My mother worked the second bell shift at the dye-works. You could see the river from our window. The water was blue-green. Not like here." He frowned. "It's not blue-green now. I don't think anything here is really its color anymore."

"Why not?"

He looked at her with the terrible patience children have for adults asking questions they should already know the answers to. "Because they took us off the maps," he said. "You told me. Nobody remembers us, so nobody looks at us, and if nobody looks at you—" He shrugged, a small sharp gesture older than his eight years. "You stop being looked-at. That's what Gran used to say. *We are a country that must be described to exist.*"

The words went through Freya like a splinter seeking bone. Her order had done this. Not with armies—with ledgers, with treaties, with a silence maintained for eleven generations by people who recited an oath they didn't understand in a language they weren't allowed to learn. She had spent her whole life oiling the hinge of a prison and calling it a sacred trust.

The road bent around a hillside, and the city appeared.

* * *

Ilvaren sat in a river valley the way a crown sits on a head—as if the land had grown it deliberately. Walls of pale stone, towers, a great bridge across a wide slow river, rooftops going down to the water in terraces, and rising above it all a citadel and, beside it, slender and impossibly graceful, a bell tower taller than any structure Freya had ever seen, its spire lost in the low grey-gold haze that served this country for sky.

It was beautiful. It was intact. Every roof was whole, every street lamp stood ready, and the whole of it was rendered in colors drained toward grey the way a fire drains toward ash—not ruined, not decayed, merely *fading*, a city keeping itself together through sheer habit.

And it was full of people.

Freya's breath caught. Figures moved in the streets below—walking, carrying baskets, leading a horse to water, sweeping a step. From this distance they looked alive, ordinary, a market town going about its morning. It was only as they came down the switchbacking road and through the outer gate—unguarded, its portcullis raised and rusted open—that she began to see what Tam had meant.

The people were translucent.

Not all equally. A woman selling nothing from a stall of empty baskets was nearly solid—you could see the grain of her skin, the wear on her apron. But the man beside her buying nothing was fainter, his outline wavering like heat-shimmer, and beyond them the crowd thinned by degrees into ghosts, into suggestions, into figures so faint that Freya could see the wall behind them through their chests. They went about their business anyway. A ghost swept a step that did not need sweeping. Two faded men played at a board game whose pieces had long since gone invisible, moving their hands over the squares with perfect familiarity.

None of them looked at Freya and Tam. That was the worst part. A stranger walking into your city, a child in a keeper's coat, a woman armed with a knife and forty years of duty—any living city would have turned to stare. These people glanced, registered nothing, and returned to their habits, as if newcomers were simply one more thing the country could no longer afford to notice.

"They can't see us," Tam whispered. His hand had found Freya's sleeve and gripped it. "Or—they can't *quite*. Look."

An old man had stopped at the mouth of an alley. He was staring directly at Tam—or at the space near Tam—and his faded face was working, struggling, like a swimmer fighting a current. His lips moved. No sound came. He raised one hand, translucent to the wrist, and pointed, and his finger trembled with the effort of pointing, and then whatever he had caught hold of slipped, and his arm dropped, and he turned back to his wall and resumed staring at nothing.

"He almost remembered strangers existed," Tam said. His voice cracked in the middle. "Freya. He almost—"

"I know." She pulled him close. Around them the city went on being a city the way a hearth keeps a coal alive overnight: barely, stubbornly, waiting for morning that had stopped being scheduled. "Look at me. We're going to fix this. I don't know how yet, but I swear it on the gate itself—we're going to give them back."

"You can't swear things on the gate anymore," Tam said. "You broke it."

"I'm learning to swear things on better things."

A bell rang.

It came from the great tower above the citadel—one deep, round note, rolling out over the roofs and down the streets and across the river, and every person in the square below them stopped what they were doing. The sweeper stood with her broom raised. The merchants froze mid-gesture. Even the nearly-solid woman at the stall went still, her face turning upward, and on every translucent face in sight there was, for the length of that single toll, an expression of such naked, hopeless attention that Freya's throat closed.

Waiting. They were all waiting. For the bell to mean something. For someone, somewhere, to be ringing it *to* them.

The note died. The city resumed. The sweeper swept.

"Second bell shift," Tam said quietly. "My mother's shift. Somebody still rings them. Or the bells ring themselves. I used to know which." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, briskly, the way boys do when they have decided crying is a later activity. "The palace is that way. The tall tower points at it. Everyone knows the Queen takes visitors at the grey hour, which is"—he squinted at the colorless sky—"now, probably. It's always sort of now, here."

"You've been here before," Freya said. It wasn't a question.

"I lived here." He looked up at her, and in the drained light of a forgotten capital his eyes were the only truly vivid thing in sight, brown and fierce and wet. "Before the fall through the light. Before the valley. This is my *home*, Freya. It doesn't remember me, and it's my home."

She knelt on the cobbles of a dying city and took his face in her hands, and said the only true thing available: "Then we'll make it remember. Starting with me. My name is Freya Hartwell, I was the keeper of the Hollow Gate, and I broke my oath to bring you home. Remember that, Tam. However faint this place gets—hold onto that."

He nodded. Took a shaking breath. Straightened his spine the way she'd seen him do before saying words that fit stones.

They walked toward the palace together, two living figures moving through streets of the almost-forgotten, and behind them, at the mouth of the square, the old man by the alley turned his head one more time—just slightly, just enough—and this time, when his lips moved, a sound came out. One word, in the language of the gate, in the language of the boy, in the language of a country erased from every map but its own:

"*Vessia.*"

Said like a question. Said like a prayer. Said like a man testing whether his own country's name still weighed anything at all.

Far ahead, above the citadel, the bells began the next hour, and in the highest tower of the unmapped palace, a light that had no business existing in a fading country kindled in a window, and waited.
