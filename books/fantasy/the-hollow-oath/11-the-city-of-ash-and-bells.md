## 11. The City of Ash and Bells

Tam knew the road before he could name it.

That was the strange thing about coming home to a country that wasn't supposed to exist: his feet remembered everything, and his mouth had no words for any of it. Not in the valley tongue, anyway. The words were all Vessian, rising in him like water finding old channels—the word for the bend where the road dipped toward the river, the word for the smell of wet limestone after rain, the word for *almost there* that mothers said in a certain sing-song voice when children whined on long walks.

*Mhalen,* he thought. Almost there. And then, because he couldn't stop it: *Mama.*

"Tam." Freya's hand settled on his shoulder. She'd been doing that more and more—touching him lightly, like checking that he was still real. "Is this it? Is this your city?"

He nodded. He didn't trust his voice yet.

They stood on a rise above the river plain, and below them, spread out in the grey afternoon light, lay Vessia's capital. He knew its name now—it had come back to him somewhere on the road, arriving whole one morning like a bird landing on a fencepost. *Sorrenvale.* The City of Bells. He could see the bell towers even from here, nine of them, ten, rising over the rooftops like stone flowers turned permanently upward. He could see the river curving through it, silver-grey, and the bridges with their little carved saints, and the market square with its striped awnings, and—

And nothing moved.

Not nothing-nothing. There were people down there. He could see them, small as ants from this distance, walking the streets, standing in doorways, sitting on benches by the river. But they moved the way smoke moves, or the way dust motes move in a shaft of light—slowly, softly, without any of the bustle and jostle that cities were supposed to have. No carts rattling. No dogs running. No children chasing each other between the market stalls.

No sound at all, except the bells.

The bells were ringing. Tam realized he'd been hearing them for the last mile without noticing, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. All the towers, ringing at once, deep and slow and mournful—but when he looked at the bell towers, straining to see, there was nobody in them. No ringers pulling ropes. No hands on the bells. The bronze mouths swung open and shut in the empty air, ringing for no one, rung by no one.

"They're always ringing," Tam said. His voice came out small. "Aren't they."

Freya crouched beside him, her cloak pooling grey-green around her boots. She looked tired. She'd been looking tired since they came through the gate, since the road swallowed them and spat them out into a country that the world had agreed to forget. But her eyes were steady, and she was looking at the city the way she looked at the gate's carvings—with that hungry, careful attention, like she was reading something written very faintly.

"Tell me about it," she said. "Whatever you remember. It helps me understand what we're walking into."

So Tam told her. Or tried to.

"I live—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "*Lived.* On Candlemaker Street. The house has a blue door, except my mama painted over the blue because I kept scratching it, so it's green now. There's a bakery two doors down and the baker, Master Ilvo, gives me the burnt ends of the morning loaves if I carry his flour up from the cellar. There's a fountain in our square with a fish on top that spits water, and in winter it freezes into a fish made of ice, and everyone says it looks better frozen but I don't think so because then it can't spit." He ran out of breath. "I don't know why I remember the fish so much."

"Because it mattered to you," Freya said simply. "Children remember what matters. Adults forget that."

They walked down into the city.

* * *

The streets of Sorrenvale were clean. That was the first wrong thing Tam noticed, once the wonder of *being home* wore off enough for him to actually look. Clean streets, swept cobbles, shopfronts with their shutters neatly closed or neatly open, awnings unfaded, no litter, no mud tracked in from the roads. A city that looked like someone had paused it mid-morning on a perfect day, years ago, and never pressed play again.

The people were the second wrong thing.

They weren't ghosts. Tam had heard ghost stories—a boy at school used to tell them, about the pale lady who walked the river wall—and these weren't that. They had color, mostly. They had weight; he watched a woman step around a puddle, which a ghost wouldn't bother with. But their colors were wrong somehow, muted, like cloth washed too many times. Their faces were smooth in a way faces shouldn't be, not young-smooth but *unfinished*-smooth, like a carving someone had stopped before adding the fine details. And when they spoke to each other—which they did, occasionally, in low murmurs that drifted across the street like leaves—their voices had no edges. Words went soft at the ends. Sentences trailed off into nothing, and the person they were spoken to would nod slowly, as if understanding approximately.

An old man sat outside a tavern with an unlit pipe in his hand. He'd been holding it when Tam and Freya passed on the way in. He was holding it still, twenty minutes later, when they came back up the same street. He hadn't moved. He was smiling faintly at nothing, or at something only he could see, and his eyes were the color of pond water.

"Mama said the forgetting gets worse every year," Tam whispered. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. "She said when Tam was born the city was loud. She said—" His throat closed. He tried again. "She talked about it like weather. Like something happening to us that we couldn't stop."

Freya had gone very still. "Your mother talked about the forgetting? By name?"

"She called it the Greying." The word came out of him whole, with its weight attached, and he felt Freya flinch beside him. "Everyone did. It wasn't a secret. It was just... true. Like rain being wet."

They turned onto Candlemaker Street.

Tam's heart was hammering before he saw the house. He knew it by the crooked chimney first—that ridiculous chimney, leaning east, that Papa had always promised to fix and never had—and then by the green door, and then by the window box under the front window where Mama grew thyme, except the thyme was grey now too, grey as ash, though it hadn't died. Nothing here died. It just faded, and faded, and went on.

The blue door. Green paint. Home.

"TAM!"

The voice cracked across the street like a whip, and Tam spun around so fast he nearly fell.

A woman was standing in the doorway across the lane—a real woman, or almost-real, her colors faded but her face sharp, her eyes furious and bright and wet. She was middle-aged, heavyset, flour on her apron, and she was pointing at Freya with a trembling finger.

"You," she said. Her voice had edges. Whatever was eating the color out of this city, it hadn't gotten all of her yet. "You're one of *them*. One of the keepers. I know the cloak. I know the oath-knot on the clasp. My grandmother described it to me, and her grandmother before her, and every single one of them spat when they said your order's name."

"Master Ilvo?" Tam said stupidly. "You're—you're the baker?"

The woman's fury stuttered. She looked at him—at his face, really looked—and something in her expression broke and reassembled. "Little Tam Voss," she whispered. "By the Nine Bells. Little Tam. We thought—" She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. "We thought the gate took you. We thought you were gone the way the rest of it is going."

"The gate took me *out*," Tam said. "Into the valley. I don't—I don't know how. I woke up there."

"And now you've come back." Master Ilvo's eyes slid to Freya, and the fury came back up like floodwater. "With *her*. With a keeper. Child, do you know what her people did? Do you know why the bells ring with no hands?"

"Ilvo." Freya's voice was quiet. Careful. "I'm not—my order sealed the gate generations ago. Before I was born. I've spent my whole life on the other side of it, not knowing—"

"*Not knowing.*" Ilvo laughed, and it was the worst sound Tam had ever heard, because underneath the laugh was crying trying to get out. "Of course you didn't know. That was the whole design, keeper. That was the price your founders negotiated. They sold our borders to three kingdoms and bought themselves a valley where magic couldn't follow, and the treaty required that *no one remember*. Not your order. Not the kingdoms. Not even the world's maps. Erasure all the way down, so clean that the erasers erased themselves." She stepped closer. Flour drifted off her apron and hung in the air, refusing to fall. "You want to know what your order did? Look around you. Look at the streets. Look at the *people*. Every year the world forgets us harder, and every year we fade a little more, because a country is made of being seen. My mother is a rumor in her own kitchen. The council that governed this city hasn't met in eleven years because half of them can't remember why they'd gather. And the bells—" her voice finally cracked, all the way through— "the bells are on a charter. Ring at every hour, so that Vessia can always find its own way home. Rung by the bell-keepers' guild, four hundred years unbroken. Except the guild faded six years ago, and the charter magic doesn't know how to stop, so the bells ring for guildsmen who aren't there anymore. Hour after hour. Day after day. Nobody rings them. Nobody can make them stop. Nobody remembers how."

Silence. Somewhere across the city, another tower began to toll, slow and deep, and Tam understood now that it wasn't mourning music. It was a machine, grinding on with no one left to tend it. It was the loneliest sound in the world.

Freya had taken off her hood. She stood in the middle of Candlemaker Street with her bare head bowed, the oath-knot clasp glinting dully at her throat, and Tam watched her take it in—really take it in, the grey windows and the grey thyme and the old man with the unlit pipe and the flour hanging in the air like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

"I didn't know," she said again, but softer this time, and it sounded different now. Less like a defense. More like a wound. "Ilvo, I swear to you, I didn't know. And I'm going to fix it. However I have to."

Ilvo stared at her for a long moment. Then her gaze shifted to Tam, and softened into something older and sadder than anger.

"Come inside, both of you," she said. "It's cold, and the bread won't bake itself, and if I'm going to hear how a keeper means to save us, I'll hear it sitting down." She paused in her doorway, hand on the frame, and added, almost too quietly to hear:

"And you should see your mother's house, Tam. Before you decide anything. Some things the Greying takes slow. Some things it takes all at once."

* * *

Inside the bakery it was warm, and that was its own kind of heartbreak—warmth with no fire lit, warmth left over from some morning years ago that the room refused to give up. Ilvo moved through it briskly, setting out cups, cutting dark bread, and the ordinary motions seemed to make her colors sit a little straighter, as if purpose was a kind of dye.

Tam sat at the big table with his heels hooked on the chair rungs and ate the bread and tried not to cry, because the bread tasted exactly right, exactly like burnt-end loaves and cellar flour and every ordinary afternoon of his old life, and taste, apparently, was the last thing to go.

Freya didn't eat. She sat with her hands wrapped around a cup she didn't drink from, and asked questions—careful ones, gentle ones—and Ilvo answered, and piece by piece Tam heard the shape of his country's dying.

The Greying had started small, Ilvo said. A merchant whose name slipped out of trade ledgers. A street that appeared on no new map. Then faster, as the treaties took hold beyond the border—as the kingdoms that had purchased Vessia's ground sent surveyors and clerks to rewrite the land, and every rewritten line pulled a little more of Vessia out of the world's memory. The Compact, she called them, and said the word like spitting. *The Border Compact.* They had agents still, she said. Grey livery. Instruments that hummed. They came through the passes sometimes, checking that the erasure held, and lately they'd been coming more often.

"Lately," Freya repeated. "Since when?"

"Since the gate started waking." Ilvo looked at Tam. "Since a child crossed it."

Tam put down his bread.

Outside, across the whole grey city, the bells began to toll the hour—all nine towers, ten, twelve, ringing in their slow mournful round for guildsmen two hundred years faded, for a country the world had signed away, for a boy sitting in a warm bakery eating bread that tasted like home.

And beneath the bells, far off toward the eastern pass, Tam heard—or thought he heard, or maybe more than thought—another sound. Fainter. Rhythmic. Like hooves.

Ilvo heard it too. She went still, cup halfway to her mouth, and her eyes found Freya's across the table.

"Grey riders," she said. "In the city. They've never dared come into the city before."
