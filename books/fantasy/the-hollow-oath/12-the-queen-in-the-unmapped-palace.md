## 12. The Queen in the Unmapped Palace

The palace had no address. That was the first thing Freya understood about it, standing at the foot of a hill that no cartographer had ever troubled to draw: a country erased does not lose its buildings, only its coordinates. The palace of Vessia rose above the city of ash and bells like a thought someone had tried very hard to stop thinking and failed, its spires pale as bone left in milk, its windows catching a light that came from everywhere and nowhere at once.

They climbed the hill on a road paved with white stone worn soft at the edges, and Freya noticed that her own footsteps made less sound the higher they went. Not silence—she could hear Tam breathing beside her, hear the small wet click of his throat when he swallowed—but her boots on the stone produced nothing, as if the palace grounds had decided long ago which things deserved to be heard and which should simply be endured.

Tam walked close to her side. He had not let go of her hand since the city square, where an old woman had looked at him with eyes gone grey as dishwater and said something in Vessian that made him flinch. He hadn't told Freya what it was. She suspected she didn't want to know yet.

*She called me by my mother's name,* he'd whispered later, walking fast, gripping her fingers hard enough to hurt. *She thought I was her.*

Freya had no answer for that. She was beginning to suspect this whole country would be a series of questions she had no answers for.

At the top of the road stood gates of black iron worked into shapes she recognized with a lurch—the same interlocking pattern as the carvings on the Hollow Gate, the same script she had spent thirty years polishing without reading. Here, though, the letters were not decorative. They were load-bearing. They held the gates shut, or perhaps they held the gates open; from the way the iron shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone, Freya couldn't tell which.

A figure waited between them.

It took her a moment to understand what she was seeing, because her eyes kept trying to correct it, the way they corrected a face seen through warped glass. A woman stood there in a gown the color of deep water, silver-threaded at the cuffs, and she was beautiful in the way old portraits are beautiful—all fine bones and deliberate stillness—and she was also, unmistakably, fading. Her edges did not quite agree with her center. When she turned her head, the turn arrived a half-instant late, as though her body were remembering to move rather than moving. Where light touched her hair, it passed through more than it illuminated.

"You are the keeper," the woman said. Her voice was clear, unhurried, and carried the particular weariness of someone who has rehearsed a speech for decades and finally gets to deliver it. "Freya Hartwell. Last of the Order of the Hollow Oath."

"I'm the last keeper," Freya said carefully. "I don't know about the order."

"The order ended when your mentor died. You are what remained." The woman's gaze moved past Freya's shoulder, down the hill, toward the city, and something moved across her face too quickly to name. "And you brought him."

"This is Tam," Freya said, and stepped slightly sideways—not much, just enough, an instinct older than any oath—to put herself between the queen and the boy.

Queen Ilsevan of Vessia looked at Freya for a long moment, and then she smiled, and it was not a cruel smile. That was somehow worse. It was the smile of someone who has been patient so long that patience has become a kind of hunger.

"You protect him," the queen said. "Good. Someone in your order should have learned how."

* * *

The throne room was not what Freya expected, because she had expected a throne room, and instead she found a hall of names.

The walls were covered in them—thousands upon thousands, written in ink and chalk and scratched into plaster, some fresh enough to glisten, others so old they had sunk into the stone itself like fossils. They climbed from floor to vaulted ceiling in columns and spirals and careful rows, and here and there gaps yawned in the record, patches of wall scraped blank or simply faded to nothing, and Freya felt her stomach tighten as she understood what those gaps meant. Names that had been forgotten so completely that even this place, built to hold them, had lost its grip.

In the center of the hall, where a throne should have been, there was only a chair. Plain wood, a farmer's chair, unadorned. Queen Ilsevan sat in it the way other monarchs sit on gold—as if it were the most important object in the world.

"Sit," she said, gesturing to two chairs set opposite hers. They were ordinary chairs too. Everything in this hall was ordinary except the walls, and the walls were everything.

Tam sat close against Freya's side, their chairs touching. His eyes kept going to the names. His lips moved sometimes, silently, reading.

"You read," the queen observed gently.

"Some," Tam said. "Mama taught me. Before—" He stopped.

"Before the fall through the light," Ilsevan finished softly. "Yes. We know how he came to you, keeper. We have known since the day he crossed. Do you imagine the border sealed us out entirely? Your gate is a wound in us. We feel every draft."

Freya leaned forward. "Then tell me what I've spent my life guarding. Because I've read my order's records. I've read the oaths and the seals and the rituals, and none of it says *kingdom*. None of it says *country*. It says magic. It says containment. It says the valley must stay hollow."

"And your order wrote those words," Ilsevan said, "in the same century they sold us."

The word landed in the hall like a dropped bell. Tam's hand found Freya's sleeve.

"Sold," Freya repeated.

"Your founders were not wardens, keeper. They were brokers." The queen rose, and her movement had that strange delayed quality again, grief performed by a body running out of substance. She walked to the wall of names and laid one translucent finger against a column of writing. "Three hundred years ago, Vessia stood where your valley stands. Our borders ran from the Greywater to the fells. We were not a great power. We were a *specific* one—weavers, bell-founders, speakers of a language the stones themselves liked to hear. And our neighbors wanted our land without our people in it, because people make land difficult. So they approached your order—you were then not keepers but a guild of boundary-masons, respected, ambitious, and poor."

"We weren't poor," Freya said automatically. "The order has holdings. We always—"

"Because of us." Ilsevan turned. The light went through her cheekbones. "Every coin your order has ever held was payment. They took our treaty, they took our borders, they drew a circle around the heart of Vessia and swore an oath into it—a hollow oath, we called it, because it sounded like protection and meant erasure. Magic cannot enter the valley, they promised. What they did not say aloud, what they carved only where they thought no one would ever read it, is that *we* are what your valley's magic was. The gate doesn't keep sorcery out, keeper. It keeps *Vessia* in. It cut a country out of the world and closed the door."

The hall seemed to lean in. Somewhere far below, Freya realized, the bells of the city were ringing, rung by no one, marking hours for a population that grew thinner every year.

"Erasure," Freya said slowly. "You're saying the Compact didn't conquer you. They—"

"They agreed to forget us. All of them, jointly, in perpetuity. Maps redrawn, records burned, treaties signed binding each nation to deny the others' denial. It is the most thorough war ever waged, and not one battle was fought." Ilsevan's voice did not rise. It grew quieter and quieter and somehow more enormous. "And your order held the pen."

Tam made a small sound. Freya looked down. He was staring at a section of wall near the floor, low, child-height, and there was a name there in a careful schoolroom hand: *Maren Voss.* Below it, smaller: *her son, Tam.*

"Is that—" he started.

"Yes," the queen said, and for the first time her voice cracked along its faded edge. "Your mother registered you both when you were born. Every citizen of Vessia is written here. It is how we know who we are. It is why we do not vanish all at once." She looked at the boy with an expression Freya recognized, horribly, from her own mirror—the look of someone carrying a duty that has eaten everything else. "You are the last entry, Tam Voss. The newest name in a three-hundred-year ledger. And the world outside has been trying to erase you since before you could walk."

* * *

Later—she would never be sure how much later; time in the unmapped palace moved like water finding its level—servants brought food that tasted almost real, and Freya found herself alone with the queen on a balcony overlooking the grey city.

Almost alone. Tam had fallen asleep in his chair, exhausted, one hand still resting on the wall beneath his mother's name, and a faded attendant had draped a blanket over him that Freya suspected had not existed an hour ago. The palace provided. The palace was running out of things to provide.

"You hate us," Freya said. It wasn't a question.

"I pity you," Ilsevan answered. "Hate requires believing someone had a choice. Your order's founders chose. You inherited a locked room and were told it was a lighthouse." She folded her hands—Freya watched the balcony rail show faintly through her fingers and had to look away. "But yes. There is anger. Three hundred years of watching my citizens thin out like breath on glass. Do you know what forgetting does, keeper? It is not dramatic. No one screams. A baker stops being remembered by his customers, and so he bakes less, and so he fades a little, and his wife holds his hand and feels it grow lighter, and she does not scream either. She grows quiet. The children go first—they need to be named most often. Then the old. My court is a hall of nearly-empty coats." She smiled again, that patient, terrible smile. "I am the last who can afford to fade slowly, because a country needs its queen until the very end. Even if the end is a formality."

"Why am I here?" Freya asked. "Really. You could have sent anyone to fetch the boy. Why open the door for *me*?"

"Because you are the lock, Freya Hartwell." The queen turned to face her fully, and up close her eyes were extraordinary—dark, fierce, entirely present, islands of the unfaded. "The gate answers your blood and your oath. It opened a crack for the boy because cracks are what wounds make. But it will open *fully* for its keeper. Only for its keeper."

"Open it to do what?"

"To give us back." Ilsevan stepped closer, and her voice dropped into registers Freya felt in her sternum. "Here is justice, keeper, offered plainly, because I am done with plainness being mistaken for weakness. First: the names. Every founder who signed the betrayal, every mason who cut the seals, every elder who kept the secret and let generations tend a prison believing it was a lamp—I will give you their names, their deeds, the full record your order burned on our side of the border. Your order owes you the truth. I can pay that debt."

Freya's breath caught. Thirty years of reciting an oath she didn't understand. Thirty years of her mentor's evasions, the sealed correspondence, the polite deflections. *The full record.*

"Second: restoration. Open the gate fully. Let Vessia reclaim its borders—the borders that were ours, that were drawn away from us by treaty and theft. The valley returns to the map. My people return to the world. The Compact's erasure is undone, and every nation that profited from our disappearance learns what it costs to unmake a country." She paused, and her fading hands trembled, just slightly, with something that was not weakness. "You would not merely be forgiven, keeper. You would be the one who ended the longest injustice in recorded history. Your order's shame, redeemed by its last daughter. Is that not worth a door?"

The balcony was very quiet. Below, the bells rang the hour, and Freya counted them out of habit—six bells, six strokes, the same count she'd kept every evening of her adult life at the cottage window—and the familiarity of it cracked something open in her chest.

She wanted to say yes. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that shamed her: how easy it would have been. A righteous cause is the most seductive thing in the world precisely because it asks you to be good while it spends whatever it needs. And here was a queen made of grief offering her truth, redemption, and justice in a single breath, and every part of Freya that had spent a lifetime alone with an inexplicable duty *ached* to take it.

"It sounds right," Freya said quietly. "That's what frightens me. It sounds exactly right."

"It is right," Ilsevan said. "Rightness is not the trap, keeper. Ask instead what the rightness costs, and who pays." She glanced back through the doorway at the sleeping boy, at the wall of names behind him. "But I see you already know how to ask. Good. Your founders never learned."

Freya looked at her hands—keeper's hands, scarred from seal-work, steady on the worst nights of her life—and made herself ask the question forming at the bottom of her dread, the one she had been circling since the throne room.

"When the gate opens fully," she said. "When Vessia reclaims its borders. What happens to the valley? To the people living there now? Wren. The elders. The families in the village. The ones who never signed anything, who were born inside your stolen ground the way Tam was born inside your erased country."

Ilsevan was silent for a moment. Beyond her shoulder, the light passed through her like she was a window dressed in a queen's shape.

"The valley stands on Vessian soil," she said at last. "Borders must be restored to their true lines. Some few may choose to leave. Most—" and here her voice gentled into something almost kind, which was the most frightening tone it had held all day, "—most will simply be *corrected*, keeper. As we were. The wheel turns; the debt is paid. Justice is not gentle. It is only just."

Somewhere below them, in the sleeping city, a bell rang a single stroke out of hour—unwitnessed, unanswered—and Freya thought of Wren's workshop, of the smell of cut stone, of the valley's green morning light, and felt the seduction in her chest go cold and start to calcify into something harder and more useful.

Doubt.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

The queen regarded her for a long, long moment, and the fierce dark eyes held no malice at all—which was, Freya was beginning to understand, the most dangerous thing about her.

"Then I will open the gate myself," Ilsevan said. "I have waited three hundred years, keeper. I have buried ten thousand of my people into silence. I am nearly transparent, and my patience is the last solid thing I own." She stepped back, and the balcony light bent around her vanishing edges. "I would rather it be your hand. But it will be a hand."

Behind them, in the hall of names, Tam slept on beneath his mother's handwriting, and the newest entry in a three-hundred-year ledger breathed slow and small, unaware that two women had just begun deciding what his country would cost.
