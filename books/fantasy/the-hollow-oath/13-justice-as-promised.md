## 13. Justice, As Promised

The queen did not take her to the archive.

That was the first thing Freya noticed, and it unsettled her more than anything else in the unmapped palace had. A record of that weight—a betrayal four generations deep, sealed and hidden by her own order—surely deserved a vault, a guarded stair, a door with locks upon locks. Instead Ilsevan led her down a corridor hung with grey silk, through a chamber where courtiers stood so still they might have been furniture, and stopped before a plain wall of pale stone.

"The keepers built their cottage above our granary," the queen said. "Did you know that?"

"I didn't."

"No one knows anything anymore. That was rather the point." She pressed her palm flat against the wall, and for a moment Freya saw the effort it cost her—the queen's fingers were translucent at the tips, light passing through them like water through glass—and then the stone remembered itself. Seams appeared. A door swung inward on hinges that had not moved in decades and made no complaint about it.

Cold air breathed out, smelling of paper and dust and something faintly sweet, like apples left too long in a cellar.

"This is not an archive," Ilsevan said. "Archives are for things a country wants to remember. This is a wound we kept dressed. Come."

* * *

The room inside was small—smaller than Freya's own cottage, though its ceiling arched high enough to suggest it had once been part of something grander. Shelves climbed every wall, but they held no books. They held boxes. Hundreds of them, thousands perhaps, each of pale wood, each no larger than a loaf of bread, each stamped with a seal Freya recognized with a lurch that turned her stomach: the broken-circle sigil of the Keepers of the Hollow Gate.

Her order's mark. On every box. Row upon row upon row.

"What is this?" Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Correspondence," said the queen. "Ledgers. Contracts. The ordinary paperwork of an extraordinary crime." She lifted one box from the shelf as if it weighed nothing, which perhaps to her it did not, and set it on the table at the room's center. "Your founders were meticulous, Keeper Hartwell. I will grant them that. If you are going to sell a country, it is best to keep receipts."

Freya stood very still. The cold air moved against her neck. Somewhere above them, muffled by floors she could not count, a bell rang once—Ondrey's bell, or whatever remained of Ondrey to ring it—and the sound settled over the silence like snow.

"Open it," the queen said. "It should be your hand, not mine. Justice reads better in the guilty party's script."

"I'm not guilty of anything."

"No?" Ilsevan's smile was thin and old. "Then you have nothing to fear from paper."

Freya opened the box.

The documents inside were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth had done its work; the pages within were supple still, the ink dark, the hands that had written them confident. She unfolded the first sheet and found herself looking at terms of trade rendered in beautiful copperplate: *In consideration of certain lands contiguous to the Vessian marches, hereinafter described, the undersigned parties agree...*

She read the description of the lands.

She read it three times.

It was the valley. Every word of it. The river bend, the two ridges, the hollow where the gate stood—all of it named, measured, priced. Not as a place where anyone lived, though people had lived there for centuries before the keepers came. As a purchase. A parcel. An acquisition with conditions attached.

"There is a clause," the queen said softly, watching her read, "about the inhabitants. Find it. It is my favorite."

Freya's eyes went down the page. The copperplate marched on, indifferent, elegant. *The said inhabitants being of no account to the parties herein, save as tenants-at-will under the supervision of the Order of the Hollow Gate, whose duty shall be the maintenance of the border-seal in perpetuity, in exchange for which the parties herein cede to the Order the valley entire, its rents, its tithes, and its quiet.*

*Its quiet.*

"They paid you in silence," the queen said. "Do you understand? The rival kingdoms wanted Vessia's borders—our passes, our ports, our mines—but they could not agree how to divide us without war. So your founders offered a solution worthy of poets. Unmake the country. Not conquer it—conquest leaves memories, martyrs, claims that fester. Erase it. Seal the border with magic so complete that the world forgets there was ever anything to divide. And in exchange—" she gestured at the walls, at the hundreds of boxes, "—in exchange for this. The valley. A pocket of the world where their seal could be anchored and maintained, bought from kingdoms who were only ever selling what they had already stolen."

Freya set the page down. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she would have expected them to shake. But the rage that was rising in her did not rattle—it compressed, like ice forming, dense and clear and terribly cold.

"My order protected the valley," she said. "For four generations. We tended farms. We kept the passes safe. We—"

"You kept the lock," Ilsevan said. "Yes. And the lock-keeper never asks what the lock is for, does she? Only that it hold." The queen drew out another document, laid it beside the first. "Here. The oath itself. Your oath, Keeper. Read the original, not the version they taught you to recite."

Freya knew the oath. She had recited it every morning of her life since she was seven years old, kneeling on the gate's threshold stones while Maren stood behind her with a hand on her shoulder: *We keep the hollow. We hold the line. What lies beyond stays beyond, and what lies within stays quiet.* It had always struck her, even as a child, as oddly incomplete—a vow missing its middle, like a bridge with planks pried up.

The original was not incomplete. It was worse.

*We, the undersigned, having accepted in perpetuity the valley of the Hollow Gate and all its appurtenances, do swear before the Compact Powers that no magic of Vessia shall cross the border-seal, no citizen of Vessia shall pass into the world of nations, and no memory of Vessia shall be suffered to persist beyond the seal. We accept this charge knowing its cost and consenting to it. In witness whereof, we sign.*

Below, five signatures. Five names.

Freya read them twice, because the first time her eyes slid off them as if the ink were wet. Aldous Crane. Mirabel Voss. Henric Dole. Petra Sallow. And the fifth—

The fifth name was Hartwell.

"That is impossible," she heard herself say.

"It is entirely possible," said the queen. "It is, in fact, the single most possible thing in this room. Edric Hartwell. First keeper. The man who wrote the treaty, negotiated the price, and signed first among equals. Your founders did not stumble into this crime, Keeper. One of them authored it."

* * *

Freya sat down. She did not remember deciding to sit; her legs simply ended their argument with her. The chair was old and grey and held her weight without comment, and she sat in it in the middle of a dead country's wound-room and stared at a name she had grown up revering.

There was a carving of Edric Hartwell in the keeper's cottage—or there had been, when Maren was alive. A small bas-relief above the hearth, a stern-faced man holding a lantern. Freya had polished it every season. She had spoken to it sometimes, in the lonely years after Maren died, the way one speaks to a portrait of a grandfather: *I kept the seals today. The third one is wearing thin. I wish you'd left me better notes.*

Better notes. He had left her treaties.

"How many of these are there?" she asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"Boxes? Nine hundred and forty. Pages? I have never counted. The Compact required documentation of everything—every border ceded, every village reassigned, every name struck from every register in every neighboring kingdom. Erasure on that scale generates an astonishing quantity of paper." Ilsevan moved along the shelves, trailing translucent fingers across the wood without touching it. "They were proud of it, your founders. Look at the margins. Initials. Little flourishes. Mirabel Voss doodled flowers beside the clause that unmade six thousand people. Six thousand people, and she drew a violet next to it."

"Stop."

"Why? You asked to understand. Understanding is what you keepers claim to traffic in."

"I said stop." Freya was on her feet. She did not remember standing either. The chair scraped back and the sound cracked through the little room like a shot, and the rage that had been compressing in her all this time finally found its shape—not hot, not roaring, but absolute, a cold architecture of fury with her own name carved into its keystone. "You want me to sit here and admire the penmanship of monsters. You want me to read six thousand deaths in copperplate and appreciate the irony. Well, I've read it. I've read all of it. My order sold a country. My ancestor wrote the bill of sale. The valley I have given my whole life to was the *payment*, do you understand? Not the prize. The payment. Everything I am, everything I was trained to be, every dawn I spent checking those seals—I was servicing a debt. I was the interest on a stolen kingdom."

"Yes," said the queen.

"And you—" Freya crossed the room until she stood close enough to see the grain of the fading queen's skin, the way the candlelight passed through her cheekbone like light through fogged glass. "You brought me here to show me this, and every word out of your mouth has been chosen. The receipt. The violet. My ancestor's signature. You are not showing me a wound, Majesty. You are sharpening me. You want my rage pointed at the Compact, at the world, at anyone but you—because a keeper who understands what was done will agree to almost anything to undo it. Isn't that right?"

For a long moment the queen said nothing. Then she laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering motion—and something in her face shifted, some mask of courtly patience slipping just far enough for Freya to see what was beneath. Grief. Enormous, geological grief, the kind that takes centuries to accumulate.

"You mistake me," Ilsevan said quietly. "I do not need to sharpen you. You arrived already edged. You checked those seals every morning for years, did you not? And some part of you—some small, honest part—always knew the oath was wrong. Why else would it have felt like a cage? Why else did you open Maren's letters the moment the boy spoke the old tongue?" She tilted her head. "I am not sharpening you, Keeper Hartwell. I am simply refusing to let you look away. There is a difference, and it matters."

Freya's breath came hard. The rage had nowhere to go—that was the truth of it, the ugly, helpless truth. She could not strike the queen; the queen was half-shadow and offering exactly the truth Freya had crossed a forbidden border to find. She could not strike the founders; they were four generations of dust. She could not even strike the paper, though her hands ached to tear it, because the paper was the last proof that Vessia had existed at all, and burning it would finish the Compact's work for free.

Rage with nowhere safe to put it. So it stayed in her, filling her like floodwater fills a cellar, pressing against every wall of her.

"Say what you actually want," she said at last, when she could trust her voice. "You didn't bring me down here for history lessons."

"No," the queen agreed. "I brought you here because justice requires a witness. The Compact erased us in secret, behind seals, in language designed to be forgotten. Someone from the other side must stand in this room and *know*. That is you, Freya Hartwell. Last keeper of the Hollow Gate, blood of Edric Hartwell. You are the witness. And when you have witnessed enough—" the fading queen smiled, and it was the saddest thing Freya had seen since Tam fell through the light, "—then we will speak of restoration. Of borders reclaimed. Of what your valley owes."

The words *what your valley owes* landed in Freya's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard the splash, but not the bottom.

Above them, distantly, the bell rang again—and this time it did not ring alone. Underneath its tone, faint and wrong, came a second sound: hooves, or what might have been hooves, on ground that should have been empty. The queen's head lifted. For the first time since Freya had met her, Ilsevan looked afraid.

"They've found the seam," the queen said. "Grey riders, in my country. Come, Keeper. There is a house on Bellmaker's Lane I need you to see before they burn what's left of it—and there is a boy waiting there who deserves to see it too."

*Tam.* The floodwater in Freya's chest turned to ice in a heartbeat. She grabbed one document from the table—Edric Hartwell's signature uppermost, proof she would not leave behind—and followed the queen out of the wound-room at a pace that was very nearly running, while somewhere ahead, down corridors of grey silk, the bells of Vessia began to ring in earnest.
