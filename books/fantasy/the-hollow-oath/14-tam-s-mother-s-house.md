## 14. Tam's Mother's House

The city had a smell, and the smell was his mother's bread.

That was the first thing Tam knew for certain, standing in the grey street with Freya's hand heavy on his shoulder: underneath the ash-dust and the bell-sound that came from nowhere and everywhere, under the wrongness of a city where nobody's shadow moved quite right, there was the smell of rye and honey and the crust that burned a little on one side because Mama always turned her back on the oven at the wrong moment to sing to him.

"Bread," he said out loud, because saying it made it real.

Freya crouched beside him. In this light she looked older than she did in the valley, or maybe just more tired, the way a person looks when they've been walking through someone else's grief all day. "You know where we are?"

He did. That was the terrible part. He didn't remember arriving in the valley, didn't remember falling through the light, couldn't have told anyone his own street name if you'd asked him yesterday — but now that he stood in it, his feet knew. His feet knew the way the cobblestones dipped near the corner where rain always pooled, knew the three steps up to the blue door, knew that the fourth step was loose and you had to skip it or it clacked like a tongue.

"Left at the fountain," he whispered. "Then straight."

The fountain wasn't running. It stood in the middle of the square like a stone flower gone to seed, dry as old bone, and around its rim sat people who were not quite sitting there. Tam had seen them since they came through the gate — the citizens of Vessia, moving slow and pale, their edges soft like chalk drawings left out in the rain. Some of them turned to look at him as he passed. Their eyes were the worst part. Not empty. *Fading.* Like eyes seen through fogged glass, like they were trying to remember what they were looking at even while they looked.

One woman reached toward him as he went by. Her fingers passed an inch from his sleeve and stopped, as if touching him would cost her something she couldn't spare.

"Tam." Freya's voice was low. "We don't have to do this today."

"Yes we do." He heard how small his voice came out. He made it bigger. "Yes. We do."

* * *

The blue door was still blue.

That undid him more than anything else so far. The whole city was drained of color, washed grey as dishwater, but the door held on — a faded, stubborn, tired blue, the blue of a sky at the very end of evening, and Tam put his hand flat against it and felt the wood grain under his palm and started to cry without deciding to.

He hadn't knocked yet. He was afraid to knock. Because doors could be answered, and if nobody answered, then—

"Tam." Freya knelt so their faces were level. She had done that a lot since the valley, he realized. Grown-ups mostly talked down at him, over him, around him. Freya talked *across*, like across a table, like two people sitting down together. "Whatever's behind this door, it isn't your fault. Whatever happened — the fall, the leaving, all of it — you were eight years old. You were a boy. Boys get carried by rivers sometimes. By bigger things than rivers."

"What if she doesn't know me?"

He hadn't meant to say it. It came out of the place where the bad thoughts lived, the cellar under his ribs.

Freya was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had a crack in it she tried to smooth over and couldn't. "Then we'll knock again tomorrow. And the day after. I'm not going anywhere, Tam. I crossed a whole impossible country to stand on this step with you. I can stand here a while longer."

He wiped his face with his sleeve. It came away grey with ash, which somehow made him laugh, a wet broken little sound, and Freya smiled like the laughing mattered more than the crying.

He knocked.

Once. Twice. The sound went into the house and didn't come back out, swallowed whole, and for a long moment there was nothing, and Tam's heart climbed up into his throat and sat there like a stone.

Then, from inside: footsteps. Slow ones. The kind of footsteps that belong to someone who has forgotten why they walk from room to room but keeps doing it anyway.

The latch lifted.

* * *

She was thinner than he remembered. That was the first thing. The second thing was that she was *less* than he remembered — not smaller exactly, but fainter, the way the moon is fainter in the morning, still there but losing the argument with the daylight. Her hair was the same dark brown, or had been once; now it was brown the way a photograph is brown, brown-as-memory rather than brown-as-hair. Her dress was the green one with the mended elbow. She used to sew by the window while he practiced his letters, and she'd mended that elbow four times because he kept grabbing her sleeve when he wanted her to look at something.

"Mama," Tam said.

His mother looked at him.

And he watched it happen — watched her try to find him behind her eyes and fail. Her gaze slid off his face like water off wax. She frowned, gentle and puzzled, the frown she wore when he'd asked her a hard question about where the sun went at night.

"I'm sorry," she said. Her voice was right. That was the cruelest thing of all; everything about her was right except her. "Little one, are you lost? The streets aren't safe for—"

"You told me they weren't safe," Tam said fast, the words tumbling. "You said — you said never go past the tanner's lane after the bells, because the fog comes off the river and it takes the shapes of people you love, and you said if I ever saw you somewhere I shouldn't, I should run home and count to a hundred and you'd come. Mama, I counted. I counted to a hundred so many times. A thousand times. More."

Her head tilted. Something flickered behind her eyes — a candle in a draft, guttering, catching, guttering.

"That's..." Her hand rose halfway to her mouth and stopped. "That's a strange thing for a stranger to know."

"It's not strange. It's *ours*. You sang the counting song. You made it up, it doesn't rhyme properly, Papa always laughed at it—" He stopped. The name *Papa* hit something in him and he flinched from it, stepped back from it like from a hot stove. Later. Later for that. One heartbreak at a time. "Mama. It's Tam. It's your Tam. Your Tam-Tam, who hated that name, who hid your thimble in the flour jar that time, who broke the blue cup and blamed the cat and you *knew*, you always knew, and you never said, you just bought a new cup and never let the cat sit on the table again because you said cats shouldn't be blamed for boys' sins—"

"*Tam.*"

It came out of her like a cough, like a sneeze, like something the body does before the mind agrees to it. And then — Tam would remember this for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be — the fading *stopped*. Right there on the doorstep, in the grey light, he watched the color come back into his mother like water soaking upward through cloth. Brown flooded her hair from the roots. Pink touched her cheeks. Her eyes found his face and *stayed*, locked on, hungry, and her hand shot out and grabbed the front of his shirt with a grip that hurt and he never wanted her to let go.

"TAM." Louder now, cracked now, and she pulled him against her and folded around him and she was warm, she was *warm*, she smelled of bread and lavender soap and the particular smoke of their own chimney, and Tam buried his face in her dress and sobbed like the eight-year-old he was, all the cleverness and the courage and the dead-tongue words gone, nothing left but a boy holding his mother. "My baby. My baby, my baby, where — how — the tower fell, the light came, everyone said — I looked, I looked *everywhere*—"

"The valley," he got out. "I don't know how. There was light and then there was grass and a lady found me and she has an oath and a gate and she talks funny and she's *here*, Mama, she came with me—"

He twisted to point and his stomach dropped.

Freya was standing at the bottom of the steps, and she was crying too, silently, both hands pressed over her mouth, and behind her — Tam saw it clearly, though he wished he hadn't — the street had grown *dimmer*. The fountain. The seated citizens. The grey windows. As if his mother's returning color had been drawn out of everything around her, a candle relit by borrowing flame from every other candle on the street.

His mother saw it too. He felt her feel it — felt the joy in her stutter like a skipped heartbeat.

"How long," she whispered. Her arms were still around him but her voice had changed, gone thin and careful. "Maren. How long has it been?"

"A year," Tam said. "Maybe more. I don't — time is different there. There's no bells."

"No bells." She laughed, and it was the saddest sound he'd ever heard. "No. No, there wouldn't be."

* * *

Inside, the house was exactly as he'd left it, and that was almost worse than if it had changed. His wooden soldiers stood in their battle lines on the shelf, mid-war, frozen for a year. His cup — the new blue cup, the replacement cup — sat by the sink with a ring of dust in its bottom. On the table lay a half-finished letter, the ink gone brownish-grey, and Tam leaned over it before his mother could stop him.

*To whoever may still keep records,* it began, in his mother's careful hand. *My name is Maren Solvane, of Candlemaker's Row, and my son is Tam Solvane, aged seven, and he was in the bell tower when the light came. If any list exists of the taken, I ask to be told only this: whether his name is written. Whether anyone remembers—*

The sentence stopped. The ink trailed into a scratch, then nothing.

"You wrote lists," his mother said from the doorway. She'd wrapped her arms around herself, and already — he could see it, he *hated* that he could see it — her edges had begun to soften again, the pink draining from her cheeks like tide going out. "After the first month. Everyone did. We wrote our names and our children's names and pinned them to the doors, because we thought — we thought if the world forgot us, maybe the *doors* wouldn't. Maybe paper would hold." She looked at the walls, at the grey seeping back into them. "Paper didn't hold either."

"Mama, you're fading again." His voice cracked on it. "You were *better*. When I said your name you were better. Why does it go away?"

"Because remembering costs, little love." She crossed the room and knelt and took his face in her hands, and her hands were cooler than they should have been. "Everything here costs. The queen says it's the border, the seal, the theft — she says the world took our name and so the world forgets us, and forgetting eats a person from the outside in. But some of us think it's simpler and worse than that." Her thumb traced his cheekbone, memorizing it. "We think a person is only as real as they are remembered. And there's no one left to remember us. Except—"

"Except me," Tam said.

"Except you." She smiled, and it was his mother's smile, the real one, the one with the crooked tooth, and it hurt more than anything. "My Tam. Who speaks the old tongue without ever being taught. Do you know what you are? Do you understand what you carry in your mouth?"

"He's cold and hungry," said Freya from the doorway, "and he's a child, and whatever he carries, it can wait until he's eaten."

Tam's mother looked up at her, and something passed between the two women that Tam couldn't read — a long look, measuring, wary on one side and steady on the other.

"You're the keeper," his mother said slowly. "From beyond the seal. I know the face. Every child here learns the keeper's face from the warning songs. *Grey coat, iron ring, the door that must not swing.*" She rose, drawing Tam behind her, and though her body was faint as morning mist, her spine was straight as a bell-pole. "Why have you brought him back to us? To take his measure? To finish what your founders started?"

"To feed him," Freya said simply. "And then to help him. And then—" She stopped. Tam watched her choose honesty over comfort, watched it cost her. "I don't know what comes after that. But I know I'm not your enemy, Maren Solvane. I've spent my whole life guarding a door I was told kept magic out. I've learned recently it kept a *people* out instead. I'm here because your son knocked on my door, and no one who knocks on mine gets turned away. That's the only oath I've got that I trust anymore."

The silence stretched. Outside, the bells rang again — from no tower, from no rope, from nowhere at all, the way they had rung since Tam arrived, a sound like a country calling its own name and hearing no answer.

Then his mother's shoulders came down, just a little, and she stepped aside from the doorway.

"There's bread," she said. "It will be stale. Nothing stays fresh here. Nothing stays anything here." She managed the ghost of her old wry tone. "But I'll heat the oven. I remember how. I make myself remember how every single day, because the day I forget the oven is the day I'm only a shape in a house."

While she worked — and Tam watched her move through the kitchen, relearning her own hands, pausing over the flour jar as if it had spoken to her — Freya crouched next to him by the table and lowered her voice.

"Tam. When you said her name, she came back. Fully. Color, warmth, all of it." Freya's eyes were bright and fierce and frightened all at once. "Do you understand what that means? You're not just the last speaker of the dead tongue. You're the last person alive who *remembers* these people the way family remembers. You might be the most important person in this entire country."

"I don't want to be important," Tam said. "I want my mama to stay."

Freya didn't answer right away. Through the window, far off across the grey rooftops, Tam could see the palace hill, and on it a pale banner stirring in a wind he couldn't feel, and beneath it, tiny with distance, figures gathering — courtiers, soldiers, and at their center a shape so faint it was nearly a hole cut in the shape of a woman.

Queen Ilsevan's court, assembling. And they had been looking, Tam realized, not at the city.

At his mother's house. At him.

"Tam," Freya said quietly, following his gaze. "I think we need to see the queen. Today. Before she decides to come see us."

Behind them, the oven door clanged shut, and the smell of warming bread began, at last, to fill the house.
