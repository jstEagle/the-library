## 15. The Compact's Envoys

They came through the gate at dawn, and the light they brought with them was the wrong kind.

Freya knew it before she saw them—knew it in her teeth, in the old ache of the keeper's scar along her palm where she had pressed it to the seals every morning for eleven years. The gate had groaned in the night, a long low sound like a ship's timbers, and Tam had slept through it curled on the floor of the bell-keeper's stairwell while Freya sat awake listening to the country around them breathe its thin, grey breath.

Now she stood in the shadow of the archive's outer colonnade, three streets from the Palace of Unmapped Stone, and watched six riders in grey come down the Vessian road as though the road had been expecting them all along.

That was the horror of it, she thought. Not that they had crossed. That the crossing looked *permitted*.

The surveyors' instruments rode in leather cases slung across their saddles, and even from this distance Freya could hear them—a hum like a struck tuning fork that never quite finished ringing. She had heard that hum once before, in the valley, when the man called Corvess had laid his brass rings against the Hollow Gate's stones and smiled at the readings. She had thought then that he was measuring. She understood now that he had been *counting*. Counting what remained. Counting what was left to take.

"They're not supposed to be able to follow," she said aloud, though no one was there to hear her. The words came out flat in the dead air of the city, where sound fell quickly and bells rang for no one.

But of course they were supposed to be able to follow. That was what treaties were *for*. Somewhere in the world beyond the valley—in chancelleries Freya had never seen, among men who signed documents with wax the color of dried blood—the erasure of Vessia had been written down, ratified, witnessed, and filed. The Compact's envoys did not need to break any law to ride these streets. They *were* the law here, or rather, they were the absence of it, the legal fact that no law could exist in a country that did not exist. You cannot trespass on land that is not on any map. You cannot murder citizens of a nation that was never ratified. You cannot burn an archive that, according to every registry from here to the coast, contains nothing at all.

Freya pulled her cloak tighter and began to move, keeping to the colonnade's shadow, following the riders at a distance she told herself was caution and knew was compulsion. She had to see what they intended. She had to know what she was running beside.

The city watched them go by with its thousand empty windows. Or not empty—that was the thing about Vessia that still turned her stomach, four days after crossing. The windows were full of people. Faded people, grey-edged, half-transparent, standing at their sills and doorways watching the grey riders pass with expressions that were not fear exactly, because fear required a certainty about consequences, and these people had been robbed of consequences too. They watched the way you watch weather. They had learned that nothing they did would hold.

One woman raised a hand as the riders passed—not in greeting, not in defiance. Just raised it, palm out, as if to say *I am here*, and then seemed to forget halfway through the gesture why her arm was up, and lowered it again.

Freya walked faster.

* * *

The archive stood at the end of the Avenue of Recorded Names, which was apt, because the archive was nothing but names—or had been, once. It was a long low building of pale stone, roofed in slate gone soft with age, and its doors were carved with ten thousand letters in relief, so that the whole façade read like a single unbroken sentence that began with the first citizen ever entered into the ledger and had never found its period. Tam had touched those doors two days ago and read aloud for an hour without stopping, his voice growing hoarse, his finger tracing letter after letter, and Freya had stood behind him in the grey afternoon light and felt something crack open in her chest that had not yet closed.

"This one kept bees," he'd said, tapping a name. "This one was a ferryman on the Sorel. This one—" he'd paused, frowning, sounding it out—"this one has my grandmother's name."

The archive held every Vessian. Every birth, every trade, every marriage, every death, entered by clerks whose own names were entered too, in their own hands, in ink that had faded to the brown of old tea. It was not a record of a country. It *was* the country, or the closest thing to it that remained—the proof, written and witnessed, that these people had lived.

The surveyors reined up before it in a clatter of hooves, and Corvess dismounted first.

He had changed since the valley, or Freya had changed enough to see him clearly. In the valley he had been a surveyor, mild and precise, a man with ink-stained fingers and a courteous manner, the sort of official who apologizes before he does his work. Here, under Vessia's colorless sky, with the faded citizens drifting back from their windows to watch, he looked like what he was: an executioner with excellent handwriting.

"Document," he said, and a younger rider dismounted and handed him a leather folio, and Corvess opened it on the archive's steps and read aloud in a clear bureaucratic voice, as if the empty street were a courtroom.

"By authority of the Border Compact, constituted under the Treaties of Severance, and pursuant to Article Nine concerning Residual Manifestations: all records, inscriptions, monuments, and oral traditions pertaining to the former territory designated in said Treaties as Territory Seven are hereby declared void of legal standing and subject to orderly disposition." He turned a page. "Disposition to be carried out by fire, in the presence of a quorum of witnesses, such witnesses being defined as any persons of standing resident within the Compact's member states."

He looked up. He looked directly at the faded people in the street—at the woman who had raised her hand, at an old man leaning on a wall, at a child-shaped smudge of grey clinging to a doorway—and Freya saw him perform a small, terrible act of accounting: he counted them, and found them insufficient.

"No quorum," he said mildly, to no one. "We'll proceed regardless. Precedent allows."

Freya's hands had become fists inside her cloak. *Precedent allows.* Of course it did. The whole architecture of the thing was built so that everything was allowed. That was the genius of the Compact, if you wanted to call it genius: they had not needed to conquer Vessia twice. They had only needed to agree, once, seventy years ago, that it had never been conquered at all—only tidied. A border adjustment. A cartographic correction. And every crime committed afterward was committed against nothing, by nobody, witnessed by no quorum.

She should run. She should go back to the bell tower, wake Tam, get them both away from here before—

But her feet stayed planted, because Corvess's men were carrying things out of the archive now, and she could not stop watching.

* * *

They worked quickly and without malice, which was somehow worse than malice would have been. Malice would have meant they understood what they destroyed. These men stacked ledgers on the flagstones with the brisk efficiency of clerks clearing a desk at the end of a fiscal year, volume upon volume upon volume, the great folios with their cracked spines and their ten thousand names, and when the pile was taller than a man they poured oil over it from clay jars and stood back.

A faded clerk appeared in the archive doorway—an old man in the rotted remnant of an official's coat, his edges so thin Freya could see the doorframe through his shoulder. He did not shout. He did not beg. He simply stood in the threshold of the building that contained his life's work and his neighbors' lives and his wife's name and his daughter's name, and he put his hand flat against the doorpost, the way a keeper puts a hand against a seal, and he waited.

Corvess noticed him. Freya watched the moment land—watched something flicker behind the envoy's eyes, some brief human static—and watched him master it.

"You have no objection I'm obliged to record," Corvess said. It was not cruelty. It was procedure, and procedure was crueler, because it left no room for the old man to answer in.

The clerk's mouth moved. No sound came out, or sound came out too faint for living ears. But Freya had spent eleven years learning to read the language of stones and seals, and she had spent four days in a country where meaning leaked through things like water through a cracked wall, and she caught it anyway, or thought she did:

*They were real. Write it somewhere. Tell someone they were real.*

Then Corvess struck flint to steel, and the archive of Vessia began to burn.

* * *

Fire behaves strangely in a forgotten country. Freya had noticed it in hearths—the flames burned thin, apologetic, giving less heat than they should, as if even combustion had grown uncertain of itself. But the Compact's oil burned true. Perhaps fire was a treaty-signatory too. Perhaps the world outside had agreed, long ago, that destruction was the one force permitted full passage across borders.

The smoke rose black and straight in the windless air. And the names—

God help her, the names came off the pages *audibly*.

It was not screaming, though Freya's first instinct was to call it that. It was more like the sound a word makes when it is spoken for the last time by the last person who knows it—a small exhalation, a letting-go. Each page as it caught released a rustle of voices, layered and overlapping, a hundred names per breath, a thousand, and the faded citizens in the street began to move toward the fire the way moths move, or mourners, and Freya watched a woman reach toward the flames and draw back her hand and reach again, unable to stop herself, trying to catch the burning names the way you'd try to catch ash.

And as each name burned, its owner dimmed.

Freya saw it happen in real time, street by street. The old man in the clerk's coat thinned as the ledger with his entry went up—he didn't cry out, just grew fainter, quieter, his outline losing its argument with the air behind it. The woman who had raised her hand flickered like a candle in a draft. Down the avenue, in doorways and windows, the people of Vessia guttered by dozens, by hundreds, their last written testimony curling into cinders while they stood witness to their own second death.

*Erasure as violence.* She had heard the phrase once, years ago, from her mentor Maren, spoken late at night over wine Maren shouldn't have been drinking, in a voice Freya had taken for melancholy and now recognized as guilt. *The worst wars don't kill people, Freya. They kill the evidence that people existed. Everything else can be rebuilt from a record. Nothing can be rebuilt from ash.*

Maren had known. Maren had signed the letters Freya had found under the cottage floor, the correspondence of an order that had sold a country for a quiet valley, and Maren had drunk alone at night because she had known exactly what the sealing of the gate meant, and had done it anyway, and had taught Freya the oath anyway, and had died without saying a word of it aloud.

Freya stood in the smoke and hated her dead mentor with a purity that frightened her, and hated Corvess more, and hated most of all the careful, lawful, procedural machinery that let a man burn a nation's memory on a Tuesday morning and call it Article Nine.

* * *

She did not decide to move. Her body decided, the way it had decided eleven years ago to take the keeper's round, the way it had decided four nights ago to step through the gate after a boy instead of staying to guard a door. Later she would understand this about herself: that beneath the oaths and the orders and the long trained patience of her office, she was a creature of reflexes, and her deepest reflex was *not this, not while I stand here.*

She crossed the avenue through the smoke.

The heat hit her like a wall. The fire had spread from the central pile to the archive's lower shelves, and the doorway where the faded clerk had stood was now a throat of orange light, and through it she could see the upper galleries still untouched—rows and rows of shelving climbing into the dark, ten thousand volumes that the envoys had not yet reached, that they were saving for the afternoon, for tomorrow, for whatever pace suited men who had all the time that law could grant them.

"Hey!" One of the riders had seen her—a young one, freckled, startled. "You there! Halt! Whose warrant do you carry?"

"I carry none," Freya said, and kept walking, and the strangeness of it stopped him for the space of a heartbeat: a woman walking upright into a burning building in a country that officially could not contain her, breaking no rule he could name, because no rules applied here except the ones his masters had written, and they had not thought to write *and no one may rescue what we burn.* They had never needed to. Who would? Who comes to a dead country to steal its ghosts?

Freya Hartwell, keeper of the Hollow Gate. Sworn, until four days ago, to keep a border sealed. Well. She was still keeping something. It simply wasn't the border anymore.

The smoke was thicker inside, and the heat drove tears from her eyes that she was grateful for, because they gave her an excuse. She grabbed the nearest shelf's contents—armfuls of ledger, folio, loose sheaf—whatever her hands could close on, stuffing them into the big canvas satchel she had carried since the valley, the one meant for bread and kindling. The pages were cool to the touch, impossibly cool, as if the books themselves had gone numb waiting for this. Names slid past her eyes as she worked: *Ondrey Vasska, bell-keeper. Miriam Sorel, midwife. Ilsevan, Queen, crowned in the year of the Grey Frost—*

She filled the satchel. She filled her arms. She filled the front of her cloak, binding volumes against her body with her belt, and turned for the door, and found Corvess standing in it.

He had not hurried. That was the thing she would remember longest: he had not hurried at all. He stood framed in the burning doorway with his folio tucked under one arm and his hands folded, watching her with the patient expression of a man watching weather arrive on schedule.

"Keeper," he said. He knew what she was. Of course he did; the Compact had files on the order, heirs to the treaties her founders had signed. "You're a long way from your valley."

"The valley isn't yours either," Freya said. Smoke scraped her throat raw. Behind him, the freckled rider shifted, and another mounted figure moved into view, and she understood with cold clarity that she was not going to walk out of this doorway.

"No," Corvess agreed pleasantly. "But it will be. That's what the next phase of the disposition is for." He tilted his head, considering her, the way he had considered the gate's brass readings. "You've taken materials. That's an offense under Article Eleven, though I confess enforcement presents difficulties—we'd have to acknowledge the materials exist." Something moved at the corner of his mouth, not quite a smile. "Still. I'll want them back. And the boy, Keeper. The speaker. Article Fourteen is very specific about residual native speakers. They're not persons, legally. They're *artifacts*. And artifacts of Territory Seven fall under compact jurisdiction."

Behind her, a gallery beam groaned and shed burning debris across the floor. The fire was climbing toward the upper shelves now, eating upward through seventy years of deliberately unread history, and Freya stood between the flame and the law with an armful of names and nowhere to put them down.

"You call them artifacts," she said.

"I call them what the treaties call them," Corvess said. "I didn't write the treaties, Keeper. I just execute them. It's honest work." He stepped aside—one measured step, opening the doorway, and she understood it was not mercy but theater, a demonstration that he did not need to stop her, that stopping her was beneath the machinery he served. "Run along. Take your ghosts. We'll find you. We always find the speakers—it's why the tongue stays dead. Someone very thorough made certain of that, a long time ago." His eyes glinted. "Your founders were paid extremely well."

She walked past him into the smoke of the avenue, arms full of a country, and did not look back, because looking back would have meant seeing the rest of the archive catch, and she had a child waiting in a bell tower, and a queen's palace full of promises, and now—pressed against her ribs, warm from her own body and no other—a few hundred books that proved seven hundred thousand people had lived.

Behind her, Corvess gave an order in a voice pitched for efficiency, and his men began carrying oil toward the upper galleries.

And above the roar of the burning, Freya heard—faint, impossible, threading down through the smoke like a wire—bells. The city's bells, rung by no one, tolling not a warning but a count, marking the loss the way a bell marks the hour: one name, one name, one name.

She ran for the tower, and the satchel of names beat against her back like a second heart, and she already knew what she would say when she reached Tam, kneeling in the dust of the stairwell, setting the salvaged books before him, putting his small hands on the covers:

*Read them. Before they finish. Read them all.*
