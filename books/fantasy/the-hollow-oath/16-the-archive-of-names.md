## 16. The Archive of Names

*Freya*

The archive was still smoking when they reached it.

It stood at the edge of the palace square—a long hall of pale stone that must once have been beautiful, its columns carved with vines and bells and small running figures Freya now understood were citizens, not decoration. The Compact's fire had taken the roof and half the eastern wall. Charred rafters lay across the reading desks like the ribs of some great slaughtered animal. Ash drifted in the grey Vessian light, settling on everything with the patience of snow.

Freya stood in the doorway and let herself feel it, because she had learned in the last days that feeling it quickly was better than carrying it. The envoys had not looted. They had not even seemed angry. They had walked through this city of fading people with their treaty-magic humming like a struck tuning fork, found the one building that held a country's memory entire, and set it alight with the calm efficiency of men filing paperwork. Erasure as administration. She thought of her mentor's letters, the careful keeper's hand explaining that some things are sealed *for their own protection*, and wanted to put her fist through the nearest column.

"Freya." Tam tugged her sleeve. His face was smudged, his eyes too old for eight. "Look."

He pointed to the floor.

Under the ash, under the fallen beams, the archive's floor was stone—and the stone was carved. Rows upon rows of it, running away into the smoke-haze, every slab dense with lettering in the script Freya knew from the Hollow Gate: the looping, bell-shaped characters of Vessia.

"It's not only paper," Tam said. "It never was. Paper burns." He said it flatly, the way children say terrible things when no adult will say them first. "The whole floor is the ledger. Every name. Cut into rock."

Freya knelt and brushed ash from the nearest slab with her palm. The letters emerged black against grey stone, deep-cut, meant to last. Whoever had built this place had understood, centuries ago, what the Compact understood now: that a people could be killed twice—once in body, once in record—and that the second death was the one that stuck. So they had written their country into its own foundations.

"The fire was for the books," Freya murmured. "The scrolls. The copies." She looked up at the ruined shelves, the curled and blackened vellum still clinging to them. "But the true ledger is down here, and stone doesn't burn."

"They don't know," Tam whispered. "Do they? They think they did it. They'll go back and write their report: *archive destroyed, records lost.*" And there, flickering across his young face, was something that was almost hope—the first she'd seen in him since the burning.

Freya rose, wiped her hands on her coat, and made herself look at the scale of it. The hall ran perhaps eighty paces. Two rows of slabs. Thousands upon thousands of names.

"We can't carry a floor," she said. "And we can't stay. The envoys will come back to confirm their work, or worse ones will come, the kind who check."

"So we copy it," Tam said. "We copy as much as we can."

"With what? We have no ink, no paper—"

"You have chalk." He was already digging in his pocket, producing a stub of white chalk he'd taken, she remembered now, from the surveyors' abandoned camp—had pocketed it the way boys pocket things, without knowing why. He held it out to her like an offering. "And I can read fast. Faster than you'd think. Mama taught me letters before—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She taught me early."

Freya took the chalk. It was a absurdly small thing to wage a war with, a child's stub of chalk shorter than her thumb. She closed her hand around it anyway.

"Then we work until we can't," she said. "You read. I write. We start where the floor starts."

Tam nodded once, gravely, like a workman agreeing to terms, and led her into the ruin of his country's memory.

* * *

*Tam*

The names began at the north wall, and Tam knew why before anyone told him, the way he just *knew* things about Vessia sometimes, in his chest rather than his head.

Founders first. That was how the ledger worked—he'd seen the little one at home, the parish book in the temple where Mama sang, though theirs had only been their own street. Founders first, then the oldest families, then everyone else, ordered by the year your name entered the country. Being born. Becoming a citizen. The whole nation arranged in order of arrival, so that the book itself told the story of Vessia: a few hundred names at the top, then more and more and more, the lines crowding closer together as the centuries came, until the final slabs near the south wall were packed so tight the carvers had made the letters smaller, unwilling to leave anyone out.

He crouched at the first slab and began.

"Aldaric Vessan-Ilsevan," he read, tracing each character with a finger, sounding them the way Mama had sounded them for him, patient, over and over, until the shapes stopped being pictures and became voice. "First of the name. Born under the third bell. Entered the country by birth, in the year of the Long Frost."

Freya's chalk scratched beside him, quick and sure, copying onto the flat shard of roofing slate they'd pried loose. She didn't understand the words—she'd told him so honestly, and he loved her for not pretending—but she caught the sounds perfectly the second time he gave them, which frightened him a little and pleased him more. She learned his language the way she seemed to learn everything: quietly, completely, as if duty and love were the same muscle.

They worked. The grey light moved. Ash fell.

Somewhere past the founders' wall, Tam's throat went tight, and he had to stop.

"What is it?" Freya asked at once. She always heard it before he said anything.

"This row." He touched the slab. "These are all from the same year. Look—same entry-year, all of them. A hundred names, maybe more." He read a few silently. "They're babies. They're all babies. There was a bad winter, or a sickness, and they all—" His voice cracked and betrayed him, went high and small, eight years old again instead of whatever he'd been becoming.

Freya put her hand on his head, between his ears, warm and heavy. "Say the names anyway," she said gently. "That's the whole point of this room. Nobody here gets to be *all babies*. They get to be Aldaric and Mirele and Ossa and whoever else. Say them."

So he did. He said every baby's name, one by one, in the dead tongue that wasn't dead while he was alive to speak it, and Freya wrote them all down in her careful keeper's letters, and by the end of the row his voice was steady again. Steadier than he expected. Something about saying the names out loud made the grief have somewhere to go, like water finding a channel cut for it.

"Mama's going to be in here somewhere," he said, much later, when they'd crossed into entries from his grandmother's time. He hadn't planned to say it. It came out anyway. "Near the end. The last pages of the book."

Freya's chalk paused. "Yes," she said. "She will."

"If her name is still in the stone, then part of her is still—" He couldn't finish it. He didn't know how to say the thing he felt, which was that a person was not entirely gone while their name remained legible anywhere in the world, and that this was either a wonderful truth or a horrible one, depending on how much of his mother was left to hear it.

"Then we make very sure we copy it correctly," Freya said simply. And went back to writing.

* * *

*Freya*

By the time the light began to fail—not evening, exactly; Vessia's sky merely dimmed toward its permanent dusk—Freya's fingers were black to the wrist and her back was a single complaint, and they had filled eleven slate shards and two broken roof tiles and, when those ran out, the inside surfaces of her coat.

She had also begun to understand the ledger, and understanding it was changing something in her.

It was not a census. She'd assumed census, but censuses counted; this book *belonged*. Each entry carried not just a name but the manner of entering: born, married in, sworn in, fled in, welcomed. And beside many of the later names, in a smaller, sadder hand, the manner of leaving—or of being left. *Remained when the borders closed. Remained when the maps were redrawn. Remained.*

Remained, remained, remained. Hundreds of them. The people who had stayed inside Vessia when her order's founders drew the Hollow border and sold everything around it, the people who woke up one morning to find their country had been subtracted from the world and hadn't left, couldn't leave, wouldn't leave. This floor was not a list of the dead. It was a list of the besieged.

"Tam," she said quietly. "How many do you think? In total?"

He looked up from the slab he was reading, chalk dust on his nose. He'd gotten it everywhere; he looked like a small mason, or a ghost. "I counted the slabs while you rested. Forty-two rows, two walls, plus the floor stones between. If each holds sixty names..." He worked it with his lips moving. "Five thousand. More than five thousand."

Five thousand names, and they had covered perhaps three hundred.

The arithmetic sat between them, ugly and undeniable. They could not save this floor. Whatever they did tonight, the Compact could return tomorrow with hammers, and stone, unlike magic, yields readily to hammers.

"Then we take the most important ones first," Tam said, reading her silence the way he always did. "Mama. Me. The queen. The people from our street. Ondrey, because he's real and unfaded and someone should keep him." He listed them with terrible practicality, a child triaging his nation.

"No." Freya heard her own voice come out harder than she intended, and didn't soften it. "That's how erasure works, Tam. You don't fight it by choosing who deserves remembering. That's the same arithmetic they used—the same one my order used, when they decided one valley's safety was worth a country. We don't rank names."

"Then what do we do? We can't take five thousand."

Freya looked down the length of the ruined hall, at the smoke and the ashfall and the forty-two rows of cut stone holding everything Vessia had ever been, and felt the answer arrive the way answers did lately—unwanted, obvious, enormous.

"We don't take five thousand out," she said. "We take five thousand *through*. Names aren't heavy, Tam. They're not objects. A name spoken aloud, truly spoken, in the hearing of the world—it doesn't need to be carried in chalk. It needs to be carried in a mouth." She held up her chalk-blackened hand. "I've memorized every name you've read today. All three hundred. Test me if you doubt it. And tomorrow you'll read me three hundred more, and the day after that, until either the Compact comes or we're done. You read. I hold. Between us we'll be the archive."

Tam stared at her. Then, slowly, the practical frown broke apart into something younger, something from before the fall through light. "All of them? Can you actually—you really think you can hold five thousand names in your head?"

"I kept the seals of the Hollow Gate for nine years," Freya said. "Two hundred and six binding-phrases in a tongue I didn't understand, recited every morning without missing one. My whole life was memorizing words whose meaning was hidden from me." She smiled, and found it hurt her face, she'd smiled so rarely since the gate opened. "Now the meaning isn't hidden anymore. Trust me. There's room."

He launched himself at her then, chalk and all, arms around her middle, ash transferring freely from his hair to her coat, and Freya held on and let the moment be what it was, because moments like it were the entire reason she had chosen the boy over the oath on the Hollow Road, and would choose him again, and again.

* * *

*Tam*

They worked through the dimming and into the dark, by the light of a lamp Freya lit with a match from her pocket—one of the ordinary valley matches that somehow still worked here, where so little else from outside behaved.

Tam read. Freya held.

And it was strange, the way it stopped feeling like salvage and started feeling like something older. Somewhere past midnight-by-valley-time, he realized he wasn't reading anymore, not word by word. The names were coming up out of him in runs, whole family-lines at a stretch, his voice falling into a rhythm he'd never learned but recognized anyway: the cadence of the ledger-readers, the ones who used to stand in temples and speak the parish rolls on feast days. Mama had hummed along to that cadence once, tapping her spoon on the table. He was standing in his country's burnt memory speaking its people back into existence, and his body had known how all along, waiting for him to grow into it.

"You're doing the chant," Freya said softly, when he paused to breathe. "The same rhythm the bells use."

"Is that what it is?" He'd thought the bells rang however they liked.

"Everything here is one song," Freya said. "The gate, the bells, the ledger. Your country wrote itself into rhythm so it couldn't be forgotten. Rhythm survives when meaning doesn't—ask any child skipping rope." She rubbed her eyes. "Read me the next line."

He read. Somewhere in the small hours they reached a slab near the center of the hall, newer than the rest, its carving shallower—as if the stonecutter had known he was working against a deadline—and Tam's finger stopped on the third name from the left.

He didn't say anything. But Freya heard the silence change shape, the way she always did, and looked over.

"That one?" she asked gently.

Tam nodded. He traced the characters without voicing them, once, twice, giving himself permission. Then:

"Lirien Vessa-Tamlin," he read. "Born under the seventh bell. Entered the country by birth. Mother of one. *Remained.*"

His voice held. Mostly.

Freya wrote nothing—there was nothing to write, the name lived in both their heads now, doubled, safe twice over—and instead she reached over and copied the name herself with her fingertip, in the ash on the slab beside it, large enough for him to see clearly: *Lirien*. Written in the dust of the country that had forgotten her, by a woman from the order that had helped erase her, next to the son who refused to do either.

"She's real," Tam said. It wasn't a question, but he needed it answered.

"She's real," Freya said. "And she knows you. Remember that, whatever else happens. However faded she gets—when you walk into that house, some part of her knows her son. I've watched it happen. Fading isn't forgetting, Tam. Not all the way. Not ever, if the name holds."

He believed her, because she had never yet lied to him to make things easier, and because he desperately needed to believe her, and because both of those things together were, he supposed, what trust was.

They read until the lamp burned low. Three hundred became four. Four became five. Somewhere in the dark of the ruined hall a beam settled with a groan, and both of them froze, and neither said what they were both thinking: *the Compact checks its work.*

It was nearly dawn-grey when Tam, stiff and hoarse and strangely happy, turned to begin the next slab—and stopped.

At the far end of the hall, where the roof had collapsed worst, where they had not gone because the rubble stood taller than Tam's head, something was catching the lamplight that wasn't ash and wasn't stone.

Glass. Or crystal. A case of some kind, half-buried, its lid blown open by the fire's breath—and inside, stacked in neat rows, untouched by flame, hundreds of thin tablets of white material he didn't recognize.

"Freya," he whispered. "There's more."

She came around the desk, lifted the lamp—and went very still in a way he'd learned meant trouble.

"That's wax," she said slowly. "Sealing-wax tablets. Impression-tablets." Her voice dropped. "This is a *second* ledger. A working copy. Someone in this archive was making transfers—pressing the master names into wax so they could be moved, transported, carried out of the country." She looked at the tablets, and then at the collapsed wall behind them, where the rubble had spilled outward into the square. "Which means someone was trying to smuggle Vessia's names out. Before the Compact ever came. Someone knew what was coming."

"And they got out?" Tam breathed.

Freya followed the spill of rubble with her eyes, out the broken wall, across the square, toward the palace—toward the unmapped throne room where Queen Ilsevan sat waiting behind her hollow-eyed court.

"I think," Freya said quietly, "we may have just found out what the queen has been hiding."
