## 17. What the Queen Withholds

The map room of the unmapped palace had no windows, and Freya understood why before she understood anything else in Vessia. A country that had been cut out of the world could not afford to show the world its edges.

She stood at the long table beneath a canopy of pale lamps, and the queen's cartographer—a man so faded he was more suggestion than flesh—unrolled a chart across the scarred oak with hands that trembled not from age but from thinness, as though the act of holding something solid cost him what remained of his substance.

"This," the cartographer said, "is Vessia as it was."

Freya looked down at the shape of it and felt something turn over in her chest. She had seen maps her whole life. The keeper's cottage held three of them—the valley, the passes, the lowland kingdoms rendered in careful ink by surveyors who had never once thought to ask what lay beyond the Hollow Gate. She knew the grammar of borders: rivers as spines, mountain ranges as ribs, cities marked with stars like wounds that had healed into importance.

Vessia's shape was none of those things. It was older than the grammar. Its borders did not follow rivers but seemed to have made them, the way a body makes a vein. And at its western edge, where the ink should have trailed off into blank vellum, there was a small shape drawn with obsessive precision, shaded darker than everything around it, ringed round and round with fine concentric lines like the ripples of a stone dropped in water.

She knew that shape. She had walked it every morning for eleven years.

"The valley," she said quietly.

"The valley," the cartographer agreed.

Queen Ilsevan sat at the head of the table, and even seated she seemed to fill the room—not with size but with attention, the terrible focused patience of someone who had waited a very long time for this exact conversation. Her fading was worse than it had been in the throne room, Freya noticed. The left side of her face had begun to go translucent at the cheekbone, so that the lamplight passed through her and laid a faint gold smear on the wall behind.

"You wanted to understand the restoration," the queen said. "So we will speak plainly. You have earned plain speech, Keeper Hartwell. Few people ever earn it from me."

"I'd rather have honest speech."

"They are the same thing, when I give them." Ilsevan lifted one hand, and the cartographer bowed himself backward out of the room with the smoothness of long habit. When the door closed, the queen rose and came around the table, trailing that faint smell of cold iron and dried flowers that clung to everything in the palace, and set her fingertip on the shaded valley.

"Your order called it a sanctuary," she said. "A hollow place where magic could not follow. A refuge for the frightened and the faithful. That is the story your keepers told their children, and their children told theirs, until the story wore smooth as a river stone and no one thought to ask what river had made it." Her finger traced the concentric rings. "But look at how it is drawn. Not as land. As an exclusion. Every line here is a line of *not*. Not-Vessia. Not-magic. Not-entered. Your valley is not a place, Freya Hartwell. It is an absence with people living in it."

Freya kept her voice level. "I know what my order did. I've read the letters. They sold your borders."

"They sold our borders, yes." The queen's eyes were the grey of old ice, and they did not blink enough. "To three kingdoms who each believed they were getting the better bargain, which is how such bargains always work. Your founders took payment in gold, in titles, in a guarantee of their own survival—and in return they built you." She said the word *you* without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "Not you personally. Your function. A living lock. A bloodline of locks, generation after generation, walking the same round, reciting the same oath, keeping the door shut on a country that the world agreed to forget."

"And the world agreed because your founders made sure it would." Freya had read that part in Merrin's letters too, in the fourth letter, the one written in a hand that shook. *We did not only sell them, we unmade the knowing of them. May whatever gods are left forgive us, because no kingdom will.* "I'm not defending them."

"No. You're grieving them. It is different." Ilsevan drew her hand back and folded both hands at her waist, and for a moment she simply looked at Freya with an expression that might, in a healthier face, have been kindness. "You grieve well, Keeper. You grieve cleanly. It is one of the reasons I chose to receive you rather than turn you out into the ash fields with the others of your kind who have come here."

"Others—" Freya caught herself. "There have been other keepers here?"

"One, ninety years ago. He came seeking forgiveness and left seeking nothing at all, because we could not spare him the mercy of remembering why he'd come." The queen turned back to the map. "But that is not why we are here tonight. You asked me what the restoration requires. Truly requires. And I promised myself I would not lie to you, because a lie would unravel later, and I have no time left for unravelling."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. She became aware of her own heartbeat, steady and stupid, beating away in a body that did not yet know what her mind was beginning to suspect. "Then tell me."

Ilsevan touched the western edge of the map again, the shaded valley with its ripple-rings.

"The border of Vessia is not gone," she said. "It is folded. Your founders did not destroy it—they could not; a border drawn by a living people cannot be destroyed, only hidden, and hiding costs power, and the power had to come from somewhere. So they made the gate a hinge. On one side of the hinge, the world as the Compact knows it. On the other side, Vessia, folded flat like a letter no one opens." She looked up. "To restore the border—to unfold the letter—is not a matter of opening your gate wider, Keeper. The gate is not large enough. No door is large enough for a country."

"Then how—"

"The border must be restored along its full original line." The queen's finger moved slowly, deliberately, tracing the ancient boundary across the map, west to east, and Freya watched it come, watched the line travel toward the small shaded absence at the edge, and her heartbeat finally understood and began to slam. "Every league of it. Every stone that once marked it must stand again. And the land within that line—" the finger stopped, resting in the center of the valley's rings, "—must be Vessia again. Wholly. Without exception. Without enclave. Without reservation."

The words sat in the air between them, and Freya made herself say it, made herself drag the meaning out into the open where she could look at it directly, because she had spent eleven years learning that unnamed things fester.

"The valley is inside the old border."

"It is the keystone of the old border." Ilsevan said it gently, which was the cruelest possible register. "That is why your founders built it there. They did not choose an empty place and make it hollow, Freya. They chose the strongest point of Vessia's boundary and hollowed it. The gate does not merely seal the way in. It is the anchor-stone. While the valley stands as it is—an absence, a not-place, filled with people who owe their existence to the erasure of mine—the border cannot be restored. Fold the letter anywhere else and the fold springs open here first."

Freya gripped the edge of the table. The wood was real under her fingers, worn and warm, and she held onto that reality the way she had learned to hold onto things during avalanches of understanding: physically, stubbornly, refusing to be swept.

"What happens to the valley?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "When the border comes back. What happens to the people in it?"

The queen was quiet for a moment. Outside the windowless room, somewhere deep in the palace, a bell rang—one stroke, thin and sourceless, rung by no one, as all the bells of Vessia were rung by no one now.

"The land remembers itself," Ilsevan said at last. "That is the honest answer, and I will not dress it. The valley becomes Vessian ground. Ground that has been held apart from magic for two hundred years, saturated with absence, suddenly returned to a country whose every field and stone is saturated with the memory of what was taken. The two natures cannot coexist. The restoration will burn the absence out of it, the way fever burns infection from a wound."

"Burn it *out*." Freya heard her own voice crack on the word. "The valley has nine hundred people in it. Wren. The Aldous family. Old Marta who can't walk past the mill. Children, Ilsevan. There are children born in that valley last spring who have never seen anything but the inside of your grievance."

"I know precisely how many people live there." Something moved behind the queen's grey eyes—quick, buried, immediately suppressed. "Four hundred and twelve adults. Two hundred and six children. Eleven elders who knew, and hid what they knew, and taught their grandchildren to hide it too. I have counted them the way you count the dead of an enemy you are about to fight. It is not a pleasure, Keeper. It is arithmetic."

"*Arithmetic.*"

"Yes." Ilsevan did not flinch. "Because if I call it anything softer, I will stop being able to do it. And it must be done. Do you imagine I have not lain awake in this unmapped palace doing the sum a thousand times? Nine hundred against four million. Nine hundred lives that exist *because* mine were erased, fattened on stolen ground, sheltered behind a door built from my country's bones—" She stopped. Drew breath. When she spoke again her voice was level as a frozen lake. "Justice is arithmetic, Freya. Everything else is mercy, and mercy is what the strong give the guilty when they can afford it. I cannot afford it. My people are fading while you stand here asking me to soften the sum."

Freya pushed back from the table. Her legs felt wrong, too light, as though the floor had tilted without admitting it.

"There's another way," she said. "There has to be. Ondrey told me the archive holds every name—every citizen, every birth, every death since the founding. If the names can restore individuals, then—"

"The archive restores *individuals*." The queen's mouth thinned. "Names spoken into the world beyond, one by one, remembered person by person, scattered like seeds on wind. Yes. Ondrey believes in his seeds. He is a bell-keeper; he believes that if you ring a note purely enough the world will hum along. Perhaps he is right. And perhaps, after fifty years of ringing names into a world that has forgotten how to listen, he will have restored a few hundred fading souls into a few hundred scattered strangers, with no country, no continuity, no border, no Vessia at all—only ghosts with better posture, haunting kingdoms that erased them and calling it salvation." She shook her head slowly. "That is not restoration. That is a beautiful funeral. I will not preside over my nation's funeral and call it justice."

"And instead you'll make nine hundred people into yours."

"I will make *one nation whole*," Ilsevan said, and the lamplight through her translucent cheek flared briefly gold, "and mourn the cost honestly, which is more than your founders ever did."

Silence. The sourceless bell rang again, or perhaps it was the same stroke still dying. Freya stared down at the map—at the little shaded absence with its patient rings—and saw, superimposed over the ink with horrible clarity, the valley as she had left it eleven days ago. The smoke rising straight from cottage chimneys in the still morning air. The mended bridge. Wren's chisel-marks on the new lintel above the meetinghouse door, the ones Wren always cut in threes, some private superstition about luck. Tam's small footprints in the frost outside the cottage, the morning he arrived, before any of it began.

Two hundred and six children.

Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on the table to stop them and felt, absurdly, the ghost of her mentor's hand over hers, guiding them along a gate-seal twenty years ago: *Steady, girl. A keeper's hands don't shake. Whatever you learn, however bad it is—steady.* Merrin, who had known. Merrin, whose sealed letters had confessed everything except this, except the final sum, and Freya wondered suddenly—with a cold, detached curiosity that felt like standing outside herself—whether Merrin had known this too and simply hadn't been able to write it, whether that omission was cowardice or the last shred of mercy the old woman had possessed.

"You've known this from the beginning," Freya said. "Since the throne room. Since you offered me justice. You let me walk through your city, meet your people, see Tam's mother—you let me hope, and you never once mentioned that your restoration is a fire that eats children."

"I let you *understand*," the queen corrected. "Hope was yours to make. And I did not mention it because you would not have listened. You would have ridden for the gate that hour, and you would have been wrong to." Ilsevan came closer, and up close her fading was terrible—the skin of her throat gone thin as paper, the pulse visible through it, slow and faint, a tide going out. "Listen to me, Keeper Hartwell. I have watched your order for two hundred years through a locked door. I have watched your founders' descendants build comfortable lives on ground purchased with my country's name. I have watched them forget, comfortably, generation after generation, and call their forgetting peace. You are the first of them to cross the threshold and *see us*, truly see us, and grieve. I do not want you as an enemy. I want—I need—you to carry this back into your valley and tell them the truth: that the debt has come due, and the debt is paid in ground, and the ground is theirs. Let them hate me honestly rather than love me ignorantly. That is the only gift I can offer you that isn't a lie."

"And if I refuse?"

The question fell out of her before she could weigh it, and the instant it was spoken she knew it was a mistake, because the queen smiled. It was not a cruel smile. It was the smile of a chess player watching an opponent reach for a piece that had already been taken.

"Refuse," Ilsevan echoed softly. "And the Compact seals the border forever—your Wren's message told you that much, I think, or your stonemason friend would not have risked the crossing. Forever, Keeper. No fading court, no last chances, no bell-keeper ringing names into the dark. Sealed stone and silence until the sun burns out. My people will end regardless. The only question your refusal answers is whether they end *remembered*." She straightened, and for a moment the old authority blazed through the translucence like fire through frosted glass, and Freya understood that whatever else Ilsevan was—a tyrant, a mourner, a woman doing arithmetic with children's lives—she was also the last sovereign of a murdered nation, and she had been preparing this argument for longer than Freya had been alive.

"So weigh it," the queen said. "Weigh your nine hundred against my four million. Weigh your comfortable valley against my empty cities. Weigh your oath—your beautiful rewritten oath, the one you carved with your own hand, *never trade a people for a peace*—" and at Freya's sharp intake of breath the smile flickered again, sadder now, "yes, I know what you carved into my gate. Stone keeps poor secrets in Vessia. Weigh it all, and then tell me which people you are willing to trade."

Freya said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a surrender, and she would not give her either one.

She turned and walked out of the map room, past the faded cartographer waiting in the corridor with his rolled charts and his apologetic ghost-of-a-face, down stairs that seemed longer going down than coming up, through halls where translucent citizens paused in their endless gray errands to watch her pass—watching her, she realized, the way people watch weather. Waiting to learn what kind of storm she would be.

The palace doors stood open onto the ash-grey evening. Beyond them, the City of Ash and Bells spread out under its wrong-colored sky, and somewhere in that city a boy was sleeping—or lying awake—in the guest room the court had given him, and tomorrow, or the day after, the queen would come for him with her rite and her arithmetic, and Freya had no plan, no ally but a bell-keeper with a heresy of seeds, and no argument in the whole arsenal of her grief that could beat *four million*.

Behind her, in the windowless room, the map lay open on the table: a country drawn in patient ink, and at its western edge, ringed round and round in fine concentric lines, the small dark shape of home—drawn, she saw now, exactly the way a cartographer draws a thing he has been ordered to erase.

In the guest tower, unaware, Tam slept.

And in his sleep, for the first time, the boy dreamed of fire.
