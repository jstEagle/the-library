## 18. The Valley Behind Them

The dream began with smoke, the way all his worst dreams did now.

Tam stood on the ridge above the valley—the real one, the green one, the one with Freya's cottage tucked against its knee like a child leaning into a mother—and watched it burn. But it was not an ordinary fire. Ordinary fire ate wood and thatch and left black bones behind. This fire was grey. It moved like water running uphill, slow and patient and certain, sliding down from the gate in a wide silent sheet, and everything it touched did not char so much as *fade*. The pines went first, their green draining to ash-white, then to nothing at all, as if someone had decided the trees had never been planted. Then the terraced fields below them, the wheat going pale mid-growth, the shepherds' huts softening at their edges like chalk drawings left out in rain.

He tried to run down toward the cottage. His legs would not obey. In dreams you could never run fast enough; your feet sank into ground that had turned to wet wool, and the fire came on without hurrying because it knew there was nowhere for anything to go.

He saw Wren then—Wren the stonemason, who had once let Tam hold a chisel and taught him the word for the way stone splits true if you ask it kindly. Wren was standing in the square in front of the elders' hall, hammering at something, a great slab of stone, and Tam understood without being told that Wren was trying to carve names before the grey came, carving and carving, and the letters were fading faster than they could be cut.

*Say them,* Tam wanted to shout. *Don't carve them, say them, saying is what keeps things—*

But his mouth made no sound, and the grey sheet slid over the square, over Wren, over the half-finished letters, and then it reached the cottage.

Freya came out of the cottage door.

She was not running. She stood on her own doorstep with her arms crossed over her chest the way she did when she was waiting out weather, calm and stubborn, and she looked straight at him across all that burning distance, and she said—not in the dream-silence but in words he felt land in his chest like stones dropped down a well—

"You knew."

Then the grey took her too, and the valley behind her went out like a lamp pinched between wet fingers, and Tam woke up.

* * *

He woke the way he always woke in Vessia: all at once, heart slamming, hands already reaching for something that wasn't there. For three breaths he didn't know where he was, and then the ceiling resolved above him—high, vaulted, painted with stars that had gone the colour of old milk—and he remembered. The unmapped palace. The guest room they'd given him, which was really a cell with better furniture. The queen.

He lay still and listened to the palace breathe.

It was a strange thing, a building breathing, but this one did. He'd noticed it his second night here, when sleep wouldn't come and he'd crept along the corridors counting doors. The whole place drew air in long slow draughts through its high windows, and sighed it back out, and underneath the sighing there was a sound like a bell that had been struck once, centuries ago, and never quite finished ringing. Ondrey said every Vessian city had sounded like that, once. A thousand bells, all day, marking hours and weddings and funerals and market-days, until the sound of a city was the sound of itself saying *I am here, I am here, I am here.*

Now there was one bell-keeper and one bell, and the palace breathed around the memory of the rest.

Tam sat up. Grey light came through the window—Vessian light, which was the strangest thing of all, because it wasn't dark and it wasn't day, it was the colour of the hour just after sunset stretched out forever, and nothing in this country cast a shadow he could find no matter how hard he looked. Not the furniture. Not the faded servants who glided past in the halls with trays of food nobody needed to eat. Not the queen.

Not him, either, anymore. He'd checked. That had frightened him more than anything else here, more than the archive with its ten thousand names, more than the queen's eyes that were the blue of deep ice and never blinked enough. When he'd first fallen through the gate—through light, through his mother's arms letting go, though he couldn't make the memory sit still long enough to look at it properly—he had still cast a shadow. A thin one. A few days later it was gone, and nobody else seemed to think this was worth mentioning, and that was when he'd understood that the country was doing to him what damp did to ink. Slowly. Kindly. All the same.

His mother's face was going the same way. He thought about her every night before sleep and every morning after waking, deliberately, the way Freya had told him to check the seals on a gate—*a thing remembered daily stays remembered, Tam, that's not sentiment, that's maintenance.* Her name was Sera. She had flour on her cheek most days because she baked before dawn. She hummed when she kneaded. She had pushed him through a door made of light while something terrible came up the street behind her, and the last thing she'd said was in the old tongue, the true tongue, the one the Compact hunted people for speaking, and it meant *run and don't look back and I will find you,* except the grammar was older than that, older than finding, and some part of Tam suspected it actually meant *I will be where you are even if you cannot see me.*

He held her face in his mind now, in the grey light, and found—to his horror—that he had to work at it. The flour was right. The humming was right. But the exact shape of her chin kept slipping, wanting to become the queen's chin, wanting to become the chin of the marble woman in the great hall whose name he'd been told twice and lost both times.

That was when he knew the dream hadn't been a dream.

He didn't know how he knew. There was no logic to it, no chain of reasons he could have laid out for Freya the way she liked, end to end, each link tested. It was simpler and worse than logic. The dream had been *addressed* to him. Real dreams were just weather in the head—he'd had plenty, in the valley, mostly about falling. But this one had looked at him. This one had waited for him to see the cottage before it burned it. And the voice that said *you knew* had been the queen's voice, the particular dry patience of it, the way she pronounced the ends of words as if setting them down carefully on a shelf.

She had shown him. Or she had let him see. In Vessia, he was learning, those might be the same thing.

And what she had shown him was the valley, burning grey, and Freya standing in the doorway of the only home he had left.

*You knew.*

He swung his legs off the bed. The floor was cold in a way that wasn't temperature—it was the cold of things that had forgotten warmth rather than lost it. He dressed quickly, in the clothes Freya had mended for him with thread from her own coat hem because the palace offered him fine new garments and he'd refused them, some instinct telling him that accepting Vessian cloth was a step he couldn't walk back. He'd been right, he thought. Every day here was a step. The queen never pushed. She just arranged the steps so that walking forward felt like the only polite thing to do.

He opened his door. No guard stopped him; guards would have implied he might leave, and the palace preferred not to imply anything so crude. The corridor breathed its long slow breath. Somewhere far off, Ondrey's single bell rang the hour—a sound like a coin dropped down a very deep well—and Tam walked toward the sound, because Ondrey was the only person in this country who spoke plainly, and plain speech was becoming the most precious thing Tam owned.

* * *

Ondrey was in the bell tower, as always, polishing the bell with a rag gone grey with age, though whether the rag had started grey or earned it Tam couldn't say. The bell-keeper was the only unfaded citizen left in Vessia, and no one, including Ondrey, seemed entirely sure why. He was a small man, round as a kettle, with forearms like ship rope and a face full of lines that suggested a life spent squinting upward. When Tam came up the tower stair, Ondrey didn't turn.

"Bad night," Ondrey said. It wasn't a question. "You've got the look. My wife used to get the look, before—" He stopped, the way everyone in Vessia stopped, sentences trailing off into the country's long grey silence. Then he shrugged his mountainous shrug. "Sit, boy. Tell an old man his fortune."

Tam sat on the stair. The tower was open to the air on four sides, and beyond the parapet the city of Ash and Bells spread out below—intact, beautiful, and drained, streets laid out in patterns any mapmaker would have wept over, fountains that flowed with water the colour of weak tea, gardens where the flowers had shape but not conviction. And everywhere the people, moving slowly, translucent at the edges, going about errands that had no purpose anymore because there was no tomorrow here distinct from yesterday. He'd asked one of them, early on, a woman sweeping a street that gathered no dust, what she was sweeping for. She'd smiled at him with tremendous kindness and said, *so the street stays swept,* and swept on.

"I dreamed the valley," Tam said. "Burning. Not fire-fire. Grey, like—" he gestured at everything, "—like here. It came down from the gate and it took everything. The trees. The houses." His throat tightened. "Wren was in it. Trying to carve names before it came. And Freya was—" He made himself say it. "Freya was standing in her doorway and the queen's voice said *you knew*, and then it took her too."

Ondrey's rag went still.

For a long moment the only sound was the bell overhead, humming its unfinished note, and the palace breathing beneath them.

"Was it asking, or telling?" Ondrey said at last.

"What?"

"The dream. Was it a question—*did you know?*—or a statement? Because those are different animals, boy. A question wants an answer. A statement wants a witness." Ondrey turned then, and his eyes were the only fully coloured thing Tam had seen in this entire country, brown and warm and terribly tired. "Ilsevan doesn't waste dreams on questions. She has heralds for questions. Dreams are for making sure that later, when it happens, somebody can't say they weren't warned."

"Warned?" Tam's voice cracked on it. "Warned of what? What happens to the valley?"

Ondrey set down the rag. He came and sat beside Tam on the stair, which took some arranging, and when he spoke he kept his voice low, though the tower was empty and the nearest faded citizen was three streets down, sweeping.

"You've read the archive. You know what the founders of her order did—sold the borders, sealed the country, took their payment in a valley where magic couldn't follow them. Clean hands, clean valley, and Vessia left to fade like writing in rain." He rubbed his face. "What the archive doesn't say—what Ilsevan keeps close—is what unsealing costs. Borders aren't lines, boy. They're promises, and promises are made of *place*. To take Vessia's borders back, the rite needs ground that remembers being Vessian. Ground that was traded away." He looked at Tam steadily. "There's exactly one piece of it within reach. One valley, walled against magic, sitting fat and safe on stolen earth, full of the traitors' grandchildren."

The cold that went through Tam had nothing to do with the tower air.

"No," he said. "No, the valley's people aren't—they're not traitors, they're just *people*, they're farmers, Wren makes gravestones for people who can't pay, the elders are stupid and frightened but they're not—" He ran out of words and grabbed the only one that mattered. "Freya didn't do it. Freya wasn't even *born*. She broke her own order's rules to get me through the gate. She chose me over the oath."

"I know," Ondrey said gently. "I've watched her walk these streets like a woman taking inventory of a debt. She's a good creature. Best thing this rotten court's seen in a century." He sighed, and the sigh seemed to come up from his boots. "But Ilsevan isn't punishing the valley, boy. That's the trap of her. Punishment would be simple, and simple things fade. She's *restoring*. She's spent two hundred years watching her people go transparent, and she has found the one act in all the world that puts colour back into them, and it requires a valley, and the valley is full of strangers who will never even understand what was taken from them. Justice, she calls it. And the worst part—" his voice dropped further, "—the worst part is that she's not wrong about the debt. She's only wrong about who should pay it."

Tam stared out at the grey city. Somewhere below, the woman was still sweeping her clean street. Somewhere beyond the gate, the valley lay green under a proper sky, unaware, planting winter wheat, arguing about fence posts, alive.

"She showed me the dream," Tam said. "Why show *me*? Why not Freya?"

"Because Freya would stand in the doorway," Ondrey said simply. "You saw it yourself. Arms crossed, waiting out the weather. Ilsevan knows Freya won't move for threats and won't bend for arguments. But you—" he tapped Tam's chest with one rope-hard finger, gently, "—you're the crack in her wall. The keeper saved you. You're the one person alive whose word can reach her before the rite does. Ilsevan showing you the burning is either mercy or strategy, and with queens it's usually both." He paused. "Or it's a test. To see if you'll warn her. Because if you do, and Freya runs, the valley empties itself, and the rite gets its ground without anyone having to be blamed for what the grey finds there."

The tower hummed around them. Tam thought of Freya's hands—capable, chapped, steady on a seal-stone, gentle on his hair the night they'd fled the gate with the world groaning open behind them. He thought of her voice teaching him the difference between remembering and maintaining. He thought of her standing in a burning doorway, saying *you knew.*

If he warned her, he might be playing the queen's game. If he stayed silent, he was keeping the queen's secret, and the secret had Freya's face on it.

Either way, the clock was running. He could feel it now that he knew to feel it—the rite gathering somewhere in the palace's deep rooms like weather, the queen's patience thinning by the hour, the Compact's riders surely not idle beyond the border. Days. Maybe less.

"There's a third thing," Tam said slowly. "Isn't there? There's always a third thing. You've been circling it since I sat down."

Ondrey smiled, and for a moment the lines of his face rearranged into something young. "Clever boy. Yes. There's a third thing, and it's why I keep this bell polished, and it's why I'm the last man in Vessia who hasn't gone transparent—but it's not mine to give, and it isn't safe, and it will cost whoever carries it more than they'll want to pay." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and looked out over his grey kingdom with an expression Tam couldn't read. "Come find me tomorrow, after you've done what you're going to do tonight. Because you *are* going to do it—I can see it eating you. You're going to go to the keeper and tell her what the dream showed you, queen's game or no."

"How do you know?"

"Because she pulled you out of a flood, metaphorically speaking, and people like us don't let our people drown twice." Ondrey clapped him on the shoulder, and the weight of that hand was the realest thing Tam had felt in weeks. "Go on, then. And Tam—" the bell-keeper's voice followed him down the stair, quiet and urgent, "—when you warn her, don't tell her to run. Running is what the dream wants. Tell her to *listen*. There's a difference, and the difference is the third thing, and the third thing is the only door out of this that doesn't burn."

* * *

The queen's wing of the palace was easy to find and hard to approach. Not guarded—nothing here was guarded—but the air changed as Tam came near it, thickening like the air before a storm, and the faded courtiers drifted away from the corridor as if carried on a current. At the end of it, double doors of black wood stood open onto a hall lit with candles that gave light but no heat, and Queen Ilsevan stood at a window looking out at her unmapped country, nearly transparent herself, her profile worn smooth as sea glass.

She didn't turn. "You slept badly," she said. "I felt it from here. The palace tells me things about its guests."

Tam stood in the doorway with his heart going like a hammer and thought: *she'll know. She'll read my face the way Freya reads weather.* And then he thought: *good. Let her.*

"I need to send a message," he said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. "To Freya. Tonight."

The queen turned then, slowly, and her ice-blue eyes settled on him with an expression he couldn't name—something between sorrow and satisfaction, the look of a woman watching a piece slide exactly where she had hoped it would, and hating herself a little for hoping it.

"A message," she repeated softly. "Of course, child. We'll arrange it." And she smiled, and the smile was kind, and kind was the most frightening thing it could have been. "Tell me everything you dreamed."
