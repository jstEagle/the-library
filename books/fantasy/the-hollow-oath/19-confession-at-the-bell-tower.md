## 19. Confession at the Bell Tower

Freya found the queen where she had known she would find her, though no one had told her and no map of the unmapped palace existed to be consulted. She simply climbed, following the sound of bells, up staircases that smelled of cold stone and older incense, through corridors where the tapestries had faded not from age but from being forgotten—thread by thread, color by color, the way a name fades when no one speaks it. The palace of Vessia did not decay. It *diminished*. And Freya, who had spent her whole life in a valley where nothing diminished, felt each step upward as a small theft against her own certainty.

The bell tower stood at the crown of the palace, open to the sky on four sides, its great bronze bell hanging silent in a frame of black oak. Queen Ilsevan stood beneath it with her back turned, her hands folded behind her, watching the horizon. Even from behind, Freya could see how far the fading had gone since their first meeting. The queen's outline wavered now, like heat above summer stone. Where her hair should have caught the grey light, there was only a suggestion of it, a memory of gold worn thin.

"You came without your soldiers," the queen said, without turning. Her voice was still strong. Whatever was eating her country had not yet reached her voice. "That was wise of you. I have very few left to give you, but what I have would have made this conversation difficult."

"I didn't come for a conversation."

"No," Ilsevan said. "You came for an answer. I can hear it in your footsteps. Keepers walk like women carrying questions they've already guessed at." She turned then, and her face was kind, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. "Ask it."

Freya's hands had gone cold inside her gloves. She had rehearsed this on the climb—every word, every accusation, arranged like stones across a ford. Now the arrangement scattered.

"The valley," she said. "The people in it. My people. Tell me where they came from."

The queen regarded her for a long moment. Above them, the great bell stirred in some draft Freya couldn't feel, and gave off a single low note—not rung, merely breathed upon—and the sound went through Freya's chest like a hand pressing on a bruise.

"Sit, if you wish," Ilsevan said. "This is not a short answer. Though perhaps," and something flickered across the ruined landscape of her face that might once have been a smile, "you would prefer to hear it standing. Like a sentence."

* * *

Freya stood.

"Your order calls it the Hollow Gate," the queen began, walking slowly along the tower's edge, her footsteps making no sound at all. "A door sealed by an oath. A ward against magic. A wall between the valley and the wild world beyond. That is what the keepers were taught, generation after generation, each one handing down the lie like a family heirloom, polishing it, never opening the box to see what was actually inside." She paused at a narrow window and looked out over her city—the grey rooftops, the streets where figures moved like reflections in dark water. "Do you know what the gate actually is, Keeper Hartwell?"

"A border."

"A border, yes. But borders are drawn on land, and land does not care about lines. No—" the queen turned, and her eyes, at least, remained vivid, brown and fierce as a hawk's, the last unfaded thing about her— "your gate is a border drawn on *people*. Your founders did not seal away magic. Magic is everywhere; it is in the rain and the bread and the bones of children. What they sealed away was a kingdom. A living nation. Vessia, entire—its cities, its fields, its dead in their graves, its children in their cradles—cut out of the world like a piece cut from cloth, and the edges stitched shut so neatly that within three generations, no one outside remembered the cloth had ever been whole."

"And my valley?" Freya's voice came out flat. "Where does it sit?"

"Ah." The queen closed her eyes briefly. "Now we arrive at it. Your valley, Keeper, sits on Vessian ground. The richest ground we had. The river valley. The orchards. The lowland wheat. When the Compact carved us out of the world, they needed a place to put the gate—a hinge, an anchor for the sealing—and they needed people to tend it. People who would never question, because questioning would cost them everything they knew."

"They took farmers," Freya said slowly. The words tasted like ash. "They took farmers and settled them on stolen land and told them they were holy."

"They took the *dispossessed*," Ilsevan corrected gently, and that gentleness was the worst blade of all. "Refugees. Families broken by wars your founders' patrons had started. They offered them a green valley, safety, prosperity—on one condition. An oath. A duty. Guard the door, keep the seals, ask nothing. And those refugees swore, because their children were hungry, and hunger makes theologians of us all." She let that settle. "Those refugees are your ancestors, Keeper. Their descendants farm the valley floor today. Every cottage, every mill, every fence post in that valley stands on ground that belonged to someone whose name your elders burned."

Freya thought of Wren's face when Wren had spoken of the elders' hidden ledgers. Thought of the square at harvest, the festival lanterns, the children running the keeper's road with garlands in their hair. *Built on it. All of it built on it.* Her whole life, her sacred round, her candles and her seals and her lonely faithful mornings—they were maintenance work. Janitors' duties. The sweeping of a floor over a buried crime.

"You're telling me my people are guilty."

"I am telling you your people were *used*," the queen said, "and then made comfortable, so they would never look down." She moved closer, and Freya fought the urge to step back—not from fear, but from the strange vertigo of proximity to someone half-transparent, as though standing too near might let her see through the queen to the wall behind. "But yes. There is guilt. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. Your founders signed the treaty. Your founders took payment. Your founders chose a valley full of strangers' lives over a nation's survival, and they dressed the choice in holiness because men have always found it easier to pray than to repent." Her voice did not rise. It grew quieter and more precise, the way a blade is honed. "And the comfort they purchased with that betrayal has passed down, hand to hand, like an inheritance. You were raised in a house built on our graves, fed from our orchards, taught to guard the lock on our prison. If none of that is guilt, Keeper, tell me what name it goes by."

* * *

The wind moved through the tower, and for a moment the bell above them hummed with held sound, all its griefs gathered in bronze and waiting.

Freya looked at her own hands. Keeper's hands. Scarred from rope-work on the seals, nicked from chisels, stained with tallow and ink. Hands that had done honest work, she had always believed. Honest, solitary, faithful work. She thought of the oath she'd recited every dawn since she was nine years old, syllables she'd never understood until a soaked child spoke them at her door like keys.

"If what you say is true," she said, "then the debt belongs to the dead who signed. Not to the farmers below. Not to the children."

"Doesn't it?" Ilsevan tilted her head. "Tell me, Keeper—when a man steals a field, and dies rich, and leaves the field to his son, and the son knows—or half-knows, or chooses not to know—whose field it was, and farms it anyway, and his son after him—who owns the harvest?"

"The law would say—"

"Your law. The Compact's law. The law written by thieves." The queen's composure cracked, just once, a hairline fracture through which Freya glimpsed something vast and burning: two hundred years of a whole nation's grief compressed into one woman's chest. "There is no law for what was done to us. That is rather the point. We were erased *lawfully*. Every signature notarized. Every border ratified. The most legal crime in history, and it was committed by your founders with their own pens, and the interest has compounded every year since, paid in what you see around you." She gestured at the tower, the city, the fading light. "Paid in *this*."

Freya wrapped her arms around herself. "So the valley pays. That's your justice. The valley burns, the descendants of the traitors die, and the scales balance."

"The valley *returns*," the queen said softly. "To us. To what it always was. Is that so different from any other restitution? When a thief returns a stolen thing, do we call the returning a punishment?"

"When the thief's grandchildren are living in it. When returning it means—" Freya stopped, because saying it aloud made it real: the valley behind the gate, the smoke of morning fires, Wren's workshop, the children, Tam's small bed in her cottage, all of it consumed by a rite to feed a fading queen's ledger.

"Yes," Ilsevan said quietly. "It means that. I will not dress it in softer words than yours." She came closer still, close enough that Freya could see the tower wall faintly through the queen's shoulder, and her voice dropped to something almost tender. "I know how it sounds. I have had two hundred years to find gentler phrasings and there are none. But consider, Keeper—consider fairly—what your refusal costs. Every year, more of us go. Not killed. *Forgotten*, which is worse, because killing at least admits the victim existed. My archivists cannot remember their own mothers. My soldiers have forgotten why they stand watch. Last winter I lost the word for 'river' in the eastern districts, and now the rivers there run unnamed, and unnamed things fade fastest of all." Her hawk-bright eyes held Freya's. "You hold the last key to the last door of a dying country. And you hesitate because the price is the comfort of the comfortable. Because the innocent must suffer alongside the guilty. Yes. I know. I have lain awake in this tower for decades composing arguments against myself, and I have never once found one that saved both my people and yours. Someone must pay. The only question left to us—*the only question*—is whether it will be a nation that did nothing but exist, or a valley that profited from its burial."

"That's not the only question," Freya said.

"It is the only one that has ever mattered."

"Then you've been asking it wrong for two hundred years."

For the first time, something moved behind the queen's eyes that was not grief or fury—something startled, almost young. "And what would you ask instead, Keeper Hartwell? Speak carefully. I have executed better diplomats than you for less."

* * *

Freya drew a breath that shook going in and came out steady.

"I'd ask who else could pay."

The silence stretched long enough that Freya heard her own heartbeat.

"There is a third party in this debt," she went on, and the words came faster now, tumbling, the argument assembling itself even as she spoke it, stone by stone across the ford. "The Compact. The kingdoms that drew the borders, wrote the treaties, took the payment, and walked away clean. Your quarrel isn't only with a valley of farmers—it's with the heirs of the men who did this, and they're still rich, still sitting on the proceeds, still sending surveyors with humming instruments to hunt a child because he speaks your tongue. They erased you *and* they armed the lock. And when the gate opens—if it opens—they will come to erase you again, permanently, and this time they'll bury the grave too." She stepped forward, into the space between them. "You want to collect from the poorest parties to the crime because they're the ones within reach. That's not justice, Your Majesty. That's just arithmetic."

"Do not lecture me on arithmetic," the queen whispered, "in a country that is running out of numbers."

"Then don't make the valley's children the remainder."

Something passed over Ilsevan's face like cloud-shadow—doubt, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. For a moment Freya believed she had reached her. Then the queen straightened, and the fading outline of her firmed with terrible resolve, the way a dying fire flares before it goes out.

"You are eloquent," she said. "And you are right about the Compact—they are coming, and they will show neither of us mercy. Which is precisely why I cannot afford your third path. Hope is a luxury of nations that still exist, Keeper. Mine requires certainty, and certainty requires the rite, and the rite requires the gate, and the gate requires the key." Her gaze drifted toward the tower stair, downward, toward the rooms where Tam slept under guard she pretended was courtesy. "The boy speaks Vessia as no one born outside these borders ever has. He is the living proof that my country survived its erasure. He is the last native tongue of a nation, and tomorrow he becomes its voice again—willing or not."

Freya's blood went cold. "Tomorrow."

"The Compact's envoys breached the outer marches an hour ago. Treaty-magic, old and ugly. By week's end they will reach the gate, and if it is not opened from this side first, they will seal it forever—with my people entombed and your boy stranded here as a specimen." The queen smiled, and it was the saddest thing Freya had seen in a life of sad vigils. "So you see, Keeper, you need not choose tonight. The choice has been made for us both. Rest while you may. Tomorrow at dusk, Vessia rises."

She turned back to the window, dismissal and despair in equal measure, and Freya descended the stairs with the queen's arithmetic ringing in her ears—and one other sound, lower, from the tower above: the great bell, breathing again in the wind, gathering itself to speak.

She had until dusk.

She had a child to steal, an archive of names to carry, and no plan worth the name—but she had, at least, stopped believing the queen's sums.
