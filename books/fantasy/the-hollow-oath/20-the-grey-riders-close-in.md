## 20. The Grey Riders Close In

They came out of the ash-light like a tide coming over a seawall—slow, inevitable, grey.

Freya saw them first from the bell tower, where she had gone to think and had instead spent an hour watching Ondrey's hands move across the ropes. The old bell-keeper rang no calls anymore; the city's bells rang themselves at odd hours, mourning-sounds from throats that no one pulled. But he climbed anyway, every morning, because his hands remembered work even when his city had forgotten how to need it.

"Riders," she said.

Ondrey did not look up. "I know. I felt them in the rope."

Freya leaned into the tower's open arch and counted. Nine riders on the ridge road east of the city, moving in the tight formation of men who had drilled together until thought became unnecessary. Their livery was the same grey the surveyors had worn in the valley—the Border Compact's colour, if a colour could be called the absence of one—and their instruments were slung across their saddles in oiled canvas: long brass cases, tripod legs, the geometry of things built to measure and to unmake.

"They're not riding for the palace," she said.

"No." Ondrey finally turned, and his face was the last fully solid thing in Vessia—a face with weather in it, lines carved deep by sixty years of wind and rope-burn. "They're riding for the archive."

The word landed in Freya's chest like a dropped stone. The archive. The hall where Tam had knelt among the fallen shelves two nights past, reading names aloud in his clear child's voice while grown citizens wept to hear their own histories spoken. The ledger of a country: every Vessian who had ever drawn breath, written down so the forgetting could not take them whole.

"How fast?"

"By dusk, if they push." Ondrey coiled the last rope with a precision that seemed, in this fading place, almost defiant. "And your queen will not lift a hand to stop them. Her soldiers are memories wearing uniforms. They can hold a line the way a photograph holds a door shut."

* * *

Tam knew they were coming before anyone told him.

He was in the queen's antechamber, sitting where he'd been sat—again—while the rite was prepared, when the feeling came over him. It was the same feeling as the valley, the day the surveyors arrived: a pressure behind his ears, a taste like coins, the sense of something wrong walking toward him on purpose.

He pressed his palm flat against the floor. In Vessia the stones still answered him sometimes—not magic, exactly; the valley had taught him that magic couldn't live here either, not really. But memory lived here, and memory soaked into stone the way rain soaked into chalk. When he touched the flagstones and listened, he could feel footsteps. Old ones. New ones.

These ones were new, and there were nine of them, and they were carrying iron boxes that hummed.

He ran. He didn't ask permission; asking permission took too long and the queen's court moved through honey-thick hours. He ran through corridors hung with portraits whose faces had gone soft as wet paper, down stairs worn hollow by feet that had mostly stopped existing, out into the grey afternoon with his lungs burning and his mother's name caught in his throat like a splinter.

He found Freya in the lower square, talking urgently with three of the less-faded citizens—shopkeepers, or what passed for shopkeepers in a country where goods grew dim on the shelves.

"TAM—" She saw his face and stopped mid-sentence.

"The riders." He bent double, gasping. "Nine of them. Grey coats. The humming boxes—I felt them in the stones. They're going to the archive, I know they are, the stones told me, the way the ground tells you before thunder—"

"When?" Freya's hands were already on his shoulders, steadying him, and Tam noticed—as he always noticed—that her grip never wavered, even now, especially now.

"Dusk. Maybe sooner."

Freya looked at the sky, which in Vessia was always the hour before a decision. Then she looked at the three citizens, and Tam watched something pass between them without words—the look of people who had been robbed so many times they could recognize a thief's walk from a mile off.

"The archive," Freya said. "We move everything we can carry. Now."

* * *

The archive of Vessia stood at the end of a street called the Long Remembering, which had once—Tam said, pointing at foundations under the weeds—been lined with lamplighters' houses. It was a low stone building, older than anything else in the city, its roof sagging but its walls true, because the founders had known that walls were all a country needed to keep its word.

Inside, the damage from the Compact's earlier raid still showed: scorched shelves, ash drifts against the north wall where the first burning had happened, the smell of it still living in the wood. But the citizens had worked since then, and the surviving ledgers had been gathered, wrapped in oilcloth, stacked like sleeping children along the east wall.

There were more than Tam could count. He tried anyway, because counting felt like protecting.

"We take the name-ledgers first," Freya said, already moving, pulling oilcloth back with hands that didn't hesitate. "The civic records after. If we can't save everything, we save the people."

Ondrey arrived with a handcart, then two more citizens with wheelbarrows, then a girl of perhaps fifteen who said nothing but began wrapping ledgers with the efficiency of someone who had done this in a dream many times. The work went fast and silent. Outside, the light thickened toward evening, and somewhere beyond the rooftops, faint as a toothache, Tam could feel the humming getting closer.

It was during the third load that the first rider appeared at the end of the street.

He didn't charge. That was the terrible part. He simply sat his horse at the mouth of the Long Remembering, grey and patient as a headstone, and behind him the other eight resolved out of the dusk one by one until the street was a corridor of them.

A man dismounted. His coat was better than the others', his boots polished, and when he spoke his voice carried the pleasant flatness of someone reading from a document.

"Lady Keeper. Master Tam." He inclined his head to each of them precisely. "Envoy Corvane, Border Compact, Treaty Enforcement Division. I regret the lateness of our arrival. The roads through unmapped territory are difficult to hold in one's mind."

"You burned this place once already," Freya said. She stepped slightly in front of Tam, and Tam hated how much he loved her for it.

"We burned a record of an anomaly." Corvane examined the loaded cart with the mild interest of a man checking inventory. "Treaty Nine-Seven provides that territories rendered non-existent by accord shall remain so. Names are, unfortunately, existence. You're carrying existence in a wheelbarrow, Lady Keeper, and I'm afraid it cannot leave this street."

"And if it does?"

"Then we do what treaties require." He said it without heat, without cruelty, and somehow that was worse. "You've seen our instruments work. Sealing is not violence, whatever your order's romantics claimed. Nothing dies. Things simply... stop being reachable. A border closes like a book."

Tam felt Freya's shoulders change—some tension gathering, some decision being weighed in the muscles of her back. He gripped the handle of the handcart harder.

"There are children in those books," Freya said quietly. "Births. Whole lives. You'll seal living people inside a closed book."

"I'll seal a treaty violation inside its proper covers." Corvane turned and walked back to his horse, unhurried. "You have until full dark to reconsider. After that, my division begins its work, and I should warn you—we've brought enough treaty-magic to close this country permanently. Anyone inside the border when it seals will be sealed with it. Permanently unreachable." He mounted. "Including you, Lady Keeper. Including the boy. The Compact has no extradition arrangements with nowhere."

The nine riders withdrew to the far end of the street and waited, grey shapes against greyer sky, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Inside the archive, the silence afterward was the kind that has weight.

"They mean it," Ondrey said at last. "Full dark. They'll begin."

"Then we have hours to get these carts through streets they control." Freya was pacing now, thinking aloud, her voice tight. "The gate is half-open—it held when Wren came through, it'll hold going back—but the road to it runs past the ridge, and the ridge is where they'll set their instruments, because that's where the border is thinnest—"

"Wren." Tam said the name and then froze, because saying it made him realize something enormous. "Wren came through the gate. From the valley. Alone. At night."

"So?"

"So Wren knows the way through the hills that isn't a road. The mason's paths. Wren cut stone in this valley for thirty years—Wren knows where the ground opens."

Freya stopped pacing. For a moment she just looked at him, and Tam saw the exact moment hope arrived and was immediately distrusted, examined, found possibly genuine.

"That's a child's plan built on a stonemason's legs," she said. "But it might be the only plan we have."

"It's a good plan," Tam said, with the full authority of an eight-year-old.

"It's a desperate one. Those aren't always different." She turned to Ondrey. "How many ledgers can your people move tonight?"

"All of them," Ondrey said simply. "We've been waiting sixty years for someone to give us a reason to run. Don't take that from us now."

They worked through the last of the light. Carts were loaded, hidden in cellar doors along the street, distributed among houses so no single loss would be total. The faded citizens moved with a strange grace, passing ledgers hand to hand like a bucket line fighting a fire that had been burning for two generations.

And then, just as the first stars appeared in the unchanging Vessian sky, the air at the edge of the square shivered.

Not the riders. Not the Compact. Something smaller, wearier, covered in valley dust and breathing hard.

Wren stepped out of the shadow of the bell tower, a maul over one shoulder and blood on one sleeve, and looked around at the grey city with an expression Tam had seen once before on Freya's face—the look of someone seeing the impossible and refusing to be impressed by it.

"Gate's still holding," Wren said, "but barely, and I've got news that won't keep." Wren's eyes found Freya's, and Tam watched the stonemason's face go grim as quarry rock. "The Compact's second force reached the valley an hour ago. Twelve more riders, heavy instruments. They're not waiting for negotiation, Freya. They're setting the sealing array on the valley side of the gate right now."

Freya went very still. "Sealing from both sides."

"At dawn," Wren said. "Maybe sooner. And here's the rest of it, and you'd best hear it standing up." Wren wiped dust and sweat from their face with the back of one bloodied hand. "The elders sent me with terms. Their terms. The Compact offered the valley a covenant—leave the gate to be sealed permanent and whole, and the valley keeps its peace, its protections, its quiet. All they ask in return is that the valley formally renounce the keepers. Your office, Freya. Your name struck from the rolls. You and the boy left on whichever side of that door closes last."

The square was silent except for the bells, ringing their ownerless mourning overhead.

"They sold you," Wren said, and their voice cracked on it, angry and ashamed at once. "The same as the founders sold Vessia. Sixty years of silence and now this. I told them what they could do with their covenant, and I ran the mason's paths all night to tell you, because somebody in that valley had to refuse to sign."

Freya sat down slowly on a cart of rescued names, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. Tam came and stood beside her, and put his hand on the oilcloth over the ledgers, and felt the names underneath, warm as sleeping birds.

"Dawn," Freya said finally. "Both sides. We're sealed in a dead country with a queen who wants a child for her rite, and the only door out closes in six hours."

"Aye." Wren hefted the maul. "So. What's the plan, Keeper?"

Freya looked at the grey riders at the end of the street. At the fading city. At Tam.

"We're going to need a bigger miracle than a mason's path," she said, "but start with the path. We move tonight—all of us, all the names. And Wren—" her voice hardened into something Tam had never heard in it before, something forged rather than born, "—when we reach that gate, I'm not going through it to escape. I'm going through it to end this. Whatever it costs."

Above them, the bells of Vessia began to ring, though no hand touched them, and this time Tam understood what they were saying.

Hurry, the bells said. Hurry. Hurry.
