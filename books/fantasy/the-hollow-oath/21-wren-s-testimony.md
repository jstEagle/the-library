## 21. Wren's Testimony

Wren had cut stone for thirty years, and in all that time had learned one law above every other: a wall tells you what it's hiding, if you know how to listen.

The listening was the hard part.

* * *

The summons came at first light, before the frost had lifted off the mason's yard. A boy from the elder's row — not a child of the valley proper but one of the Hartwell cousins from the low farms, all elbows and borrowed gravity — stood at Wren's door with his breath steaming and his eyes fixed anywhere but on Wren's face.

"Elders want you at the meeting house," he said. "Before the market bell."

"Before the market bell." Wren wiped stone dust from their hands and considered the boy. "That's an hour I haven't been summoned to since the flood year."

"They said it was about the gate."

Something moved through Wren then, cold and quick as water finding a crack. "About the gate," they repeated.

"The keeper's gone," the boy said, and now he did look up, and there was fear in him, plain as a chisel mark. "Ain't nobody seen her since two nights past. And the elders are asking questions about the old work. The *old* old work. They want a mason who knows the foundations."

Wren knew the foundations. That was the trouble.

"I'll come," they said, and reached for their coat, and did not let themselves think about how strange it was that the elders needed a stonemason's testimony at all. The valley had kept its own counsel for longer than anyone living could count. It had never once asked its people what they knew.

It was asking now. Which meant something had broken.

* * *

The meeting house was the oldest building in the valley, older than the church, older than the mill, older — though nobody said this aloud — than any of the families who claimed deep roots here. It was built of the same grey stone as everything else, but Wren had always been able to feel the difference under their palms: the meeting house walls were cut by hands that understood stone as something alive. Every block sat against its neighbor the way a word sits against the next word in a sentence too old to translate.

Six elders sat around the long table. Old Marta Corrin at the head, her knuckles like river stones. Bram Hollis, who ran the granary and had never in his life given away so much as a handful of barley without exact accounting. The others Wren knew by name and trade and nothing deeper, because that was how the valley worked — you knew your neighbors' surfaces the way you knew the surface of a pond, and you did not go wading.

There was one empty chair. Everyone's eyes touched it and slid away. The keeper's chair. Freya Hartwell had not sat in it since she was nineteen years old and newly sworn, and it had stayed empty for her the way a hearth stays swept for someone expected home.

"Wren." Marta Corrin's voice was dry as lime mortar. "Sit. We have questions, and we're told you're the one who can answer them true."

"Told by who?"

"By your grandmother," said Bram Hollis, "before she died. She left a letter with us. To be opened only if the keeper failed to appear for three rounds running."

The words went into Wren like a nail driven clean to the heartwood. Three rounds. Freya had missed three rounds of the keeper's circuit. Whatever was happening at that gate, it had gone past the point where the valley could pretend not to notice.

"My grandmother," Wren said carefully, "was a mason. Like me. Like her mother."

"Your grandmother," said Marta, "was the last of the *cutters*. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, and it's the difference we've come to talk about."

Wren sat down slowly. Their hands, which had never shaken over stone, were not entirely steady over the table's old wood.

* * *

Here is what Wren knew, and had known since they were nine years old, kneeling in the foundation trench of the new tithe barn with their grandmother's hand heavy on their shoulder:

The valley was not built. It was *arranged*.

Every child learns the story: the founders came over the mountains generations ago, fleeing wars no one names anymore, and found a green hollow where the air sat quiet and no storm ever broke, and they gave thanks and settled. A blessed valley. A sheltered valley. The word everyone used was *sheltered*, and it was always said warmly, the way you'd say *beloved*.

But a mason's hands learn other stories. When Wren was nine, digging that foundation trench, their spade struck something that rang wrong — not rock, not root, but a flat, deliberate surface. Their grandmother had climbed into the trench, looked at what the spade had uncovered, and gone very still.

It was a boundary stone. Cut square, dressed smooth, and carved along one face with a line of letters in no alphabet Wren had ever been taught — letters that seemed to shift when looked at directly, the way water shifts over stones.

"What is it?" Wren had asked.

Their grandmother had looked at the stone for a long moment. Then she had taken off her coat, laid it over the carving, and said, in a voice Wren had never heard her use before or since — a voice like a door being closed:

"It's nothing. It's old field marking. Fill the trench and don't speak of it."

They had filled the trench. But Wren had felt, through the soles of their boots, the way the ground hummed above that buried stone — a hum like a held breath — and they had spent thirty years not speaking of it, and thirty years listening anyway.

Because a mason listens. That was the whole trade. And what Wren had heard, over thirty years of laying foundations and repairing walls and cutting grave-slabs, was this:

The valley's oldest buildings all faced the same way. Not toward the sun, not toward the road, not toward the church. Every structure raised by the founding generation — the meeting house, the first millrace, the low arches of the spring houses — was oriented toward the Hollow Gate, the way iron filings orient toward a lodestone. As if the whole settlement were less a village than a garrison. As if the founders had not come here to build a home but to *hold a position*.

And the graves. Wren had cut enough grave-slabs to know the valley's dead better than its living. The oldest graves in the churchyard — the ones worn featureless, older than any family name still current — all lay with their feet pointing *away* from the gate. All of them. Without exception. In a valley where every other tradition had bent and frayed with time, that one rule had held absolute across centuries, and no one living remembered why, and no one living had ever thought to ask.

Except, apparently, the dead had been made to face that way on purpose. You do not arrange a graveyard by accident. You arrange it the way you arrange a defensive line.

* * *

"You want to know what I know," Wren said to the table of elders, and their voice came out flatter than they intended. "I'll tell you what I know. I know my grandmother covered a carved stone with her coat and called it field marking. I know every old foundation in this valley points at the gate like a compass needle. I know our dead are buried facing out, like sentries. And I know that none of you ever asked me about any of it until the day the keeper disappeared."

Silence. The fire cracked in the meeting house hearth, and outside, the market bell rang the hour, and no one moved.

Then Marta Corrin did something Wren had never seen an elder do. She reached into the breast of her dress and drew out a letter — yellowed, sealed with plain wax, addressed in a hand Wren recognized with a jolt: their grandmother's, the same firm strokes that had marked every cutting order Wren ever received.

"She left two letters," Marta said. "One for the elders. One for the mason. We were told to deliver yours only after you'd spoken, so we'd know whether you'd been told enough already." She set it on the table and pushed it across the wood. "You've spoken. Read it."

Wren broke the seal. The wax crumbled like old bread.

*To whoever cuts stone after me —*

*I hope it's you, little wren. I hope it's nobody.*

*You'll have noticed by now what the old work points at. I'm sorry for that. Noticing is the burden our family has carried since the beginning, and we carried it alone because the others chose not to carry anything at all. Understand this clearly, because it's the truth at the bottom of the well:*

*The founders didn't find this valley. They bought it. Or rather, they were paid to take it — paid in land and title and silence by kingdoms whose names were written in the ledger we keep locked under the meeting house floor. What they took was a border. The valley isn't sheltered, child. It's a wall. A wall with people living inside it, comfortable, generation after generation, on ground that was taken to build the wall itself.*

*The gate doesn't keep magic out. It keeps a country out. A country called Vessia, which the world agreed to forget, and which our founders helped make forgotten, because forgetting was the price of the quiet air and the kind seasons and the good soil we've farmed ever since.*

*We are not the keepers' neighbors. We are the keepers' accomplices. Every harvest grown on this soil, every child raised in these houses, every winter passed warm — all of it bought with a country's erasure. The elders have always known. The elders have always chosen the harvest.*

*If you're reading this, the bill has come due. Don't let them tell you we didn't know. We knew. God forgive us, we knew, and we planted anyway.*

*Cut true, little wren. And when they ask you to lie for them again — as they will ask, as they have always asked — remember the boundary stone under the tithe barn, and remember that stone does not care whose comfort it serves. Stone only remembers what was done to it.*

*— Your grandmother, who was a coward, and loved you*

Wren read it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading had gone through them like cold water through a crack, pooling somewhere beneath thought.

The elders watched. None of them looked surprised. That was the worst of it — none of them looked surprised. Marta Corrin's face was composed as a cut lintel. Bram Hollis studied his own folded hands. These were people who had received their own version of this letter years ago, perhaps decades ago, and had folded it away and gone on weighing grain and settling disputes and presiding over festivals, and had said nothing to anyone, ever, because saying something would have cost them the valley's peace, and the valley's peace was worth more than the truth. It had always been worth more than the truth. That was the founding transaction, repeated faithfully every single day.

"You knew," Wren said. Their voice sounded foreign in their own ears. "All of you. You've known since before I was born."

"We knew what we were told to know," said Bram Hollis, and even now his voice had the careful cadence of a man counting stock. "The Compact sends its surveyors every few years. The Compact holds the treaty. The Compact says the arrangement stands, and so the arrangement stands. That's not betrayal, mason. That's continuity."

"Continuity." Wren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "You mean the harvest. You mean the quiet air. You mean we sold a country and we've been spending the money for six generations and calling it providence."

"We mean the valley survived," Marta Corrin said, and for just a moment something flickered behind her composure — something tired and old and possibly ashamed, buried deep, like the boundary stone under the tithe barn. "When the world outside tore itself apart, war after war, plague after plague — we were safe. Our children lived. Is that a crime?"

"It's a *price*," Wren said. "Paid by somebody else. Paid by people who had names, who had a country, who had children of their own — and we buried our dead facing away from them so we'd never have to look."

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past on the market road, ordinary as morning, and Wren thought: *this is what it sounds like inside a lie. This is exactly what it sounds like. Bells and carts and frost lifting off the fields.*

Home had always been the simplest thing in Wren's life. Stone was complicated; people were complicated; but home had been a fixed point, load-bearing, unquestioned. Now they sat in the oldest room of that home and understood that its every foundation pointed at a wound, that its peace was a payment, that the kindness of the seasons was interest collected on a theft. The valley had not changed. The valley had been exactly this all along. What had changed was Wren, and there was no unseeing it, no more than a mason can unsee a crack once it's found — you can plaster over it, you can paint it, but your thumb will always find it, always, every time you pass.

"The keeper," Wren said at last. "Freya. Did she know any of this?"

"No," Marta said. "The keepers were kept honest. Kept ignorant. It was easier to make them guard the door if they believed it was a holy duty instead of a dirty one." She paused. "She's beyond the gate now. With the boy. And the Compact's riders came through yesterday asking after both of them, with papers that give them authority to — " Marta stopped, and chose her next words with visible care. "To resolve the situation."

"Resolve it how?"

"That," said Bram Hollis, "depends partly on you."

"On *me*?"

"The Compact's envoys want the old records. The ledger under this floor. The survey maps. Everything that documents the original arrangement." His eyes went to the letter in Wren's hands. "Your grandmother's family cut the boundary stones, mason. Your family holds the knowledge of where every marker lies, every anchor-point of the seal. If the Compact means to close the border permanently — to seal it so nothing crosses either way, ever again — they'll need a cutter who knows the old work."

The meeting room went very quiet. Wren understood, with the clarity that comes in the moment before a wall fails, what was being asked. Not asked — *arranged*, the way the valley arranged everything, smoothly, without anyone having to say the ugly thing aloud. They wanted Wren to guide the Compact's hands. To help drive the final nails. To cut the last stone over the tomb of a murdered country, and be paid in the valley's continuing comfort, the same wage their family had drawn for six generations.

"And if I refuse?" Wren asked.

Marta Corrin met their eyes, and in hers there was no threat, which was somehow worse than a threat. There was only the long patience of someone who had watched every cutter before this one arrive at this same crossroads and choose the harvest.

"Then you'll be refusing your grandmother's whole life," she said gently. "And ours. And the valley's. Think on it, Wren. Think on what home costs, and who pays it, and whether you're the one who gets to decide the bill has been paid enough."

* * *

Wren walked home through the market square without seeing a single stall, a single face. The valley went on around them, warm and ordinary: bread baking, children shouting by the well, an old man mending a fence rail with unhurried hands. Six generations of stolen peace, wearing aprons and straw hats. Every smile suddenly a signature on a contract Wren had never been allowed to read.

At the yard, Wren did not go inside. They went to the back wall — the old wall, the one section of their property built on foundations older than the cottage itself — and pressed their palm flat against the stone, the way they had done since childhood, the way their grandmother had taught them without ever explaining why.

*Listen,* the lesson had been. *Stone remembers.*

And the stone remembered. Under Wren's hand, deep below the frost line, the buried boundary stones of the valley hummed their held breath, and threaded through that hum, faint as a voice through a wall, came something new. Something that had not been there a week ago.

Singing. Far off, on the other side of everything, a country that did not exist was singing.

Wren took their hand off the wall as if burned. Then, deliberately, they went to the shed, took down their heaviest chisel and their father's old sledge, and began loading a cart — not with tools for the Compact's work, but with rope, bread, oilskin, and the long iron pry-bar used for shifting foundation stones.

Because there was one thing Wren knew that the elders had not counted on, one thing a mason knows and a magistrate doesn't: a wall built to be permanent is built by people who assume no one will ever stand on the *other side* of it, pulling.

Somewhere beyond the gate, a keeper and a child were trapped in a country the world had buried alive. And the Compact meant to finish the burial with Wren's family's chisels.

Over their dead craft, they would.

Wren threw the tarp over the cart, harnessed the mule, and turned toward the ridge road — toward the Hollow Gate, and the groan of ancient seals, and whatever waited on the far side of the wall their ancestors had built.

Behind them, in the meeting house, Marta Corrin was already writing to the Compact's envoys.
