## 22. The Queen's Coronation Oath

The summons came at the hour the bells went quiet.

Freya had learned the palace's rhythms in the three days since Wren's warning—when the bells rang, the city stirred faintly, like a sleeper turning over; when they stopped, the stillness deepened until it felt like drowning slowly in grey water. So when every bell in Ilsevan fell silent at once, all of them, from the great cracked bell in the tower above the gate to the small hand-bells in the shrine niches, she knew before the messenger arrived what it meant.

*It is time,* the messenger said—a woman in court grey whose face was smooth as river stone, her eyes holding that terrible half-transparency Freya had come to dread, as though someone had begun erasing her and stopped halfway through to reconsider. *Her Majesty requests the keeper's presence at the Coronation Rite.*

"Coronation," Freya repeated. "Vessia has a queen."

"The rite is not for crowning." The woman's mouth barely moved. "It is for *returning*. But it is called coronation because a country is crowned with its borders, and its borders are crowned with its name, and its name is crowned with—" She paused, and something flickered behind her eyes, some last unerased instinct toward honesty or perhaps only confusion. "—with a voice. Come and see. Her Majesty wishes you to witness."

Freya's hands had gone cold. She thought of Tam, who had been taken that morning to be fitted with ceremonial robes—the queen's tailor had come with measuring cords woven from something pale and fine, and Tam had looked back at her over his shoulder with an expression she had told herself was only nervousness. A child dressed for a ceremony. Children were dressed for ceremonies all the time. It meant nothing.

It meant nothing, she had told herself, the way one tells oneself anything that must be true in order to keep walking.

"I'll come," she said.

* * *

They led her down through the palace's lower levels, past halls where the architecture grew older and stranger the deeper they went—walls of fitted stone without mortar, then walls of a material she had no name for, dark and faintly warm to the touch, veined with lines of silver that pulsed very slowly, like the heartbeat of something enormous sleeping beneath the city. The air changed too. Above, Vessia smelled of ash and cold bells and old rain. Down here it smelled of earth after lightning, of green things growing in the dark, of a magic so old and concentrated that even Freya, who had spent her life in a valley where such things could not exist, felt it press against her skin like deep water.

The chamber at the bottom was round, and vast, and wrong.

Wrong, she thought, in the way the sky beyond the gate had been wrong—that first glimpse through the breaking seal, when she'd seen a road and a city under a sky the color of a bruise healing. This room had been built by people who understood that a border is not a wall but a wound, and who had built accordingly: the floor was a map. Not drawn on the floor—the floor *was* the map, rivers running in channels of actual water, mountains rising in ridges of actual stone, forests rendered in living moss gone grey as old fur. And around the edges, where the map ended, ran a ring of empty space, a moat of blankness, waiting.

The borders of Vessia, missing their final ring. The shape of a country with its edges torn off.

Tam stood at the center of the map, on a low dais of white stone, and Freya's heart stopped and restarted painfully.

He wore robes of undyed wool, heavy and plain, belted with a cord of the same pale fiber as the tailor's measuring strings. His hair had been combed. Someone had washed his face. He looked, horribly, like a child prepared for church—and he was looking around the chamber with wide eyes, and when he saw her his whole body turned toward her like a plant toward sun, and his mouth opened—

"Keeper." Queen Ilsevan descended the steps at the chamber's far side, and the court parted for her, and Freya understood that whatever she said next would happen in front of two hundred fading witnesses and would change nothing unless she chose her ground carefully. "You honor us."

"Your Majesty." Freya bowed the minimum the moment required. Up close, the queen was worse than she remembered. Three days ago Ilsevan had been a woman with translucent patches, like frost on a window. Now the frost had spread. Freya could see the pillars behind her through her shoulder. Only her eyes remained fully solid, and they burned with a ferocity that seemed to consume everything else about her, fueling itself on her own substance. "I wasn't aware I'd be attending a coronation."

"A return," Ilsevan said again. "Words matter tonight, keeper. Tonight we speak the world back into its proper shape." She gestured, and a courtier brought forward a cushion of grey velvet, and on the cushion lay a book.

Freya knew books. She had spent her life among them—the gate's registers, her mentor's letters, the order's dry and lying chronicles. This was not a book in any way she knew. Its cover was made of pressed flowers gone brown with age, hundreds of them, layered and lacquered, and beneath the flowers she could see the shadow of pages that seemed to move slightly, like leaves stirring in wind that did not reach this far underground.

"The Ledger of Voices," Ilsevan said. "Every citizen of Vessia, entered by hand, from the first year of my line to the last. When a child is born, their name is spoken into this book and the book remembers it. When a person dies, the name is spoken again, and released." Her transparent fingers hovered above the cover without touching it. "There are four hundred thousand names in this book, keeper. Four hundred thousand voices. And outside these borders, not one of them is remembered. Not one. Your order saw to that."

"I know what my order did," Freya said. "I've read the record. I came here knowing it."

"Then you know why the rite requires what it requires." The queen's voice softened, and that softness frightened Freya more than any threat could have. "A border is a promise made in a voice. To restore the border, the promise must be renewed—in the tongue the land was named in, by the last throat that carries it natively. There is exactly one such throat left in all the world." Her burning eyes moved, gently, terribly, to the dais. "And he is standing on it."

* * *

"No."

The word came out of Freya flat and hard, a door slamming. Heads turned throughout the court. Somewhere in the ranks of the faded, a woman made a small sound—not disapproval; something closer to hunger.

"Keeper." Ilsevan did not raise her voice. She never needed to. "Walk with me."

"I'm not leaving him."

"You will not be leaving him. You will be walking ten paces, and he will remain in your sight the entire time, and if you cannot manage ten paces of conversation then you are not the woman who crossed a dead country to argue justice with me." The queen turned and walked, and after a heartbeat of fury so dense it tasted like copper, Freya followed—ten paces, eleven, keeping Tam in the corner of her eye the whole while. He watched her back. She made herself smile at him. He did not smile back.

"The boy speaks the language," Ilsevan said quietly. "Natively. Instinctively. He does not merely know it; it knows him. Do you understand what that means? The land of Vessia was named in that language. Every river, every hill, every boundary-stone. The words and the ground are the same thing—they always were; that is what your order never comprehended, what none of the Compact kingdoms ever comprehended. You think names are labels. Here, names are *bones*." She stopped beside the map's edge, beside the blank ring where the borders should have been. "To restore the border, the land must hear its own tongue spoken over it, from its center, by its last native child. That is all the rite is. Words. He stands there, he speaks the naming, and Vessia returns."

"And what happens to him?" Freya asked. "When he finishes speaking?"

Ilsevan was silent for a moment too long.

"That long enough," Freya said. "That silence is answer enough."

"The rite takes what it takes." The queen's jaw tightened, and for an instant the transparency in her face seemed to recede, as if anger briefly made her more solid than grief ever could. "I will not lie to you, keeper. I have watched my people fade for sixty years. I have signed the death warrants of entire villages with a stamp that says *relocated*. I have buried citizens who dissolved before the shovels were ready. And now I am offered the restoration of everything—all of it, every field and family and bell—if one child gives his voice to the land that made him." Her hands, those half-gone hands, closed into fists. "Do you imagine I have not lain awake weighing it? Do you imagine I do not see his face when I close my eyes? He has your stubbornness, you know. He refused the fitting robes twice. He asked me if his mother would be restored. I told him yes. I should not have promised what I cannot—"

"What happens to him," Freya said again, each word dropped like a stone into a well, "when he finishes speaking."

Ilsevan looked at her, and for the first time since the throne room, Freya saw the queen look away first.

"The naming requires a voice given wholly," she said. "Not a recitation. A giving. The child would speak until the speaking was done, and the speaking of four hundred thousand names, in the old cadence, over the full measure of the land—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He would not survive the rite, keeper. The land would take his voice entire, and a person is not a person without a voice. He would become the border. That is what a border is. That is what they have always been. Someone gave themselves, once, so that a line could stand between a people and the wolves. We built our oldest kingdom on that truth and called it holy."

The chamber tilted. Freya heard her own breathing, ragged and loud. On the dais, Tam shifted his weight and tugged at the belt of his ceremonial robe, an ordinary child's fidget, and the sight of it nearly broke her open.

"He's eight years old," she said.

"He is the last Vessian."

"He is a *boy* who fell through a gate."

"He is the key," Ilsevan said, "and keys are made to turn," and something in Freya went past anger into a place she had never been, a cold bright country where the heart beats very slowly and the hands are perfectly steady.

"Then find another key."

"There is no other. I have searched for sixty years. Speakers die, keeper, or fade, or are hunted by your Compact friends—and the ones who remain remember only fragments, prayers and songs, not the full naming. The bloodline narrowed and narrowed until it came down to one child wandering out of a snowstorm onto your doorstep." The queen's burning gaze fixed on her. "Fate, if you believe in fate. Or the last cruelty of a history that has never once spared us. Either way, the mathematics do not change. One child, or four hundred thousand dead and dying. One voice, or a nation's."

"And you can say that," Freya whispered, "with your own grandson's age face looking at you from the archive portraits—"

"Do not." The word cracked across the chamber like ice splitting. Around them the court flinched as one body. Ilsevan stood trembling, and Freya saw, with a strange distant clarity, that the queen's fury was costing her substance—that each word spent in rage visibly thinned her, the way a candle spends itself fastest when it gutters. "You do not get to invoke my dead at me, keeper. You do not get to stand in the house their betrayal emptied and lecture me on the cost of children. My son died unnamed. Unnamed! The book has his entry and the world has his absence and there is not one grave anywhere outside these borders that bears his name because *your order sold the very concept of his name*—" She caught herself. Drew breath. When she spoke again her voice was level and cold and infinitely tired. "Yes. I can say it. I have had sixty years to learn how. Justice is not gentle, Freya Hartwell. I warned you of that in the throne room, and you stayed anyway. You read the record of the betrayal and you stayed. Why do you suppose that was? Because some part of you already knew that a debt this size could only be paid in the coin you least wish to spend."

* * *

They let her go to him then. Perhaps the queen calculated that Freya's presence would steady the boy for the rite; perhaps even Ilsevan had limits to what she could watch. Freya crossed the map of Vessia—her boots splashing shallowly in the stone-cut river channels, crushing grey moss beneath her heels, walking the whole breadth of a murdered country in forty steps—and climbed the dais, and took Tam's face in her hands.

His cheeks had been scrubbed pink. His eyes were red-rimmed but dry, and that was worst of all, because she knew what it meant when a child this young had already learned to ration his tears.

"Hey," she said softly. "Look at me. Just me."

"I know what it does," Tam whispered. "She told me. She said it would hurt and then it would stop hurting and then I'd be part of the country forever and everyone would come back." His chin trembled once and steadied. "Is that true? Will everyone come back?"

Freya's throat closed. She thought of the faded court above, the hollow-eyed men and women who had begged her in whispers to refuse the queen; she thought of Ondrey the bell-keeper, solid as oak amid all that translucence, saying *there is a third path, keeper, if you're brave enough to hate the right things*; she thought of the archive, the salvaged ledger pages hidden beneath the floor of the bell tower, four hundred thousand names waiting for a voice that did not have to be a child's.

"It might be true," she said honestly, because he deserved honesty more than comfort. "But listen to me. Listen." She waited until his eyes locked on hers. "Nothing happens tonight. Whatever she says, whatever the robed ones chant, however far the rite gets—you are not a key. Keys don't get to say no. You do. Can you remember that? You can always say no."

"But she said—"

"I know what she said." Freya glanced up. Across the chamber, the queen had begun to don her own raiment—a mantle of grey worked with silver thread, attended by courtiers whose hands passed through the fabric as often as gripped it. The court was arranging itself in rings around the map, outer to inner, eldest to youngest, a country gathering to watch itself be reborn. Torches Freya hadn't noticed before were being lit along the walls, and their flames burned a color she didn't like: a pale, hungry violet, the color of the sky she'd seen through the broken seal. "Tam. When I nod—you remember the game we practiced. The words that sound almost right but aren't. *Vessia* instead of *Veshia*. The river-name that wants an extra syllable. Everything we drilled in the bell tower."

His eyes widened. "Here? Now?"

"Now or never, love." She smoothed his hair, once, the way her mentor Maren had once smoothed hers, a hundred years of keepers compressed into a single gesture. "You bought us hours once already. Buy me enough to finish what I started."

Below the dais, a horn sounded—one long note, ancient and cracked, blown by a herald whose lips were nearly transparent enough to see the horn's mouthpiece through. Queen Ilsevan ascended the steps to the edge of the map, the Ledger of Voices borne before her on its cushion, and raised her arms, and the violet torchlight bent toward her as if leaning in to listen.

"Citizens of Vessia," the queen said, and her voice filled the round chamber the way water fills a vessel, "we begin the return."

The first syllable of the rite rolled out over the map, and every river channel in the stone floor began, very softly, to flow.

Freya took Tam's hand and waited for her moment, watching the queen's mouth shape the opening invocation, counting the cadence, finding the seam in the ceremony where a mispronounced word would bite deepest—and found, instead, that she was already praying to no one at all that Ondrey's third path was real, because in ninety seconds' time she was going to need a miracle with a hook in it.
