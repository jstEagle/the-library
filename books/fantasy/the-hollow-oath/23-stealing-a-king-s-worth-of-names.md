## 23. Stealing a King's Worth of Names

They taught him to lie with his mouth before they taught him anything else in the valley, Tam thought later — though back then, standing on the cold marble of the unmapped palace with a hundred faded faces watching him, it didn't feel like lying. It felt like holding something in his hands and pretending his hands were empty.

Freya had drilled him through the night, her voice low under the whisper of the bell tower, her finger tracing the shapes of the rite in the dust of the floor.

"You know these words better than anyone alive," she'd said. "That's why they need you. And that's why you can break them."

"I don't want to break them," Tam had said. "I want to say them right."

"I know." She had knelt so their eyes were level, and her face had been tired in a way he'd never seen before, tired all the way down to the bone. "But listen to me, Tam. If you say them right, the rite finishes tonight. And when it finishes, the gate opens all the way, and everything behind it — Wren, the valley, every living soul there — burns to feed the borders coming back. Do you understand? Your mother's house comes back. And it stands on ashes."

He hadn't answered. He had thought about his mother's outline in the doorway of their house, the way she had almost-remembered him, the way her hand had almost-touched his hair. He wanted her whole more than he wanted almost anything in the world.

Almost.

"So," Freya had said, "we steal time. You're the only one who can speak the rite. Which means you're the only one who can decide how fast it goes."

"How do I slow it down?"

A small, fierce smile had crossed her face — the same one she wore when she mended a seal on the gate and dared the world to object. "You're eight years old and you learned two languages. Mispronounce."

* * *

The throne room of Vessia had been beautiful once. Tam could see its bones under the grey: pillars carved with wheat and rivers, windows of colored glass that should have thrown red and gold across the floor but only threw a pale wash, like light through dirty ice. The court stood in ranks on either side of the long carpet, lords and ladies and soldiers in uniforms whose colors had drained out of them, and none of them moved except to breathe, and some of them didn't seem to do even that.

Queen Ilsevan sat on a throne of black wood at the far end, and she was the most solid thing in the room, which wasn't saying much. Tam could see the pillar behind her shoulder through her sleeve.

"Begin," she said.

Tam stood at the center of the room, on a circle of silver inlaid in the floor — the keyhole, Freya had called it, though it looked more like a sun to him, or a wheel. Around the rim of the circle, the first words of the rite were engraved, and the archive lay open on a stand beside him, the great ledger of names, every Vessian who had ever been counted, and his own name was in it, near the end, in his mother's handwriting. He had found it the night before. *Tamasin vel Orrey.* It had made his chest hurt in a way he couldn't explain, seeing himself written down like he was real.

He put his hand on the ledger. He took a breath. And he began.

*"Vessia, hear your children—"*

The words came out of him easily; that was the terrible part. They lived in his mouth the way his own name did. But he bent them as he spoke, just slightly, the way Freya had shown him — a vowel stretched too long here, a consonant swallowed there, the accent of a child who had been away from home too long and half-forgotten his mother's tongue. Every mistake was small. Every mistake was true enough that no one could call it false. And every mistake made the silver circle under his feet glow a little less.

He felt it happen. When he spoke clean — really clean, the way the words wanted to be said — the circle warmed under his bare feet like a stone in afternoon sun, and something far away turned over, the gate in the valley shifting in its sleep. When he spoke crooked, the warmth faded, and the turning stopped.

The queen leaned forward on her throne.

"Again," she said, when he finished the first passage. "From the top. Properly this time."

"That was proper," Tam said. "That's how we say it."

"It is not how *I* say it."

"You've been gone a long time," Tam said, and immediately wished he could pull the words back, because the whole court went still, the way a forest goes still when something predatory moves through it.

But the queen only smiled, and her smile was worse than anger. "Yes," she said softly. "I have. Begin again, child. Slower. We have all the patience in the world. It is the one thing we have left."

* * *

They broke at midday — if it was midday; the grey light outside the windows never changed — and a servant led Tam to a small room off the throne room where bread and cheese waited on a table. The servant was a woman with kind eyes and edges that kept going soft, like a drawing left out in the rain. She watched him eat with an expression he couldn't read.

"You speak beautifully," she said. "For someone raised outside."

"Thank you," Tam said, because Freya had told him: be polite, be small, be forgettable. A forgettable child buys hours.

"My son spoke like you," the woman said. "Before." She touched her own cheek, as if checking whether her face was still there. "He would be grown now. If growing were still a thing that happened here."

Tam's throat went tight. "What was his name?"

She frowned, and Tam watched her reach for it and miss, her hand closing on empty air, and something in his chest cracked open. That was what forgetting did. It didn't kill you. It just made you reach for your own child and find nothing there.

"I don't—" she began, and then the queen's voice came through the wall, thin and clear as a wire: *"Bring the boy back."*

The woman straightened at once, her grief folding itself away like laundry, and held the door for him. "Don't keep Her Majesty waiting," she said, and her voice was pleasant and hollow, and Tam walked past her with his bread unfinished because finishing it felt like agreeing to something.

* * *

The second session was worse, because the queen had learned.

She didn't ask him to repeat passages anymore. She came down from the throne herself — Tam hadn't expected that, hadn't expected the Queen of Vessia to stand three feet away from him, close enough that he could see the fine cracks spreading through her like frost on a window — and she listened with her head tilted, cataloguing every error.

"*Vel*," she said, after his third attempt at the opening invocation. "You said *vul*. In the old speech, *vel* means 'belonging to.' *Vul* means 'buried under.' You are telling your country its children are buried beneath it."

"They sound the same," Tam said.

"They do not." She crouched to his height, and up close her eyes were the worst thing he had ever seen, because they weren't cruel. They were desperate. "Say it again. *Vel.*"

"*Vel,*" Tam said, perfectly, because refusing outright would get him taken away from the rite, and Freya had been very clear: never refuse. Refuse and they find another way, another voice, another key. Bend. Only bend.

"Good," the queen breathed. "Again the passage. All of it."

And so it went, hour after hour, the queen circling him like a patient hawk, catching his errors one by one, correcting them, making him say each word clean before moving on — and Tam discovered the awful arithmetic of it. Every correction she made bought him less. In the morning, his mistakes had slowed the rite to a crawl. By late afternoon, she had sanded most of them away, and the silver circle glowed warmer and warmer under his feet, and somewhere beyond the walls, beyond the grey country, he could feel the Hollow Gate straining against its seals like a dog hearing its name.

His hands had started to shake around the middle of the afternoon. Not from fear, exactly. From the effort of holding two things in his mouth at once: the truth of the words and the lie he was telling with them. His tongue knew how the rite should sound. Making it sound wrong was like walking with one shoe on backward — possible, but every step cost something.

The court watched. That was the part that frightened him most. They never spoke, never shifted, never whispered to each other. They just stood in their ranks with their hollow eyes and watched a child dismantle and reassemble the last hope of their nation, and Tam kept searching their faces for anger, for pity, for anything, and finding mostly weather. Fog with eyes. Except — once, when the queen had turned to consult the archive, an old man near the front of the ranks had looked directly at Tam and very slowly closed one eye.

Winked.

Tam nearly dropped the ledger. He stared, and the old man's face went blank again, smooth as everyone else's, and Tam couldn't decide if he'd imagined it. But his heart was hammering, and he clutched that wink to himself like a coin, like proof that somewhere under the fog, people were still in there. People who maybe didn't want what the queen wanted.

People who might help, if helping were possible.

"The next passage," the queen said, returning. "The naming of the border-stones. This is the heart of it, child. Speak it as your mother would have."

And that was the cruelest thing she could have said, and maybe she knew it, because Tam's mother was a fading outline in a grey house, and the queen's voice was gentle when she said it, and gentle was so much harder to fight than sharp.

Tam opened his mouth.

* * *

He got through the border-stone passage by thinking about Freya.

That was his trick now, the one he'd found in the long grey afternoon: he anchored every clean word to her. *This stone belongs to Vessia* — the way Freya said *stone*, with her keeper's flat valley vowels, and the word came out crooked without him even trying. *This river rises within the oath* — and he heard her voice reciting the old broken oath by the gate, all those mornings, not understanding it, and the crookedness crept in like damp. He was speaking Vessian through a screen of Freya, and the screen bent everything, and the queen grew quieter and quieter as the light refused to change outside the windows.

Finally she raised a hand, and the circle's glow died, and Tam's legs nearly folded under him.

"We rest," the queen said. Her voice was silk pulled tight over something with edges. "Tomorrow we finish."

"Tomorrow?" Tam kept his face blank, the way Freya had taught him — blank like a gate, giving nothing. "I thought—"

"You thought a child's stumbles could outlast a queen's patience." She crouched again, and this time she took his chin in her fingers, and her touch was cold and barely there, like being held by smoke. "Listen to me, Tamasin vel Orrey. I have read your name in the ledger. I have waited three hundred years inside my own erasure. Do you imagine I cannot tell a bent word from a true one?"

Tam's blood went to ice. "I don't—"

"I let you bend them." Her thumb brushed his jaw, almost tender. "Because a rite spoken under protest is a rite spoken twice — once in the mouth and once in the heart, and the heart's version fails. So tomorrow, you will not merely speak the words. You will mean them. You will stand in that circle and give me your country back with your whole heart, or I will go and collect the heart's price myself." Her eyes flicked toward the window, toward the west, toward the valley, and Tam understood with sick certainty exactly what price she meant. "There is a village sleeping behind that gate, little key. Full of the grandchildren of the men who sold us. I have been generous for three centuries. My generosity has an end, and it is very close."

She released his chin and rose, and the court bowed as one body, a field of grey stalks bending in wind that wasn't there.

"Take him to the bell tower," she said to the guards. "Let him sleep beside his keeper. Children should dream of home."

* * *

The guards walked him through corridors that had once been gold and were now the color of old teeth, and Tam counted his winnings the way Freya had taught him to count seals: what holds, what's cracked, what's gone.

One day. Maybe two. That was what his stammering had bought — a day, perhaps a little more, scraped together out of bent vowels and a child's bad memory, and the queen had known the whole time, and had let him do it anyway, because she believed she could make him mean it.

Could she?

Tam lay on the narrow bed in the bell tower room, Freya breathing slow and deep on the cot across from him, and stared at the ceiling and asked himself the question honestly, because Freya always said honesty was the sharpest tool a keeper owned.

If he spoke the rite with his whole heart — if he meant every word — his mother would come back. Whole. Warm. She would say his name the real way, *Tamasin*, with the soft ending, and she would cook, and sing, and the bells of the city would ring with actual hands on the ropes. Everything he had lost since the fall through the light would come back.

And Wren would burn. And the elders in the square. And the children Tam had never met, who had done nothing except be born on stolen ground they didn't know was stolen.

His chest ached. He pressed his fist against it, hard, as if he could hold both halves of himself together by force.

Across the room, Freya stirred. "Tam?" Her voice was thick with sleep. "You all right?"

"They know," he whispered. "The queen knows I've been doing it wrong. She says tomorrow I have to mean it, or she'll take the price from the valley herself."

Silence. Then the cot creaked, and Freya was sitting up in the dark, and even in the grey gloom Tam could see her eyes, awake and burning.

"Then tomorrow," she said quietly, "we don't give her a rite. We give her something else. Get dressed, love. There's someone I need you to meet — the only man in this city who still rings the bells."

Out in the dark above them, unhurried, unowned, a single bell tolled once — though Tam had seen the tower at dusk, and there had been no one in it.

Someone was listening. Someone, maybe, was ready to talk.

Tam pulled on his shoes and followed Freya down the stairs, into the ringing dark.
