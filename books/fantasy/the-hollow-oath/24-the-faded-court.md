## 24. The Faded Court

Freya had expected the palace to be a fortress.

It was not. It rose from the grey heart of Vessia like something grown rather than built—towers of pale stone that seemed to have been carved by patient water rather than any chisel, windows tall and narrow as slitted eyes, banners hanging motionless in air that never stirred enough to lift them. Even the banners were fading. Freya could see it plainly now that she knew what to look for: the dye thinning at the edges like breath on glass, the sigil of Vessia—a bell within a circle of wheat—half-dissolved into suggestion. In another year, perhaps two, there would be nothing on those poles at all but the memory of cloth.

She stood in the vast antechamber where Queen Ilsevan had left her, and she listened.

The bells of the city rang somewhere below and behind, though no one rang them. She had heard them all through her first sleepless night in the unmapped palace, marking hours that did not quite match the hours she had kept her whole life in the valley. They rang slightly too slow, as if the whole country were breathing under water. Tam had slept through them without stirring, curled on a pallet in the corner with one hand still fisted in his blanket the way he did when he dreamed of falling. But Freya had lain awake and listened, and beneath the bells she had heard another sound: footsteps. Hundreds of them. A city moving through its streets at all hours, going about lives it no longer fully owned.

Now, waiting for a summons that might not come, she decided to go down among them instead.

The queen had given her leave to walk the palace—"walk anywhere, Keeper Hartwell; you will find we have few secrets left worth guarding"—and so Freya passed through doors that stood open because no one remembered how to close them, down staircases whose steps were worn in shallow crescents by centuries of feet, out onto terraces where gardeners tended roses that bloomed the color of fog.

The first citizen she met was a woman sweeping a corridor that needed no sweeping.

Freya understood this only gradually, standing aside as the woman worked: the stone floor was immaculate. It had been swept so many times, by this same woman or her predecessors or perhaps by this woman over and over again without knowing it, that there was nothing left to remove. The broom moved in long, practiced strokes anyway. The woman's hands were translucent at the fingertips. When she reached the end of the corridor she turned, began again, and did not look at Freya at all—or rather, looked at her the way one looks at weather.

"Good morning," Freya said.

The woman paused. Something flickered across her face—not surprise exactly, but the ghost of surprise, the shape surprise had left behind. Her eyes found Freya's and held them with an effort that was almost physical, like a swimmer treading water far from shore.

"Morning," the woman said. Her voice was soft and slightly frayed at the edges, a voice played too many times. "You're from beyond."

"I am. My name is Freya Hartwell. I keep the gate in the valley."

"The gate." The woman's grip shifted on the broom. "The door they put between us and everything." There was no bitterness in it. That frightened Freya more than bitterness would have. It was said the way one says *the rain* or *the winter*. A fact of the world. "I am Marta Sollen. I was a seamstress." A pause, long enough for Freya to see the effort behind the next words, like someone hauling a bucket up from a very deep well. "I had a daughter. I think—I believe I had a daughter."

"You don't remember?"

"I remember the shape of remembering." Marta Sollen smiled, and the smile was worse than anything else, because it was warm. It was a mother's smile with nothing left inside it. "Her name started with an L. Or I want it to. Some days I'm certain. Some days I think I made her up so the house wouldn't be so quiet." She resumed sweeping. "Forgive me. You'll be wanting the queen's justice. Everyone who comes wants justice."

"Is that what you want?"

Marta stopped again. For a moment her outline sharpened—the way a candle flame steadies when the draft dies—and Freya saw, just for a heartbeat, a living woman standing in a clean corridor with a broom in her hands and a question in her eyes that no one had asked her in years.

"No," she said. "No one asks us what we want. The queen speaks for us. She has always spoken for us." Then, quieter, almost ashamed: "But if anyone asked—if anyone truly asked—we would say we want our names back. Not the borders. Not the wars. Just... to be called by what we are, before we are nothing at all."

She swept on. Freya stood watching until the woman rounded the far corner and the sound of the broom faded, and then she walked on, and the corridor took her where it would.

* * *

The palace spilled into the city the way a river spills into its delta, and the city of Vessia's capital—which had a name, Freya realized, which must have a name, and which she could not hold in her mind no matter how hard she pressed—opened itself around her street by street.

It was beautiful the way ruins are beautiful, except that it was not ruined. Every building stood whole. Roofs were tiled, shutters hung straight, lamps waited in their brackets for evening. But everything was grey. Not the grey of dust or neglect—the grey of things seen through frosted glass. Colors existed here, she saw, but faintly, apologetically, as if they had been asked to keep their voices down. A market square opened before her where vendors stood behind carts of bread and apples and bolts of cloth, and the bread was grey-gold, the apples grey-red, and no one was buying anything, because no one was hungry anymore, or perhaps because hunger required wanting and wanting required being certain that you existed to do it.

They noticed her. That was the thing that undid her, walking through that market: they noticed her.

Not all at once. But heads turned, slowly, one after another, the way flowers turn toward light they can barely feel. A man mending a net looked up. Two children chasing each other around a fountain froze mid-game and stared. An old man on a bench set down the cup he hadn't been drinking from. And in their turning, their outlines firmed. Color crept back into faces like blood returning to cold hands. For as long as they looked at her—from beyond, from the world that still remembered itself—they were more real.

"Keeper," someone whispered. The word moved through the square like wind through wheat. *Keeper. Keeper. From the valley. One of the oath-keepers.*

And then they came.

Not running. Nothing here ran; urgency itself seemed to fade in this air. But they converged, dozens of them, hands reaching not to grab but only to touch—to touch her sleeve, her wrist, the hem of her cloak—as though contact with the unforgotten world might anchor them. A fishwife gripped Freya's forearm with fingers gone nearly transparent and spoke fast, urgent, in Vessian, and Freya understood maybe one word in ten, and the fishwife saw her incomprehension and switched, painfully, word by word, to the common tongue:

"—my son. He went through. Before. Before the door closed. He was a boy, he'd be a man now, forty years, is he alive out there? Do they remember him? Is his name written anywhere?" Her eyes were enormous. Her face was trying to cry and had half-forgotten how. "Tell me he's somewhere. Tell me he's *somewhere*."

"I don't know," Freya said, and hated every syllable. "I'm sorry. I don't know your son."

The fishwife's hands fell away. She nodded, once, twice, three times, as if agreeing to a sentence handed down. Around her the others pressed closer, and the questions came from every side, overlapping, desperate, gentle:

"Is it true the world forgot us on purpose?"

"My brother was a cartographer. Did they burn his maps? Are his maps still somewhere?"

"Do they tell stories about us? Even wrong ones? Even lies? Lies would be enough. Lies mean someone thought of us."

"We didn't do anything," said a young man near the fountain, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "We were just *here*. We were born here. What did we do?"

An older voice cut through them, dry and hard as flint: "We signed a treaty with liars, boy. Same as everyone. Now hush and let the keeper breathe."

Freya turned toward the voice and found herself looking at a small crowd of elders, and among them a woman who stood straighter than the rest, whose greyness sat differently on her—not faded so much as *weathered*, like stone left out in rain. This one's eyes were clear. Too clear. She looked at Freya the way the valley elders had looked at strangers in Freya's childhood: measuring, weighing, deciding.

"You're the one the queen has taken in," the old woman said.

"She hasn't taken me in. I came with a child. One of yours. The last speaker."

"Yes. Tam." The name moved through the crowd like a spark through dry grass—*Tam, Tam*—people saying it under their breath, tasting it, and Freya watched with astonishment as the mere speaking of the child's name brightened them, literally brightened them, color rising in cheeks and lips like dawn coming up over a frozen field. A name spoken aloud was a small resurrection. She understood that now. She understood why the queen wanted the rite. She understood, and it terrified her, how easy it would be to want it too.

"The queen will restore us," the old woman said, and it was not a statement so much as a test. She was watching Freya's face for the answer underneath.

"That's what she says."

"And what do you say, Keeper?"

Freya looked out over the square—at the fishwife with her lost son, at the boy by the fountain, at Marta Sollen who had come down from the palace and stood now at the edge of the gathering with her broom forgotten, listening—and she said the truest thing she had.

"I say the queen speaks of borders and justice and the old ground soaked in old blood. And I say I've been walking your city since morning, and not one soul I've met has asked me for a border. They've asked me for their names."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere above, the bells rang the hour, slow and drowned-sounding.

Then the fishwife seized Freya's hand again and pulled it to her own chest, pressing it flat against her sternum, against a heart that beat faintly, like something heard through a wall.

"Bless you," she breathed. "Bless you for saying it. We're forbidden—no, not forbidden, we don't dare, she grieves so beautifully when we disagree—but listen to me, Keeper, listen: there are those among us who beg her to stop. Who beg her every season. And she looks at us with such sorrow, such *loving* sorrow, and she says, *would you remain ghosts forever, then?* As if the choice were ghosts or war. As if there were nothing between."

"There isn't," said the old flint-voiced woman. "That's the truth of it, girl. The queen isn't lying. The rite will make us solid. It will also drown a valley of living children to pay for it, and she knows, and she does it grieving, and grief makes a poor shield against a blade." She spat, delicately, on the grey cobbles. "My husband served her grandfather. I loved this queen when she was a girl. I am telling you: the ruled are not their ruler. Whatever you decide in that pale court of hers, decide it about *us*, and not about *her*."

"And what should I decide?" Freya asked. "If you know a path, tell me. I'll walk it."

The old woman's mouth twisted. "If I knew a path I'd have walked it eighty years ago. All I know is what we are." She raised her chin at the market, the grey bread, the grey apples, the children playing a game whose rules they were forgetting mid-play. "We are not conquerors, Keeper. We never were. Conquest is the queen's grief wearing armor. Underneath the armor we are bakers and netmenders and a seamstress who cannot recall her daughter's name, and we would trade every inch of the old empire for one person beyond that gate who could say *Vessian* and mean a neighbor and not a ghost."

Around her, softly, hesitantly, the others began to nod. Not all. Freya marked that carefully, honestly: some faces stayed fixed on the queen's promise, hungry for the restoration, willing to pay whatever price grief demanded. But most—most of them—nodded. And some did more than nod.

A man in a baker's apron stepped forward, and his hands shook as he took Freya's sleeve.

"Refuse her," he whispered. The words came out ragged, torn up from somewhere deep, and as he spoke his outline burned brighter, brighter, spending himself like a candle burning at both ends. "When she asks you—and she will ask you tonight or tomorrow, she has already chosen the hour—refuse her. Refuse her and we will bless your name until there is no one left to say it. Refuse her even if it means we stay like this. Even if it means we fade. Better to fade honest than to rise on the drowning of children. Better—" His voice failed. He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. His wife appeared beside him, equally faded, equally bright with the effort of speech, and together they said it, both of them, in unison, like a prayer learned by heart:

"*Better to be forgotten than to be forgiven for that.*"

Freya stood in the middle of them with her throat closed and her eyes burning, and she thought of the valley: of Wren's chisel ringing in the quarry, of old Sorrel's honey cakes, of children sledding down the pasture hill in February, none of whom had ever voted for their founders' treachery, all of whom would burn beneath the queen's righteous tide. And she thought of the fishwife's son, somewhere beyond the gate, alive or dead, named or unnamed. Two peoples, both betrayed by the same dead oath-breakers, pointed at each other's throats by history while the actual traitors mouldered safe in their graves.

"There may be another way," she heard herself say. "I don't know it yet. But I swear to you I'll look for it before I let her open that gate, and I'll look harder than I've ever looked at anything in my life."

The baker's wife made a sound that was either laughter or weeping. The crowd did not cheer—they were too faded for cheering—but something passed through them like warmth through a cold house, and for a few minutes the market square of a forgotten country was the most vivid place Freya had ever stood.

Then the bells changed.

They had been ringing their slow drowned hours, but now the rhythm altered—a new pattern, three quick notes and a long toll, repeated, insistent. The effect on the crowd was immediate and terrible: they dimmed. All of them, everywhere, like lamps turned down by an unseen hand. The fishwife's grip loosened. The baker and his wife drew back, their brightness guttering out, their eyes going vague and distant as the summons worked through them.

"The queen calls the court," the old flint woman said. Her own voice had gone thinner, but her eyes stayed sharp, and she fixed them on Freya with the last of her strength. "You hear that pattern, Keeper? Three and a toll. It means she's ready to begin. Go up. Go up and look at her closely when she makes her offer, and remember the baker and his wife, and remember that she will weep while she does it."

Freya was already moving, pushing through the parting crowd, taking the palace stairs two at a time while the three-and-toll rang overhead. Behind her the market sank back into its grey dream. Ahead, at the top of the stairs, the doors to the throne hall stood open, spilling pale light, and beside those doors—so quietly that she almost missed him—stood a broad, grey-bearded man in a bell-keeper's apron, the only citizen in the entire palace whose outline did not waver at all.

He watched her come, and he did not bow.

"Keeper Hartwell," he said, in a voice like a struck bronze bell, low and clear and utterly steady. "Before you walk into that room and hear what the queen has prepared—there is something you need to know. There is a third road. And I am the only one left alive who remembers where it starts."

He glanced once at the open doors, at the throne beyond, and then back at her.

"But we haven't time for all of it now. So first answer me one question honestly, and everything depends on your answer." He leaned close, and his faded eyes were the sharpest thing in all of Vessia. "Are you willing to lose everything—your name, your home, everyone who loves you—to give these people theirs back?"
