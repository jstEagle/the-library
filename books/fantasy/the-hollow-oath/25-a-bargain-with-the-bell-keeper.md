## 25. A Bargain with the Bell-Keeper

Freya found him the way she suspected he had meant to be found: by following the sound of a single bell through streets that had forgotten how to echo.

The city of Vessia had a hundred towers, Tam had told her, and every one of them had held bells once. Now only one rang, and only one man pulled its rope, and when Freya climbed the spiral stair inside it—her hand trailing the wall because the darkness was total and her breath kept catching on something she couldn't name—she understood why the sound carried so strangely. It wasn't echoing off stone. It was echoing off absence. The bell's voice went out into the grey air and came back thinner, as if the city itself were eating the sound to keep itself alive.

She emerged onto the belfry platform into light the color of old pewter.

The man at the rope was tall and stooped, dressed in a keeper's coat that might once have been blue, with rope-burned palms and a face weathered like a river stone. He was the first person she had seen in Vessia who cast a shadow.

"You're not faded," she said, before she could think better of it.

He didn't stop ringing. The bell swung above them, vast and green-black, and each note fell over the city like slow water. "No," he said. "Not yet. I've been arguing with the fading for sixty years, Keeper. I find if you keep busy, it loses interest." He glanced at her sideways, and his eyes were sharp and brown and entirely alive. "You'd know something about keeping."

"Freya Hartwell," she said. "Keeper of the Hollow Gate."

"I know what you are." He rang again. "I know your order's face better than my own mother's, and my mother I can barely remember. That's the trouble with being the last one who remembers anything. Everyone else is a stranger wearing a memory."

Below them the city spread out, grey-roofed and grey-walled, whole and ruined at once. She could see the archive district from here—or what remained of it after the Compact's envoys had come through with their treaty-magic and their fire. A black scar across three blocks. She had spent two days hauling scorched ledgers out of the rubble with Tam, her arms blistered, her heart in pieces, salvaging names one charred page at a time.

"You're Ondrey," she said. "Tam spoke of you. The bell-keeper."

"The boy remembers me?" Something moved across the old man's face—hope, quickly mastered. "He used to sit on that stair there, third step from the bottom, and ask me why the bells needed ringing if nobody listened. I told him nobody listening was the *reason* they needed ringing." He let the rope go slack at last. The bell swung down to silence, and the silence was enormous. "Sit, Keeper. My legs are older than this tower, which is saying something, because this tower was standing before your valley had a name."

Freya sat on the low parapet. Below, far off, she could see the palace where Queen Ilsevan was preparing a rite that would unmake the world outside the gate—and the boy at the center of it, whether he willed it or no. She had left Tam sleeping in the guest quarters with a lie on her lips: *rest, we'll decide together.* She hadn't decided anything. She had only run out of ways to say no to a queen whose grief was larger than her cruelty and nearly as large as her rightness.

"Ilsevan sent you," Freya said. "Didn't she. To talk me round."

Ondrey laughed—a dry, cracked sound, like a door opening on rusted hinges. "Ilsevan doesn't send me anywhere. Ilsevan stopped seeing me years ago; the faded don't perceive what won't fade alongside them. No, Keeper. I sent myself. Because you've been walking this city for three days looking like a woman trying to drown quietly, and I have watched enough people drown to know the look."

* * *

He poured her something from a clay jug that lived in a niche behind the bell mechanism—some spirit, harsh and honeyed, that burned all the way down and then bloomed warm in her chest. It was the first thing she had tasted in Vessia with any color to it.

"My order broke an oath," she said, when she could speak again. "They sold this country. Sold its borders, sold its people into forgetting. And now their descendants live warm and safe in a valley built on the proceeds, and the queen means to take it back, and she'll burn every living soul in that valley to do it, and she calls it justice, and the worst part—" Her voice caught. "The worst part is that I understand her."

"Aye," Ondrey said. "That's always the worst part. If wickedness were strange, we'd never fall for it." He settled himself against the bell frame, joints crackling. "So. You've been offered two roads. The queen's road: open the gate wide, let Vessia pour back out and reclaim what was stolen, restore the borders, resurrect the nation. And behind that road stands a valley full of farmers and millers and children who never signed any treaty and never sold anyone. Or the Compact's road: seal the border forever, let the last of us fade to nothing, keep the world tidy and blank where we used to be. Have I got the shape of it?"

Freya said nothing. He had the shape of it exactly.

"There's a third road," Ondrey said.

Her head came up.

"It's an old idea. Older than the queen's rite, older than the Compact's treaties. It comes from before the betrayal, back when the keepers and the Vessians were still friends, and it was written down by people who never imagined it would matter." He leaned forward, and in the pewter light his living eyes were fierce. "Tell me what you salvaged from the archive, Keeper. All of it. Every scrap."

"Names," Freya said slowly. "Ledgers of names. Census rolls, guild registers, birth records going back six centuries. Tam can read them—he's a native speaker, the script opens to him like water. We saved... perhaps a third of the collection. Maybe more. It's hard to count ash."

"A third of a country," Ondrey murmured. "Better odds than most of us get." He turned his cup in his rope-scarred hands. "Here is what your founders understood, and what the Compact has spent two hundred years making sure everyone forgot: a country is not its borders. Borders are just lines drawn by men with armies. A country is its *names*. Its people, called and counted and known. The Compact erased Vessia not by burning it—burning leaves ruins, and ruins can be mapped—but by unwriting it. Taking the names out of the world's mouth. And the queen's answer is to put the whole nation back, borders and armies and grievances intact, and make the living pay for the dead's erasure." He shook his head. "It would work, too. For a while. And then it would be the same story again, new lines, new soldiers, some other valley full of someone else's children. Erasure answered with conquest is just erasure wearing armor."

"So what's the third road?"

"You speak the names," Ondrey said simply. "Into the world beyond the gate. Not Vessia restored—not the nation, not the borders, not the throne. Each person, individually. Every name in those salvaged ledgers, spoken aloud beyond the border, remembered where the forgetting happened. The world took these people out of its mouth. Put them back in. One by one."

Freya stared at him. Something in her chest was moving—something that felt dangerously like hope, and she had learned in the last weeks to distrust hope the way sailors distrust calm seas.

"What would it do? Exactly?"

"I don't know *exactly*," Ondrey said. "Magic in this country is thin; everything here is thin, including me. But I know what it did in the small trials, long ago. When a Vessian's name is truly spoken beyond the border—spoken with intent, by someone with the standing to speak it—the fading stops. Reverses, sometimes. The person solidifies. Not here—" he gestured at the grey city—"here we're anchored to a country the world refuses to see. But the person *carries*. They become real wherever real things are recognized. My grandmother was a trial subject, in the last years before the betrayal. Her sister fled north past the old border, married a fisherman, and one day spoke my grandmother's name to her own daughter, told her stories, kept the name alive in a foreign kitchen. My grandmother swore to her dying day that on the nights that girl spoke of her, she could feel her own hands." He held up his own hands, gnarled and solid. "Faded folk can't feel their hands, Keeper. Most days I barely can. So I believe her."

"And the queen knows this? This third road exists?"

"She knows. She considers it surrender. A country of scattered individuals, living under other flags, speaking their names into strangers' kitchens—that's not Vessia restored, she says. That's Vessia *dissolved*, politely. She'd rather burn a valley than be dissolved." Ondrey's mouth twisted. "And I understand her too, before you say it. She has watched her entire nation evaporate around her for two centuries. She wants something she can point to. A border is a thing you can point to. A name whispered in a fisherman's hut is not."

"But you don't want that either. A border."

"I'm a bell-keeper," Ondrey said. "I have spent my life making a sound that asks nothing and gives everything. I don't want walls. I want to be *heard*." His voice roughened. "I am the last unfaded citizen of Vessia, Keeper. Do you understand what that means? It means I remember all nine hundred thousand of us, or I did once, and every year I remember fewer, and soon I will remember only myself, and then not even that. The queen's road saves the nation and spends the valley. The Compact's road saves the valley and spends us. Your third road—" he looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and alive— "your third road spends *you*."

There it was. The hook in the bait. Freya had felt it from the moment he'd said *there's a third road*, the way you feel a barb through silk, and she made herself ask anyway. "What does it spend of mine?"

"To speak the names into the world beyond, the speaking must carry authority. The world will not take a name back from just anybody—it took them out under treaty, under law, under an oath sworn by your order's founders. To unmake an oath's work, an oath-breaker must stand in the breach. Someone of the founding blood, of the founding office, must publicly, formally, *before witnesses and before the world*, break the Hollow Oath and take the erasure onto themselves." He said it flatly, without softening. "The oath isn't just words, Keeper. You know that. It's a working. It holds the border shut and holds the forgetting in place, and it's woven through the one who keeps it. Break it clean, and the breaking has a price, and the price is written in the old texts your founders were careful to hide: *what the oath-holder gives to unmake the silence, the silence takes from them in return.*"

"Which means?"

"Your order sealed a people out of the world's memory," Ondrey said gently, horribly. "To undo it, you give the world your place in it. Speak the names, and the world forgets *you*, Freya Hartwell. Your valley, your cottage, your twenty years of rounds at dawn. Wren and the elders and every soul who ever knew you—they'll wake with a hole shaped like a keeper and no keeper in it. The names come back. You go into the silence in our stead." He drained his cup. "That's the third road. Hope, with a hook in it. I told you I wouldn't sugar it. Sugar is for people with time."

* * *

The wind came up off the grey rooftops and stirred the dust on the belfry floor, and Freya sat very still and let the arithmetic of it move through her.

Tam. Twenty minutes ago she had left him sleeping, and the thought of him now was like a hand closing around her heart. He had crossed half a dead country holding her sleeve. He had read charred census pages by candlelight until his eyes swam, sounding out the names of the vanished like a child learning letters, because each name he read was somebody's grandmother coming back a little. He loved her—she knew it the way you know weather, wordlessly and completely—and he was eight years old, and the bargain Ondrey described would leave him waking in a valley where no one remembered the woman who had pulled him out of the rain.

But the alternative. The queen's rite, three days hence, with Tam as the living key. The valley burning. Nine hundred thousand names traded for a few thousand lives, and the wheel turning, and turning, and turning.

"Wren," Freya said suddenly. "If I do this—if the valley forgets me—is there any way—"

"I don't know," Ondrey said. "Perhaps memories can be smuggled, the way your friend smuggled herself through a half-open gate. Perhaps love is a badger that won't stay fenced. I'm a bell-keeper, not a philosopher. What I know is the shape of the choice, and the shape is this: someone must break the oath and pay the price, or everyone in this city finishes fading within the year, the queen burns your valley first, and the Compact seals the ashes. Those are your roads, Keeper. I've laid out all three." He rose, groaning, and set his hand on the bell rope with the familiarity of sixty years. "You don't have to answer tonight. But answer soon. The queen moves her rite forward every time the Compact moves theirs, and the two of them are racing each other toward the same cliff, and the rest of us are tied to the axles."

"One question more." Freya stood. Her legs felt distant, like borrowed things. "Why me? There must be others of the founding line. The elders in the valley—"

"They're complicit, not consecrated," Ondrey said. "Complicity doesn't unmake a working. Only the office does. The Hollow Gate has had one true keeper at a time since the beginning, and the last true keeper before you died believing the lie, gods rest her." He paused, rope in hand. "You're the first keeper in two hundred years to walk through that gate knowing what it is. That makes you the first keeper in two hundred years with the standing to break it properly. Cleanly. In front of the world." He almost smiled. "Congratulations, Freya Hartwell. You are, at long last, essential."

He rang the bell then—one great note, deep as a buried river—and the sound rolled out over the grey city, and in the streets below Freya saw faded figures pause, mid-stride, mid-gesture, heads tilting toward the sound like plants toward a sun that had gone out long ago. Listening without knowing why. Remembering without remembering.

"That's why you ring it," she said quietly. "Every day. Nobody listens."

"Somebody always listens," Ondrey said. "Even if it's only the fading. Especially then." He looked at her over his shoulder, an old man silhouetted against a sky the wrong color. "Go home, Keeper. Sleep if sleep will come. And when you wake, ask yourself one question, and be honest with it, because honest is the only currency this bargain takes: *if the valley forgets you, was anything you did there real?*" He smiled thinly. "I asked myself that about Vessia sixty years ago. It's why I'm still ringing."

Freya descended the stair in the dark, her hand on the wall, her heart hammering out a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with a decision already forming in her like a bone knitting.

She found Tam awake when she returned—not asleep at all, sitting cross-legged on the floor surrounded by salvaged ledger pages, reading names aloud in a whisper, giving them back to the air one by one. He looked up when she came in, and his face went carefully blank, the way it did when he was afraid of hoping.

"You met Ondrey," he said. "You've got the look. He gave you the look."

"He gave me a third road," Freya said, and sat down beside him among the scattered dead of a forgotten country. "And it has a hook in it."

Tam waited. He was very good at waiting; the last weeks had taught him that too.

"The road costs me everything I am," Freya said. "To everyone but you."

The boy was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached over and straightened one of the charred pages, smoothing it flat with small, deliberate hands, and said, in the language of a country that no longer existed:

"Then we should start reading faster."
