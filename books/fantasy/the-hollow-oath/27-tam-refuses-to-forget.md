## 27. Tam Refuses to Forget

Tam woke to the sound of Freya's boots on the stairs, and pretended not to.

He had gotten good at that lately—good at lying still with his eyes shut while the palace of Vessia breathed around him, good at listening through walls the way he used to listen through the floor of his mother's house for her footsteps coming home. Listening was the first thing a small person learns when the world gets too big. His mother had taught him without meaning to; she'd worked late at the dye-works in the Tanners' Quarter, and he had learned to know her step among all the other steps on the cobbles below, the particular drag of her left heel where the stone had worn it crooked. He could have picked her footsteps out of a thousand.

He could not pick them out of silence anymore. That was the trouble with this country. Everything here was turning into silence.

The boots paused outside his door. A long moment. Then they moved on, down the corridor, away toward the tower stair, and Tam opened his eyes.

He knew where she was going. He had followed her the night before—not far, just far enough to hear the bell-keeper's voice rising up the stairwell like smoke, low and urgent and old. Ondrey. The last man in Vessia who still cast a shadow you could trust. Tam had heard only pieces: *an oath-breaker*, *the names spoken aloud*, and then a word that had stopped him cold on the stair, a word he had carried up to bed like a stone in his chest.

*Forgotten.*

He hadn't understood all of it. He was eight, and half the adult world was a locked room. But he understood enough. In Vessia, forgetting wasn't an accident. It was a price, and prices here were always paid by someone, and the someone was never the one who set the cost.

So now, instead of sleeping, Tam got up.

* * *

The palace was greyer at night, if grey were a thing that could deepen. It was. Tam had learned that too. The walls of the Unmapped Palace did not quite hold their own shape anymore; in the corners of rooms, where no one looked, the stone went soft and vague as wet paper, and the tapestries hung with their scenes unravelling into threads of nothing. Once, he had watched a serving-woman pour wine from a jug that forgot itself halfway through the pour, so that the last cupful fell through the air and struck the table without a sound. No one else had seemed to notice. That was the worst part—the not-noticing, everyone walking through a country that was quietly letting go of itself, smiling the way people smile in dreams.

Tam walked the corridors barefoot, counting doors to keep himself brave. Eleven doors to the gallery, seven more to the west stair, and then down, down past the archive level where the burned smell still lived in the stone from what the Compact's men had done, down to the ground floor and the little door behind the arras that Ondrey used, because the great doors of the palace had grown too proud to open for anyone the queen hadn't summoned.

Ondrey was there, of course. Bell-keepers were always somewhere, Tam thought; that was their whole nature. They were people who stayed near the things that rang.

The old man sat at a trestle table with a lamp turned low and papers spread before him—the salvaged pages, the ones Freya and Tam had carried out of the burning archive in their shirts and satchels, ink smeared with ash and, in one corner, with blood that might have been Tam's own from a splinter of hot shelving. Ondrey was reading them the way he read everything: slowly, thoroughly, with his lips moving, as though each name needed to be handled twice before it would keep.

"You should be abed," Ondrey said, without looking up. Bell-keepers heard everything. That was the other half of their nature.

"I couldn't sleep." Tam came and stood at the table's edge. "Is she here? Freya?"

"She was." Now Ondrey looked up, and his eyes in the lamplight were the colour of old bronze, the only truly dark thing in the whole grey country. "She'll be back within the hour. She wanted to see the list entire before she—" He stopped. His mouth, which had been moving toward a word, closed around it like a door.

"Before she what?" Tam asked. And then, because Ondrey didn't answer, because the old man's silence had a shape to it, a shape Tam recognized the way you recognize a room you've been locked in: "Before she pays for it. Isn't that right? Before she gives herself over to be forgotten."

Ondrey set down his pen with great care, the way Freya set down the gate's iron key. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody told me. I listened. I'm good at listening." Tam climbed onto the bench across from him, knees against the table edge, so they were face to face. "You said it needs an oath-breaker. You said the oath-breaker takes the erasure onto herself. I heard the word *forgotten*. I heard her say, after, when she came upstairs—she thought I was asleep—she said, 'It's fair. It's more than fair.' She said it twice, like she was trying to make it true."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere above them, faint as memory, one of the bells gave a single toneless hum and went quiet again, the way bells do when the wind finds them but no hand does.

"Sit," Ondrey said, though Tam was already sitting. The old man folded his hands. For a while he studied the boy the way he studied damaged pages—with patience, and something underneath the patience that looked almost like pain. "Then you know the shape of it," he said at last. "The bargain is honest. I don't offer dishonest bargains; I haven't time left for them. The names must be spoken into the world beyond, in the voice of one bound by the broken oath—that is the lock, and only a keeper's voice fits the key. Spoken truly, spoken fully, every name in the ledger: and Vessia's people re-solidify, wherever the world scattered them, remembered as themselves. Not as a nation. Not as a border. As mothers and dyers and bell-keepers and boys. But the speaking has a weight, child. The oath was made to hold a silence, and breaking it costs the breaker her place in the silence's keeping. Your keeper would walk back into her valley a stranger. Her cottage would not know her. Her friends—" his bronze eyes did not flinch, "—would look at her the way one looks at a traveller on the road. Kindly, perhaps. From a distance."

"The valley forgets her," Tam said. "Everyone."

"Everyone but me," Ondrey said, "who am not of the valley. And everyone but you, who are half of us already. And that, boy, is why I am telling you plainly, because you would have pried it out of the walls eventually. Yes. She buys a country for you and loses her own home to do it. She knows. She agreed with her whole heart. She is, in my long experience of human beings, entirely too good at agreeing to terrible terms on behalf of other people."

Tam looked down at the papers. Names ran down them in columns, hundreds of names, thousands—Vessa Lindqvist, dyer of the Tanners' Quarter. Ondrey Maren, keeper of the bells. And there, in a column near the middle of the second page, in his mother's own careful hand because she had helped compile the census two winters before the end: *Marla Vessin, dyer of the Tanners' Quarter.* His mother. Written down. Real. One name among thousands that a woman from another world was about to trade her whole life to speak.

His throat hurt. He made himself look up.

"No," Tam said.

* * *

Freya found them an hour later, which was how Tam knew his plan had worked, because she came down the stair quickly, with that stride she had that was half keeper's patrol and half running, and stopped dead in the doorway of the undercroft when she saw the two of them sitting together at the lamplight with the ledger spread between them and Tam's chin set the way it was set.

"What's happened?" she said. "Tam?"

"He's been negotiating," Ondrey said dryly. "With me. At length. He lost most of the rounds, I'm sorry to report. But he would not yield the last one, and he made me swear I'd put it to you in front of him, with witnesses, so that you couldn't soften it or set it aside. So." The old man leaned back. "Witnesses present. Keeper Hartwell. The boy refuses."

"Refuses what?" Freya came forward slowly. "Refuses the rite? Tam, we talked about this, we don't have to do anything, I told you no one will make you—"

"Not the rite." Tam stood up on the bench so he was nearly her height, which mattered, he decided, and he was glad Ondrey didn't tell him to get down. "I refuse *your part*. I heard everything. I heard what it costs. You don't get to be forgotten."

Freya went very still. It was the stillness she had, the keeper's stillness, the one she wore like armour—he had seen it turn aside the surveyors' questions and the queen's arguments and once, memorably, a thrown knife of pure treaty-magic. He loved that stillness and he hated it, because it meant she was about to be reasonable, and reasonableness was how adults swallowed terrible things whole.

"Tam," she said gently. "Sit down. Let's talk about—"

"No. You let me talk. I listened to you for weeks, so you can listen to me now, it's fair, it's *more than fair*"—and he threw her own words back at her and saw them land, saw her flinch as though the phrase were a hook she'd swallowed. Good, he thought fiercely. Feel it.

"You're going to say it's my choice too," he went on. "You're going to say you chose it freely, with your eyes open, for me and for my mother and for all of them." He gestured at the ledger, at the thousands of names. "And maybe that's true. But here's what I figured out tonight, sitting with Ondrey, doing sums. Listen to my sums, because I checked them twice.

"If you do it your way, then tomorrow morning there's a country full of people who remember themselves, and one of them is my mother, and she gets to be real again—and I lose you. Not dead-lost. Worse. Walking-past-you-on-the-road lost. You'd be a stranger in your own cottage. Wren wouldn't know you. The valley children wouldn't know you. *I'd* be the only one, besides Ondrey, and Ondry lives here. So think about what that means. Think about it properly, the way you taught me to think about the seals—one piece at a time until the whole shape shows.

"It means you buy me a mother and sell yourself out of being mine. It means I get everything and you get erased. That's not a bargain. That's just a sacrifice wearing a bargain's clothes. And I'm not going to be the boy who let her do it. I won't carry that. I'd rather stay faded forever than be saved like that. Do you understand me? I'd rather be grey."

The lamp flame bent. Neither adult moved.

"Say the rest," Ondrey murmured. "You made me witness it. Witness all of it."

Tam nodded. His hands were shaking now, and he pressed them flat against his thighs to stop them, the way Freya had taught him to steady himself on the wall of the Hollow Road when they'd run. "So here's my counter-offer. Here's what I came down here to propose, and I've been practising it since midnight so I wouldn't cry in the middle of it, and if I cry anyway you have to pretend I didn't.

"Split it."

Freya's brows drew together. "Split it?"

"The cost. The erasure. Whatever the forgetting is, whatever the price weighs—it's paid by the voice that speaks the names. Right? That's the rule, Ondrey? The lock wants an oath-breaker's voice?" He looked at the old man, and Ondrey, slowly, with the expression of a man watching a door open in a wall he had believed solid for three hundred years, nodded. "Then use two voices. Half the names in hers. Half the names in mine."

"Tam." Freya's voice cracked clean down the middle, all the keeper's stillness gone out of it. "No. Absolutely not. You're eight years old. You are not taking any part of any curse—"

"I'm the last Vessian," Tam said. "That's what everybody keeps saying. The queen says it. The Compact says it. Ondrey says it. Well, fine. Then I'm the native voice, aren't I? The ledger's written in my tongue. I can read every name in it—I proved that in the archive, when you couldn't. Half these names I can pronounce better than you can. Some of them I *knew*. Marla Vessin, dyer of the Tanners' Quarter—" his voice wobbled and he hauled it straight again, furious at it, "—that's my mother, and nobody on earth has more right to speak her name than I do. If the rite needs voices, then it should have the voice of the country in it, not just the voice of the order that broke it. That's not me being brave. That's just correct. Even the queen would say it's correct."

"And the cost?" Ondrey asked softly. "If the cost splits, child—if the forgetting falls on both the speakers—do you understand what you're asking? You'd share her exile. The valley would forget you both."

"I know." Tam made himself hold her eyes. It was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the road, harder than the fall through light. "But listen to the shape of it, the whole shape, the way she taught me. If we split it, then neither of us is alone in it. She walks back into the valley a stranger—but I walk in beside her, a stranger too, and strangers can be strangers together. Two travellers on the road, remember? That's what she called us the night she took me in. Two travellers. Well. Let's actually be that. All the way. Not her carrying me safe across and paying with her whole life while I stand in the doorway holding my shoes."

He jumped down off the bench, because his legs needed to be under him for the last part, and he crossed the space between them and took her hand—her rough, seal-scarred keeper's hand that had pulled him out of a river and out of a burning archive and out of a hundred smaller darks.

"You told me once the oath was hollow because it was made of words nobody meant," Tam said. "You said a real oath has to cost the one who swears it, or it's just noise. So. Fine. Then let ours be real. Let it cost both of us, evenly, honestly, like a proper oath. Half the names in your voice. Half in mine. And if the valley forgets us—then we'll be forgotten together, and we'll build the remembering together too, from nothing, the same way you built our whole life from nothing when I knocked on your door soaked through. You didn't ask the oath's permission for that. Don't ask its permission for this either."

Silence filled the undercroft, thick as the grey outside the windows. Above them, distantly, one of the great bells stirred in the wind and gave a half-tone, unfinished, like a word someone started and abandoned.

Freya looked down at him. And Tam watched something move through her face that he had no word for yet in either of his languages—something that was grief and fury and love all melted into one metal, cooling, hardening, taking an edge. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and very steady, the voice she used for the worst truths.

"They'll never allow it," she said. "The queen will call it contamination of the rite. Ondrey's law may permit it, but Ilsevan wants a keeper's debt discharged whole, not halved. And the Compact is crossing the ridge even now. We may not have until morning to argue theology with a dying monarch."

"Then we'd better start arguing fast," said Tam.

Ondrey began, quietly, to laugh—a rusty, bell-like sound, the first laughter Tam had heard in Vessia that didn't sound like it was apologizing for itself.

"Oh, keeper," the old man said, wiping his eyes with a knuckle scarred by four centuries of rope and bronze. "Wherever did you find this one?"

"On my doorstep," Freya said. Her hand tightened around Tam's. "In the rain. Speaking a language I didn't know I knew." She looked down at him, and the last of the stillness broke, and what came through it was fierce and bright and entirely unkeeperly. "All right, Tam Vessin. Counter-offer accepted. God help us both, we split it. But understand what you've done—you've just made us co-conspirators, and the first conspiracy of your life is against a queen."

Outside, far off beyond the city's grey roofs, a light that was not starlight rose over the eastern ridge—white, orderly, advancing in ranks. The Compact's lanterns, coming down at last toward the unmapped country.

And beneath the palace, deep in the stone, something vast and patient shifted its weight, as though the gate itself had heard the new terms, and was considering them.
