## 28. The Compact at the Gate

### Freya

The bells began to scream at dawn.

Not ring—scream. Freya had heard Ondrey's bells every morning since they came to the city of ash and grey light, and they had a voice like patient water, like something that had been saying the same thing for centuries and did not mind saying it again. This was different. This was metal struck in panic, over and over, no rhythm to it, no meaning. A language with all its words torn out.

She was on the palace steps before she finished lacing her boots.

Below her, Vessia's capital lay under a sky the color of old pewter, and across that sky—across it, through it, as if someone had dragged a nail through wet plaster—a seam of white light had opened above the eastern wall. It hung there without falling, without widening, humming at a pitch Freya felt in her back teeth. Treaty-magic. She had never seen it before, but she knew it the way you know a debt collector's knock: by what it did to your chest.

"They came through the gate," Wren said beside her. The stonemason had not slept; the dust of the archive still clung to Wren's sleeves, and their hands were wrapped in fresh bandages where the burning names had scorched them. "The half-open one. The one I came through. They didn't even have to force it. We left it ajar."

Freya's stomach dropped through the steps, through the stone, through whatever held this country up. "How many?"

"Enough." Wren's jaw worked. "I counted banners. Nine companies. And machines, Freya. Machines like nothing—I don't have words for them. Iron frames on wheels, and each one singing the same note as that light."

The same note as the gate. Of course. You could not seal a border permanently unless you understood the frequency of the door itself, and who had spent three hundred years studying the Hollow Gate? Not the keepers. The keepers had swept its steps and recited an oath they didn't understand. The Compact had built instruments that hummed along to it.

"The queen," Freya said. "Where is the queen?"

"Summoning the court." Wren hesitated, and Freya saw it—the flinch, the small turning-away—that had become familiar in this country whenever anyone spoke of Ilsevan. "Freya. She's moved the rite forward. Tonight. Sundown."

"Sundown?" Freya heard her own voice go flat and cold. "It took us weeks to prepare for the original date. The archive is barely catalogued. Tam has only just—" She stopped. Tam. Where was Tam?

"He was in the bell tower with Ondrey," Wren said. "Last I saw."

Freya took the steps two at a time.

* * *

### Tam

Tam heard the bells change and dropped the rope.

Ondrey caught it before it swung wild, his faded hands closing around the braided leather as if it were a living thing. Even now, months after the counter-oath had begun to work in him—the last unfaded citizen, the man who had refused to forget—the bell-keeper looked like a sketch of himself done in charcoal and then half-erased. But his eyes were solid. His eyes had always been solid.

"That's the alarm," Ondrey said. "We haven't rung it since—" He paused, calculating, and Tam watched him fail to find the year. That was what fading did. It didn't take your memories all at once; it took the pegs you hung them on. "A long time. Since the sealing."

"What does it mean?"

"It means someone is coming up the road that isn't a road." Ondrey set the rope down gently, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "Come. Your keeper will be looking for you, and you should not be found apart today. Not today of all days."

But Tam didn't move. He was looking out through the tower's arched window, east, toward the seam of white light hanging over the wall, and beneath it—he squinted, pressed his face to the cold stone—movement. Small figures in grey. Grey riders. Grey livery. The color of the men who had come into the valley pretending to be surveyors, whose instruments hummed near the gate, whose masters had burned the archive of names while Tam and Freya carried armfuls of it into the dark.

They were here. In Vessia. Walking on Vessian ground.

His mother's ground. His mother's city, three streets away, where a fading outline of a woman sometimes remembered his name and sometimes remembered only that she had loved someone once, terribly, and couldn't recall whom.

"TAM." Ondrey's hand landed on his shoulder, warm despite everything. "Boy. Look at me. Whatever happens tonight—and much will happen tonight—you remember one thing. Words are heavier than iron. Heavier than treaties. You are the last native speaker of this country, and that makes you the heaviest thing in it. Do you understand?"

He understood. That was exactly what frightened him. The queen needed his mouth. The Compact feared his mouth. Everyone in this whole unmapped country wanted something from eight years old and forty pounds of him.

"I want Freya," he said, and hated how small it sounded, and said it anyway, because Ondrey had told him once that a man who won't say what he wants deserves what he gets.

"Then let's go find her," Ondrey said.

* * *

### Freya

She found them both in the map room—or what had been the map room, before the queen ordered every chart of Vessia's erased borders unrolled and pinned to the walls, so that the room had become a country wearing its own skin. Freya stood in the doorway catching her breath and watched Tam run to her, and caught him, and held on longer than dignity allowed.

"You felt it too," she said into his hair.

"The bells." He pulled back, chin up, doing the thing he did when he was scared and refusing to look scared: standing straighter. "And the light. Is it the Compact?"

"It's the Compact."

From the shadows at the far end of the room, Queen Ilsevan spoke. Freya still hadn't learned to stop startling at her—the queen faded in and out of visibility depending on the light and, Freya suspected, depending on her own will. Now she stood fully present, which meant she was spending herself to do it, burning whatever remained of her substance to appear solid before her court and her enemies.

"Keeper Hartwell." The queen's voice was dry silk. "You've seen the sky."

"I've seen it. And I'm told you've moved the rite to sundown."

"I have."

"Then we should talk about why, and we should talk about it honestly, because I am done being managed by dead women and fading queens both." Freya kept her voice level. Behind her, she felt Wren shift, and Tam go very still. "Those machines outside. Those companies of riders. Tell me what they're for."

Ilsevan considered her for a long moment. When she answered, she did not soften it.

"The Border Compact was founded on the Partition Treaties—the same documents your order's founders signed when they sold my borders. Every nation that took a piece of Vessia kept a clause: that if the erased country ever stirred, they would return and finish the erasure. Permanently. No more fading, Keeper. Fading leaves ghosts, and ghosts leave stories, and stories leave boys who wash up in valleys speaking dead tongues." Her gaze flicked to Tam, and something moved behind it—not cruelty, Freya thought. Arithmetic. "What comes tonight is not fading. It is excision. They will cut Vessia out of the world so cleanly that even memory will close over the wound."

"And your answer," Freya said slowly, "is to rush your restoration rite. To open the gate all the way. To reclaim the old borders—including the valley—before they can seal you out."

"My answer is that my people have perhaps one night left in existence, and I intend to spend it making them real."

"And the valley." Freya stepped closer. The map room smelled of dust and old ink and, faintly, of the queen herself—an odor like air after lightning. "The valley sits on Vessian ground. You know it. You've always known it. If the gate opens fully, if the borders come back—what happens to everyone living there?"

For the first time since Freya had met her, Queen Ilsevan looked away first.

"The rite requires the gate," the queen said. "The gate requires anchoring. The valley is the anchor."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer there has ever been." The queen turned back, and her transparency caught the window-light and threw it strangely, so that for a moment she seemed made of frost. "Your order built a paradise on stolen ground, Keeper. I am sorry the bill has come due in your lifetime rather than theirs. But justice does not check whose house burns when the ledger is settled."

*It does,* Freya thought. *It must. Or it's just fire with better handwriting.* But she didn't say it. Not yet. Not with nine companies of grey riders camped outside the walls and a child's hand gripping the back of her shirt.

"When at sundown?" she asked instead.

"Dusk. The moment the sun touches the western hills." The queen studied her. "You're calculating something, Keeper. I can see it. You calculate the way my generals used to."

"I'm calculating how long it takes to walk from here to the gate."

Something like respect crossed the queen's ruined face. "Four hours, if the roads hold. Less if they don't."

"Then we'd best start now."

* * *

### Tam

They left the city through the western postern, a small party: Freya, Wren, Ondrey, and Tam himself, because no one had even suggested leaving him behind, which Tam appreciated and resented in equal measure. Behind them the bells had stopped screaming and started ringing something else—Ondrey had sent word ahead, a pattern, a warning toll—and ahead of them the old royal road ran white and empty toward the hills where the Hollow Gate waited in its valley of quiet.

The country around them was beautiful the way a photograph is beautiful. Fields of grey wheat that never ripened. Orchards heavy with fruit no one harvested. Villages where figures stood in doorways watching them pass, some nearly solid, some barely a shimmer against the stone, all of them silent. As they walked, more joined the roadside—not following, just standing, the way people stand for a funeral procession.

"They know," Tam said quietly. He walked beside Freya, matching her long strides with two of his own. "Don't they. They can feel it. Both sides coming."

"They know," Freya said.

"If the queen opens the gate, the valley—" He couldn't finish it. He saw it anyway: Mrs. Aldery at the bakery. The miller's dog. The children he'd begun to learn the names of. Wren's workshop with its chisels lined up like teeth. All of it under the white light, all of it consumed for anchor-stone.

"I know," Freya said. "Keep walking."

Wren fell back to walk with him. The stonemason's bandaged hands were steady on the straps of their pack. "Whatever happens at that gate," Wren said, low, "there's a third thing. There's always a third thing. Stone teaches you that—every wall has a seam, every seam has a give. We just have to find it before sundown."

"Freya already found it," Tam said. "Ondrey's way. The names. She's going to do the counter-oath, isn't she. She's going to break her own oath and speak all of them." He looked up at Wren. "And the cost—"

"—is hers alone, according to the terms," Wren said grimly. "Yes. I've read the bargain. So has she. Neither of us likes it."

"She'll be forgotten. By everyone in the valley. By—" Tam's throat closed. "By me?"

Wren was quiet for three strides. Four. "The bargain says forgotten by the valley. You were born outside it, boy. Whether that protects you or damns you, nobody knows. Nobody's ever tried this."

Ahead, the road crested a rise, and Tam saw the hills where the gate lay—and stopped dead.

The white seam in the sky wasn't only over the eastern wall anymore. It ran along the horizon now, a jagged stitch of light marking the line where the world ended and Vessia began, and along that line, moving with terrible order, marched the grey companies. And between the companies, at intervals, stood the iron machines on their wheels, each one throwing off a hum Tam felt in his fillings, in his bones, in the Vessian words stacked in his chest.

They weren't waiting for sundown to seal the border.

They were already drawing it.

* * *

### Freya

Freya saw it too, and did the arithmetic she'd promised the queen she was doing, and did not like the sum.

The Compact's line stretched from the northern crags to the southern marshes—a full perimeter, riders and machines spaced like beads on a wire. They were not besieging Vessia. Sieges were for places that existed. They were performing surgery: cutting the country free of the world along the exact seams of the old treaties, and once the cut was closed, nothing would cross it. Not armies. Not names. Not a counter-oath spoken from inside.

Which meant the names had to be spoken from outside. From the valley side. From the gate itself, at the exact moment—

"Freya." Wren had come up beside her, breathing hard. "Tell me you see it."

"I see it. The sealing closes at dusk, or before. The queen's rite begins at dusk. And the counter-oath needs an oath-breaker standing on the threshold with the wind of both worlds in her face." Freya laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Everyone's schedule is the same schedule. Isn't that tidy. Three hundred years of history and it all lands on one evening like guests arriving for dinner."

"Then we run," Wren said simply.

"We run," Freya agreed. She crouched, took Tam's face in her hands—his eyes wide and grey as the road, his jaw set in that stubborn line she'd learned to read like weather—and said, "Listen to me. When we get to the gate, things will happen fast, and some of them will happen to me, and you are not to stop them. Whatever you see. Whatever it costs. Do you hear me?"

"No," Tam said.

"Tam—"

"You said the keeper's word matters more than anything. You said oaths are promises made out loud so the world can hold you to them." His voice shook but didn't break. "Well, I'm saying it out loud. I'm not letting you carry those names alone. We split the cost. You and me. That was the bargain, the real one, the one we made in the archive when the smoke was still coming off the shelves. You don't get to renegotiate because tonight is scary."

Behind him, Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Ondrey, faded and steadfast at the rear of their little column, bowed his head as if witnessing a contract.

Freya looked at the boy for a long time—at the last Vessian, the first of something new, eight years old and heavier than iron—and felt the clock in her chest tick down toward sundown.

"All right," she said finally. "Together. But you stay behind me until the last word."

"Deal," said Tam, who was learning to negotiate.

They ran. Down the white road, past the standing dead who raised their insubstantial hands as they passed—not in farewell, Freya realized, but in blessing, or in hope, or simply in the oldest human gesture there is: *remember us*. Ahead, the seam of treaty-light brightened as the afternoon aged. Behind them, in the unmapped city, the bells began to ring out the hours, counting down.

And at the center of the world, at the hinge between the valley and the vanished kingdom, the Hollow Gate began—for the first time in three centuries—to glow.

Sundown was four hours away.

The Compact's machines would reach it in three.
