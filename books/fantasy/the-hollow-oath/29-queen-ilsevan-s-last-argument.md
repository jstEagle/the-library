## 29. Queen Ilsevan's Last Argument

The palace had stopped pretending to be a palace.

Freya noticed it on the third morning, in the way the great doors of the audience hall no longer swung but simply *were*—open or closed depending on whether anyone remembered to want them otherwise. The banners of Vessia still hung from the rafters, silver stags on grey, but their colors had gone thin as breath on glass. When a draft moved through the hall, the fabric did not stir. It was as though even the wind had forgotten them.

Queen Ilsevan sat where she always sat, on a throne that was more suggestion than stone, and Freya could see the pillars behind her now. Two days ago there had been only a shimmer. Yesterday, an outline. This morning, when the light came through the high windows at a certain angle, Freya could count the fluting carved into each column, could see the hairline crack running up the second pillar from the left—the kind of detail that belonged to a real place, a place someone had built and someone else had swept and a third person had complained about for forty years.

The queen was becoming transparent in the same direction. Inside out.

"Keeper." Ilsevan's voice still carried; whatever was eating her, it had started with her edges and worked inward, saving the voice for last. "You've been standing in my doorway for some time. In my country we'd call that an answer."

"I haven't answered," Freya said.

"No," the queen agreed. "That is itself an answer. The cruelest one available to you, I think, because you know what waiting costs here." She lifted one hand—a gesture that should have been imperious and instead looked like something reaching through water—and indicated the bench beside the throne. "Sit. I won't ask again after today. Not because I'll be angry. Because I may not remember how to ask."

Freya did not sit. She had learned, these past days, that Ilsevan's gentleness was not a mask over cruelty but a genuine thing that coexisted with it, the way a surgeon's steadiness coexisted with the cutting. That was what made her dangerous. A tyrant you could hate cleanly. The queen you could only hate crookedly, with your conscience snagging on every step.

"You sent for me," Freya said. "So say it. Whatever this is—say it plainly. I'm tired of being handled."

Something flickered across the queen's face—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, arriving late. "Plainly, then. You are going to refuse me. I have watched you refuse me for six days in a hundred small ways: the way you position yourself between Tam and my heralds, the way you sleep with your boots on, the way you read the archive by candlelight as if the candle were evidence and you a magistrate building a case against me. So let us skip past the refusals to the thing underneath them." She rose. Rising took her longer than it should have; Freya saw her grip the arm of the throne and saw her fingers pass slightly *into* the stone before finding purchase. "Let us talk about what you think you're protecting."

"The valley," Freya said. "Four hundred people who never signed your war. Farmers. Children. Wren, who walked through a half-open gate into a dying country because she thought I might need help. None of them chose this."

"No," Ilsevan said. "None of them chose it. Do you know who else didn't choose?" She began to walk, slowly, along the dais, and Freya followed because there was nothing else to do with her body. "Come. I want to show you something, and I want you standing in it when you see it."

* * *

They went down.

The palace had undercrofts—Freya had known that since her first night, had felt them the way you feel stairs in the dark—but she had not been permitted below until now, and she understood suddenly that permission had never been the obstacle. Readiness was. The queen had waited until Freya had read the treaties, until she had seen the burned archive, until she had held Tam while he wept over his mother's fading outline and understood exactly what erasure did to the ones left holding the shape of a person.

The stair was worn concave in its centers, the way old stairs go when generations of feet agree on where to step. Torches burned in iron brackets, and Freya realized with a lurch that they were the only things in the undercroft burning at full brightness. Everything else down here had gone grey. Grey walls. Grey dust. And along both sides of the corridor, set into niches like saints in a cathedral wall—

"People," Freya said.

"Citizens," Ilsevan corrected quietly. "My census. My dead who are not dead. Come closer. They don't mind. Being seen is the only kindness left that works on them."

Freya made herself look. A woman in a baker's apron, flour still ghosting her forearms, her face turned toward an oven that was no longer there. A man in a soldier's coat two centuries out of fashion, his hands folded around a letter he had perhaps never finished writing. A girl of maybe ten, holding a wooden horse by one leg, her mouth open mid-word—mid-*name*, Freya thought, mid-someone's name, a mother's or a friend's, called out once and never answered.

"They're not ghosts," Freya said, and hated how her voice shook. "Ghosts are finished. These people are—"

"Suspended," Ilsevan said. "Paused. The world forgot Vessia, Keeper, and forgetting is not a knife. A knife is quick and leaves a body and a grave and mourners. Forgetting is a slow tide. It comes in over everything and does not kill, precisely. It *unhappens*. First the maps. Then the treaties. Then the trade, the letters, the marriages abroad, the songs. Then the names. My archivists fought for the names—that ledger you saved from the Compact's fire. Every name in it is a person the world agreed, without ever voting, to stop remembering." She stopped before the girl with the wooden horse. "This is Maren Voss. She was calling for her brother. Her brother was outside the border that day, trading in Kellin, and when the tide came he remembered everything except that he had a sister. He lived a long life, Keeper. A happy one, by all accounts. He died at seventy-one with children and grandchildren and not one thought of Maren, because the thought had been taken from him before he knew it was his."

Freya's throat was tight. "And she stayed like this. Waiting."

"She stayed like this because the oath your founders swore did not merely close a gate. It closed a *ledger*. It told the world: this debt does not exist. And the world, which wanted very much to believe it, believed." The queen turned, and in the torchlight her transparency was almost beautiful, the way frost on a window is almost beautiful. "Now. Here is my argument, since you asked for plain speech. You keep saying the valley's four hundred did not choose this. True. But consider what *did* happen to them, Keeper, because I promise you it happened. Your valley exists because Vessia was sold. Its soil was purchased with our borders. Its peace was purchased with our names. Every harvest those farmers have ever pulled from that ground grew out of a grave the ground refuses to acknowledge. They did not choose it—but they have *eaten* from it every day of their lives, and so did their parents, and their grandparents, back to the elders who knew and kept the silence and called themselves keepers of a gate instead of heirs of a theft."

"That's inherited guilt," Freya said. "I won't buy it. No court in any honest country convicts children for their grandfathers' crimes."

"No," Ilsevan said softly. "No honest court does. But no honest court lets the grandchildren *keep the house*, either. There is a difference between punishment and restitution, and you keep hearing the first word when I say the second. I am not asking you to punish the valley. I am asking you to give back what was taken. The borders. The name. The memory. Let Vessia stand in the world again, whole, and let the ledger reopen, and let every Maren Voss in this dark finish her sentence—let her brother's descendants learn her name and grieve her properly, which is all she has ever asked." Her voice did not rise. It lowered, which was worse. "And yes. The gate must open fully for that to happen, and the valley lies inside the old borders, and the magic that keeps the valley quiet will break, and I will not lie to you about what breaking it means. But hear the other half of the arithmetic, Keeper. Justice delayed has already murdered my people. Every year of your order's silence was a year of this tide coming in. You did not inherit a duty to protect the innocent. You inherited a *debt*, and debts do not care how innocent the debtor's children are. They come due. They are due *now*. And every hour you spend weighing your four hundred against my thousands, more of my citizens go grey. More of them forget why they're waiting. Some of them—" and here, for the first time, the queen's voice cracked, a fine clean fracture like ice giving way, "some of them have begun to forget *that they are waiting*. Do you understand? They are not suffering anymore. They are dissolving into contentment. That is what your valley's peace is made of, Freya Hartwell. It is made of my people becoming too faded to mind."

* * *

Freya walked afterward. She did not decide to walk; her legs simply took her, up out of the undercroft, through halls whose banners hung unstirring, out onto a terrace that overlooked the city of ash and bells.

Vessia spread below her in the flat grey light. Intact. That was the horror of it—intact, roofed and walled and cobbled, chimneys that should have smoked, bells that should have rung, a whole living country paused mid-breath like the citizens in the wall. Somewhere down there was Tam's street. His mother's house, where a woman who was mostly outline had held her son's face in hands growing less solid by the day and said his name like a woman remembering a word in a language she used to speak.

He had cried that night. Not loudly. Eight-year-olds cry the way old soldiers do, efficiently, having learned that loudness buys nothing. He had said, *If she goes all the way grey, will she still know me when it stops?* And Freya had said, *Yes,* because it was true, and then he had said, *Then we have to make it stop,* and Freya had held him and said nothing, because the truth was that making it stop meant the rite, and the rite meant Tam, and Tam was the key, and keys get turned.

She stood on the terrace a long time.

And the terrible thing—the thing she would carry afterward, the thing she would carve into the gate years later in words plainer than any oath—was that the queen was *right*. Not entirely. Not in the ending. But in the spine of it, in the load-bearing truth: justice delayed had murdered these people. The valley's peace was bought. Her order had not guarded a border; it had guarded a *transaction*, and she was the current holder of stolen goods, and every day she hesitated, the tide came in another inch over Maren Voss and the baker and the girl with the wooden horse.

Freya found herself doing the arithmetic she had sworn not to do. Four hundred against thousands. Living comfort against suspended lives. And beneath it, whispering, the seductive corollary: *the valley's people are descendants of traitors anyway; Wren's own testimony proved the elders knew; perhaps a hard justice now prevents a harder one later; perhaps the boy—*

She caught herself there. Physically caught herself, one hand gripping the terrace balustrade so hard the stone bit her palm, because she had just heard her own thoughts reach for Tam as a cost she might be willing to count.

*No,* she told the grey city, told the queen, told the founder-keepers whose portraits hung in her cottage like saints. *Not him. Find another line. Any line but that one.*

But the queen's voice was still in her ear, patient and reasonable and grieving: *every hour you weigh, we fade.* And Freya stood on the terrace and understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than terror would have, that she was going to say yes. That there was no version of her conscience that could walk out of this country leaving Maren Voss unfinished. That she would find a way to square it—to save the valley some other way, to shield Tam somehow, surely, surely there was a way—and that this was exactly how righteous causes swallowed good people. One near-agreement at a time.

She turned back toward the throne room to tell the queen yes.

She got as far as the doors.

They were already open, and the sound coming through them stopped her on the threshold: bells. The bells of Vessia, silent for two hundred years, ringing—not the slow mourning peal she had heard on her first night, rung by no one, but a fast, urgent, alarm-bright clamor, and beneath it shouting, and beneath that a smell she knew from the burned archive and had prayed never to smell again.

Smoke.

Down in the city, at the edge of the square, the air had torn open along a seam of treaty-light, and through it came the grey riders of the Border Compact—and behind them, on the far side of the widening breach, Freya could see the sky of her own valley, blue and ordinary and terribly far away, as Queen Ilsevan rose from her half-transparent throne and said, in a voice gone suddenly young and hard:

"The Compact is sealing the border, Keeper. Whatever you were going to decide—you have about an hour to decide it faster."
