## 30. The Rite Begins

Tam felt the rite begin the way you feel a storm coming through your teeth.

He was standing in the great hall of the unmapped palace, between Freya and Ondrey, when the queen's voice changed. It had been hard and young since the breach tore open in the square below, but now it went *old*—old in a way that made his skin crawl, because it sounded like the stones of Vessia themselves had started talking through her mouth. The words she spoke were words he knew. Words from the archive. Words from his mother's lullabies, bent into shapes they were never meant to hold.

*Vessia anu sel. Vessia anu kor.* Vessia above all names. Vessia before all doors.

"No," Tam said. His voice came out small in the huge grey hall, and he hated that, so he said it again, louder. "No. You promised you'd wait. You promised—"

"I promised to consider," Queen Ilsevan said, without turning around. She had risen from her throne and was walking toward the far end of the hall, where the great doors stood open onto a balcony, and beyond the balcony the sky of Vessia hung like a held breath. "The Compact has taken that consideration away from me. They are sealing my country's grave while I stand in it, Keeper's boy. I will not be buried politely."

"You're not burying *yourself*," Tam said. "You're burying the valley."

"Tam." Freya's hand came down on his shoulder, warm and heavy. She didn't say *stop*. She never said stop. She just put her hand there, like an anchor, and let him keep talking.

"The valley has children," he said. "There's a girl named Maret who gave me bread on my first day. There's old Piotr who can't walk fast. You'll burn them. Wren told us. You'll burn all of it, and call it justice, and it won't bring back one single bell-ringer or baker or—" His throat closed. "It won't bring back my mother. Not really. Not the way I want her back."

For a moment—one heartbeat—the queen stopped walking.

He saw it happen to her, that heartbeat: the fading outline of her flickered, and something moved behind her face that might have been grief, or might have been memory, or might have been nothing at all but wind through a ruined house. Then it passed, and she was stone again, and stone does not argue with children.

"Ondrey," she said. "Bring the key-child to the circle."

"He is not a key," Ondrey said. The bell-keeper's voice was quiet, but it filled the hall the way his bells did, low and round and impossible to ignore. He hadn't faded—not quite, not yet—and standing next to the queen he looked like a man made of color standing next to a drawing of a man. "Majesty. He is a child who speaks. That is all any of us ever were. Children who speak, until someone decided our speech was a border problem."

"Ondrey." The queen turned then, and Tam saw what the rite was already doing to her: the last of her color was draining upward, out of her hands, out of her face, gathering somewhere behind her eyes like light behind a shuttered window. Spending herself. "I have loved this country longer than it has loved itself. Do not make me spend my last hour arguing with its bell-keeper."

"Then don't argue," Ondrey said. "Listen. There is a third path. I have told you of it. The names can go out into the world as *names*, not as armies—"

"And be scattered? Be remembered the way travelers remember a song they heard once in a market?" Her laugh was terrible because it was almost gentle. "A people is not a song, Ondrey. A people is a *place*. Borders. Ground under feet. I will give them ground."

"You will give them a graveyard with the graves dug by someone else," Freya said.

Tam had never heard Freya sound like that. Not angry, exactly. Worse than angry. Certain. She stepped forward, putting herself half in front of him, and the queen's eyes—grey going whiter at the edges, like frost climbing glass—fixed on her.

"Keeper Hartwell," Ilsevan said. "Your order sold my country for a valley full of quiet. And now you stand in my hall and lecture me on the cost of taking it back."

"Yes," Freya said simply. "Because somebody in my order should have, four hundred years ago, and nobody did. So it falls to me. Queen—I know what you're about to do. I've read the founders' letters. I know how the rite works. The gate doesn't open onto Vessia. It opens *through* the valley. To restore your borders, the gate must consume everything inside the old ones that isn't Vessia. Every farm. Every roof. Every person."

"They are traitors' descendants."

"They are *people*." Freya's voice cracked on it, just slightly, and Tam saw her fists close at her sides. "They bake bread. They mend walls. They tell their children stories that aren't true about why the gate is sealed, because their grandparents lied to them, the way everyone lies to children to keep them from being afraid. That's not treason. That's ignorance wearing its grandfather's coat. You cannot execute a whole valley for a coat."

The queen looked at her for a long moment. And Tam, watching, felt hope rise in him like water in a dry well—because the queen's face did something complicated, something almost human, something that looked like a woman standing at the edge of her own decision and looking down.

Then the horns began.

Not Vessian horns. Compact horns—flat, mechanical, wrong, the sound of instruments built to measure rather than to sing. They came from below, from the square, from the seam of treaty-light that was widening even now, and with them came a hum Tam felt in his back teeth. The surveyors' instruments. The sealing engines. He had seen them up close once, in the worst hours, and he would never forget the way they made the air look *ruled*, like graph paper laid over the world.

"There it is," the queen said softly. "My hour, spent. Come, then."

"Majesty—" Ondrey started.

"*Come,*" she said, and the word hit the hall like a bell struck too hard, and every faded courtier in the shadows straightened at once, hollow-eyed, obedient, moving toward the balcony like grey water finding its level. "The rite begins now, with the Compact at my door or without them. Better with them. Let the nations that erased me watch me un-erase myself."

She swept toward the balcony, and the court flowed after her, and two of the faded guards—Tam couldn't think of them as anything else, though they were barely more than outlines with swords—moved toward him with hands outstretched.

"Don't touch him," Freya said, and her hand went to the keeper's iron at her belt—not a weapon, never a weapon, but the faded guards stopped anyway, because some things are older than fading, and a keeper's iron is one of them.

"Hartwell." The queen paused at the balcony doors without turning. "You may come and watch. Both of you. I owe the boy's order that much honesty—to see what justice looks like when it finally arrives. And perhaps," and here her voice dropped into something almost kind, which was worse than cruelty, "perhaps watching will change your mind. You have an hour of your own, Keeper. The valley's hour. Spend it deciding whose side forgetting is on."

Then she was through the doors, and the court flowed after, and the guards withdrew, and Tam was left in the vast grey hall with Freya's hand on his shoulder and Ondrey's hand suddenly on the other one.

"The bell tower," Ondrey said quietly. "Now. While they're assembling. There is a way down from the tower to the archive quarter that the palace doesn't remember exists. The palace forgets things, same as the world does. It's how we've survived it."

"The archive," Tam breathed. "The names. If the rite needs me—if I'm the key—then maybe the names are the lock, and if we move the lock—"

"Clever boy." Ondrey's grip tightened. "Clever and doomed if we stand here admiring it. Run first. Admire after."

* * *

They ran.

The palace corridors blurred past—grey halls, grey banners with the Vessian sun worn to a ghost of itself, faded servants pressed against walls watching them pass with eyes like smudged charcoal. Tam ran in the middle, Freya ahead, Ondrey behind, and the whole time the hum of the Compact's engines grew beneath his feet like a second heartbeat, a heartbeat with no blood in it, only arithmetic.

And underneath the hum, underneath everything, he could feel the *other* thing starting.

The rite.

It didn't need him yet—that was the horror of it. He had thought, somehow, that it would wait for him, that the magic would knock politely at the door of his consent. But the queen had begun it with her own fading body as the first fuel, spending what was left of herself to prime the working, and Tam could feel it moving through Vessia like a tide coming in. The bells of the city had begun to ring—no hands on the ropes, no ringers in the towers, just the bronze remembering its purpose all at once across the whole grey city, a thousand bells tolling in a rhythm that was not celebration and not mourning but *summons*.

"She's calling the country to witness," Ondrey shouted over the din as they burst out onto the stair that wound up the bell tower. "Old law. A restoration must be witnessed by the people or it doesn't bind. She's waking every Vessian soul that still has enough shape left to watch."

"Can they stop it?" Freya called back.

"They won't want to!" Ondrey laughed, and it was a broken sound. "That's the tragedy of it, Keeper! They'll stand in the square and *cheer*! Justice! Their borders back! They don't know the price—they can't know it, the queen has kept it locked in treaty-cipher for four hundred years—and by the time they learn it, the fire will already be lit!"

They climbed. Tam's legs burned. Through the tower's open arches he could see the city falling away below, and beyond the city, far off at the edge of everything, the seam of treaty-light burning white against the grey horizon—and through it, impossibly, a sliver of blue.

Valley sky.

His stomach turned over. Because he knew that sky. He had lived under it for months now. He knew the particular softness of it, the way clouds came over the western ridge in the afternoon, the way it smelled after rain. And it was visible through the breach the way a face is visible through a closing door—as if the whole valley were leaning in to watch what happened next, not knowing it was the thing on the table.

"It's already tearing," he whispered. He stopped on the stairs, and Freya stopped above him, and looked back, and followed his eyes to the distant blue. "Freya. Look. The gate—it's not waiting for the rite anymore. The Compact's sealing and the queen's opening, they're both pulling on it, and it's—"

He didn't have the word. In Vessian he might have; Vessian had a word for a door torn rather than opened, *selvane*, a wound-door. But he was thinking in valley-speech now, half the time, and valley-speech had no word for it, because the valley had never imagined it.

The gate was tearing wide.

He could feel it through the soles of his feet, through the stone, through whatever it was inside him that made him the last speaker and the living key—a great slow ripping sensation, like cloth giving way stitch by stitch, except each stitch was a seal his order had set four hundred years ago and each one coming loose let a little more of two worlds lean against each other. Blue sky through a white seam. Grey city through a blue one. And in between, thin as wire, the valley itself, stretched between the pulling forces like a rope in a tug-of-war where both teams meant to win by cutting it down the middle.

"We have minutes," Freya said. Her face had gone the color of the palace stone. "Maybe less. Ondrey—the archive—"

"The archive will be ash or worse by now if the Compact reached it," Ondrey said grimly, climbing again. "But the master copy was never in the archive. Where do you think I've been keeping the bells ringing all these years, child? Someone had to hold the count." He patted the bell tower wall as they climbed, fondly, terribly. "Every name. All of them. Ringed into the bronze, stroke by stroke, year by year, while the world forgot. The Compact burns paper. Let them try to burn a bell."

Tam's heart slammed. "Then we can still—"

Above them, the top of the tower opened onto the belfry, and the belfry was *screaming*.

All the bells at once, the great ones and the small, swinging with no hands, and the sound was so enormous Tam clapped his palms over his ears and still felt it in his ribs, in his teeth, in the marrow of him. And through the belfry's wide arches, the whole of Vessia lay spread out below—the grey city, the grey fields, the grey river, and everywhere, rising from the streets and squares and doorways, the people. Faded figures emerging from houses that had forgotten them, gathering in the square, turning their smudged faces up toward the palace, toward the bells, toward the queen standing now on her balcony with her arms raised and her body nearly transparent and her voice carrying over everything:

*"Witness! Witness, my people! What was taken is taken back! What was erased is written again! Vessia anu sel—Vessia above all names—"*

And the crowd roared, thousands of fading voices, and the sound should have been joyful and was, it was, joy like a fire in a library.

Below it all, at the edge of hearing, the Compact's engines hummed their flat arithmetic, and the seam of light widened, and through it the valley's blue sky shivered like water touched by a finger.

"Freya." Tam grabbed her sleeve. His voice was lost in the bells; he wasn't sure she could hear him; he said it anyway, and watched her read his lips, and watched her face break and re-form into the thing she did instead of crying, which was deciding. "The names. If we're going to do it—Ondrey's way—we have to do it *now*. Before her rite finishes. Before their seal closes. Before—"

Before the valley stops existing. He didn't say it. He didn't have to.

Freya looked out over the roaring grey country, at the queen dissolving into her own triumph, at the blue sliver of home trembling in the widening wound of light. Then she pulled the keeper's iron from her belt and held it up, and the nearest bell—a small one, a hand-bell hanging by the arch—swung toward it, eager, answering.

"Then we start counting," Freya shouted, over the bells, over the horns, over the tearing of the world. "Ondrey—how many names?"

Ondrey closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet and steady and absolutely calm, the calm of a man who had spent forty years alone with bronze and had made his peace with exactly this moment arriving.

"All of them," he said. "Eleven thousand, four hundred, and six."

The bells tolled. The seam widened. Somewhere below, the queen's voice rose toward its final invocation, and the first syllable of it went through Tam like a nail through the map of himself—

*Selvane.*

Wound-door. Open.

And the sky over Vessia split from edge to edge, and through it poured the blue of home.
