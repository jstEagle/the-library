## 31. Freya's Choice

The rite had a sound before it had a shape.

Freya heard it first as a pressure behind her eyes, then as a low tone rising through the marble of the unmapped palace, through the flagstones of the square, through the bones of the bell tower where Ondrey stood with his hands folded and his face grey as ash. It was the sound of a country being pulled through a keyhole.

Below her, in the great court of Vessia, Queen Ilsevan raised her arms, and the light that answered her was not light at all but absence—the colour of the sky Freya had seen through the broken seal, the wrong-coloured sky that belonged to no map. The queen's outline flickered like a candle in wind. She had spent nearly all of herself already, and what remained she was spending gladly, furiously, on this.

And Tam stood at the centre of it.

They had bound him—not cruelly, nothing so honest as rope, but with ribbons of pale script that wound up his arms and around his chest, the rite's grammar made visible, holding him upright at the heart of a circle of fading courtiers. His mouth moved. He was mispronouncing. Freya could see it even from the tower steps: the little deliberate stumbles he'd practised with her in the archive's ruins, the swallowed vowels, the consonants set down in the wrong places like stones laid to trip a walker. Every error bought them minutes. Every minute, the queen's patience thinned like ice over a well.

But the minutes were gone now. The Compact had come.

Freya felt their treaty-magic before she saw them—a grinding at the edge of perception, the sensation of a page being pressed flat. Through the palace's open colonnade she could see the far horizon of Vessia, and along that horizon the world beyond was closing like a book. Grey riders on grey ridges. Instruments humming. Somewhere out there, men with signatures and seals were doing to an entire country what they had once done to its name: smoothing it away, filing it under *never was*.

And this time they would not leave. Wren's warning still rang in her—*they mean to seal it permanent, Freya, with you and the boy on the wrong side*—and beneath the warning, the older one, the queen's own confession delivered without apology: *the valley burns. That is justice.*

Three futures, and every one of them a knife.

Let the rite finish, and Tam died as the living key, and the valley's people burned as traitors' seed. Let the Compact finish, and Vessia died unmourned, unrecorded, erased so thoroughly that even grief would have nowhere to stand. Or—

Or the third path. Ondrey's path. The one with a hook in it, and the hook was shaped exactly like her own heart.

Ondrey stood beside her at the top of the tower stair, the last unfaded man in a country of ghosts, and in his hands he held the salvaged ledger: the Archive of Names, every citizen of Vessia written in ink that refused to fade because it had been written by people who refused to be forgotten. He had carried it up two hundred steps on legs that trembled, and he had not complained once, because complaint was a luxury the faded had lost and he guarded his humanity like a coal cupped in both palms.

"The counter-oath," he said again, quietly, as if the words might break if spoken loudly. "You know the terms."

"I know the terms." She had read them three times by candlelight while Tam slept against her shoulder. They were simple the way a blade is simple. To speak the names into the world beyond, an oath-breaker must swear them—one of the order that had sold Vessia, standing in the breach and unsaying the founding lie word by word, name by name. And the cost of unsaying was symmetry: what the keepers' oath had hidden from the world, the breaking would hide from the world in turn. *She would be forgotten.* Not killed. Unremembered. Her cottage, her rounds, her twenty years of solitary competence—all of it would stand empty of her in the minds of everyone she had ever known. Wren. The elders. The children in the square.

Tam.

That was the part she hadn't told him. That was the part he had found anyway, because eight-year-olds who have lost everything develop a genius for spotting the next loss coming.

"You don't have to," Ondrey said. "There is no shame in—"

"Yes, there is." She took the ledger from him. It was heavier than paper had any right to be; it weighed like a country. "There's shame everywhere in this. I'm just choosing which kind I can live inside."

Below, the queen's voice rose to its full register, and the rite lurched forward.

Freya went down the stairs two at a time.

* * *

The court of the unmapped palace had been built for coronations, and it received this one with terrible aptness. Banners that should have been crimson hung grey as fog. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, translucent as breath on glass, watching their queen with hollow eyes—and Freya remembered what they had told her in the corridors, what they had begged her in whispers when the queen's attention turned elsewhere: *we do not want conquest. We want to be remembered. Stop her if you can.*

She walked down the centre of the court and did not run, because running would bring guards, and guards would bring the queen's attention too soon. She walked like a keeper on her rounds, which was the only way she knew how to walk toward something frightening: steadily, with her hands busy, with the work ahead of her larger than the fear behind it.

Tam saw her first. His eyes went wide, and the ribbon-script around his arms tightened as he tried to turn, and Freya shook her head once, small and sharp: *don't move, don't fight it yet, let me get close.* He understood children's signals better than adults'—he stilled, and kept stumbling over his syllables, magnificent, brave, buying her the last few steps.

Queen Ilsevan turned.

Up close, the queen was almost done. Freya could see the pillars of the hall through her shoulder. What remained of her face was beautiful the way the last line of a poem is beautiful—because it is ending, and knows it. Her eyes were the only solid thing left about her, and they were fixed on Freya with something worse than hatred: recognition.

"Keeper," the queen said. "You've come to watch justice done at last."

"I've come to stop it."

"Of course you have." Ilsevan smiled, and the smile cost her; her cheek went briefly transparent. "Your order stopped my country for four hundred years. Why should its last daughter be different? Come, then. Stand there. Watch your valley burn and tell yourself it was necessary. You've had good teachers."

"The valley doesn't burn." Freya lifted the ledger. "No one burns. Listen to me, Ilsevan—your people don't want this. I've talked to them. They don't want borders back. They want their names back. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"They are *faded*." For the first time the queen's voice cracked, and underneath the crack was not rage but grief, vast and old and worn smooth as river stone. "They are half-erased and they do not know their own minds. I know their minds. I have carried them for four hundred years alone—"

"You carried your justice," Freya said. "They carried their names. And the names are here." She held the ledger high enough for the faded courtiers to see, and she watched them see it—watched a woman near the wall press her translucent hand to her mouth, watched an old man straighten as though called by rank, watched a hundred ghosts remember, all at once, that they had been somebody. "Every name, Ilsevan. Your whole country. I can give them back—not the borders, not the war, not the graves of the valley's people. The names. Each of them. Into the world beyond, where forgetting can't reach."

The queen stared at the ledger, and for one suspended moment Freya thought—hoped—that grief might bend.

Then the doors of the court burst inward, and the moment died.

Grey livery. Grey instruments. The Border Compact had not waited for the sealing magic to grind its slow way across the horizon; they had breached, spending treaty-power like water, and now surveyors filled the colonnade with the flat-eyed purpose of men doing arithmetic. Their leader—Freya knew his face from the ridge, the one who had shattered the first seal with a crowbar and a permit—raised a rod of black metal, and the air around it began to press flat.

"By authority of the Compact of Borders," he said, in the tone of a man reading a weather report, "this territory is declared non-extant. All persons within are advised that continued existence is unauthorized."

Unauthorized existence. Freya had crossed into a dead country, watched a child's mother fade to an outline, seen an archive burn, and somehow those words were the ones that lit the fuse in her chest.

"No," she said.

The leader glanced at her with mild interest. "Keeper. Your treaties expired with your order's relevance."

"I said *no*." And she opened the ledger.

* * *

Later—if there was a later; if the world kept records of moments like this, which was rather the point—she would try to describe what it felt like to begin, and fail, because there was no describing the feeling of a country pouring through you.

Ondrey had taught her the frame of the counter-oath in the bell tower, and it was short, because the important oaths always are:

*"I am the daughter of the oath-breakers. I inherit their debt. I pay it in the only coin left: the truth, spoken aloud, name by name, until none is owed. Let the hiding end. Let the remembering begin. And let the price fall on me, who chooses it."*

She spoke it into the court of the unmapped palace, and the words did not echo—they *landed*, each one striking the flagstones like a dropped stone, and the grey banners shivered, and the Compact's leader took a step backward despite himself, because some things are older than treaties and his instruments knew it.

Then she put her finger on the first line of the ledger, drew a breath that hurt, and spoke the first name.

"Aldric Vessane of the Lower Terraces."

It was like opening a door in a house she had lived in all her life and finding a country behind it. The name left her mouth and became itself somewhere in the air—a presence, a weight, a person restored to the record of the world. In the ranks of the faded court, a soldier who had been barely a smudge suddenly had a face, and the face was weeping.

"Maren Ostrevik, bell-founder, of the Street of Nine Bells."

Another. A woman near the colonnade solidified from fog to flesh, gasping, clutching her own wrists as if checking they were real.

"Pell Harrowgate. Senna Ilvane. The brothers Corin and Doss Ammerlin. Old Yeva, who kept the gate-shrine on the Salt Road—"

The queen made a sound.

Freya forced herself to look at Ilsevan, because she owed her that. The queen stood motionless amid the re-solidifying crowd, and her expression was the most terrible thing Freya had ever seen: hope. Raw, disbelieving, furious hope, the hope of a woman watching her life's work being accomplished in a form she had never permitted herself to want. Her lips moved—*stop her, stop her, this is not the way*—but no command came. Because her people were becoming real around her, one name at a time, and a queen cannot order the sun to rise differently when it is rising.

Freya kept reading.

The names came faster now, and each one cost her something she couldn't itemize—it was like bleeding from a place she didn't have a map for—but she had spent twenty years learning to work through discomfort in silence, alone, at dawn, with cold hands on cold stone. This was the same discipline pointed at a different door. She gave them the merchants and the millers, the midwives and the mapmakers, the children—God, the children, dozens of them, small names that hit her like fists, and beside her she heard Tam sobbing and saying each name after her in perfect native Vessian, doubling them, rooting them, his voice cracking on the ones that belonged to neighbours, to friends, to—

"—Ilsa Vessane," Freya read, and Tam's voice broke entirely, because that was his mother, and the ribbon-script binding him burst like rotten thread as the rite lost its anchor, and he ran to the fading outline of a woman in the third rank who was becoming, name by spoken name, a person again.

The Compact's leader recovered his nerve.

"Seal it," he said, and drove the black rod down against the floor, and the treaty-magic came.

Freya felt it coming the way you feel weather change—in the teeth, in the scar tissue of old duties. It came as flatness, as the pressing-down of the page, as the smooth grey erasure rolling across the court toward her like a tide with intent. It would strike her mid-name. She knew that. She kept reading anyway, because that was the whole design of the counter-oath: the names must be spoken *into the world beyond*, and the world beyond was arriving, right now, as a wall of forgetting, and so she would speak the names directly into the teeth of it and let the erasure carry them—let the Compact's own magic become the courier of the thing it had come to destroy. Every name the sealing struck would be flung outward, past the border, past the ridges, into the ears and archives and dreams of the nations that had signed Vessia away. They had come to erase a country. They would spend their power broadcasting its citizens instead.

It was, she thought distantly, almost funny. Almost.

The grey tide reached the first rank of courtiers and broke around them like water around pilings—the restored could not be flattened, not anymore, not with their names alive in the world. But it reached *her*, and she was not restored; she was the hinge, the debt-payer, the one who had volunteered to hold the erasure's other half.

It went through her like winter going through a coat.

She kept reading. "Tomas Bellwether. Anka Rill. The ferryman Joss, of the river crossing at—" Her voice came out strange, thinning at the edges, and she understood with a calm that frightened her more than panic would have: this was the price arriving. Not death. Subtraction. With every name she gave to the world, the world took a piece of her in exchange—her name in Wren's mouth, her face in the elders' memory, her twenty years of rounds and rituals and quiet competent loneliness, all of it being paid out, coin by coin, to balance a four-hundred-year-old ledger.

Her hands were going transparent. She could see the ledger's pages through her fingers.

"Keep reading," Ondrey said. He had come down the stairs after all, the stubborn old bell-keeper, and he stood between her and the Compact's rod with his frail body and his unfaded heart, and his voice was iron. "As long as your voice holds, girl, the names hold. Read."

She read.

The court blazed now with returned people—solid, weeping, shouting names of their own, catching the ledger's list and carrying it onward in a hundred voices, a relay of the resurrected. The Compact's surveyors faltered; their instruments shrieked and cracked, unable to flatten what ten thousand mouths were insisting upon. The grey tide thinned, wavered, and began, unbelievably, to recede.

And Freya Hartwell stood at the centre of it, giving herself away line by line, and felt the last of her own name go loose in her chest like a tooth ready to drop.

The final page. The final names. Her vision was greying at the edges, or perhaps that was the world forgetting her already, beginning its gentle thorough work. She found the last entry—she had known whose it would be; Ondrey had shown her, weeks ago it seemed, a lifetime ago—and she read it aloud with the last full strength of the last keeper of the Hollow Gate:

"Queen Ilsevan Vessane, last of her line, who kept faith when no one else would."

The queen came fully, finally real—solid, mortal, mortal-eyed—and looked at Freya across a court full of her living people, and did not say thank you, because there was no word large enough, and did not say forgive me, because some debts aren't asked forgiven.

What she said was: "The Compact will regroup. Watch the gate, keeper. Watch—"

But Freya was already falling, and the world was already letting go of her name, and the last thing she saw was Tam turning from his mother's arms, Tam screaming her name—*Freya, FREYA*—a name that landed on her like rain on glass and slid off, unheard, unknown, a boy crying out for a stranger.

Then the grey took the rest, and the keeper of the Hollow Gate was no one at all.
