## 32. The Queen Undone

Tam heard the names before he understood what they were doing to him.

They came through the tearing air of the gate like rain through a broken roof—Freya's voice, cracked and steady and burning, speaking word after word after word into the white light of the Compact's sealing magic. Each name struck something in his chest, a small bell rung by a hand he couldn't see. He knew these bells. He had known them all his life without knowing it: in the grey city where no one rang the bells, in his mother's house where her outline flickered like a candle behind glass, in the hollow place inside him that had been shaped like a country.

The first name was *Maren Ostrevik*. He didn't know who that was. But when Freya spoke it, a woman three rows back from the throne dais gasped—a real gasp, wet and sudden—and Tam watched color climb up her throat like ivy reclaiming a wall. Her hands went from translucent to flesh. She pressed them against her own face as if checking whether she still owned one.

*Maren Ostrevik*, said Freya again, because the rite required every name twice, once for Vessia and once for the world beyond.

And Maren Ostrevik wept, and said, "That's me. That is me. I'm a cooper's daughter, I lived on Bellows Lane, I had a dog named—" and then her voice failed her because the memory of the dog was arriving all at once, along with everything else, forty years of fading reversing in a single breath.

Tam stood at the foot of the dais with his fists clenched so hard his nails bit crescents into his palms. He had done his part. His mispronunciations had bought them hours; his true tongue, spoken now without fear, had helped Freya shape each name correctly, had caught her when she stumbled on a vowel that did not exist in any living language but his. He had fed her the words like a boy feeding thread to a seamstress. And now the seam was closing over the whole wound of Vessia, and the queen—

He looked up at Queen Ilsevan.

She was standing very straight at the top of the dais, both hands resting on the armrests of a throne that had gone grey as driftwood decades ago. The rite had been torn out of her control. He could see it in the set of her shoulders, in the way her fingers gripped the wood—not with rage, not yet, but with the terrible stillness of someone watching their life's work be carried off in a direction they never chose. Around her, her court was waking. Lords and ladies and clerks and soldiers, faded to the color of old paper for longer than Tam had been alive, were flooding back into themselves like ink returning to water. Somewhere on the left a man began to laugh and couldn't stop. Somewhere on the right a woman was calling a name over and over—*Piotr, Piotr*—and another voice answered her, and they found each other in the crowd and held on.

The queen did not move toward them. She did not move at all.

And Tam saw, with the awful clarity children sometimes get, that the color was not coming to her.

It was draining instead. Slowly, gently, the way water leaves a basin with a hairline crack. Her restoration was going somewhere—outward, downward, into the people she had spent an age trying to save—but none of it stayed. It passed through her like light through glass. She had poured herself into the rite's making, Tam realized. Or the rite had simply taken her, the way a fire takes the last of the wood, because she was the one thing left in Vessia that had refused to fade on its own, and refusal burns hottest of all.

"No," he said. It came out small. He said it louder. "No—you have to stop, you're—"

"TAM." Freya's voice cut across the square like a whip-crack, but even mid-rite, even with the Compact's sealing light crawling toward her like frost across a window, she heard him. She always heard him. "Stay with the names. Don't stop. Whatever happens—don't stop."

So he didn't stop. He climbed two steps of the dais because he could not bear to shout the words up at a woman who might be dying, and he kept feeding Freya names out of the salvaged ledger clutched against his ribs—the archive they'd pulled from the burning stacks, pages soft as moth wings, every citizen of a country that officially had never existed. *Yeva Solmark. Danya Ilves. The brothers Kask, Aldo and Renn.* Each one landed somewhere in the crowd and made a person whole.

And the queen watched her people come back to life, and began, very quietly, to let go.

* * *

Later—he would remember this later, when there was a later, when the world had settled into its new and stranger shape—Tam would try to describe what fading looked like from close up, and he would fail, because it did not look like dying. It looked like being finished.

Queen Ilsevan's edges softened first. The hard line of her jaw, the sharp architecture of her cheekbones—all the angles she had sharpened herself against for a century of waiting—went gentle, went indistinct, went the way of a coastline seen through morning fog. But her eyes stayed. They stayed clear and dark and terribly awake, fixed on the square below her, on the crowd that was remembering itself person by person while she forgot herself moment by moment. And she was smiling. Not the smile she had worn in the unmapped palace, when she'd offered Freya justice and borders and blood. This one was smaller. This one was almost private.

"You see," she said, and Tam realized she was talking to him, because Freya was too far away and too deep in the rite to hear anything but names. Her voice had changed too—it had lost its resonance, the palace-stone echo of it, until it sounded like just a voice, a tired woman's voice, speaking to a boy on some steps. "You see how it was supposed to work. All of us together, or none of us. One restoration, whole. A country returned entire." She exhaled, and the exhale took something with it; her crown, which had sat crooked on her head since the day Tam first saw her, slipped further and she did not reach for it. "I never imagined being saved in pieces."

"Freya's doing it right," Tam said fiercely. He didn't know why he needed to defend her to this woman, only that he did. "She's giving everyone back. Every name. Even the ones you'd given up on."

"I know." The queen's gaze drifted across the square. It found a soldier near the fountain whose uniform had just remembered its buttons; the soldier was staring at his own hands, turning them over, saying something under his breath that might have been a prayer or might have been a curse or might have been, in Vessian, which Tam now dreamed in, both at once. "Look at them. Look at how loud they are." Something moved through her face that Tam had no word for—grief and pride holding each other up. "A hundred years I planned our return. Armies. Treaties. Borders drawn back onto maps that had forgotten us. And it turns out all it wanted was one keeper willing to break her oath, and one child willing to say the words wrong on purpose, and—" her breath caught, and thinned, "—and someone to read the ledger aloud. I built a fortress around a door that only ever needed opening."

"The valley," Tam started, "the valley's people didn't—"

"They didn't," she agreed, and there was no anger left in it, which frightened him more than anger would have. "I told myself they did. Traitors' children, living warm in stolen quiet, while my people faded like breath off glass. I needed them guilty. Guilt is a kind of border too, you understand. As long as someone owed me, I still existed. Debt was the last country I had." She looked down at her hands. They were nearly transparent now; Tam could see the grey stone of the dais through her fingers, could see the faint ghost of rings she must have worn a century ago. "But your keeper wouldn't take the debt. She kept refusing the clean story. Justice for us, ruin for them—she wouldn't sign it. And I hated her for it. Right up until about an hour ago, I hated her for it." The almost-smile again. "And then she opened her mouth and started reading, and every name cost her something—I can hear it, boy, can't you? Each one takes a piece of her. And she keeps reading anyway. That's not justice." Her voice frayed. "That's love wearing justice's coat. I never learned the difference. I think that's why the color won't stay in me."

"Maybe it will," Tam lied, because he was eight years old and she was dying in front of him and lying was the only tool he had. "Maybe when the last name's spoken—"

"When the last name's spoken, there will be nothing left to speak it *for*." She said it gently, the way you tell a child the dog is not sleeping. "Don't grieve ahead of time. It's rude to the moment." Then, softer, looking past him at the gate, where Freya stood wrapped in light: "Listen to me now. I have perhaps a hundred words left in me and I mean to spend them well."

* * *

The Compact's sealing magic had stopped advancing. Tam noticed it only peripherally—the white frost-light at the gate's edge hung motionless, straining against something it could not name, because you cannot erase a people who are being re-membered faster than your treaties can unmake them. The grey riders stood at the threshold like men who had arrived to arrest a river. Their instruments hummed and whined and read nothing. There was nothing left on their charts to find.

The queen followed his glance, and her last strength gathered itself like a wave.

"She will tell you the Compact is beaten," she said. "She may even believe it. Listen to me, Tam of the Bellows Lane, son of Yeva Solmark—" and hearing his mother's name in the queen's mouth, spoken with the full weight of the restored tongue, undid him more than anything else that day. "Erasure is patient. It has budgets. It has offices. The men and women who burned our archive did not burn it in rage—they burned it in *procedure*. Rage ends. Procedure clocks in every morning. When I am gone and this square goes home to its suppers, the Compact will withdraw, regroup, and begin again in the only language it truly speaks: paper. New surveys. New maps. New histories in which tonight never happened and no such country ever stood here. They cannot undo the names Freya Hartwell has spoken—but they can make the world too busy, too comfortable, too far away to care that the names exist. Forgetting doesn't need a spell, child. It needs only everyone looking elsewhere." Her hand rose—it weighed nothing now, it was barely a suggestion of a hand—and touched his cheek, cold as a bell in winter. "Tell her. Tell the keeper: the oath is rewritten, the door is open, and open doors must be *tended*. Not locked. Tended. There is a difference, and kingdoms rise and fall on the difference. Guard the remembering. That is all any keeper ever needed to do, and it is the one duty my order—" her mouth twisted, "—my order's order, her order, never once performed."

"I'll tell her," Tam said. His voice broke in the middle. "I'll tell her every day if I have to."

"I believe you." She sank—not fell, sank—onto the throne that had once been hers, that was now mostly a shape the eye slid off of. Below them, Freya's voice climbed toward the final page of the ledger. Tam could see her shaking. He could see Wren beside her, steadying the book, and beyond them the whole waking multitude of Vessia turned toward the sound of its own names like flowers finding a sun they'd forgotten existed.

"One more thing." The queen's eyes had begun to dim, but they found his and held. "Your mother. Yeva Solmark, Bellows Lane, third house from the corner with the blue door. She is awake now. She is looking for a small boy who fell out of the world." The ghost of the almost-smile, one last time. "When you go to her—and you will go to her—tell her the queen is sorry. Not for the war. Not for the fading. Tell her I am sorry I let a country become a cause, when it was always supposed to be a home. You are the proof, Tam. You were the home, walking around, the whole time. I was too busy guarding the idea to notice the child." Her hand lifted again, weightless, and settled briefly on his head like a blessing or like snow. "Go be someone's child. Let the queens do their own grieving."

"Your Majesty—" he started, uselessly, the way children start sentences at funerals.

"Ondrey will ring the bells," she said. "See that he does it properly. We always rang them flat at endings. It's in the manual. There was a manual. Ask him—" and here her voice finally went thin as paper, and Tam leaned in to catch the last of it, the very last words of the last queen of Vessia:

"—ask him to teach you the third bell. Someone should know it who isn't a bell-keeper. Someone should know it who's a *beginning*."

* * *

Freya spoke the final name at sunset.

*Kessa Ilsevan*, twice—once for Vessia, once for the world beyond—and the crowd below the dais made a sound Tam had never heard a crowd make, a sound like a held breath of a hundred years finally let go. Because the last name in the ledger, the name Freya had saved for the end, the name the queen had never offered as her own, was the queen's.

Kessa Ilsevan. Cooper's granddaughter, Bellows Lane born, crowned at nineteen in a country with four years left to live.

Tam heard it land in the square and saw hundreds of faces turn upward toward the throne—to thank her, to mourn her, to see her—and found only empty air and a crown rolling gently to a stop against the armrest of a chair that held no one.

But Tam, half-Vessian, immune to forgetting, saw what the others could not. For a single heartbeat, where the queen had been, there was a figure of pure evening light, standing, unbent, uncrowned, looking out over her people with her hands loose at her sides like a woman stepping off a long shift. She was not fading anymore. She was simply *done*—finished the way a bell is finished when the note has fully left it and the metal is allowed, at last, to rest.

Her light turned. Found Freya, swaying on her feet at the gate, wrung out and alive. Found Tam on the steps.

And where her mouth would have been, without sound, without breath, in the dead tongue that was now Tam's inheritance and Freya's borrowed one, came the last words of Queen Ilsevan of Vessia—carried on no air, written directly onto the inside of Tam's skull in letters of cooling gold:

*Tend the door. They will come back with better maps.*

Then she was gone, entirely, and the bells of the grey city—every bell in every tower, Ondrey somewhere among them with his rope-worn hands—began to ring, flat and low and perfect, the ending-bell, the one Tam promised himself he would learn.

He got down off the dais on legs that shook, crossed the square through a crowd that was laughing and weeping and holding its own history like something breakable, and reached Freya just as her knees gave. He caught her arm. She caught him back. Behind them the Hollow Gate stood wide open for the first time in a hundred years, humming softly, patient as a held-open hand—and far beyond it, tiny in the distance, Tam could see the grey riders withdrawing in good order, unhurried, unbroken, already turning toward whatever office would receive their report.

"Your majesty's last words," Tam said, when he could speak.

Freya blinked down at him, exhausted, smoke-smudged, her voice destroyed from a thousand names. "*What?*"

"She said to tell you." He swallowed. "Tend the door. They'll come back with better maps."

Freya Hartwell looked at the open gate for a long moment. Then she put her hand on his shoulder and turned them both, together, to face it.

"Then," she said, in her ruined voice, "we'd better learn everything there is to know about doors."
