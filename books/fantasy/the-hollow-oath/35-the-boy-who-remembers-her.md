## 35. The Boy Who Remembers Her

Tam woke before the sun, the way he always did now, with his mother's name already in his mouth.

*Marisol. Marisol Vane.* He said it once into the dark of Wren's spare room, where he'd slept for three nights since coming back through the gate, and felt it settle somewhere behind his ribs like a stone dropped into a well. In Vessia, names had weight. Here, in the valley where magic went thin as winter milk, they had a different kind of weight—the kind that held things down. Held them *here*.

He dressed quietly. Wren was still asleep in the next room, snoring the slow, stony snore of a man who worked with his hands all day and dreamed about it too. Tam let himself out through the back door, past the mason's yard with its blocks of pale granite and its half-finished lintel, and stood in the blue-grey light looking down the lane toward the keeper's cottage.

It looked wrong.

That was the only word he had for it, and he hated how small the word was. The cottage wasn't ruined. Nothing about it was ruined. The roof was whole, the chimney straight, the herb garden along the south wall still growing in its tidy rows because gardens didn't need to be remembered to keep growing. But the gate in the garden fence hung open, and no one had oiled its hinges, and the path to the door was furred over with weeds that had no business being there after only three days. Three days of forgetting, and the valley had already begun to let her go the way it let everything go—softly, without malice, the way snow melts off a roof you've stopped looking at.

The Hollow Gate itself stood at the valley's head, quiet now. Truly quiet, for the first time in Tam's memory of it. When he'd first come stumbling down out of the hills, soaked and starving and speaking a dead tongue, the gate had hummed against his skin like a struck bell. Now it was just stone. Old stone, carved over with words in two languages—one of them his, one of them Freya's—and between the carvings, the new oath she had cut herself, plain letters in the common speech:

*To remember. To shelter. To never trade a people for a peace.*

He could read both languages now. That was the strange inheritance of what he'd become—not fully Vessian anymore, not fully anything the old ledgers would have recognized, but something with roots in two soils. And because of it, because he was *half* of here and *half* of there, the forgetting that had taken Freya's name out of every mouth in the valley could not get its fingers around him. He remembered her. All of her. The way she'd knelt in the rain to look him in the eye. The way her hands shook when she read her mentor's last letter but her voice hadn't. The way she'd said *I choose you* not to him exactly, but to the whole shape of the world, loud enough that even a country made of fading people had heard it.

And the valley had forgotten.

Not cruelly. That was the worst part. He'd walked into the village yesterday and Mrs. Aldery at the bakery had smiled at him—smiled at *him*, the stranger boy who'd arrived last autumn, though she'd fed him honey rolls all winter when Freya brought him down for market—and asked if he was lost, poor lamb, and should someone fetch the reeve? She'd been kind. Kindness without memory, like a fire lit in an empty room.

Wren remembered. Wren had smuggled the memory out somehow—Tam didn't understand all of it, some trick of stone and oath that the mason wouldn't explain—but Wren was one man, and one man's remembering sat in him like a splinter, hurting, unable to spread.

But Tam was not one man. Tam was a boy who spoke the gate's native tongue, and the gate was the oldest thing in the valley, older than the cottages, older than the square, older than the elders and their careful silences. Stone listened to stone. And Tam had spent the walk down from Wren's yard turning something over in his mind, the way he turned over river stones, looking at the underside.

If the valley had forgotten Freya because the forgetting lived in the valley's stones—

then maybe the stones could be taught to remember again.

* * *

The cottage door wasn't locked. It was never locked; Freya had told him once that a keeper's door stayed open because a keeper's whole life was answering knocks. He stepped inside and the smell hit him first—dried lavender, lamp oil, the ghost of porridge—and then everything else, all at once, the way grief does.

Her cloak on its peg. Her boots by the hearth, mud still dried on the left one from the day they'd run the hollow road. The table where she'd laid out the gate inscriptions and traced the Vessian letters with one finger while he sounded them out, patient as spring water. A cup on the table, half-full of tea gone cold and dark, the way she'd left it the morning she'd walked up to the gate to face the Compact and never came back down as herself.

Nobody had touched any of it. Not because they were preserving it. Because nobody knew whose house this was. The forgetting hadn't erased the cottage—it had erased the *meaning* of the cottage, and a house without meaning is just walls waiting to be reclaimed by weather.

Tam stood in the middle of the room and let himself cry, briefly, the way he'd learned to in Vessia: fast and quiet, tears spent like coins you couldn't afford to waste. Then he wiped his face on his sleeve, because his mother had hated a sulky face at breakfast, and got to work.

He took the cup and drank the cold tea himself, which felt like a kind of communion. He hung the cloak straighter on its peg. And then he went out into the garden, knelt at the gatestone by the fence—the little one, the ordinary one, the one Freya's hand had worn smooth opening it ten thousand mornings—and pressed his palm flat against it.

"Freya Hartwell," he said, in the common tongue, clear and deliberate, the way you speak to something hard of hearing.

The stone did nothing. Of course it did nothing. Stones weren't bells.

But he hadn't expected them to be. Not yet.

He switched tongues. The Vessian word for *remember* was also the word for *re-member*—to make a member again, to join back to the body—and when he said it now, laying his other hand beside the first, he felt the faintest tremor under his palms, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Not magic. The valley barely allowed magic. But language was older than magic, and the gate-tongue had never been magic at all, really. It had been *agreement*. Words that things agreed to answer to.

"Freya Hartwell," he said again, in the gate-tongue this time, and the stone under his hands warmed by a degree he might have imagined. "Keeper of the Hollow Gate. She kept your round. She walked your roads. She broke a false oath so that a true one could live."

He said it the way Ondrey had taught him names were spoken in the archive—not as information but as *offering*. You didn't tell a stone a name. You gave it the name, and the stone decided what to do with the gift.

Then he stood, brushed off his knees, and walked to the next stone. The gatepost at the lane's end. And spoke her name into that one too.

* * *

By midmorning he had done eleven stones, and his voice was going rough, and he had learned something important: the valley was listening, but slowly, the way a big animal wakes.

The gatepost had been second. By the time he reached the well-cover in the square—stone number nine—he'd begun to notice small changes. The baker's boy, hauling flour sacks, had stopped in the lane outside the cottage and frowned at the open garden gate, and said, to nobody, "Someone ought to latch that." Not *whose gate is that*. Just the instinct of care arriving before the memory it belonged to, like feeling the shape of a word before you know its meaning.

At the well, an old woman drawing water had looked at him strangely and said, "You talk to stones, lad?" and when he said yes, she'd laughed—but she'd laughed the way you laugh at a joke you almost catch, and she'd drawn her water slower than usual, glancing back at the keeper's cottage twice.

Stone eleven was the mounting block by the churchyard wall, and when Tam pressed his hands to it and gave it Freya's name and her deeds, he felt the tremor stronger than before—a shiver in the granite, brief as a bird's shadow. And across the lane, Mrs. Aldery stopped sweeping her step and stood very still, her broom held off the ground, her face doing something complicated, like a woman trying to recall a dream from years ago.

"Freya," she said. Just the name. No meaning attached. She said it the way you say a word too many times until it goes strange—and then she frowned at herself, puzzled, and went back inside.

Tam's chest ached. It was working, but it was working the way frost works on a window—slowly, from the edges in. Eleven stones. There were hundreds in the valley. The gate alone had more than that in its foundation alone.

He needed help. And there was exactly one person who could give it, and that person was currently sitting in the square pretending very badly to be interested in the price of nails.

"Wren," Tam called, "you're terrible at following me secretly."

Wren crossed the square with his rolling gait, big hands hooked in his belt, his weathered face arranged into an expression of elaborate innocence. "Wasn't following. Was buying nails."

"You bought nails yesterday."

"Masons buy a lot of nails." He crouched down to Tam's level, and his voice dropped, and the innocence fell away like a mask slipping. "Eleven?"

"Eleven." Tam rubbed his throat. "It works, Wren. It's slow, but it works. The stones carry it. I can feel them holding it, like—" he searched — "like water held in a wall. Seeping."

Wren looked toward the churchyard, where the old woman was still standing with her bucket, staring at nothing. "Mrs. Tarn saw her husband's name cut on the war memorial this morning," he said quietly, "and cried without knowing why. That was you?"

"The memorial is stone twelve. I did it yesterday evening."

Wren blew out a long breath. Then he rolled up his sleeves, which Tam had learned meant a decision had been made. "Right. I'm a stonemason, lad. I've set every foundation stone in this valley worth setting. I know where they sleep." He held out one huge hand, palm up—an offer, or an oath, or both. "You speak. I'll show you the stones. We'll do it proper, like a building. Foundations first."

Tam put his hand in Wren's. It swallowed his whole one, warm and rough as bark.

"Foundations first," he agreed.

* * *

They worked for six days.

They started, at Wren's insistence, with the Hollow Gate itself—not the great sealed door, which slept now and wanted sleeping, but the gatehouse stones, the arch, the threshold where Freya had stood her rounds for twenty years. Tam spoke her name into each one, and her deeds, and Wren laid his own palms beside the boy's smaller ones, and where Wren touched, the words seemed to sink deeper, as if the mason's lifetime of honest work with stone had earned him some standing in their silent parliament.

From the gate they worked outward, the way ripples work. The boundary stones along the shepherd's track. The bridge footings over the cold river. The lintels of houses—Wren knew which lintel was under which roof, knew the quarries each block had come from, knew the masons' marks cut into hidden faces. At each one, Tam gave the stone the same offering: *Freya Hartwell. Keeper of the Hollow Gate. She kept your round. She sheltered the last Vessian. She broke a false oath so a true one could live.*

And the valley began, slowly, to remember.

It was not dramatic. That surprised him at first, and then it didn't. Everything true in the valley happened slowly—that was the whole point of the place, the whole poisoned gift of it. Things grew slowly. Snows melted slowly. Grudges were held slowly, over generations, in silence. So the remembering came the same way.

On the third day, the reeve paused outside the cottage on his morning round, and stood looking at it for a long time, and finally knocked on the door of a house he did not know he knew, and stood bewildered on the step when no one answered, saying to Wren, "I don't know why I'm here. I feel like I'm late for something."

On the fourth day, Mrs. Aldery baked honey rolls without deciding to, and set one aside on a saucer, wrapped in cloth, and couldn't have told anyone why, and put it in her window anyway.

On the fifth day, children playing in the square began, unprompted, to skip-count in a rhythm none of them recognized, and the rhythm was the cadence of the keeper's round, the pace of Freya's footsteps on her nightly walk, carried in the stones under their feet and rising up through their games like water finding a level.

And on the sixth day, Tam stood at the thirteenth stone of the churchyard wall—the last one, Wren said, the keystone of the whole quiet architecture they'd built—and gave it her name one final time, and felt the valley take a breath.

Not a wind. A breath. The whole basin of it, hills and river and field and town, inhaling the way a sleeper inhales when a dream turns sweet, and Tam felt it pass through the soles of his feet and up through his half-Vessian bones, and he knew, with the certainty of the gate-tongue, that it was done. Not finished—remembering was never finished, it was a duty like any other, a round to be walked—but *done*, seeded, rooted, growing now on its own.

* * *

She came down from the far side of the hills at dusk, walking the old drover's road, unnamed and free, with Wren's testimony folded in her pack and the look of a woman returning to a house she expects to find empty.

Tam saw her from the square, where he'd been waiting every evening for six days, and his heart slammed so hard against his ribs he thought the nearest stone must feel it.

She stopped at the edge of the village. He watched her take it in—the lamplight, the smell of bread, the ordinary evening—and watched her brace herself to walk through it unseen, a ghost in her own home. Watched her square her shoulders and start forward anyway, because that was who she was; she would have walked through her own erasure with her chin up and her boots muddy.

She passed the well. Passed the bakery, where Mrs. Aldery was setting out the morning's last rolls. Passed the churchyard wall.

And at the churchyard wall she stopped.

Tam couldn't see her face from the square. He only saw her stand there, very still, one hand rising slowly to rest on the thirteenth stone, the way you touch a door you're afraid has locked behind you. He saw her shoulders change. Saw her head bow, just slightly, the way it bowed when she read something true and unbearable.

Then she lifted her head, and turned, and found him across the distance, across the lamplight and the evening and the whole width of the forgetting—and said his name.

"TAM."

Not wondering. Not questioning. Certain, the way dawn is certain, and the sound of it went through the square like a bell through water, and Mrs. Aldery dropped a roll, and the reeve came out of his office, and the old woman at the well turned with her bucket forgotten in her hands, and one by one, like lamps being lit down a long street, faces turned toward the woman at the wall and *knew her*.

"Freya?" Mrs. Aldery's voice, cracked wide open. "Freya Hartwell—child, where have you *been*—"

And the street filled, and the names came back into mouths the way birds come back to a roof at morning, and Tam stood in the middle of it with tears running freely down his face and made no move to wipe them, because his mother had hated a sulky face at breakfast but she had never once said a word against weeping for joy.

Freya crossed the square to him through all of it, through the reaching hands and the voices, and knelt the way she had knelt in the rain the first day, to look him in the eye.

"You spoke my name into the stones," she said. Her voice was rough as quarry rock.

"Thirteen stones," Tam said. "Wren counted. He says thirteen is structurally significant."

Behind her, Wren wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand and pretended he hadn't.

"It shouldn't have worked," Freya said. "The forgetting took me whole. It doesn't give things back."

"I know." Tam shrugged, and felt the gate-tongue settle in his chest, warm, his now, his mother's language and his father's country and his own to spend. "But I'm not from here all the way. And I'm not from there all the way either. I'm from *between*. And between is where doors are."

Freya laughed—a wet, broken, wonderful sound—and pulled him into her arms, and above them the first stars came out over the Hollow Gate, quiet in its valley, keeping nothing but the memory now.

They stood there a long while, the whole valley around them, saying her name over and over as if to make up for lost time—as if they could never say it enough again.

And it was only later, much later, when the crowd had thinned and the lamps burned low, that the reeve came to Freya's elbow, hat in his hands, wearing the expression of a man carrying news heavier than he'd like.

"Keeper," he said—the word coming back to his tongue like a returned exile—"there's something you should know. Riders came through this afternoon, while you were—while none of us remembered you. Grey livery. They asked questions about the gate." He hesitated. "They left a letter. They said... they said the Compact considers the matter of Vessia *unfinished*."

Freya's arm tightened around Tam's shoulders.

And in the silence that followed, every stone in the valley seemed to lean in and listen.
