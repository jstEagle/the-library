## 36. Reckoning in the Valley

The valley had not forgotten her. That was the first thing Freya understood, standing at the top of the keeper's road with the morning mist still pooled in the hollows below, and it was the last thing she had expected.

She had braced herself for blankness—for the polite, puzzled faces of strangers, for Wren's grief made real in the empty cottage and the unanswered questions. She had rehearsed what she would say to people who no longer knew her name: *I am the keeper. I was your keeper. I gave up being remembered so that a dead country could be remembered instead.* She had carried those words across the border of Vessia like stones in her pockets, heavy and necessary, and now they were useless, because Tam had spoken her name into the valley's stones and the valley had listened, slowly, the way stone listens to water—imperceptibly, and then all at once.

Wren had told her on the far side of the gate, before they crossed back: *It isn't whole yet. Some remember you as a shape. Some remember you as a story their grandmother told. Old Marta asked me last week who had been tending the gate all these years, and when I said your name she wept without knowing why.* And Tam—Tam had spent three evenings walking the valley's edge with his palms flat against fence posts and door lintels and the milestone at the crossroads, saying *Freya Hartwell* into each one, patient as a boy planting seeds he might never see flower.

So the faces that turned toward her as she came down into the village were not blank. They were worse, in a way. They were ashamed.

She saw it in the way people looked away from her, then looked back. In the way the baker's wife set down her basket and pressed her hand to her mouth. In the way old Marta, wrapped in grey wool against the autumn chill, stood at the edge of the square and did not come forward, because coming forward would have meant admitting how long she had known—or half-known—and how long she had chosen not to ask.

Freya had imagined this homecoming a hundred times on the road, and in none of those imaginings had she been the one everyone was afraid of.

*They aren't afraid of you,* Wren had said. *They're afraid of what you know.*

And she did know. That was the whole of it. She knew what was written in the archive she had carried out of a burning country, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant. She knew what Wren had testified—that the elders had known, generation after generation, that the valley sat on ground bought with someone else's erasure; that the keepers' oath was not a ward but a lid; that every family in this valley lived in a house built on the foundation of a country that officially had never existed. She knew, and soon they would too, because there was no version of justice that let her carry it alone.

The elders had agreed to meet her in the square. It had taken Wren three days to arrange, and even then only because Wren had threatened, gently and in writing, to read the archive aloud themselves if the elders refused. Freya had been proud of them for that—the stonemason who had smuggled a memory out through a half-open gate, who had spent years chiseling other people's names into other people's graves and had decided, somewhere along the way, that silence was heavier than any stone they'd ever cut.

Now Wren stood beside her at the mouth of the square, arms crossed, jaw set, watching the crowd gather with the expression of someone counting load-bearing walls.

"They came," Wren said quietly. "All of them. I didn't think they would."

"People come to fires," Freya said. "Even when they're afraid of burning."

"Is that what this is?"

"It's whatever they decide it is. I'm only bringing the record."

Tam was waiting near the well, and when he saw her he crossed the square at a run and took her hand—not clinging, the way he had in Vessia, but holding it the way a sailor holds a rail. He had grown somehow in the weeks since the gate sealed itself, or perhaps he had simply stopped shrinking. He wore a coat the valley women had made him, and around his neck, on a cord, hung the single page he had refused to surrender: the record of his mother's name, Aldissa Vane, citizen of Vessia, bell-ringer's daughter, remembered.

"Are they going to shout?" he asked.

"No."

"The queen said they would. Before—" He stopped. He still spoke of Ilsevan carefully, the way one speaks of a storm that has passed through. "Before she faded, she said the valley people would never listen. She said they'd earned their forgetting."

"The queen was nearly right about everything," Freya said, "and entirely wrong about that. Look at them. They came. Nobody made them."

He looked. The square was fuller than market day—farmers with their hats off, the miller's family, the schoolteacher with her ledger clutched like a shield, children perched on the well's rim because children always found the highest ground. And at the front, arranged in their usual semicircle of chairs that had not been moved in anyone's living memory, the elders: seven of them, ancient and stiff-collared, Elder Corvane among them with his hands folded and his face arranged into something between dignity and dread.

Corvane had been the one, Wren told her, who kept the oldest papers. Who had read the founding letters as a young man and locked them away again and told no one, because his own grandfather had signed one of them. Freya caught his eye across the square, and he did not look away, and that, more than anything, told her he had already confessed himself—in the only place he could, which was the privacy of his own skull, for forty years.

She climbed the steps of the meeting hall, unfolded the small table Wren had carried out for her, and laid the archive upon it.

* * *

"I am not here to accuse anyone," she began, and her voice carried further than she expected, the way voices do in cold air over silent crowds. "I want to say that first, before anything else, because everything after it will sound like accusation, and I need you to hear the difference."

She let the silence sit.

"In Vessia there is an archive. There *was*. The Compact burned most of it—every ledger of names, every record of who the Vessians were. What I have here is what a child and a bell-keeper and I pulled from the flames. It is the true census of a country of some four hundred thousand souls. Every name. Every trade. Every street." Her hand rested on the oilcloth. "And beneath the names, in the founding ledgers, is the reason the country needed a census at all. Because a country that is erased from maps will eventually be erased from memory, and then from the world, unless someone writes it down."

An elder—Hestia, the weaver, whose hands had never once been still in Freya's memory—spoke without raising her hand. "We know why you went, girl. We've had Wren's account. Say the rest."

"Yes," Freya said. "Say the rest. That's exactly what I'm going to do."

She opened the founding ledger to the page she had read forty times and would read forty more, and she read it aloud.

She read the treaty of the Hollow Valley: the agreement signed by the nine founders of her order and the envoys of three neighboring kingdoms, trading away the borders of Vessia in exchange for a sanctuary—a valley where magic could not enter, where the wars of the wider world could not follow, where their descendants could live in peace. She read the price, itemized in the founders' own careful hand: *the northern passes, the river provinces, the capital and its bells, and the names thereof, surrendered into oblivion, that no claim may ever be raised nor grievance inherited.*

She read the oath they swore afterward, the one she had recited every morning of her life without understanding it—the words that had seemed, in the gate's old tongue, like a ward against magic. *We seal the door. We keep the quiet. What lies beyond does not lie at all.* Not a ward. A sentence. A spell of unbeing, renewed by every keeper who spoke it faithfully, sustained by every valley family who taught their children that the gate was sacred and its purpose unquestionable.

When she finished, no one shouted. That was the thing she would remember afterward, telling Tam about it years later: no one shouted. The square held a silence so complete she could hear the well rope creaking in the wind, and somewhere a dog, confused by the stillness, barked twice and thought better of it.

Elder Hestia broke it. "My grandmother wove the grey cloth," she said, in a voice like dry flax. "The livery cloth. For the surveyors. We thought—we were told—it was for mourning."

"It was for mourning," Freya said. "Just not ours."

A sound went through the crowd then, not a gasp, something lower and worse—the sound of a hundred private reckonings happening at once, of grandmothers' stories rearranging themselves, of every odd rule and strange prohibition suddenly finding its explanation. The festival they weren't allowed to hold near the gate. The questions children were told not to ask. The way the elders always chose the surveyors' lodgings, always fed them best, always sent them off with gifts, as though appeasing something.

Old Marta rose from the crowd. She was ninety-one and walked with two sticks, and it took her a long time to turn and face the others, and when she finally spoke her voice was thin but it reached every corner.

"I knew," she said. "Not all of it. But I knew there was something under our peace, the way you know there's a body under a floor. My mother told me once, when I was young enough to be told such things and old enough to forget them on purpose: *the valley was bought, Marta, and we are what was bought with it.* And I forgot her on purpose, because the peace was good, and my children were warm, and what would knowing have cost me except everything?" She looked at Freya. "That's the confession of the whole valley, keeper. Write it down if you're writing things down. We didn't sell anyone. But we ate at the table, and we stopped asking where the food came from."

No one contradicted her. Several people wept openly. Elder Corvane stood, slowly, and Freya watched him take the longest walk of his life—from his chair to the table, ten paces, each one costing him visibly—and lay a leather folder beside the archive.

"The founding letters," he said. "The originals. My grandfather's signature is on the third. I have known since I was nineteen years old." His voice failed and recovered. "I told myself I was protecting the valley. I told myself the truth would break us. I find, this morning, that the truth has broken us anyway, and the only thing my silence protected was me."

He turned to the crowd, and Freya stepped back, because this part was not hers.

"I resign," Corvane said. "And I submit myself to whatever the valley decides, knowing that what I hope it decides is nothing, and knowing that hoping so is the same appetite that kept me silent." 

* * *

What the valley decided took three days, and Freya insisted on sitting outside the deliberations, because she had learned in Vessia that the people who make the judgment should not be the people who arrange the room.

They gathered in the square on the third evening, and the schoolteacher read the resolution aloud, because the schoolteacher had insisted that whatever was done should be done in front of the children, so that the children would learn what a reckoning looked like when it was done properly.

There would be no trials. Corvane's resignation was accepted, and he would spend his remaining years copying the archive—every name, in fair hand, onto good paper—one copy for the valley's hall, one for every household that wanted it, until the names of Vessia were as ordinary in valley mouths as the names of neighbors. The elders' hidden papers would go into an open case in the meeting hall, unlocked, dusted weekly, so that no document in the valley would ever again be secret. The festival that had been forbidden near the gate would be held there—held *at* the gate—every year, in honor of the country next door that no longer existed anywhere except in memory and in the mouths of its speakers.

And the oath would be rewritten. That task fell to her, the schoolteacher read, if she would take it: the keeper's round would continue, but the words would change, and the words would be voted on in open assembly, and no child would ever again recite a promise they couldn't understand.

When the reading ended, the schoolteacher folded her paper and looked at Freya directly. "The valley asks one thing more," she said. "Not of the keeper. Of Freya Hartwell. They ask whether she can forgive them. And they ask it knowing they have no right to ask, and knowing that forgiveness is not owed, and asking anyway, because the alternative is to live unforgiven, and they have done enough of that."

Freya stood in the silence with four hundred thousand names in a bundle against her chest and one boy's hand in hers, and she thought about Queen Ilsevan, who had been offered every form of justice except the kind that costs the injured nothing, and who had died choosing the expensive kind because no one had ever shown her the cheap one.

"There's nothing to forgive," she said finally. "That's not kindness. It's arithmetic. You didn't do it. You inherited a house with a body in the cellar, and you didn't dig, and I understand why, because I recited the oath every morning for twenty years and never once asked what I was sealing. If we start forgiving each other for that, we'll be at it until we die." She lifted the archive slightly. "But forgetting—forgetting we can stop. Starting tonight. Corvane, bring your pen. We begin with the first name in the ledger, and we don't stop until the last."

* * *

They copied names until midnight in the meeting hall, and the valley came in shifts, as though summoned by something older than decision—farmers still in their field boots, the baker's wife with flour to her elbows, children too excited to sleep, each taking a page and a pen and sounding out the strange beautiful syllables while Tam moved among them, correcting gently, teaching the music of his mother's tongue one name at a time.

Freya stood at the window near dawn and watched the light come up over the Hollow Gate, quiet now, its seals dim, its door closed not as a prison but as a headstone—and beside it, already, Wren and two apprentices setting the first stones of the memorial arch where the forbidden festival would stand.

Tam appeared at her elbow, sleepy-eyed, his voice rough from a night of names.

"You're staying, then," he said. Not a question. "You're going to be the keeper again."

"I'm going to be something again," Freya said. "Whether it's keeper depends on what the valley votes the word means."

"And the new oath? Have you thought what you'll carve?"

"I have one line so far." She looked at the gate, at the pale stone going gold with morning. "*Never again a door that closes on someone else's country.*"

Tam considered this with the gravity he brought to everything now, this child who had buried a nation and helped raise a valley. "It needs a second line," he said. "Something about opening."

Freya smiled, and was about to answer—when the knock came at the meeting hall door, soft and deliberate, and Wren opened it onto a traveler standing in the grey dawn: a woman, mud to the knees, road-worn past exhaustion, holding out a letter sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"From the Border Compact," the woman said. "For the keeper of the Hollow Gate. They know what she did. And they're coming to discuss it."
