## 37. The New Keeper

The summons came the way all important things came in Hart's Hollow: quietly, and by foot.

Freya saw them coming from the cottage window—seven figures walking the lane from the village square, moving at the unhurried pace of people who had rehearsed this on the way and were now afraid they'd forgotten their lines. Wren led them, of course. Behind the stonemason walked Elder Maren, who had not left her chair in two years except for funerals; the cooper's widow, Ansel; old Tobbe with his bad hip; three others whose faces Freya knew better than she had ever let herself admit, back when knowing them had felt like a small betrayal of her solitude.

She put the kettle on before they knocked. Whatever this was, it would go easier with tea.

They filled her kitchen the way a stone fills a streambed—all the water of their purpose forced to one side, pooling, waiting. Maren took the chair by the fire because her age entitled her to it and no one would dream of disputing. The rest stood or perched awkwardly on stools built for one keeper who had never expected company.

"You've read the record," Maren began, and stopped, and started again. "All of it. In the square."

"I read it," Freya said. "I wrote most of it."

"Aye." The old woman's hands were folded on her stick like a bird settling its wings. "And you stood there while it was read, and you didn't flinch when your own order's name was dragged through the mud of it. When *your* name was."

"It should have been dragged further. I kept the seals for eleven years without once asking what was behind them."

"And we let you," Tobbe said simply. "We let you, and our grandparents let theirs, and somewhere back along the line somebody decided that a quiet valley was worth more than an honest question." He looked around at the others, then back at her. "That's what we've come about. The question. And what to do with it now that we've finally asked it."

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Freya lifted it off before it could, poured, and set seven cups on the table with the economy of long habit. Her hands did not shake. She noticed that they didn't, and was quietly proud of it.

"Say it plainly," she said. "I've had enough of plain things arriving dressed in ceremony."

Wren laughed—a short, rough sound—and pulled out the stool nearest the stove. "Then plainly. The elders met last night. All night. We're asking you to be the keeper again."

The word landed in Freya's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash.

"Not like before," Wren went on quickly, reading her face the way he read grain in timber. "Not the seals. Not the recitation. Not—" he gestured vaguely at the ceiling, at the hills beyond it, at eleven years of rounds and oaths and solitary suppers "—*that*. The gate doesn't need guarding anymore. It sealed itself, quiet as a grave after rain. There's nothing behind it now but stone and memory, and memory doesn't need a warden with keys. It needs someone who'll tend it."

"Tend it," Freya repeated.

"Maren says it better. She's been practicing."

Every eye turned to the eldest among them. Maren unfolded her hands from her stick with visible effort, as though the words she'd carried up the hill weighed more than her body did.

"When I was a girl," the old woman said, "the gate was a thing we didn't look at. You'd walk the ridge road and keep your eyes on the far hills, same as everyone, because looking felt like knocking. My mother told me the Hollow Gate was where the valley kept its shame, and shame is best left in a locked room. That's what we were taught. All of us. A whole valley taught to walk past a door with its eyes down." She paused. "A country behind that door, Freya. A whole living country, and we were taught to look away from it. And we did. For six generations, we did."

No one spoke. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, Tam's voice rose and fell in the yard—he was talking to the goats again, in both his languages, the Vessian words rolling out smooth as river stones while the Common tongue stumbled after. Freya found herself listening to it the way you listen to rain: without effort, with gratitude.

"What we're asking," Maren said, "is that the gate become something else. Not a lock. Not a wall. A memorial. A place where the names live—the ones you spoke, the ones the boy carries, the ones we burned and buried and pretended never existed. We want people to come to it. We want children to come to it. We want them to *look*, Freya. Eyes open, heads up. And we want someone standing there who understands why that matters. Someone who looked away longest, and came back."

"That's a cruel way to put it," Ansel murmured.

"It's an honest way," Maren said. "She'll not thank us for dressing it softer than it is."

Freya sat with her untouched tea and let the well inside her finish filling. Eleven years she had kept this duty alone, reciting an oath in a dead ritual tongue whose meaning her own founders had deliberately obscured—she understood that now, understood that the obscurity *was* the point, that an oath you couldn't parse was an oath you couldn't argue with. They had built a prison out of language and called it holiness. And she had climbed its walls every morning of her adult life and called it service.

"They asked me this morning too," she said at last. "The gate and I had a conversation about it."

Tobbe blinked. "The gate talked to you?"

"The gate does nothing but talk, if you've learned its grammar. Mostly it says *stone*. Today it said something more like *yes, if*." She smiled faintly at their faces. "It's a memorial already, whether we make it official or not. The names are carved into it—every one I spoke during the rite, and every one since. It grew them overnight, like frost ferns. Have any of you been up to look?"

Silence. She hadn't expected otherwise. Six generations of looking away did not unlearn themselves in a season; it would take years, maybe a lifetime, maybe several. That was precisely why they needed a keeper—not to bar the door, but to stand beside it and say *look*.

"I'll accept," Freya said, and watched relief move through the room like wind through wheat. "On my terms."

"Name them," Maren said at once, and Freya loved her a little for the speed of it.

"First: no oath in the old tongue. Not one word of it. That language was built to hide meaning, and I won't preside over another hidden thing. If I swear anything, I swear it in words a child can understand."

"Done," said Wren.

"Second: the round changes. I'll still walk it—there's value in a person showing up to a place regularly, in weather fair and foul. But I'm not checking seals. There are no seals. I'm checking the names. Reading them. Saying them aloud so they stay spoken. If a traveler comes to the gate asking questions, I answer them. All of them. Even the ugly ones. *Especially* the ugly ones."

Ansel shifted. "Some of those answers will shame families in this valley. Living families."

"Yes," Freya said. "Third term: nobody gets to decide which parts of the truth get tended and which get pruned. The archive stays public. The record stays public. If a descendant of Aldous Hartwell—that would be me, incidentally—has to hear what Aldous sold and for how much, then so does everyone. Justice without cruelty, but also justice without mercy for the comfortable. Those aren't opposites. I learned that from a queen."

Maren closed her eyes briefly. "Ilsevan."

"She was almost right, in the end. That's the most dangerous thing a person can be." Freya turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Fourth, and last. I don't do this alone. The keeper's cottage held one person for eleven years and it nearly hollowed me out—I know the shape of that hollowness from the inside. Tam lives here. Tam stays here. He's not my ward or my apprentice or my burden. He's—" she searched for the word and found it had been sitting there for months, patient as a planted seed "—he's my family. Any arrangement the valley makes with me is made with him. He'll learn the gate. He may keep it after me, or he may not; that will be his choice, made freely, when he's grown. But he learns it beside me, not beneath me."

"He's eight," Tobbe said gently.

"He's the last native speaker of a murdered country and the first citizen of whatever comes next. He's carried more weight than any of us ever will. He can carry a title too, when he wants it—or refuse it, which would teach us all something." She looked around the table. "Those are my terms. All of them, or none."

Wren stuck out a hand across the table, calloused palm up, grinning like a man who'd bet on the right horse. "All of them. Done and done."

* * *

They swore it at the gate three days later, under a sky rinsed clean by overnight rain.

The whole valley came, or near enough—two hundred souls gathered on the ridge road where the ground flattened before the Hollow Gate, and Freya stood before them feeling the strangeness of being *seen* by all of them at once. For eleven years she had been a fixture, like the milestone or the lightning-split oak: present, useful, unexamined. Now two hundred pairs of eyes rested on her with attention, and attention, she was learning, was the rarest currency in any world.

Tam stood at her right hand, scrubbed and solemn in a shirt Wren's wife had sewn him, grey thread worked at the collar in a pattern Freya recognized—the interlocking rings of Vessia, copied from the gate itself. He had insisted on the pattern. He had insisted on being here at all, in fact, and when the elders had suggested that perhaps the ceremony was an affair for adults, he had looked at them with the flat, ancient patience he sometimes wore like borrowed clothing and said, "It's my country's door. I'll be there." No one had argued.

The old oath-book lay on a table between them, its vellum stiff with two centuries of handling. Freya had considered burning it. She had stood in the cottage with it over the cold stove one night, weighing the satisfaction against the loss, and in the end she had set it back on the shelf—because the record they'd read in the square included the oath-book's crimes, and a people trying to remember must keep even the documents that shamed them. Especially those. Instead she had written across its first page, in ink that could not be mistaken for anything but deliberate:

**Superseded. See below.**

And below, on fresh paper stitched into the binding, in her own plain hand, the new oath. She read it aloud now, her voice carrying down the ridge road in the clear post-rain air, and she had chosen every word the way a mason chooses stone—for fit, for weight, for endurance.

*"I will remember the names, and speak them, so that no people is erased while I draw breath.*

*I will shelter whoever knocks, and ask their story before I ask their papers.*

*I will keep this gate open to truth and shut only to cruelty.*

*I will never trade a people for a peace, a child for a kingdom, or the living for the past.*

*I am the keeper of the Hollow Gate, and the door I keep is memory itself."*

No archaisms. No inversions. No words chosen to obscure. When she finished, the silence that followed had a different texture from the silences of the old ceremonies—less like held breath, more like ground settling after a felled tree.

Then Tam stepped forward, as they had agreed he might, and spoke in Vessia's tongue—four lines, low and clear, the sounds rising off the stones of the gate like heat off summer rock. Freya understood enough now to catch the shape of it: *a door remembers what opens it; a name remembers who speaks it; I am the last and the first; let the door stay kind.* The gate answered. There was no other word for it. Along its face, the carved names brightened by a degree, the way coals answer breath, and a murmur ran through the crowd—fear in some throats, wonder in most, and in a few, Freya suspected, the beginning of something like devotion, which she would have to watch carefully. Devotion had built the prison. She intended to build a garden, and gardens required weeding.

Elder Maren came forward last, leaning on Tobbe's arm, and pressed into Freya's hands not a keyring—the old keys had been melted down for the memorial bell, all but one, which hung now above the gate as a relic of what doors used to be—but a simple iron stylus and a block of the pale soapstone the valley quarried. "For the additions," Maren said. "When new names need keeping. There will always be new names."

"There will," Freya agreed, and took them, and felt the weight of a duty remade settle onto her shoulders—not lighter than the old one, but shaped differently, the way a yoke shaped for the animal actually pulling it sits easier than one inherited from a ghost.

Afterward there was bread and cider on the ridge, and children daring each other to touch the gate's carved letters, and old Tobbe telling anyone who'd listen about the day the sky through the gate had been the wrong color, improving the tale with each retelling. Freya stood a little apart, watching, and Wren drifted over to stand beside her, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing for a long while.

"You look different," he said finally. "Than you did a year ago. Looser. Like a wall that's decided to be a bridge."

"Don't ruin it with flattery."

"That wasn't flattery, that was masonry talk. Highest compliment I've got." He chewed a stem of grass. "You'll do it, then. The long work. Names every morning, rain or shine, for the rest of your life."

"For the rest of my life," she said, and heard, to her own surprise, no dread in it at all. "Someone has to show up to a place regularly. It turns out that's half of everything worth doing."

Down the slope, Tam was teaching two of the cooper's daughters to count in Vessian—one, two, three, their small voices mangling the vowels joyfully—and Freya watched him correct them with a gravity that made her chest ache. Tomorrow he would begin his lessons at the gate in earnest. Tomorrow the first travelers from beyond the valley would arrive, drawn by rumors of a door that remembered. Tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow—the days stacking up like cut stone, ready for building.

But tonight, she thought, watching the light go gold and then rose over the quiet gate, tonight there was only this: a valley learning to look, a door learning kindness, a boy counting to ten in a language the world had tried to kill, getting it wrong, laughing, starting over.

From her pocket, Freya drew the soapstone and the stylus, and turned them over in her fingers, and smiled.

There would always be new names.
