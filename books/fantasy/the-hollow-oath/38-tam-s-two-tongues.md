## 38. Tam's Two Tongues

The schoolhouse had been a granary until two winters ago, and it still smelled faintly of rye and dust when the morning sun came through the south windows. Tam liked that about it. He liked that the valley did not build things new when old things could be made to hold something else — that doors became tables and barrels became benches and a boy from a country that no longer existed could become, without anyone making a fuss about it, a teacher.

There were nine of them today. Nine children sitting on benches too tall for their legs, swinging their heels against the wood in a rhythm none of them knew they were keeping. In the back, as always, sat Wren, arms crossed over a broad stonemason's apron, pretending to whittle a piece of scrap oak and fooling no one. Wren never pretended well. It was one of the things Tam loved most about them.

"Say it again," said Mira, who was seven and missing a front tooth and had appointed herself, sometime in the autumn, Tam's fiercest defender and most demanding student. "The one for snow."

"Snegh," Tam said. The word came out of him soft and round, the way his mother used to say it when she pressed her palm to the frosted window of their flat above the bakery. *Snegh.* He could still feel her hand, warm on top of his head, guiding him toward the glass. "It's not like your word for snow. Yours is a thing that falls out of the sky. Ours is — " He stopped. Thought. Held up both hands, palms down, and let them drift slowly downward, wiggling his fingers. "Yours falls. Ours *settles*. Like it's coming home."

The children tried it. *Snegh* went around the room nine times, nine small mouths shaping a sound their grandparents' grandparents had helped bury, and Tam stood at the front with chalk dust on his sleeves and felt something in his chest that he had no Vessian word for either, because Vessia had been erased so thoroughly that even its language had gaps where joy had once lived unremarked.

"That's not how you say it," muttered Dole, who was eleven and skeptical of everything, including weather. "That's just noises."

"It's not noises." Mira rounded on him with the full authority of a missing tooth. "It's a *language*. Freya says languages are people wearing sound instead of skin."

Tam laughed before he could stop himself. That wasn't quite what Freya had said. What Freya had actually said, late one night at the kitchen table with the archive ledger open between them, was: *A language is a country you can carry in your mouth, Tam. They took Vessia off the maps, but they can't take it off your tongue unless you let them.* He had repeated it to Mira the next day in the simplified way children repeat things, which is to say he had improved it.

"Dole," Tam said, "come here. Give me your hand."

Dole came, scowling, suspicious, the way boys are when they suspect they're about to be taught something. Tam put Dole's hand flat against his own throat.

"Now say *snegh* the way I said it. Feel that?"

Dole said it wrong first — all hard edges, English vowels jammed into Vessian clothes — and then, because he was stubborn rather than stupid, he said it again, slower, and Tam watched his eyebrows go up as his fingers found the hum of it, the deep rolling place at the back of the throat where the old words lived.

"Oh," Dole said. Just that. But his scowl was gone.

"That's the difference," Tam told the whole room. "Your language sits in the front of your mouth. Ours sits *here*—" he touched his throat again, "—down where the voice comes from. My mother said we speak from the chest because Vessians were farmers and bell-ringers before they were anything else. Words you have to feel in your bones don't get blown away by wind."

He didn't say: *and they didn't get blown away by treaties either, though everyone tried.*

He didn't need to. Everyone in this valley knew the story now. It hung in the square where the elders had read the record aloud, all of it, every name of every founder who had sold a kingdom for a quiet valley. It was carved now, plain and unhurried, into the Hollow Gate itself in Freya's own handwriting. The children had grown up in the last year learning it the way they learned the names of trees — not as a wound but as a fact of the landscape, something true that they walked past on their way to somewhere else.

Tam turned back to the board, where he had written the day's six words in careful chalk letters. Not the gate's formal script — the living hand, the quick version, the one mothers used for shopping lists and fathers for marking tool handles. His mother's hand.

*Snegh. Dom. Mlko. Zvon. Ime. Matka.*

Snow. House. Milk. Bell. Name. Mother.

He had chosen them deliberately, though he'd told no one that. Six words that built a life if you laid them end to end. A house with snow settling on its roof, milk on the table, a bell in the tower, a name to be called by, a mother calling it.

"Today," he announced, "we're going to do names."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet around Tam when the subject turns to the archive. Even the heels stopped swinging.

Every child in the valley knew about the ledger. It lived now in a cedar chest behind the bell tower, under lock, tended by Ondrey, whose hands had finally stopped shaking in the spring. Every Vessian citizen's name was written there — forty thousand and some — and since the night Freya had spoken the first hundred aloud while the Compact's sealing magic burned across her shoulders, the work of speaking the rest had become the valley's shared labor, a little at a time, like carrying water. You did not read names quickly. You read them the way they deserved: each one given its full weight, its full sound, its full right to exist.

But the children's names — those were different. Those weren't in any ledger.

"Names in Vessian aren't just sounds," Tam said. He picked up the chalk. "They're little sentences. Every name means something. Mine — Tamvel — means 'he who stayed by the door.' Because my father's family were gate-wardens, back when Vessia had gates worth warding." He wrote it out. "So when someone says my name, they're saying a whole small story about me. Who wants to know what theirs would be?"

Nine hands shot up, even Dole's, though Dole pretended afterward that he had only raised his to scratch his ear.

They spent the morning on it. Tam worked through the children one by one, sounding out meanings, building names from roots the way his mother had taught him roots fit together. Mira became *Miravel*, "the bright argument," and she declared it the best thing anyone had ever given her and threatened to bite anyone who shortened it. The miller's daughter became *Lumessa*, "still water holding light." Dole fought hardest and surrendered fastest: *Dolesk*, "the stone that stays," and when Tam explained it, the boy went red to his ears and looked at Wren in the back row, and Wren gave him the smallest nod in the history of nodding, and that was that.

By midday the granary-schoolhouse held nine new Vessian names, spoken aloud, written in chalk, owned outright by children who would carry them whether or not any border ever acknowledged them again.

That was the point. Tam understood it better than most adults gave him credit for. The queen — Ilsevan, whose fading face he sometimes still saw in dreams, not as a warning anymore but almost as a blessing — had wanted a nation restored, borders drawn, debts collected. What she had gotten instead, because Freya had refused her and then saved her anyway, was something stranger and smaller and, Tam thought, harder to kill: a language loose in the world, attached to no flag, carried in mouths that had never seen Vessia and never would. You cannot erase a language that lives in children. The Compact's treaty-magic had no clause for it. Tam had checked. He'd asked Freya to check twice.

Languages didn't need countries. Countries needed languages, and when they lost theirs, they died quietly in their sleep. But a language in a child's mouth was a fire in wet grass — it moved, it spread, it came up where nobody planned for it.

* * *

In the afternoon, the bell tower.

Ondrey met them at the door with the great iron key in both hands, the way he always did, though the lock had been rusted open since winter and the key was ceremony more than security. Some things should be done properly or not at all; that was Ondrey's whole philosophy, and the valley was gentler for it.

"You're certain?" Ondrey asked. For the hundredth time. He asked every time, and every time Tam answered the same, and every time Ondrey nodded as though hearing it fresh.

"I'm certain."

"Because a name spoken in a tower carries differently than a name spoken on the ground. That's not magic. That's acoustics and intention. But it matters."

"I know, Uncle Ondrey."

Ondrey's eyes crinkled. He was the only person in the valley Tam called uncle, and he had never once pointed out that they shared no blood, no country, and barely a generation of acquaintance. Some kinships you build. Tam's mother would have approved of Ondrey. She had approved of anyone who rang bells with their whole body.

Inside, the tower smelled of rope and pigeon and cold stone. The children filed up the spiral stair behind Tam, whispering, because towers demanded whispers — that was another law nobody had written down but everybody kept. Through the narrow windows the valley showed itself in slivers: rooftops going gold with late light, the terraced fields, the dark line of the ridge, and far below at the valley's mouth the Hollow Gate, small from this height, quiet, carved over with its new oath that anyone could read and no one needed to fear.

Freya was down there. Tam could feel it, the way he'd been able to feel her presence for months now — not magic, nothing so grand, just the orientation of his heart, the way a compass needle finds north without knowing why. She was walking the keeper's round, probably. Checking seals that no longer sealed anything dangerous, tending a door that had become a memorial, standing her watch because she had chosen it, freely, in plain words she herself had written. Some evenings Tam went down and stood the last stretch of the round with her. Neither of them talked much on those walks. Talking wasn't the point.

"Ready?" he asked the children.

They gathered at the belfry rail, nine small faces pink from the climb, breath steaming in the cooling air. Below them the square lay empty except for a dog and the evening market stalls being folded away.

"The ledger has everyone who ever belonged to Vessia," Tam said. "Everyone the world forgot. And every night, someone reads names from it so they stay remembered. But there's one name that isn't in the ledger." His throat tightened, and he let it. He was eight years old when he fell through the light; he was nearly ten now, and he had learned that grief didn't get smaller, it just got a vocabulary. "Because the ledger only holds citizens, and my mother wasn't a citizen of anything by the end. She was just my mother. So tonight, we say her name ourselves. We give her a country of nine voices and one bell."

He looked at the children. They looked back, solemn, ready, their new names tucked inside them like stones in pockets.

"Mira. You'll ring. Slow, like a heart that's resting. Everyone else — after the third toll, we speak together. Ready?"

Mira took the rope in both fists. She was strong for seven; she hauled hay for the Hedleys in exchange for first pick of the apples. She waited for Tam's nod, and then she pulled.

The bell spoke.

Its voice rolled out over the valley the way *snegh* settled — not falling, coming home — and Tam closed his eyes and let the sound move through his chest, through the old Vessian place where the language lived, and he opened his mouth.

"*Sera Tamvelin,*" he said, and the children said it with him, nine voices plus one, clumsy and earnest and true. "*Sera Tamvelin. Sera Tamvelin.*"

Her name. Her real name, the one meaning "she who rises early to bake bread for whoever needs it," the one he had not spoken aloud since the fall through the light, because for a long time speaking it had felt like tearing something. It did not feel like tearing now. It felt like setting a lamp in a window.

Three times they said it, timed to the three tolls, and the bell carried each one down into the square, over the rooftops, out along the fields to where Freya stood at the gate. Tam knew the exact moment it reached her, though he couldn't see her face from here. He felt the small hitch in the world, the pause of footsteps. Then the footsteps resumed, slower, and he pictured her standing with her hand on the gate's carved stone, listening to a dead woman's name come home across the valley, and he found that he was crying and smiling at the same time, and that both were fine.

The bell faded. The pigeons resettled. Mira let go of the rope and wiped her nose on her sleeve with tremendous dignity.

"Was that her?" whispered Lumessa, the miller's daughter. "Is she remembered now?"

"She was always remembered," Tam said. "She was remembered by me the whole time. Now other people remember too." He thought about how to say the rest, the important part, the part that had taken him a year to understand. "That's all remembering is, really. One person telling another person, and meaning it. Borders can't stop it. Treaties can't stop it. It's the slowest kind of magic there is, and it works."

* * *

They came down the tower stairs as the lamps were being lit, and the valley smelled of woodsmoke and the last bread of the day, and Tam walked home through streets where people nodded to him — not the careful, pitying nods of his first weeks, when he'd been the strange soaked boy who spoke the dead tongue, but ordinary nods, neighbor nods, the nods you give someone who belongs.

Wren walked him as far as the cottage door, as Wren often did, not because the lane was dangerous but because company is a habit worth keeping.

"You were good today," Wren said. "With Dole."

"He's not stubborn. He's scared of being bad at things."

"Aye. Same thing at that age." Wren leaned on the doorpost, whittling knife still in hand, a curl of oak falling away. "You know what strikes me? A year ago I was mending a wall with the elders, and none of us could say the word Vessia out loud. Knew it, mind. Knew it in our bones, the way you know a debt. Couldn't say it." Wren shook their head slowly. "Now there's children shouting it across the millpond. Miravel bit Aldous yesterday for getting her name wrong on purpose."

"On purpose?"

"Aldous is Aldous." Wren pushed off from the doorpost. "Get inside. Freya will be up from the gate soon, and she'll pretend she isn't hungry, and you'll pretend you believe her."

Tam laughed and went in, and lit the lamp, and set the kettle on, and put out two cups without thinking about it, because that was the shape of the evening, that was the shape his life had taken: a cottage, a kettle, a keeper coming up the hill with frost in her hair, a boy who spoke two tongues and belonged in both.

He was slicing bread when he heard her boots on the path, and something in him settled — *snegh*, he thought, something in him settled like snow coming home — and then the boots stopped.

Stopped abruptly. Halfway up the path.

Tam went to the window. The lamplight spilled yellow across the dooryard, and beyond it the lane ran down toward the valley road, and on the road, at the edge of the light, stood figures. Travelers. Three of them, maybe four, travel-worn, mud to the knee, the kind of dusty distance on them that said they had walked a very long way.

And one of them — a woman, grey-cloaked, her hood fallen back — was looking up at the cottage with an expression Tam recognized down in his chest, in the old Vessian place where the words lived.

It was the expression of someone who has followed a rumor across half the world. Someone who heard that in a certain valley there was a gate that remembered, and children who spoke a language the maps had buried, and that the door there did not turn people away.

Behind him, the kettle began to sing.

"Tam," Freya said quietly from the doorway, her hand already finding his shoulder. "Set out more cups."
