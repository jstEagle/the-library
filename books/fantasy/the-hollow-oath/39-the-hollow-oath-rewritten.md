## 39. The Hollow Oath, Rewritten

The chisel was her mentor's.

Freya had taken it from the bottom drawer of Aldous's desk three days after his death, wrapped in oilcloth as if he'd known she would need it and had left it there the way he left everything—deliberately, without explanation, trusting her to find the meaning when the meaning was required. For two years it had sat in her own drawer, unused. Chisels were for stonemasons. Keepers kept. That had been the shape of her whole life: she maintained what others had made, recited what others had written, guarded a door whose purpose no one alive could tell her.

Now she sat on the cold granite lip of the Hollow Gate with the chisel warm in her fist and a mallet in the other, and she was going to make something of her own at last.

Below her, the valley lay in early autumn light. The harvest had come in heavy this year—the first good year since the gate went quiet—and the fields wore their stubble like shaved fur, gold going grey at the edges where the frost had begun its nightly visits. Smoke rose from the village chimneys in straight, unhurried columns. Somewhere down there, children were shouting in two languages, and neither one was strange to anyone anymore.

She had not slept well. She never slept well before a carving. Even as an apprentice, learning to dress plain fieldstone under Aldous's critical eye, she had lain awake the night before each lesson running the angles through her mind until dawn. Some part of her had always understood that stone remembered everything you did to it. Every slip of the chisel stayed. Every hesitation showed. You could fill a mistake with dust and prayer, but the stone knew.

This stone especially. This stone had been listening for four hundred years.

* * *

The old inscription still ran across the gate's face in the dead tongue of Vessia—or the living tongue, rather; Freya had stopped calling it dead the day Tam corrected her, with the gentle ferocity only an eight-year-old can manage. *It's not a dead language. It just went somewhere else for a while.* The carved words were worn shallow now, softened by centuries of weather and by the salt-rain that had fallen during the sealing, when the sky over Vessia had wept for a week and the founders had stood beneath it with dry eyes and signed their names to the treaty below.

Aldous had taught her to read those letters when she was younger than Tam—not their meaning, which he claimed not to know, but their shapes, their music. *Say them every morning,* he'd told her. *You don't need to understand a vow to keep it.*

That, she now understood, had been the whole disease of her order in a single sentence. They had built a religion out of not understanding. Obedience without comprehension, duty without question, generation after generation of keepers standing rounds before a door they'd been forbidden to open, guarding a silence they'd been forbidden to examine. Her ancestors hadn't been wicked. That was the thing that had taken her longest to accept, sitting through the long evenings of Wren's testimony while the elders sat pale and silent in the square. They had been *careful*. They had inherited a crime so large it looked like landscape, and they had tended it the way you tend any landscape: without wondering who had been buried there.

She ran her thumb along the old letters one last time. She would not erase them. Erasure was the Compact's trade, the queen's grief, the sin at the bottom of everything. The old oath would stay, legible, a wound kept clean so it could finally heal. But beside it—below it, where the blank stone waited like a held breath—she would carve something new.

She had rewritten it forty times on paper. In the end she had burned the papers, because the words that survived the burning were the ones that fit.

Three lines. Plain speech, no tongue but the common one, no words a child couldn't carry.

*I will remember.*
*I will shelter.*
*I will not trade a people for a peace.*

* * *

"You're going to blunt that edge if you keep staring at it," said Wren.

Freya didn't startle—she'd heard him coming up the switchback road for the last five minutes, his uneven gait as familiar as her own heartbeat—but she turned anyway and found him leaning on the gatepost with a basket on his arm and the expression of a man who has delivered bread to the same door for thirty years and intends to be delivering it long after both of them are gone.

"Stonemasons," she said. "Always judging."

"Only when someone's about to abuse good tools." He set the basket down on the granite: bread still warm, hard cheese, a jar of Tam's honey—the boy had declared war on the hives in early summer and won a truce involving three stings and half a comb. "Elders sent it. They wanted to come themselves."

"They didn't want to come themselves."

"No," Wren agreed comfortably, sitting down beside her with the groan of a man whose knees predated the treaty. "But they wanted to have wanted to. That's progress. Six months ago they wanted to pretend none of this ever happened, and now they want to be seen wanting better. Give them another year and they might actually do something." He squinted up at the gate, at the worn Vessian script, at the blank space below it. "That's where it goes, then."

"That's where it goes."

"Deep enough to outlast weather?"

"Deep enough to outlast forgetting." Freya turned the chisel over in her hands. "That's the real enemy, Wren. It was never magic. It was never the Compact, even—they're just men with treaties and bad consciences. It's forgetting. It moves quiet and eats everything, and it doesn't even know it's hungry."

Wren was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its bread-delivering ease. "You know I carried you. All winter. Every market day, every wedding, whenever anyone started to say your name like it was slipping off the shelf—I put it back." He tapped his own chest. "Up here. Where it doesn't fade."

"I know." She touched his shoulder. "I'm here because of you. Both of us are." Down in the village, a bell rang—Ondrey's tower, the noon hour, rung by the last unfaded hand in all of vanished Vessia, or formerly vanished, or whatever grammar covered a country that had dissolved into its people like sugar into tea. Ondrey rang it every day. He said a country that had given up its borders owed itself a heartbeat, and a bell was as good a heart as any.

"The whole valley came to the reading," Wren said. "In the square. Did Tam tell you?"

"He told me."

"Stood in the rain, half of them, while you read the record aloud. The treaty. The prices. The names of the ones who signed." He shook his head slowly. "I thought there'd be anger. There was some. Old Marta threw a turnip at the elders' bench—poor aim, bless her. But mostly people just listened. Like they'd been waiting their whole lives for someone to say it out loud, and now that it had been said, they could finally put the burden down." He looked at her sideways. "You did that with no trials. No vengeance. Just the truth, read in a clear voice. I've been a stonemason fifty years, Freya Hartwell, and I'll tell you: that's the hardest kind of building there is. Building a thing that holds without walls."

Freya looked out at the valley—at Marta's turnip patch, at the schoolhouse where Tam taught his afternoon class of nine children, at the road that climbed toward the gate and no longer ended in fear.

"The founders thought a peace was a wall," she said. "Something you build between yourself and what you're afraid of. And then you pay people to stand in front of it forever and never ask what's on the other side." She lifted the chisel. "I want to build the other kind. The kind that's a door."

* * *

The first stroke rang out across the valley like a struck bell.

Stone chips leapt bright as sparks. Freya braced her feet, squared her shoulders, and drove the chisel into the granite with all the patience Aldous had beaten into her twenty years ago: let the tool do the work, let the stone tell you its grain, never fight what wants to come away. The letter took shape slowly—a capital *I*, its serif flaring clean. Then the second stroke, the third. *Will.* The word sat solid in the rock.

She worked through the afternoon. The sun crossed the sky and began its long slide toward the western ridge, and the shadows of the gate's arch swung around her like the hands of an enormous clock. Sweat ran into her eyes. Her shoulders burned. Twice she stopped to sharpen the edge on the whetstone, and once—when a flaw in the grain threatened to split the word *shelter* down its middle—she stopped entirely, laid her palm flat against the stone, and simply breathed until she could hear which way the rock wanted to go.

The rock wanted, she decided, the same things she did.

She thought of Tam as she carved. Of course she did; she thought of Tam in everything now, the way a river thinks of its bed. She thought of the soaked, shivering boy at her door two years ago, speaking a language that made the seals flicker, and how her first instinct had been fear, and how her second instinct—the one that mattered, the one that had saved them both—had been to bring him inside and put soup in front of him. *Shelter.* It was the easiest line to carve because she'd already carved it, in living flesh, before she ever touched the stone.

She thought of Queen Ilsevan, transparent as smoke at the end, giving up her rite with a grace that had broken Freya's heart more thoroughly than rage ever could have. *Justice delayed has already murdered my people.* The queen had been almost right. That was the trap of her, the terrible seduction—she was nearly right, right enough to kill for, wrong by exactly the width of one child's life. *Never trade a people for a peace.* Freya had learned that line in the unmapped palace, watching a faded court watch their queen prepare to spend a boy the way the founders had once spent a country.

And she thought of the names. Nine hundred and forty-one of them, spoken aloud at the gate while the Compact's sealing magic tore through her, every syllable a coal she'd swallowed. She still woke some nights with one of them on her lips—a woman named Selka Voss who had died before Tam's grandmother was born, a ferryman called Idrin whose name meant *still water*. The names lived in her now the way scars live in skin. She would carry them until she died, and then Tam would carry them, and then the children in his classroom, and that was the whole point. A country didn't need a border. It needed to be said.

By late afternoon the third line was done. She stood back, arms trembling, and read the gate entire:

The old oath above, in Vessian letters worn soft as sea glass—the vow of silence, of sealed doors, of a peace purchased with a people.

And below, deep-cut, sharp-edged, in plain common script:

*I will remember.*
*I will shelter.*
*I will not trade a people for a peace.*

Wren had gone down the hill an hour past, carrying word that the carving was underway, and the path below was already filling. Freya watched them come—elders in their formal grey, walking carefully, hats in hand; farmers still dusty from the fields; Marta with her cane and her unrepentant throwing arm; the schoolchildren in a ragged double line behind Tam, who walked in front with the grave authority of a boy who had once been the last speaker of a nation and was now the first teacher of a new one. Ondrey came too, having rung the bell early and locked his tower, his unfaded face solemn as Sunday.

They gathered in the wide flat space before the gate, and no one spoke. Freya wiped her hands on her trousers, took up the keeper's staff—it felt lighter than it ever had—and stepped to the center of the stone floor.

"Aldous Coldharbour taught me the old round when I was eleven years old," she said, and her voice carried in the mountain quiet. "He told me to say the words every morning, whether or not I understood them. I obeyed him for twenty years. I stood this round in snow and sickness and grief, and I never asked the gate what it was hiding, because asking wasn't part of the duty." She paused. "I don't blame him for that. He was keeping a silence his own teachers handed him, and theirs was handed to them, all the way back to the day the ink dried on the worst bargain this valley ever struck. Silence is heavy. It gets heavier the longer it's carried. My order carried it until it crushed the very country we were standing on."

Tam, at the front of the crowd, caught her eye. He nodded once, small and fierce.

"So today I walk the round one last time as a duty," Freya said. "And tomorrow, if the valley still wants a keeper, I walk it for the first time as a choice. And these are the terms." She raised the staff and touched it to the new-carved words, one line at a time, the way she had once touched it to the seals. "*Remember:* the gate stands as a memorial. What was done to Vessia is carved here beside the old lie, and every keeper who follows me will learn both, and speak both. *Shelter:* any soul that comes up this road seeking refuge will find it, whatever tongue they speak, whatever border they fled. The Compact's hunters included. Especially them, if it comes to that—we were hunters' granddaughters once ourselves. And the last line is the load-bearing stone, the one the whole arch rests on." Her voice roughened. "*We will not trade a people for a peace. Not our people. Not anyone's. Not for safety, not for silence, not for anything under this sky.*"

She lowered the staff. For a moment nobody moved.

Then old Marta—of all people, old Marta with her turnip-throwing arm—stepped forward, leaned heavily on her cane, and pressed her palm flat against the fresh-cut letters. "Good stone," she pronounced, in the voice of a woman whose judgment on root vegetables and masonry had governed the village market for sixty years. "Deep cut. Will outlast us all."

And that broke the spell. People came forward in twos and threes, laying hands on the gate, on the old worn words and the new sharp ones alike. The elders came last, grey and awkward, and Freya watched the eldest of them—Hedda, who had signed the order to bar Tam's rescue riders two years ago—stand before the inscription for a long time before touching it. Her fingers trembled. She didn't look at Freya. She didn't have to. Some reckonings are conducted entirely in stone.

The sun went down behind the ridge and lit the gate's face copper-gold, and the new letters drank the light and held it.

* * *

Long after the crowd had gone down the mountain with lanterns bobbing like a string of low stars, Freya stood the round.

Out of habit, at first. She walked the circuit she had walked ten thousand times: east post, south seal, west post, north seal, the full turning of the gate, checking what no longer needed checking. The seals were quiet. They had been quiet since the names were spoken—not broken, not active, just *resting*, like a dog grown old and content by the fire. Magic pooled gently against the threshold and lapped there and went no further, and Freya no longer feared it. It was only the world, pressing close, curious, wanting in.

Halfway through the second circuit, she realized she was smiling.

She stopped at the eastern post and looked out. Night had come down soft over the valley. Below, the village windows glowed; the bell tower stood black against the deep blue; somewhere a fiddle was playing, badly, at a festival she had deliberately not been invited to because the elders had finally learned that she hated speeches and loved being left alone with her work. The wind came up the road smelling of woodsmoke and cut hay and the far-off cold of the high passes.

For twenty years this view had been a wall's view. She had stood here watching for threats, counting strangers, measuring the world outside against the safety within. Tonight she stood the same ground and saw something else entirely: a valley holding its light up against the dark like a cupped flame. Not a fortress. A lamp.

Her feet ached. Her shoulders screamed where the mallet had found them. There was stone dust in the creases of her palms that she suspected would be there until she died, and she found she hoped it would be.

At the top of the road, small and quick-footed against the starlight, Tam came climbing back up—he'd slipped away from the fiddling and the cakes, as he always did at the end of such nights, because festivals were for other people and the gate was theirs. He reached her at the eastern post and stood beside her without a word, the way he'd stood beside her for two years, and together they looked down the dark road.

"Read it to me again," he said. "The new part."

So she did, aloud, in the ordinary night air, no ceremony at all: *remember, shelter, never trade.* And Tam repeated it back in Vessian, translating it into the language of the erased country, making the new oath bilingual the way everything in their lives was becoming, and the gate drank in both versions equally, and the seals glowed faintly, softly, like embers deciding to sleep.

They were still standing there when Tam stiffened.

Down the road, far below where the lamplight ended, something was moving. Two figures, maybe three, walking up the switchbacks in the dark—unhurried, unafraid, carrying a lantern of their own. Travelers. The first true travelers to seek the Hollow Gate since the world had begun, piece by piece, to remember that there had once been a country here, and that a door remained.

Freya's hand found Tam's shoulder. The old fear rose in her, twenty years deep—and then she breathed, and let it go, and picked up the lantern instead.

"Well," she said. "Someone's come knocking."
