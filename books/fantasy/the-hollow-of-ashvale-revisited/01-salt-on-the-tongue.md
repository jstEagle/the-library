## 1. Salt on the Tongue

The arsenic arrived with the honey.

Elodie Fairweather tasted it before he ever touched the spoon — a faint metallic whisper beneath the sweetness, like a coin left too long in a beggar's mouth. He let the porridge sit on his tongue for a slow count of five, cataloguing as he went: oats from the northern farms, cream gone slightly over-churned, wildflower honey, and underneath it all, white arsenic, roughly enough to kill a grown man in four hours if untreated. Six hours if the man was fat and stubborn, which King Aldric was both.

He swallowed.

That was the part no one understood about his work. Everyone imagined the royal taster's courage lived in the swallowing. It didn't. The courage was in the waiting afterward — the long, dull hours of standing at the edge of the king's breakfast chamber while a man who ruled half the coast ate eggs and complained about the weather, while Elodie's body quietly decided whether today would be the day three years of accumulated poison finally found some corner of him that had stopped forgiving.

His body always forgave. That was the trouble with it.

"Fairweather." The steward, Corvin, snapped his fingers twice, the way he did when he wanted to seem important to someone who outranked neither of them. "His Majesty asks if the honey is off."

"The honey is excellent," Elodie said. "Tell him I said so."

Corvin squinted at him. Stewards always squinted at tasters, as though loyalty were something you could catch by looking hard enough. "You're pale."

"I'm always pale. It's the profession." He bowed, shallow and correct, and withdrew through the servants' corridor with his hands folded behind his back so no one could see them shake.

Not from fear of dying. From arithmetic.

Someone had poisoned the king's breakfast. Again. Third time this season, and each dose larger than the last, each one more careless — this batch hadn't even been stirred properly; the crystals would have gritted between the teeth of anyone less obliging than a taster's tongue. Which meant one of two things: either the poisoner was getting sloppy, or the poisoner *wanted* the attempt noticed. Wanted the panic. Wanted the king frightened and the physicians summoned and the whole palace murmuring about enemies within the walls.

Elodie had survived worse kitchens than this one. But he had learned, in thirty-one years and three of them royal, that the dangerous thing was never the poison itself. The dangerous thing was what people did when they were afraid of it.

He walked out through the Mint Gate at midmorning, past the warden who checked his taster's badge — a small lead disc stamped with the crown's sigil, ugly enough that no one had ever tried to steal it — and down into the city proper, where Ashvale smelled the way it always smelled: fish smoke and coal smoke and the green rot of the harbor tide, all of it braided together into something that visitors called stink and locals called home.

Home. He turned the word over as he walked, testing its weight. Three years in the palace barracks, but his rooms were here, on Cordwainer Lane, above a cobbler who snored like a landslide. His rooms were here because his *work* was here. Both of his works.

The charm-market opened where Cordwainer Lane bent toward the water — not a market, officially, nothing so honest as a market. Just a stretch of stalls and doorways where certain goods changed hands quickly and quietly, where a woman might sell you a knotted thread for your aching back or a sachet for dreams, and neither seller nor buyer would use the word *magic*, because in Ashvale the word was a pyre with letters around it.

Elodie's stall was a folding table and a strip of canvas awning, wedged between Bettina's candle shop and a man who sold eels. He'd chosen the spot for its ordinariness. Nobody looked twice at a food-taster selling trinkets. Tasters were famous for odd habits; everyone knew they were half-superstitious, touching iron, spitting salt. A luck-knot or two fit the character perfectly.

He set out his stock with the care of a man arranging cutlery: six luck-knots of braided red thread, three fever-charms pressed in wax and dried lavender, a jar of dream-sachets, and — wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his satchel, where it stayed unless he liked a customer very much — a single warding-bell cast from pewter and old nails, tuned to a note only bad intentions could hear.

Bettina leaned across from her candles, flour on her forearms, her voice pitched under the market noise. "Slow day?"

"Every day's slow now. People are frightened to be seen buying."

"People are frightened to be seen *breathing*," she said. "Have you heard? They posted a new edict at the bakery. Big letters. Silver for names."

"I heard." He hadn't read it yet. He'd been avoiding the bakery since Tuesday. "Silver buys a lot of bread these days."

"It buys a lot of silence, too, if you're smart." She looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable — Bettina had known his grandmother, which meant she knew things about him he preferred unspoken — then retreated to her candles and left him alone with his knots and his dread.

The morning passed the way mornings passed: slowly, and with too much time to think. A dockhand bought a dream-sachet for his wife without meeting Elodie's eyes. An old soldier haggled him down on a luck-knot and paid anyway, muttering that luck was a fool's purchase, which was exactly what soldiers said right before buying more. Elodie made change, smiled the smile that didn't reach anywhere, and kept one eye on the lane.

Because that was the other thing about the charm-market. You were never alone in it. There was always someone across the way, leaning in a doorway, watching a little too long. Usually it was nothing. Usually it was a thief deciding whether you were worth the trouble, and thieves were almost restful compared to the alternative.

Almost.

She came at noon, when the light off the harbor turned everything the color of tarnished silver. Wren Saltmarsh — though he wouldn't learn her name until later, and later seemed very far away just then — sixteen maybe, seventeen, with wind-cracked hands and her father's heavy boots and an expression like a girl carrying something breakable across ice.

"You're the knot-man," she said. Not a question.

"I'm a man who sells knots."

"My mother says yours hold." She set a coin on the table — a full silver, too much, and they both knew it. "For my father. He fishes the deep water past the shoals, and the last three boats that went out there came back short-handed." Her jaw tightened. "One didn't come back at all."

Elodie looked at the coin. Then at her hands, which were working against each other in her lap, the way hands do when their owner has decided crying is a luxury.

The safe answer was no. The safe answer was always no: *I'm sorry, miss, I sell trinkets, thread and wax, nothing more.* The safe answer kept the stall ordinary and the taster boring and the floorboards over his real stock undisturbed. He had given the safe answer perhaps two hundred times in three years, and every time, some small part of him had died of it quietly, the way a fire dies when you feed it nothing.

Rent was rent. But it wasn't rent that moved his hand.

"Deep water past the shoals," he repeated. "Current runs wrong there after storms. Takes things it shouldn't." He reached under the table, past the display knots, to the ones he'd tied properly — tied the way his grandmother had taught him, with intention worked into every crossing, each braid a sentence and the finished knot a promise. He chose one with seven strands, red thread through black, and laid it in her palm. "This one holds against current. Tell him to wear it inside his shirt, against the skin, and never tell anyone where he got it. Especially not—"

He stopped himself. *Especially not the wardens.* But saying it aloud was a kind of confession, and the lane felt suddenly very wide and very listening.

Wren's fingers closed around the knot. "Thank you," she said, and there was a ferocity in it that startled him — gratitude with teeth, gratitude like a weapon she intended to keep. "My mother said you were decent. She said don't trust anyone in this market, but the knot-man's decent."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She says wise women get burned in this city." Wren said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report the weather, and then she was gone, boots striking the cobbles, back toward the harbor and the boats and whatever the sea intended.

Elodie sat with the silver coin in front of him for a long moment. Too much money. He pushed half of it back toward the edge of the table for the next customer who needed credit, an old habit, a small rebellion, and told himself — as he told himself daily — that today had been fine. Today had been careful. Today nobody had seen anything worth denouncing.

Across the lane, a figure in a hooded cloak shifted its weight against the cooper's wall. Had been there an hour, maybe more. Not moving. Not buying. Just *there*, the way a splinter is there under skin — ignorable until suddenly it isn't.

Elodie began packing his stock, unhurried, a man closing up early because the fish smell was bothering him, nothing more. Luck-knots into the satchel. Fever-charms into the tin. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was one of the things the palace paid him for, along with the tongue and the stomach and the strange, cursed blood that took arsenic like a sponge takes water.

But his heart was going like a drum in a procession, and he caught himself doing the arithmetic again, the nightly arithmetic, the one he never let finish:

*If they come for me, the floorboards take ten seconds to open. The charms burn in the stove in under a minute. The ledger —*

The ledger. His grandmother's ledger, ciphered in her cramped hand, hidden inside the false spine of a book of grain tariffs. The one thing he could not burn and could not explain and could not, despite three years of trying, bring himself to destroy. Every night he told himself he should feed it to the stove and be free of it. Every night he climbed the stairs instead and lay awake listening to the cobbler snore below and the city breathe around him, and the ledger sat in its hiding place like a heart in a chest, patient, waiting.

Because his grandmother had written things in it. Things about binding. About a name. And Elodie Fairweather, who could taste poison in honey from across a room, had never once managed to make himself read past the first page.

He closed the satchel. The hooded figure was still there.

Still there when he folded the table. Still there when he nodded to Bettina, who frowned at him over her candles and mouthed something that might have been *go home*. Still there, he was nearly sure, when he turned up Cordwainer Lane toward his rooms, and he did not look back to confirm it, because looking back was how you told the watcher that you'd seen them, and seen-things demanded explanations, and explanations in Ashvale this season ended at the ash-pits outside the walls.

At his door, he paused. Below him, the harbor bells rang the change of watch. Somewhere across the roofs, the palace sat on its hill with its poisoned breakfasts and its forgetting king, and somewhere in the lanes, a fisherman's daughter was carrying a knot of red thread to a man who would live or drown depending on whether Elodie's grandmother's craft still held after all these years.

And nailed to the bakery door three streets over, in letters big enough to read from a moving cart, an edict waited for him with his name not yet on it — but with room. Room for many names. Silver for each.

Elodie went inside, barred the door, and pulled up the first floorboard.

* * *

The stock came out in bundles: charms wrapped in wool, knots coiled like sleeping snakes, the pewter bell cupped in his palm with its bad-intention note silent as always. He fed half of it to the stove and watched the flames take the lavender and wax with a smell like a garden dying, and he kept back only the ledger and the bell and one luck-knot he could not have explained keeping if asked, except that it was the first one he'd ever tied well, and a man ought to be allowed one thing he can't explain.

He was still crouched at the stove when the knocking started.

Three raps. Paused. Three more. The rhythm of a neighbor, not a warden — wardens knocked like they were breaking it down already — but Elodie's whole body went tight as wire anyway, and he stood very slowly, the last unburnt charm still in his hand, and listened to the stairs creak under someone's weight.

Through the door, muffled by oak and his own hammering pulse, a voice he knew. Bettina's. Low and fast and wrong.

"Elodie. Open the door. They've taken the midwife off Tanner's Row." A breath. "They're coming down the lane."
