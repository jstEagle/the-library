## 2. The Edict Nailed to the Bakery Door

Elodie smelled the paper before he saw it.

That was the thing about Ashvale — the city announced itself in smells before anything else. Bread and brine from the harbor lanes, tallow smoke, the iron tang of the mint running day and night. And now, layered over all of it like a stain that wouldn't wash out: fresh ink and wet paste, the smell of government.

The edict was nailed to the bakery door at the corner of Tallow Row, three sheets of it, nailed high so no one could tear them down without a ladder. The baker's boy was already standing beneath them, reading aloud to a gathering crowd in the flat, careful voice of someone who'd learned his letters from a priest and didn't trust them.

*"By order of His Majesty King Aldric, Second of That Name, Protector of the Realm and Keeper of the Peace: let it be known that within the walls of this city there dwell persons gifted with unlawful arts..."*

Elodie stood at the edge of the crowd with his market basket on his arm and did not move. Moving drew eyes. He had learned that much in three years of walking between two lives — the taster's white apron in the morning, the charm-seller's shadowed stall by evening — and the first rule of both was the same: be furniture. Be the wall people lean against and never look at.

*"...persons who traffic in charms, hexes, blessings, bindings, and other works forbidden by crown law. Any subject denouncing such a person to the wardens shall receive one silver mark upon confirmation..."*

One silver mark. Elodie did the arithmetic without meaning to. One mark was half a week's rent. One mark was a coat for winter, or medicine, or a debt paid off with something left over. The crown wasn't asking its subjects to be brave. It was asking them to be hungry, and then paying them for it.

*"...and any person found harboring, concealing, or aiding the gifted shall share their punishment."*

The punishment went unspoken on the page, but everyone in the crowd knew it anyway. It was in the word they used for it, the word that had started appearing in tavern talk over the past month, first as rumor, then as fact: *ash-burning*. Not hanging. Hanging was quick and left a body the family could bury. Burning was different. Burning was a message written in a medium that lasted.

"Read it again," said a woman near the front. "The part about the silver."

The baker's boy read it again.

A man Elodie recognized from the fish market — big shoulders, gull tattooed on his forearm — spat into the gutter. "They'll have half the street denounced by Friday," he said. "Half the street deserves it, too. Old Hessa with her teas. The widow Crane and her cards."

"The widow Crane tells fortunes with playing cards," said the woman. "My gran does the same on feast days."

"My gran doesn't take coin for it," said the man, and the crowd made a sound that wasn't quite laughter and wasn't quite agreement, and moved on to the business of the day, which was bread, and which suddenly felt very far away.

Elodie bought a loaf because not buying one would have been stranger than buying one. He counted out his coppers slowly, met the baker's eye — the baker looked tired, and looked away first — and walked home with the edict's words sitting in his stomach like a stone swallowed whole.

***

Bettina was burning her charms when he got back to Cordwainer Lane.

She'd dragged her worktable out into the middle of the lane, the scarred old table where she cut wick and poured tallow and rolled the little votive candles she sold to chapel-goers, and on the table she'd built a small pyre of things Elodie recognized with a sick lurch: knotted cords, dried bundles, little cloth sachets stitched shut with colored thread. Her stock. Her craft. Twelve years of it, maybe more, going up in a fire she'd fed with lamp oil so it would catch fast and burn clean.

She stood beside it with her arms folded, watching, while the neighbors gathered at their doors and windows to watch her do it.

"Bettina." Elodie set his basket down. "What are you doing?"

"What does it look like?" She didn't turn around. Bettina was sixty if she was a day, built like a dock bollard, with forearms that could wring a chicken's neck without ceremony. "It looks like a woman protecting her own neck."

"They're candles. They're *candles*, Bettina. Half the chapel buys—"

"The chapel ones are blessed proper, by priests, with the god's name said right." Now she turned, and her face was grim as a closed door. "These aren't those. These are the others. The ones I make for the girls who can't conceive and the old men who can't sleep and the mothers who want their sons back from the sea. The ones that *work*, Elodie." She said the last word quietly, but the whole lane heard it. "You don't leave things that work lying about your house when there's silver on the table for finding them."

"You could hide them."

"Hide them." She laughed, short and hard. "I've lived on this lane forty years. You think I don't know my neighbors? You think Mrs. Pell across the way wouldn't sell me for a mark and sleep fine after? She'd sleep *better*. People always do." She nudged the pyre with her foot. A knot-cord caught, flared, and was gone. "No. Better it burns here, in daylight, where everyone can see an honest woman destroying her own sin before the crown has to. Let them watch. Let them say, *well, Bettina burned hers, she can't have been one of the real ones.*"

"And is that true?"

She looked at him then, really looked, and for a moment something passed over her face that might have been pity, or might have been warning. "Go home, Elodie."

He went home. Behind him, the fire crackled, and the lane watched, and somewhere in the smoke was twelve years of small mercies — fevers broken, babies coaxed into the world, griefs eased by a knot tied right and a word said soft — all of it ash before noon.

***

His rooms were above the chandler's shop at the top of Cordwainer Lane, two rooms and a slanted attic he rented from a landlord who lived comfortably elsewhere. He'd chosen them for three reasons: cheap, close to the palace kitchens, and the floorboards.

The floorboards were the reason now.

He worked fast and quiet, though the noise hardly mattered — the whole city was making noise today, doors slamming, voices low and urgent, the wardens' boots already audible in pairs on the main streets, walking their new routes. He pried up the board under the bed, the loose one, the one that had been loose when he arrived and had never been fixed because landlords fix nothing that isn't leaking or on fire.

His stock came up in three bundles wrapped in oilcloth.

Not much, really, when he saw it all together. He'd told himself for years it was a modest trade, a side living, nothing that would interest anyone important. But laid out on his bed in the grey light through the window, it looked like evidence. Luck-knots, a dozen of them, red thread and river-clay beads. Fever-charms, six, the willow-bark and feverfew sewn into green felt. Warding braids for doorposts. Three satchels of salt charmed against nightmares. A jar of honey blessed — *charmed*, he corrected himself, *blessed is what priests do* — against coughing. And underneath everything, wrapped separately, the thing he was most careful with: a small ledger of his own, not of sales but of recipes. Knot-patterns. Wordings. Which charm for which hurt, and how to say the words so they took.

He'd written it in his grandmother's shorthand. That was the dangerous part. Anyone who read it would know it wasn't a grocer's book.

He wrapped each bundle tight, stacked them in the cavity between the joists, and pressed the board back down. Then he scattered flour dust and candle-drippings over the seam until it looked like every other neglected floorboard in Ashvale, and sat back on his heels, and listened to his own heart going like a fist on a door.

It wasn't enough. He knew it wasn't enough. Wardens with pry-bars and a warrant could have the boards up in ten minutes. But warrants needed names, and names needed denunciations, and denunciations needed—

Needed what? A neighbor with a grudge. A customer with a guilty conscience. Someone who'd seen him at the stall by the fish market, seen the knots change hands, seen the coins. Someone hungry, or frightened, or both.

He thought of the fisherman's daughter, Wren, who'd bought a luck-knot for her father's boat three weeks ago and come back twice since just to talk. He thought of the midwife on Sallow Street. He thought of old Hessa with her teas, whose name had been said aloud under the bakery awning not two hours ago, casually, the way you'd mention weather.

Fear didn't arrive like a knock at the door. That was the thing nobody told you. Fear arrived like weather. It came in over the rooftops all at once, touching everyone, changing the light, and there was no door you could close against it, and the only question was what kind of creature you'd turn out to be when it settled in.

Outside, Bettina's fire had burned down to a black smear on the cobbles. Through his window he could see her sweeping the ash into a dustpan, unhurried, thorough, a woman erasing herself in public and daring anyone to comment.

Down the lane, two wardens stopped a young man Elodie didn't know and asked him questions Elodie couldn't hear. The young man pointed — not at anyone, just at the ground, shrugging, helpless — and the wardens moved on.

For now.

Elodie ate half the loaf standing up, tasting nothing, cataloguing nothing. Then he put on his coat, checked that his taster's badge was visible on his breast — the little pewter disc, the king's mark stamped in it, the one document in Ashvale that still meant *hands off* — and went out into the weather.

Whatever came next, it would come to everyone. The best he could do was be boring when it did.

He locked his door behind him and did not look back at the floorboards, because looking would make them interesting, and interesting was the one thing he could not afford to be.

* * *

He was halfway down the lane when he heard the boots behind him — two sets, unhurried, the particular rhythm of men who wanted to be heard coming — and a voice, official and almost apologetic:

"Master Fairweather? A moment, if you please."
