## 3. What the Blood Remembers

The Archive of the Mint kept its poisons in the same room as its poetry.

Maren Voss had never decided whether this was an accident of shelving or a joke someone had died for making. Either way, it suited her. She had spent eleven years among these shelves, and in eleven years she had learned that the most dangerous documents in Ashvale were never locked away. They were misfiled. They were boring. They were ledgers with cracked spines and clerks' initials on the first page, and no one — not the Lord Protector's men, not the witch-hunters with their iron pokers, not even the archivists themselves — thought to look twice at a book that smelled of dust and arithmetic.

She looked twice at everything. That was why she'd been disgraced.

Well. That, and the letter.

She pulled the taster ledgers from the third shelf, back row, where the light from the high windows went to die. Nine volumes, covering thirty-one years of royal meals. Each entry recorded the dish, the taster assigned, the wait observed, and — when the crown could afford it, which was rarely — a physician's assay of the taster's blood, drawn quarterly as a matter of protocol nobody believed mattered.

Nobody believed it mattered because the assays always said the same thing. *Subject healthy. Subject healthy. Subject healthy.* The physicians who wrote them were paid by the meal, and the tasters were paid by the year, and the whole apparatus existed so that if a king ever did die of poison, there would be a ledger to point at and a name to hang.

Maren carried the nine volumes to her desk in the corner, the one with the legless drawer she'd propped up on a brick, and began to read.

* * *

Her name was still on the door downstairs. That was the cruelty of it. MAREN VOSS, KEEPER OF RECORDS, painted in gold leaf by a sign-writer who had charged the crown four silver marks for the flourishes. She walked past it every morning like walking past her own gravestone. Officially, she was no longer Keeper of anything; officially, she was a "consultant," which was what the Mint called people it wanted nearby but never again in charge. Unofficially, she was the only person in Ashvale who had read the correspondence between King Aldric's grandfather and the College of Physicians regarding "the melancholia of succession," and who understood why those letters had been burned, catalogued as burned, and then quietly re-shelved by someone who couldn't bring herself to destroy them.

That someone had been Maren, twenty-six years old and certain that history deserved better than fire.

Twenty years later she knew better than anyone that some things deserved exactly that. She shelved them anyway. Habit, or hope. She had stopped trying to tell the difference.

She opened the earliest ledger and worked forward, copying every blood-assay into her own notebook, because the first rule of scholarship was that you never trusted a record you hadn't transcribed yourself. The early entries were dull in the way she loved: dates, dishes, quantities. Roast capon, two tasters present, no adverse reaction. Wine from the southern valleys, one taster, no adverse reaction. A soup in the winter of Aldric's father's reign that had been laced — this noted in a clerk's cramped hand, almost apologetic — with something the assay called simply *bitterwater*, and the taster, a woman named Osla Fen, had shown nothing worse than a headache.

Maren paused over that one. Bitterwater killed at three drams. The soup had held five.

She read on. The pattern, once she started looking for it, was everywhere, and it was nowhere, and it was arranged so neatly that she might have missed it entirely if she hadn't spent two decades learning to read what records refused to say.

Tasters died. That was the truth under all the tidy columns. In thirty-one years, the crown had employed nineteen food-tasters, and seven of them were marked in the margins with a small cross and a date — dead of fever, dead of flux, dead of "a wasting," dead within weeks of a suspicious meal that the assay had pronounced harmless. The court had blamed bad luck. Bad water. Weak constitutions. The physicians had shrugged, collected their fees, and written *subject healthy* right up until the subject wasn't.

But here was the thing Maren's fingers found before her mind did, tracing down the columns: the tasters who survived were not the ones who ate less. They were the ones whose bloodwork changed.

Slowly. Incrementally. Quarter by quarter, the assays of the long-serving tasters showed a drift — iron counts rising, then falling; liver markers climbing into ranges the physicians annotated with increasing bewilderment; and always, always, a notation Maren had seen a hundred times without ever seeing it: *accustomed.*

Accustomed to arsenic. Accustomed to bitterwater. Accustomed to compounds that had no business being accustomed-to, because the human body did not become accustomed to poison. It accumulated it. It stored it in bone and liver and hair until the storehouse failed. Every physician in Ashvale knew this. It was the first lesson of toxicology and the last word on why tasters were paid well and buried young.

And yet here were six tasters across three decades whose bodies had done the impossible — not merely surviving the dose but metabolizing it, clearing it, growing *stronger* against it with each passing season — and whose deaths, when they came, came of nothing at all. No poison in the blood at the end. Just a sudden quietness, like a lamp running out of oil.

Except one.

Maren turned to the last volume, the current one, and found the name she had come looking for, though she told herself, in the way scholars tell themselves things, that she was only following the data.

FAIRWEATHER, E. Appointed three years past. Quarterly assays attached.

She spread the pages out across her desk like a hand of cards.

The first assay, taken his first month, was ordinary. A young man's blood, slightly undernourished, nothing remarkable. The second showed the beginning of the drift — faster than any of the others, as if his body had skipped the introduction and gone straight to the argument. By the fourth, the physician's notes had stopped being clinical and started being frightened. *Values inconsistent with life, frankly,* someone had written in the margin, and then crossed it out, and then written beneath it, smaller: *retest.*

Retested. Same values. Retested again by a different physician, one brought in from outside the palace, who had apparently demanded to know whether the samples had been switched. They had not been switched. Elodie Fairweather's blood, drawn plain from his arm on an ordinary Tuesday, contained enough accumulated arsenic to kill a plow horse, enough bitterwater to fell a garrison, and traces of four other compounds the outside physician had identified and then refused to put in writing — and the man himself had walked out of the infirmary complaining only that the needle bruised.

*Subject healthy,* the final assay said, in the original physician's defeated hand. And then, after a long gap in the ink where the pen had hovered: *Subject more than healthy.*

Maren sat back.

The lamp had burned low. Somewhere above her, through the vaulted ceiling, she could hear the Mint's day shift stamping coinage, a sound like slow rain. Her notebook lay open beside the ledgers, filled with her own tight script, and she looked at what she'd written without quite reading it, because her mind had gone somewhere else — back, the way it always went, to the sealed folio on the top shelf that she was not permitted to open and had opened anyway, eleven years ago, on the night before her disgrace.

The correspondence about the melancholia of succession. King Aldric's grandfather writing to the College of Physicians in a hand that deteriorated across four letters from firm to trembling to barely legible, describing how he kept forgetting the faces of his own children, how he would find himself standing in rooms he didn't recognize, how the crown felt — and Maren had never forgotten the phrase, had never been able to unlearn it — *the crown feels heavier than gold should feel, as though it is eating.*

The College's reply, drafted by a physician named Corvus whose other writings had all been destroyed: *Your Majesty describes symptoms consistent with poisoning, but our assays find none. We therefore advise you to consider whether the poison may be of a kind that does not live in blood.*

*A kind that does not live in blood.*

She had thought, at twenty-six, that this was poetry. Madness dressed in Latin. She had filed it under CURIOSITIES and told herself she'd saved it from the fire out of principle.

Now she sat in a lamplit corner with nine ledgers fanned around her like a losing hand, and she understood that Corvus had been writing prose. Precise, terrified prose. There was something in the palace that fed on kings, and it did not leave its signature in blood — but blood was where it left everything else. Because whatever it was, whatever ancient thing wore the crown and picked the host and drank the memory out of a dynasty generation by generation, it needed tasters. It needed the crown protected. And somewhere in the machinery of the court, without anyone deciding it, the office of royal food-taster had become something other than what it claimed.

A filter. A sieve. Or — Maren's pen stopped moving — a vessel.

The tasters who died were the ones who resisted. The ones whose bodies fought the accumulation and lost. But the ones who *accustomed* — the ones whose blood learned to hold poison the way a riverbed holds water — they lived. They lived and lived and lived, healthy as horses, full to the brim with death, and their assays grew stranger every quarter until the physicians gave up asking questions, because the crown paid for *subject healthy* and not for understanding.

They weren't being tested for poison.

They were being *ripened.*

Maren wrote the word and hated it immediately and did not cross it out. Ripened. Like fruit. Like something being grown toward a harvest, season over season, dose over dose, until it was ready to be — what? Eaten? Used? Worn?

Fairweather, E. Three years of service. The fastest drift on record. The outside physician's frightened marginalia. *More than healthy.*

She thought about the man himself, though she'd met him only twice, both times in corridors, both times brief. A slight young man with careful hands and a market-stall accent he tried to sand off in the palace's presence. He had bowed correctly and looked at nothing and given the impression, which Maren recognized because she manufactured the same impression daily, of a person holding very still so that no one would look too closely.

Everyone in Ashvale was hiding something. Most people hid debts, or lovers, or opinions. She wondered what a man carrying three years of poison in his veins hid, and whether he even knew he was carrying it, and whether — this was the thought that made her set down her pen — he knew what he was being grown *for.*

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape, the sum that had ended careers before hers and would end more: the tasters ripened, the kings hollowed, the successions came on schedule, and the thing in the crown endured. Corvus had seen it. Aldric's grandfather had felt it. Someone, generations back, had known enough to be afraid, and that fear had been burned along with everything else, except for the scraps that foolish young archivists refused to feed to pyres.

Scraps were not enough anymore. Scraps had bought her twenty years of exile in her own library. What she needed now was a living document — a body that remembered what had been done to it, even if the man inside it didn't know.

She looked at Fairweather's final assay. *More than healthy.* The ink had dried unevenly, as if the physician's hand had shaken on the last word.

Immunity, Maren thought, is only half the story. Nobody becomes immune to poison. Something becomes immune *in* them. And whatever that something is, it has a shape, and shapes can be read, and I am — whatever else they've taken from me — still the best reader in this building.

She closed the ledgers, stacked them, and returned them to the third shelf, back row, arranging each spine to the exact angle she'd found it at. Then she stood for a moment in the dark aisle with her notebook pressed to her chest, feeling the old dangerous warmth kindle behind her sternum, the one that had gotten her disgraced and would probably get her ash-burned and that she had never once managed, in fifty-one years, to put out.

Hope. Scholars called it hypothesis. It behaved identically.

Tomorrow she would request the personnel file. Tomorrow she would learn where the taster Fairweather slept, and what he did with his evenings, and whether a man who tasted death for a living had ever once wondered why it never tasted him back.

Tonight, she allowed herself one indulgence. She took down the sealed folio from the top shelf — CURIOSITIES, SUCCESSION, DO NOT CIRCULATE — and broke, for the second time in her life, the wax seal that wasn't hers to break.

Inside, folded small, was a page she had never noticed before, tucked behind the correspondence. Not a letter. A fragment of a household ledger, older than the others by a century at least, in a woman's careful hand. Most of it was inventory — candles, flour, a length of blue thread — but at the bottom, in ink gone brown as old blood, was a single line that Maren read three times before her heart let her breathe:

*The binding holds while the binder's line endures. God forgive me, I have made certain it will endure.*

Below the line, a family name, half-eaten by damp.

It began with F.
