## 4. Charms for the Desperate

The edict went up on a Tuesday. By Thursday, half the market had learned to be afraid quietly, and the other half had learned to be afraid loudly, and Elodie Fairweather had learned neither, because rent was due at the end of the month and fear did not pay the cooper.

He told himself this every morning while he laid out his stock beneath the false bottom of his basket: the luck-knots braided from river-grass and knotted seven times against bad weather; the sleep-sachets of dried valerian and something else he'd never name aloud; the small clay beads fired with salt and ash that kept milk from souring and, if you believed hard enough, kept fevers from settling in a child's chest.

None of it was powerful. That was the point. He had spent three years learning to make his magic small — small enough to pass for craft, for superstition, for the harmless habits of a city that had always muttered charms over its doorways and then pretended it hadn't. A luck-knot was just knots. A fever-bead was just clay. Anyone could look at them and see nothing, which was the only kind of safe there was now.

The baker's boy had nailed the proclamation to the bakery door with four iron nails, one for each corner, as if the words might otherwise escape. *By order of the Lord Protector: the practice of the gifted arts is forbidden within the walls of Ashvale. Reward of ten silver marks for information leading to the arrest of any practitioner.* Ten marks. Elodie made eleven marks a year selling charms, and he sold bread on the side to make up the difference. Ten marks was a year of a neighbor's loyalty, priced and posted.

*Constitution*, the edict said too, further down, in smaller letters. *Persons of suspicious constitution may be detained for examination.* Nobody quite knew what that meant, which was how the crown liked its threats — vague enough to stretch around anyone.

Elodie read it twice, bought his loaf, and walked home through streets where every second doorway had a scorch mark on the sill where someone had burned their own protection overnight. Bettina next door had done hers in the open, standing in the street in her nightdress feeding dried herbs into a dish like she was cooking breakfast, daring anyone to say a word. Her face had been terrible. He hadn't been able to look away.

His own stock went under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, all except what fit in the basket's false bottom. Rent was rent.

* * *

The market on Friday morning looked like any market, which is to say it looked like a lie everyone had agreed to tell together.

The charm-sellers were gone — old Ivo who sold horse-blessings, the twins from Tanner's Row with their love-philtres that were mostly clove oil and confidence. Their stalls stood empty or had been converted overnight: one now displayed candles, ordinary tallow, aggressively ordinary; another sold buttons. Ashvale was a city of button merchants now, apparently. It had been for two days.

Elodie kept his corner by the fish stalls, where the smell covered everything and buyers came close and spoke low out of habit anyway, haggling over mackerel. He didn't display anything. People knew. People had always known; that was the strange grace of Ashvale before the edict, that everyone pretended not to know things, and the pretending was itself a kind of kindness. Now the same pretense cut both ways. The woman buying mackerel beside you on Friday might sell your name on Saturday for ten marks and a clear conscience, because wasn't it practically civic duty?

His first customer was a rope-maker's widow who wanted a knot against nightmares. She paid without looking at him, took the bundle of river-grass he passed under the counter like contraband, and left so fast she knocked over a crate of eels. He righted the crate. His hands were steady. Three years of tasting poison taught a body steadiness; nothing in the king's breakfast had ever rattled him the way a widow's frightened back did.

A fisherman bought a wind-knot for his nets. Two sisters split a sleep-sachet down the middle because they couldn't afford a whole one between them, and he let them, taking less than it cost him, and hated himself for the arithmetic even as he did it.

And then, near midday, when the fish smell was thickest and the crowd thinnest, the hooded buyer came.

Not to him. Not yet. Just across the lane, by the eel-cart, where a figure in a grey hooded cloak had stopped and turned and was looking — not at the fish, not at the cart, but at the space where Elodie stood behind his little table of nothing. Watching. The hood was pulled well forward. Underneath it, Elodie could make out the pale suggestion of a chin, nothing more.

He'd seen watchers before. Every charm-seller had. Usually they were curious children, or drunk men, or the occasional zealot who wanted to work up the nerve to denounce someone and needed a good long look first. You learned to read them. Children got bored. Drunks wandered off. Zealots left quickly once they'd seen enough, walking fast, already composing their claim on the reward.

This one did none of those things. This one stayed.

Ten minutes. Fifteen. Long enough that the eel-seller started giving the hooded figure the sidelong look reserved for people who blocked trade. Long enough that Elodie began cataloguing details the way he catalogued flavors — automatically, usefully. Grey cloak, good wool, no badge. Boots: plain, but well-made, the leather creased in a way that suggested long walking rather than long sitting. Hands folded inside the cloak. Stillness like a held breath.

It was the stillness that bothered him most. Fearful people fidgeted. Guilty people moved. Whatever stood across the lane had arranged itself into patience, and patience, in Ashvale this week, meant either a hunter or a fool, and fools didn't wear boots like that.

Elodie sold a fever-bead to a grandmother and did not take his eyes off the lane.

* * *

The child's mother came at dusk, when the stalls were folding and the light had gone the color of weak broth.

Elodie knew her — Tam, the cooper's wife, or maybe the cooper's sister; the family relations of Cooper's Lane were a tangle he'd never fully mapped. She came fast, head down, and gripped his wrist across the table with fingers that shook.

"They said you might—" she started, and stopped, and he watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, watched the fear and the need fight for the same breath, and need won, because it always does when the thing on the other end of the scale is a child. "My boy. Josper. Fever three days. The physician came and bled him and charged me eight coppers and the fever's worse, and my sister says — she says you can—"

She couldn't finish it. Saying the word *charm* out loud in the open street, after the edict, after the nails in the bakery door — it stuck in her throat like a fishbone.

"Say nothing," Elodie said quietly. "Come to the alley behind the chandlery. Five minutes."

She went. He packed his table — nothing on it, ever, nothing — and stood a moment longer, scanning the lane.

The hooded figure was gone. He told himself that meant nothing. Hooded figures left; lanes emptied; dusk made everyone into shadows. But he found himself checking the mouths of the alleys as he crossed to the chandlery, reading the street the way he read a spoonful of broth, hunting for the wrong note underneath.

Tam was waiting in the dark behind the chandlery, wringing her apron. Up close he could see the fever on her too, the secondary sickness mothers caught from watching — red eyes, cracked lips, three days of not sleeping.

"How old is Josper?" he asked.

"Four." Her voice cracked on the number. "Four in spring."

Four. The age when fevers went into the chest and didn't come back out, when the physician's bleeding accomplished nothing except a lighter purse and a weaker child. Elodie had a fever-bead in his stock. He also had better than a fever-bead, if he was honest with himself, which he tried very hard not to be: he had a working, a real one, salt-and-ash-and-breath, the kind of thing that would get a man burned if the wrong person heard about it. He hadn't made one in months. He'd promised himself he wouldn't again. Small magic, he'd told himself. Nothing that could hang us.

Rent was rent. And four years old was four years old.

"Listen to me," he said, and took her hands to stop the wringing. "What I give you is clay and salt and a word over it. That's all it is. If anyone asks, it's a bead from the apothecary, cost you a copper, and you prayed over it yourself, because praying isn't forbidden yet." Her face twitched — almost a laugh, almost a sob. "You put it in his sleeping shirt, at the throat. You don't tell your sister where it came from. You don't tell anyone. Can you do that?"

"Yes," she whispered. "Yes."

"And Tam." He waited until her eyes came up to his. "If the fever breaks, it broke on its own. Physicians break fevers. Mothers break fevers. Clay doesn't break fevers. Say it back."

"It broke on its own."

"Good." He pressed the bead into her palm and closed her fingers over it, and over it, quiet, so quiet it was barely a sound at all, he gave it the word. He felt the small warmth of it leave him — the working was always warm going out, like blood, like breath in winter — and saw her feel it too, and saw her eyes go wide and wet.

"That's not clay," she breathed.

"That's clay," he said firmly, "that I've overcharged you for. One silver. Rent is rent."

She paid it. She would have paid five. He took the one and hated the whole transaction — the taking, the hiding, the teaching a desperate woman to lie about her own child's rescue — and watched her go down the alley quick and light, lighter than she'd come, carrying hope in a sleeping shirt.

Then he stood alone in the dark behind the chandlery and felt the guilt arrive, right on schedule, braiding itself through the stubbornness like two strands of the same cord. He could not have refused her. He knew that. He also knew that every refusal he failed to make was one more thread connecting him to the pyre, and that somewhere in this city tonight a man was counting ten silver marks against his conscience and finding the marks heavier, and that the hooded figure in the lane had watched him for twenty minutes and gone *somewhere*.

Decency was a debt with interest. He kept paying it anyway. What else was a man supposed to do — become the kind of person who turned away a cooper's wife in an alley? Bettina had burned her herbs rather than be accused, and he understood her, he did, and he also knew he'd have done it differently, and that differently was exactly the road to ash.

He went home the long way, past the bakery, past the edict flapping on its four iron nails. In the dark the paper looked pale as skin. *Suspicion of constitution.* He touched his own wrist without meaning to, where the warden-physicians would one day, if it came to that, find three years of arsenic and hemlock and worse sitting in his blood like a library nobody was meant to read.

His rooms were cold. He lit no lamp. He lifted the floorboard, checked the oilcloth bundles — all there, all quiet — and put them back, and ate bread standing up in the dark, and listened to the city breathe around him: shutters, footsteps, somewhere a baby crying and stopping, and stopping, and stopping, which meant fed or dead, and he made himself believe fed.

* * *

Three nights later, the knock came at his door after midnight, and his heart went into his throat before his feet hit the floor — because honest people did not knock at midnight, and guilty people did not knock at all.

But it was Tam again, hooded, shivering, and this time she didn't wring her apron. This time she grabbed his sleeve and pulled him half out onto the landing and whispered so fast the words ran together:

"The fever broke. Last night. He asked for porridge, Elodie, he *asked for porridge* —" and then her voice dropped lower still, dropped into the register of true fear, the one below gratitude. "But there's a woman been asking after you. At the market. Hooded. Grey cloak. My husband says she talked to the fish-stall men, asked who sells the knots, asked who bought them. He thought you'd want to know."

Grey cloak. Good boots. Patience.

"I didn't tell her anything," Tam said, urgent, gripping his sleeve harder. "I swear it on Josper's life, I said I didn't know any charm-seller, I lied, I lied good—"

"You did right," Elodie said. "Go home. Lock your door. Don't come here again — if the fever comes back, take him to Saint Verity's and pray loudly where people can hear."

She went. He barred the door behind her and stood in the dark of his own rooms, listening to the floorboards settle, thinking about the arithmetic of a hooded woman asking questions in a market full of frightened people, each question worth ten silver marks to whoever answered it honestly.

She wasn't a zealot. Zealots didn't ask the stall-men; zealots went to the wardens. She wasn't a thief; thieves didn't watch for twenty minutes first. She was something patient and well-booted and thorough, and she was mapping him, knot by knot, customer by customer, the way he'd once mapped poisons in the king's breakfast — noting each one, waiting to see which dose proved fatal.

Elodie sat down on the edge of his bed in the dark and did not sleep, and somewhere below the fear, quieter than the fear, an older instinct stirred — the taster's instinct, the one that said: *when something unknown is coming for you, learn its flavor before it reaches your tongue.*

Whoever she was, whatever she wanted — she would find him.

He only hoped, watching the grey line of dawn creep under his door, that he'd taste her purpose before she tasted his blood.
