## 5. The King Who Forgets His Own Name

Maren Voss had spent eleven years learning to read a court the way other scholars read manuscripts — for what had been erased, not what was written. The palace of Ashvale announced itself in gilt and trumpets, but its true text lived in the margins: which name a chamberlain hesitated over, which portrait had been turned to face the wall, which chair at council stood empty and unremarked upon. Erasure had a grammar. She had been fluent in it since the day they stripped her office, her seal, and her access from her, and left her with nothing but the habit of noticing.

So when she entered the presence chamber on the morning of the autumn levee, she did not look at the king first. She looked at the servants.

They were arranged along the walls in their livery of white and gold, thirty of them, still as candlesticks. And every single one of them was watching King Aldric with an expression Maren could only describe as *careful*. Not reverence. Not boredom. Careful — the look of people who had learned that the man on the throne might say anything, and that their survival depended on receiving whatever he said as if it were wisdom.

She had seen that expression before. In the archives, in the marginalia of old chronicles. It was the face courts wore during regencies. During madnesses. During the long quiet years when everyone knew and no one said.

*Well,* she thought, smoothing her plain grey skirts — she had dressed deliberately drab, a disgraced archivist's costume, invisible as dust — *let us see what we are being careful about.*

The herald announced her by a title she no longer held, because the herald's list was three years out of date and no one had bothered to correct it, and this too was a kind of text: the court had stopped updating its records of itself. Institutions forgot the way bodies forgot, cell by cell, and no one marked the moment the patient stopped being whole.

King Aldric sat beneath the canopy with the crown of Ashvale on his head.

That was the first wrong thing. Levees were ceremonial; kings wore the circlet of state for them, the light band of gold and sapphire, not the crown proper. The crown proper — the Hollow Crown, the old chronicles called it, though Maren had never found a chronicle that explained why — was for coronations and funerals. Heavy. Ancient. Its history predating the dynasty, which should have been impossible, and which every archivist before her had filed under *tradition* the way one files away a sound in the night.

He was wearing it to a levee. And he was wearing it the way a man wears a hat two sizes too small — tilted, gripping the arms of the throne, his jaw set as though against pain.

"Approach," said the Lord Chamberlain, and the line of petitioners moved forward, and Maren moved with them, third from the end, holding a petition she did not care about — restoration of access to the western archive annex, technically her right, formally denied — because a petition was the only honest way to stand in a room you were not welcome in.

The first petitioner was a wool merchant asking relief from a river toll. Aldric heard him out. His answers were correct. Warm, even. He had been a beloved king once; the warmth was real, or had been, and it lingered in him like heat in a stone after the fire is gone.

The second petitioner asked for a charter for a hospital. Aldric granted it, and added, unprompted, "My daughter would have liked that. She was always —" and stopped. A small stop. A hairline crack in plaster. The chamberlain murmured something about Her Highness's memory being honored, and the king smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which were fixed on a middle distance where, perhaps, a girl was running through an orchard.

Maren felt the back of her neck prickle. She catalogued the feeling and kept it. Scholars learned early that the body noticed things before the mind consented to.

The third petitioner was a woman petitioning for widow's pension, and it was here, in the ordinary middle of an ordinary morning, that the morning stopped being ordinary.

Aldric looked at her for a long moment. Too long. The chamberlain shifted his weight. And then the king leaned forward, and his face changed — lit up, actually lit up, with an expression so nakedly joyful that several courtiers visibly relaxed, relieved to see their king looking like himself again —

"Sera," he said. "Sera, you came home."

The woman froze. "Your Majesty?"

"You cut your hair." He was almost laughing. "You swore you never would. Come, take off that cloak, let me see — Sera, your mother wrote to me, did I tell you? She wrote that you —"

"Your Majesty." The Lord Chamberlain's voice, gentle and firm, the voice of a man who had done this before. Many times. You could hear the practice in it, the worn-smooth routine of it, and that frightened Maren more than anything else in the room. "This is Mistress Halloway, of Mill Row. Petitioning regarding her pension."

The joy went out of the king's face the way water goes out of a cracked bowl. He looked at the woman — really looked, and Maren watched him do the arithmetic, watched him understand that the daughter he had just greeted was dead, had been dead four years, had died in the fever winter, and that he had known this, had buried her, had — 

"I beg your pardon," Aldric said quietly. "Mistress Halloway. Of course. Your petition is granted. Full pension. Backdated."

And then, under his breath, so softly that only someone standing as close as Maren stood, watching his mouth as closely as she watched everything:

"That's twice this month."

*Twice.* He was counting. The king was keeping count of his own dissolutions, and the crown — 

The crown.

Maren made herself look at it properly. She had been avoiding it, the way one avoids looking directly at the sun or at an accident, and now she understood that the avoidance itself had been data. The crown of Ashvale was old work, pre-dynastic, its circlet wrought in a pattern scholars called merely *archaic*: interlocking bands that seemed, if you let your eye follow them too far, to be turning inward. Spirals feeding spirals. A design that looked less like ornament than like anatomy.

And the king was staring at it. Not wearing it — staring at it, down along the bridge of his own nose, his eyes crossed slightly with the effort, as a man might stare down at his own chest to watch his heart beat. As if it were a part of him. As if it were a *mouth*, and he were waiting to see whether it was open.

It was, Maren thought, the most obscene thing she had ever witnessed, and it was happening in full view of three hundred courtiers, and none of them saw it, because none of them were looking, because looking had been trained out of them, because the court's entire immune system consisted of not-seeing.

The physicians would call it melancholia. They were already calling it that — she had heard them in the antechamber, low-voiced, professional: *the king grieves, the king ages, the king's humors darken.* Grief was a safe diagnosis. Grief honored the dead and flattered the living and required nothing of anyone but sympathy. Melancholia was horror dressed in mourning black, invited in, given a chair.

But grief did not make a man wear his funeral crown to a levee. Grief did not make him count his lapses. And grief — Maren had read enough of the private papers of enough dead kings to know this — grief did not make a monarch lean toward his own crown the way a starving man leans toward a table.

* * *

She got her audience — two minutes, granted by the Chamberlain with the weary courtesy owed to a woman whose disgrace was famous and whose expertise was still occasionally useful — and she used them badly, on purpose.

"Your Majesty petitions are heard," she said, kneeling, eyes down, playing the humble suppliant while doing nothing of the kind. "I am Maren Voss, formerly First Archivist. I have been compiling a genealogy of the crown regalia. For the record."

"A genealogy," Aldric repeated. His voice had gone careful. Careful the way the servants were careful.

"The regalia of Ashvale is older than the dynasty, sire. That is unusual. Most kingdoms' crowns are younger than their kings' bloodlines. Ours is older. Someone made it *for* something, before there was a throne to put it on. I wondered if Your Majesty had ever wondered."

Silence. Long enough that the Chamberlain stirred, preparing to sweep her out.

And then Aldric said, in a voice so quiet and so raw that it did not sound like a king at all, it sounded like a man alone in a room at night:

"Every day. Every day for — I don't know how long. I sit with it on my head and I think: *this is not mine. This has never been mine. Something else is wearing me.*"

The Chamberlain coughed. "Your Majesty tires —"

"Yes," Aldric said, and the shutters came down, the king reassembled over the man like armor closing, plate by plate. "I tire. Thank you, Archivist. Or — former Archivist. Your petition is noted."

Dismissed. But as she rose, curtsied, backed away, Maren saw him do it again — the downward stare, the little tilt, the listening. And she saw, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down her spine, that his lips were moving. Shaping words without voice. Either praying, or answering.

*Something else is wearing me.*

She walked out of the presence chamber on legs that felt borrowed, through corridors that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, past guards who did not see her, and she thought: *I need the ledgers. I need the taster ledgers, the physician rolls, the succession records, all of it, every scrap of the palace's own forgetting, because the king is being eaten alive in front of three hundred witnesses and the witnesses have been taught to applaud.*

There was a pattern. There was always a pattern. Kings of Ashvale died beloved and young-ish and conveniently, one after another after another, going back further than any archivist had thought to ask why, because the question was impolite and archives are polite places.

Maren Voss had stopped being polite the day they took her office.

She reached the archive stairs as the noon bells rang, and it was there, fumbling for her stolen-copied key, that she remembered the detail that would keep her awake for the rest of her life: the king had called his dead daughter *Sera*.

And the servant whose name was Sera — a laundry maid, young, brown-haired, quick — had been dismissed from the presence chamber that same afternoon. Reassigned. Gone by evening, quietly, the way inconvenient things go in palaces.

Because the crown, Maren thought, turning her key in the lock, does not like to be reminded of names it has already eaten.
