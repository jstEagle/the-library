## 6. Denounced

The knock came at dusk, and Elodie knew before he opened the door.

Not because of anything mystical — his gift had never worked that way, no tingling warnings, no whispers of fate. He knew because of the *quality* of the knocking. Three raps, spaced too evenly, delivered by someone who knocked for a living. Bettina knocked like a neighbor, quick and apologetic. Wren's father, Tam Voss — no relation to Maren, though Ashvale was small enough that everyone assumed otherwise — knocked like a man asking to be let into a harbor. But this knock belonged to men who expected doors to open for them, and were merely giving the door a chance to be polite about it.

Elodie set down the fever-charm he'd been retying — the third one that week, ash-colored thread on river-stone — and looked around his single room above the chandler's shop. The floorboards under the bed. The loose brick behind the stove. The hollow in the chimney where smoke had eaten away the mortar. His stock was already hidden; it had been hidden since the edict went up on the bakery door, since Bettina fed her life's work to a street fire with her jaw tight and her eyes anywhere but the flames.

He opened the door.

Four wardens in the grey-and-silver of the Crown Hunt, and behind them, half-hidden in the stairwell shadow, a face he knew.

Tam Collier. Wren's father. A broad man gone soft at the middle, hands like knotted rope, a beard he trimmed with the same knife he used for gutting fish. He'd bought a luck-knot from Elodie two years ago for his wife's safety on the crossing to Sennet Isle. He'd bought another when Wren was born — no, that had been before Elodie's time. He'd stood in this very doorway last month, laughing, saying *you've got your grandmother's hands, boy, steady as tide-work*, and Elodie had felt something rare and warm: being known. Being trusted by someone who remembered his grandmother, who could say her name without spitting.

Now Tam wouldn't look at him. He stared at the wall beside Elodie's head as if there were something fascinating written there.

"Elodie Fairweather," said the lead warden — a woman, tall, grey at the temples, with the bored voice of someone reading a list she'd read a hundred times. "By order of the Crown Hunt, you are detained for questioning regarding suspicion of the gifted arts."

"I'm a food-taster," Elodie said. It came out steady. Good. Three years of swallowing poison taught you how to keep your voice level while everything inside you screamed. "Royal warrant. I taste for the palace."

"We know what you are." She produced a folded paper. Iron-gall ink, official seal. "We also know what you sell."

*What you sell.* Not *what they say you sell*. The distinction landed cold.

"May I see—"

"You may step out of the doorway."

They didn't shove him. That was almost worse. They moved through his room with the terrible courtesy of professionals, lifting his mattress, running fingers along shelf-joins, tapping walls in the rhythm warden-tappers used — listening for the hollow note of hidden things. Elodie stood in the corner with his heart going like a gull in a storm and catalogued, uselessly, everything they would find if they were good: nothing. The charms were under the floorboards beneath the bed frame, wrapped in oilcloth, buried under a false layer of salt-fish he kept for exactly this reason. The fever-charm in his hand — he'd palmed it when he opened the door, and now it sat against his palm like a coal, and he couldn't drop it, couldn't be seen dropping it, so he just held it and waited for his hand to sweat it into evidence.

The woman warden crouched at the hearth. Tapped the chimney brick. Elodie's whole life narrowed to the sound of her knuckle on stone.

Solid. Solid. Solid.

She moved on.

"Nothing here," said one of the younger wardens, disappointed.

"Nothing visible," the woman corrected. She turned to Elodie, and for the first time really looked at him — not at the room, at him, with the flat professional attention of someone assessing livestock. "Fairweather. You're the palace taster. The one who doesn't sicken."

"I'm careful."

"Three years on the rotation. Arsenic twice. Belladonna once, in the Hallemas vintage. Hemlock extract in the spring, before the physician caught it." She recited it like a grocer listing prices. "Men have died from a tenth of that load. You walk home every evening and buy herring."

"The palace pays me to swallow what would kill others. That's the trade."

"Mm." She glanced at Tam, still frozen in the stairwell. "And you say he sold you a charm."

Tam's mouth worked. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough as net-cord. "A luck-knot. For my boat. Two years back."

"That's not a charm. That's knotwork. Fishermen tie knots for luck all the time."

"It was *tied different*," Tam said, and now he did look at Elodie, and Elodie wished to God he hadn't, because there was no hatred in the man's face. There was something much worse. Shame, wearing desperation like a coat too big for it. "I told them it was tied different. I told them what he said when he gave it over. He said—" Tam's voice cracked and he pushed through it, hating himself audibly, "—he said, *keep it dry and keep it secret, and the sea will keep what's yours.*"

Silence. Even the wardens seemed to understand they were standing inside something that had broken.

"Keep it secret," the woman repeated softly. "Words of binding. Classic hedge-form." She looked back at Elodie, almost apologetic. "You'll come with us. Questions first. The Mint has cells for those awaiting examination."

"And if I'm cleared?"

"Then you're cleared." She said it the way people say *if it rains, it rains.*

They took him down the stairs past Tam, and in the narrow turn of the stairwell their shoulders nearly touched, and Elodie stopped. He couldn't help it. One question, burning worse than any poison he'd ever swallowed.

"Was it the silver?"

Tam flinched. "No." And then, quieter, wrecked: "Wren's sick. Fevered three days. The physicker wants a crown a visit and I've got four children and a boat that leaks." He finally met Elodie's eyes and it was unbearable, because Elodie understood instantly, completely, in one sick lurch: Tam hadn't sold him for greed. He'd sold him because Elodie was the only person Tam knew who might actually be able to cure a fever with thread and stone — and Tam couldn't ask, not after the edict, not with wardens paying silver for names. So he'd done the arithmetic that fear makes us do. Sold the charm-maker to get the charm-maker's help dragged into reach. Or maybe just to survive. Maybe both. Fear doesn't itemize.

"You could have asked me," Elodie said quietly. "For Wren. You never needed coin. You never needed to give them my name."

"I know." Tam's eyes shone. "That's why I can't—" He turned away. "I'm sorry, boy. Your grandmother, she'd have—"

"Don't," Elodie said. "Don't say her name today."

* * *

The cells beneath the Mint smelled of wet stone and old iron and, faintly, of ink — the particular sour tang of the interrogation ledgers, which were kept close because paper was power here. They took his boots, his belt, the fever-charm from his palm (the woman warden turned it over twice, sniffed it, and set it aside with a grunt that might have meant anything), and put him in a cell with a straw pallet and a slot-window at street level, through which he could see, if he pressed his cheek to the cold stone, the ankles of passersby and the occasional dog.

He could also hear the market. Faintly. The evening cries winding down. Somewhere a fishwife arguing about mackerel. The ordinary music of Ashvale going about its business while he sat in a hole for the crime of having steady hands.

He should have been afraid. He was, distantly, the way you're afraid of weather — aware of the storm front moving in, the pressure change, the certainty of it. But the sharper feeling, the one that kept him pressing his cheek to the stone and breathing through his mouth, was the betrayal itself, working through him like a slow venom.

Because here was the thing Elodie kept circling: he wasn't angry about the arrest. Arrest had always been the tide-line of his life. Sell charms long enough in a city that burns the gifted, and the arithmetic only has one sum. He'd known it. He'd kept selling anyway, because rent is rent and because a fever-charm in a child's chest is worth more than a caution. He'd made his peace with the risk the way sailors make peace with drowning — respectfully, daily, without expecting it to be painless.

But Tam. Tam had been *kind* to him. Tam had spoken his grandmother's name with respect, had brought him smoked eel at midwinter two years running, had once told Wren — Elodie had heard him, through an open window — *that's the Fairweather boy, from good stock, you mind your manners near him.* And all of that warmth had been real, Elodie was sure of it. That was the poison in it. Tam hadn't been wearing a mask for two years. Tam had simply been a man standing on a deck in rising water, and when the moment came, he'd thrown over the nearest thing that floated instead of his own child.

Which was, Elodie admitted to the dark, exactly what Elodie himself might have done. And that was the worst discovery of the night: not that Tam was a traitor, but that Tam was *ordinary*, and ordinary men denounce each other, and the edict hadn't created monsters. It had just handed every drowning man a price list.

He slept badly, in snatches, waking each time to check that his hands still felt like his own.

* * *

Morning brought bread — coarse, stale-edged, but bread — and water, and company.

The woman across the corridor had been singing when they brought him up to the day-cell, a low looping tune he didn't recognize, and she stopped when she saw him look over. Middle-aged, forearms like a laundress's, calm as a millpond. "Midwife," she said, by way of introduction, as if answering a question. "Sera Unn. You?"

"Elodie. Taster."

Her eyebrows rose. "The one who doesn't die."

"News travels even down here."

"Especially down here." She shifted closer to the bars separating their cells. Up close he could see she had ink stains on her fingers, of all things — a midwife who kept records. "They took the boy this morning. The whistler."

"What boy?"

"Little lad from Tanner's Row. Whistles and the wind answers. Or so his neighbors say." Her voice was dry as old vellum. "Personally I think the wind answers anyone who listens properly, but try telling the Hunt that."

Down the row, a smaller voice, thin and defiant: "It *does* answer."

Elodie turned. In the far cell, a boy — eleven, maybe twelve, knees drawn up, hair cropped short for lice — regarded him with the flat challenge of a child who has already decided adults are useless. "It answered me when my da died. Came right down the chimney. So they can say what they like."

"It answered you," Elodie agreed gravely. "Wind's like that. Picks its favorites."

The boy studied him, deciding whether he was being mocked. Whatever he found in Elodie's face, he apparently approved, because he uncurled slightly. "You're the taster. My mam says you eat poison for the king like it's porridge."

"Not quite porridge."

"Why don't you die, then?"

"Sera Unn asked the same question," the midwife observed.

"Did she?" Elodie leaned his head back against the stone. Above, faint through the ceiling, boots crossed the Mint floor — the interrogators arriving, the day's machinery starting. "Honest answer? I don't know. I've wondered myself. Three years of the rotation and my blood just... takes it. The physicians call it constitution. Write it in the ledger like it's a virtue."

"And is it?" Sera Unn asked. There was something underneath the question, some current he couldn't read — interest sharpening into something hungrier.

"My grandmother used to say nothing about a body is a virtue. Everything's a trade." He smiled thinly. "I thought she meant it about fish prices."

The boy laughed — a bright, startling sound in the dark — and then hushed himself, glancing at the ceiling, and the laughter dying in a child's throat in a government cellar did more to make Elodie afraid than the arrest had managed all night.

Boots on the stairs. The door at the top opening. A voice calling down, unhurried:

"Fairweather. Captain Rell will see you now."

* * *

Captain Rell turned out to be nothing like Elodie had imagined during the long dark hours — he'd pictured either a brute or a fanatic, and got instead a tired man of forty-odd with ink on his cuff and the posture of someone whose back hurt from sitting at desks. His office above the cells was clean, well-lit, and stacked with papers organized with a precision that bordered on devotion.

"Sit," Rell said, and poured two cups of chicory from a pot on his stove, sliding one across. "It's terrible. Drink it anyway."

Elodie drank it. It was terrible.

Rell opened a ledger, ran a finger down a column. "Elodie Fairweather. Twenty-six. Royal food-taster, three years' service, commendation from the Master of Household for diligence. No prior charges. No denounced associations on record until—" he turned the page, "—yesterday. One informant. Name withheld per statute." He looked up. "You know who it was."

"Yes."

"Does it matter to you that I can't tell you whether the information was accurate?"

"It was accurate."

Rell's pen paused. "We searched your rooms. We found nothing."

"I'm careful."

"So I've heard." Rell set down the pen and laced his fingers, studying Elodie with an expression that wasn't cruelty or even suspicion — it was curiosity, pure and disciplined, the look of a man doing sums that wouldn't balance. "Here's my problem, taster. The Hunt exists to find the gifted. Gifted means: does things bodies can't do. Now, most of the people in my cells are liars, madmen, or unlucky — a woman whose bread rose wrong, a man whose cow birthed a two-headed calf, as if the cow answers to me." He gestured vaguely downward, toward the cells, toward the boy and the midwife. "But you. You're documented. Three years of poisons that kill trained men, and your bloodwork reads clean. The palace physicians wrote 'remarkable constitution' and moved on, because the alternative questions were inconvenient." He leaned forward. "So I'm asking the inconvenient question. Why don't you sicken, Fairweather?"

"Good fortune," Elodie said. "And a strong stomach."

"Both of those run out." Rell tapped the ledger. "I've requested your bloodwork records from the palace archive. All three years. There's a pattern in them I want to see with my own eyes. Until then, you stay below. Suspicion of constitution — it's not a charge, technically. It's a holding category. I invented it last month because the edict forces me to hold people I can't charge, and I refuse to write 'witch' next to a name I can't prove anything about."

Something loosened slightly in Elodie's chest — not trust, nothing like trust, but the recognition of a fellow creature trapped in the same machine, doing his own arithmetic. "And if your pattern shows something?"

Rell was quiet a moment. When he answered, he looked at the wall rather than at Elodie, and his voice dropped into something almost private. "Then I'll have to decide what I serve. The crown, or whatever's wearing it." He caught himself, straightened, the desk-man armor clicking back into place. "That was off the record. There is no record. Go back downstairs."

At the cell door, Elodie paused. "Captain. The boy who whistles. And the midwife. They're—"

"On my list for examination tomorrow." Rell's jaw tightened. "Examination. Yes."

The word sat between them, heavy as a pyre log.

Elodie went back down the stairs to the smell of wet stone and ink, past Sera Unn's cell, past the boy's. He pressed his cheek to the slot-window again. Above, morning traffic. A fish-cart rattling past. And there — he'd know that stride anywhere, that rolling gait built by thirty years of decks — Tam Collier crossing the lane toward the Mint entrance, a small paper-wrapped parcel in his hand, and Wren's red scarf looped around his neck.

Coming to bring food to the daughter of the man he'd sold. Or coming to beg forgiveness. Or both. With Tam, Elodie suspected, it would always be both.

He watched until the ankles disappeared through the door, and lay back on the pallet, and did the only thing left to do with betrayal: carried it.

Somewhere above, a bell rang the hour, and in the corridor a warden's voice said, bored, to someone new: "The archivist is here again. Third time this week. Says she needs the taster's bloodwork."

Footsteps descending. Measured. Patient. The steps of a woman who had already read the ledger, and found the pattern, and knew exactly what lived in Elodie Fairweather's veins.

The lock of the day-cell clanked open.
