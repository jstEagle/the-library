## 7. Cells Beneath the Mint

They took him down through the Mint itself.

Elodie had walked past the Royal Mint a hundred times — a squat grey building wedged between the tanners' row and the canal, always smelling of hot metal and horse dung, its windows too high to see through. He had never wondered what was beneath it. Now he knew: a staircase of worn granite descending farther than any cellar had a right to go, lit by oil lamps set in niches every ten steps, each flame burning perfectly still, as if the air itself had been taught not to move.

The warden ahead of him didn't speak. The one behind kept a hand near the short iron baton at his belt, though Elodie's wrists were bound in front of him with cord, not chain, and he'd offered no resistance since they'd marched him out of his own doorway with his breakfast still half-eaten on the table. He'd thought about resisting. He'd thought about it the whole way through the streets, while neighbors watched from windows and pretended to sweep their steps. But there had been four wardens, and Bettina had been among the watchers, and her face when she saw him — white, stricken, one hand pressed flat against her doorframe as if to hold herself upright — had told him everything about what resistance would buy him. Nothing. And it would cost Bettina her composure in front of the wrong eyes.

So he'd walked. Down the stairs. Past the lamps that wouldn't flicker.

At the bottom was a corridor of cells cut into the living rock beneath the Mint — because stone remembers nothing, Elodie realized, and understood the design. No wood. No plaster. No cloth older than a season. If you were going to interrogate people for magic, you built your prison out of things that couldn't hold an echo.

"Cell nine," said the warden behind him. His voice was young. He sounded like he was reciting it to keep from thinking about it.

They put him inside and barred the door, and then there was only the dark, and the lamp outside his cell throwing a blade of light across the floor, and the sound of his own breathing.

Elodie sat down against the wall and did what he always did when the world went sideways: he catalogued.

Stone dust. Wet granite. Lamp oil, cheap, cut with something resinous — pine, maybe, or whatever the Mint bought in bulk. Sweat. His own fear-sweat, which he could taste at the back of his throat like copper coins left in milk. And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, coming through the rock itself: ink. Iron gall ink, gallons of it, the sharp vinegar-and-oak-apple bite of it soaked so deep into the stones above that three floors of rock couldn't muffle it.

Of course, he thought. They mint coins down there, but they *count* them up top. Ledgers. Years and years of ledgers.

It was a stupid thing to find comforting. He found it comforting anyway. Somewhere overhead, people were writing things down — names, amounts, dates — and a world where someone was keeping records felt, for the moment, like a world where he might still exist in it tomorrow.

* * *

He didn't know how long he'd been in the cell when the whistling started.

It came from somewhere to his left, through the rock — thin, tuneless, more breath than melody, the way a boy whistles when he isn't trying to make music, just to prove the air is still his. It wandered up and down without settling anywhere. Then it stopped. Then it started again, and this time Elodie noticed something strange about it: the lamp in the corridor guttered. Just once. A single stutter of flame in time with the whistle, like a dog turning its head toward a sound.

Then it steadied again, and the whistling resumed, and Elodie sat very still in the dark and told himself he had imagined it.

"You hear it too, then."

The voice came from his right, low and dry, an older woman's voice roughened by smoke or years or both. Through the wall, close enough that he could feel the vibration of it.

"The lamp," she said, when he didn't answer immediately. "Did it jump?"

"I don't know what I saw," Elodie said carefully.

"Hm." A pause. "Smart. Dumb. Depends what they do with us next." Another pause, longer. "You're the taster."

His stomach dropped. "I don't—"

"Don't bother. I'm Sera Ostwick. Midwife. Forty years attending births in Lowbridge and the Tanner Quarter, and eleven days in this hole, and I'll tell you how I know: they brought you past my cell this morning and the wardens weren't holding you like a criminal. They were holding you like a specimen. Nobody marches a pickpocket down here gentle." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Also, you're famous. Fairweather, the king's tongue. Half the market says you can taste poison like wine."

"That part's true," Elodie admitted. "The rest is embellishment."

"And is it true they found nothing in your rooms? Not one charm? Not one sigil, not one dried herb?"

"They found flour," Elodie said. "And salt. And some very old tea."

Silence from the other cell. Then: "You're telling me you're clean."

"I'm telling you they found nothing."

"That's not the same question, taster."

"No," Elodie agreed quietly. "It isn't."

* * *

They brought food at what he guessed was evening — a bowl of barley gruel passed through a slot in the door by the young-voiced warden, who also, without looking at anyone, whispered "there's a draft near the ceiling, third stone, it helps" before moving on. Elodie filed that away. The gruel was terrible. He ate it anyway and catalogued it thoroughly — barley gone stale, water from the canal cistern, boiled too fast, no salt — because cataloguing was the rope he climbed down into sleep on.

In the morning — or what he decided was morning, based on the shift-change footsteps — the whistling boy was brought past.

Elodie heard him before he saw him: two sets of boots, one dragging, and between them a sound like wind caught in a keyhole. The warden said "keep walking," and the dragging feet stopped anyway, right at the gap where Elodie's cell door met its frame, and through that finger-width of space a face appeared. Twelve years old, maybe thirteen. Hollow-cheeked, filthy, hair cropped short and unevenly, the way they cut it in the charity houses. His eyes were enormous in the lamplight and entirely unafraid.

"You're the poison man," the boy said.

"Taster," Elodie said. "What's your name?"

The boy grinned, quick and bright, showing a missing canine. "Tam. You want to hear something?"

"Tam—" started the warden.

But the boy was already whistling, and this time it wasn't tuneless. It was a rising note, held long and steady, and Elodie felt it in his teeth the way you feel a struck tuning fork, and every lamp in the corridor — all six of them, the whole length of the hall — leaned. Bowed. Bent their flames toward the boy like grass toward sun, and held there, quivering, for the length of one held breath.

Then Tam ran out of air, laughed, and let the note fall, and the flames stood back up straight as soldiers.

"Wind answers me," he said, matter-of-fact. "Has since I was small. Mam said it was just good lungs." The grin faded. "Mam sold me to the hunters for eight marks. Said it was better than the alternative. She cried about it after, I think. Or before. Hard to tell with her."

"Tam," the warden said, and there was something raw under the routine of it, "come on, lad."

The boy looked at Elodie once more through the gap. "You don't smell scared," he said. "Everybody down here smells scared. Even the midwife, and she pretends best." He cocked his head. "You smell like the palace. Like pennies and candle wax. How'd you get palace-smell, poison man?"

"Tam." The warden's hand closed on the boy's shoulder, gently, and turned him away. "Come on."

Elodie sat back against the cold wall and pressed his palms flat to the stone and breathed until his heart stopped hammering.

*Straighten up,* he told himself. *You've swallowed enough arsenic to kill a platoon. You are not going to be undone by a child with good lungs.*

Except the lamps had bent. He hadn't imagined that. And somewhere in the marrow-deep quiet of himself, in the place where the poisons lived and pooled and had never once made him sick, something had turned over in its sleep — slow, heavy, attentive — the way a great fish moves far below a boat.

*Not now,* he told it. *Whatever you are. Not now.*

* * *

Captain Rell came on the third day.

Elodie heard him before he saw him too, but not by sound — by absence. The corridor had a rhythm: wardens shuffling, prisoners coughing, Tam whistling, the midwife muttering half-prayers to saints she probably didn't believe in anymore. When Rell arrived, all of it went quiet at once, the way birdsong stops before weather. Even the lamps seemed to burn straighter.

He was a broad man, grey at the temples, in a hunter's black-and-silver that fit him like it had been grown rather than tailored. His face was the problem. Elodie had expected cruelty — the market was full of stories about what the hunters did in the cells beneath the Mint, most of them exaggerated, none of them reassuring. What he got instead was tiredness, and behind the tiredness, worse: attention. Rell looked at people the way Elodie tasted food. Completely. Without flinching from what he found.

They put Elodie in the interrogation room, which was a lie of a name — just another cell with a table and two stools and a lamp bolted to the wall. Rell sat down across from him, laid a folder on the table, and did not open it.

"Do you know why you're here?" he asked.

"Suspicion of constitution," Elodie said. "I heard the charge read. It's a new one on me."

"It's new on everyone. I helped write it." Rell's voice was flat, unhurried. "The edict covers 'the gifted.' Charms, sigils, hedge-work. But the Crown wanted a category for people who are wrong in ways we can't yet name. Suspicion of constitution. It means: something about this person does not add up, and I would like to know what." He opened the folder at last. "Your turn to be a category. Congratulations."

He turned the folder around. Inside were pages Elodie recognized with a lurch — the taster ledgers, copied in a clerk's neat hand. Three years of them. Every dish, every suspicion, every trace.

"Arsenic," Rell said. "March, two years ago. Enough in a single plate of partridge to kill a grown man inside the hour. You reported 'a bitterness, faint, possibly the glaze.'" He flipped a page. "Monkshood, in a honey-cake, intended — we now believe — for the Lord Protector himself. You flagged it within seconds. Ate a full portion during verification, per protocol." Another page. "White mercury salts. Hemlock extract. Concentrated ergot. Taster Fairweather, I have read your file twice, and I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to understand that I am not asking it as a hunter."

He folded his hands on the table.

"Why aren't you dead?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, faint through tons of rock, a bell rang the hour.

"I don't know," Elodie said. Because it was true, mostly, and because he was very good by now at saying true things that concealed other things.

Rell studied him. It wasn't pleasant. Being looked at that way was like being swallowed slowly, with commentary.

"You're lying," Rell said. "But it's an honest kind of lying. You don't know *why* you don't know." He leaned back. "Let me tell you what I think, and you can correct me. I think you've known something is wrong with you for a long time. I think you hide it well, because hiding is how you survived the market districts, and the market districts teach hiding better than any school. And I think—" he tapped the ledger pages, once, lightly— "that whatever is wrong with you, it started before the poisoning. The poisoning is just the loudest symptom."

Elodie said nothing. His hands, he noticed, were steady. Three years of tasting for a paranoid crown had trained even his hands.

"My daughter gets fevers," Rell said abruptly.

The non sequitur landed strangely in the bare room. "Sir?"

"She gets fevers. Bad ones. Four, five nights running, and the physicians shrug, and my wife sits up with her wringing cloth in cool water, and last winter a woman came to our street — a charm-seller, a *witch*, by the edict's definition — and she sold my wife a knot of red thread for a crust of bread. My wife hung it over the child's bed." He paused. "The fevers stopped."

"Coincidence," Elodie said, very evenly, "is common."

"Yes," Rell agreed. "It usually is." He gathered the folder, stood, and for a moment stood looking down at Elodie with an expression that had gone somewhere past duty into a country he clearly didn't have a map for. "Here's what happens now. Your case goes to review. Review, in the current climate, means ash unless someone speaks for you. Do you have someone who might speak for you, taster?"

"No one," Elodie said. And it should have been despair, saying it. It felt instead like handing over the last coin in his purse and finding his pocket lighter but his back straighter.

Rell nodded slowly. At the door he stopped, hand on the bar, and spoke without turning around.

"The boy who whistles. Tam. He's twelve. Under the edict, gifted children are remanded to the Crown's custody for 'assessment.'" A silence. "There is no assessment. There's a yard behind the Mint and there's a pyre schedule and there's a clerk who writes *remanded* in one column and *concluded* in another, and I sign both columns, and last night I counted how many names are in each." He pulled the bar. "Eat your gruel, Fairweather. Whatever's wrong with you — I hope it keeps you alive a while yet. Someone in this building ought to be."

* * *

That night the cells talked.

It started with the midwife, Sera, tapping on the wall — a rhythm, deliberate, and after a moment Tam answered it from further down, and Elodie realized they'd worked out a code in eleven days, because people do, because solidarity grows in the dark like mold and just as stubbornly.

"He asked you why you don't die," Sera's voice came through the stone, dry as paper. "Didn't he. I heard the ledger pages. That's the same question they asked me, first day. Forty years of births and not one mother lost under my hands — not one, in a city where one in five goes to God — and they asked me how. As if doing your work *well* was the crime."

"What did you tell them?" Elodie asked.

"I told them clean hands come from washing them." A pause; the dry laugh again, sadder this time. "They wrote that down. Probably as evidence."

From further along came Tam's whistle, soft, aimless — and the lamp outside Elodie's cell swayed gently toward the sound and back, like something nodding off to music. Elodie listened to it and felt the strangest sensation rise in his chest, warm and terrible at once. Three years of double life, of selling luck-knots with his eyes on the door, of tasting poison alone in a marble pantry while courtiers smiled at him without seeing him — and here, in the worst place he had ever been, surrounded by a woman who delivered babies and refused to apologize for keeping them alive, and a boy whose lungs could bow fire, he felt less alone than he had in the whole gilded stretch of those years.

"We should stop meeting like this," he said to the wall.

Sera snorted. Tam laughed, a real laugh, echoing down the corridor, and every lamp flickered with it, and for a moment — one absurd, borrowed moment — the cells beneath the Mint almost felt like a kitchen.

Then boots in the corridor. Many boots, unhurried, and a voice Elodie didn't know giving orders in the clipped cadence of the upper palace, and the young warden unlocking Elodie's door with hands that shook slightly.

"On your feet," the warden said. "You're being transferred."

"Transferred where?" Sera called sharply through the wall. "Transferred *where*?"

"To the Archivist's Tower," said the newcomer at the door — a woman in plain scholar's grey with hunter credentials pinned at her collar, credentials so crisp they looked drawn rather than issued, held by a face that was doing rapid, furious arithmetic behind calm eyes. She looked at Elodie the way a person looks at a document she has risked everything to requisition.

"Fairweather," she said. "My name is Maren Voss. You're mine now — officially a research asset, unofficially the answer to a question I've spent nine months asking." Her gaze flicked once, quickly, to the ledger copies still lying in Rell's abandoned interrogation room, and back. "Walk with me, and don't ask questions until we're above ground. And taster—" she paused, and for just a moment the bureaucratic mask slipped, and underneath it was something urgent and almost afraid,

"—your grandmother's name was Wrenna Fairweather. We need to talk about what she buried."
