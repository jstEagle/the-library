## 8. An Offer Written in Iron Gall

The Lord Protector's office smelled of tallow and old decisions.

Maren Voss had been waiting outside it for two hours, long enough to memorize the grain of the door, the pattern of water stains on the ceiling plaster, and the exact rhythm of the guard's boots in the corridor — left foot heavier than the right, a man favoring an old knee injury, probably from the border campaigns. She catalogued these things because cataloguing was what she did instead of praying. It had never once worked as well.

At the fourth hour, the door opened.

"Voss." The Lord Protector's secretary was a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular pallor of someone who read everything his master wrote. "He'll see you now. You have until the bell."

"Generous," Maren said, and went in.

Corvane Ashgrave, Lord Protector of Ashvale, did not look up when she entered. He was writing — or performing the act of writing, she suspected, the way some men performed piety — with a heavy iron-gall pen that scratched like something small dying on the page. The office behind him was all dark wood and darker maps. A fire burned too hot for the season. Everything about the room said: *I am busy being powerful; your presence is an interruption I am permitting.*

Maren had spent eleven years in the royal archives. She knew how to wait out a performance.

She stood. She waited. She counted the scratches of his pen — forty-one before he finally set it down and looked at her with the flat gray eyes of a man who had executed his first traitor at nineteen and found the work agreeable.

"The disgraced archivist," he said. "You have some nerve petitioning this office."

"I have some *information*, my lord. The nerve is incidental."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not amusement. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man who kept a kennel of clever dogs and appreciated one that bared its teeth correctly.

"You were dismissed from court service four years ago," Corvane said. "Falsifying provenance records. Selling archive access to collectors. Your name is a byword for—"

"For inconvenient accuracy," Maren said. "Yes. That's the official version. I've read it. I wrote half of it myself, actually, before they decided to write the rest for me."

The pen rolled slightly under his fingers. He let it.

"You petitioned for an audience," he said, "on the matter of His Majesty's health. The College of Physicians has already briefed me. Melancholia. Grief for the late queen. Age."

"The College of Physicians," Maren said, "has never once looked inside a king's head, because none of them can. I have. Or rather — I've read what's been written there. Four hundred years of it."

She took out the ledger.

It was a modest thing, calf-bound, its spine cracked white with age. She laid it on the desk between them with the care of a woman laying down a card she'd been holding close to her chest for three days without sleep.

"What is this?"

"Taster ledgers. Royal food-tasters are examined after every service — blood drawn, humors charted, reactions recorded. It's bureaucratic immortality, my lord. Every poison that ever touched a royal table is written down in these books, along with who swallowed it and what it did to them." She opened it to a ribboned page. "This is the current taster's record. Elodie Fairweather. Three years of service. Read the entries."

Corvane read. Maren watched him read — watched the gray eyes move down columns of dates and substances and outcomes, watched the performance of boredom falter somewhere around the second column and not recover.

"Arsenic," he said slowly. "Twice. Hemlock extract. Belladonna in the winter wines. Monkshood—" He stopped. Looked up. "These doses would kill a horse."

"These doses killed three previous tasters," Maren said. "Their records are in the same volume. Fairweather's bloodwork defies every entry that precedes it. Substances that should have dropped him where he stood register as *nothing*. Not resistance. Not tolerance. Nothing. As if his body had simply... agreed to be poisoned and declined to notice."

"An unusual constitution."

"An impossible one." She turned the page. "Unless you understand what constitution actually means, my lord. What a body is. What it *holds*."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the bells began marking the quarter-hour — the sound of her audience bleeding out.

"I don't have time for hedge-philosophy," Corvane said.

"No," Maren agreed. "You have time for exactly one thing: keeping the crown working. So consider this. His Majesty called Lady Amaranth Vey by the name of a scullery maid last week. He signed a warrant with his left hand. He stared at his own crown for eleven minutes — I timed it from the gallery — as if it were something that might eat him. And yesterday he asked the Master of Coin whether his daughter had come in from the orchard. His daughter, my lord, has been dead six years."

"Melancholia."

"Grief doesn't progress in *stages of forgetting upward*, Lord Protector. Kings forget servants. They do not forget their daughters first and their own names last. This isn't a mind failing. It's a mind being *consumed* — systematically, from the top down, in order of preciousness. Something is eating Aldric Ashvale alive, and it started with the parts of him that made him loved."

Silence. The pen had stopped rolling.

"And your evidence," Corvane said at last, "is a book."

"My evidence is four hundred years of books." She pulled the second ledger from her satchel — older, water-damaged, its margins dense with ciphered notes. "Every host king follows the same arc. Beloved, then hollow, then conveniently dead within five years of the hollowing. Then a succession — always smooth, always suspiciously unopposed — and the new king is beloved again. Do you know how many dynasties have ruled Ashvale, my lord? Officially? Three. Unofficially?" She tapped the ledger. "One. It's not a family. It's a *tenant*."

She hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. It was the kind of sentence that got archivists burned. But Corvane didn't reach for the bell-pull. He leaned back instead, and Maren recognized the posture with a cold drop in her stomach: the posture of a man recalculating.

Because here was the thing about Corvane Ashgrave that the court gossip never got right — he wasn't a zealot. Zealots were easy. He was a *caretaker*, the worst kind of man to bargain with, because he genuinely believed the realm was his patient and any treatment was justified. The witch-hunts, the pyres in the market square, the edict nailed to bakery doors — those weren't cruelty to him. They were surgery.

And Maren was about to ask a surgeon to keep one knife sheathed.

"There is a thing under the palace," she said. "Older than the dynasty. It wears the crown the way a hermit crab wears a shell, and it feeds on inherited memory — which is why it needs kings, and why each succession makes it stronger. It was bound once, generations ago, by a woman named Fairweather. A binder. Her notes are in this ledger, in cipher, and I've broken most of the cipher." She met his eyes. "And her bloodline still runs in Ashvale. In one man. The taster whose blood won't hold poison — because it's spent three years learning to hold *him* instead."

"The taster is in the cells beneath the Mint."

"Yes."

"Denounced by his own customers. Suspicion of constitution." Corvane smiled thinly. "My hunters are more perceptive than you credit them, Voss. They caught the one man in Ashvale who matters, and they don't even know it."

"They'll burn him Thursday."

"They burn the guilty on Thursdays."

"He's not guilty. He's the only weapon in this city that works against the thing wearing your king." Maren leaned forward, palms flat on four hundred years of evidence. "Release him into my custody. Formally. As a research asset — my credentials may be disgraced but they're still valid enough to file the paperwork, and you have the authority to suspend a burning with a signature. I will deliver you the Hollow's true name, its binding rite, and a king who remembers his own daughter's face. Or I will deliver you nothing, and you can go back to burning midwives while the real monster learns your name next."

The bells finished. The room went very quiet.

Corvane rose. He walked to the window — the window that overlooked the Mint, though the cells themselves were underground and invisible, which Maren suspected was the entire point of the view. When he spoke, he didn't turn around.

"Do you know what happens if you're wrong, Voss? If this 'Hollow' is a story you built out of marginalia and grief? I release a convicted charmer into the custody of a disgraced archivist, the taster disappears into your tower, and when the king dies — and he *will* die, melancholia or no — I answer for it. My neck. My office. Possibly my ash."

"Yes."

"That's your argument? *Yes?*"

"My argument is arithmetic, my lord. Weigh it with me." She stood, and her voice stayed level through sheer professional discipline. "If I'm wrong, you lose one taster — a man the pyres were going to take anyway — and you lose me, and I'm already ash in every way that counts. Two lives against the possibility — the *demonstrated, documented, four-hundred-years-documented possibility* — that the crown itself is the enemy. If I'm right and you refuse, you keep your clean hands and your clean pyres, and you preside over the death of the last person alive who can save the king you're supposed to be protecting." She paused. "That's the whole ledger. There are no other entries."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire, eating its fuel the way everything in this city ate.

Then Corvane turned. He crossed to the desk, drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, and dipped the iron-gall pen. He wrote quickly, in the cramped hand of a man who had signed too many death warrants to waste ink on mercy — and yet this was mercy, of a kind, and they both knew it, and both of them would have denied it under oath.

"*By order of the Lord Protector,*" he read aloud as he wrote, "*the prisoner designated Fairweather, E., taster, is remanded to the custody of M. Voss, archivist emerita, for purposes of research pertaining to His Majesty's condition. Said prisoner remains property of the Crown. Said research will report weekly. Failure of said research to produce results within thirty days returns prisoner and custodian both to standard procedure.*"

He blotted it. Held it out. Did not let go when she took it.

"Standard procedure," he said quietly, "is ash. For both of you. Understand that I am not saving anyone, Voss. I am *investing*. And if your parasite turns out to be a fairy tale, I will feed you both to it personally — metaphorically speaking — and sleep soundly after."

"I understand completely," Maren said. "That's what makes you dangerous."

His eyes narrowed. She took the order and left before he could decide to be insulted.

* * *

The corridor outside was dimmer than she remembered, and colder. Maren walked it fast, the order pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and only allowed herself to stop when she reached the stairwell down toward the Mint — the stairs she'd need to descend within the hour, papers in hand, to collect a stranger whose blood was a map and whose grandmother was a ghost.

She stopped because her hands were shaking.

Not from fear. Fear she could have managed. It was the arithmetic that shook her — the clean, brutal arithmetic she'd just performed aloud in that office, weighing lives like ledger entries, trading a fisherman's trust and a taster's terror for a chance at the truth. *Two lives against the possibility.* She'd said it like a scholar citing sources. She'd made Corvane's kind of calculation and called it scholarship, and the worst part — the part that would keep her up tonight, if tonight ever came — was that she'd do it again. Tomorrow. Without hesitation. Because the thing in the king was real, and it was hungry, and clean hands were a luxury for people who hadn't read what she'd read.

Her mentor, Archivist Ondrey, had told her once: *The archive doesn't make you wise, girl. It just shows you the price of everything.* He'd died in disgrace too, eventually. She was beginning to understand why he'd laughed so much near the end.

Maren straightened. Smoothed the order. Checked the wax seal with a thumb — intact, iron-gall sharp, the Lord Protector's sigil biting into red like a small wound.

Thirty days. One diminished taster. One crypt full of ciphered warnings. And above it all, a king spending his memories like a dying man's coins.

She started down the stairs toward the cells, telling herself the shaking was just the cold.

It was a lie, and she filed it away with all the others.
