## 9. The Archivist's Bargain

They did not return me to the cells. That was the first strange thing.

The warden who came for me at dawn didn't chain my wrists. He simply unlocked the door, looked at a paper in his hand as though it might bite him, and said, "Fairweather. You're transferred. Research custody." He pronounced *research* the way a man pronounces the name of a disease he doesn't believe in.

"Transferred where?"

He didn't answer. He walked me up out of the mint's underbelly — past the boy who whistles wind, who watched me go with his grey eyes and said nothing, because there was nothing to say — and into a carriage with no crest on its door. No crest meant someone important enough not to need one, or someone important enough to want none.

I had spent three years tasting other people's food and reading what was in it. I read carriages the same way. This one smelled of lamp oil, old paper, and — faintly, underneath — of iron gall ink, that particular sour-metal tang that gets into everything an archivist touches. I'd tasted enough documents by accident over the years to know it.

So: a scholar had bought me. I turned that over during the whole rattling ride through Ashvale's waking streets and could not decide whether it was better or worse than the axe.

* * *

Maren Voss was not what I expected, which is a stupid thing to think, because I hadn't known what to expect. She was perhaps fifty, sharp-faced, with ink stains on three fingers and the fourth finger of her left hand wrapped in a bandage where a pen had clearly won an argument. Her tower room above the old chancery was crammed floor to rafters with ledgers, and every ledger was labeled in a hand so precise it looked printed.

"You're shorter than your grandmother," she said, by way of greeting.

The words landed like a dropped plate. "You knew my grandmother?"

"I never met her. I've read her." She gestured at a table where a ledger lay open, weighted at both corners with brass. "Sit. Eat something. You look like a man who hasn't trusted food in three years."

"I haven't."

"Smart." She pushed a covered dish toward me anyway. Bread, cheese, an apple cut in slices. "It's not poisoned. I checked it myself, which I imagine is more than anyone's ever done for you."

That was true, and it undid me a little. I sat. I ate. The bread tasted like bread, which these days counted as luxury.

She let me finish before she began, which told me she was crueler than Captain Rell but in a more civilized way.

"Do you know why you're alive, Fairweather? Not why they released you. Why you're alive at all. Three years on the taster's roster. Arsenic twice. Aconite once — do you know what aconite does to an unprotected man? He dies with his boots on, mid-scream. You registered nothing. The court physicians called it constitution." She tapped the open ledger. "Constitution is what physicians write when they don't want to say *miracle* or *fraud*. It's neither. Do you know what you are?"

"A man with a strong stomach."

"You're a vessel." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Your body doesn't just resist poison. It takes it in. Holds it. Every dose you ever swallowed is still in you — transformed, bound into your blood like pigment into paint. You are the most poisonous man in Ashvale, Elodie Fairweather, and the only person it will never hurt is you."

I set down the apple slice. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Years of practice at being steady while the world went wrong.

"That's a grim party trick," I said. "Why am I here?"

"Because of this." She turned the ledger toward me.

It was old — older than any ledger I'd seen in the palace, its leather gone soft and dark as plum skin. The pages were ciphered, columns of symbols that almost resolved into letters and refused. But at the head of the first page, in plain script, faded to the color of weak tea, was a name.

*Fairweather.*

"My grandmother's hand?" I asked. I'd seen her writing on recipe cards, on the flyleaves of herbals. Something in the slant of it —

"No. Your grandmother's grandmother." Maren watched me take that in. "Though your grandmother wrote in it too. The last entries are hers. Sibyl Fairweather. Ring any bells?"

"She died before I was born. My mother never spoke of her. Which, in my family, meant there was something to not speak of."

"There always is, with binders."

"Binders." I tasted the word. Under the ink and the lamp oil, under the bread, I could taste something else now — fear, mine, rising like bile. "Madam Voss. I was arrested on suspicion of constitution. I'm currently a research asset, which I understand is a step above kindling and a step below property. If you've bought me to interrogate me about witchcraft, you should know the crown burns people for less than whatever you're about to say."

"The crown," Maren said, "is currently being eaten from the inside, and I have neither the time nor the patience to be delicate. So I'll say it plainly."

She closed her eyes for a moment, the way a woman does before diving into cold water.

"There is a thing beneath the palace. Older than the dynasty. It has worn six kings the way a glove wears through at the fingers. It feeds on memory — inherited memory, the deep store a bloodline carries — and when a host is emptied, it arranges a convenient death and moves to the heir, because the heir comes pre-loaded with fresh stock. King Aldric is its current coat. It sits under the crown itself. We call it the Hollow, because that's what it leaves."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, cart wheels ground over cobbles. Ordinary sounds. The world refusing to stop for the end of it.

"All right," I said slowly. "Say I believe you. Say I even understand you. What does that have to do with me?"

"Your family found it first. Generations back. And your family bound it — once, badly, temporarily, at a cost I can barely read in these pages without wanting to burn them. The binding has been fraying for decades. Your grandmother, Sibyl, made repairs. Then she stopped. Either she died, or—" Maren's mouth twisted. "Or there wasn't enough of her left to hold a pen."

"Left of her after what?"

"After the binding took its price." Maren laid her bandaged hand flat on the page, gently, as if the ledger were a sleeping animal. "A binder pays in identity, Fairweather. Memory. Selfhood. The rite requires memories freely given — willingly offered, that's the phrase, repeated in four different hands across two centuries, because apparently everyone thought it was negotiable. It isn't. The Hollow eats what you love until there's no one home to miss it."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply informed me we were leaving.

"And you want me to do it again," I said. "That's the bargain. That's why you paid for a poisoned man out of a cell. You need a Fairweather to rebind your monster, and I'm the last one on the shelf."

Maren didn't flinch. I'll give her that. Most people flinch.

"No," she said. "I want you to unbind it. There's a difference, and the difference is the only reason I'm not ashamed of this arithmetic. A rebinding buys another century and costs another Fairweather their entire self. An unbinding ends it. But an unbinding needs two things I don't have." She raised one ink-stained finger. "Blood of the binder's line — yours, close enough to the original working that the palace wards will taste kinship in it. And a gift." The second finger. "The gift your family carried alongside the binding craft. The thing that lets you read what's in a substance without dying of it. Your tongue, Fairweather. Your cursed, miraculous tongue."

"My tongue reads arsenic. It doesn't read kings."

"It reads memory. That's what poison-tasting actually is — you've been reading intention, residue, echo, your whole life, and calling it a strong stomach. The gift runs in your line. It ran in hers." She nodded at the ledger, at the tea-faded name. "And it's why your body holds poison instead of dying of it. You were built for this work, generation by generation, whether anyone remembered building you or not."

I looked at my hands. Ordinary hands. Baker's-adjacent, market-worn, the hands of a man who sold luck-knots and fever-charms under floorboards and tasted regicide for wages. They didn't feel built for anything. They felt like mine.

"You're telling me my family wrote my fate," I said. "Generations ago. Before my mother, before her mother. Someone decided there would always be a Fairweather with a hollow-proof tongue and a body like a poison jar, and then everyone after just... fell into the mold without knowing it."

"Yes."

"And nobody asked me."

"You weren't born yet."

"That's not an answer. That's a schedule."

For the first time, something moved behind Maren Voss's sharp face that might have been respect, or apology, or both wearing the same coat. "No," she said. "Nobody asked you. That's the truth of inheritance, Fairweather. It's a contract signed by the dead and honored by the living who never saw the terms. I've spent twenty years in archives learning exactly how much of history is people paying debts they didn't incur." She closed the ledger. "You can refuse. I want that said plainly too. Walk out of this tower, disappear into the coast towns, change your name, and maybe the Hollow never notices you exist. Maybe Aldric empties out, dies conveniently, and the thing moves into the prince, and Ashvale gets another forty years of beloved kings going gently, politely mad. Maybe you die old."

"And if I stay?"

"Then we learn the rite together. All of it. Every cost, written down, before you agree to anything. I'm an archivist, not a priest. I deal in what's recorded, not what's promised." She slid the ledger across the table toward me. "But understand what refusal means. It doesn't mean the story skips you. It means the story happens without your consent instead of with it."

I touched the ledger's cover. Old leather, warm from the lamp. Under my fingertips I could almost feel the shape of the cipher beneath, like text pressed through from the far side of a page.

And there — I don't know how to describe it except honestly — I *tasted* something. Faint, at the very edge of the sense I'd spent three years pretending was ordinary. Bitterness and smoke and something sweet underneath, like herbs burned in honey. Like a voice.

Like a voice saying a word I almost knew.

My own name, spoken in a cadence I'd heard somewhere I couldn't reach.

I pulled my hand back. Maren was watching me with the expression of a woman watching a theory stand up and walk.

"You felt it," she said. "Didn't you."

"I don't know what I felt."

"That was Sibyl Fairweather. Residue. Echo. Whatever word you prefer. She's still in those pages, Fairweather — what's left of her, which may be more than anyone guessed, because the binding was written in her blood and her blood is in you." Maren leaned forward. "The question isn't whether your family wrote your fate. They did. The question is whether you read the rest of the contract before you sign anything yourself."

Outside the tower window, Ashvale went about its morning. Smoke from ten thousand chimneys. The market opening. Somewhere, hunters nailing new edicts to new doors. Somewhere, Bettina sweeping ash that used to be charms off her step.

Somewhere, a king forgetting his daughter's name one syllable at a time.

"One condition," I said.

Maren waited.

"Before I agree to anything — before I touch that rite, that blood, any of it — I want to know what happened to Sibyl. All of it. What she gave, and what was left. If my family's price is my price, I'll see the bill first."

Maren Voss smiled without warmth, the smile of a woman who had hoped I'd ask exactly that.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we learn to read your grandmother's cipher. Because the answer to your question, Fairweather, is written in her own hand — and I'm beginning to suspect you're the only one alive who can taste it."

* * *

That night I slept in a cot in the tower's upper room, under a roof of old slate, with the ledger locked in a chest two floors below and still, somehow, present in the room with me. The way a debt is present.

I dreamed of a kitchen I had never stood in. Dried herbs hanging from beams. A woman with my mother's hands stirring something in a black pot, humming a tune I almost knew, and when she turned her face was smooth as an egg — blank, featureless, waiting to be filled in.

She held the spoon out to me.

In the dream, I understood perfectly what was being asked.

I woke before dawn with the taste of burnt honey in my mouth, and lay listening to Ashvale wake below the window, and tried to remember my mother's face, and could — thank all the quiet gods, I could, every line of it — and held onto it the way a drowning man holds a rail.

Then I got up, and went down to learn what my grandmother had paid.
