## 10. Grandmother's Ledger

The cipher took three days to break, and it broke on a Tuesday, over cold mutton, because Maren Voss had learned long ago that the mind solves best when the body is fed and bored.

She had the ledger spread across her worktable in the tower — four hundred pages of Fairweather hand, bound in cracked calfskin, its margins eaten by silverfish and one long-ago spill of something acidic. The first hundred pages were ordinary: taster's records, receipts for nutmeg, complaints about the quality of palace flour in the reign of Aldric's grandfather. It was page one hundred twelve where the handwriting changed.

The letters grew smaller. Denser. And they began to be written in columns, right to left, each character a small deliberate knot of strokes that meant nothing in any alphabet Maren knew — which was seven, two of them dead, one of them technically illegal to read aloud.

"It's not a substitution cipher," she said, for perhaps the ninth time that evening. Elodie sat across from her, turning a dried sprig of rowan between his fingers like a man worrying a tooth. "Substitutions crack under frequency analysis. This has no frequencies. Each symbol appears once or twice, never more."

"Then what is it?"

"Shorthand." She tapped the page. "Personal shorthand. She wasn't hiding it from enemies. She was writing fast, for herself, because she was the only person who ever needed to read it." Maren leaned back until her chair creaked. "Which means we can't decode it. We can only *remember* it."

Elodie looked up. "I've never seen this book before in my life."

"No," Maren said. "But you're her blood. And there are kinds of memory that don't live in the head."

* * *

They had tried the obvious routes first. Maren had spent the better part of two days cross-referencing the symbols against every archival shorthand system in the Ashvale collection — chancery hand, mint-clerk notation, the physician's script used for anatomical notes, even the thieves' cant marks scratched into doorposts by debt-collectors. Nothing matched. The symbols were too organic, too idiosyncratic. Some looked like leaves. One recurring mark resembled a cup seen from above, a circle with a crescent bitten out of it. Another looked unmistakably like a tongue.

On the second night Elodie had picked up the ledger, held it near the lamp, and gone very still.

"What," Maren said.

"It smells like her kitchen." He said it flatly, embarrassed. "That's absurd. This book is older than I am. Paper doesn't keep smell for sixty years."

"Paper absolutely keeps smell for sixty years. I've catalogued enough of it to know." She took the volume back and inhaled along the gutter of the binding, low near the spine where the glue held. Beneath the dust and the tallow and the faint iron tang of old ink, there it was: rosemary, scorched butter, woodsmoke. A kitchen preserved in fat and ash. Someone had handled these pages with those smells ground into their fingers, hundreds of times, over years.

"She lived with this book open," Maren said slowly. "Not stored. Open. On a table she cooked beside."

"Or she wrote it in the kitchen."

"Or both." Maren pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began copying the most frequent symbols in a column. "Which gives us our way in. Shorthand isn't random, Elodie. Even private shorthand grows out of habit. She'd abbreviate what she said most often. So — what does a binder say most often?"

He didn't answer immediately. When he did, his voice had dropped. "The name?"

"The name," Maren agreed. "Whatever it is."

* * *

The breakthrough, when it came, came through Elodie's hands rather than hers, and Maren would think about that for the rest of her life.

He had fallen half-asleep at the table around midnight, chin on his fist, the lamp burning low. Maren kept working, reading symbols aloud in nonsense syllables just to hear their shapes, when she noticed his lips moving. Not words. Sounds. Small clicking, humming sounds, rhythmic, like a child counting under his breath.

"Elodie."

"Hm?" He surfaced.

"You were making noises."

"I was?" He frowned, rubbed his face. Then he did it again — not deliberately; it simply came out of him, a soft rising hum with a click at the end, *hm-ah-tk*, and his eyes went wide. "That's the cup-symbol. That one." He pointed without touching. "*Hm-ah.* That's what my mother called — no. What my grandmother called when she — " He stopped. His jaw worked. "There's a word in my family. We don't use it as a word anymore. My mother used it as a sound, when she burned milk, or cut herself — the way you'd say a curse. I always thought it was just a curse."

"Say it again."

"*Saveth.*"

Maren wrote it down. Then she found the cup-symbol, the one that appeared forty-one times in the ledger, more than any other mark, and set the two side by side.

Forty-one occurrences. In a binder's private record of an unclean thing. Of course. You do not write a parasite's true name in full. You abbreviate it. You wear it down to a pebble you can carry in your mouth without cutting yourself. Every language does it with gods and demons alike.

"Saveth," Maren whispered, and felt the tower lean slightly, the way a ship leans when the wind finds it. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, which was somehow worse than flickering.

"That's not the whole name," Elodie said. His hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the floor might tilt further. "That's the nickname. The diminutive. Like calling a wolf 'dog.'"

"No," Maren said. "It's worse than that. Look." She flipped forward through the ledger, to the pages densest with symbols, and laid her finger under a sequence: cup-mark, leaf-mark, the strange double-stroke like a closed eye. "Here. *Saveth* plus two modifiers. And here — " another page — "*saveth* plus a different modifier. And here — " a third. "Three forms. Three aspects. A thing like this wouldn't have one name. It would have a name for how it eats, a name for how it hides, and a name for how it's bound. Three handles on one pot."

"And she wrote all three down."

"In cipher, abbreviated, in her own kitchen, next to her recipes." Maren laughed — a short, ugly sound she hadn't intended to make. "Because where else? Because the safest place to hide the end of the world is between instructions for pickled herring."

* * *

Decoding went faster after that, though "faster" still meant four more nights of close, exhausting labor. Elodie's ear for the sounds unlocked the phonetic layer; Maren's archival instincts reconstructed the grammar. The shorthand turned out to be logographic — each symbol a compressed idea, not a letter — which meant they weren't reading her spelling but her thinking, laid out in knots.

What emerged, entry by entry, was a woman named Sefa Fairweather who had been, by profession, a royal food-taster, and by necessity, something considerably more.

Maren read the early entries aloud as they resolved them, because some things needed air:

*"Day 3 of the sweetening. His Majesty asked after my mother's health by name. Last month he could not recall he had a mother. The physicians call it improvement. Improvement would be him remembering wrongly. This is him remembering what is put in front of him."*

And later:

*"It does not take by force. That is the horror no one will write down. It takes what is offered. A king offers everything — that is what coronation IS, an offering — and so it sits in the crown like a spider in a web it did not have to spin, and waits, and is fed by love itself. You cannot starve it. Love is its grain."*

Elodie listened with his arms crossed tight, and Maren watched him grow paler entry by entry, and understood that he was hearing his own death warrant being drafted sixty years early.

It was near dawn on the fifth day that they reached the passage that mattered.

The symbols here changed character — larger, slower, pressed hard into the paper hard enough to emboss the following three pages. Whatever Sefa Fairweather had written here, she had written it knowing it might be the last thing she ever clearly thought.

Maren translated it line by line, speaking carefully, the way you walk on ice:

*"The binding holds because I hold it. Understand this, whoever comes after: the knot is not in the seal beneath the throne. The seal is only the anchor. The rope runs through a living mind. It must be a mind the creature has tasted and found sweet — a taster, a feeder, one whose memory is rich with flavor and want. It accepted my offer because my memories were worth eating. That was the trap I built: I made myself the finest meal in Ashvale, and it has been chewing me for nine years, slowly, politely, and I am nearly gone."*

Elodie made a sound. Maren did not look up. Looking up would have ended her.

*"To unbind it entirely — not to hold it, but to END the holding, to give the king back his own mind — the rope must be handed over willingly. Not seized. Offered. It cannot take what is freely given unless it is truly given, with love attached, or it will know the gift is poisoned and refuse the meal. Whoever does this must pour their remembered life into it — the tastes, the loves, the rooms they slept in, the sound of their own name — all of it, willingly, gladly, as a feast. And when it has swallowed them whole—"*

The next symbols took Maren an hour. Her hands shook, and she hated them for it.

*"—there is no coming back. I searched. There is no clever trick, no anchor, no thread. I looked for one for six years because I wanted there to be one. The binder who gives everything loses everything. What walks away from the rite wears their face and answers to their name and knows nothing else about themselves. My daughter will know me. I will not know her. This is the shape of the price. Write it plainly so no descendant of mine can pretend otherwise."*

The tower was silent. Somewhere below, the city woke: cart-wheels, gulls, the mint's great hammers beginning their day.

"There it is," Elodie said finally. His voice was steady in the way Maren had learned meant the opposite. "The family business. Explains why nobody talked about her."

"Your mother never mentioned her?"

"My mother said her mother died 'before her time.' Everyone assumed childbirth or fever." He turned the ledger toward himself, studying the embossed pressure of the final lines, reading the ghost of the writing through the paper like braille. "She didn't die. She just stopped being anyone."

"Worse. Listen to the tense." Maren traced the last legible entry, dated eleven months after the others, in a hand gone loose and childish, the shorthand abandoned for plain slow letters as if the writer had forgotten how to compress anything. *"Today I saw a girl in the market with my mother's shawl and I cried and did not know why. A kind man walked me home."*

"She lived," Maren said quietly. "For years, probably. In her own house. With people who loved her. And she was a stranger in all of it." She closed the ledger. The clasp clicked like a small lock engaging. "That's your inheritance, Elodie Fairweather. A map with a warning printed across it in your grandmother's own hand: *this road costs exactly what you are.*"

* * *

They argued about it, afterward. Maren had expected that. What she hadn't expected was which side of the argument she'd find herself on.

"We don't use it," she said. "Not yet. We decode the rest first — the seal's location, the feeding hours, every scrap about the creature's nature. Knowledge first. The rite is the last door, and we don't touch it until every other option is dead."

"You're protecting me." He said it without accusation, which was somehow harder to bear.

"I'm being rigorous. There's a difference, and I'll thank you to remember it."

"Maren." He turned the ledger toward her again, open to the middle pages, and she saw that he'd marked something during the night — a sequence she'd translated but not connected. "Read this one again. The one about the king before Aldric."

She read it. *"The old king died gently in his sleep, beloved, hollow, and the creature passed to the son's crown within the hour of crowning. It does not die with the host. It inherits. Understand: killing the king kills nothing. Killing the king FEEDS it a finished meal and delivers it fresh."*

"So if we do nothing," Elodie said, "Aldric dies within a few years — he's already more hollow than man — and the Hollow moves to whoever wears the crown next. And the hunts go on. And the pyres go on. And in thirty years some other taster with the wrong ancestry finds this same ledger and reads this same warning." He pushed the book gently across the table. "You told me my family wrote my fate. Fine. But they also left the tools. I'm not pretending the price isn't real. I read the same lines you did." His voice caught, steadied. "I'm asking you to help me spend it well."

Maren looked at him for a long moment — this quiet man with poison in his veins and his grandmother's stubbornness in his jaw, sitting in her tower among her books, volunteering to become a stranger so that strangers he'd never meet could live unhaunted.

"You should sleep," she said. "We start on the seal entries tomorrow. And Elodie —" she waited until he paused at the stair — "if we do this, we do it completely. No heroics, no shortcuts, no half-offered memories. A partial gift is a refusal, and a refused gift tells the creature exactly who we are and what we intend. If you step into that rite, you step in all the way."

"I understand."

"Good." She gathered the ledger against her chest, feeling the kitchen-smell rise off it one last time. "Because tomorrow we find out whether the seal beneath the throne can even be broken — and I promise you, whatever your grandmother wrote about that, it's worse."
