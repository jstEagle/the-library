## 11. First Taste of the Hollow

They came for him at dawn, which Maren had told me was deliberate.

"Nothing done in this palace happens at a natural hour," she'd said the night before, spreading a diagram across her desk like a cook laying out a recipe. "The Hollow feeds at midnight and sleeps shallow through the morning. If you must stand in front of it, stand in front of it when it's drowsy."

"And if your arithmetic is wrong?"

She hadn't answered that. She'd just rolled up the diagram and looked at me with those grey archivist's eyes, the ones that catalogued everything and committed to nothing, and said, "Wear something plain. You're being presented as a servant, not a spectacle."

So I wore my oldest coat, the one with the burn-hole near the cuff from Bettina's brazier three winters back, and I let two wardens walk me down out of Voss Tower into the grey light of Ashvale's palace district.

I had lived in this city my whole life and never once stood inside the palace walls. From the market districts it looks like a mountain wearing a hat — all spires and banners, the kind of thing you point at children to make them behave. Up close it was worse than grand. It was *hungry*. The corridors were wide enough for processions and empty enough for echoes, and every surface seemed to be listening. The stone underfoot was pale and veined, and Maren had told me once — casually, the way she dropped terrible information like loose change — that the older wings were built of memory-stone, quarried from places where the past pooled thick, because the Hollow liked to live in a house that remembered.

The wardens didn't speak to me. One of them kept his hand on the pommel of his sword the whole way, not threatening exactly, just *ready*, the way you keep a hand on a dog's collar in a room full of strangers. The other carried a sealed letter with Maren's forged credentials, which she had spent half the night perfecting. *Research asset, Lord Protector's warrant, escort directly to the presence chamber.* Bureaucracy is its own kind of magic. It got us through three checkpoints without a single question, which frightened me more than questions would have. A city where nobody asks anything is a city already asleep.

We climbed. Of course we climbed. The throne room sat at the top of everything, because kings always want their power uphill of everyone else's.

Somewhere around the third stairwell, my mouth started doing something strange.

You have to understand what my mouth does. Three years of tasting for the crown — arsenic in the porridge, nightshade in the wine, antimony in the honey-cakes — had trained my tongue into an instrument. I could read a dish the way Maren read a ledger: provenance, intention, history. A cook's fear tasted different from a cook's pride. A poisoner in a hurry left sharp edges; a patient one left silk. My mouth was the most honest part of me, and it had been quietly screaming since we passed the second checkpoint.

What it was telling me now was simple: *the air has a flavor.*

Not dust, not incense, not the tallow-and-iron smell of soldiers. Something underneath all of that, something old and cold and faintly sweet, the way a cellar smells just before the mold blooms. Like sweetness that had forgotten why it wanted to be sweet. I swallowed and the taste stayed, sitting on the back of my tongue like a coin.

"Stop gawking," said the warden with the sword, though I hadn't been gawking. I'd been tasting. There's a difference, and only one of us knew it.

* * *

The doors to the presence chamber were taller than my whole house, black oak banded with iron, carved with the faces of dead kings. I counted eleven faces before I made myself stop. Maren would have known all their names. Maren would have known what they died of. I suspected, in the way you suspect a storm before the clouds arrive, that they had all died of the same thing.

The doors opened without anyone touching them.

That was the first moment I nearly turned around. Doors that heavy do not move on their own, and yet they swung inward smooth as water, and the warm dark of the throne room breathed out at us — candle-smoke and lilies and that cold cellar-sweetness underneath, stronger now, so strong my eyes watered.

"The taster," announced a courtier whose name I never learned, "for His Majesty's morning meal."

The throne room was mostly empty at this hour, thank whatever gods still bothered with Ashvale. A handful of courtiers drifted near the walls like moths that had given up on the flame. Servants arranged a long table with the silent choreography of people who had learned that noise near the king shortened their lives. And at the far end, on a dais beneath a canopy of cloth-of-gold, sat King Aldric.

I had seen him twice before, from a distance, at public tastings — the ceremonial ones where the crowd watched a boy in a white apron sip from the royal cup and live, and cheered, because there is nothing a crowd loves more than watching someone else risk dying. He'd looked like a king then. Broad, silver-bearded, a man built for portraits.

The man on the dais now was smaller. Not aged — *smaller*, the way a coat goes smaller when there's less inside it. He sat very straight, the way children sit when they've been told to sit straight, and his hands rested on the arms of the throne with the careful stillness of someone holding on. The crown sat on his head like it belonged to someone else. Like it was waiting for someone else.

And he was staring at nothing. At a point about a foot above the heads of everyone in the room, at some private horizon none of us could see.

My mouth flooded with that cold sweetness, so thick now I could barely taste anything else, and somewhere behind my sternum a voice that wasn't a voice said, very clearly:

*Oh.*

It didn't say anything else. It didn't need to. It was the sound a fisherman makes when he feels the line go tight — recognition, attention, the beginning of appetite. Something in that room had noticed me the way I notice arsenic: not with alarm, but with professional interest.

The warden at my elbow cleared his throat. "Forward," he muttered. "Do your work."

I walked. My legs did it without consulting me, which was probably for the best.

* * *

The morning meal was laid on a side table beside the dais: bread, butter, smoked river-trout, a small pot of plum preserve, and a glass decanter of white wine gone gold with age. A steward lifted each cover for my inspection with the bored efficiency of a man who had done this ten thousand times and expected the world to keep working.

I did what I had done every morning for three years. I tasted.

The bread was fine — good sour starter, no bitterness of any alkaloid, no almond ghost of cyanide. The trout was clean. The preserve was plums and sugar and nothing else. I worked through them methodically, letting each flavor tell me its story, and the routine of it steadied me the way prayer steadies other men. This I knew how to do. Whatever waited on that dais, the trout was still just a trout.

Then I came to the wine.

I poured a measure, swirled it, and raised the glass — and the cold sweetness in the air *moved*. I felt it shift like water shifting when something large turns over beneath it. The whole room's flavor tilted toward me, toward the glass, toward the small golden mouthful I was about to take, and I understood with absolute animal certainty that I was not tasting the wine alone.

I drank.

For one heartbeat it was only wine — late-harvest, honeyed, a little oxidized, from a vineyard south of the city that I recognized by its faint green-stem finish. And then, underneath, arriving the way a shadow arrives ahead of the dog that casts it, came the rest.

Memory. That's what I tasted. I didn't have the word for it yet — Maren would give me the word later, would say *memory-echo* in her flat scholar's voice while I tried not to shake — but I knew what it was anyway, because I had grown up on my grandmother's knee and she had taught me that everything carries a little of what happened to it. The wine carried the vineyard's sun. And wrapped around the wine, soaked into it like rot into a floorboard, was the king.

His memories. Or what was left of them. They came to me in fragments, flavor-fragments, the way grief comes: rain on orchard leaves. A small hand in a large one. A girl laughing — laughing, laughing, the laugh going thinner each time I found it, like a coin worn smooth by handling. Someone had been drinking this wine for years, and something had been drinking *him*, and both of them had left their residue in the glass.

I set the decanter down. My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: my hands did not shake.

"It's clean," I said, to the steward, in my ordinary working voice. "No poison in any of it."

And the king looked at me.

He didn't turn his head so much as *aim* it, the slow rotation of a man surfacing from deep water, and his eyes found mine across twenty feet of polished floor, and for a moment — one breath, maybe two — there was someone home behind them. Someone exhausted and drowning and ferociously, desperately alive.

His lips moved. No sound came out. But I have spent three years reading mouths across banquet tables, reading the shape words make before they're spoken, and I didn't need the sound.

*Run.*

One breath. That's all he got. Then the light behind his eyes went out like a lamp shuttered, his gaze slid off me back to its private horizon, and the cold sweetness in the air settled over the room again, thicker than before, satisfied. Fed. Or amused. With a thing that old, I couldn't tell the difference, and I'm not sure there was one.

A courtier near the wall coughed delicately. Somewhere a servant refilled a dish. The machinery of the court ground on, grinding a king into paste, and everyone present had agreed — by silence, by etiquette, by the simple cowardice of keeping their posts — not to notice.

I bowed to the throne, because that was what the body in the white apron was supposed to do, and inside the bow I let my face do whatever it needed to do, where no one could see it.

*Run,* the king had said. Not *help me.* Not *save her* or *avenge me* or any of the things a proud man says. *Run.* The first coherent thought of a drowning man, spent on a stranger. Spent on *me.*

I straightened. I turned. I walked back down the length of that enormous room with my spine straight and my stomach full of ice, and the whole way, the cold sweetness followed me — not blocking my path, not reaching for me, just *watching*, the way a cat watches a mouse that has foolishly decided to come back tomorrow.

At the doors, I made the mistake of glancing back.

Aldric was looking at his own hands. He had lifted one from the armrest and was turning it over slowly, palm to fingers, like a man checking whether he still owned it. The crown caught the candlelight and threw it back in little cold flames.

And under the armrest of the throne — I saw it only because the angle was right, only for a second — there were scratches in the wood. Fresh ones. Small ones. Letters, or the beginning of letters, dug by fingernails by a man who no longer trusted himself to hold a pen.

I didn't see what they spelled. The doors closed between us, tall and black and patient, and cut him off from me like a page turning.

* * *

The wardens walked me back down through the listening corridors, and I kept my eyes forward and my breathing even, because I had learned long ago that panic is a flavor too, and I refused to serve it.

But my mind was doing what my mind does when it can't afford to scream: it catalogued.

The wine was clean. The king was not. The poison in that room wasn't in the food — it was in the *chair*, in the *crown*, in the air itself, and it had looked at me with three years of swallowed death walking around inside my blood and it had found the menu interesting. Maren had told me what it was. The Hollow. An eater of memory, older than the dynasty, wearing Aldric like a coat it had grown fond of. She'd told me, and I had nodded, and I had understood it the way you understand a map of a place you've never been.

Now I had been there. Now I knew the taste of it — cold, sweet, patient, endless — and knowing a taste means knowing a thing in the only way I have ever truly known anything.

He had whispered *run* with the last of himself, and I had walked out instead, because running would leave him alone in there with it, and because Maren needed me upright and useful, and because — I'll be honest, since honesty is the one luxury I've never managed to give up — because I was too afraid to run and too stubborn to admit it.

In the tower, hours later, Maren took one look at my face and put down her pen.

"You tasted it," she said. Not a question.

"It tastes like a cellar before the mold comes." My voice came out level, which surprised me. "Like sweetness that forgot what it was for. And it knows me, Maren. It looked at me the way I look at an unlabeled bottle."

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside her window, Ashvale went about its morning — carts, gulls, the market bell — a city that had no idea what sat on its throne, eating its kings one memory at a time.

"There are scratches under the armrest of the throne," I said. "Fresh ones. He's leaving messages. To whoever comes next."

Maren's pen rolled off the desk. She didn't pick it up.

"Aldric," she said softly, "is still writing."

And that — more than the cold tongue along my spine, more than the whisper, more than the wine full of a dying man's laughter — that was the moment I stopped being a taster who was afraid, and became something else. Because a man who still writes is a man who can still be reached.

"We go back," I said. "Soon. Before it finishes the page."
