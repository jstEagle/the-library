## 12. Rell's Report

The report was four pages long, and Captain Dain Rell hated every line of it.

He sat in the Mint's administrative office with a stub of candle burning down beside him and the interrogation transcripts spread out like something he'd rather not touch. Outside, Ashvale rattled through a cold night — cart wheels, a drunk singing badly two streets over, the Mint's own furnaces ticking as they cooled. Inside, everything smelled of ink and iron filings and the particular staleness of rooms where people had cried.

The quill hovered. He'd been hovering for an hour.

*Prisoner one: Sela Marsh, midwife, forty-one years of age.* He knew the words by heart now. He'd written them twice already and torn up both drafts because the first was too honest and the second was too honest in a different way.

The truth about Sela Marsh was this: she was guilty of nothing except knowing things. She had attended the birth of Rell's own niece nine years ago, back when he was still a sergeant walking the Tannery beat with wet boots and a full heart about his work. She had hummed while she worked. She had put a sprig of rosemary over the door "for luck," which at the time everyone in the room had smiled at, including him.

Under the new edict, that sprig of rosemary was evidence.

*Subject admits to practice of folk remedy. Subject's charms consist of knots, herbs, and spoken blessings. No demonstration of substantive gift observed under questioning.*

That last part was true, and it would save no one. The hunters didn't need demonstrations anymore. They needed names, and the Lord Protector's men had learned that a transcript with the word *admits* in it was as good as a confession written in blood. Rell could have falsified it — softened her answers, made them ambiguous — but the second interrogator, a pale young warden named Corvus who took notes in beautiful copperplate and never blinked enough, kept copies of everything. Falsifying meant losing his commission, and losing his commission meant someone worse sitting in this chair tomorrow.

That was the arithmetic of it. That was always the arithmetic. Every choice came down to *me or someone worse*, and somewhere along the road from Tannery beat to this desk, Rell had stopped noticing how much of his honor he was feeding into that equation.

He wrote: *Recommend standard processing.*

Standard processing meant the pyres on Wailing Hill. Three a week now, weather permitting. The city had started calling them *lightings*, the way you call a funeral a *celebration*, because language bends before people break.

* * *

The boy was in cell nine.

They'd given him to Rell specifically because Rell was known for a light hand — that reputation had followed him up through the ranks like a dog that wouldn't stop trailing him, useful sometimes, humiliating others. *Give it to Rell,* the wardens said. *He'll get them talking without breaking anything.* It was meant as praise. Lately it felt like being handed a knife and told what a good job he did of cutting gently.

Cell nine held a boy of maybe twelve named Pip Ossel, arrested three days ago for whistling at a windmill.

That was the whole crime. A miller had reported that when the boy whistled, the sails turned against the wind. Rell had read the denunciation himself, filed in the hunter's neat bureaucratic hand: *Witness observed subject produce vocalization; witness observed rotation inconsistent with meteorological conditions; witness requests consideration per edict schedule B.*

Rotation inconsistent with meteorological conditions. Somewhere a clerk had actually written those words and gone home to supper.

Rell unlocked the cell and stepped inside with the lamp. Pip sat on the cot with his knees drawn up, a small sharp-faced kid with a cowlick and chapped hands, wearing the gray smock they gave prisoners so their own clothes could be burned afterward, just in case. Magic clung to cloth, apparently. To thread, to wood, to breath. To everything except the consciences of the men hunting it, which seemed to Rell the one place it never stuck at all.

"Evening," Rell said.

Pip looked up. He didn't answer. They never answered, at first. But this one watched him with a flat, assessing look that Rell recognized from soldiers half again his age — the look of someone deciding whether the person in front of him was going to hurt him, and planning around either answer.

"I brought bread." He set the loaf on the floor between them, an offering across no-man's-land. "It's not poisoned. I know how that sounds, coming from us."

"It's not the poison I'm worried about." The boy's voice was hoarse from disuse. "You're writing things down about me."

"I write things down about everyone."

"Then you're the worst one." Pip pulled the loaf toward him anyway, broke off a piece, held it, didn't eat. "The others just hit you or shout at you and then it's done. You sit there and you're nice, and then you go upstairs and make me real on paper. My mum says paper's how they got her brother. Not rope, not fire first — paper. Someone wrote him down and then he was gone."

Rell stood there with his lamp and his keys and the weight of the report in his breast pocket like a stone sewn into the lining.

"Your mother," he said carefully. "She's in the women's block?"

"She's not anywhere. They didn't take her. Yet." Pip tore at the crust. "She told me to stop whistling a hundred times. I told her it wasn't doing anything. And maybe it wasn't. Maybe the wind just liked me." His voice cracked on the last word, and he covered it by chewing, jaw working hard, eyes fixed on the middle distance where twelve-year-olds keep the thing they can't afford to cry about.

Rell lowered himself onto the stool by the door. Protocol said stand. Protocol said keep the authority of height and uniform. He sat anyway, and saw the boy notice it, and saw the boy decide not to be moved by it.

"The miller," Rell said. "Did you do anything to him? Before he denounced you?"

"He shorted my da on grain for two seasons. Everyone knew. Nobody said, because he's friends with the wardens' quartermaster." Pip shrugged, a small bitter gesture far too old for his shoulders. "I didn't whistle at him. I want that understood. I whistled at the wind, and the wind turned the sails, and the miller looked out his window and saw free flour walking away from his ledger, and that's the whole story. That's your *rotation inconsistent with conditions*."

Rell almost laughed. It caught in his throat and turned into something else, some sound that made the boy look at him sharply.

"You don't believe it either," Pip said.

"What I believe doesn't matter."

"It matters to me." The boy set down the bread. "You're going to write it all down tonight. I can tell. You've got the face. My teacher had that face when she wrote letters home about boys who were going to be beaten. Kind face, busy hand." He leaned forward. "So write this down too, since you're writing. Write that I'm not sorry. Whatever it turns, whatever it does — when the wind answers me, it's the only time anything in this whole rotten city has ever answered me back. Write that."

The candle in the corridor guttered. Somewhere below, a furnace door clanged.

Rell thought about his niece, nine years old, humming with the midwife's rosemary over the door. He thought about the report upstairs, four pages of careful, damning truth. He thought about the fact that he had served the crown of Ashvale for nineteen years and could not remember, precisely, at what point the crown had become a thing he served the way you serve a fever — faithfully, helplessly, waiting for it to pass.

"I'll write it down," he said quietly.

Pip frowned, suspicious of mercy the way prisoners learn to be suspicious of everything.

"Not the sorry part," Rell added. "The other part. That the wind answered you." He stood, took up the lamp. "Get some sleep, Pip."

"It's Ossel," the boy said. "Pip is what my mum calls me. If you're making me real on paper, use the name that'll still be right after she's done calling me anything."

Rell paused in the doorway. There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't be a lie or a promise, and he had no currency left for either. He locked the cell and walked the long corridor past cells seven and eight — the midwife, silent behind her door; the tanner's widow, asleep or pretending — feeling every lock turn behind him like a stitch closing over something living.

At the end of the corridor he stopped, put his palm flat against the cold stone, and stayed there longer than any captain of wardens should.

* * *

Back in the office, the report waited.

He read it through once more, hunting for the loophole, the soft sentence, the place where a man with a conscience could hide a comma of mercy inside official prose. There wasn't one. The edict had been designed by people who understood exactly how officers like him thought, and they had built a machine with no commas in it.

*Recommend standard processing. Recommend standard processing. Recommend—*

His quill touched the final page — the boy's summary. *Subject P. Ossel, age approx. twelve. No substantive gift confirmed. Denunciation rests on single witness testimony of questionable motive.*

There. Right there. *Questionable motive.* It was true — the miller's grain debts were a matter of record, Rell had checked, spending an hour in the toll ledgers he'd have sworn an hour ago he didn't have the stomach to open. One honest clause. Would it save the boy? Almost certainly not. The hunters read reports the way wolves read tracks: they found what they wanted in them. But it would slow them. A day, maybe two. And Corvus's beautiful copperplate copies couldn't unwrite a fact that was also true.

Rell stared at the words. *Questionable motive.* It was such a small rebellion. It was nothing. A candle guttering in a gale and calling itself a flame.

And yet his hand shook as he blotted it, because he understood suddenly that this was how it went for everyone — not one grand refusal, not a dramatic treason with trumpets, but a comma here, a delayed filing there, a loaf of bread carried down to a cell, each small mercy a splinter working loose in the beam of a dam. He had spent nineteen years believing duty was a wall he stood on. Tonight, holding the pen, he understood that walls were made of individual stones, and stones could be pulled, and the question — the only question that mattered — was what you were willing to become when the water started coming through.

He signed the report. *D. Rell, Captain of Wardens.* His signature looked the same as it always had. That seemed obscene somehow — that the outside of a man shouldn't change when the inside did.

He sealed it, addressed it to the Lord Protector's bureau, and set it in the dispatch tray, where it lay among dozens of identical packets, each one containing somebody's mother, somebody's son, somebody's rosemary over a door.

Then he sat back down, pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and began — slowly, in the cramped private shorthand he used for things that were not official and never could be — to write a different kind of report. Names. Cells. What each prisoner had actually said when no one was grading their courage. The midwife's exact words: *I have caught more babies in this city than you have arrested people, Captain, and not one of them ever came into the world hollow.* The widow's. The boy's.

He didn't know what it was for. Evidence for trials that might never be honest. Testimony for a day he couldn't imagine arriving. Or just the stubborn human habit of writing things down so they'd stay true somewhere, even if only in a drawer.

A knock at the door. One of the night wardens, cap in hand, apologetic.

"Beg pardon, Captain. Dispatch rider's come up from the palace. Bureau wants the Fairweather file — the taster they took Tuesday. Says the Lord Protector's office has released him into the custody of some archivist woman. Voss, her name is. Orders are to strike him from the suspect rolls entirely."

Rell looked up. "Released? Into whose custody?"

"Archivist's. Palace authority, countersigned twice." The warden shifted. "Odd, sir, isn't it? We take a man off the street for suspicion of constitution, and a week later the palace itself calls him an asset. Makes a fellow wonder what the constitution's for."

"Thank you, Hobb. That'll be all."

When the door closed, Rell sat very still in the cooling room, listening to the Mint tick around him like a great iron heart slowing.

Elodie Fairweather. The taster who didn't sicken. Rell had questioned him only briefly before the archivist's summons — a quiet man, watchful, with the particular patience of someone accustomed to swallowing things. In the cell he had answered every question honestly and somehow told Rell nothing, and when Rell asked him, almost despite himself, *Are you afraid of us?*, the taster had considered it seriously, as if it deserved a true answer, and said:

*I'm afraid of what you're afraid of. It's bigger than you, Captain. Ask yourself why the palace wants me, and then ask who's giving the orders you sign.*

Rell had logged none of that. Some questions you don't put on paper, because paper has a way of answering them years later, in front of witnesses.

Now the palace had taken its taster back, and the hunts rolled on, and tomorrow there would be lightings on Wailing Hill, weather permitting, and a boy in cell nine who wanted the wind written down as a witness.

Rell opened his drawer, slid his unofficial pages beneath the blotter, and blew out the candle.

In the dark, he heard the furnaces tick, and the wind moving over the Mint's high chimneys — turning, just slightly, against the direction of the night — and he could not decide, as he sat alone in the black, whether he was imagining it, or whether something in Ashvale had finally begun, very quietly, to change its mind.
