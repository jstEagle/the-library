## 13. Training the Tongue

The first thing Maren Voss did was ruin my breakfast.

She set a bowl of porridge in front of me in the tower's morning room — plain oats, a knob of butter, a drizzle of honey — and then, before I could lift the spoon, she laid her hand flat over it.

"Not yet," she said. "Tell me what's in it."

"Butter. Honey. Oats. Salt." I frowned at her. "I watched you make it."

"You watched me make *that*," she agreed. "Now tell me what else is in it. Close your eyes. Open your gift — deliberately, not the way you've been doing it all these years, half-shut, flinching from every flavor like a man walking past a dog he's afraid will bite."

"I don't flinch."

"Elodie." She said my name the way an archivist reads a marginal note: flatly, precisely, with the implication that the text said otherwise. "You have spent three years tasting poison for a living and you have never once let yourself taste anything *fully*. You skim. You catalog. Arsenic: present, four grains. Bitter almond: trace. You take what you need to survive and you seal the rest away behind glass, because the rest is where the danger lives. Am I wrong?"

I wanted to tell her she was wrong. The trouble was that she wasn't.

It had been nine days since they'd moved me into Voss Tower under the fiction of a research custody writ — nine days of ledgers, ciphered notes, and the slow, vertiginous education in what my grandmother had done and what the crown on King Aldric's head actually was. Nine days of knowing that the only reason I was alive and free and eating porridge in a warm room was that my blood was a key to something ancient and hungry beneath the palace.

And nine days of Maren watching me with those gray archivist's eyes, patient as dust, waiting for me to be ready for whatever came next.

"Fine," I said. "Show me how."

* * *

"Stop thinking of it as tasting," she said. We had pushed the porridge aside — I would eat it cold later, and I did — and she had cleared the table instead, laying out her instruments like a surgeon: a copper coin worn smooth, a scrap of wool, a quill nib crusted with old ink, a key of no lock I recognized, a river stone, and one object she kept face-down at the edge of the arrangement, refusing to explain it.

"Tasting is what the tongue does," she went on. "What *you* do — what your family has done, whether they admitted it or not — is read. Memory has weight. It has texture, temperature, savor. Everything a person handles long enough soaks in them the way brine soaks into wood. Most people can't perceive it. Your grandmother could. So, apparently, can you — you've just been reading with your eyes shut."

"And if I open them?"

"Then you'll wish you hadn't, probably," she said cheerfully, "at least at first. Echoes are loud when you're not used to them. Start small." She slid the river stone across the table. "This came out of the Ash two summers ago. Tell me its history."

"It's a rock."

"It's a rock that spent forty years in a riverbed and three hundred before that inside a wall that burned. It remembers both. Read it."

I picked up the stone. It was just a stone — cool, dense, gritty at one edge where it had chipped. I held it the way I'd hold any object and waited for something to happen, and nothing did, and I felt the particular embarrassment of a man who has been told he has a talent performing incompetence in front of a woman who clearly regretted her investment already.

"You're gripping it," Maren said. "Like it owes you money. Loosen your hand. And stop listening with your ears. You taste with more than your mouth — you know this. When you tested the king's wine and caught the hemlock, where did you feel it? Not your tongue first. Somewhere behind it. Somewhere deeper."

She was right, damn her. There was a place — I had never had a word for it, a hollow spot behind the bridge of the nose, down the throat, somewhere near the root of the self — where flavors arrived *before* they arrived, where the knowledge of a thing preceded the evidence of it. Three years of poisons had taught me to trust that place and fear it in equal measure.

So I stopped gripping. I rested the stone on my open palm, closed my eyes, and let myself fall backward into that hollow place.

The world went strange.

Not dramatic — that's what I'd expected, some theatrical rush of visions, and when it didn't come I nearly gave up. Instead it was quiet, and then it was *textured*, the way a dark room becomes textured once your eyes adjust. The stone had a flavor, but not a flavor in the mouth. A flavor in time. Cold first, and wet, and moving — the long patient roll of the river over it, decades compressed into a single sensation, water as a continuous presence rather than an event. And underneath the water-cold, older, charred at the edges: heat. Smoke without fire. A falling. Masonry groaning somewhere far above it, once, in a voice like a struck bell, and then silence, and then the river finding it, covering it, keeping it.

My eyes were streaming when I opened them.

"Burned wall," I said. My voice came out rough. "A tower or a chimney. It fell — no, it was *pulled* down. Someone took it apart on purpose. Then the river." I looked at Maren. "How long was it in the water?"

"Forty-one years, give or take. The wall was the old customs house. Pulled down by order of—" she paused, and something flickered across her face, "—by royal decree. Two hundred years ago. You got the demolition and the river. The burning happened before the stone was cut. That's deeper than most readers ever reach on a first attempt."

I set the stone down very carefully, as though it might go off.

"That was—" I started, and found I didn't have the end of the sentence. *That was the first time in three years I've touched anything without being afraid of it.* *That was the first time since my grandmother died that I've heard the world talk back.* "That was a rock," I finished, inadequately.

"Yes," said Maren, and allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Now do the coin."

* * *

We worked through the morning. The coin was easy after the stone — money is handled so often, by so many hands, that it comes pre-saturated, a palimpsest of palms. I read a baker's flour-dusted grip, a child's tight fist, a soldier's sweat, and one touch, brief and recent, that tasted of iron-gall ink and lamp oil and made me look up sharply at Maren, who said only, "Mine. I paid for the bread we're having at lunch. Go on."

The wool taught me grief, which I hadn't been prepared for. It had belonged to someone who wore it while sitting by a sickbed — I couldn't have told you how I knew, only that the echo had that quality, the long low hum of vigil, hands wringing fabric in the dark. I put it down and had to look out the window for a while at the ordinary gray morning over Ashvale's roofs until my throat behaved.

"The nib," Maren said, when I was ready.

The nib was harder. Ink is intention made visible — she'd told me that earlier, offhand, and I was beginning to understand she never said anything offhand. Writing carries the writer's state the way amber carries insects. This nib had written a great many things, and most of them were lies.

I sorted through them with growing unease. A merchant's inflated invoice, smooth and slippery as oiled cloth. A love letter written by someone who felt nothing, which tasted oddly of vinegar. A denunciation — I knew what it was before I knew what it was, because the flavor of it turned my stomach the way certain poisons did: fear braided with greed, the specific savor of a person selling another person's name. It was fresh. Days old. Written somewhere in the lower city.

"There are hunters' informers using this ink," I said. "Or this brand of it. Maren—"

"I know whose stationer sells it," she said, unbothered, making a note. "I bought the nib from his shop specifically. You need to learn what denunciation tastes like, Elodie. In the weeks ahead you'll be reading a great many documents connected to the hunt, and you cannot afford to mistake fear for guilt or guilt for loyalty. Ink doesn't lie about *how* someone felt. Only about what they claimed." She tapped the page. "Again. What else is on that nib?"

I went back in, and this time, beneath the invoices and the letters and the sour taste of betrayal, I found something small and clean: a child practicing letters. *A b c.* Over and over, tongue between teeth, the concentration coming off the strokes like warmth from a stone. Somebody's kid, learning to write, hoping to be praised.

"Well?" said Maren.

"Somebody's daughter learned her alphabet with it," I said, "after the informer used it last."

Maren stopped writing. For a moment she just looked at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the archivist assessing an asset, but a woman remembering why she'd ruined her career over old papers. "Yes," she said quietly. "That's exactly what it's for. That's what your gift is *for*, Elodie. Not the poisons. The poisons were just the door it happened to come through."

* * *

In the afternoon she brought out the ledger.

I had seen it before — of course I had; it was the reason for everything, the ciphered binding-notes of Ivy Fairweather, hedge-witch and royal taster, the grandmother I remembered mostly as smoke-smell and strong hands and a laugh like a hinge that needed oiling. But I had seen it as Maren saw it: a document. A problem. A code to be broken.

"Today," Maren said, setting it between us on the table, "you're going to read it the other way."

The leather cover was cracked and dark with age. The clasp was brass, shaped like a knot — and even before I touched it, even before I opened the gift at all, I knew the knot. I'd been carving its like into luck-charms since I was eight years old, the interlaced pattern my grandmother taught me, except hers had never been decoration. It was a binding-signature. She had signed her own journal the way she signed everything: with a lock.

"She warded it," I said. "Against readers."

"Against everyone," Maren corrected. "For sixty years I have known this ledger existed and I have not been able to crack more than a tenth of the cipher. The text itself fights you — the substitutions shift, the grammar doubles back. I assumed she was simply brilliant. Now I suspect she was simply thorough. The words are the outer lock." She looked at me. "You may be the inner one. Blood of her blood, craft of her craft. Touch the clasp and read."

"What if there's something in there she didn't want anyone to find?"

"Then we'll have a very interesting conversation about consent with a dead woman," Maren said dryly, "and I'll lose, because dead women with wards always win arguments. Touch it, Elodie. We are running out of time faster than either of us says aloud. The king forgets more every week. Last Tuesday he signed an execution order addressed to a man who died in the last reign. Whatever she left us in there, we need it soon."

I touched the brass knot.

Cold. That was the first thing — a cold that had nothing to do with metal temperature, a cold like the attention I'd felt in the throne room when the Hollow had noticed me across thirty feet of marble. But this cold was different in kind, the way a winter stream differs from a winter grave. This cold was *deliberate*. It assessed me. I had the unmistakable sensation of being tasted by something small and fierce and extremely suspicious.

*Ivy,* I thought, absurdly. *It's me. It's Wren—* no. Wrong name. *It's Elodie. Your daughter's son.*

Whether the thought reached it or the blood in my fingers did, I don't know. But the cold relented, unfolded, and became something else entirely: a doorway.

I fell.

Not visions — *voice*. Her voice, the one I had spent years pretending not to hear in my memory, roughened by pipe smoke and market wind, and it was saying words I had never heard her say, and they were not ciphered, because she had never meant them for cipher. They were meant for whoever came after. Meant, maybe, for exactly this moment.

*"If you're reading this, then the blood held, and I'm sorry for what that cost you. Listen well, child, because I'll only say this once and then the ward closes and I am only ink again.*

*The Hollow does not want power. Power is a tool, and tools bore it. What it wants is the pantry — the whole inheritance of a life, every meal and grief and lullaby, eaten down to the salt. It cannot take what is freely kept, and it cannot refuse what is freely given. Remember that. Both halves matter. Free-kept and free-given. That is the lock and that is the key, and I built my binding on the giving side because I was young and it seemed a fine trade.*

*It was not a fine trade. I do not remember my mother's face. I do not remember the sea. Write down everything you love, child, and hide the writing, because someday you will need to know what was yours.*

*One more thing. It knows its name. It hates its name. Names are the one memory it cannot digest — it swallowed its own long ago and it has been starving ever since. Starving things bargain. Make it bargain.*

*Now close the book. You've had enough for one day. Wash your hands, eat something, and sleep. Even readers must eat.*

*With all the love I have left, which is more than you'd think —*

*— Ivy Fairweather, who was born with another name and gave it away too."*

The voice ended. The ward folded shut behind it like a door closing in an empty house, and I came back to the morning room with tears running freely down my face and Maren's hand hard on my shoulder, shaking me, saying my name, saying it again.

I told her. All of it. She wrote as fast as I spoke, iron-gall flying, and when I reached the part about names — *it swallowed its own, it cannot digest them, make it bargain* — her pen stopped dead, and she stared at the middle distance with the expression of a scholar watching ten years of dead ends suddenly arrange themselves into a road.

"The true name," she whispered. "In chapter eleven of the cipher — the fragment I could never resolve — I thought it was a title. *The Unfed. The Hollow Below the Crown.* But it's not a title. It's a description of its wound. Its actual name exists, and it's somewhere in her cipher, and she hid it from herself because—" Maren pressed both hands flat against the table, steadying something. "Because if she'd known it, the Hollow might have taken it from her. From her memories. She locked it away from her own mind and left it only in ink."

"So we break the cipher."

"We break the cipher." She was already pulling pages toward her, already sorting, her mind visibly gone into the archive's deep stacks. "The shifting substitution, the doubled grammar — it's keyed to something personal, it always is, it has to be—"

But I had stopped listening. Because I was looking at the ledger, at the brass knot-clasp, and I was realizing what else her voice had given me besides instructions.

*Wash your hands, eat something, and sleep.*

Sixty years dead, and her first instinct had been to feed me.

"Maren," I said. "The cipher. Would she have keyed it to a recipe?"

Maren looked up. "Why?"

"Because she taught me one when I was seven. Honey and black bread, for courage. She said the measurements mattered more than the words, and she made me say them back until I dreamed them." My heart was going strangely. "Every cipher needs a key phrase. And she was the sort of woman who would hide the key to her life's work inside the one thing she made sure I'd never forget."

Maren went very still. Then she pulled the ciphered pages across the table, turned to the earliest entry, and slid them in front of me together with a blank sheet and a pen.

"Measurements," she said. "Exactly as she taught you. Don't change a single grain."

I dipped the pen. Outside, Ashvale went on with its gray afternoon — bells, gulls, somewhere in the lower city the faint smell of smoke that might have been a bakery or might not — and I began to write my grandmother's recipe for courage, line by line, while the lock on her life's work waited to see if I still remembered it true.
