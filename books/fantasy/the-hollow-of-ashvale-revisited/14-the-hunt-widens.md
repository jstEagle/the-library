## 14. The Hunt Widens

The ash from Bettina's own charms still stained the cobbles outside her shop when the first silver changed hands.

She saw it happen the way you see weather coming — a hush in the lane, a knot of people forming around a man she'd sold bread to for eleven years. Tomas Grell, widower, occasional drunk, dependable customer. He stood on the step of the warden's post with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, and a warden counted six coins into his palm. Six. Bettina had sold him a day-old loaf that morning for two coppers and he'd haggled her down to one.

Six silver for a name.

She couldn't hear whose name it was. She didn't need to. The way the crowd leaned in — hungry, careful, ashamed of its hunger — told her everything. By evening the whole market would know, and by morning someone else would be standing on that step with their hat in their hands, because that was how it worked now. Fear was a currency, and Ashvale had never been rich enough to resist spending it.

Bettina closed her shutters early and sat in the dark behind her counter with her hands flat on the wood, feeling for the ghosts of what she'd burned. Three weeks ago she had carried her whole stock into the street — the fever-knots, the milk-charms, the little braided cords against nightmare — and fed them to a brazier while her neighbors watched. It had been a performance. That was the shame of it, the part she didn't say out loud even to herself: she hadn't burned them because she believed they were wicked. She'd burned them so there would be witnesses, so that when the hunters came through with their lists, someone would remember Bettina the baker standing righteously in the smoke.

It had worked, too. For three weeks it had worked.

Now Tomas Grell had six silver, and the lists were getting longer, and the warden's post had started keeping hours after dark.

* * *

The edict had been nailed to the bakery door before dawn, and by the time Bettina unlocked her ovens half the street had read it twice. She read it a third time anyway, because that was what fear did to you — made you check the same terrible arithmetic over and over, hoping the numbers had changed.

BY ORDER OF THE LORD PROTECTOR, it began, in that official hand that always looked like it was pressing too hard on the page. THE GIFTED ARE HEREBY DECLARED ENEMIES OF THE CROWN. ANY PERSON HAVING KNOWLEDGE OF CHARM-WORK, HEATHEN PRACTICE, OR UNLICENSED ARTS SHALL REPORT SAME TO THE WARDENS. REWARD OF SILVER SHALL BE PAID FOR TRUE DENUNCIATION. PENALTY FOR CONCEALMENT SHALL BE EQUAL TO THE CRIME CONCEALED.

Equal to the crime concealed. That was the sentence everyone read four times. Not a fine. Not a warning. Equal. If your neighbor charmed a fever out of her child and you said nothing, you burned beside her. The crown had not built a trap; it had built a mirror, and set every street in Ashvale in front of it, and waited to see what the city would do with its own reflection.

What the city did, mostly, was burn things.

Old Halvard brought out his mother's prayer-bones and smashed them in the gutter rather than be found with them. The Widow Senn took down the protective horseshoe her late husband had nailed over her door forty years ago and left it in the road like something dead. And Bettina — Bettina, who had learned herb-craft from her grandmother the way other girls learned knitting, who could ease a colic or sweeten a sour udder or tie a luck-knot that actually held — Bettina filled a brazier with her life's work and lit it while the street watched, and kept her face smooth as fresh dough the whole time.

"You're doing right," the butcher's wife told her afterward, squeezing her arm. "Better safe."

"Better safe," Bettina agreed, and went home, and did not sleep.

Because here was the thing she could not say aloud: the charms had been true. Not grand magic, nothing that would make the wardens' eyes widen — but real. A fever-knot tied with the right words pulled heat out of a sick child; she'd seen it work a dozen times. Her grandmother's milk-charm had saved more calves than the village vet. These were small mercies, kitchen-magic, the kind of thing that lived in the space between medicine and prayer, and the crown had declared war on them, and Bettina had burned hers in the street like a coward holding up her ticket.

And now the midwife was in the cells.

* * *

Everyone knew Sorrel. That was the problem with arresting a midwife — you arrested half the city's history along with her. Sorrel had put her hands under nearly every woman in the market district for thirty years. She'd delivered Bettina herself, and Bettina's two sons, and she'd sat with Bettina through the worst night of her life when the third one came too early and wouldn't stay, and she'd held Bettina's hand and said, *some seeds are meant for a different soil, love,* and somehow that had been bearable when nothing else was.

They took her on a Tuesday. No denunciation, at first — just a summons, then a search, then the cells beneath the Mint, because they'd found dried herbs in her kit and a birthing-stone carved with an old sign nobody living could read anymore. The wardens called it charm-work. Every woman on the street called it what it was: thirty years of catching babies.

But then came the second part. Then came the whisper that Sorrel wasn't just accused — she was being questioned about *others*. Names. Who had come to her for charms. Who had paid her in herbs instead of coin. Who had asked her, in the privacy of a birthing room, for the small forbidden helps that mothers ask for when a child won't thrive.

And Bettina understood, lying awake in the dark with her husband's slow breathing beside her, that she was on whatever list Sorrel might give up. That everyone was. That the arithmetic of the edict had reached into the cells beneath the Mint and was working itself out down there in the dark, cell by cell, and that Sorrel — proud, stubborn, seventy-year-old Sorrel — was the only wall between the wardens and half the women in Ashvale.

She also understood what it would cost to keep that wall standing.

* * *

The market turned on itself the way a body turns on an infection — slowly, then all at once, then everywhere.

It started with the small betrayals, the ones that could pass for accidents. A tanner mentioned to a warden that the cooper's wife bought "odd herbs." A boy reported that old Halvard still muttered over his food before eating, which was grace, but try explaining grace to a warden with a ledger. The fishmonger's stall grew a hand-painted sign: NO CHARMS SOLD HERE, which was such a naked piece of theater that Bettina almost laughed, except that two days later the cooper's wife added one too, and then the chandler, and then it wasn't funny anymore, it was a map — every sign a confession of terror, every terror a wound the city was picking open.

People stopped greeting each other. That was the truest measure. Ashvale's market had always run on talk — prices shouted across lanes, gossip traded like small change, complaints about the weather binding strangers into temporary kinship. Now the lanes went quiet. People looked at their feet. A conversation with the wrong person, overheard by the wrong person, was six silver waiting to happen.

Tomas Grell got his six silver on a Thursday. On Friday he bought bread from Bettina, and she sold it to him, and neither of them looked at each other, and the loaf weighed heavy in his hands like something he owed her.

By the next week there were men she didn't know loitering near the well, men with clean boots and nothing to sell, listening. One of them bought a roll from her and ate it standing in her doorway, watching the street over her shoulder, and thanked her with a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

That night she found the chalk mark on her doorframe. A small thing — a single stroke, low, where a child might reach. She scrubbed it off before her husband saw. She never learned if it meant *watched*, or *safe*, or *next*, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

Sorrel's granddaughter came to the back door on a Sunday, hood up, in the hour between last light and lamplighting when honest people are indoors and the lane belongs to shadows.

Bettina almost didn't answer the knock. That was the new reflex — the flinch, the frozen moment, the calculation of witnesses. She hated herself for it and answered anyway, and there stood Ilsa, seventeen years old, eyes red-rimmed, hands shaking around a covered basket.

"They let me bring her clean linen," the girl whispered. "One visit. They watched the whole time, but she — Gran got close, at the end, and she said—" Ilsa's voice cracked and reassembled itself. "She said to tell the baker: *the list is long, and my memory is old.*"

Bettina's mouth went dry. *My memory is old.* Seventy years of names, and a mind the wardens thought they could crack. But Sorrel hadn't said *I'll give them nothing.* She'd said *my memory is old* — which meant she was choosing what to forget, holding the names behind her teeth, and it was killing her, and she needed Bettina to know that the wall was holding but only barely.

"What do they want from her?" Bettina asked, though she knew.

"Names. Mothers. Anyone who ever—" Ilsa glanced past Bettina's shoulder into the dark kitchen, as if the walls had grown ears along with everyone else. "There's a hearing. Ten days. After that they transfer her to the palace courts, and Gran says no one comes back from the palace courts."

The basket. Bettina looked at it. "What's this?"

"Her kit. What they didn't take. They gave it back to me — said it was evidence of nothing." Ilsa's laugh was a horrible small sound. "Evidence of nothing. Thirty years of catching babies, evidence of nothing, unless they decide otherwise."

Inside the basket, under the linen, Bettina found the birthing-stone with the old carved sign, wrapped in cloth like something wounded. And folded beneath it, a scrap of paper in Sorrel's cramped hand — not a list, thank all the quiet gods, but a note, five words long:

*If they come asking, remember nothing.*

Which meant: *if they come asking you about me, deny me.* Which meant Sorrel had already decided to spend whatever remained of her old memory protecting the names, and wanted Bettina armed with ignorance when the questions came.

Bettina stood in her kitchen holding a piece of paper that could hang them both, and felt the full weight of the edict settle onto her shoulders like wet wool.

* * *

Here is what cowardice looks like from the inside: it looks like prudence. It looks like keeping your head down, feeding your family, saying nothing, surviving. Bettina had told herself all of it for three weeks. *You burned the charms. You're clean. You have sons. One of them is nine. What good are you to anyone ash on the pyre?*

And every night the arithmetic shifted a little, the way rot spreads through a wall — invisible, patient, structural.

Because she *had* sold charms. Because Sorrel knew it. Because Sorrel was holding that knowledge behind her teeth at cost of her own flesh, down in the dark beneath the Mint, and every day Bettina stayed silent and safe, she was letting a seventy-year-old woman pay her debt for her.

And because — this was the part that finally broke her, on the ninth night, standing at her window looking down at the lane where she'd burned her life's work — because of what the wardens were doing in the birthing chambers.

Ilsa had heard it from a laundress who served the warden barracks: they weren't just questioning Sorrel about charms anymore. They'd brought in a physician from the palace, some gray-faced man who examined the birthing-stone and asked strange questions. Had Sorrel attended births in the upper town? In the palace itself? Had anything been *taken* at those births? Taken — as if a newborn could lose something before it had anything to lose.

Bettina didn't understand it. But she understood that the hunt had stopped being about hedge-witches selling luck-knots, and that the questions were coming from above — from the palace, from wherever the Lord Protector's shadow fell longest — and that Sorrel, who had caught half the city's children with these two hands, knew something the gray-faced physician wanted badly enough to keep an old woman in a cell for.

Ten days until the hearing. Nine, now.

* * *

She told her husband on the tenth night, because she was not a liar in her own house, whatever else she was.

"I'm going to get her out."

Marek set down his cup very carefully, the way he did when the ground moved. "Bettina."

"The hearing's tomorrow. After that she goes to the palace and she's gone, and everything she knows goes with her, and everything she's protecting gets dragged out of her anyway, because they have ways, Marek, everyone knows they have ways—"

"And if they catch you?" His voice stayed low. Twenty-two years of marriage had taught them both to fight in whispers. "If they catch you, it isn't the pyre for you alone. Equal to the crime concealed. Me. The boys. The boys, Bettina."

"I know the arithmetic." She'd done it a hundred times, laying awake. "I've done it every night since Ilsa came. And here's what I keep finding: I can raise our sons in a city where I let Sorrel hang alone, and I can feed them and clothe them and watch them grow into men who learned from me exactly what a person does when it's their turn in the cells. Or I can be worth what she spent."

"It isn't your debt."

"No," Bettina said. "It's worse. It's a gift. She's paying for all of us and she never asked us for anything, and that's what makes it ours."

Marek looked at her for a long time. Outside, the curfew bell rang, and somewhere down the lane a door closed softly — everyone indoors, everyone hiding, the whole street holding its breath. He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he looked ten years older and twenty years clearer.

"The laundry carts go out of the Mint yard at dawn," he said. "Before the wardens change shift. There's a gap — Doss used to drive one, before his lungs went. He owes me for the winter's flour." He said it flatly, a man reading a bill of sale, and Bettina understood that he had been doing the same arithmetic she had, every night, in the dark beside her, and had simply been waiting for her to finish first.

"Marek—"

"Don't thank me." He stood, and blew out the lamp, and in the darkness his voice came steady and terrible. "Thank me by making sure it works. Because if it doesn't, I'll need you to have been worth it too."

* * *

The plan was simple, which is the only kind that survives contact with fear. Doss's cart, the linen delivery Ilsa was still permitted, a false bottom under the washing. The gap at the shift-change. The east road out of the Mint yard ran past the tanneries, where the smell was so foul that no warden lingered.

What no plan could account for was the waiting. Bettina spent the last night before dawn sitting in her kitchen with the birthing-stone in her lap, turning it over and over, feeling the old carved sign under her thumb. Her grandmother had worn one like it. *For the hands that catch,* the old women called them. Protection for the threshold where life came through — the one door in a house that magic had always guarded without apology, back before the crown decided doors were crimes.

Somewhere across the sleeping city, Sorrel was sitting in the dark with her old memory, rationing it out name by name, buying the street another day with pieces of herself. And tomorrow, if the gods of thresholds and laundries and desperate bakers were kind, Bettina would walk her out through a gate built on the assumption that no one would dare.

The sky was going gray at the edges when Marek came down the stairs already dressed.

"Bettina," he said quietly. "There's someone at the back door."

Her heart stopped, restarted wrong. Wardens didn't knock. Informers didn't come at dawn. She crossed the kitchen and lifted the latch, and there on her step, hooded and hollow-eyed, stood Ilsa — no basket this time, no permission slip, just the girl herself, swaying with exhaustion, and behind her in the blue pre-dawn shadow, leaning on Ilsa's arm with her wrists still ringed in raw skin—

Sorrel.

"They moved the hearing up," Ilsa whispered. "To this morning. So last night — Gran, how did you—" 

"Thirty years of catching babies," Sorrel rasped, and her voice was iron filings and gravel and the most beautiful sound Bettina had ever heard. "You think I don't know every loose stone and bribed turnkey in that building? Get me off this step, baker. We haven't got till the cart."

Bettina pulled them both inside and threw the bolt, and only then did she see what Sorrel clutched against her chest with both freed hands, wrapped in the same cloth as the birthing-stone: papers. Dozens of them, stolen pages, smuggled out against her heart.

"Not my testimony," Sorrel said, following her eyes. "Theirs. Records. From the palace births." She pushed them into Bettina's arms like a live coal. "Something up there has been *feeding* on the newborns, girl. And I stole the proof — which means every warden in Ashvale is about to learn exactly what's missing from that cell."

Above the rooftops, the first bell of dawn began to ring — and then stopped, cut short, and started again in a pattern Bettina had never heard before.

The alarm at the Mint.
