## 15. What the Crown Eats

The archives of Ashvale kept their secrets the way the mint kept its coin: behind iron, under seal, and accessible only to those who already knew what they were looking for.

Maren Voss had spent eleven years of her life in those stacks before they stripped her title and turned her out, and she knew every trick of the place — which clerks could be bribed with plum wine, which ledgers were misfiled on purpose, which shelf-ends sagged because someone a century dead had hidden things inside them. But she had never needed any of that skill as badly as she needed it now, working by lamplight in her own tower, with Elodie Fairweather asleep one floor above her and a parasite wearing the crown of Ashvale three streets away.

She started, as all honest research starts, with a list of kings.

It was a short list. That was the first thing that bothered her. Ashvale was old — older than its own records claimed, older than the founding myth of Aldric's line — and yet the official regnal rolls named only nine kings across three hundred years. Nine. Neighboring realms of similar age counted twenty, thirty, forty reigns. Dynasties rose and fell elsewhere like weather. In Ashvale, the succession moved like a procession: slow, solemn, and always in the same direction.

She wrote the names out on butcher's paper and pinned them above her desk.

Aldous I. Bertram. Aldric I. Edmond. Corwin. Aldous II. Bertram II. Edric. Aldric II. And now Aldric III, who called his dead daughter by a servant girl's name and stared at his own crown the way a starving man stares through a bakery window.

Three names repeating. She saw the pattern in the repetition itself — *Aldric* again, *Bertram* again, *Edric*, *Edmond* — names resurrected generation after generation, as if the kingdom kept reaching for them. Or as if something in the palace kept reaching, and the grieving country obliged.

Maren pulled the accession rolls next.

Here the clerks' hands grew careful. Each new king's coronation was recorded with the same liturgical language: *the crown was set upon him and he received it gladly.* The same phrase, she noticed, in every single roll across three centuries. Not merely traditional — identical. The same seven words, the same ink flourishes even, copied so faithfully that later scribes must have been tracing the earlier ones rather than writing fresh. She had catalogued enough forgeries and enough rituals to know the difference between reverence and instruction. This was instruction. Someone, at some point, had decided exactly how that sentence must look forever.

*He received it gladly.*

She sat back and looked at that line for a long time.

Then she began pulling the death records, and the night went strange.

* * *

Kings of Ashvale did not die of war. That much she'd known — the realm had fought its border skirmishes like everyone else, but no monarch had fallen in battle since before the rolls began. What she hadn't registered, until the deaths lay spread before her in a row, was how *tidy* they all were.

Bertram: died in sleep, aged fifty-one, "after a long contentment."

Aldric I: died in his chair by the orchard window, aged fifty-three, "smiling."

Edmond: died mid-prayer, aged fifty-four.

Corwin: died at table, between the fish course and the sweet, aged fifty-two.

Aldous II: died in the garden he loved, aged fifty-five.

Bertram II: died in sleep, aged fifty.

Edric: died listening to music, aged fifty-three.

Aldric II: died watching rain, aged fifty-four.

Every king beloved at his passing. Every king mourned with genuine public grief — the broadsheets confirmed it, the funeral accounts confirmed it, the weeping crowds described in dispatch after dispatch. And every king dead within a two-year band of age, in circumstances of almost comic gentleness, never ill in any documented way beforehand, never wounded, never failing. One year beloved. Two years beloved. Then, somewhere past fifty, quietly finished, like a candle pinched rather than burned down.

Maren had read plague chronicles. She had read assassination inquiries, succession crises, the ugly arithmetic of poisoned cups. Death was usually messy and usually contested. This was neither. This was *harvest.*

The word arrived in her mind uninvited and refused to leave.

She thought of Elodie's grandmother's ledger, the ciphered pages they had only half decoded, and one line in particular that had stopped them both cold three nights ago:

*It does not kill the king. It finishes him. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of the danger.*

At the time Maren had read it as metaphor — the kind of flourish hedge-witches used when they wanted their notes to sound oracular. Now, with eight tidy royal deaths arranged in front of her like courses at a banquet, it read as procedure.

But it was the succession records that finally made her get up and pace the tower room, because the pattern there was worse than the deaths. It was worse in a way she couldn't immediately articulate, and she hated herself for the several minutes it took her to find the shape of it.

Each heir, at coronation, was stronger than the last.

Not politically — politically the throne's power waxed and waned with wars and harvests like any other realm's. She meant the *host*. The court physicians' private journals, which a disgraced archivist could still purchase from a certain clerk with a taste for plum wine, recorded the kings' constitutions with brutal frankness. And across three hundred years, the trend ran one direction. Aldric I had begun forgetting things at forty-eight. Edmond at forty-six. Corwin at forty-one. By the time you reached Aldric II, the lapses began at thirty-seven, and the physicians' notes grew correspondingly stranger — one of them, a man named Halvard, wrote in the margin of his final casebook: *His Majesty mislaid the word for "daughter" today and reached instead for a word I do not know. It sounded old.*

*Sounded old.* A word older than the dynasty, surfacing through a king's eroding mind like a stone through melting ice.

And each successive king lasted *less time* between coronation and decline — not more, though each host was younger and healthier when crowned. Whatever wore the crown was getting better at it. Faster. Hungrier. Three hundred years of practice eating kings, and the meals came quicker now.

Maren stood at her window in the grey hour before dawn, looking down at the sleeping city, and made herself say it plainly, because plainly was the only way she trusted anything.

Something had ruled Ashvale for longer than any house on the regnal roll. Longer than the Aldrics, longer than the Bertrams, longer than the founding myth itself. It had worn nine faces and buried nine bodies and attended nine state funerals where it wept, presumably, most convincingly of anyone present. It had chosen its hosts young and beloved and full of memory — because memory was what it ate, and a king was simply the richest pantry in the kingdom. It grew fat on a reign, thinned its host, finished him gently amid universal grief, and slid into the next crown before the bells finished tolling.

And every cycle, it fed deeper. Every cycle, the kingdom forgot a little more of itself and never knew what it had lost. Old words surfacing and dying in dying minds. Founding myths that didn't quite match the ruins. A city whose oldest stones remembered things its oldest books did not.

Ashvale wasn't ruled by a dynasty. Ashvale was a larder with walls around it.

* * *

Elodie found her at midmorning surrounded by paper, still in yesterday's clothes, and had the decency not to comment on it. He set down bread and hard cheese and a pot of honey and said only, "You've the look of someone who's been reading all night and wishes she hadn't."

"I've been reading three hundred years," Maren said. "Sit down. I need you to tell me if this sounds as mad aloud as it looks written down."

So she laid it out for him — the nine names, the repeated phrase, the tidy deaths, the accelerating declines, the word that *sounded old*. He listened the way he seemed to listen to everything, completely, his whole attention settling on you like a hand. When she finished, he was quiet a moment, turning his cup without drinking.

"My grandmother's ledger," he said slowly. "There was a page we couldn't read — the binding-notes, the ones in the oldest cipher. You said the early pages referenced a court. A taster's post." He looked up. "You think she was close to it. To the thing itself."

"I think your family has been circling this thing for generations, and I think the ledger knows more than either of us has dared read yet." Maren pulled the ciphered pages toward her. "But first — the roll. Look at the coronation formula. Tell me what you taste in it."

That was their shorthand now, half joke and wholly serious: she brought the documents, and he brought the gift she was only beginning to understand he had — an ability to read what objects remembered, what ink carried, what intention clung to old paper like smoke to wool. He'd recovered his grandmother's voice from the ledger's binding once already. Maren had watched him do it, and had not slept well afterward, and had immediately begun planning how to make him do it again.

He took the accession roll in both hands. Closed his eyes. His lips parted slightly, the way they did — she was learning his tells, cataloguing them against the day it would matter.

"It's cold," he said at last, quietly. "Underneath the ink, it's — there's a blessing written over something else. Like paint over a door." His eyes opened. "The words are for the king. The thing underneath is for... whoever set the words there. It's an agreement, Maren. The sentence isn't tradition. It's a *contract clause.* 'Received it gladly.' Gladness is part of the terms."

"Consent," Maren said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The same principle as the unbinding rite. Your grandmother's notes — willing memories, freely given. The Hollow works in consent. It can't simply take the crown; the host has to receive it gladly. So it makes sure every heir is raised to want exactly one thing."

"To be king," Elodie said. "To wear it. To be glad."

"And then it eats the gladness out of him from the inside, one memory at a time, and calls the result a long contentment." Maren heard her own voice go flat and hard, the way it went when feeling became useless. "Nine kings. Do you understand what I'm telling you? It isn't a curse on the Aldric line. It isn't even a parasite that got lucky once. Ashvale has never — in all its recorded history — had a free king. We have never once, not for a single reign, governed ourselves."

The silence after that had weight. Down in the street below the tower, a cart rattled past, and someone was singing, some nonsense market tune, and the ordinary sound of it was obscene.

Elodie put down the roll carefully, as if it might wake.

"The midnight meal," he said. "You said the feeding runs deepest at the king's midnight meal. That's why the timing matters for the rite."

"That's the window. But Elodie—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "I need you to hear the scale of this before you decide anything. I came into this thinking we were saving one king from one parasite. That's not what this is. This is the oldest continuous government in the known world, and it is a stomach. If we fail tomorrow night, it doesn't matter. It finds the next heir, the gladness is arranged, and in forty years some archivist like me notices the same pattern and weeps into her wine. But if we succeed—" she gestured at the butcher's paper, the nine names, "—we're not curing a sick man. We're decapitating three hundred years of quiet rule. Everything downstream of that crown changes. Everything."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, in the mild voice she was learning meant he had already decided something, "My grandmother bound it once. At the cost of everything she was. She left us the map precisely so someone wouldn't have to pay that price again — or so someone would know the price before paying it. Either way." He shrugged. "Rent is rent, Maren."

It was the phrase he'd used about selling forbidden charms to desperate people, back when she'd first pried his history out of him, and hearing it applied to this made her chest hurt.

"You may not survive it," she said. "Or survive it as no one."

"I know."

"And I have to write the contingency letters tonight, and I am going to hate every word of them."

"I know that too." He pushed the honey pot toward her, an absurd small kindness. "Eat something. Kings die of forgetting; archivists die of not eating. Different mechanism, same ending."

Despite everything, despite the nine graves and the contract clause and the word that sounded old, Maren laughed — a short, cracked sound, but real.

* * *

Alone again in the evening, she returned to the one thread she hadn't yet pulled, because it was the one that frightened her most personally.

Halvard, the physician of Aldric II's decline, had kept marginalia beyond medicine. In his last surviving casebook, purchased at shameful expense, he'd written observations he clearly never intended for the court's eyes — speculations about "the appetite in the crown," about dreams the king reported of "a hall of doors, all open, all leading to rooms I have lived in," and, on the final page, in a hand gone shaky:

*I have checked the oldest registers. Before the Aldrics there are no kings listed. Before the Aldrics there is no *before*. Yet the palace foundations run deeper than the founding date allows, and the masons' marks in the lowest courses match no guild ever chartered. Something was here when we built over it. Something let us build. I begin to suspect the city was not founded. I begin to suspect it was stocked.*

*Stocked.* Maren closed the casebook and sat with the lamplight guttering, and let the full weight of it settle onto her shoulders, where it would stay.

A larder with walls. A kingdom as a keeping-cellar, stocked and tended and rotated, generation by generation, while the thing below the crown grew older and more practiced and more patient than any enemy humanity had ever named.

Tomorrow at midnight, Elodie Fairweather would walk into that cellar carrying the only weapon anyone had ever found — a body full of poison and a heart full of memories it was willing to give away.

Maren took out fresh paper. She dipped her pen. And she began, in a steady hand, to write the letter she prayed she would never send — beginning, because honesty demanded it, with the words:

*If you are reading this, the Hollow has won again, and here is what you must know about how long it has already won...*
