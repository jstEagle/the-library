## 17. The Fisherman's Daughter

She was waiting for him at the corner of Brackwater Lane for three days before he noticed her.

That was the thing about Wren Tallow — she had her father's patience and none of his appetite. Old Tallow could wait on a dock from dawn until the herring ran; Wren could wait on a person. She'd done it once before, two years ago, standing outside his stall while he sold a fever-charm to a cooper's wife, not speaking, not leaving, just watching with those grey eyes the color of harbor water in November, until finally he'd asked what she wanted and she'd said, "A knot that holds," as if it were the most obvious thing in the world.

Now she stood at the lane's mouth where the cobbles gave way to mud and the smell of the fish-market began, hood up against the drizzle, and did not approach.

Elodie saw her on the third day because Maren's tower work had made him careless — or honest, which amounted to the same thing. He'd been walking home from the archive with a satchel of copied pages hidden under a false bottom, moving with the careful casualness of a man who'd learned to hide things young, when some animal part of his brain snagged on a familiar silhouette and refused to let it go.

He stopped. He considered walking on. There were a dozen reasons to walk on: the edict still nailed to the bakery door, the silver price on names, the fact that he was now carrying evidence of conspiracy in a bag over his shoulder. There was also one reason to stop, and it was shaped like a girl of nineteen who had once bought luck from him with coins counted out of a mending jar.

He crossed the lane.

"You've been standing there three days," he said.

"Two." Her voice was rougher than he remembered. "Yesterday I lost my nerve."

"That must have been difficult. It's such a long walk."

It came out sharper than he'd intended. He watched it land — watched her flinch the way you flinch at a door slamming, not because it surprised you but because you'd been braced for it all day anyway. Good, some small bitter voice in him said. Let her carry something heavy too.

"I need to give this back." She pulled something from inside her coat, and there it was: the luck-knot. Grey-green wool, frayed now at one end, salt-stiff from years of sea air, tied the way he'd taught himself to tie them in his grandmother's kitchen before he understood what half the words in her ledger meant. A knot that holds. He'd sold her that one the week after her mother died, back when selling charms had been simple commerce and not a hanging offense, back when he'd charged her four coppers and thrown in a blessing for free because she'd looked like she needed one more than the money.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because it doesn't work anymore." She held it out flat on her palm, the way you'd return a broken tool. "I've been wearing it since I was seventeen. Every crossing. Every storm. And then my father—" Her jaw worked. "And I thought, either it never worked, or it worked right up until the moment it mattered most, and I don't know which is worse."

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, and both of them did the thing everyone in Ashvale did now — checked the driver's face, measured him, dismissed him, all in half a second. Fear arriving like weather, undeniable and communal. You couldn't even have a conversation in the open without performing it.

"Not here," Elodie said. "The chapel steps on Gull Street are dry. Nobody prays anymore, so nobody watches there."

* * *

The chapel of Saint Ferren had been deconsecrated eleven years ago and was now used mostly by couriers napping between routes and children playing at soldiers among the fallen roof tiles. Its saint had been some minor protector of thresholds, which struck Elodie as darkly funny these days — a god whose whole jurisdiction was doors, in a city where every door might open onto a warden.

They sat on the steps under the broken arch, out of the rain, and Wren set the luck-knot between them like an offering on an altar nobody tended.

"Say it plainly," Elodie said. "Whatever you came to say. I'm tired of Ashvale sentences, where everything means its opposite."

"My father denounced you."

"Yes. I was there. Well — I wasn't there, but I heard about it afterward, in a cell under the Mint, which is nearly the same as being there." He kept his voice level. He'd practiced level voices for three years in the king's dining hall, tasting arsenic with a pleasant face. This should have been easy.

"He didn't want to." 

"No," Elodie agreed. "He wanted the silver. Those aren't the same thing either."

"He owed money to the mint-factors. After the season failed. And they were going to take the boat, and the boat is—" She stopped, pressed her knuckles against her mouth for a moment, started again. "The boat is everything. You know what it is. You grew up on these lanes same as me."

"I grew up above a charm-stall with a woman who taught me letters in cipher so the wardens couldn't read my homework," Elodie said. "So no, Wren, I don't know about boats. But I know about everything being one thing, and knowing that if you lose it you lose yourself. That's why I hid my stock under the floorboards instead of burning it in the street like Bettina did. We're all one bad season from becoming informers. I understand it better than you think."

"Then why does it sound like a knife when you say it?"

Elodie looked at her. Rain dripped off the broken arch between them, ticking against the stone like a slow clock. In the chapel behind them, two courier boys were arguing over dice, their voices echoing off saints' niches that hadn't held statues since before either of them was born.

"Because it is one," he said finally. "Here's the truth, since you asked for plain. I sat in Rell's cell and I thought about your father, and do you know what I felt? Not rage. Rage would have been clean. I felt *stupid*. I felt like the fool in every market story — the man who trusts his customers, who remembers birthdays, who gives credit in a hard winter. I built a whole life on being liked, Wren. Being useful. Being *safe* because people needed charms and I was the one who sold them. And your father took all of that apart in an afternoon for forty pieces of silver and I couldn't even hate him properly, because hating him meant admitting the whole architecture was rotten from the start."

Wren was crying now, quietly, the way harbor people cry — without drama, without sound, tears just doing their work while the face stays busy. He remembered her mother had cried the same way, at the funeral, standing at the graveside with her hands folded like a woman waiting for bread to prove.

"I went to the cells," Wren said. "After they took you. I stood outside the Mint for an hour trying to make myself go in and tell them they had the wrong man. Tell them it was me, that the charms were mine, that Father didn't even know what half of them were."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I'm a coward. Because they'd have taken me too, and then who would have paid Father's debts? Because I stood there doing sums, the same sums he did." She laughed, wet and awful. "You see? It runs in the family. We're accountants of the soul. Everything weighed, everything costed."

Elodie picked up the luck-knot. It was lighter than he remembered, or his hands had changed. Three years of poison will change your hands — thicken the skin, steady the tremor, teach them to hold a cup of certain death without spilling. He turned the knot over. At the frayed end, where the wool had worn through, someone had tried to mend it — badly, with mismatched thread, the stitches of a girl who could splice a net in her sleep but had never sewn anything soft.

"You mended it," he said.

"It was coming apart. I didn't want—" She wiped her face with her sleeve, furious at herself for crying, which only made it worse. "I didn't want it to come apart before I could give it back. That seemed like one more theft."

They sat with that. Down in the chapel the dice game ended; one boy crowed, the other swore, footsteps faded into the rain. Saint Ferren, protector of thresholds, watched nothing from his empty niche, and Elodie thought about thresholds — about how you can't stand in a doorway forever, how every forgiveness is a door and you have to choose which side of it to live on.

"Maren Voss got me out," he said.

"The archivist. People talk. They say she bought you like a horse."

"She bought me like a research asset, which is worse. A horse gets fed whether it's useful or not." He almost smiled. Almost. "I'm working for her now. Against the thing that's wrong with the king."

Wren's head came up sharply. "You know what's wrong with him?"

"I know some of it. Not all. Less than I'll know next month, more than anyone at court knows, probably. And if I told you any of it, you'd be carrying it, and everything you carry is something a warden can torture out of you, so let's agree you know nothing except that I'm alive and employed." He said it gently, but he said it firmly, and she nodded, because harbor people understand cargo limits.

Then, quieter: "Your father. What happened to him?"

"He drinks now. He sits in the Anchor and drinks and doesn't look at anyone, and when the hunters' bell rings in the night he goes white and checks the locks twice." She stared at her own hands, chapped red at the knuckles, netter's hands. "Mother would have flayed him. Mother would have marched down to the Mint herself and offered them her life for yours, and she'd have been insufferable about it afterward too."

"Would she?"

"She'd have brought you soup every day for a year and mentioned constantly how much trouble it was." The ghost of a smile crossed her face and died young. "I'm not her. I stood outside doing sums."

"Wren."

"Don't forgive me." It burst out of her fast, like something she'd rehearsed on all those days at the lane's mouth. "Don't you dare forgive me quickly. Everyone forgives quick in this city, it's cheaper than thinking. Bettina burned her own charms and we all nodded like that fixed something. The priest at Saint Osla forgave the denunciations from the pulpit, said we were all frightened, said God weighs fear kindly. Well, maybe He does. I don't. I stood outside a prison doing arithmetic while you sat inside it, and if you forgive me today, tomorrow, this week, then I get to put it down, and I don't deserve to put it down yet."

The rain came harder, drumming on tile and stone, filling the silence she left. Elodie held the luck-knot in his closed fist and felt its shape press into his palm — the old shape, the shape his grandmother's hands had taught his mother's hands had taught his. A knot that holds. He thought about what holding actually meant. Not gripping. Not clutching. Bearing weight without letting go and without pretending the weight isn't there.

"You're right," he said.

Her breath caught.

"You're right that quick forgiveness is cheap. So here's the expensive kind." He opened his fist and looked at the knot. "I'm not giving this back to you today. I'm keeping it — for now. And here's what keeping it costs me: every time I look at it, I have to remember that trust is a loan, not a gift, and that I lent it carelessly and paid interest in a cell. That's mine to carry. And you—" he held her gaze, and it was hard, harder than facing the Hollow's attention in the throne room had been, because the Hollow had only wanted to eat him and this girl wanted to be *worth something* to him— "you carry yours. You don't get absolution from me. You get a debt, and you pay it the only way debts like this get paid: you spend years being someone who wouldn't do the sums. Who stands outside prisons and walks in. Who burns nothing and betrays no one, even when the boat's on the line. And maybe someday, when I've seen enough of that person, I'll hand this back and tell you it holds again. Not before."

Wren stared at him. Then she did the last thing he expected: she laughed — one short, cracked note, real despite everything.

"That's the cruelest kindness anyone's ever given me."

"I sell charms. I know the difference between what people want and what works." He tucked the knot inside his coat, over his heart, where it sat like a small cold coal. "One more thing. Your father."

"What about him?"

"If he comes to you drunk someday and says he wants to confess, or apologize, or turn himself in — don't talk him out of it, but don't let him do it stupidly either. There's going to be a reckoning in this city, Wren. I've seen the ledgers. Not the wardens' kind. The real kind. And the men who denounce in panic are different from the men who inform for profit, and somebody's eventually going to have to sort one from the other, fairly, in daylight. Make sure your father lands on the side of the sorting that can live with itself."

She frowned. "What ledgers? What reckoning—"

"That's the part you know nothing about." He stood, brushed off his coat, and offered her his hand — not to shake, just to help her up, an ordinary courtesy, absurdly domestic after everything. She took it. Her grip was strong and salt-rough and shaking slightly.

At the bottom of the chapel steps she paused, hood up, half-turned back toward him.

"The fever-charm," she said. "For Tam Cooper's boy. When you sold it — that was after the edict. You knew what it could cost you, and you sold it anyway, because a child was burning up."

"He lived, I heard."

"He lived." She looked at him a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes — not forgiveness, not gratitude exactly, something less finished than either. "My father sold your name for forty silver. You sold a child his life for six coppers and threw in the blessing free. I've been doing the sums, Elodie Fairweather. I know which side of the ledger I come from."

She walked off into the rain toward the harbor, and he watched her go until the grey water-color of her coat dissolved into the grey water-color of the morning, and then he stood alone under Saint Ferren's broken arch, protector of thresholds, and pressed his hand against the luck-knot over his heart.

It didn't feel warm. It felt like a weight, willingly carried, which he was beginning to suspect was the only kind worth having.

When he got back to the tower, Maren was waiting in the archive room with a lamp lit and her sleeves pushed up, and the expression on her face was the one she wore when the archive had shown her something she wished it hadn't.

"We have a problem," she said. "Someone's been asking questions about you. Not the hunters." She slid a page across the table — a shipping manifest, recently copied, with one line circled in her precise iron-gall ink. "A fisherman named Tallow has been telling people his daughter met you on Gull Street this morning. And the fisherman's creditors have been asking who his daughter met, and why, and offering to forgive debts for answers."

Elodie put his hand over his coat, over the knot, and found it colder than before.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

"Judging by the tide tables," Maren said, "until tomorrow's boats come in."
