## 18. Counterfeit Wards

The seal of the Ashvale Hunter Corps was a masterpiece of bureaucratic vanity: a crowned fist clutching three arrows, pressed in black wax, ringed by the motto *Vigilance Is Mercy*. Maren Voss had spent eleven years in the royal archives and four months in disgrace, and she had learned one thing above all others in that time.

Nobody reads the fine print.

She sat at her tower desk at midnight with a hunter's travel warrant open before her, turning it toward the candlelight the way a jeweler turns a flawed stone. The paper was right — she'd bought it from a stationer who supplied the Corps and asked no questions of a woman who paid in old coin. The wax was nearly right; she'd practiced the fist-and-arrows forty times on scrap until her wrist ached. The language was perfect, because language was her native country and she had never once been caught trespassing there.

What was wrong was the ward.

Every credential the Corps issued carried a verification charm stitched into the ink — invisible to the eye, but it would warm under a warden's thumb and whisper its authenticity to any detection-lens passed over it. A forged warrant without the charm was a dead man's permission slip. It would pass a bored gatekeeper. It would not pass Captain Rell's people, and Rell's people were the only ones whose attention mattered now.

Maren set down the warrant and looked across the room at Elodie Fairweather, who was sitting on her floor because he said chairs made him feel like he was being audited, grinding dried rowan bark in a mortar with the absent rhythm of a man who had done small illegal work his whole life.

"I need you to make ink lie," she said.

He didn't look up. "Ink already lies. That's most of what ink does."

"I need it to lie *convincingly*. To other liars. Professional ones."

That got his attention. He set the mortar down and came to the desk, wiping his hands on his apron — he'd claimed an apron within a day of arriving in her custody, some instinct of the market asserting itself even in an archivist's tower — and bent over the warrant. His eyes moved across it the way they must have moved across a king's breakfast plate: cataloguing, weighing, tasting without tongue.

"This is the verification stitch," he said, tapping a place where the ink looked, to her, entirely ordinary. "My grandmother used to talk about these. Bureaucratic charms. She called them the most dishonest magic in the world, because it's magic pretending it isn't." He glanced up. "You want me to counterfeit a counterfeit."

"I want you to make this paper say what the Corps' own papers say. Can you?"

He was quiet for a moment. She had learned to read his silences in the two weeks since she'd pulled him out of the cells beneath the Mint — silences of refusal, silences of fear, and this one, which was new to her: the silence of a craftsman measuring a job against his pride.

"Show me a real one," he said.

* * *

Getting a real one was the kind of problem Maren had been solving her entire career, back when her career had still existed. The archive kept copies of everything — that was the point of an archive — and among the seized-documents folios were dozens of confiscated warrants, taken from accused witches over the years as evidence of forgery or espionage, misfiled for decades because no archivist after her departure had understood the filing system she'd built.

Her filing system. Her keys. The archive had never changed its locks.

She went at dawn, dressed in the gray wool of a woman nobody looks at twice, carrying a legitimate research requisition signed by the Lord Protector's office — the one piece of paper in Ashvale that opened every door, purchased with the only currency she had left: her complicity. The young archivist at the desk knew her name the way children know the name of a ghost in a story. He let her into the stacks and did not watch her, because watching S. Maren Voss handle documents was like watching a priest handle relics; it did not occur to him that the ghost might steal.

She took three warrants. Two from dead men whose families had fled the city years ago. One from a living woman — a hedge-healer named Sorrel Quist, arrested in spring, whose file Maren checked with cold thoroughness before taking anything. Still in the cells. Alive. Which meant the theft could be returned, or denied, or — if it came to that — confessed as part of a larger crime worth dying for anyway.

She told herself all of this on the walk home, and believed perhaps sixty percent of it.

Elodie met her at the door, took the warrants, and held them the way other men hold snakes.

"These are people," he said.

"These are documents."

"You know what I mean." He laid them flat on the desk with exaggerated gentleness, as if they might bruise. "Sorrel Quist. I know her work. She delivered half the babies in Tanner's Row before they took her. She used to trade me fever-charm components for headache draughts." His jaw worked. "If we're going to do this, we do it knowing whose paper we're copying. I'm not erasing her. I want her name in my head while my hands are on it."

Maren had negotiated with Lord Protectors and outlasted three Directors of Archives, and nothing any of them had said to her landed quite like a poison-taster insisting on holding a stranger's name in his mouth while he committed forgery.

"Quist," she said. "Noted. In the ledger, if you like. We'll keep accounts."

"We should keep accounts." He was already pulling his mortar closer, grinding something that smelled of iron and rain. "Everything we take from someone, we write down. So if we survive this, we can give it back."

It was, she thought, the strangest moral accounting she had ever heard, and also the only one that had ever made her want to be a better person rather than merely a correct one.

* * *

The work took four days.

He began by reading the genuine stitches — not with instruments but with his gift, holding each warrant to his lips the way a sommelier holds a glass, breathing across the ink. Maren watched him do it and felt the particular vertigo she'd been living with since the cells: the sense that the world's categories were quietly rearranging themselves. She had spent her life believing knowledge lived in books. Here was a man who read paper with his mouth.

"Layered," he murmured. "Three layers. The outer one is just — authority. The smell of officialdom. Anyone's lens would catch it and stop looking. Under that, a true-name thread. Whoever issued it, their signature woven in. And under *that*—" he paused, and his face did something complicated, "—a leash."

"A leash?"

"A way to call the paper home. Or burn it. If the Corps decides a warrant's been compromised, they speak a word and the stitch eats itself, and whoever's holding the paper is standing at a gate with nothing." He looked up at her. "Your Lord Protector gave you access to hunters' credentials. Think about what that means about how much he trusts you."

"I think about very little else," Maren said. "Can you copy it without the leash?"

"Can I copy it *with* the leash and make it answer to us instead?" He smiled, and it was the first smile she'd seen from him that wasn't armor. "Grandmother's ledger has a whole section on re-threading binding-work. I thought it was about knots. It's about exactly this."

So they worked. He built; she verified. His magic found the shape of the stitch; her scholarship confirmed it matched the Corps' documented standards — she had, in another life, written the monograph on Ashvale's credential seals that every warden trainee memorized without understanding. Between them they produced a warrant that was, in every testable particular, more authentic than the authentic ones, because Elodie wove the authority-layer slightly warmer than regulation, the way a counterfeiter makes a coin a hair too heavy.

"Who tests your papers?" he asked on the third night, not looking up from his work.

"Gate wardens, checkpoint officers, occasionally Rell's inspectors."

"And if an inspector asks you questions? Your cover story?"

"I am Maren Voss, disgraced court archivist, operating under Lord Protector's writ to research the king's condition. Every word of it is true."

"That's not a cover story. That's just you."

"It's the best kind," she said. "A lie needs maintenance. The truth maintains itself. I've simply attached it to purposes its authors didn't intend." She tapped the warrant. "They gave me a key because they thought I was broken. Broken things get handed around. Nobody guards a broken thing."

Elodie was quiet a moment. "You're not broken."

"Thank you."

"I mean it as an observation, not a comfort. You're the least broken person I've ever been locked in a room with." He threaded the last filament of the stitch and blew gently across the page, and the ink settled, and the warrant lay there humming with borrowed authority. "There. Sorrel Quist's paper, wearing our names. Write it in the ledger."

Maren wrote: *Borrowed from S. Quist, cell nine, Mint. To be returned or repaid.* Then, after a pause, she added the date and her initials, because ledgers were the one religion she had never lost faith in.

* * *

They tested the first warrant on a Tuesday, at the Saltgate checkpoint, because Saltgate had the meanest warden on rotation — a thick-necked man named Brack who had once turned away the Bishop of Lowmarsh for insufficient documentation and dined on the story for a year.

Maren walked through the checkpoint with the forged writ inside her coat and her heart performing what she recognized, clinically, as arrhythmia. Brack held out his hand. She gave him the paper. He turned it over, squinted at the seal, and then — the moment she had rehearsed failures for — pressed his thumb flat against the verification stitch.

Warmth bloomed under his thumb. Authority whispered its little whisper. *I am what I claim. Vigilance is mercy.*

Brack grunted, handed it back, and waved her through with the profound indifference of a man who had already forgotten her face.

On the far side of the gate, Elodie fell into step beside her — he'd come through separately, as her hired porter, carrying a satchel of archive copies no one bothered to inspect because porters are furniture. They walked four streets in silence before either spoke.

"It worked," he said.

"It worked."

"You didn't breathe the whole time. I counted."

"I was managing my respiration deliberately."

"You were terrified."

"I was *thoroughly* afraid, yes, and it changed nothing, which is the entire difference between fear and cowardice." She stopped at the corner where the lane bent toward the tower and allowed herself, for exactly three seconds, to lean against the wall and laugh — a short, unlovely, honest sound that surprised them both. "We just walked through the crown's own gate wearing the crown's own skin. Do you understand what we've made? Not a forgery. A *passport*. We can go anywhere. The archive annex. The Mint records. The palace crypts, eventually, when we're ready."

Elodie was watching her with an expression she couldn't file. Not admiration exactly — she'd been admired, and it had always slid off her like rain off slate. This was something more specific. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man discovering that the person beside him in the dark is the same species.

"You enjoy this," he said.

"I enjoy being competent at something that matters. It's been scarce."

"No — I mean you enjoy *this*. Us. The—" he gestured vaguely at the space between them, at the forged paper, at the whole conspiracy of it, "—the scheme."

Maren considered denying it. She was, by temperament and training, a denier of comfortable things. But the ledger discipline had gotten into her somewhere along the way — his ledger, the account of debts — and she found she didn't want to enter a false line.

"Yes," she said. "I haven't had a collaborator in nine years. Colleagues, subordinates, superiors, informants. But a collaborator — someone whose competence I trust absolutely and who trusts mine —" she shook her head. "I'd forgotten the arithmetic of two. It does sums that one can't."

Elodie nodded slowly. "My grandmother used to say magic done alone is just talking to yourself with consequences."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have run the archive better than any Director I served."

"She'd have burned it down first. As a statement."

Maren laughed again — twice in one day, an unprecedented expenditure — and they walked home through the blue end of the evening, two criminals with excellent paperwork, and neither of them said the word that was quietly building itself in the space between them, because both of them had survived long enough to know that some things die when you name them too early.

* * *

They made six warrants in total. One for Maren. One for Elodie, listing him as a contracted consultant — *constitution specialist*, she wrote, enjoying the joke that only three people alive would understand. Two blank, for contingencies. One for Bettina Odo, the neighbor who had burned her own charms in the street, whom Elodie refused to stop worrying about and Maren had begun, without deciding to, worrying about too. And one they argued about for two full days: a warrant for Wren, the fisherman's daughter.

"She denounced you," Maren said. "Her father traded your name for silver. Why would you—"

"Because she came back." Elodie's voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was holding something fragile. "You weren't there. She stood in the rain outside the tower and couldn't even knock. Whatever her father did, she's carrying it, and she's sixteen, and this city is about to eat girls like her for breakfast. If the hunts widen — and they will, the Hollow gets hungrier as the king thins — she'll need to move. Everyone will need to move."

Maren entered it in the ledger: *One warrant, issued freely, no debt incurred.* It was the first entry she'd ever made that wasn't a borrowing or a repayment, and her hand hesitated over it, and then she understood that this was what the ledger was actually for — not debts. Gifts. The debts were just how you learned to make them.

* * *

On the seventh night, the trouble came, as trouble does, wearing the uniform of routine.

Maren was at the archive annex, using her new freedom to pull succession records — the pattern she'd begun to see, king after beloved king after conveniently dead king, needed documentary spine — when she heard the footsteps behind her in the stacks. Measured. Booted. Not an archivist's shuffle.

She turned with the requisition already in hand, the Lord Protector's seal facing outward, her face arranged into the mild boredom of legitimate business.

Captain Rell stood between the shelves. He was alone, which was somehow worse. His eyes went to the requisition, to her face, to the folio open on the reading table — succession records, 200 years of them, flagged with her own marginal notes in a cipher any trained cryptographer would salivate over.

"Archivist Voss," he said.

"Captain Rell."

"My inspector at Saltgate remembers you. Tuesday. Says your porter had ink on his fingers." Rell picked up the edge of the folio, turned one page, another, reading her cipher with the unhurried patience of a man defusing something. "Old Fairweather cipher, this. Family work. Very rare."

Maren calculated escape routes and found none worth the walking. "Research into the king's melancholia. Under Protector's writ. You're welcome to verify the writ."

"Oh, I verified it Tuesday." He closed the folio gently. Set it down. And then he did the thing she would turn over in her mind for weeks afterward: he leaned in, lowered his voice, and asked, not as a hunter but as a tired man in a corridor —

"The taster. Fairweather. When you look at him — at what his blood does, at what he says about the king —" Rell's jaw tightened. "Is the king still in there? Is there anything left to save? Because I have forty people in my cells, Archivist, and orders to take forty more, and I need to know whether the thing I'm serving is a crown or a mouth."

The question hit the exact seam she'd been guarding. She thought of Aldric calling his dead daughter by a servant's name. She thought of Elodie tasting the wine and going white, whispering *it noticed me*. She thought of the ledger entry she hadn't written yet, and of lies, and of how a lie needs maintenance and the truth maintains itself.

"There's something left," she said. "Less every day. If you want to know what's eating it — and I think you already half-know — then you'll want to see what we find next. Follow the paperwork, Captain. You're good at that. Better than your superiors suspect."

Rell held her gaze for a long five seconds. Then he straightened, became a captain again, and said, loudly enough for the desk clerk to hear, "Your requisition is in order, Archivist. Mind the closing hour."

He walked out of the stacks. At the door he paused, and without turning around, said:

"Tuesday's porter should wear gloves."

Then he was gone, and Maren stood alone among two hundred years of dead kings, holding a forged life in her pocket and a terrible new variable in her ledger — a hunter who had seen the forgery and filed it nowhere, a man who had asked the one question she couldn't afford to answer and then chosen, for reasons she couldn't yet price, to walk away.

She gathered the folio, signed the register in her own true name, and hurried home through the dark to tell Elodie that their beautiful counterfeit world had just developed a witness — and that the witness might be the most dangerous ally they would ever have.
