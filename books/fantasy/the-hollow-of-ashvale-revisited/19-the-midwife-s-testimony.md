## 19. The Midwife's Testimony

Bettina had not slept in four days, and the not-sleeping had a shape now — a long grey corridor she walked every night with her eyes open, listening to the floorboards of her own house as if they might confess. The charms were gone. She had burned them herself, eleven years of careful work gone up in a stink of tallow and rosemary ash while the neighbors watched from their windows and pretended not to. That had been the bargain she'd made with her fear: burn them small, burn them first, and maybe the fire would pass over.

It was the midwife who broke the bargain.

Her name was Odile Sarn, though half of Ashvale knew her only as the woman who came when the birthing went wrong, because the licensed physicians cost more than most of the Salt Lane households earned in a season. Bettina had known her for nine years, since Odile had pulled Bettina's nephew out of a breach birth by candlelight with hands steady as a carpenter's. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a door, with a laugh that could be heard through two walls. She did not look like a woman who could fit inside a flour chest.

But there she was on the fourth night, standing in Bettina's kitchen at an hour when no honest person knocked, soaked to the knees from the drainage channel behind the tannery, her grey hair plastered to her skull, and her eyes — Bettina would remember those eyes until she died — her eyes were the flat, wide-open eyes of a hare that has heard the dog and decided not to run yet.

"They took Hesper," Odile said. "The girl who assists me. They came at dusk. Two wardens and a clerk. The clerk read the charge off a paper like he was reading a market list." Her voice was level, which was worse than weeping. "Hesper is nineteen. She can't even write her own name."

"Odile—"

"I need you to hide me, Bettina. One night. Maybe two. Then I have to get this to someone who can use it." She pressed a hand flat against her sternum, and Bettina saw that under the wet shawl she was holding something wrapped in oilcloth, held the way you hold a child, or a wound.

"Use it how? Use it *where*?" Bettina hissed, already moving anyway, pulling back the rug, prying up the loose board by the hearth where she'd hidden nothing for three weeks because everything was already burned. The cavity was empty and dark and smelled of old smoke. "There's nowhere left to take anything. Everyone's turned everyone in. The baker's boy informed on his own grandmother for the silver."

"I know what they're saying about me," Odile said, following her down onto her knees without being asked, folding her long body into the space beside the chimney with the terrible economy of a woman who had spent forty years making herself small in other people's rooms. "I know what it costs you. I'm not asking you to like it."

Bettina got the board back down and stood there in her own kitchen with her heart going like a hammer on wet leather. Above her, through the ceiling, she could hear old Pemmy coughing in the room she rented, awake, always awake these days, everyone awake and listening.

"Tell me what's in the bundle," Bettina said. "Before I decide."

* * *

What was in the bundle was a ledger. Not a charm-book, not a grimoire — a ledger, kept in a midwife's blunt hand, columns ruled in charcoal: dates, mothers' names, births attended, weights, complications, fees owed and forgiven. Forty years of Ashvale's arrivals, written down by a woman who believed that a life should begin with its name in ink.

And then, beginning six years ago, a second column. A column Odile had never shown anyone, headed with a single word she'd had to invent because no other word existed: *taken.*

"Six years ago I attended a birth in the palace quarter," Odile said, sitting wrapped in Bettina's spare blanket at the kitchen table while Bettina boiled water neither of them would drink, because boiling water was something to do with your hands. "A warden's wife. Fine healthy boy, eight pounds, easy labor as those things go. And when he came out — Bettina, when he came out, he didn't cry."

"Babies don't always cry straight off."

"He didn't cry at all. He opened his eyes and he looked at me. Newborns don't look, you know that. Their eyes swim, they fix on light and shadow, they don't *look*. This one looked at me like an old man looks at a stranger across a table. And the room went cold. Not draft-cold. Cold like a cellar door opening somewhere below the world." She turned her cup slowly on the table, around and around. "And then it passed, and the boy cried, and he was a normal squalling infant, and I told myself I was tired. I told myself that for six years."

"How many?"

"Eleven. Eleven births in the palace quarter and the streets around the Mint, all within a certain radius — I marked them on a map, it's in the ledger. Every one the same. The cold. The looking. And afterward—" her voice caught for the first time, and she let it, because some things deserved to catch — "afterward the children were *dim*. Not sick. Not damaged, God forgive me for saying it wrong. Just dimmer than they should have been. Slower to know their mothers' faces. One little girl, three years old now, has never once said a word, and her mother swears she used to babble words she couldn't possibly have known. Words from her grandmother, who died before the child was born."

The water boiled over. Neither of them moved to lift it.

"It eats," Odile said simply. "Whatever it is, whatever's up there wearing the crown and calling itself our king's good health — it eats what children are born carrying. All that they come in with. The old knowing. My gran called it the first-light, the memory a soul brings into the world before the world rubs it off. It drinks the first-light, Bettina. From newborns. Like a man taking milk from a table he hasn't paid for."

"And you wrote it down." Bettina's voice came out strange to her. "Forty years of catching babies, and you wrote down the thing that's stealing them."

"What else does a midwife do?" Odile said. "We keep records so the next one knows. So nobody has to learn it twice." She looked up, and the hare-eyes were burning now. "They'll burn me for it if they catch me. Hesper's arrest wasn't chance — someone talked, someone always talks — but they didn't find the ledger because it wasn't there to find. I need it to reach people who can do something besides run. You knew the archivist. Voss. Before she fell."

Bettina stared at her. "That was years ago. And she's — they say she keeps to her tower. They say she's half-mad with old books."

"They say a lot of things," Odile said. "I say she's the only person in this city who ever read the palace records all the way through, and if anyone can tell me what's been living above the Mint for longer than any of us, it's her."

* * *

Getting Odile out of Salt Lane took two days, and Bettina learned things about her neighbors in those two days that she would carry like stones in her shoe for the rest of her life.

She learned that Tam Wicker, who bought a fever-charm from her every winter for his rheumatism and had wept with gratitude over it, now wore the armband of a crown informer and checked the lane twice a day from his doorway. She learned that the widow Anselm, whose granddaughter Bettina had taught letters, had been paid eleven silver pieces — eleven, the price of a decent coat — for naming three women as gifted, two of whom had never done magic in their lives and one of whom was dead. She learned that fear, given time and a little official encouragement, did not make people cruel so much as it made them *small*, and that smallness looked exactly like cruelty from the outside.

She smuggled Odile out in a fish cart on the second morning, under a tarpaulin of herring barrels, past Tam Wicker's doorway, past the notice-board where the edict hung with its neat crown seal and its promise of silver, out along the river road to the foot of Maren Voss's tower, where the tide-smell mixed with the smell of old vellum drifting from a high window.

"You don't have to come up," Odile said, wringing out her skirt hem on the cobbles. "You've done enough. More than enough. No one could say—"

"No one could say what?" Bettina heard her own voice come out hard and was surprised by it. "No one could say I helped a midwife deliver testimony against the crown? Odile, they burned my charms in the street and it didn't save me from being afraid for one single night. There's no safe side of this anymore. There's just sides." She looked up at the tower, narrow and stubborn against the grey sky, and felt something in her chest that was not quite hope but was at least its cousin. "Besides. Somebody has to carry the basket."

Maren Voss answered the door herself, which surprised Bettina — she'd expected servants, wards, ceremony. What she got instead was a thin, sharp-eyed woman with ink on three fingers and the look of someone who had not been sleeping either, and who took one long look at Odile's face and stepped aside without a word.

Upstairs, in a room where every surface was paper, Odile unwrapped the ledger and laid it open on Maren's table, and began, in her flat midwife's voice, to read the column headed *taken.* Names. Dates. The radius marked on the map, a circle drawn in charcoal around the Mint and the palace heights like a noose lying on the city. Eleven children. The cold. The looking. The dimming.

Maren listened without interrupting, which Bettina would later understand was the discipline of a lifetime in archives: the truth arrives however it arrives, and your job is not to flinch. But when Odile reached the account of the third child — the girl who spoke her dead grandmother's words — Maren's hand came down flat on the table, and stayed there.

"The birthing chambers," Maren said quietly. "Below the east wing. I catalogued those rooms' inventories fifteen years ago and thought the ledgers made no sense. Provisioning records for a household wing that hosted no households. Food sent down that no one ate. Candles by the gross." She looked up, and her eyes had gone bright and terrible. "It isn't only the king. It was never only the king. It's been *farming* us."

The word dropped into the room and sat there. Farming. Bettina thought of her burned charms, of Hesper in a cell, of eleven silver pieces for a grandmother's name, and understood suddenly that all of it — the edict, the hunt, the informers, the whole machinery of fear — might be nothing but a fence around a field.

"There's more," Odile said. She turned to the last page of the ledger, and her blunt finger stopped on an entry near the bottom, dated nine days ago. "This one I attended myself, two nights before they came for Hesper. A laundress in the palace service. And in the birthing chamber — I was alone with her for the worst hour, the physicians having better things to do — she kept talking. Fever-talk. And she said a thing I've turned over a hundred times since." Odile looked up. "She said, *it counts them, it counts them all, but it's hungry for the ones who taste.*"

Silence. Somewhere below them the river worked at its pilings.

"Taste," Maren repeated.

"That's what she said. *Hungry for the ones who taste.* I wrote it down exact." Odile tapped the page. "I assumed it was fever nonsense. But you're the reader. Is it nonsense?"

Maren was already pulling ledgers toward her, three and four at once, her hands moving with sudden purpose, and Bettina watched the grief go out of the room and something harder arrive in its place — not comfort, nothing so kind as comfort, but direction. Grief with somewhere to go.

"It's not nonsense," Maren said. "It's a description. Someone in that palace knows what it wants, and it isn't crowns." She looked up at Bettina, and then at Odile, and her voice dropped low. "I think I know who it means. And I think we are very quickly running out of time to be wrong."

Outside the high window, the bells of the Mint began to ring the hour, and beneath their bronze voices, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of drums — the hunters, marching again, another street, another door, another name read off a paper like a market list.
