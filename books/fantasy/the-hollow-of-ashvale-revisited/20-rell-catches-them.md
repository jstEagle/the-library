## 20. Rell Catches Them

The archive at night smelled of dust and tallow and old iron-gall ink, and Elodie had learned to love it the way you love a hiding place — completely, and with the knowledge that it wouldn't hold forever.

He sat cross-legged on the floor between the shelves of the restricted stacks, three levels below the tower's reading room, copying pages by the light of a single shuttered lamp. Maren had taught him the trick of it: a tin shield around the flame, a dark cloth over his shoulders, work in bursts of thirty heartbeats and then stillness while his ears did the watching. He was getting good at it. Three years of tasting for a paranoid court had trained him to notice everything, and noticing, he was discovering, was most of what magic turned out to be.

The page under his hands was his grandmother's. Her ciphered binding-notes, transcribed into Maren's plainer hand where they'd managed the translation, left raw where they hadn't. *The Hollow feeds deepest at the midnight meal,* she had written, in a script that slanted hard to the right, as if the words were running from something. *It will not take what is not given. Remember this. It is the only mercy in the whole ugly architecture of the thing.*

Elodie copied it out twice, because the first time his hand had shaken on *mercy* and Maren would want a clean copy.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, he could hear Maren moving in the reading room — the particular rhythm of her pacing that meant she was working a problem, three steps and turn, three steps and turn. She'd gone up an hour ago to check the ward-schedule of the palace guard rotations, which she kept in her head because, as she put it, paper could be seized but a grudge could not.

*Three steps and turn.* The rhythm changed. Stopped.

Elodie's pen lifted off the page.

Then boots. Not Maren's soft archivist's slippers — boots, weighted, deliberate, more than one pair, coming down the tower stair with the unhurried cadence of men who did not need to hurry because hurry was for people who could be outrun.

He pinched out the lamp.

Darkness took the stacks whole. He stayed exactly where he was, knees folded under him, pages flat beneath his palms, and breathed the way he'd been taught to breathe when swallowing something bitter: slow, shallow, patient. Around him the archive settled. Somewhere a mouse moved through the shelving with the confidence of a creature that had never once been accused of anything.

The door at the top of the stair opened without a knock. Light spilled down in a long blade and cut across the floor two feet from Elodie's knee.

"Archivist Voss." A voice like a door closing. "You'll forgive the hour."

"Captain Rell." Maren's voice came down cool and level, and Elodie marveled at it even now, even terrified. "I might ask the same of you. The hunter credentials you carry were forged by my own office before your time. I remember the seal. It's slightly wrong — the ash-leaf should have five points, not six — but I suppose no one checks anymore."

A pause. Elodie could hear Rell deciding whether that was insolence or simply fact.

"The Lord Protector wants a full inventory of restricted materials," Rell said. "Everything touched since the edict. You'll stand aside, please."

"I'll do nothing of the sort. This is crown archive property under charter—"

"Madam." Something shifted in the voice. Not softer, exactly. Wearier. "I've sealed four houses tonight. There's a midwife in the cells beneath the mint who attended a birth last spring and made a charm against colic, and there is a boy of eleven who whistles wind, and I have to write reports on both of them by morning that will end their lives. Do not make me add insubordination to the list. Stand aside."

Boots on the stair. Two men, maybe three, descending with the lamp. Elodie pressed himself back against the shelf behind him and felt the spines of a hundred books press back, indifferent.

They found him the way you find anything in a small dark room: immediately.

The lamp came up. Elodie blinked into it, hands raised, ink on his fingers, a page of dead woman's cipher half-copied beside him like a confession written out fair.

"Well," said one of the hunters, a young one with a nervous mouth. "Captain. Looks like—"

"I can see what it looks like." Rell stepped past the young man and stood over Elodie for a long moment. He was not what Elodie had expected, though he couldn't have said what he had expected. Grey at the temples. A face built for endurance rather than command. His eyes went to the pages, and Elodie watched him read — actually read, lips barely moving, the way literate men read when they forget themselves — and Elodie understood with a lurch that this man was not stupid, and that stupid men would have been so much safer.

"Fairweather," Rell said. "The taster."

"You know me?"

"I interviewed you in the cells beneath the mint. You don't sicken. I wrote it up as 'suspicion of constitution' because the alternative was writing nothing at all, and the Lord Protector doesn't pay for nothing." He crouched, unhurried, and picked up the topmost page between two fingers, the way Elodie himself might lift a suspect spoon. "This is the Fairweather hand. We have samples. Your grandmother was burned for witchcraft forty years ago, and her papers were supposed to be destroyed."

"They were," Maren said from the top of the stairs. "I reconstructed them. Badly, in places. If you're going to arrest someone for the errors, arrest me."

"Maren." Elodie didn't look up at her. "Don't."

Rell rose. He looked at Maren, then back at Elodie, and something worked behind his face like a man doing sums he didn't want the total of.

"Both of you, then," he said. "Conspiracy against the crown. Trafficking in forbidden texts. Unlicensed—" he turned the page over, read another line, and stopped. Elodie saw his jaw tighten. "*Unbinding rites.*"

The word dropped into the room and lay there.

The young hunter shifted. "Captain? Shall I fetch irons?"

"No."

"Captain, protocol says—"

"Protocol says I report what I find, and what I find gets people burned, and I am looking at it right now and trying to decide if I'm a man who does that." Rell handed the page back to Elodie — handed it, deliberately, fingers releasing it only when Elodie had taken the weight — and dismissed the young man with a flick toward the stair. "Wait above. Both of you wait above. If anyone asks, we're conducting an inventory."

The hunters withdrew. The door closed. And then it was the three of them in the lamplight among the shelves, and the silence had a different texture entirely.

Maren came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, watching Rell the way she watched a cipher she hadn't cracked yet.

"You read the notes," she said.

"Some." Rell walked the length of the stack aisle, reading spines without touching them. "Enough. I've spent two years hunting the gifted, Archivist. Do you know what I've caught? A woman who could keep milk sweet an extra day. A boy who dreams true twice a year and hates it. A man who could find lost keys. I have burned—" he paused, corrected himself with visible effort, "—*they* have burned eleven people, and the sum total of their crimes would not fill one honest afternoon. And meanwhile the king stands at his window calling for a daughter who has been dead six years, and the physicians write *melancholia* in their ledgers, and everyone above the fourth floor pretends the crown doesn't watch us back."

He turned.

"So before I decide what I am tonight, taster, you're going to answer one question. One. And if you lie to me, I'll know, because I've spent two years learning how liars taste, metaphorically speaking, and you strike me as a man who's bad at it."

Elodie's mouth was dry. The pages sat in his lap, his grandmother's running script, her warning about mercy. Above them, boots crossed the reading room floor and stopped.

"Ask," he said.

Rell came close enough that Elodie could see the tiredness in him properly — not the tiredness of late hours but of long carrying, the kind that doesn't come off with sleep.

"Is the king still in there?"

Not *is there a demon*. Not *is it treason*. Elodie heard the question underneath the question, the one Rell would never say aloud: *when I gave the order to seal the palace gates last month, when I signed the feeding-hour protocols, when I saluted the shape on the throne — who was I saluting?*

And Elodie, who had tasted the king's wine and felt the cold attention turn upon him like a tongue along his spine, who had heard Aldric spend one lucid breath on a single whispered word — *run* — thought about lying. Thought about how easy it would be. *No, Captain. Nothing human left. Nothing to save.*

But Rell had asked because he wanted the truth, and Elodie had learned in the cells beneath the mint that this man collected truths the way other men collected coin — not to spend, but because he could not bear to be poor in them.

"Yes," Elodie said. "He's still in there. What's left of him hoards little things. His daughter's laugh. Rain on an orchard. He spends them like coins to stay himself, and he's nearly out." He held Rell's eyes. "Last week he was lucid for the space of a breath, and do you know what he did with it? He told me to run. Not help me. Not free himself. *Run.* That's what's left of Aldric of Ashvale, Captain. A man using his last coin to warn a stranger."

Rell didn't move for a long moment. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, the young hunter coughed and pretended it was nothing.

"That's not an answer that makes my duty simpler," Rell said finally.

"No," Maren agreed quietly. "It's the kind that makes it impossible. Those are usually the true ones."

Rell looked at her, then at Elodie, then at the pages in Elodie's lap — the unbinding rite, the willing memories, the whole terrible arithmetic of it — and Elodie watched him arrive at the edge of understanding and stop there, deliberately, the way a man stops at the lip of a well he isn't ready to look down.

"I found nothing tonight," Rell said. "An inventory of restricted materials, conducted per the Lord Protector's instruction. The archive is in order. Archivist Voss was cooperative." He straightened his belt, and his hand wasn't quite steady. "The midwife in the cells — Sera Dune. She's scheduled for questioning tomorrow. If her testimony were to become... complicated, if the evidence against her were to develop doubts, I would need to know that before morning. Not after. Before."

"We can do complicated," Maren said.

"I assumed." At the foot of the stair he paused, back to them, and Elodie saw him roll his shoulders once, like a man taking up a weight he intended to carry until it killed him. "Taster. When this ends — however it ends — I'll want the whole account. Everything you learn about what's wearing the crown. Not for the Lord Protector. For me. I've saluted that thing every morning for two years, and I find I need to know what I was saluting, even if the answer is worse than ignorance."

"You'll have it," Elodie said.

Rell nodded once and climbed the stairs, and the door closed behind him with the soft, final sound of a man signing something.

* * *

They didn't speak for a while after that. Maren relit the lamp and sat down across from Elodie amid the scattered pages, and for a long time they just listened to the tower settle.

"He'll hang for this," Elodie said eventually. "If it goes wrong. Whatever happens to us, he hangs higher."

"Yes." Maren gathered the ciphered pages and squared their edges with small precise taps. "Which means we cannot let it go wrong. That's the trouble with decency, Elodie — it creates obligations. Bettina sheltering the midwife created obligations. Rell leaving us our throats created obligations. Every act of courage in this city is currently a debt someone else will be executed for." She looked up, and there was something raw under the archivist's composure. "So we finish it. Soon. The midnight meal, the rite, all of it. Because tomorrow I forge testimony for Sera Dune, and the night after that Rell lies in an official report, and every day we wait, the pile of people standing on our trapdoor gets heavier."

Elodie looked down at his grandmother's handwriting. *It is the only mercy in the whole ugly architecture of the thing.*

"There's something else," he said. "When Rell read the page — the unbinding rite — he stopped at one line. I saw where his eye went." He turned the sheet toward her and tapped the passage. *The binder must offer what he loves, freely given, and the Hollow must swallow it whole.*

Maren read it. Read it again. Then she closed her eyes briefly, the way she did when the archive gave her exactly what she'd feared.

"He understood," she said. "What it costs."

"He understood. And he still walked out of here and lied to his own hunters for us." Elodie exhaled slowly. "Maren — he's going to figure out the rest of it. What I'm planning to give. And when he does—"

"When he does, he'll either try to stop you or help you," Maren said, "and I genuinely don't know which frightens me more." She began stacking the pages into her case, quick and businesslike, armor going back on piece by piece. "Come. We work until dawn. If we're going to spend the lives people are lending us, we'll damned well spend them on a finished rite."

They climbed toward the reading room together. And as Elodie passed the window at the stair's turning, he looked out over the sleeping city — the mint, the market, the distant black bulk of the palace where a hollow king was spending his last coins in the dark — and he thought about the question Rell had asked, and the answer he'd given, and how strange it was that the truth, spoken plainly to an enemy, had bought them the night.

Tomorrow the hunters would come asking their captain what he'd found.

And Rell, Elode suspected, was already practicing the lie.

* * *

Dawn found them still at the table, the rite spread out in Maren's clean translation, every step annotated, every cost named. Elodie had just dipped his pen when the bell above the tower door rang — the signal they'd agreed on, three pulls, Bettina's pattern — and Maren went very still.

"That's early," she said. "She doesn't come before light unless—"

The bell rang again. Twice. A pattern none of them had agreed on.

Through the window, in the grey first light, Elodie saw figures at the tower gate. Hunters. Six of them. And at their head, face pale as whey, the young hunter from the archive — the one Rell had sent to wait above.

Behind him, hands bound, flanked by his own men, walked Captain Rell.

The young hunter called up, voice cracking on the morning air: "By order of the Lord Protector! The captain was found concealing evidence of conspiracy! Open the tower, or we open it!"

Maren's hand found Elodie's arm. Below, Rell lifted his head and looked directly up at the window — and shook it, once, sharply. *Say nothing. Open nothing.*

But already the hunters were moving for the door, and somewhere across the waking city, the midnight meal was only hours away.
