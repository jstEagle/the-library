## 21. Preparations

The tower smelled of tallow and old paper, and Maren Voss had not slept properly in nine days.

She knew this because she had begun keeping a ledger of her own failures — a habit learned from the archivists who trained her, who believed that anything measured could be mastered. Column one: hours of sleep attempted. Column two: hours achieved. The gap between them had become its own kind of document, an accusation written in her own hand.

Three hours last night. Two the night before. On the fourth night she had fallen asleep at her desk with her cheek on Grandmother Fairweather's ciphered binding-notes and woken with a line of ink pressed into her skin like a scar: *the binder must give freely, or the giving is theft.*

She sat now at the long table beneath the arrow-slit window, the rite spread out before her in pieces — literally in pieces, because she had cut the old woman's notes apart three days ago to reorder them, and had been regretting it ever since. Cipher-work was easier when the text stayed whole; the mind held a shape better than scissors did. But there had been no choice. The original sequence was written for a binder standing inside the crypt, reading by candlelight, with hours to spare between each step. They would have none of those things.

They had a window instead. One window, narrow as a knife's edge.

* * *

"The midnight meal," Maren said, for the fourth time that week, because saying it aloud was the only way she trusted it. "Every night, without variation. I checked the household rolls going back eleven years. Whatever else the Hollow has changed about Aldric — his speech, his memory, his face when he thinks no one is watching — it has never once missed the midnight meal."

Elodie sat across from her, turning a copper coin over his knuckles. He did this when he was thinking, she'd noticed. It was a taster's habit, she suspected — something to keep the hands honest while the tongue worked. The coin had been in his pocket since before his arrest; she'd returned it to him with the rest of his effects, and he treated it now like a talisman, which amused her in a grim way. A luck-charm made of ordinary money. Half of Ashvale had burned for less.

"Feeding schedule," Elodie said. "It keeps feeding schedule."

"It keeps *ritual*." Maren pulled the reassembled notes toward her. "That's what we keep failing to say plainly, and it matters. This thing isn't an animal gorging itself. Animals are careless. It eats at fixed hours, in fixed forms, through vessels it has spent centuries perfecting. Wine first, then bread, then the sweet course — always in that order, always at midnight. It is liturgy. And liturgy has rules, and rules can be turned against the one who keeps them."

"You sound like my grandmother's ghost."

"I sound like someone who has read your grandmother's handwriting for three weeks and developed opinions." She tapped the page. "Here. The third stanza. Read it again."

Elodie set down the coin. He read slowly — not from dullness, she'd learned, but from care. He read the way he tasted, she imagined: letting each thing sit on the tongue before deciding what it was.

"*When the eater is deepest in the eating,*" he recited, "*its attention is a door left open. What enters then, enters unremarked.*"

"That's our window. Not the whole meal — the middle of it. After the wine, before the bread. The Hollow will be drawing up whatever memories live in the king's food, pulling them through the crown, sorting them, swallowing them. For perhaps two hundred heartbeats, it will be looking inward. That's when you speak the name. That's when you open yourself." She paused. "That's when I break the seal below."

"And if we're wrong about the timing—"

"Then it looks outward, and it sees us, and everything after that happens very quickly." Maren kept her voice level. Fear, in her experience, was like ink: it bled unless you contained the margins. "I've spent nine days trying to find a version of this plan where we aren't wrong about the timing. There isn't one. So we make ourselves fast, and we make ourselves certain, and we accept that certainty is a luxury the dead archivists never promised anyone."

Elodie nodded once. Then, quietly: "Two hundred heartbeats. You counted."

"I'm an archivist. Counting is the whole profession."

He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to ease either of them had managed in days, and it lasted perhaps four seconds before they both bent back over the table, because there was so much left, and the window was coming whether they were ready or not.

* * *

They built the rite the way masons build an arch — each piece placed against its neighbors so the whole could stand on tension alone.

Maren laid out the components on the table like instruments on a surgeon's cloth. Grandmother Fairweather's notes, deciphered and transcribed in her own hand, the cipher-key burned the night before in the tower hearth — no sense leaving the true name lying about where a curious hunter might find it. A copy of the palace's feeding inventory for the next three nights, obtained through channels she chose not to examine too closely. The binding-seal's location, mapped from records two hundred years stale: the crypt beneath the throne room, behind the wall the masons had bricked up and the ledgers had politely forgotten. And the letters of transit she had forged herself, because Rell's people watched the mint gates now, and bureaucracy remained the only magic Ashvale still permitted.

"Say it back to me," she told him. "All of it. In order."

Elodie closed his eyes. "At the changing of the watch, you enter the undercroft through the coal passage with the forged credentials. You reach the sealed wall by the second bell. You wait for my signal — the king's cup set down twice." He opened one eye. "Which means I have to get close enough to the king to set down his cup twice."

"You've set down his cup every morning for three years."

"In a dining room full of witnesses who expected me to. Tomorrow night the court will be thin — the Protector's feast for the harvest tithe pulls half the nobility to the east wing. Thin, but not empty. And the Hollow knows my blood already. When I spoke the name in the throne room—" He stopped. Rubbed his arms as though cold. "It noticed me, Maren. It looked at me the way a man looks at a purse he hasn't decided to steal yet."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you weren't the one it looked at." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. He picked the coin up again, turned it. "I'm not refusing. I want that understood. I'm telling you what I felt, because you taught me that evidence matters more than comfort."

Maren sat back. "You're right," she said. "And I'm sorry. And the answer doesn't change." She drew a breath. "The rite requires a binder the Hollow will accept offerings from. Your grandmother bound it once; the palace stone remembers her blood; the wards let you pass as kin. If I stood where you'll stand, it would eat me before I finished the first syllable. There is exactly one person alive who can do this, and he's sitting across from me turning a coin."

"I know." He set the coin down flat, deliberately, the way a man puts away a toy he's too old for. "I just wanted it said aloud once, by both of us, so neither of us can pretend later that we didn't know."

"We know," Maren said.

Outside, the wind came off the ash-flats and worried the shutters. Somewhere below in the city, a dog was barking at nothing, or at something. Neither of them remarked on it.

* * *

The contingency letters took her until dawn.

This was the part no archive had trained her for. Archivists preserved the past; they were not required to write instructions for a future that included their own absence. But Maren had watched enough trials in the mint cells' aftermath to understand how evidence worked in Ashvale: it survived only if someone innocent carried it, and innocence was currently the most perishable commodity in the kingdom.

So she wrote four letters.

The first went to Captain Rell — unsigned, untraceable, delivered by a laundress paid to forget the errand. It contained nothing but dates and a location: the crypt, the seal, the hour. If things went wrong, Rell deserved to know why the palace had screamed in the night. Whether he used that knowledge to save them or to condemn them, she genuinely could not predict, and she had stopped pretending otherwise. He had asked his one question — *is the king still in there* — and walked away from arresting them, and that single act of uncertainty had bought him a place in her calculations that she resented and relied upon in equal measure.

The second letter was longer, and harder. It was addressed to no one, to be opened only if both she and Elodie died, and it laid out the entire truth of the Hollow in plain language: its name, its age, its method, its centuries of kings. Every succession strengthened it. Every beloved, hollowing, conveniently dead monarch had fed the thing that wore the crown next. She wrote it all in a hand steadied with wine, and when she finished she read it aloud to the empty room, and her voice did not shake until the final line: *If you are reading this, then Ashvale has been ruled by a parasite for longer than it has been ruled by men, and the only two people who tried to end it are dead, and you must decide quickly whether that was failure or martyrdom, because the difference determines what you do next.*

The third letter was to the archive itself — a formal bequest of her papers, her position argued for posthumously, her disgrace formally contested with citations. Even facing a parasite older than the dynasty, some habits were load-bearing.

The fourth letter she wrote last, and slowest, and it was short.

*To whoever finds Elodie Fairweather afterward:* it began, and then she sat for a long time with the nib poised, because she did not know who that would be, or what state he would be found in, or what a person ought to be told about a man who might wake up as a stranger wearing his own face.

In the end she wrote: *His name is Elodie. He gave everything willingly. Whatever he has lost, he lost on purpose, for a city that arrested him. Be patient with him. Start with bread.*

She sealed it, and then, because sealing felt insufficient, she copied it twice more and hid the copies in three different places, because hope, in her experience, survived best in redundancy.

* * *

They rehearsed at midnight, because if the body learns a thing at the hour it must perform it, the hour becomes part of the skill.

Maren played the Hollow. It was absurd and she knew it — a grey-haired disgraced archivist sitting in a kitchen chair with a napkin draped over her shoulders like a mantle, doing her best impression of an ancient hungry god — but absurdity was a price she'd long since stopped counting. Elodie stood across the table and practiced the opening words of the rite, the invocation of kinship, the speaking of the true name.

"No," Maren said, for the sixth time. "Your voice climbs at the end. You're asking permission. The name isn't a question."

"How do you ask a thing like that without it being a question?"

"You don't ask. You *state*. You are kin to the binder. The name is yours by inheritance. Say it like you're correcting a clerk who's misspelled it."

Elodie laughed despite himself — a short, exhausted sound. "My grandmother corrected clerks?"

"Your grandmother bound a god. Clerks would have been a rest day." She folded the napkin mantle aside. "Again. From the top."

He ran it again, and again, and by the eleventh repetition the name came out of him flat and certain and terrible, filling the little tower room the way water fills a cellar, and Maren felt the hair rise on her forearms and knew — not believed, *knew*, the way she knew a primary source from a forgery — that somewhere beneath the palace, something old had shifted in its sleep and dreamed briefly of a voice it hadn't heard in generations.

"Good," she said, when she trusted her voice. "That was good. Stop there tonight. We don't ring the bell more than we must."

Elodie stood breathing hard, as though the syllables had weight. Perhaps they did. "How long have we got?" he asked. "Before — the night."

"Tomorrow is the Protector's feast. Midnight after that." She calculated, and hated the number. "Thirty-one hours."

He nodded. He gathered his coat, his coin, his few things — he kept almost nothing, she'd noticed; three years of expecting arrest teaches a man to travel light even in his own rooms. At the door he stopped.

"Maren. The letters. I saw you writing them. Four seals' worth of wax this week."

"You count my candles now?"

"I taste everything. It spreads." He didn't turn around. "One of them had my name on the outside. I wasn't prying. I just — saw."

For a moment she considered lying, and dismissed it; they had passed beyond the economy of lies, she and this man. "It says to be patient with you," she said. "Whoever finds you. It says start with bread."

Elodie stood very still. Then: "Good," he said quietly. "That's — yes. Good." And he went down the stairs and out into the dark toward the rooms the wardens had already ransacked once, and Maren watched the door a while before returning to her table.

* * *

Sleep refused her, so she worked.

The last task was the one she dreaded most: preparing the space where Elodie would stand. Grandmother Fairweather's notes specified it precisely — the binder must offer from ground the palace remembers, within sight of the crown, with the king's eyes open or near enough to it. Which meant the offering floor was the dais itself. Which meant Elodie would stand where kings stand, in the exact spot where the thing had eaten a dozen reigns, and speak.

Maren went through the rite one final time, alone, checking each element against the transcription. The name. The kinship claim. The opening of the self — *willingly given, freely offered, or the giving is theft.* The signal. The seal below. The window: two hundred heartbeats, after the wine, before the bread.

Everything accounted for. Everything except the variable no preparation could touch: what a man gives up when he opens his memory like a pantry to something that has eaten kings. She had read the accounts of the last binder. *Lost herself entirely,* the marginalia said, in a hand that shook. Grandmother Fairweather had known the cost and paid it, and her grandson had read those same notes and volunteered anyway, and Maren had accepted his volunteering because there was no one else, and she would carry the arithmetic of that acceptance to whatever grave received her.

She blew out the candles except one. In its small light she looked at the four sealed letters lined on the table's edge, wax still glossy, and thought about how a life reduced to contingencies could fit in one hand.

Thirty hours now. Below her, the city slept around its secrets — the pyres' cold rings in the market square, the cells under the mint, the palace on its hill with something ancient smiling through a beloved king's face. Above her, the wind worried the shutters again.

She picked up her pen, and began the final entry in her working journal: *Tomorrow we commit. May every letter I wrote tonight go unread.*

The ink dried. Somewhere far off, the first bell of the new day rang, and the countdown she had been holding at arm's length for nine days finally touched her skin.
