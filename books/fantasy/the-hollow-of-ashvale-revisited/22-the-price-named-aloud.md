## 22. The Price Named Aloud

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Maren finally set down her pen.

She had been writing for an hour — contingency letters, she'd called them, though she hadn't said contingency to whom. The scratch of the nib was the only sound in the tower room, and Elodie had learned in the past weeks that Maren Voss wrote the way other people prayed: steadily, stubbornly, as if the act itself might hold the world's edges together.

"Say it," he said.

Maren didn't look up. "Say what?"

"The price. You've been circling it for three days. You write a line, you cross it out, you look at me like I'm a ledger entry you can't balance. Say it aloud. I'm a taster. I've swallowed worse than words."

She capped the inkwell with more care than the gesture required. Then she folded her hands on the desk and looked at him across the guttering candle, and Elodie saw something in her face he had only seen once before — the night she told him about his grandmother, the night his whole life turned out to have been written by someone else's hand.

"You know most of it," she said. "You've known since the ledger. But knowing a thing and naming it are different acts, and I won't let you walk into that crypt having done one and not the other."

"Then name it."

"The rite requires willing memories." Her voice was level, archival, the voice she used for facts too large to survive any other tone. "Freely offered. That part is true, and it is also the smallest part of the truth. It isn't *a* memory, Elodie. It isn't a handful. The Hollow feeds on inherited memory — on everything a self is built from. To unbind it, you must open the whole pantry. Everything you love. Everything you remember loving. Every taste, every face, every word spoken to you by anyone who ever mattered. It will take all of it, because it has been starving for three hundred years, and it will not stop at half a meal."

The candle flame leaned. Outside, Ashvale murmured its midnight murmur — cart wheels, a drunk singing two streets over, the mint's great stamping engines keeping their patient time beneath everything, as they always had.

"My mother's kitchen," Elodie said.

"Yes."

He hadn't meant to say it first. He had meant to be composed about this, the way he was composed about arsenic — cataloguing, professional, unbothered. But the words came out of him before he could weigh them, the way the worst poisons were always the ones that tasted of nothing at all.

"The smell of it. Bread on Thursdays, fish on Fridays, the burnt honey cake she made every year on my birthday because she refused to admit she couldn't bake. The way the light came through the east window and turned the flour dust gold. I don't—" His throat closed. He worked through it the way he'd been taught: breathe, swallow, wait. "I don't get to keep that."

"No."

"Wren's face."

Maren was quiet a moment. "If you mean the girl who brought back your luck-knot — yes. That too. And everything attached to it. The market lane where you met her. Her father's betrayal, if you count that as love's shadow, which the rite likely will. Love doesn't come in clean portions, Elodie. The Hollow eats the whole animal."

Elodie stood. He hadn't decided to stand; his body simply declined to keep sitting while this happened. He walked to the narrow window, where Ashvale lay below in its scatter of lamps, the charm-market dark now, the bakery where Bettina had burned her own work just another roof among roofs.

"And my name," he said. "The taste of my own name. That's what you called it once. What did you mean?"

"I meant that somewhere in you is the memory of being named. Your grandmother speaking it over you as an infant, or your mother, or whoever held you first. It's the oldest memory you own — older than language, almost. Older than thought. And it is the keystone." She rose and joined him at the window, not touching him, never touching him; Maren Voss touched paper, not people. "When the Hollow takes everything else, that memory goes last. And when it goes, Elodie — if it goes — you will still be alive. Your heart will beat. You will eat bread and taste bread, because the tongue survives even when the man doesn't. But you will not know who is doing the tasting."

* * *

They sat again, and Maren laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only mercy she trusted.

"There are three outcomes. First: the rite fails. The Hollow refuses the offering or breaks the binding mid-rite. In that case it consumes you entirely — body, blood, gift, all of it — and then it has a taster's constitution inside a king's crown, and Ashvale is finished. I put the odds of that at perhaps one in five."

"Cheerful."

"Second: the rite succeeds completely. The Hollow takes everything and releases Aldric. You die on the throne room floor with a full belly and no self at all. The archivists would call that a clean binding. I would call it a burial."

"And third."

"Third." She hesitated, and Elodie realized with a strange, distant surprise that Maren Voss — who had bargained with the Lord Protector without blinking, who read death ledgers like novels — was afraid to finish the sentence. "Third, the anchor holds."

"Wren's knot."

"She believes it will. A thread of you tied outside the Hollow's appetite — hair and hemp and intention, old hedge-craft, the kind your grandmother practiced. If it works, one small warmth survives outside you, and after the feeding, that thread can call you back. Not all of you. Perhaps not even a tenth of you. But enough that you wake up *someone*, even if that someone is mostly a stranger wearing your hands."

Elodie laughed. It surprised them both — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it.

"A stranger wearing my hands. Do you know, that's been my whole life? Three years of tasting poison for a crown, three years of selling charms under floorboards, and the entire time I was already a stranger — to the city, to the law, to myself. At least now it'll be honest."

"That is not courage talking. That is exhaustion dressed as courage, and I've read enough false entries to know the difference."

"Then what does courage talk like?"

Maren considered the question seriously, because she considered everything seriously. "Courage says: I understand exactly what I am losing, and I choose it anyway, and I do not pretend the loss is small. So let me make sure you understand. Close your eyes."

"Why?"

"Because I am going to inventory you, Fairweather, and I want you to feel every item leave the shelf."

He closed his eyes.

"Your mother's kitchen," Maren said quietly. "Gone. The Thursday bread, the burnt honey cake, the gold light in the flour dust. Gone. Your first taste of salt water — you told me about it, the day we decoded the cipher, you talked for ten minutes about a boy licking spray off his fingers on the harbor wall. Gone. The fever-charm child, the one you couldn't refuse — her mother's gratitude, the weight of the coin you wouldn't take. Gone. Wren's face. The luck-knot in her hair. The morning she came to beg forgiveness and you gave it, which cost more than anger would have. All of it, gone. Your grandmother's voice from the ledger — the fragment we pulled out of the binding, the one that made you cry and pretend you hadn't. Gone. And last, last of all, the taste of your own name in someone else's mouth. *Elodie.* Gone."

The silence afterward was enormous.

When he opened his eyes, Maren was watching him with the expression of a woman performing an autopsy on someone still breathing.

"Well," Elodie said. His voice came out steady, which felt like a small miracle, or a small betrayal. "That's the menu."

"You're crying."

"Tasters cry. It washes the palate." He wiped his face with his sleeve, roughly, like a boy. "All right. Here is what I understand, so you can write it down in whatever ledger you keep of fools. I understand that I may walk into that crypt a man and crawl out of it nobody. I understand that Wren will introduce herself to me tomorrow and every day after, and I will not know why her face makes my chest hurt until someone tells me. I understand that I will eat bread and it will be the first time, every time, forever. I understand that the king dies either way, lucid or hollowed, and that the only version of his death that ends the hunts and saves the midwife and the whistling boy and Bettina and everyone else this crown has fed to its pyres — the only version that does any of that — runs through me."

He stood again. This time it was deliberate.

"I understand the price. I've tasted it. It tastes of nothing, which is how I know it's the real poison."

* * *

He went down the tower stairs into the cold, because he needed to move, and Maren let him go, which he took as a kind of respect.

Ashvale at night was a different city — softer, guiltier. He walked without direction and found, without surprise, that his feet had chosen the harbor. The fish-smell and the tar, the masts knocking together like nervous teeth. Somewhere out there was the boat of the man who had sold his name for silver, and somewhere ashore was the daughter of that man, who had carried a luck-knot back to him through streets full of informers because she believed a debt of honor mattered more than a father's safety.

Wren found him, the way she always seemed to. She came along the quay with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, and she didn't ask why he was standing at the rail staring at black water, because Wren had the rare intelligence of knowing when questions were just knives with better manners.

"You've decided," she said.

"How did you know?"

"Because you stand differently when you've decided. Before, you lean. Now you're planted." She came to the rail beside him. "Maren sent for me?"

"Maren sent for you," he confirmed. "There's a thing she wants to try. An anchor. Old craft. She thinks — we think — that some thread of me might survive outside the Hollow's reach, if it's tied right, if it's given freely, if—"

"If I give it." She pulled her hand from her pocket. On her palm lay the luck-knot, frayed now, sea-stained, the little charm he'd sold her a lifetime ago in a market that had since tried to burn itself clean of people like him. "I've been carrying it since the cells. I kept meaning to return it properly and kept failing, and then I understood I wasn't supposed to return it at all. It was waiting for this."

"It might not work," Elodie said. "Maren puts the odds low. And even if it works, what comes back won't be — I won't be—"

"I know what you won't be." Wren's voice didn't waver. She was a fisherman's daughter; she knew weather, and she knew that you didn't argue with it, you prepared for it. "You won't know me. You won't know the harbor or the market or your own name. And I will walk up to you every morning and say, hello, my name is Wren, and you'll say, pleased to meet you, and it will break my heart fresh every single day." She looked at him then, straight on, and her eyes were bright and furious and certain. "And I will do it anyway. Because you forgave my father, which cost you more than hating him would have, and I watched you pay it. So don't you dare tell me what I can afford."

Elodie's throat worked. "You should hate me a little. It would be easier."

"Nothing about you has ever been easy. That's rather the point of you."

She took his hand — hers rough with rope-work, his soft with ink and tasting-spoons — and pressed the knot into his palm, and closed his fingers over it.

"Hold onto this," she said. "Not tonight. Tomorrow, when Maren does her tying. But hold it now, so your hands learn it. So there's a shape in your skin that remembers me even if nothing else does."

They stood at the rail a long time, saying nothing, while Ashvale breathed behind them and the black water breathed in front, and somewhere far above, in a palace on the hill, a dying king was spending his last coins of memory one by one to stay himself through the night.

* * *

Near dawn, Elodie climbed back to the tower and found Maren awake, of course, surrounded by papers, the candle replaced by grey window-light.

"I'll do it," he said.

"I know. I knew before you left." She slid a sheet across the desk toward him. "But I need it in writing. Not for the archive. For *after*. If the third outcome holds — if you wake up a stranger — you deserve to hear, in your own hand, why. People who lose themselves deserve at least a letter from who they were."

Elodie read the page. It was simple, brutal, exact:

*I, Elodie Fairweather, royal taster, hedge-charmer, grandson of the binder, give freely what the rite requires: my mother's kitchen, the harbor salt, the burnt honey cake, the fever-child's mother, Wren's face and Wren's forgiveness, my grandmother's voice, and the taste of my own name. I know it may all be taken. I know I may wake as a stranger to myself. I choose it anyway, with my eyes open, so that Aldric may die a man and Ashvale may stop burning its own.*

Below it, in smaller script, one more line — Maren's addition, or perhaps his grandmother's, passed down through cipher and ledger and three generations of stubborn women and one stubborn man:

*A binder gives. A parasite takes. Remember the difference, whoever you are when you read this.*

Elodie picked up the pen. His hand was steady. Three years of holding cups to a poisoned king's lips had taught him that much, at least: steadiness was not the absence of fear, only fear that had learned its job.

He signed his name.

And as the ink dried, he noticed — absurdly, terribly — that he paused over the letters, savoring them. *E-l-o-d-i-e.* The shape of it, the small domestic music of it, the way it had sounded in his grandmother's mouth three hundred years and one lifetime ago.

One last taste, he thought, of the thing I'm about to lose.

Outside, the palace bell began to toll the hour, and far beneath the throne room, in a crypt that smelled of old wax and older hunger, something ancient stirred toward its midnight meal — and waited for him.
