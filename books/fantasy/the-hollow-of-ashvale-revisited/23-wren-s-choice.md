## 23. Wren's Choice

Wren had learned to read the tower from the street.

Not the archivist's tower itself — nobody went in or out of that one without papers — but the small window above the tannery row where a light burned at odd hours, and the alley door where a boy with quick hands took messages and gave none back. She had learned which nights the lamplighter passed early and which nights he didn't come at all, because on those nights the city felt like it was holding its breath, and she had learned to hold hers with it.

Three weeks since her father had traded a name for silver. Three weeks since she had stood in Elodie Fairweather's doorway with his luck-knot in her fist and asked him for something no one had ever given her before: forgiveness she hadn't earned.

He'd given it. That was the worst part. Anger would have been a debt she could work off. Forgiveness was a weight, and he had pressed it into her hands like bread into a beggar's, warm and heavy, and said, *Carry it however you can.*

So she carried it. And now she was going to carry something else too, if he would let her.

She heard about the rite from Bettina.

The old charm-seller came to the fish stalls at dawn, when the gulls were loudest and no one listened to anyone, and stood beside Wren pretending to inspect mackerel. Bettina had not sold charms since the edict. She had burned them herself in the street rather than be accused, all forty of them, and Wren had watched from her father's boat and thought: *there goes a woman burying her own children.* But Bettina hadn't stopped being what she was. She'd just gone quiet about it, the way a river goes quiet under ice.

"You've been asking after the taster," Bettina said, not looking at her.

"I've been asking after everyone."

"You've been asking after him." Bettina turned a mackerel over with one finger, unimpressed by it. "The midwife told me. The midwife tells me everything now; I'm apparently the only person in Ashvale who doesn't sell information." She let that sit. "They're going under the palace tomorrow night. Midnight meal. The Hollow feeds deepest then."

Wren's hands kept working. Wrapping, tying, handing over coins. Her mother had taught her that — never stop your hands, whatever your face is doing. "What's a Hollow?"

"The thing wearing the king." Bettina said it the way you'd say *the thing living in the wall*, flat and practical, because terror spoken plainly loses half its teeth. "And your taster means to feed it. Himself. His memories. Everything he loves, laid out like a banquet, laced with three years of poison." She finally looked at Wren, and her eyes were old and kind and pitiless. "Maren Voss thinks he might survive it as an empty man. Rell thinks he won't survive it at all. Nobody's asked him which he prefers, because there isn't a preference to be had. It's him or the king, and the king's already mostly gone."

Wren wrapped the last mackerel. Tied it. Handed it over. Took the coin.

"Where do they keep him," she said, "before tomorrow?"

* * *

The answer was: nowhere good, and everywhere guarded, except that Maren Voss had forged hunter credentials so convincing that Wren later learned two actual hunters had saluted the woman carrying them, and Maren had saluted back, which must have cost her something.

Getting in was easier than Wren expected. Getting in was almost insultingly easy, and she understood why when she saw the tower's interior: this was a place built by someone who had spent years assuming no one would ever come for her, and who had recently begun building for company anyway. There were two chairs at the desk now. There had clearly only ever been one before.

Elodie was asleep in the second chair, head tipped against the wall, a ledger open on his chest. He looked thinner than he had in her father's shop. He looked like a man who had been eating well and sleeping badly, which was its own particular kind of wrongness on a face like his.

Maren Voss looked up from her writing and did not seem surprised to see a fisherman's daughter standing in her tower holding a paper-wrapped parcel of smoked herring.

"You're the one who denounced him," the archivist said.

"My father did."

"And you're the one who apologized."

"I did."

Maren studied her the way Wren imagined she studied ledgers — looking for the line that didn't add up. "He forgave you."

"He did."

"That was foolish of him." But she said it softly, and she stepped aside from the chair, and that was the whole conversation. Archivists, Wren decided, were people who believed everything important could be recorded, and therefore saw no need to say anything aloud twice.

She sat down across from Elodie and waited.

It didn't take long. He woke the way she suspected soldiers woke — all at once, hand half-closing on the ledger before his eyes finished opening. Then he saw her and something moved through his face that she couldn't name, some weather front passing over calm water.

"Wren." He closed the ledger. "How did you—"

"Bettina. The midwife. Everyone talks; nobody says anything true, so the truth travels faster." She set the parcel of herring on the desk between them. "I brought food. You look like a man about to attend a banquet, and I know for a fact you haven't eaten since yesterday."

He looked at the parcel. Then at her. Then he laughed — short, startled, real — and she realized she'd been holding her breath since dawn waiting for exactly that sound.

"Maren," he called toward the stairwell, "we have a guest."

"We have a security problem," Maren's voice came back, but it had no teeth in it, and footsteps descended, and soon the three of them were eating smoked herring out of paper at midnight in a forbidden tower while the city below slept around the thing that ruled it, and Wren thought: *this is the strangest supper of my life, and I once ate seal blubber on a dare.*

* * *

She waited until Maren had gone back up to her letters. She waited until the lamp was low and Elodie had walked her to the alley door, because that was the polite thing and also because she needed the walk to find the words, and even then she nearly lost them at the threshold.

"They told me what you're giving it," she said. "The Hollow. Your memories."

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"Everything it wants. Which turns out to be everything I love." He said it lightly, the way her father described storms — *bit of a blow coming, nothing to do but batten down* — and she recognized the voice because she used it herself, every day, at the stall, at home, everywhere a girl wasn't allowed to be afraid.

"My mother's kitchen," he went on, when she didn't answer. "Three years of tastes — every poison, every breakfast, every cup of wine I ever tested. Wren—" and here he paused, and she watched him decide whether to say the next part, and watched him say it anyway, because he seemed constitutionally incapable of lying to her, which after her father was either a gift or a wound, she hadn't decided. "Your face. The luck-knot. The day you came to my door. Those are near the top of the pantry, Maren says. The things we love most are the richest feeding. It will take those first."

The alley was very quiet. Somewhere a cat knocked over a bottle and thought better of staying to explain itself.

"So I'll forget you," he said. "If I live. Maren thinks the self survives the way a wick survives a candle — the shape of me, maybe, the habits. But the man who tied that knot for you, the man who forgave your father — that man gets eaten tomorrow night, and whatever stands up afterward will be a stranger wearing his coat."

Wren had come here with a plan. She'd rehearsed it on the walk over, standing in doorways, mouthing words at the dark. The plan had involved arguing. The plan had involved telling him not to go, finding some other way, making him promise —

But he'd already made his promise, hadn't he. To the king. To Ashvale. To a dead woman's ledger and a living archivist's hope. And she had watched enough men argue with the sea to know how those arguments ended.

So she threw away the plan, and reached into her coat, and took out the luck-knot.

He went still when he saw it. Utterly still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"I've been keeping it," she said. "Since the arrest. I meant to return it sooner, and then I meant to return it never, because it felt like the last piece of you that my family hadn't broken." She turned it over in her palm. In the lamplight she could see what she'd done to it — the new thread worked through the old weave, fine and pale, unmistakable to anyone who knew where to look. "And then Bettina told me what you're doing, and I sat up all night thinking about pantries and banquets and things it eats first, and I thought —"

She stopped. Started again.

"It eats what you love. You said so. It takes the richest memories first, because they're the best feeding." She held the knot out between them, on her open palm, the way you'd hold out a bird. "So don't carry it in. Leave it here. With me."

Elodie didn't take it.

"Wren—"

"Tied with my hair." She said it fast, before her courage could hear itself and quit. "I cut it tonight. My grandmother did the same thing — she was from the Salt Islands, they tie hair into knots there for sailors, so that something of the person stays ashore holding the rope. It's not magic. Or — maybe it is, the small kind, the kind that was never illegal because no one with power ever needed it. But it's *mine*, and it's *outside* you, and if the Hollow eats every memory in your head tomorrow night, it can't eat this one, because this one lives in my pocket and my keeping and my stupid stubborn heart."

Still he didn't move. His eyes had gone bright in a way that frightened her more than anything the edict had done.

"You'd remember me," she said. "That's what I'm offering. Not — I can't stop you going, I know that, I've known it since Bettina opened her mouth. But you'll wake up in that tower as a stranger, and a stranger is going to need someone to tell him who he was. And I—" her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through, the way you push through surf, "—I owe you a debt I can't pay in silver, and my father owes you one he'll never pay at all, and I am *good* at remembering things, Elodie. Fish prices, tide tables, faces, debts, birthdays. Ask anyone on the stall-row. I remember everything. So let me remember *you*. Let me be the ledger. Let me stand outside the banquet with the knot in my fist and hold the rope."

The silence went on so long she began to count her own heartbeats, and got to eleven.

Then Elodie Fairweather reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward something you're afraid will startle — and picked the luck-knot off her palm.

But he didn't put it in his pocket. He didn't tuck it away safe. He held it up between them, turning it, finding her hair in the weave, and Wren watched a man memorize her gift with the same attention he gave to arsenic in a king's cup, cataloguing it, fixing it, taking it in through every sense he had left — because that was his gift, wasn't it, taste and memory and the reading of things — and when he finally spoke his voice was rough as net-rope.

"If it works," he said, "if I wake up empty — show me this first. Before anything. Before my own name. Show me the knot and tell me whose hair is in it, and make me learn it until I can't unknow it again."

"That's the plan."

"And if I die—"

"Then I'll burn it on the pyre with you, and the Salt Islands can judge whether I did it right."

Something broke loose in his face then — not tears, or not only tears, but the thing under tears, the thing that lets them fall. He laughed and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand like a boy, and said, "You know this is the most magical object in Ashvale now. Maren will want to study it. She'll write a paper."

"She can read it over my shoulder."

"She'll be insufferable."

"Good. Give her something to be besides terrified."

He closed his hand around the knot, and Wren watched him do the last thing she'd hoped for and hadn't dared ask: he lifted it to his mouth, briefly, the way a taster tests a cup — and whatever he found there, whatever flavor her hair and her intent and her terrible stubborn love had soaked into the fibers, he nodded once, satisfied, like a craftsman approving another's work.

"It holds," he said. "Gods help me, Wren, it actually holds. I can feel it — a thread running out of me and into the knot and stopping there, anchored, *yours*. If the Hollow strips me to the studs tomorrow, this stays standing. One room in the house it can't enter."

"One room," she agreed. "Rent's paid through winter."

He laughed again, wetter this time, and then the tower door opened above them and Maren Voss's voice came down the stair, clipped and urgent and afraid in the way of people who have trained themselves out of showing it:

"Elodie. We have a problem. Rell's second just reported the conspiracy — the palace seals at midnight, all gates, and they've moved the feeding up. We go *now*, or we don't go at all."

Wren's stomach dropped through the alley stones.

Elodie looked at her. She looked at him. And then, because there was nothing else in the world to do, she kissed him — quick and fierce, salt and smoke and herring, the least romantic kiss in the history of Ashvale and the only one that mattered — and pressed the luck-knot into his hand one final time.

"Come back and give it back to me," she said. "In person. I want to watch your face when you don't know who I am and choose me anyway."

Behind him, up the stairs, Maren called again, sharper.

Elodie ran.

Wren stood alone in the alley with the cold coming in off the harbor, listening to his footsteps fade into a palace that was sealing itself shut, and did the only practical thing left to a woman who loved a man walking into a banquet held for his own devouring.

She went to find Bettina, and asked her to teach her everything she knew about waiting.
