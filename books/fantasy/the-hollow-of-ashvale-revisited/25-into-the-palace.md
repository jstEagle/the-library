## 25. Into the Palace

The gate Rell left unbarred was the servants' postern on the river side of the Mint, and it was the smallest act of treason I had ever seen: a bar lifted two inches off its bracket, a lamp doused, and a man in a captain's coat standing with his back to it, staring very hard at the water as if the whole plan depended on him noticing nothing.

"Captain," Maren said softly.

"Don't say my name." Rell didn't turn. "I heard a noise at this gate an hour ago. I investigated. I found nothing. If something comes through that gate tonight, I will be very disappointed in my sentries." He paused. His shoulders were squared like a man carrying a beam. Then, over his shoulder, just once: "Is he still in there? The king?"

"He's spending his last coins," Maren said.

Rell nodded like a man who'd been paid in a currency he hadn't asked for. "Midnight bell rings four times before the feeding hour ends. After that, the wardens change shift and you're dead." He walked away down the quay without looking back, and I understood we would never speak again unless history went strange.

Then the postern stood open before us, and there was nothing left between me and the house that knew my name but a doorway and my own cowardice.

* * *

I want to say I walked in bravely. What I actually did was stand in the dark for the length of three breaths, cataloguing — because cataloguing is what I do when I'm afraid, the way other men pray. Cold iron hinges, oiled recently. River mud and tallow smoke. Under all of it, faint as a fingerprint on glass, something older: stone dust, beeswax, and a sweetness I couldn't place until I could, and then my stomach dropped through the cobbles.

Honey and ash. My grandmother's kitchen smelled of honey and ash. This corridor did too.

"Maren," I whispered. "It smells like her."

"The palace remembers her," Maren said. She had the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child. "That's not a comfort. That's a warning. Places remember the people who hurt them."

We went in.

* * *

The first ward caught us forty steps past the gate. There was no arch, no rune-carved lintel, none of the theater the hunter-priests used on market streets. It was simply a place where the air thickened, where the lamplight from Maren's shuttered lantern seemed to slow like syrup off a spoon, and then I felt it — a taste arriving on my tongue though my mouth was shut and empty.

Copper. Old copper, and under it something like the smell of a struck flint, and under *that*, attention. A vast, patient, mineral attention, turning me over the way I turned a spoonful of broth.

"It's tasting your blood," Maren murmured. "Stand still. Don't offer it anything. Wards are stupid, but this one is old, and old things get suspicious when you're polite."

The taste deepened. It went past my tongue into my teeth, my gums, the soft hollows behind my eyes where three years of poison lived like silt in a riverbed. I felt the ward read me the way I read arsenic in wine — layer by layer, patient, thorough. And then something shifted in its attention, a kind of recoil, or recognition, and a word arrived in my skull that I did not hear so much as remember hearing:

*Kin.*

The air loosened. The lamplight ran free again.

"What did it say?" Maren's hand was on my arm. "Elodie. Your face went white as flour."

"It said kin." My voice came out strange. "Maren, the palace thinks I'm *kin* to it. To the binder. Not to the Hollow — to *her*. Like the stones can't tell the difference between the woman who bound their master and the boy walking in wearing her blood."

"Good," Maren said, and then, honestly, because she was Maren: "And terrible. Come on. Every step closer, it'll know more about you than I do."

* * *

The servants' corridors were empty, which was worse than full. Midnight kitchens should breathe — banked fires, a cat somewhere, the sour ghost of the day's work. These halls held nothing but polish and silence, and the silence had texture. It pressed. And everywhere, growing stronger as we climbed toward the state floor, the honey-and-ash smell braided itself with something colder underneath. Wet slate. Deep well water. The Hollow's scent, if a thing that ate memory could be said to have one.

At the second stair landing, the palace began to resent me.

I don't know how else to put it. The stones didn't speak and no door slammed, but the temperature dropped each time I passed a threshold, and the walls did something walls shouldn't do — they leaned. Just slightly. The way a room leans when everyone in it has decided to dislike you and is being polite about it. Once, passing a tall window that looked out on the inner court, I caught our reflection in the black glass, and for half a heartbeat the reflection showed three people instead of two: me, Maren, and behind us a small straight-backed woman with her hair in a working braid, hands folded, watching me with an expression I couldn't read because I had never seen her face before and yet knew it better than my own.

I spun. Empty corridor.

"She was here," I said. "She's *in here*. In the stone."

"She bound the Hollow in the crypt below the throne room," Maren said quietly. "Where do you think a binding goes? Into the air?" She touched the wall — flat, warm, ordinary-looking granite — the way you'd touch the flank of a horse you didn't trust. "Your grandmother is load-bearing, Elodie. Every stone she set her seal into remembers her hands. And now those same stones are reading your blood and finding her in it, and I imagine it feels to them like being haunted by a debt."

"That's not how hauntings work."

"No," Maren agreed. "It's how *families* work. Keep moving."

* * *

We nearly died at the gallery.

Not to hunters — to grief. We had cut up through the laundry stair to avoid the great hall, and we came out sideways into a long portrait gallery that Maren swore was disused, and it was, mostly, except that the palace was already unraveling at the edges, the Hollow's feeding running deeper tonight than any night before, and doors in this building had begun opening onto things that were not rooms.

The door at the far end of the gallery stood open on an orchard.

Rain fell inside it. Real rain, silver in moonlight, falling onto real mud, and apple trees heavy with fruit that glistened, and beneath the trees a girl of perhaps eight was laughing at something outside the frame. The sound carried down the gallery clear as a bell. I knew whose laugh it was. Everyone in Ashvale above the age of ten knew Princess Isolde's laugh from festival days, bright and hiccupping, gone eleven years now.

"Aldric's hoard," Maren breathed. "A memory he spent — or one the Hollow pried loose. The palace is leaking. Doors are forgetting to be doors." She grabbed my sleeve. "Do not go near it. Memory-stone isn't stable when it's bleeding. You walk into that orchard, you might come out the other side of someone's childhood, and there is no guarantee the other side has an exit."

But I had already stopped walking, because the rain-smell had reached me, and under the wet earth and apples there was a thread of honey and ash, and the little laughing girl turned her head as if she'd heard my heartbeat across eleven years and a gallery of portraits, and looked directly at where I stood.

She didn't see me. I understood that even as my chest seized. But she looked toward me anyway, the way you look toward a sound in another room, and she said — to the trees, to the rain, to whoever had been standing in that orchard with her on whatever long-gone afternoon the palace was remembering:

*"Tell Papa the green ones are sweeter. He never believes me."*

The door closed itself. The gallery was only a gallery again: dust sheets, cold marble, painted ancestors glaring down out of gilt frames. My face was wet. I wiped it with my sleeve and told myself it was nothing, and Maren had the grace not to look at me while I did it.

"The armrest," she said finally, gently. "If he's spending memories tonight, he's spending them on purpose. Buying time. Buying — messages. Come on. We're close."

* * *

The last ward was the worst one, and it wasn't a ward at all.

It stood before the double doors of the antechamber to the throne room — two hunters in grey, posted where Rell's schedule said no hunter should be until the shift change — and for one long frozen moment the three of us regarded each other in the candlelight like players who'd lost their scripts.

Then the taller one yawned, enormously, and said, "About time. Rell said midnight, and it's a quarter past, and I'm not standing here listening to that throne room *chew* for another hour, Fairweather or no Fairweather."

"You know my name," I said stupidly.

"Whole garrison knows your name." He jerked his thumb at the doors, behind which — and I heard it now that he pointed — came a low rhythmic sound, almost subsonic, felt in the back teeth more than the ears. Like a jaw working somewhere very large. "Rell briefed the honest ones. There's maybe six of us who know what's actually sitting on that throne, and we drew lots for who'd hold this door, and I won, which Toma says means blessed and I say means cursed. Go do whatever it is you're going to do. And taster—" He hesitated, and the hardness fell off his young face and showed the boy underneath. "My sister had a fever-charm once. Brown cord, little glass bead. Bought off a stall in Tanner's Row before the edict. It worked. I never told anyone that. Thought you should hear it tonight, of all nights."

I looked at him. I had sold a hundred fever-charms. I could not remember which one might have been his sister's, and the not-remembering hurt worse than the arrest had.

"Thank you," I said. It was all there was.

* * *

Beyond the antechamber, Maren peeled away downward, into the throat of the palace, toward the crypt and the binding-seal and her contingency letters. At the stairhead she gripped my forearm hard enough to bruise.

"The rite needs both ends lit. I break the seal below; you open the pantry above. Not before. When the bell strikes midnight — the true strike, not the quarter-bells — you speak his name, and you *offer*. Willingly given, Elodie. The moment it becomes a fight instead of a gift, everything your grandmother built dies with her." Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "And if I'm not back up those stairs by the fourth bell—"

"Then I do it alone, and you send the letters anyway."

"I hate the arithmetic of you," she said, which from Maren was a love poem, and then she was gone down the dark stairs with the ledger against her chest, and I was alone in the antechamber of the king of Ashvale, listening to the thing on the throne eat.

* * *

I stood at the doors a while. I'll admit it. I stood there with my palm flat against carved oak older than every dynasty the histories bothered to record, and the wood was warm — body-warm, skin-warm — and it pulsed very faintly, once for each of my heartbeats but a half-step behind them, like something learning my rhythm.

Through the grain, in a voice made of pressure rather than sound, the palace spoke. Or the binder in the palace did. Or the two of them, grandmother and stone, so long braided together they'd become one argument.

*Fairweather.*

Not welcome. Not warning. Just my name, spoken the way you spit a seed.

"I know," I whispered to the door. "You don't want me here. She didn't either, maybe. But she came anyway, and I'm her blood, and I'm coming too."

The warmth under my palm shuddered. The pulse quickened, offended, alive. Somewhere far beneath my feet, iron rang against stone — Maren, reaching the seal — and the whole palace seemed to inhale around me like a great beast scenting blood it recognized.

Inside the throne room, the chewing stopped.

The silence that followed was total, attentive, and hungry, and it was aimed at me. Through four inches of ancient oak, something wearing a king turned its face toward the door and *knew* — not suspected, not wondered, but knew — exactly whose footsteps stood in its antechamber, and whose grandmother's hands were set into every stone between it and the sky.

The doors began to swing inward on their own weight, slow as a mouth opening, spilling light the color of deep water across the floor at my feet.

I stepped through.

Behind me, far off across the sleeping city, the first true stroke of midnight began to fall.
