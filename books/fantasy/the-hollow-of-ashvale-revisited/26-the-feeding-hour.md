## 26. The Feeding Hour

The bell in the chapel tower strikes the first stroke of midnight, and Aldric knows it by feel before he knows it by sound — a vibration in his teeth, a coldness climbing his jaw like frost finding a windowpane.

*Twelve strokes,* he thinks. *Or eleven. Or I have already lost the count.*

He is sitting in the dark of the audience chamber because he cannot sleep and cannot remember why he wanted to sleep and cannot remember what sleep was for, only that once there had been a bed with curtains the color of river-stone and a woman who laughed at him when he tangled himself in them like a boy playing at war. Her face is a smudge now. A warm smudge. He keeps it anyway, the way a beggar keeps a coin worn too smooth to spend.

The crown is heavy tonight.

It is always heavy, but some nights it *pulls*, and this is one of those nights. He can feel it drawing on him the way a well draws water — patiently, without malice, as though he were not a man at all but a depth to be plumbed. His hands rest on the arms of the throne, and under his palms the old oak is cool, and he presses down against the pull the way a drowning man presses against current.

*I am Aldric,* he tells himself. *Aldric of the house of—*

Of the house of—

The second stroke falls. The name goes with it.

*No.* He grips the armrest. *No, I had it. I had it a moment ago.*

There is something in the room with him. There is always something in the room with him, but most nights it is content to sip, to take the small things first — the name of his second horse, the smell of the stables where his father taught him to sit a saddle, the taste of the honey-cakes his mother bought from a cart by the mint. Small coins from a small purse. He barely notices those losses anymore. It is the large ones he fights for, the ones he hoards in the cellar-rooms of himself behind doors made of will: his daughter's laugh. Rain on the orchard roof the morning she was born. The weight of her asleep against his chest, three days old, smelling of milk and new linen.

Those he does not spend lightly.

But the crown is pulling harder than usual tonight, and somewhere below the flagstones, below the crypt, below the memory of the palace itself, he can hear it swallowing.

* *

He has learned to map his own mind the way a general maps a city about to fall.

There are the outer districts — the names of ministers, the protocols of court, the treaties signed and unsigned. Those fell first, years ago, and he did not even hear the gates go. Then the market quarter: faces of servants, birthdays, the layout of his own apartments. Then the temple district, where his prayers used to live, and he wept for a week without knowing why, only that something sacred had been taken and he could not say what.

Now there is only the citadel left. The keep at the center. A handful of rooms, hoarded and defended, each one lit by a single memory burning like a lamp in a window.

His daughter's laugh is the brightest of them.

She would be twenty-two now. He knows this the way he knows arithmetic, coldly, without any warmth attached — but the laugh itself, that he still has whole. It happened in the orchard, the summer she was seven. She had been chasing a wasp with a wooden sword, shouting that she would defend the apples or die, and the wasp had flown up her sleeve, and she had shrieked and spun and fallen into the grass — and then, instead of crying, she had started to laugh. That wild, gasping, undignified laughter children have, the kind adults forget they were ever capable of. And he had lain down in the grass beside her and laughed until his ribs hurt, and the orchard had smelled of apples and cut green stems, and the sun had come through the leaves in coins.

That is the room he defends. Every night, when the crown pulls, he stands at its door in the dark of himself with his back against it.

Tonight the door is shaking.

* *

The third stroke. The fourth.

Footsteps in the corridor — soft ones, the padded tread of the night-servants bringing his meal. He should eat. He is told he must eat; the physicians say his color is poor, though their faces, when they say it, wear a look he has learned to recognize and cannot name. Pity, perhaps. Or fear dressed as pity. They look at him the way men look at a house they believe haunted.

The door opens. Candlelight. Two servants, heads bowed, bearing a covered dish and a cup of wine.

"Your majesty."

Your majesty. He holds onto that. It is a rope thrown across a gap. Whoever he is, whatever remains, he is still *your majesty*, and the words fit him like an old coat, and while they fit he is not yet nothing.

"I am not hungry," he says, and hears with a small shock how thin his voice has become, paper over a hollow.

"You must eat, sire. The physicians were clear."

The physicians. Yes. He eats because he is told to eat, sleeps because he is told to sleep, sits in this chair hour after hour because the crown — no. Because duty requires it. Because—

Because the thing beneath the palace likes its king visible. Likes him seated in the high chair with the metal circle on his head, a sign hung out over an empty shop. *Open for business. The kingdom continues. Nothing has changed.*

He takes the wine because refusing has consequences he cannot articulate but can feel, like weather coming. The wine tastes of iron and dust. Everything tastes of iron and dust now; he mentioned it once, years ago, and a taster was dismissed, and he cannot remember which taster or why that mattered, only that the guilt arrived later, like a bill for a debt he never knowingly contracted.

The servants withdraw. The door closes. The fifth stroke falls, and the pulling begins in earnest.

* *

This is how it works. He understands the mechanics now, in the wordless way one understands digestion or dying.

The crown is a mouth. Not metaphorically — he has stopped trusting metaphors, because metaphors let you pretend. The crown is a *mouth*, and beneath the palace, in the crypt his grandmother's grandmother sealed and every generation since has quietly forgotten, something ancient wears his line like clothing and feeds through the metal on his head. It eats memory. It has eaten his. And it is patient, so patient, taking only what he will not immediately miss, keeping the shell of him plump and presentable for the court, for the ambassadors, for the portraits.

But lately it has grown careless. Greedy. Lately it pulls hard enough that he feels the loss as pain, bright and specific, and he has begun to understand what that means.

It is finishing. Whatever long slow meal it began when they set this circlet on his head at his coronation, it is approaching the last course. And when a parasite finishes its host, the host becomes convenient to lose.

*Aldric grows beloved,* he remembers reading once, in some archive, some ledger — whose? when? — *then hollow, then dead. Each succession strengthens it.* He does not know if he read that or dreamed it. He does not know if it matters. He knows only that his father died strangely lucid, in one terrible clear hour after years of vagueness, gripping his hand and saying words he could not then understand:

*It will come for the crown, boy. Spend yourself before it spends you.*

He thought his father mad. He sat by the bed and nodded and patted the papery hand, and within the month the bells rang and the crown came down onto his own head and settled there like a key turning in a lock.

The sixth stroke. The seventh.

In the dark of himself, the door to the orchard-room shudders. Beyond it he can hear grass moving, sun in leaves, a child's voice winding up toward laughter — and around the door, pressing in from all sides, the cold attention of the thing that feeds. It is not cruel. That is the worst of it. It is no crueler than winter. It simply wants, and what it wants is everything he is, and it has waited longer than his dynasty has existed, and it does not intend to be denied.

He stands at the door. He puts his back against it. His shoulders ache with effort that has no muscle.

*Not her,* he says, to the dark, to the mouth, to whatever listens. *Take the treaties. Take the ministers' names. Take my mother's face, take the hymns, take the year of the flood, take all of it — but not the laugh. Not the laugh.*

And the dark considers him. He can feel it considering, the way one feels a large animal's attention cross a paddock. And then, gently, almost tenderly, it begins to take everything else instead — sweeping through the outer rooms of him like a tide through a ground floor, and he lets it, he *opens the doors himself*, because a bargain is a bargain and he has so little left to trade.

The eighth stroke.

* *

He loses the flood. He loses the harbor-master's name and the harbor itself, the gulls and the fish-smell and the day he was seasick in front of the delegation from Vessany. He loses his wedding — the whole bright afternoon of it, gone like breath off glass — and he staggers, in the chair, in the dark chamber, and grips the armrests, and does not cry out because kings do not cry out.

He loses rain. All rain. The sound of it on every roof he has ever slept under.

He loses his own reflection at various ages, a whole gallery of young men who were him, thinned away to nothing.

And through it all the orchard-room holds, because he has spent everything else buying its safety, and the thing beneath the stones honors its bargains — it always has, that is the horror, it is scrupulous — and the laugh stays. Sun through leaves. Apples. His ribs aching. Her seven years old and invincible.

The ninth stroke.

But he can feel it now, circling. Reconsidering. The bargain was for tonight, and tonight is nearly spent, and tomorrow night the price will be higher and his purse nearly empty. He has perhaps one coin remaining beyond the laugh — one small true thing — and he understood, suddenly, in the tenth stroke's cold vibration, what his father had meant.

*Spend yourself before it spends you.*

If he waits, the laugh goes the way of the rain, silently, in his sleep, unspent and unmourned by anyone who could mourn it. But if he spends it — if he *chooses* the spending — then for one moment he is not a larder being emptied. For one moment he is a man making a purchase. And a purchase leaves a mark. A purchase changes the world outside his head.

He needs the world outside his head to change. Just once. Just slightly. Before the shop closes forever.

* *

The eleventh stroke.

He rises from the throne. His legs hold, barely; the chamber swims; the candles gutter in air that has gone cold enough to see his breath. The crown pulls downward, furious now, hungry, cheated of its schedule, and the pain in his skull is like a hook behind the eyes — but he is king, gods help Ashvale, he is still king inside this ruin, and his body still obeys the old drill-ground commands.

*Kneel.*

He kneels. Not before the throne. Beside it. At its right hand, where the armrest curves down toward the seat, low, close to the floor, hidden by the drape of cloth unless a man knelt exactly here and looked exactly thus.

He has a knife. Of course he has a knife; a king's boot-knife is the last honest thing a king owns. He drew it from his boot on the ninth stroke, when the plan assembled itself in him out of fragments — his father's voice, the ledger, the arithmetic of a dying purse — and now he sets the point against the underside of the armrest, against the old oak, older than him, older than his father, wood that has drunk the sweat of nine generations of hands.

The crown screams. He hears it not with his ears but with his teeth, his bones, the wet grey matter of him — a silent pressure like the whole sea leaning on a door. *Give it. Give the laugh. Give it give it give—*

"No," Aldric says aloud, to the empty chamber, in a voice cracked and thin and utterly certain. "You may not. This one is mine."

And he opens the door of the orchard-room himself.

Sun through leaves. Apples and cut stems. A girl with a wooden sword falling backward into summer grass. And then the laugh — the whole of it, unbroken, gasping and wild and undignified, the truest sound his life ever held — rising up through him like water finding its level, pouring out through his hands, into the knife, into the wood.

He carves as he spends. Letter by letter. The blade bites deep because his grief drives it deep. F-A-I-R-W-E-A-T-H-E-R. Seven letters and a lifetime. He does not entirely know why the name — the name surfaced with the laugh, tangled in it, a taster's name, Fairweather, someone safe, someone *outside*, someone whose blood is wrong in a way the ledgers whisper about — and he does not question it. When you are drowning you do not audit the rope. Someone outside the walls. Someone the thing has not yet eaten. Someone who might, someday, kneel exactly here, exactly thus, and read.

The twelfth stroke dies away.

Under the armrest, in the dark, in the old oak of his fathers, the word is finished: FAIRWEATHER.

And the laugh is gone.

He kneels there a long time, emptied, feeling the shape of the loss the way the tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. The orchard-room is dark now. Cold. Just a room. He wanders its bare interior in the dark of himself and finds nothing, and understands that soon there will be no *he* to wander anything, and feels — not fear, not anymore — something quieter. Relief, almost. The tide going out.

But the mark is made. The coin is spent. Somewhere in the world above, in the ordinary morning that is coming whether he sees it or not, a word exists now outside his skull, carved by his own hand, and nothing that eats memory can uncarve it.

*Fairweather,* he thinks, tasting the syllables like a stranger's cooking. *Someone is coming. Someone with blood the ledgers fear.*

The candles burn low. The crown settles on his brow, sated for tonight, patient again, and King Aldric — what remains of him, the watchman of an empty citadel — bows his head over his spent treasury and begins, out of habit, out of love, out of the last stubborn reflex of a self with almost nothing left to be self *with*, to hoard.

Tomorrow night it will feed again.

But tomorrow night, he suspects — the thought arrives whole and calm, a gift from whoever he used to be — tomorrow night, it will finally meet the Fairweathers.
