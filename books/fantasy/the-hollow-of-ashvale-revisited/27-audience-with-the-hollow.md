## 27. Audience with the Hollow

They dressed him for it.

Not in chains — Maren had argued that point and won, though Elodie suspected the victory had more to do with appearances than mercy. Chains would raise questions. A gift-taster presented to the crown wore clean wool and an expression of humble gratitude, and so that was what he wore: a grey coat Bettina's needle had let out at the shoulders, a shirt with a collar stiff enough to feel like a small polite execution, and boots Rell had requisitioned from stores because Elodie's own had a split sole that clicked on stone.

"A taster," Rell said, adjusting the collar himself, which was the closest thing to tenderness the captain permitted. "You're being presented as a taster. You will taste. You will bow. You will say nothing unless spoken to."

"And if I'm spoken to?"

Rell's jaw worked. "Then you'll lie beautifully, or you'll die quickly. I'd prefer the first."

It was past midnight. The palace above them hummed with the particular silence of a court that never fully sleeps — servants padding through corridors, guards shifting weight, somewhere a clock counting hours nobody trusted anymore. Below, in the crypt, Maren was already moving toward the binding-seal with grandmother's ledger pressed to her chest like a breastplate. Elodie had watched her go down the stair and had wanted — absurdly, childishly — to call after her. *Come back.* He hadn't. She'd have hated it.

Instead he stood in an antechamber off the great hall while a chamberlain with ink-stained cuffs read over his presentation papers for the third time.

"Elodiah Fairweather," the chamberlain said. "Royal food-taster, third grade. Seconded tonight from the household of—" a thin pause, the bureaucratic equivalent of a shrug— "the Lord Protector's office. You are here to sample the midnight vintage. Do you understand your role?"

"I taste things," Elodie said.

The chamberlain looked up sharply, decided the answer was either insolence or idiocy, and chose not to investigate which. "You taste things. Good. The king has been... particular lately about his wine. The physicians want a fresh palate." He said *fresh* the way a man says *unspoiled meat* — clinically, with a faint nausea underneath.

The doors opened.

The throne room of Ashvale was built to make a person feel like a coin dropped into a very large purse: small, round, and expected to be spent. Pillars rose into dark the torches couldn't finish. Banners hung dead-still in air that should have moved them. And down the length of it, past ranks of courtiers arranged like cutlery, the king sat beneath the canopy with the crown on his head and his hands folded in his lap with terrible, patient stillness.

Elodie had seen Aldric once before, years ago, from the taster's gallery — a broad man then, ruddy, loud in the way kings are allowed to be loud. The thing beneath the canopy had kept the shape but lost the weather of him. His face was smooth in the wrong way, like fruit stored too long. His eyes were open and did not blink often enough, and when they did blink it came late, like a memory of blinking rather than the act itself.

And the crown.

He'd felt it from the doorway. Everyone probably did, if they let themselves — a pressure behind the eyes, a sweetness at the back of the throat like the smell of overripe pears. Three years of professional tasting had trained Elodie to name what touched his tongue before he flinched from it, and what reached him across that hall was old, and hungry, and patient in the way only something that eats centuries can afford to be.

*There you are,* said nothing, in a voice made of nothing. But he felt noticed all the same, the way a mouse feels the owl before it hears the wing.

The chamberlain announced him. "The gift-taster, Your Majesty, sent up with the midnight vintage."

Courtiers turned. Elodie walked.

The floor remembered feet. That was the thing no one warned you about palaces this old — the stones soaked up everything, processions and funerals and coronations, and walking them was like wading through shallow water full of other people's days. He tasted the floor the way he tasted wine: dust, candle-fat, fear layered in strata, and under it all, older than the dynasty banners, something cold coiled through the foundation like a root system. Like the palace itself had been planted around a seed.

*Grandmother walked these stones,* he thought. *She knew this cold. She named it.*

That thought steadied him the way a handrail steadies a man on a swaying deck. He was not the first Fairweather to cross this floor toward that crown. He was the second. And the first had left him instructions.

A page brought the wine — a black glass bottle, unmarked, poured by hands that didn't quite look at what they served. Elodie took the goblet, knelt at the proper distance, and raised it for the ritual sip.

The moment it touched his tongue, the world narrowed to a single point of cold.

The wine was fine. Old. Notes of cherry and iron and something buried deeper — but threaded through all of it, unmistakable to a tongue like his, was the taste of the cup's last drinker. Not Aldric. *Before* Aldric. And before her, another, a ghostly sediment of swallowed reigns, each one a thin ring in the wood of the thing, like counting a tree's years by its meals.

Kings. It had drunk kings the way he drank wine. Slowly, savoring.

He lowered the goblet. He did not drink again.

"Your Majesty," said the chamberlain smoothly, "the vintage is pronounced sound."

"It is," Elodie heard himself say, before he could stop it.

Silence, of the kind that has weight and temperature. Several courtiers stopped breathing in the manner of people hoping to become furniture. The king's head began, slowly, jointlessly, to turn.

"No," said the king — and the voice was wrong, doubled, a chord instead of a note, Aldric's timbre wrapped around something vast speaking from inside a space too small for it. "No. That voice knows me."

The court froze. Truly froze — not etiquette-freeze but animal freeze, the whole hall becoming one held breath. A lady's fan stopped mid-snap. A guard's spear-tip trembled.

Elodie stayed on one knee, goblet steady, heart hammering against his ribs like a fist on a door. Every instinct he owned screamed at him to lower his eyes, mumble, retreat into the gray anonymity of service. He let the scream pass through him and set it aside like a dish cleared from a table.

He looked up.

"You know me," the king said again, and now the doubling thinned, and for one horrible instant there was only one voice, an old man's voice, tired and lost. Then the cold flooded back in behind it, and the thing wearing Aldric leaned forward on its throne with an interest that had nothing human in its geometry. "Little vessel. Little poisoned cup. I have tasted you in the walls. You carry such a *pantry*."

"I do," Elodie said.

His own voice sounded very small and very clear, the way a bell sounds in an empty church. He had rehearsed this in Maren's tower until the words wore grooves in him. Say it plain. Say it once. Names have edges; use the edge.

He spoke the true name.

Not aloud, exactly — the ledger had been precise on this. It was less a word than a key turned in the back of the throat, three sounds folded into one breath, a name shaped the way a wound is shaped. Grandmother's cipher had rendered it as a diagram of the mouth itself. He gave it to the hall.

Every torch guttered at once.

The banners, dead-still all evening, shuddered as though a door had opened somewhere in the architecture of the world. Courtiers cried out or didn't — several simply sat down where they stood, folding like emptied clothes. The chamberlain made a noise like a stepped-on flute. Somewhere high above, glass cracked in a window that no wind had touched.

And the king stood up.

It was wrong, how fast it was. One moment the figure slouched beneath the canopy; the next it filled the space before it, robes hanging from shoulders that seemed suddenly broader, shadow falling longer than the torchlight could account for. The face remained Aldric's face, but it was a mask worn by something that had gotten careless about the fit — the eyes gone silver-dark, the pupils blown wide as wells.

"*Who speaks that,*" it said, and the doubled voice filled the pillars, and dust sifted down from rafters that had held their dust for a hundred years.

Elodie rose. Slowly, because rising fast in front of an apex predator reads as flight. He set the goblet down on the flagstones with a small, deliberate click, and straightened, and stood in the sight of the abyss with his hands open at his sides.

"My name is Elodiah Fairweather," he said. "My grandmother bound you. I've read her hand. I know what you eat. I know what you are."

The hall held still around them, two hundred courtiers playing dead, and between the throne and the taster stretched ten paces of stone that felt like the whole width of the world.

The Hollow tilted the king's head. The motion had curiosity in it, actual curiosity — the hunger paused, the appetite leaning sideways to examine. It was worse than rage. Rage he could have braced against. This was the look of a connoisseur finding an unfamiliar vintage in the cellar.

"Fairweather," it repeated, and the name came out rolled and savored, turned over the tongue like a coin bitten for gold. "The binder's line. We wondered where you'd gone. We wondered why the stones went quiet whenever a Fairweather walked them." The silver eyes narrowed, and behind them, far behind, something flickered that might have been a man — a hand twitching at his side, fingers curling as though grasping at a rail. "*We ate her,*" it said softly, almost tenderly. "She poured herself out and we drank every drop, and she was *sweet*, little taster. Full of salt and fury and names. Is that what you've come to be? Another vintage?"

"No," Elodie said.

His knees wanted to fold. Sweat ran cold between his shoulder blades. Every hair on his body stood at attention, and beneath the terror ran the training — three years of swallowing death in small doses and cataloguing it calmly. *Name the poison. Name the dose. Hold the cup steady.* This was only the largest cup he had ever been asked to hold.

"I've come to negotiate."

Something happened to the hall then that Elodie would spend the rest of his life failing to describe. The Hollow *laughed* — but the laugh came out through the whole building, through the stones and the pillars and the bones of the dead kings in the crypt below, where Maren was, right now, kneeling at a seal with a chisel in her hands. He felt the laughter pass up through his boot-soles and knew, with sick certainty, that she felt it too.

"Negotiate," the Hollow said, delighted, dreadful. "The mouse opens parley with the cat. Oh, binder's-get. Oh, little laced cup. Very well." The king's body descended back onto the throne with unhurried grace, robes settling, the mask settling, civilization reassembling itself over the hunger like ice over deep water. Around the hall, courtiers began cautiously breathing again, some weeping without noise. "We are old, and we are bored, and you are the most interesting thing to walk this floor in a century. Speak, taster. Amuse us. Tell us what you could possibly hold that we would want."

Elodie breathed in. Tasted the air: torch-smoke, fear-sweat, pear-sweetness, cold.

*Everything,* he thought. *I'm holding everything I love, and I'm holding it out to you like bait on a hook.*

"I'll tell you," he said. "But first — look at your king. Look closely. Tell me how much of him is left."

The silver eyes flickered. And in their depths, for a single unguarded instant, the Hollow hesitated —

and below the floor, faint as a heartbeat through stone, Elodie felt a seal begin to crack.
