## 28. The Negotiation

The court did not breathe.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. Three hundred courtiers frozen mid-curtsey, mid-sip, mid-whisper — a chamberlain with a goblet halfway to his mouth, a lady-in-waiting caught between one blink and the next. Dust hung in the light from the high windows like it had been told to wait. Even the fire in the great hearth had gone still, flames standing up straight as soldiers at inspection.

Only two things moved in that room.

The Hollow, wearing King Aldric like a coat cut too large, turned its head toward me.

And me.

The true name still sat on my tongue where I'd spoken it, and I could feel the shape of it lingering — *Hollow-that-was-Vess, Hollow-of-the-Second-Binding* — the way a strong spice lingers after the dish is done. Maren had warned me that speaking a true name was a door opened from both sides. She hadn't lied. Something enormous was looking through the door now, and its attention had weight. It pressed on my chest like deep water. It pressed on my teeth.

"Gift-taster." The voice came out of Aldric's throat but it wasn't his voice. It was smoother, older, the voice of someone who has had centuries to practice sounding reasonable. It rolled around the throne room and came back off the stone walls slightly changed, as if the room itself were tasting it. "You know what most men do when they learn my name?"

"Run," I said. My voice sounded small. I made it stand up straight anyway. "Or kneel."

"They run or they kneel," the Hollow agreed. "You did neither. You stood in my hall and you said my name out loud, in front of my food." A pause. The king's face smiled without the king behind it, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near the eyes because there was nothing behind those eyes to reach. "I find I'm curious about you, Elodie Fairweather."

It knew my name. Of course it knew my name. It had tasted it already — I'd felt that cold tongue along my spine three nights ago over a cup of wine, and I understood now that what I'd taken for a glance had been a full reading. It had gone through me the way I went through the king's breakfast: cataloguing, noting the arsenic, filing everything away.

"You've been feeding near me for years," I said. "Every meal I tested for him."

"I don't feed near tasters. Tasters are bitter." The Hollow leaned forward on the throne, and the crown settled on Aldric's brow like a mouth settling around something it intended to swallow slowly. "But you — you're not a taster, are you? You're a pantry. Three years of poison stored in living flesh, and not one drop of it souring you. Do you know how rare that is? I have worn eleven kings. I have drunk their fathers' memories and their sons'. I have never once found a vessel who could hold venom and keep his sweetness."

Flattery. I recognized it the way I recognized almond in a custard — immediately, and with a sinking feeling, because almond was usually there to cover something worse.

"I'm flattered," I said.

"No, you aren't. But you're polite, which is rarer." The Hollow rose. Aldric's body rose with it, tall and wrong, joints moving a half-beat late as if the king's body were being operated by puppet strings attached somewhere very far away. It descended the three steps of the dais unhurried. The frozen court watched it go with eyes that couldn't follow, and somehow that was worse than if they'd all been screaming.

It stopped six feet from me. Close enough to smell — and this is a thing I will carry to whatever grave is left for me — close enough to smell that Aldric's body smelled of nothing at all. No sweat, no oil, no skin. Kings are supposed to smell of something. This one smelled of clean stone in an empty room.

"Here is what I offer," the Hollow said. "And understand that I do not offer twice."

* * *

"Aldric grows old," it said. "Aldric was always going to grow old. That is the flaw in every host — they come to me young and beloved and full, and within twenty years they are spent, and I must begin again with the heir, and beginnings are expensive. Successions are messy. Civil wars. Regencies. Half of Ashvale burned last spring over a disputed claim three generations back, and I lost forty years of accumulated memory in the archive fires. Do you know what it costs me, Fairweather, when Ashvale burns its own history?"

I thought of Bettina's charms curling in the street brazier. I thought of the ash on the cobbles that never quite swept away. I said nothing, which the Hollow apparently took for agreement.

"But you," it went on. "You could end all that. A vessel-taster. Not a king — kings are temporary, kings are meat. A constant. Someone who stands at the center of the court, tastes everything that enters it, holds everything I harvest, and never sours. Your body already knows how. Your blood has been practicing for years." The smile again, patient as erosion. "Serve me, and I keep this shape. The beloved king. The peace. Ashvale keeps Aldric — or the idea of Aldric — for another century instead of another decade. No succession crisis. No regency. No more hunts, even. I'll call them off myself; fear is a seasoning I can spare. And in exchange you taste for me forever, and your city keeps its king, and nobody else burns."

There it was. Laid out neat as a merchant's scales.

And here is the thing I am not proud of: for the space of three heartbeats, maybe four, I wanted it.

Not the serving. Never the serving. But the arithmetic — gods forgive me, the arithmetic was beautiful. One man's freedom against a city's peace. My hands against the pyres. I had spent weeks watching Ashvale eat itself alive with informers and silver and fear, and here was a voice offering to switch it all off like pinching out a candle. All it cost was me. And I had already decided, coming down through Rell's unbarred gate, that I was prepared to spend exactly that.

That's what made it dangerous. The Hollow wasn't offering me anything I hadn't already priced. It had read me, and it knew the exchange rate I'd set on my own life, and it was offering to pay it — only in coin I could live to see spent.

"No," I said.

The stillness in the room got stiller. Somewhere in the walls, in the memory-stone Maren had described, I felt something shift its weight, the way a great house settles at night.

"No?" The Hollow tilted Aldric's head. "Curious. Most men need longer than that to refuse eternity."

"It's not eternity I'm refusing." I kept my hands loose at my sides. Maren had drilled me on this in the tower until my knees ached: *it will read your body faster than your words, so give it nothing to read.* "Eternity's fine. It's the terms."

"The terms are generous."

"The terms are a leash with a long rope." I took a breath. Here was the part I'd rehearsed in front of a mirror like a fool, and here was the part that had to sound unrehearsed, or none of the rest would land. "You said it yourself — eleven kings. Beloved, then hollow, then conveniently dead. Every time, you start over with the heir, and every time, you come out stronger, because each new host arrives pre-seasoned with the memories you farmed out of the last one. I'm not refusing your offer, Hollow-of-the-Second-Binding. I'm declining to be the twelfth entry in a ledger that always ends the same way."

Something moved behind Aldric's eyes. Not anger. Interest — the cold, bottomless interest of a thing that has not been contradicted since before this dynasty had a name.

"Continue," it said.

* * *

"You feed on inherited memory," I said. "On what people hand down. Grandmother to daughter, king to heir, master to apprentice. That's the vintage you prefer — I read your ledger, or your binder's ledger, which amounts to the same thing. Fresh memory is wine. Inherited memory is a cellar." I swallowed. My mouth had gone dry, and some traitor corner of my brain noted that dryness would ruin my articulation, and some other corner noted that even now, facing the oldest hunger in Ashvale, part of me was still cataloguing like a cook. Maybe that part was the only reason I was still standing.

"So here are my terms," I said. "Me. Freely. Everything I am — three years of tastes, my mother's kitchen, every face I love, my own name spoken in my grandmother's voice. Willing memories, given whole, not harvested, not torn. You know the difference. You've eaten both kinds, and you know which one nourishes and which one just fills the belly and leaves you hungry in an hour. What I offer is a feast compared to anything you've scraped out of eleven dying kings."

"And the price?" The word came out of it slow, like it was checking the coin for clipping.

"You release Aldric. Entirely. His mind, his name, his remaining days — such as they are — returned to him clean. Not a husk I prop up for show. Him. Whatever's left of him, he gets back."

For a long moment the Hollow said nothing, and the silence had texture, layers, like standing inside a struck bell.

Then Aldric's face did something I hadn't seen it do in any of Maren's descriptions of the hollowed king. It hesitated. The expression flickered — hunger calculating against appetite, appetite against something older and stranger that might, in a creature like this, have been the nearest thing to doubt.

"You would give up everything," the Hollow said. "Taste, home, love, self. You'd walk into me empty-handed except for the gift itself. And for what? One old man's last few years. He won't thank you. He barely exists anymore. I've eaten nearly all of him worth eating."

"He carved a word under his armrest last night," I said. "In the dark, with his last saved-up scrap of himself. He spent his daughter's laugh — the last coin he had — just to leave one word for whoever came next. FAIRWEATHER." My voice cracked on the second syllable and I let it crack, because hiding it would have cost more strength than showing it. "You don't spend your last treasure on a warning for a stranger unless there's still a king in there worth saving. So don't tell me what he's worth. I've tasted the evidence."

The Hollow looked at me for a long time.

And I realized — with the vertigo of a man discovering the floor is deeper than he thought — that it wasn't deciding. It was remembering. Somewhere under eleven reigns and however many centuries, under the crown and the hunger and the patience, there was a fragment of the thing my grandmother had bound, and my grandmother had bound it with willing gifts too, and some sediment of that first bargain still lay in it like silt in a well. It remembered what freely given memory tasted like. It had been starving for it ever since, and harvesting kings was just drinking from puddles while it waited for rain.

"Yes," it said finally, and the word came out almost gentle, which frightened me more than any roar could have. "Yes. I remember her now. She stood where you stand. She also negotiated. Her terms were worse — she gave everything and asked nothing, because she loved this city more than she loved herself, and I have dined on that kind of soul exactly once in all my long appetite." The head cocked. "You're not like her."

"No," I agreed. "She was better."

"She was. And yet." Aldric's ruined mouth curved. "You offer what she offered, and ask what she wouldn't. Release. Terms. A contract." It spread the king's hands, palms up, an oddly human gesture on a body that wasn't human anymore. "Very well, pantry-taster, vessel-who-does-not-sour, grandson-of-my-binder. I accept your counter. Willing memories, freely given, all that you are and all that you love — and in exchange, the old man walks free of me, whole as I can make him."

My heart slammed once, hard, against my ribs.

"But," the Hollow said.

There is always a *but*. There is always a hook in the bait, a bitter note under the sweetness, arsenic in the custard. I kept my face still and waited, because if I'd learned one thing from three years of tasting for a poisoned court, it was that the second flavor is the true one.

"The giving must happen below," it said. "Where she bound me. The seal is there, and seals are doors, and doors open from both sides. Your archivist has already found it — yes, I know about the archivist, I know about the captain, I know about the girl with the knot in her hair; I have been wearing this court longer than you have been alive, child, and you did not sneak past me, you were *admitted*." Let that land, its tone said. Let me remind you who holds the room. "Go down. Break the seal. Give yourself to me at the binding-stone, and I will keep my side of the bargain to the letter."

"To the letter," I repeated.

"I always do." And there it was — the second flavor, the true one, arriving late and bitter on the back of the palate. To the letter. Not to the spirit. Every contract I'd ever heard of that ended in ruin had been honored to the letter.

But I'd known that was coming. Maren had known. We'd planned for it, argued about it across her desk by lamplight, her quill stabbing the air: *it will find the loophole, Elodie, it always finds the loophole, our job isn't to close every loophole, our job is to make sure the loophole we leave open is the one we chose.* Below, at the binding-stone, with the seal broken and the rite begun — that was exactly where we wanted this to happen. The Hollow thought it was steering me toward its own ground.

It was steering me onto mine.

"Then we're agreed," I said.

"We are agreed." The Hollow extended Aldric's right hand, and after a moment — because refusing would have broken the negotiation and I wasn't ready to break it yet — I took it. The king's hand was cool and dry and light, light as a bird's bone, and beneath the skin I felt nothing. No pulse. No tremor of the man. Just the vast, patient pressure of the tenant, and underneath the tenant, faint as a coal under ash, one small stubborn warmth that pressed back against my palm for half a heartbeat before it was covered again.

*Aldric,* I thought. *Hold on. I'm coming down.*

The Hollow released my hand and turned back toward the throne, and as it climbed the dais the frozen court began, degree by degree, to move again — the goblet completing its journey to the chamberlain's lips, the blink finishing itself, the whisper resuming mid-word, three hundred people seamlessly convinced that no time had passed at all. Only the king's shadow on the wall stayed wrong a moment longer, stretched and many-jointed, before it remembered how to be a man's.

"Gift-taster." The Hollow settled back onto the throne, crown gleaming, king-shaped, beloved. "Return at midnight. Come hungry."

I bowed — low, correct, a servant seeing the king properly seen off — and walked backward three steps before turning, because turning your back on that thing felt like a luxury I hadn't earned.

At the great doors I paused, one hand on the oak, and looked back once. From this distance the figure on the throne could have been any king in any portrait: remote, gilded, safe. Except that the eyes were fixed on me, tracking me across a hall of moving, oblivious people, and the mouth behind the beard was smiling with what I now understood, with a lurch, was anticipation.

Below, in the crypt, a lamp was already burning. Maren was already waiting. Somewhere between the throne room and the binding-stone, hunters were closing ranks above us, and a captain was choosing, this hour, whether to be a soldier or a man.

Midnight was four hours away.

I had a banquet to poison, and I intended to bring every course.
