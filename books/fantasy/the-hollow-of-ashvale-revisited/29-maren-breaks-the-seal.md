## 29. Maren Breaks the Seal

The crypt beneath the throne room smelled of cold stone and old tallow, and Maren Voss thought, absurdly, that it smelled like the archive. Like her whole life, compressed into a single breath: dust and wax and the particular sweetness of paper left too long in the dark.

She had descended by a stair that did not appear on any plan of the palace she had ever catalogued — forty-one steps, each one worn into a shallow bowl by feet that had come this way for centuries, though no record existed of anyone coming this way at all. That was the thing about the Hollow's house. It kept its own ledgers. It simply never let anyone read them.

Her lantern threw her shadow long against the wall, where carvings spiraled upward like smoke frozen mid-rise. She had copied those carvings once, years ago, from rubbings made by an archivist named Dellet — a name that appeared in three separate indexes and then vanished from the rolls entirely, as if the palace had digested him. The carvings were older than the dynasty. Older than the crown. They showed a king kneeling before a mouth, and the mouth was not a monster's. It was beautiful. That was the detail Maren had never been able to shake, all these weeks of cipher-work and sleepless arithmetic: the mouth was beautiful, and the king knelt willingly.

*Not tonight,* she told the carving. *Tonight he kneels to us.*

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them with a scholar's detachment, noting the tremor, cataloguing its cause — fear, obviously, but fear braided with something worse, something she refused to name until she had finished what she came to do. Above her, muffled through thirty feet of stone, the midnight court was assembling. She could hear nothing of it, really — only the deep organ-note of the palace settling, the way a great beast settles before it feeds.

She set her satchel on the crypt floor and unpacked it with the same care she brought to handling first editions: grandmother Fairweather's ledger, wrapped in oilcloth; the iron stylus; the small hammer with its head of grave-glass; and the letter.

The letter she had written four nights ago, by candlelight, in her best archival hand, because if she was going to die in a hole under a throne she wanted her handwriting to be legible. It was addressed to no one. Or rather, it was addressed to whoever found it, which in Ashvale usually meant the same thing as no one.

She unfolded it now and read it aloud, because her grandmother — a real grandmother, a woman who had died ordinary and warm — used to say that terror shrinks when you make it speak. Her voice came out steady enough. That surprised her.

*"To the finder of this letter. My name is Maren Voss, formerly Second Archivist of the Royal Rolls, dismissed on charges I will not dignify by repeating. If you are reading this, then either I am dead, or I have succeeded, or both — the two outcomes being, as of this writing, difficult to distinguish.*

*Below the throne of Ashvale there is a seal. It is not a lock. Locks keep things out. This keeps something in — or rather, it kept something in, once, and has been failing for longer than any living memory can measure. A woman of the Fairweather line bound it, generations past, and paid for the binding with every scrap of herself she owned. Tonight another Fairweather pays again, differently, and my part is small and cowardly and essential: I am to break the old seal so the new offering can reach the thing it was made for.*

*If you are a hunter reading this, know that I have broken no law your edicts recognize, because your edicts were written by the thing I am trying to kill. If you are a scholar, know that everything I learned is in the ledger beside this letter, and that I have annotated it properly, because even at the end I could not help myself.*

*And if you are Elodie Fairweather — if some part of you survives to read this — know that I am sorry. Not for bringing you here. For the arithmetic. There was a moment, in the Lord Protector's office, when I traded your freedom for my purpose, and I have told myself since that it was the only trade available. That may even be true. But I want it written down somewhere, in ink, in my own hand: I chose. Clean hands are for people who never touch anything that matters.*

*The rest is procedure. Procedure steadies me. Forgive me the length of it."*

She folded the letter along its creases and set it atop the ledger, weighted with a stone, where someone might someday find it. Then she turned to the seal.

* * *

It sat in the floor at the center of the crypt: a disc of black stone perhaps two feet across, ringed with script so old it predated the common alphabet, worn nearly smooth. Maren had spent three nights translating it by lamplight in her tower, cross-referencing Dellet's rubbings against the Fairweather cipher, and the translation had frightened her more than anything else in this affair — more than the Hollow's true name, more than the king's hollowed eyes.

The script was not a warning. It was a recipe. Instructions for renewal. Whoever bound the Hollow had known that bindings fail — that everything fails, given appetite and time — and had left maintenance instructions for their descendants. And the descendants had never come. Generations of Fairweathers had lived and died above this stone, tasting kings' food, selling charms in the market, carrying the poison in their blood without knowing why, and not one of them had climbed down these stairs to renew the work.

*Because they didn't know,* Maren reminded herself. *The knowledge was taken, or hidden, or eaten.* The Hollow fed on inherited memory. Of course the binding-lore had been the first course. Of course Elodie had grown up thinking his immunity was a curse and nothing more. The parasite had tidied its own cage, century by century, nibbling away the instructions until only the cage remained.

She knelt beside the seal. The stone was colder than the air around it, cold in a way that felt directional, like the cold was going somewhere — down, and further down, into whatever dark throat the crypt had been built over.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty room, because her grandmother's advice worked on more than terror. "Procedure."

Procedure, as she had reconstructed it from the ledger, ran thus: the original binding had been anchored by a seal-stone keyed to the binder's bloodline. As long as the seal held, the Hollow's attention stayed fixed downward, inward, on its own containment — a splinter in its mind it could not dig out. Breaking the seal would release that fixation. It would also, briefly, blind the creature to everything except the site of its oldest wound. In that blindness, Elodie's offering — freely given memories, laced with three years of swallowed venom — could reach it undefended.

That was the theory. The theory required precision. Break the seal too early and the Hollow would have time to recover, to taste the trap in the air. Too late and Elodie would stand before an anchored god with nothing but his nerve. Somewhere between the two lay a window measured, according to the ledger's cipher, in heartbeats. Maren had calculated it at roughly two hundred. She had checked the calculation eleven times.

She took up the grave-glass hammer.

Above her, faintly now, she heard footsteps — many of them, moving across the throne room floor, the court gathering for the king's midnight meal. And beneath the footsteps, another sound, one she might have imagined: a slow, wet rhythm, patient as tide. The Hollow feeding early. Warming up.

"You've had centuries," she murmured to it. "You can spare me five minutes."

The first strike of the hammer rang wrong — not the flat crack of stone but a sound with texture, a sound like a word spoken underwater. The seal-script flared dull red along its grooves and went dark. Maren struck again, on the second ring of characters, and this time the cold rose up through her knees, through her chest, and she tasted — actually tasted, on her tongue, though she had no gift and never had — something ancient and attentive turning over in its sleep.

*It knows,* she thought. *It knows I'm here.*

Of course it knew. It had always known. It had simply never bothered to care about one archivist with a hammer. She struck a third time, a fourth, working outward along the rings as the ledger prescribed, and with each blow the carvings on the walls seemed to lean closer, the beautiful mouth widening by fractions no honest measurement would confirm.

Between strikes she spoke the counterwords, the ones the cipher had preserved — the binder's own phrases, passed down in a dead dialect, meaning things like *I am the debt* and *the account remains open*. Her accent was certainly atrocious. She hoped whatever listened cared more about intent than pronunciation.

On the seventh strike, the hunters arrived above.

She heard them clearly now — boots, armor, the barked cadence of command. Someone shouting about a gate left unbarred. Someone else shouting about a taster missing from his cell. The palace, which had slept through centuries of quiet parasitism, was suddenly, catastrophically awake, and every minute of noise overhead was a minute Elodie's window was shrinking.

Maren struck the eighth ring. The seal cracked — a real crack this time, a fissure running across the black stone like lightning frozen in slate — and from the fissure rose a smell she had no name for: the smell of a memory leaving a room. Nostalgia and rot together.

Her hands were steady now. That was the strange mercy of ritual, she thought — you gave your terror a job to do, and while it worked, you worked. Nine. Ten. The fissure widened. Red light pulsed in the grooves, slower now, like a heartbeat winding down.

Eleven.

The seal broke.

Not shattered — *opened*, the two halves of the black stone parting along the fissure like a book falling open, and from the gap poured a silence so complete it pressed against her eardrums. The wall-carvings went still. The cold reversed itself, flowing back down into the gap, and Maren understood with a scholar's terrible clarity that she was looking at the oldest wound in Ashvale: the place where a woman very much like her, very much like Elodie, had once knelt and said *enough*.

She set down the hammer. Took up the ledger. Found the final page, the one with the window calculated in heartbeats, and began to count.

One. Two. Three.

Above her, the boots stopped. The whole palace held its breath.

Somewhere overhead, a door opened onto the throne room, and a young man with poisoned blood walked toward a king wearing a hungry crown — and Maren Voss, disgraced archivist, keeper of other people's memories, counted heartbeats in the dark and refused, with every stubborn fiber of her well-annotated soul, to pray.

Forty-one. Forty-two.

The counting was the only prayer she had, and she meant every number.
