## 30. The Offering

The throne room at midnight was colder than a cellar, and Elodie understood why: cold kept things fresh. Meat kept in the cold. Memory, too.

He walked down the aisle of black stone with his hands open at his sides, palms forward, the way Maren had drilled him for three nights running. *Open hands mean offering. Closed fists mean contest.* The Hollow had watched him speak its true name — had turned on him like a great eye turning — and then it had done something worse than rage or hunger. It had waited. Curious. Patient as an unpaid bill.

King Aldric sat slumped beneath the crown like laundry left on a hook. His eyes were open. Somewhere very far behind them, Elodie suspected, a man was still spending coins he no longer had.

*You brought it,* the Hollow said. Not aloud. It arrived in the marrow of his teeth, in the wet dark of his inner ear, in the taste of copper that wasn't there. *You brought the taster to the feast. That is polite of you.*

"I brought myself," Elodie said. His voice sounded small under the vaulted ceiling, and he let it sound small. Small things got eaten last. "That's the whole of my luggage."

Maren was below, in the crypt, her hands on the original seal. He couldn't hear her. He didn't need to. If she did her part, the Hollow's roots would be loosened just enough for the rite to take hold; if she didn't, he would die here as an appetizer and never know the difference. He had decided, somewhere around the third contingency letter she'd read aloud to steady herself, that not knowing was a kind of mercy. You could not flinch from what you refused to watch.

*Speak your price,* said the Hollow. *All flesh has one. Even yours, little ledger of poisons.*

"My price is simple." Elodie stopped six paces from the throne — close enough to be heard, far enough that the thing wearing the king would have to lean forward to reach him, and leaning forward meant wanting, and wanting meant opening. "I offer memory. Freely given. Freely taken."

*Whose?*

"Mine. All of it." He swallowed, and his mouth tasted of nothing, which was its own strange comfort — three years of poison had burned his palate down to smooth blank stone, and now that stone was going to be the plate on which he served himself. "Every meal I ever tasted. Every face I ever loved. Every name I ever answered to."

Silence, but not empty silence — the silence of a vast throat deciding whether to swallow.

*Why would you give this?*

"Because you're holding a king hostage," Elodie said. "And I'm tired of watching people be eaten by things that call themselves necessary."

---

It began, as all his work began, with breath.

Maren had taught him that much true about his gift: that tasting was only attention made liquid. You breathed in, and the world came with the breath — intention, history, want. For three years he had used it to find arsenic in porridge and nightshade in wine. Tonight he would use it to pour out everything he had.

He closed his eyes. Opened the pantry.

His mother's kitchen came first, because it always did. The low ceiling stained amber by decades of pipe-smoke. The smell of onions surrendering in butter. Her hands, flour to the wrist, pressing dough with the heels of her palms the way she pressed everything — firmly, without apology. The way she'd hum when she thought no one listened, off-key and unbothered, a song with no name that he had never once been able to forget even when he tried.

*Yes,* the Hollow said, softening, the way any predator softens at the smell of blood. *More.*

He gave it more.

Three years of the palace kitchens unspooled behind his eyes like a merchant's inventory. The king's breakfasts, catalogued and spat into silk. Arsenic in the honey-cakes, week after week, a slow silver thread of it he learned to taste the way other men learn their letters. Nightshade in the wine on the Feast of Lanterns. A bitter alkaloid in the almond milk, so subtle it took him four sips to catch it, and by then he'd already swallowed, because swallowing was the job, swallowing was the whole point of him.

He gave the Hollow the taste of every poison he'd ever carried, and he gave it honestly, with the texture of each still intact — the chalky grit of antimony, the green-hot bite of foxglove, the slow warm numbness of hemlock spreading through his gums like a secret told too close to the ear. He gave it the fear that came with each one, too, because memory without feeling is just inventory, and he wanted this thing gorged, not browsing.

And threaded through all of it, braided in like colored wire through grey wool, he gave it Wren.

Her face. Gods, her face — the salt-scoured freckles, the chapped lips that went crooked when she laughed, the particular stubborn tilt of her chin that was her father's chin before her father sold that chin for a bag of silver. He gave the Hollow the day she stood outside Maren's tower with the luck-knot in her fist, hair tied into the cord with her own two hands, saying *you hold a thread of me, and the thread doesn't break*. He gave it the weight of forgiveness — how heavy it was, heavier than anger, how carrying it changed the way a man walked.

*Stop,* said the Hollow. But it said it the way a drunk says stop at the fourth cup, meaning the opposite.

Elodie smiled. And opened deeper.

He gave it his own name. Spoken in his grandmother's voice — the voice he'd pulled from the ledger's binding, thin and crackling as old paper, saying *Elodie Fairweather*, with the particular music she put in it, the note that meant *you are mine and I am glad*. He gave it the vertigo of discovering your family wrote your fate. He gave it the tower room where Maren taught him to read echoes in ink, the wonder reawakening after years of hiding, the first time he'd touched a page and tasted the archivist who wrote it — ambition, ink-stains, loneliness — and understood for the first time that he had never been alone in his gift, only alone in his silence.

The Hollow fed.

It fed the way fire feeds on a hayrick, fast and total and blind with appetite. Elodie felt the pull of it — vast, tidal, a suction at the base of his skull like the world's largest drain — and he rode it instead of fighting it, because fighting it would close the pantry door, and the pantry door had to stay open until the very last course.

Because here was the arithmetic Maren had helped him see, the beautiful terrible sum at the bottom of the page:

Three years of immunity were three years of accumulation.

Every poison he had swallowed and survived had not vanished. It had gone into him — into his blood, his bones, the deep tissue of his remembering. His body had learned to hold what should have killed him, yes. But memory is not stored in the mind alone. Memory lives in the body's whole archive, and his body's archive was steeped, saturated, marinated through and through, in enough venom to kill a regiment.

He had not come to feed the Hollow.

He had come to poison it.

*What—* said the Hollow, and for the first time its voice lost the ancient patience, the bored hunger of something that had eaten kings since before dynasties had names. *What is this. What have you—*

"The bill," Elodie said, through teeth that were beginning to chatter, through vision graying at the edges as his own memories poured out of him like wine from a breached cask. "For three years of meals. Itemized."

The Hollow convulsed.

He felt it convulse in the stone floor, in the crown, in the air itself — a shudder that ran up through the palace's foundations, through the memory-stone walls that had forgotten themselves in Maren's reports, through the frozen court standing like candle-wax figures along the walls. King Aldric's body jerked on the throne, spine arching, mouth opening on a sound that was half scream and half something else — relief, maybe, terrible and total, the relief of a man whose captor has finally, finally been stabbed.

The Hollow tried to release him. Elodie felt it try — felt the great suction reverse, felt it attempt to spit him out the way a snake spits a toad too bitter to keep down.

Too late. He had offered freely, and freely given cannot be ungiven. The rite his grandmother had written in ciphered blood did not permit refusal once accepted. The pantry door did not close until the pantry was empty.

So the Hollow ate.

It ate the arsenic-laced memory of his mother's kitchen. It ate the foxglove-bright recollection of Wren's crooked laugh. It ate his own name, spoken in love, steeped in three years of accumulated hemlock and nightshade and antimony, every syllable a coated pill, every warmth a vehicle for the venom sleeping in it.

And the venoms woke.

Not in him. In *it*.

Elodie dropped to his knees on the black stone. He hadn't decided to. His legs had simply resigned. The gray at the edges of his vision crept inward, patient as frost. He was aware, distantly, of the court unfreezing around him — of shouts, of Captain Rell's voice cutting through the panic like a blade through canvas, of hunters pouring through the gate Rell had left unbarred, too late, so late, arriving at a battle that was already ending.

*You knew,* the Hollow said. Its voice was changing. Shrinking. Unraveling at the edges like a rope fraying under load. *You knew what you were. You came here as a—*

"A taster," Elodie said. The word cost him something — cost him the memory of learning it, actually, he could feel that specific memory sliding away even as he spoke it, the classroom, the first coin earned, gone, dissolving into the Hollow's dying gut where it curdled among the poisons. "It's the family trade."

The crown slid from Aldric's brow.

It hit the dais steps with a sound like a bell falling down a well, and the sound it made on impact was wrong, was *dying*, was the ring of a thing that had fed for centuries suddenly finding nothing left to draw on. Where it struck, the black stone bleached grey, then white, then crumbled to powder fine as ash.

Aldric collapsed forward. Rell caught him before his head found the steps.

Elodie tried to stand. He managed, instead, to fall over sideways, cheek against stone that was rapidly forgetting how to be stone — he could feel it going soft and uncertain beneath him, could feel the corridor beyond the doors opening onto someone's childhood, a summer field, the smell of cut grass bleeding through a wall that no longer remembered being a wall—

*Maren,* he thought, and could not remember who Maren was, and thought it anyway, because some names go deeper than memory. *Someone named Maren. Someone is supposed to come.*

The last thing he saw clearly was the throne room ceiling, vaulted and vast and beginning, gently, at the highest point, to forget itself — plaster rippling like water, like flour, like dough under the heels of a woman's palms in a kitchen with a low amber ceiling, a kitchen he could no longer picture, a woman whose humming he could no longer hear, a song with no name going wherever unnamed songs go.

Then the drain at the base of his skull reversed, and everything poured out, and Elodie Fairweather — taster, hedge-witch, binder's grandson, man — followed his memories down into the dark, laced to the last drop with poison, and the Hollow of Ashvale died choking on the life of him.

* * *

He dreamed, or something adjacent to dreaming.

In the dream there was a pantry. Shelves to the ceiling. Every jar labeled in a hand he almost recognized. He walked the aisles looking for one jar in particular — he couldn't have said which, only that it mattered, only that it was warm — and the shelves stretched longer the deeper he went, and the labels grew older, and somewhere far behind him a door stood open letting in a draft that smelled of salt and rain and a girl's chapped-lip laugh.

He reached for a jar. His hand passed through it.

*Hold on to something,* said a voice that might have been his grandmother's, or might have been his own, or might have been no one's. *Something held outside. Something tied with hair. Hold on, and come back poor, but come back.*

In the dream, Elodie Fairweather closed his empty hand around a thread he could not see, and pulled.

Far above him, in a tower room full of contingency letters, an archivist looked up from her desk as if someone had knocked.

And far below the throne room, in corridors that were forgetting their own names, something began, slowly, painfully, like bread rising in a cold kitchen, to remember it was a man.

The Hollow's death-scream was still ringing in the stones when the first of the palace walls came down.
