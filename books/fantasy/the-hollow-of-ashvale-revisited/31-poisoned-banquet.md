## 31. Poisoned Banquet

The Hollow ate him like a king at a feast.

That was the image that came to Elodie as it happened — absurd, domestic — the thing in the crown settling in with its napkin tucked, reaching for dish after dish after dish without once pausing to wonder why the courses kept coming, why the taster stood so patiently at its elbow, why no one had ever fed this generously before.

He opened his memory the way Maren had taught him to open a taste: deliberately, fully, from the first note to the last.

His mother's kitchen went first.

He felt it leave — not torn, *served*. The smell of scorched butter on an iron pan. The blue chipped bowl she stirred batter in while humming off-key. The way flour dust hung in the morning light like weather indoors. He offered it up whole, lovingly arranged, and the Hollow took it with a shudder of pleasure that ran through the throne room like a tremor through a struck bell.

Every courtier in the hall stood frozen mid-gesture — a lady with her fan half-raised, a guard whose yawn had become something else entirely. The candles burned without flickering. Somewhere far below, he knew, Maren Voss was breaking a seal older than the dynasty, and her hands were shaking, and he could not help her, and she would not have wanted him to try.

*Come on,* he told the thing wearing the king. *There's more.*

And there was.

Three years of tastes. He poured them out in order, the way he'd catalogued them every dawn of his working life: the king's breakfast, day one — brown bread, salted butter, honey from the palace hives, and beneath it all, faint as a signature, arsenic. Day two, the same bread, different poison. Day three. Day four. He gave the Hollow the entire ledger of his tongue, three years of mornings spent dying by increments in a gilded room, each dose noted, swallowed, survived. He gave it the copper bite of antimony. The almond ghost of cyanide in a wedding gift of candied fruit. The bitter green of hemlock steeped in wine meant for a duke who'd grown inconvenient.

It drank them all down. Of course it did. It could not know yet what it was drinking.

Memory, Maren had said, is not a story. It's a *substance*. It has weight, texture, aftertaste. And every one of these memories had been steeped, for three years, in poison — not around it, not beside it, but through it, the way a riverbed is made of the water that runs over it. Elodie's immunity had never been a wall. It had been a marriage. The toxins lived in him now, woven into the fabric of what he remembered, inseparable from the taste of his own childhood bread.

You cannot take the memory of a poisoned man's life without taking the poison.

He'd understood it only the night before, lying awake in the tower while Maren wrote contingency letters she pretended were prayers. He'd sat up and said, "Maren. What if I'm not offering him memories? What if I'm offering him a meal I've already laced?"

She'd looked at him for a long time. "Then you'll be the first taster in history to serve the dish that kills the diner."

"And me?"

"Immunity isn't armor, Elodie. It's debt." She'd turned back to her letters. "You've been paying interest for three years. Tomorrow you pay principal."

Now, standing on the dais beneath the crown that was not really a crown, he paid.

Wren's face came next, and this was the course he'd dreaded, the one he'd almost hidden at the back of the pantry where a starving thing might miss it. But the rite required willingness — freely given, wholly given — and you cannot give a gift while hiding half of it behind your back. So he gave her: the fish-market lane, her hair escaping its braid in the wind off the harbor, the luck-knot pressed into his palm still warm from her pocket. Her father's face when the coin changed hands. Her voice saying *I'm sorry* through a door he hadn't opened fast enough. Forgiveness sitting in his chest like a stone he'd chosen to carry.

The Hollow took Wren's face and made a sound.

Not aloud. Nothing about it was aloud. But the whole hall heard it anyway — a deep, wet, satisfied sound, the sound of appetite meeting abundance, and the frozen courtiers swayed on their feet like reeds in a current, and King Aldric, slumped in his throne with his eyes rolled white, began very quietly to weep.

*Yes,* Elodie thought, dizzy now, holding himself upright by will alone because his knees had filed their resignation. *Keep eating. There's so much left.*

His own name. His grandmother's voice saying it — *Elodie Fairweather, come here, boy, look what I found* — a memory he'd recovered from her ledger's binding, a fragment of a woman who had unmade herself to cage this very thing. He served it up with both hands. Let it choke on the family that had been killing it slowly for generations.

The Hollow feasted.

And then it stopped.

* * *

The silence afterward was worse than the feeding.

For one long moment nothing moved anywhere in the world. The candles did not gutter. The guards did not breathe. Elodie stood empty-handed in the wreckage of his own mind — he could feel the gaps now, vast and clean-edged, rooms swept bare — and watched the thing in the crown finish swallowing.

Then the crown *shrieked*.

No — Aldric shrieked. Or the body did; the distinction had stopped mattering. The king came off the throne like a man pulled by hooks, spine bowing, hands clawing at the gold band as if he could tear it free of his skull, and the sound he made was not a word in any language but it contained, unmistakably, the shape of *poison, poison, POISON*.

Elodie fell to one knee. Not from the rite — from relief, terrible and shameful, watching the parasite convulse. Three years of immunity becoming three years of venom. Every dose he'd ever catalogued, ever swallowed with a steady face while the physicians watched his eyes, now detonating in a gut that had never learned to refuse a meal.

*You should have asked what was in the food,* he thought, and laughed — a cracked, wheezing sound that echoed strangely in the frozen hall. Taster's humor. Even now. Especially now.

The Hollow fought. Of course it fought. It had worn kings for longer than any dynasty, and things that old do not die easily; they *shed*. Elodi felt it scrabbling through his emptied mind like a rat through a burned house, looking for anything still edible, any crumb of him it could keep — and finding the pantry bare except for the poison, which was everywhere, which was the walls themselves now, because he had held nothing back. Willingly given, wholly given. That had been the requirement. That had also been the weapon.

Aldric collapsed against the armrest of the throne, and the shrieking stopped, and what replaced it was worse: a whisper.

"Fairweather."

Not the Hollow's cold attention. A human voice, rusted from disuse, cracking on the name. The king's eyes cleared — actually cleared, white giving way to a gray so pale it was nearly silver, and in them was a man. A tired, dying, *lucid* man, looking directly at Elodie for the first time since the taster had entered his service three years ago.

"You're—" Aldric coughed. Blood at the corner of his mouth, or something darker than blood, blackish, old. "You're the grandmother's boy. The binder's line." His hand crept along the armrest, trembling, and stopped just short of Elodie's shoulder. "She smelled of rosemary. I was a child. She told my father's father—"

"Your Majesty," Elodie managed. His own voice sounded foreign. Most voices would, he supposed, when you couldn't remember your own. "Don't talk. Save it."

"Save it?" The king laughed — a real laugh, brief and awful and human. "Boy, I have spent six years saving myself one coin at a time. My daughter's laugh. Rain on the orchard roof. The weight of a sword I was given at seven and hated." He drew a breath that whistled. "I have exactly none of it left to save. It ate the hoard. All of it. Everything I hid, everything I buried—" His hand finally landed on Elodie's shoulder, light as ash falling. "But I am *here*. Do you understand? For the first time in six years, there is a *me* here to be here."

Below them, through the floor, through the memory-stone of the palace itself, Elodi felt a concussion — Maren breaking the seal, or the seal breaking Maren, or simply the binding coming apart at last, the cage his grandmother had built with her whole self finally opening from the inside. The air in the throne room changed. The frozen courtiers sagged, several collapsing outright; a woman began screaming somewhere near the doors; the candles remembered how to flicker.

And the Hollow died.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. Things that feed on memory cannot make memories of their own ending; they can only be *forgotten*, and the palace forgot it with a long, sighing groan through its stones, corridor by corridor, ward by ward. Elodie felt it go the way you feel a great fire go out — a sudden absence of heat, a darkness where the light had been pressing against your eyelids. The crown slid from Aldric's brow and rang on the marble steps like a dropped bell, and no one moved to pick it up, and it lay there, suddenly just metal. Just a hat. Just the oldest murder weapon in Ashvale, cooling.

"It's leaving," Aldric whispered. "Or dying. I can't tell. Does it matter?"

"No," Elodie said. "I don't think it does."

* * *

They got the king down onto the steps of the dais because he insisted on the floor — "Thrones are for the thing that wore me," he said, with the ghost of a smile — and Elodie sat beside him, holding a hand that gripped back with startling strength for a man visibly coming apart.

Because that was the price of the exorcism, and they had all known it, and knowing had not made it kinder. The Hollow had eaten Aldric's mind down to the joists. What remained — the lucid core, the stubborn last coin of selfhood that had carved FAIRWEATHER under the armrest hours ago — was held together by nothing now. No parasite stitching him into function. No hunger keeping the shape inflated. Just a dying brain, briefly freed, burning whatever was left of itself in one final clear hour.

"I can feel it," Aldric said conversationally, as if discussing weather. "The edges. Going soft. Like bread left out." He flexed his fingers. "How much time would you say a man has, taster? You'd know. You've tasted everything else."

Elodie considered lying. Considered it seriously, the way you consider a door out of a burning room.

"An hour," he said. "Perhaps two."

"Good." The king nodded briskly, absurdly, like a merchant agreeing to terms. "Then we won't waste it on grief. Grief keeps. Work doesn't." He turned his head, and even hollowed and bleeding from the ear, Aldric had a king's voice when he chose to use it. "Help me up. There are orders to give before I'm a rumor."

"Your Majesty—"

"The hunts end tonight. All of them. Every pyre unlit, every denunciation voided, every prisoner in the Mint cells released with a pardon and an apology I'll dictate now while I can still hold a sentence together." He was trying to rise; Elodie caught his arm. "And Rell. Where's Rell? The captain with the honest eyes who couldn't decide if duty meant obedience. He seals the palace tonight. Let him be the one who unseals it. Let him carry it forward — someone has to, and I won't be here to carry anything but a headstone."

"Rell turned his coat for you hours ago," Elodie said quietly. "One gate, unbarred. It's how I got in."

Aldric closed his eyes. Something moved across his face that might have been the memory of pride, arriving too late to be felt properly. "Then he's already carrying it," he said. "Good. Good."

The hall was waking around them in fragments — guards kneeling, a chamberlain weeping into his sleeves, someone shouting for physicians who would arrive to find nothing medicine could reach. Through a high window, Elodie could see the sky going the color of iron before dawn. Somewhere below, Maren was climbing toward them through a palace that was forgetting its own rooms, and he thought: *soon*, and the thought had no face attached to it, no warmth, just logistics. He noticed that absence the way you notice a missing tooth. He decided to grieve it later, if later came with enough of him left to do the grieving.

"Boy," Aldric said. His grip tightened. His silver eyes were fixed on Elodie with terrible clarity, and in them was the last question of a king spending his final coin. "My daughter. Lysa. Before the end — tell me true. Would she have known me? This last year. The staring. The servants answering questions I didn't know I'd asked. Would she have looked at me and seen her father?"

Elodie thought about lies. About kindness. About a girl dead eight years who could no longer be hurt by either.

"She'd have known you," he said gently. "Fathers are easier to recognize than kings."

Aldric studied him for a moment — the long, weighing look of a man who had spent six years being lied to by everything, and knew precisely the taste of mercy when it was placed on his tongue.

"Liar," the king said, smiling. "Thank you."

Then his hand slackened, his gaze drifted toward the window and the graying sky, and he began, softly, to dictate orders — the hunts ended, Rell named regent-protector, the pardons drafted — his voice still clear, still lucid, a king working against the clock of his own dissolution while Ashvale's first honest dawn in centuries broke over a city full of ashes, and somewhere beneath the throne room, in a crypt filling with hunters' boots and candlelight, Maren Voss read aloud from a letter she had written to a man who could no longer remember her name.

* * *

Elodie stayed until the dictation faltered.

It took less than an hour, as promised. The sentences shortened. Then the pauses lengthened. Then, between one order and the next, Aldric paused too long, frowned faintly, and asked the chamberlain — politely, with perfect dignity — whether the gentleman beside him might be introduced, as he seemed to have forgotten making his acquaintance, and he was quite sure he would have remembered, because the gentleman had kind eyes and the look of a man who had recently done something enormous and paid for it.

Elodie introduced himself. "Elodie," he said, and found he had to check — truly check — inside his own hollowed head for the rest of it. "Fairweather. Your taster, Your Majesty."

"Ah," said King Aldric, with the ghost of the smile again. "The taster. Yes." His hand found Elodie's sleeve and patted it once, twice, like a man confirming something is real. "You should eat something, my boy. You look half-starved."

Outside, the bells of the Mint began to ring — not alarm, not yet, just the hour, ordinary and enormous. Dawn was coming up over the rooftops of Ashvale, and in the market districts, women who had burned their charms in the street were waking to edicts dissolving on bakery doors, and in the cells beneath the Mint, a midwife and a boy who whistled wind were hearing boots descend — many boots, and keys, and a captain's voice calling for lanterns and stretchers and, above all, *gentleness*.

The king's breathing slowed. The physicians hovered. Elodie stood, legs shaking, holding on to the armrest of a throne he refused to sit in — and under his palm, in the wood, he felt the shallow-carved letters of his own name, cut by a dying man's last act of selfhood, and understood that this was the only monument he would ever get, and that it was, all things considered, a good one.

Then the doors burst open, and Maren Voss came through them with blood on her sleeves and a hunter's credential forged in her own hand still pinned to her coat, saw Elodie standing alive amid the wreckage of everything, and stopped as though she'd walked into glass.

"You're—" she started, and stopped again, searching his face the way he'd searched his own mind. Looking for herself in it. Finding, he could see, nothing familiar looking back.

"Yes," Elodie said. "I'm afraid so. Who are you? And why does it feel like I was always going to ask?"
