## 32. The King's Last Hour

The first thing Aldric knew was the taste of rain.

Not the memory of it — the actuality, cold and mineral on his tongue, though he was lying on stone beneath a ceiling painted with stars that some dead craftsman had gilded four hundred years ago. The rain-taste came from inside him, from a place the Hollow had never managed to hollow out. His daughter, three years old, holding her cupped hands to the sky in the orchard and insisting he drink. *Papa, the sky is leaking.*

He had spent that coin already. He'd felt it go — the last bright piece of her laugh, paid out into the dark like a beggar's final copper. And yet here it was again, faint as breath on a winter window, coming back to him from somewhere outside his own skull.

Someone was weeping nearby. It took him an absurd length of time to understand that the sound was his own throat.

*Get up,* he told himself. He had no idea how much of him remained. He felt like a house after a fire — walls standing, rooms open to the weather, most of what had lived there gone to ash and carried off on the wind. But the walls were standing. For now. Whatever the taster had done — whatever poison he had poured down the Hollow's ancient throat along with his own life — it had bought Aldric this: one hour, perhaps two, of being King of Ashvale instead of a crown's puppet wearing his face.

He used them the way he had once promised himself he would use any hour given back to him. Badly, quickly, and without apology.

* * *

Getting up was a lesson in humility. His body had been sat upon for years — not idle, exactly; the Hollow had walked it through audiences and hunts and feasts with a borrowed grace that courtiers had mistaken for vigor — but it had not been *his* for longer than he could bear to calculate. His legs shook. His hands would not close properly around the armrest when he gripped it.

The throne room was in chaos. That registered first, before even the faces: overturned braziers, guards at the doors with their pikes half-lowered and their eyes darting everywhere like men in a burning building, courtiers pressed against the far wall in a clump that stank of terror-sweat even from across the hall. And at the center, near the foot of the dais, three figures — the archivist woman, Voss, grey-faced and gripping a satchel like a shield; Captain Rell, sword drawn but pointed nowhere, which told Aldric more about Rell's character than any report ever had; and the young man kneeling between them.

Fairweather. The taster. He looked like a man who had been emptied by candlelight — pale as tallow, eyes open but seeing some middle distance where, presumably, the rest of his life was still draining away.

Aldric opened his mouth and found his voice was a ruin. He tried anyway.

"Guards."

It came out cracked, barely above a whisper, but the quality of it did the work. There is a particular grain to a king's voice that no impostor fully acquires — something in the assumption of being obeyed — and thirty years of it lived in Aldric's throat even now. Every pike in the room swung toward him. Every conversation died.

"Your Majesty—" someone began, and then stopped, because no one knew what to call the situation. Their king had been sitting among them for years. Their king had also, apparently, just been exorcised in front of them by a food-taster. Etiquette had not prepared anyone.

"I am Aldric," he said, "fourth of that name, son of Maric, and I have been a prisoner in my own body since before some of you were knighted." He let that sit. Let them look at him — really look, many of them for the first time in years, because the thing behind his eyes had learned to make eye contact the way a fish learns to ride a bicycle: adequately, and only when watched. "Captain Rell."

"Your Majesty." Rell crossed the floor at a march, sheathed his sword without being told, and went to one knee. Aldric noticed the man's hands were trembling and chose, graciously, not to notice it aloud.

"How many hunters are in the palace tonight?"

"Two companies under Lord Protector Hale's writ, sire. They sealed the gates at midnight on his order." A muscle moved in Rell's jaw. "One gate was left unbarred."

Aldric looked at him for a long moment. "By whose order?"

"Mine," said Rell, in the voice of a man signing his own death warrant and finding the pen lighter than expected. "I judged the king might prefer a door."

"The king judges you correct." He turned his head, slowly, taking inventory of the hall. "Where is Hale?"

"Gone, sire. When the Hollow began to die, he ran. Toward the river, my sergeant thinks. He'll be out of the city by dawn unless—"

"He won't run far." Aldric heard his own voice flatten into something old and cold, and was briefly glad the Hollow hadn't been able to counterfeit it; it would have made the creature too convincing. "A man who fed Ashvale's children to that thing for thirty years does not get to become a quiet merchant in some coastal town. Find him. Bring him back alive — alive, Captain, I want him to *see* what happens next — and put him in the cells beneath the Mint. The ones with the good locks. He built them."

Rell bowed. Around the hall, the clump of courtiers had begun to breathe again, which meant they had begun to think again, which meant they were beginning to understand that the world had turned over in the space of an hour and their careers were now unmoored. Aldric almost pitied them. Almost.

"More orders, sire?" Rell asked.

"Yes." He gripped the armrest and hauled himself upright by degrees, refusing every offered hand on principle — a king must stand at least once on his own bones. From under the armrest his fingertips found the carving he had made with the last of his mind's strength, hours or minutes ago, when the Hollow had been busy drowning. FAIRWEATHER. He traced it once, like a signature on a treaty. "The witch-hunts end tonight. Not tomorrow, not after deliberation — tonight. Recall every company. Every edict nailed to every door comes down. No more pyres in the market squares."

"Sire," said one of the senior clerks, a dry stick of a man named Ossel whom Aldric had appointed twenty years ago precisely because he was incapable of enthusiasm, "the edicts bear the crown's seal. To rescind them requires—"

"They bear *that thing's* seal," Aldric said, and jabbed a finger at his own crown, which lay toppled on the dais steps where Fairweather's rite had knocked it loose. Even fallen it looked hungry. "Do you imagine I signed those proclamations? Do you imagine I *knew*? Every name burned, every silver piece paid to informers, every child dragged from its mother's kitchen — all of it done with my face and my seal while I screamed behind my own eyes." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. Let them hear the king scream. It was, he suspected, the most honest sound the throne room had contained in a generation. "Take them down, Master Ossel. Burn the edicts if you like. Consider it the first fire in Ashvale that kills nothing."

Ossel bowed so deeply his nose nearly touched his ledger. Somewhere in the clump of courtiers, a woman began quietly crying, and no one shushed her.

* * *

There followed, in the space of perhaps ten minutes, the fastest reordering of a kingdom that Ashvale had seen since its founding. Aldric dictated and Rell wrote, standing, using the captain's own back as a desk. The hunts dissolved. All prisoners held on suspicion of the gift were released into the custody of the archivist Voss pending honest trial — *honest*, he repeated twice, looking at Rell until Rell nodded, because Aldric remembered enough of the last three years' paperwork to know that the word *trial* had been doing very little work around here. Informers were not to be lynched; neither were they to be rewarded. The ledgers of denunciation were to be seized and read aloud, eventually, publicly, however long that took and whatever it cost. "A city that burned people," he said, "can at least be made to read their names."

"And the succession, sire?" Rell asked, pen hovering.

That was the question, wasn't it. His queen had been eleven years dead. His daughter — his daughter had been dead nine years, taken by a fever that Aldric now understood had been no fever at all, but the Hollow pruning the family tree, harvesting the brightest memories in the royal bloodline before they could take root. He had no heir. He had perhaps an hour. He looked at the man kneeling at the foot of the dais with a drawn sword and an unbarred gate behind him, and made the arithmetic of trust.

"Captain Rell," Aldric said. "Kneel properly. Both knees."

Rell went white. "Sire, I'm a soldier. My family kept a ferry on the Greywater for six generations. The lords will never—"

"The lords will do what I say or discover how much of my father's temper survived the parasite." He extended the signet ring from his finger — it had swollen on him over the years, the Hollow's appetites expressed even in his knuckles, and it came away leaving a pale band of skin that had not seen sun since the girl was alive. "Protector-regent. Until the council convenes and the realm finds its footing, you hold my seal and my authority. You will convene trials — real ones. You will protect the gifted, who are henceforth citizens of Ashvale with the same rights as anyone else, and I want that written so plainly that no future regent can squint at it and see permission. And you will be accountable for every order you give, because I am told—" he glanced, unexpectedly, at the taster, "—that accountability is going to matter in this city for a long time."

Rell took the ring with both hands, like a man accepting a live coal. "I don't deserve—"

"No," Aldric agreed pleasantly. "But you left a door unlocked for a dying man, which puts you ahead of everyone else in this building. Deserving is for saints. Governing is for the merely decent. Go — the city doesn't know yet, and cities wake up angry."

Rell went, moving fast, barking orders that reshaped the night. And then it was quiet, or as quiet as a throne room ever gets, and Aldric was alone with two people: an archivist clutching her satchel, and a taster kneeling on his stones.

He descended the dais. It took a while. Nobody hurried him, which he appreciated, and nobody pretended not to watch, which he forgave.

"Your Majesty." Voss bowed, crisp and precise, a woman who had clearly rehearsed this meeting in her head for weeks and was now discovering reality had skipped past the rehearsal. "I— the archives contain your grandmother's correspondence, and if there is anything I can do to ease—"

"Later, Archivist. You've earned later." He looked at her steadily. "You broke a binding-seal in my crypt tonight with hunters at your heels. I know, because I could feel the crypt. It's the only part of this palace the creature never quite digested." Something passed over her face — surprise, and under it grief — and Aldric understood suddenly that she had lost something down there too, or been prepared to. "We will speak of it. Not tonight. Tonight, go write everything down before your hands stop shaking. Everything. Future generations of Ashvale will need to know exactly how stupid we were, or they'll manage to be stupid again in new ways."

She bowed again — deeper this time — and withdrew to a corner with paper and ink, already writing, already salvaging.

Which left the boy.

* * *

Elodie Fairweather stayed on his knees, which irritated Aldric, so he lowered himself — gracelessly, joints cracking like green kindling — onto the cold stone beside him. Two kings of Ashvale, seated on the floor. One crowned for thirty years and absent for most of them; one uncrowned and present for one terrible hour.

They regarded each other.

"You're the granddaughter's grandson," Aldric said. "Sera Fairweather. She bound the thing the first time. Before my grandfather's reign — no. During. She came to the palace when I was small. I remember—" and he stopped, astonished, feeling for the memory and finding it intact, a small warm coal the Hollow had somehow never pried loose, maybe because he had guarded it too jealously, hidden it in the same pocket where he kept his daughter's laugh. "I remember a woman with flour on her sleeves scolding my grandfather in front of the whole court. Telling him his wards were 'pretty scribbles.' Everyone was horrified. Grandfather laughed. It's the only time I ever saw him laugh honestly."

"She talked about the palace," Elodie said. His voice was thin, worn down to thread. "In the notes. I didn't understand until tonight that she meant — that she'd been here."

"Oh, she'd been here. And she left us a debt." Aldric studied the young man's face. It had the transparency of extreme expenditure — the look of a lamp burning its final inch of oil. "You gave it your memories. All of them, I'd guess. Freely offered, laced with three years of arsenic." He shook his head slowly. "My grandfather spent armies trying to kill it. Scholars spent lifetimes trying to understand it. And the answer was a cook's grandson with poison in his blood and the nerve to set a table for a monster."

"It seemed rude to serve it anything clean," Elodie said.

And Aldric laughed — really laughed, a rusty, awful, wonderful sound that startled both of them and sent a ripple through the remaining courtiers, because none of them had heard the king laugh in years and none of them had known why. The laugh hurt. Everything hurt. He was aware, distantly and without panic, of the edges of himself going soft, like wet paper. The Hollow had torn free, but it had torn free the way a rotten tooth comes out — taking root-structure, taking bone. What was left of Aldric IV was running out.

He had perhaps questions worth spending his last clarity on. He chose carefully, the way he had once chosen which memories to hoard.

"Do you know what happens to me?" he asked. "Honestly. I've had enough kindness dressed as reassurance to last a lifetime."

Voss looked up from her papers. Elodie hesitated, and Aldric raised a hand.

"Not her. You. You tasted it. You were inside the bargain with it. Tell me true."

"It's killing you," Elodie said simply. "Or — it was keeping you alive the way a hook keeps a fish alive. Now that it's gone, what it ate doesn't grow back. I don't know if it's hours or days. But it isn't long, and it won't be gentle."

Aldric nodded, absorbing it the way he had once absorbed casualty reports: completely, and with his face arranged into something useful. "Good. Truth is a better pillow than hope. Thank you." He flexed his fingers against the stone, watching them tremble, and asked the second question — the one that had been waiting in him since he woke, patient as a stone at the bottom of a well.

"My daughter. Mirele."

Elodie went very still.

"When it — when the fever took her." Aldric heard his own voice go careful, the way a man walks on ice. "Was she afraid? At the end?"

He watched the young man's face do several things in rapid succession — flinch, calculate, soften — and understood, with the strange double sight of the dying, exactly what was happening. Elodie had never met Mirele. Had never seen the birthing chambers or the sickroom. He knew nothing of her death except what archives and rumor held, and he was now, in this moment, deciding whether to hand a broken old man a truth or a mercy.

Aldric waited. He could have said *tell me true* again. He found he couldn't. Some questions you ask knowing you may receive either answer, and you must accept whichever comes, because demanding honesty and demanding comfort in the same breath is a cruelty only kings can afford, and he was finished affording things.

"She wouldn't have known me at the end," Aldric said quietly, when the silence stretched too long. "Would she. If I had come to her bedside — the thing wearing my face, reaching for her — she would have known. Children always know. She'd have cried for her real papa and he wouldn't have come, because he was screaming behind his own eyes, and she died calling for a man who was standing right there and couldn't move." His breath hitched. "Say it. Someone in this room should say it out loud. Everyone else has spent nine years telling me she slipped away in her sleep."

"That's what the histories say," Elodie said softly.

"The histories are written by archivists who were protecting me, and by courtiers who were protecting themselves, and edited throughout by a parasite with excellent penmanship." He closed his eyes. "Say it."

And Elodie Fairweather, who had nothing left to give and gave anyway, leaned close and said, in a voice like a hand on a shoulder:

"She knew you."

Aldric's eyes snapped open. "You just told me—"

"I'm telling you now." The taster's gaze didn't waver. It had the steadiness of a man who has already paid everything and cannot be billed further. "Whatever was in that room that night — whatever wore your shape — Mirele knew her father. Because a daughter doesn't know a face, Your Majesty. She knows the way you hum when you think no one's listening. She knows you check the window latch twice. She knows the smell of your sleeve. None of that was in the thing's diet — it ate memories, big ones, bright ones. It never learned to be loved in small ways. So when it bent over her bed, some part of her looked past the face and knew her papa was missing — and knowing he was missing means knowing him. She knew you. She knew you were fighting to reach her. And I would stake what's left of my soul on this: the last thing she felt wasn't fear. It was faith that you'd find your way back." He paused. "You did. Nine years late. But you did."

It was a lie. It was, Aldric thought, the finest lie he had ever been told — constructed of inference and compassion and probably outright invention, delivered by a man with nothing left, for no reason except that a dying stranger needed it. And the beautiful, unforgivable thing about it was that he *believed* it. Not as fact. As possibility. And possibility, at the edge of extinction, weighs more than fact.

"Thank you," Aldric said, and meant it with his whole diminishing self. "That was a kindness, and I know exactly what it cost you, and I will not insult you by pretending I don't."

"You'd have done the same."

"No," Aldric said, smiling. "I'd have fumbled it. I was never quick. Ask anyone." He looked over at Voss, still writing in her corner, pen scratching like a small determined animal. "Archivist. When you finish the record of tonight, add this: the king died asking better questions than he ever asked while living. And add the taster's answer verbatim. Future kings should study it."

* * *

What remained was administration, and Aldric did it standing, because he would not dictate his final orders from a chair.

To Rell, returned and ringed: hold the city gently. Feed the poor from the palace stores tonight — the cellars were full; the Hollow had liked its host beloved and well-provisioned. Open the Mint cells. Send word to the border garrisons that the realm's wars were ended too, all of them, the ones the creature had started to keep its kingdoms fat and frightened and easy to harvest. "It ruled by making us small," Aldric said. "Our job now is to become inconveniently large. Schools. Roads. Courts that answer to the accused as well as the accuser. Give them a century of boring prosperity, Captain, and let historians argue about whether I was a good king. I was not. See that the next ones have the chance to be."

To Voss: the archives, the school she apparently intended to found — she flushed when he mentioned it, having confided it to no one, and he waved off her astonishment; kings overhear things, even imprisoned ones — and one final instruction: "Find every family it touched. The midwives, the mothers, the children born in those cold rooms. What was taken from them may be gone forever, but the *record* of it isn't. Grief needs somewhere to go. Give it shelves."

And last, to the hall itself, to the frightened courtiers and the guards and the servants peering in from the doors: he told them the truth, all of it, in plain words, because he was done with plain words being scarce in Ashvale. What had lived in the crown. How long. What it had eaten. Who had burned for the crime of being different while the real monster dined at the king's right hand. He watched shame move through the room like weather, and did not soften it.

"We burned our neighbors," he said, "to keep from looking up at the throne. That ends tonight. It will cost us years and it should."

Then his legs informed him, with democratic firmness, that the speechifying portion of the evening was concluded.

* * *

They brought him to the orchard.

Of course they did — he'd asked, and Rell, regent for all of forty minutes, had already learned that a king's last requests were the one category of command that required no countersignature. The royal orchard sat on the palace's south slope, terraced and old, the trees heavy with late apples. Rain had begun again, fine and soft, and it fell on Aldric's upturned face and he drank it the way his daughter had once insisted he drink the sky.

*Papa, the sky is leaking.*

He sat against the oldest tree, the one she'd climbed, and they brought blankets and a brazier and then, tactfully, mostly withdrew. Rell stayed within call. Voss stayed closer, writing — he could hear her pen scratching through the drizzle, recording the death of a king in real time, and he found he didn't mind. Let it be recorded. Let some student in her school, a hundred years hence, read it and weep and learn.

The taster came and sat beside him. Of course he did. Kings die surrounded by the ambitious; this one got to die beside the only person in the palace who wanted nothing from him at all.

"Tell me about her," Aldric said. "Mirele. Whatever the records say. Tell it like a person."

So Elodie told him — assembled, Aldric realized, from ledgers and servant testimony and a lock of hair pressed in a prayer book that Voss had unearthed — the story of a girl who hid plums in her sleeves and blamed the dog; who demanded the palace cooks teach her to make honey-cakes and produced, once, famously, a tray of charcoal; who had her father's crooked smile and none of his patience. Some of it was certainly embellished. All of it was true. That is the paradox Aldric carried with him down into the dark, and he held onto it the way you hold a rope in high wind.

His hands grew cold. His thoughts began to arrive from farther away, like guests shouting up from a courtyard.

"Elodie."

"Sire."

"What's your name?" he asked. "All of it. Your whole name, spoken properly. I want to leave holding something someone else gave me."

"Elodic Fairweather. Son of Tam, grandson of Sera. Of Ashvale. Taster to the crown." A pause, and then, gently correcting: "Formerly."

"Taster to the crown," Aldric repeated, tasting it. "Yes. Good. Keep that." His eyes were closing; he let them. "You'll forget everything, they tell me. Your mother's kitchen. Your own name." A breath. The rain was so soft. "But you'll learn it again. That's the difference between us, boy. I'm ending. You're beginning. Beginning is harder." Another breath, thinner. "Do it anyway."

The rain fell on the orchard. Somewhere beyond the wall, Ashvale was waking to a world with no edicts on its doors, and bells were beginning to ring — not alarm bells, not yet, just the mint's hour-bells, ordinary and oblivious, marking time in a city that had finally, at unbearable cost, been given back its future.

Aldric smiled at the leak in the sky, and spent his last coin on the taste of rain, and did not spend it alone.

* * *

They buried him three days later in the orchard, under the climbing tree, without a crown — Rell's first decree as regent: *the crown goes in the crypt, sealed, and no one wears it again.* The second decree ended the hunts permanently. The third summoned the council.

And the fourth, written in Rell's own hand and posted on every door in Ashvale where the edicts had hung, was a single line:

**Let the record show the king died free, and ask yourselves what you were doing while he wasn't.**

In Maren Voss's tower, a blank-eyed man sat by a window holding a knotted cord he did not recognize, while a red-haired girl he did not remember began, for the second time, to introduce herself.
