## 33. Collapse

Maren Voss had spent thirty years learning to read buildings the way other people read letters. Palaces were archives that stood up. Every corridor was a sentence, every door a punctuation mark, every wing a paragraph added by some dead hand with more money than taste. She had walked the under-halls of Ashvale's palace a hundred times in her archivist years, and she had believed — right up until the moment the floor forgot it was a floor — that she understood its grammar.

The first sign was small. A sconce on the crypt stair guttered and burned blue for half a breath, the way candles burn in rooms where someone has just died. Then the stone beneath her palm went warm. Not fire-warm. Skin-warm. The memory-stone of the old palace — quarried from the same seam as the binding-seal her grandmother's ledger described, stone that had soaked up three centuries of footsteps and feasts and funerals — was waking up.

And it was screaming.

She felt it through her boots before she heard it: a low vibration, like a struck bell too large to finish ringing. Above her, muffled by six feet of vaulted rock, the throne room had gone silent in the wrong way. Silence after Elodie spoke the Hollow's name should have been held breath. This silence was a held *wound*.

"Come on," Maren said to herself, because there was no one else to say it to, and took the stairs two at a time with the contingency letter still crushed in her fist.

* * *

The crypt behind her was a ruin of chalk and broken seal-work. She had done what the rite required — cracked the original binding-seal with a hammer and the Hollow's true name spoken backward, an act that had felt like tearing a page out of the world's oldest book — and now the whole architecture of the thing was coming down at once. The seal had not merely bound the Hollow. It had been a bookmark. And bookmarks exist to keep pages from moving.

The pages were moving.

She came up into the service corridor beneath the great hall and stopped so fast her heels skidded on wet stone. The corridor was wrong. Not damaged — *wrong*. The walls were the same pale granite they had always been, but the torch-brackets were iron ones from sixty years ago, replaced in her own lifetime by bronze. A tapestry hung on the left wall that she knew only from inventory ledgers: *The Orchard at Dusk*, lost to moth in the reign of Aldric's grandfather. It hung there whole, colors vivid, smelling faintly of apples that had rotted before Maren was born.

The palace was remembering. All at once. Every version of itself it had ever been, surfacing like drowned bodies.

And somewhere above, something vast and ancient was convulsing, and its death-throes were tearing holes in the memory-stone, and the memories were pouring out sideways into the halls.

"Elodie," she said aloud, to fix the word in the air. "Elodie Fairweather. You are going to walk out of here. I have decided this."

It helped. Slightly.

* * *

She found him forty yards along the corridor, exactly where the hunters' escort had stood when the world broke — except the escort was gone, scattered or fled or perhaps simply *unremembered*, and Elodie stood alone in the middle of the passage with his eyes open and empty.

He looked, Maren thought, like a house after the family has moved out. Everything still standing. Nothing home.

"Elodie." She caught his arm. His skin was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. "Can you hear me? Squeeze my hand if you can hear me."

Nothing. His pupils were enormous, fixed on some middle distance that wasn't in the room. His lips moved once, shaping a word without sound. She leaned close and caught it:

"*—more.*"

More. He'd given the Hollow everything the rite demanded — the kitchen, the tastes, Wren's face, his own name — and either it had taken more than agreed, or he had offered more than required, because that was apparently what Fairweathers did, they *overpaid*, they bound parasites and fed kings and died of generosity—

Later, she told herself. Grieve later. Move now.

She got his arm over her shoulders. He was taller than her by half a head and heavier than he looked, and she was fifty-one years old with a scholar's arms, and it did not matter, because the alternative was leaving him, and she had already done her arithmetic for one lifetime and refused to do more.

They went toward the east stair. It was the nearest way out that Rell's unbarred gate might still cover.

The corridor disagreed.

Halfway to the turning, the wall on their right simply stopped being a wall. There was no crack, no collapse — the granite thinned like fog and became, seamlessly, a summer morning. Maren stumbled to a halt at the edge of a kitchen garden she had no business seeing sixty feet underground: bean rows, a scarecrow wearing a real coat, a woman in an apron kneeling in the dirt with her hands full of green things, humming. Sunlight fell across the flagstones where torchlight had been. The smell of turned earth and rain reached them, impossibly, from the inside of a mountain.

The woman looked up. Her face was ordinary and radiant and long dead, and she smiled at Maren as if Maren were expected.

"Don't look at it," Maren said, to Elodie, to herself, to anyone. "Don't look at it, don't answer it—" Because the ledger had been clear on this, buried in her grandmother's ciphered marginalia in a hand gone shaky with age: *the dying Hollow spends what it holds. What it holds is everyone. Do not accept hospitality from the past.*

She hauled Elodie past the garden's edge. For one step, their feet were on soil instead of stone; she felt the give of it, the damp softness, a whole summer morning pressing up against her soles. Then the vision thinned and they were stamping on granite again, and behind them the woman in the apron was still kneeling in her beans, waiting for someone who would never come back down the path.

Elodie made a sound. Small. Lost.

"I know," Maren said. "I know. Keep walking."

* * *

The east stair was not where the east stair should be.

She knew this within four steps, because archivists count steps the way sailors count waves. The staircase descended when it should have risen. Its treads were worn in a curve that belonged to a different century's traffic. At the landing, a door stood open onto a nursery.

Not a ruined nursery. A living one. A fire in the grate, wooden horses on the floor, a child's cloak hooked by the door, still holding the shape of small shoulders. On the table, bread and honey on a plate, and beside the plate a cup of milk with the skin just forming on top — fresh, minutes-old, warm in a room that had been sealed for a generation.

A little girl sat cross-legged on the rug, arranging the wooden horses in a line. Dark hair. A serious face. Maren knew that face from portraits, from coins, from the official mourning engraving that had hung in every government office for eleven years.

Princess Isolde. Dead at nine. The grief that had hollowed King Aldric long before anything wore his crown.

The girl looked up at them with the terrible patience of the remembered.

"He'll come find me," she said. "He always comes."

Maren's chest did something complicated and painful. She had read the king's private correspondence. She knew exactly who had never come, and why, and how many nights Aldric had spent in this wing before the physicians called it melancholia and locked the doors.

"He can't, sweetheart," Maren heard herself say, which was against every rule she'd just recited, but rules were for archives and this was a child. "Not tonight. Tonight he needs you to be somewhere safe."

The girl considered this with grave, royal weight. Then she picked up one wooden horse and held it out.

"Take him the brown one. So he knows."

Maren took it. She did not trust herself to speak. She put the little horse in her pocket next to the contingency letter, and pulled Elodie past the nursery, and did not look back, because she had a suspicion — scholarly, cold, certain — that the room would keep existing only while someone watched it, and she could not carry that weight and the man both.

Behind them, faintly, the child began to hum.

* * *

The stair let them out into the west gallery, which meant the stair had lied about being the east stair, which meant the palace's internal geography was now negotiable, which meant — Maren forced herself to think it plainly — that they were no longer navigating a building. They were navigating a mind. A vast, poisoned, dying mind, thrashing through everything it had ever eaten, and they were somewhere inside the meal.

The gallery stretched ahead of them, hung with portraits. She recognized the style of the frames before she recognized the faces: these were the kings. All of them. Not the official succession — the *true* one, the pattern she had pieced together from tax rolls and funeral dates and the suspicious longevity of beloved reigns. Beloved, then hollow, then conveniently dead. Generation after generation. The Hollow had ruled Ashvale longer than any dynasty, wearing each king like a coat until the coat wore through.

Every portrait's eyes were closed.

As she watched, dragging Elodie down the runner, the portraits began to open their mouths. No sound came out. But she saw the shapes of the words, and she had spent enough years with ciphered texts to read lips in three dead languages, and every single painted king was saying the same thing:

*Run.*

Or possibly — she corrected herself, chilled — *ruin*. The old pronunciation. The Hollow's name, the true one, the one Elodie had spoken upstairs to freeze a court solid. They were saying their master's name. Or cursing it. With the dead, Maren thought, it was often hard to tell the difference.

The gallery floor bucked.

Not an earthquake — an *edit*. The stone rippled like water with a stone dropped in it, and for one vertiginous second Maren felt the building trying to decide what this hallway had been, and the decision went badly. The runner carpet became grass. The ceiling became sky — real sky, grey and drizzling, Ashvale weather — and then snapped back to vaulted stone so fast her ears popped. Somewhere below them, deep in the foundations, the memory-stone groaned like a ship changing course.

Elodie sagged. She caught him before his knees hit marble, and his weight nearly took them both down.

"Stay with me." She slapped his cheek, gently, then less gently. "You gave it your memories. Fine. That was the price, we knew the price. But you're still *here*, and here is where I am, and I am extremely difficult to abandon, ask anyone at the university."

His eyes drifted toward her. Focused, briefly, on her face. His mouth worked.

"You're—" he managed. His voice sounded sanded down, all the grain removed. "Do I... know you?"

The question went through her like a splinter entering at the worst possible angle.

"Yes," she said. "Yes. I'm Maren. I'm the one who dragged you into this, which makes me the one who gets to drag you out. That's how debts work. Come on."

* * *

They were three turns from the gate when the corridors began opening onto childhoods.

That was the only way she could describe it. Doors that had been plain oak stood ajar, and through each gap poured a life. Through one: a fish-smelling kitchen, a man's laugh, a girl of maybe seven chasing a cat between the tables — and Maren realized with a lurch that she was looking at Wren's father's house, at a morning twenty years gone, at the exact warmth that the man had sold away for silver when he traded his daughter's friend to the witch-hunters. Through another door: a midwife's hands, steady, cradling something new. Through a third: a boy whistling, and wind answering, and a rooftop under a wide sky.

The Hollow, dying, was vomiting up everything it had ever harvested. Three centuries of stolen mornings, leaking into the halls of the palace that had housed it.

And the palace was drinking them back in. Walls thickened with borrowed summers. Staircases steepened with old grief. The building was reconstituting itself out of its own contents, and the architecture was becoming *opinionated* — a corridor that had led east now curved toward some memory it preferred, a doorway that had opened outward now opened inward, onto a feast hall crowded with translucent guests raising cups to a king whose face kept sliding between dynasties.

Maren did the only sensible thing an archivist could do in a collapsing archive.

She followed the catalog.

Her grandmother's ledger — copied in her own fair hand, folded against her ribs — described the binding-seal's location relative to the oldest features of the palace: the well-shaft, the ash-vent, the mint's original foundry wall. Modern wings had grown around those bones like scar tissue, but bones persist. If she could reach the foundry wall, the foundry wall would lead to the vent, and the vent opened onto the Mint courtyard, and the Mint courtyard was forty paces from Rell's unbarred gate.

Assuming the gate still existed. Assuming Rell still existed. Assuming anything outside this thrashing mind still existed in a form she could trust.

One problem at a time. Archivists survive by solving the page in front of them.

"This way," she said, and turned against the corridor's preference, and the stone hated her for it. She felt the resistance like wading upstream. The walls pressed closer. The air thickened with the smell of beeswax and incense — coronation smells, the palace trying to dress itself in ceremony, to make her turn around and join the feast and sit down among the translucent guests and become part of the collection—

"No," Maren said, with thirty years of telling petitioners *no* behind the word, and the corridor flinched.

Even a dying god, she thought wildly, recognizes a bureaucrat.

* * *

The foundry wall saved them. It was the one thing in the palace that refused to change — blackened brick older than any dynasty, soaked in three hundred years of forge-fire, too practical and too stubborn to hold memories worth eating. Against its flat, dumb, honest surface, the unraveling seemed to lose traction. The air cleared. The whispers faded to a mutter.

Elodie stirred against her shoulder.

"The well," he said. Clear. Certain. His eyes were still wrong — unfocused, a man reading something very far away — but his voice had found its footing. "Grandmother's notes. The well-shaft runs beside the vent. Cold air rises. You can follow it by feel."

"You remember her notes?"

"I remember..." His brow furrowed, a man patting his pockets for something already spent. "I remember that I knew. Does that count?"

"It counts," Maren said, and meant it more than he could possibly understand. "In my profession, knowing where the knowledge lives counts for almost everything."

They found the shaft by feel, exactly as he said — cold air breathing up out of the dark like the palace exhaling. The vent passage beyond was narrow, brick-lined, and mercifully *boring*: no visions, no doors, no dead princesses. Just brick and draft and the two of them, bent double, shuffling toward daylight.

Twice, the passage tried to become something else. Once the bricks went smooth and white and Maren was momentarily in a birthing chamber, lamplit, a woman panting, something cold moving in the corner of the room — she shut her eyes and counted bricks and it passed. Once the draft carried voices, dozens of them, whispering the Hollow's name in the old pronunciation, *ruin, ruin,* and Elodie whispered it back, once, involuntarily, like an echo given flesh, and Maren clamped her hand over his mouth and kept moving and did not let herself think about what it meant that the name still lived in him somewhere, spent or not.

Then: grey light. Grating overhead. The Mint courtyard, real and raining and gloriously ordinary, and beyond it — she pressed her eye to the rusted lattice — the postern gate, standing open, unbarred, exactly as a good soldier's treason had promised.

Rell was there. Standing in the rain beside the open gate with his sword drawn and his men nowhere in evidence, watching the palace with the expression of a man guarding an exit from a burning library.

Maren shoved the grating up and dragged Elodie out into the rain, and the three of them stood together in the courtyard, gasping, while behind them the palace shuddered like a sleeper waking from a nightmare — and then shuddered again, harder, and kept shuddering, and did not stop.

Windows lit and went dark and lit again, flickering through centuries. A tower's roofline blurred, sharpened, blurred. High in the royal wing, every bell in the bell tower began to ring at once — bells that hadn't rung in living memory, bells whose ropes had rotted — tolling out some hour that no clock in Ashvale would confirm.

"What's happening to it?" Rell asked. Rain ran off his chin. He didn't look at her. He was looking at the king's windows, high up, where a light burned steady and alone.

"It's forgetting," Maren said. "All of it. Everything it ever ate, coming back up. The palace was built on it, around it, out of it. When the parasite dies, the host—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "When the *king* dies, we may find out how much of this city was ever load-bearing."

Elodie swayed. She caught him. His eyes had cleared a fraction — enough to focus on her face, enough to frown, the frown of a polite stranger trying to place an acquaintance.

"I'm sorry," he said carefully. "Have we met?"

The question landed harder than any blow the Hollow had managed. Maren held his gaze and kept her voice level, because level was the only register she trusted herself with.

"We've met," she said. "We're going to meet again tomorrow, and the day after, and every day after that until you know my name and your own and what you paid. I keep records, Elodie Fairweather. Nothing you gave away stays lost. Not while I'm alive to file it."

Something moved behind his eyes — not recognition, but the *shape* of recognition, a hook waiting for a coat. He nodded slowly.

Above them, the bells stopped ringing all at once, mid-peal, as if the tower had forgotten what bells were for.

And in the sudden silence, from the direction of the throne room, faint but unmistakable, rising through six feet of stone and a century of secrets, came the sound of a man weeping — ragged, human, alive — and Maren knew, with the certainty of someone who had spent her life reading the last entries in other people's books, that the king of Ashvale was awake.

She turned to Rell, rain streaming down her face, the little wooden horse a small hard weight in her pocket.

"How long," she said, "do we have?"
