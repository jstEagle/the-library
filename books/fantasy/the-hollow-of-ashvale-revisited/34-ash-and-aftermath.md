## 34. Ash and Aftermath

Bettina had not slept. Nobody in Ashvale had slept, though the reasons scattered like spilled grain — some awake with grief, some with fear, some with a kind of terrible arithmetic they were doing behind their eyes, counting up names.

She stood at her window on Tannery Row and watched the sky do what it always did, indifferent to kings. It went grey at the edges first, then bruise-pink over the Mint, then that pale washed gold that meant the sun was coming whether the city wanted it or not.

The bells had stopped an hour before dawn. That was how you knew something was truly wrong in Ashvale: not bells ringing, but bells stopping. All of them at once, mid-peal, as if the whole city's voice had been cut off with one hand. Then the criers had gone out into the dark streets with torches and official paper, and their voices had carried down every lane:

*The King is dead. The King is dead. The hunts are ended. The hunts are ended.*

Bettina had heard it twice from her window and still hadn't believed it the second time either.

Behind her, in the back room where Bettina had hidden a stranger for eleven days, the midwife sat on the floor with her back against the bedframe, holding a cup of cold barley tea she hadn't drunk. Her name was Odile. Her hands were steady — birthing hands, always steady — but her face had gone somewhere else entirely.

"They'll want my testimony now," Odile said. "Won't they."

"They will," Bettina said.

"And if I give it, they'll know who hid me. They'll come asking you questions, and your name is already—" She stopped. Everyone in Ashvale had learned to stop sentences like that, the way you'd step around a hole in the road.

"My name is already mud," Bettina finished for her. "I burned my own charms in the street in front of God and the neighbors, Odile. Half this row watched me do it. The other half reported someone else while I was doing it." She turned from the window. "My name has nowhere left to fall."

* * *

By full light, the market square was full of people who didn't seem to know why they'd come.

That was the thing about Ashvale that morning — everyone came out. Bakers who should have been at their ovens. Fishwives. The lamplighters walking their rounds in daylight out of pure habit. They gathered in the square the way water gathers in a low place, without deciding to, and they stood among the stalls and the shuttered charm-booths and looked at each other as if checking, one by one, whether the person across the way was the one who had denounced them.

Because here was what the criers' paper had also said, in smaller ink, in the language of bureaucracy that somehow made it worse: *All denunciations under the Edict are hereby void. All persons denounced are freed. A general accounting shall follow.*

*A general accounting.* Bettina heard the phrase pass through the crowd in a dozen mouths by midmorning, and each time it landed differently. From some lips it sounded like justice. From others, like a blade being unsheathed slowly so everyone could watch.

Old Halvard the cooper stood near the well with his arms folded, and people gave him a wider berth than usual, because everyone knew Halvard had named three neighbors in October for thirty silver pieces, and everyone knew it the way you know which houses have sickness in them. He wasn't running. That was the strange part. He just stood there, red-faced, letting himself be seen, and once when a young woman spat at his feet he only looked down at the spit and said, "Aye," like a man agreeing to terms.

Bettina moved through the square with her basket on her arm, buying things she didn't need, listening.

They talked about the king. That was the other strangeness — the grief was real. Whatever Aldric had become in his last years, whatever hollow-eyed thing had sat the throne and forgotten his own daughter's name, the city had loved him once, and love like that doesn't drain out of a place all at once. It pools. An old woman at the cheese stall wept openly and wasn't ashamed of it. Two men argued in low voices about whether the last hunts had even been the king's will or the Lord Protector's, and neither could prove anything, and both needed to say it anyway.

And underneath the grief, everywhere, like groundwater: the pyres.

There had been four of them. Bettina made herself walk past the largest one on her way home, the one in Chandler's Field where they'd burned the midwife's sister and a tanner's son and a hedge-witch whose name most people hadn't bothered to learn until after. The ash hadn't been cleared. It lay in a grey drift against the base of the stake, and the rain had packed it into a kind of crust, and children — this was the part Bettina would remember longest — children had already begun making marks in it. One had drawn a bird. Another had traced letters, badly, maybe learning them, maybe spelling something no adult would have dared.

You couldn't look away from it. That was the sentence the whole city had woken up inside: *you cannot look away anymore.* For two years the fires had happened at the edge of vision, officially regretted, privately approved, everyone's shame arranged just so. Now the edict was ash itself and there was nothing between any citizen and the thing they had permitted. The informers knew it. The watchers who had said nothing knew it. The ones who had brought bread to the families of the accused, too quietly, too late, knew it best of all.

Bettina stood at the edge of the burnt field for a long time with her empty basket, and the wind came off the field carrying that smell — old smoke, wet char, something mineral underneath that she refused to name — and she thought about the night she had fed her own charms into a brazier on this very square while her hands shook and her heart did not break, exactly, but bent. Bent the way a beam bends before it decides.

"I'm done bending," she said aloud, to nobody, and a passing fishwife gave her a look and walked faster.

* * *

Odile gave her testimony at noon.

Bettina went with her, because Odile's hands shook for the first time since Bettina had known her, and because somebody had to open the door of the archive-house and walk in first, and it might as well be a woman whose name was already ruined.

The new authority received them in what had been the hunters' intake office — same desks, same iron-banded door, different faces. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs took down everything: the birthing chambers, the cold presence that attended certain births, the way newborns sometimes came into the world already *emptied*, eyes like windows with the shutters painted on, and how those children never quite took to the breast, never quite cried right, and how the palace physicians always came within a day, always took notes, always paid quietly and left.

"The crown was harvesting them," Odile said, and her voice held. "Memory. Whatever it was — it drank the beginnings of lives. I attended forty-one such births over nine years. I counted." She laid a scrap of paper on the desk with forty-one small tally-marks scratched into it. "I kept count because keeping count felt like fighting. I want it written down that I thought keeping count was fighting, and I want it written down that it wasn't enough."

The clerk wrote it down. Word for word. Bettina watched his pen move and thought: *this is what repair looks like at the start. Not grand. Just a man writing down true things instead of false ones, because someone finally made him.*

When they came out, the light had changed — lower, warmer, the long amber of late afternoon — and word had spread through the queue outside the archive-house that testimony was being taken from *the accused's side* now, and a woman Bettina didn't know grabbed Odile's sleeve and asked, half-weeping, whether they would take accounts from families of the burned, and a man behind her said they'd better, because he had spent two years knowing who denounced his brother and swallowing it, and the line behind them, forty deep, murmured its assent like a tide coming in.

Bettina left Odile there, surrounded, steadied by the sheer weight of the waiting witnesses, and walked home the long way. Past the bakery where the edict had been nailed. The nail holes were still in the door. Someone had filled them with wax — white wax, neatly, like a small anonymous act of surgery.

* * *

Evening came, and with it the decision she'd been carrying all day like a stone in her apron pocket.

Her floorboards still hid nothing; she'd sold or destroyed or given away everything burnable two years ago, the night of the edict, the night of the brazier and the shaking hands. But there was one thing she'd never made. Not hidden — *never made*. A charm she had designed in her head during the worst winter, when the hunts were at their height and Odile was sleeping on her floor and the whole city was holding its breath: a mourning-charm. Not luck, not protection, nothing so greedy. A charm for the dead who died wrong — unshriven, unnamed, ash-scattered. Old craft, older than the crown, the kind her grandmother had called *witness-work*: it held a name and spoke it back to the world once a season, so the dead stayed counted.

She had designed it and never built it, because building it was death, and she'd had Odile to think of, and a roof to keep.

Now the edict was void. Now the bells were silent and the clerk was writing true things. Now Halvard stood in the square taking his spitting like terms agreed, and the children were drawing birds in the ash.

Bettina worked by lamplight at her kitchen table. Willow twig, cut at dusk. Thread pulled from the hem of a garment belonging to the living — she used her own shawl, because she was alive and present and it was honest. And the name, which was the hard part, because there were too many names and one charm holds one.

She chose the hedge-witch from Chandler's Field. Sera Vane. Burned fourteen months ago for selling fever-knots to dockworkers. Bettina had bought a fever-knot from her once, years back, and Sera had laughed at her own prices and given her a sprig of dried mint besides, free, "for the tea, love, you look tired." Nobody had spoken Sera Vane's name in the square since the burning except to describe the crime. The crime had eaten the name. Witness-work existed precisely for that.

She bound the twig at midnight with seven turns and spoke the name into it the old way — not whispered, not muttered, but *spoken*, at speaking volume, in a kitchen with the shutter open, in a city where for two years that sound alone had been a death warrant.

"Sera Vane. Of Chandler's Field. Maker of fever-knots. Remembered."

The charm took. It was a small thing — a faint warmth in the twig, a smell of mint that had no business being there, the sense of a listener opening on the far side of the world. Bettina sat back and looked at it lying in her palm, this forbidden little engine of remembrance, and found she was crying, quietly, without drama, the way rain starts.

Not for Sera only. For the beam in herself, straightening. For the two years of ash she'd helped sweep under the city's collective rug with her little street-fire of self-betrayal. For Odile's forty-one tallies. For the children drawing birds.

* * *

She planted it at first light.

Not hid. Not tucked in a doorway or buried at a crossroads, the way charm-work had lived in Ashvale for two years — underground, under-floorboard, under-tongue. Planted. She dug a hole in the open ground of the market square, right beside the fountain, ten feet from the spot where she'd burned her stock, and she set the willow twig in it like a cutting, and she packed the earth with her bare hands, and she stood up and brushed off her knees.

People saw. That was the entire point, and it nearly undid her — the seeing. A dozen faces turned toward her. Halvard was there, and the fishwife from the morning, and a warden in a grey cloak who had no orders anymore and didn't seem to know what his face should do.

"That's charm-craft," someone said. Not accusing. Just establishing it, the way you'd announce rain.

"It is," Bettina said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "It's for Sera Vane, who was burned on the fourth of Thaw-month, and it will speak her name to whoever passes, once a season, for as long as it stands. If anyone objects," and here she looked directly at Halvard, and he held her gaze, and neither blinked, "they can dig it up themselves. In daylight. With their name attached."

Nobody moved. The warden looked at the twig, then at Bettina, then at the crowd, and did the bravest cowardly thing available to him, which was nothing. He walked on. And the square exhaled — not relief exactly, more like the sound a house makes when a long illness ends and the family finally sits down.

An old man Bettina half-recognized came forward and stood looking at the little mound of earth. "My wife knew her," he said. "Sera Vane. Fixed a cramp-charm for my wife's hands, wouldn't take coin." He crouched, awkward, joints cracking, and pressed one palm flat to the soil over the twig, briefly, like a man touching a grave or a blessing. "Good," he said, to the charm or the dead or the morning. "Good."

By noon there were six more mounds in the square. By evening, nineteen. People Bettina had never seen at any gathering came with twigs and thread and names, some of them weeping, some grim, one boy — the tanner's dead son's younger brother — planting his with the furious precision of a child building a fort. No wardens came. Somewhere in the palace, presumably, men in fine rooms were deciding what the new law was going to be, but the law arrived in the square first, and it arrived as a row of little mounds with names in them.

Bettina stood in her doorway at dusk, watching candle-flames begin to appear at the corners of the square where folk had lit them for the dead — ordinary candles, unpermitted for two years, suddenly just candles — and Odile came to stand beside her, smelling of soap and other people's births, restored to the world by paperwork and a friend's stubborn floor.

"You know what happens next," Odile said. "The accounting. Names. There will be ugliness. Forgiveness isn't owed just because the fires stopped."

"I know."

"And you know they're saying there's a man behind it. The one who unbound the king. A taster, they're saying. Fairweather, some name like that. They say he emptied himself doing it — that he barely knows his own face now." Odile shook her head slowly. "Emptied himself to fill the city back up. There's a story for you."

"A story worth a charm," Bettina said, and they stood together in the first true dusk of the new Ashvale, above nineteen mounds and rising, while somewhere across the city — though neither woman knew it yet, though the whole square would learn it within days — a man was waking in a tower with nothing in his hands but a knotted cord and no idea whose warmth it held.
