## 35. The Man Who Tastes Nothing

The first thing Elodie Fairweather knew was the taste of nothing.

Not the absence of flavor — he had known that before, in sickness, in grief, in the gray mornings after his mother died when bread turned to paste on his tongue. This was different. This was a door painted shut where a mouth should be. He lay still beneath a blanket that smelled of dust and lamp oil and tried to remember what dust was supposed to smell like, and could not, and understood with a calm that frightened him more than panic would have that something enormous had been taken.

He opened his eyes.

A ceiling. Beams of dark wood, crossed like the ribs of some patient animal. A window high in one wall, letting down a column of gray light in which motes drifted — and even *motes* felt like a word he was borrowing from someone else's vocabulary. He knew what light was. He knew what a window was. He knew, distantly, the way you know the shape of a country you've never visited, that these things had names and uses and histories. He simply couldn't find himself anywhere among them.

He sat up. His body worked. That was its own small miracle — every joint reported for duty, every muscle obeyed, as though the flesh had been kept running by some diligent clerk who hadn't been told about the fire upstairs. He was thin. Thinner than his hands suggested he should be, if hands could suggest anything, which apparently they could, because when he looked at them they seemed to say: *you were a worker once. You used these.*

There was a knot in his right fist.

He hadn't noticed it until now because his fingers had been closed around it since — since whenever since was. He pried them open one at a time. In his palm lay a tangle of cord, knotted in patterns too deliberate to be accident, too worn to be new. It was soft with handling. Someone had carried this thing a long time, or tied it and held it through something terrible, and the cord was threaded through with a single strand of hair, pale brown, catching the window-light.

Elodie stared at it and felt — not memory. Not quite. Something older than memory and simpler. Warmth. The way a stone holds the sun after sundown. Whoever had put this knot in his hand had loved him. That fact arrived whole, unaccompanied by any face, any name, any scene. Just warmth, and underneath it an instruction his body understood before his mind did:

*Hold on to this.*

So he held on to it.

* * *

The door opened sometime later — he had no way to measure how long; time in this room moved like syrup — and a woman came in carrying a tray.

She was perhaps fifty, sharp-faced, ink-stained to the elbow, with the particular posture of someone who has spent decades bending over desks and resents every degree of it. She stopped when she saw him sitting up. Her expression did something complicated — relief and grief and fear all trying to occupy the same face at once — and then she set it aside, deliberately, the way a person closes a ledger.

"You're awake," she said.

"I appear to be." His voice was rough from disuse. "I don't know who you are."

Something flickered behind her eyes. She had prepared for this, clearly. He could see the preparation sitting on her like armor. "My name is Maren Voss. I'm — I was the court archivist. I'm the reason you're alive."

"Was I dying?"

"You were being eaten." She set the tray on the table beside the bed. Bread, cheese, an apple cut into slices, water. Ordinary food arranged with unusual care, like an offering. "Do you know your name?"

"Elodie." It came without thought, which surprised him. "Elodie Fairweather. But I don't know why I know that, or what a Fairweather is, or—" He stopped. Started again. "It's like a house where all the furniture's been sold but the nameplate is still on the door."

Maren nodded slowly. "That's better than we hoped. When they carried you out of the palace you didn't know anything. You didn't know how to swallow properly. You looked at your own hands like they belonged to a stranger." She pulled the room's only other chair closer and sat, elbows on knees, studying him with the intensity of a woman reading a difficult manuscript. "What do you remember? Anything. Tell me everything, even if it's nothing."

He searched. It was like fishing in a river that had been dammed upstream — there was water, but nothing moving in it. "Warmth," he said finally. "This knot. Someone loved me. That's all. Everything else is — summaries. I know kings exist. I know what poison is. I know bread is food. But I don't know *bread*." He picked up a slice of apple and held it, not eating. "I was a taster, wasn't I. I can feel it in my hands somehow. They want to hold a spoon."

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "You were the king's taster. Three years."

"And I gave something away. Didn't I." It wasn't a question. He could feel the shape of the absence — not just missing memories but missing *weight*, the way a wall shows where a painting hung. "Whatever was in me, I fed it everything."

"You saved a king," Maren said. "And a city. And possibly more than a city. And it cost—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "It cost what it cost. I won't lie to you about the arithmetic. Most of it is gone. Your mother. Your childhood. Three years of tastes — and you had a gift, Elodie, you tasted *memory*, you read intention in ink and history in wine, and that entire library burned to feed the thing under the palace." Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she mastered it. "But it didn't take everything. It couldn't. Because someone anchored you."

He looked down at the knot in his palm.

"Wren," Maren said. "Her name is Wren. She's a fisherman's daughter from the Saltrow market. Her father denounced you to the hunters, and you forgave her for it, which I'm told cost more than anger would have. She tied her own hair into that knot and put it in your hand before the rite. It held a thread of you outside the Hollow's appetite. Without it, you'd be a body walking around with nobody home." Maren's eyes were dry but her voice wasn't quite steady. "She's been coming to this tower every day for nine days. Sitting in the kitchen below. Waiting for you to wake up. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Elodie looked at the knot for a long time.

"No," he said honestly. "I don't understand it. I don't remember her. I don't remember forgiving anyone, or being denounced, or any of it. But I can feel—" He pressed the cord against his sternum. "There's a warm place here. Like a coal that wouldn't go out. Is that her?"

"That's her."

"Then I'd like to meet her," Elodie said. "Properly. As a stranger does. Because strangers get to begin again, and I seem to have very little else." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Also, whoever she is, she has excellent taste in knots."

* * *

The bread was the worst of it.

Maren left him alone with the tray, and he ate because his body insisted, and the bread told him nothing. He chewed and chewed and it was texture without meaning, fuel without pleasure, and halfway through the slice he had to stop and put his forehead down on the table because the grief arrived all at once, sideways, from a part of him that remembered having been someone.

Because his hands remembered. That was the cruel arithmetic of it. His hands rose to catch a falling cup before his mind knew the cup was falling. His tongue catalogued the cheese — *goat, aged, ash-rind, slightly past its best* — and delivered the report to a self that no longer existed to receive it. Somewhere in him was a man who had made a life out of taste, who had built a whole secret self out of flavors and charms and the quiet competence of knowing things, and that man had walked into a dark room under the palace and paid himself out coin by coin until nothing was left but the purse.

Elodie sat in the gray light and wept for a stranger, which is to say for himself, which is to say he wept without quite knowing for whom, and when it passed he found the knot still in his hand, warm as ever, patient as ever.

*Someone loved him enough to anchor him.*

He turned that over. Of all the facts available to him — the dead king, the broken city, the burned hunts, the parasite wearing a crown — this was the one his mind kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. Not *what did I lose*. But *someone thought I was worth keeping*. Out of everything the rite might have taken, it had not taken that. It could not. It was stored somewhere the Hollow's appetite couldn't reach, in braided cord and a stranger's hair, and it was his, and it was warm.

He got up. His legs held. He crossed to the window and looked out over Ashvale for the first time.

The city spread below the tower in the flat gray-gold light of late morning, and it was scarred — he could see that much even without knowing what he was seeing. Empty lots where buildings should have been. Scorch-marks on rooftops like bruises. A square in the middle distance where a crowd had gathered around something — a scaffold? a pyre? no, neither; as he watched, people were raising something, a post, a pole, planting it in the ground like a tree — and though he had no memory of the edicts or the burnings, his chest tightened anyway, some inherited sorrow in the blood doing the remembering for him.

"There you are."

He turned. Maren stood in the doorway again, and behind her, half-hidden, was a girl.

She was young — nineteen, twenty — with salt-stiff hair and the wind-chapped look of someone who lived her life outdoors, and she was gripping the doorframe as though the floor might otherwise slide out from under her. Her eyes went to him and then to his hand, to the knot, and something in her face broke and reassembled itself all in the space of a breath.

"Hi," she said. Her voice wobbled and steadied. "You probably don't— I mean. Maren said. I'm Wren."

"I know," Elodie said. "I mean — I know your name. I know you gave me this." He lifted the knot. "I don't remember you. I want to be honest about that, because everyone keeps being kind to me and honesty seems like the one payment I've still got."

Wren laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, half sob. "Honest. Yeah. You always were." She came into the room slowly, like approaching a bird that might startle. "Can I—"

"Please."

She took his hand, the one holding the knot, and closed both of hers around it, around his fingers, around the warm thread between them. Her hands were cold and rough and real. "You don't have to remember me," she said. "That's the whole point of the knot. It remembers for both of us until you're ready. We'll just — start over. If you want. I can tell you everything. It'll take a while. There's a lot."

"How much is a lot?"

"You sold my father a luck-knot two years ago and told him it would keep his nets full, and it did, and then he sold your name to the hunters for twelve silver pieces, and you forgave me for it, and then you went under a palace and gave away your whole life so a king could die as himself." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "That's the short version. There's also bread. There's a lot of bread in the long version."

"Bread," Elodie repeated. The word landed strangely, heavily. "They tell me I knew bread. Really knew it."

"You taught me to bake it," Wren said. "You said flour remembers the field it came from, and if you're quiet enough, it'll tell you." She smiled through the tears now, helpless with them. "I wrote everything down. Everything you ever said to me. I filled three notebooks. Maren says between her letters and my notebooks we can build you back, piece by piece, however long it takes."

Elodie looked at her — this stranger with her cold hands and her three notebooks and her hair tied around his heart — and felt the warm thread pull taut, and understood suddenly that loss and survival were not opposites. They were the same ledger. Every page torn out made the remaining pages matter more. He had lost nearly everything, and what remained — this knot, this girl, this ink-stained archivist downstairs, this body that still caught falling cups — what remained was not the *rest* of a life.

It was the beginning of one.

"Then we'd better start," he said. "Tell me about the bread first."

* * *

That evening, Maren found them in the tower kitchen — Wren talking, Elodie listening with the ferocious attention of a man drinking after a drought — and she stood in the doorway a moment, unseen, watching the taster turn a page of Wren's notebook toward the light.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would bring the first letter. Tomorrow the slow reconstruction would begin in earnest, page by page, name by name. But tonight she let them be.

Elodie looked up and caught her there. "Maren. The city — that square below. What are they building?"

Maren followed his gaze to the distant square, where the new-planted pole stood bare against the darkening sky, and her face went carefully neutral.

"Not a pyre," she said. "A maypole, they're calling it. The first charm-post in Ashvale in a hundred years. Bettina's idea." She paused. "Though if you're asking whether the city's done reckoning with what it burned — no. It isn't. And tomorrow, so are you. There's something you should see at dawn. Both of you."

"What is it?" Wren asked.

Maren's hand rested briefly on the doorframe. "The trials start tomorrow," she said. "And the first name on the docket is the man who denounced you."
