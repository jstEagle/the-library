## 36. Relearning Bread

The first thing Elodie learned about his new life was that it smelled of dust and lamp oil and someone else's cooking.

Maren's tower smelled like all three at once, layered over years, and none of it meant anything to him. He stood in the doorway of the room she'd given him — narrow bed, one window facing the harbor, a shelf of blank ledgers she'd left there like an apology — and tried to feel something. Home. Safety. Gratitude. The words were there. The feelings came late, if they came at all, arriving like letters forwarded from an address he no longer lived at.

"You don't have to stay," Maren said from the stairs. She had a way of standing with her weight on one hip, arms crossed, as if bracing against whatever he might say next. "There are rooms at the sanctuary. Bettina offered."

"I know." He touched the doorframe. Smooth wood, worn at the corner where a hundred hands before his had done the same. "I want to remember why I'd choose this place. That's not the same as choosing it blind."

She considered that for a long moment. "That's the most you thing you've said since you woke up."

"I wouldn't know."

"No," she said quietly. "You wouldn't."

* * *

Wren came every morning.

He learned the rhythm of her before he learned her face: the knock, two soft raps, always the same; the creak of the third stair; the smell of salt and tar that entered the room a half-second before she did. His body knew her. It leaned toward the door like a plant toward light, some root-system of habit surviving in him even when every leaf had been stripped.

"Good morning," she'd say. "I'm Wren."

"I know," he'd say. "You told me yesterday."

"I know I told you yesterday." She'd sit on the window seat, hands folded, patient as tide. "Tell me what you remember."

And he would tell her. Not much. A girl in a market, laughing at something he'd said about luck-knots. A hand pressing something into his palm through a cell door — no, that was wrong, that was later, that was — he'd stop, frustrated, and she'd wait him out.

"The knot," he said one morning, holding it up. He kept it on a cord around his neck now. It was the only object in the tower that felt warm. "I know you made this. I know it matters. I can't remember making it matter."

"That's all right."

"It isn't."

"It is," she said, with a firmness that surprised them both. "Elodie. You're trying to drink the sea because you're thirsty. Take it cup by cup. Today you remember my name without being told. Yesterday you remembered that I hate eel pie. Tomorrow we'll find something else. That's how a person gets built. Everyone does it this way. You just have to watch yours happen."

He looked at her — this stranger with kind eyes who grieved him so carefully, who carried her guilt like ballast — and felt the first true thing since waking: he trusted her. He didn't remember why. He trusted her anyway, the way you trust a rope bridge you've crossed a thousand times in the dark.

"All right," he said. "Cup by cup."

* * *

The bread happened on a Tuesday.

Maren had taken to leaving food outside his door each morning — she was still writing her letters, still rebuilding him from her notes, and feeding him was apparently part of the architecture. This particular morning it was a brown loaf, still warm, from the bakery on Cooper's Lane. The bakery whose door had once held an edict nailed to it. He didn't remember that. Maren had told him. The knowledge sat in him like a stone in a shoe.

He tore off a piece. Put it in his mouth.

And stopped.

It wasn't flavor, exactly. It was everything under flavor — the wheat's whole argument with the fire, the sour ghost of the starter, the baker's heavy hand with the salt, the hour of the morning baked into the crust. Three years of tasting had been burned out of him, but the tongue, apparently, remembers the way a riverbed remembers water. For one long moment he sat at Maren's scarred table with his eyes closed and tasted bread the way a man tastes bread who has never tasted anything else, because he hadn't. Not this man. Not this self.

When he opened his eyes, Wren was watching him from the doorway, and she was crying without any noise about it.

"What?" he said. "What did I do?"

"Nothing." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, furious at herself. "Nothing. You used to make that exact face. Every time. Bread, wine, stew — you'd close your eyes like the food was telling you a secret." She laughed, wet and broken. "I thought I'd never see it again."

He held out the loaf. An offering, or maybe a request. "Sit down. Tell me what I'm tasting."

So she did. She told him about the baker, Tam, who sang off-key and gave day-old loaves to the dock kids. She told him Elodie used to trade him charms for first pick. She told him about the market in autumn, the fish stalls, the bell that rang from the mint at noon. And Elodie ate his bread and listened to a stranger describe his life, and found — this was the strange part, the part he turned over for days after — that he wasn't sad. Or not only sad. There was something underneath the grief that felt almost like greed. He wanted all of it. Every detail. He wanted to be stuffed full of himself again.

"Cup by cup," he said.

"Shut up and eat your bread," said Wren, smiling through it.

* * *

Maren's letters arrived in installments.

She had written them during the worst weeks — contingency letters, she called them, though he noticed she never said contingency *for what*. Now she read them to him each evening in the tower's archive room, surrounded by shelves of Fairweather ledgers going back four generations, and afterward she'd hand him the page and let him keep whatever stuck.

"This is from the night before we went under the palace," she said. "'If you are hearing this, Elodie, then either the rite worked or it didn't, and either way you are not the man who walked into that throne room. I want you to know that man was better than he believed himself to be. He sold forbidden charms to people who couldn't afford to be caught, because decency was the one luxury he refused to give up. He forgave a girl who betrayed him, which cost more than anger would have. He offered everything he loved to a monster so a king could die as himself. If you cannot remember being brave, remember that you were. The record says so. I wrote it down.'"

Elodie sat with that. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, Ashvale went about its evening — cart wheels, gulls, a child shouting about something joyfully.

"You make me sound like a saint."

"I make you sound like yourself. You'll notice those aren't the same thing." She passed him the page. Her handwriting was small and fierce, ink pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. "Read it back to me tomorrow. Whatever survives the night, that's yours. That's how memory works anyway — everyone keeps only what they retell. We're just doing it deliberately."

"And if nothing survives?"

"Then I'll read it again. And again. As many times as it takes." She closed the ledger in front of her — his grandmother's, the ciphered one, its binding-notes half decoded. "Your grandmother lost everything, Elodie. Completely. No letters, no anchors, no one to read her back to herself. You have me. You have the girl with the knot. You have a city full of people who owe you their king's last lucid hour." Her voice roughened. "You are not going to vanish. I forbid it. I am an archivist. Vanishing things are my enemy."

Despite everything, he laughed. It startled him — the laugh coming out of a body he was still learning to live in — and Maren looked so pleased with herself that he laughed again.

* * *

Some days were bad.

He'd reach for a memory the way you reach for a stair in the dark and find nothing there, and the falling feeling would take him — standing in his own room, looking at his own hands, knowing facts about himself the way you know facts about a country you've never visited. On those days Wren found him doing small tasks with terrible concentration. Folding linens. Sorting Maren's pens by darkness of ink. Once, alphabetizing a spice rack that had never been alphabetized, because his hands wanted order even when his head couldn't provide it.

"Tell me something true," he said to her on one of those days, not looking up from the pens.

"You're holding the good pen. The one you bought me for my birthday — no. Sorry. The one you were going to—" She stopped, breathed. Started over. "True thing. My father denounced you. I haven't forgiven him. I don't know if I will. But I come here every morning because you matter more than my anger does, and I need you to hear that on the days you can't hear anything else."

He set down the pen. "Another true thing."

"You taste bread like it's confessing to you. And when you laughed last week at Maren's terrible joke about cataloguing systems, I thought — there he is. There's some of him. He's still in there, growing back like grass through ash."

Elodie looked at his hands. Poison-eater's hands, taster's hands, a stranger's hands. But they had folded this linen. They had tied knots. They had, according to the record, done brave things.

"Grass through ash," he repeated. "Did I say that? It sounds like me."

"You say worse. You'll see."

* * *

A month in, he went back to the market.

Wren walked beside him the whole way, and he was grateful, because Ashvale hit him like weather — the noise, the crush, the smells stacking on smells until his newly-empty head swam. But under the overwhelm, something else stirred. Stall-keepers nodded to him. The fishwife pressed a smoked herring on him and refused payment, saying, "For the taster," in a voice that expected no thanks. Children stared and whispered, and one small girl ran up, pressed a wildflower into his hand, and fled.

"They know me," he said, dazed.

"They know *of* you." Wren steered him around a cart. "Half these stories are wrong. You'll enjoy correcting them."

At the bakery on Cooper's Lane, Tam the baker saw him coming and went very still. Then he pulled a loaf from the rack — the first of the morning, the best one — and set it on the counter without a word.

Elodie broke it open. Steam rose. He tasted it: the wheat, the fire, the salt, the morning.

"There he is," Tam said gruffly, and turned away to hide his face.

* * *

That evening, Maren put a fresh ledger in front of him. Blank pages. New binding. On the cover, in her hand: *Fairweather, E. — Vol. II.*

"What's this?"

"You've been living in Volume One," she said. "Everyone has. Your grandmother, the Hollow, the palace, the sacrifice — that's all finished business, and I'll keep reading it to you until it takes root. But this—" she tapped the cover "—this is the book nobody's written yet. The one where a man learns his own name from the outside. I want entries. Small ones. What bread tastes like. What Wren's laugh sounds like the first time you hear it each morning. What it's like to be you, now, cup by cup."

Elodie turned to the first page. Dipped a pen. His hand hesitated over the paper — and then, from somewhere deeper than memory, from the place where the knot lay warm against his chest, came a sentence he didn't know he knew:

*A person is not what they remember. A person is what they choose next.*

He wrote it down. Looked at it a long time.

"Maren," he said slowly. "Who taught me that?"

She went pale, then reached for her grandmother's ledger — no, *his* grandmother's — and opened it to a page near the end, where the old cipher gave way to plain hand. She read aloud:

"'The Hollow eats what we were. Let it. A binder is not what she remembers. A binder is what she chooses next.'"

The lamp hissed. Outside, Ashvale settled into evening, and somewhere below a bakery was setting its dough for morning.

"She left you more than warnings," Maren said softly. "She left you instructions."

Elodie picked up the pen again. And began, cup by cup, page by page, to write himself back.
