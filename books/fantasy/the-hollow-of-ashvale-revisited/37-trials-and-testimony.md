## 37. Trials and Testimony

The first thing Captain Rell did as regent was abolish his own office.

He signed the order at dawn on the fourth day after the king's death, in the same chamber where he had once filed reports that doomed eleven people to the ash-pyres. The ink was ordinary iron gall. His hand did not shake, though he had expected it to. He had learned something about hands in the past weeks — that they shook before the deed and went quiet after, like a storm passing over water.

The order read: *The Office of the Witch-Hunt is dissolved. All warrants voided. All prisoners released pending review. All denunciations to be heard again, this time by the denounced.*

His clerk, a nervous boy named Tam with ink-stained cuffs, read it twice and then looked up.

"Regent — sir — you can't mean the last part literally."

"I mean it exactly," Rell said. "Every person who informed on another will sit across from them. Every accused will be heard by a panel of three: one chosen by the city wards, one by the guilds, one drawn by lot. And I will testify at every trial where my reports played a part."

Tam's mouth opened and closed. "There were forty-one denunciations in the market districts alone."

"Then we'll be busy." Rell capped the inkwell. "Send notice to the archivist. Maren Voss. She'll want to oversee the records."

"She's not — people say she was mixed up in the palace business. The night the king died."

"People say a great many things," Rell said. "That's rather the whole problem."

* * *

They converted the mint's assay hall for the trials. It had high windows and good light, which felt important; Rell remembered too many rooms designed to keep the truth in the dark. He had the interrogation cells below sealed, their doors bricked shut with the prisoners' own chalk marks still visible on the frames — the tallies men and women had scratched counting days they would not live to finish. He could not bring himself to have them scrubbed away. Let the walls keep what the walls knew.

The first trial was Bettina Kessel, the charm-seller who had burned her own stock in the street when the edict came. Her accuser was a man named Odric Hale, a cooper who had sworn before the wardens that he'd seen her sell a love-charm to his wife.

Rell sat in the gallery, because regents do not judge — he had written that into the charter himself, over the objection of three guildmasters who thought power should concentrate in whoever happened to be holding it. The panels judged. Rell testified. That was all he would allow himself.

Hale stood in the well of the hall with his cap wrung in both fists, and when the panel asked him why he had denounced her, he said, "For the silver," plainly, without excuse, and something in the hall shifted — a collective exhale, the sound of people discovering that confession could be simple.

"And your wife?" asked the ward representative, a broad-faced woman named Ilse who ran the fishmongers' row.

"Left me," Hale said. "Went to her sister's in Marlow Ford. I don't blame her. I watched Bettina burn charms she'd made with her own hands, and I took coin for naming her anyway." He turned to face the woman at the defendant's table. "I'm sorry. It isn't enough. I know it isn't enough."

Bettina was a small woman with burn scars on her forearms from the night she'd fed her own work to the flames. She looked at Hale for a long moment, and the hall held its breath.

"I don't forgive you," she said finally. "Not today. Maybe not ever. But I'll take the rest of your life as payment instead of your death, if the panel agrees. You'll rebuild every stall in the market district that the hunts tore down. Starting with mine."

The panel agreed. Hale bowed his head and said thank you, and Rell found he had to look out the high window at the sky for a moment before he trusted his voice for anything.

* * *

Not all of them went that way.

The third day brought the case of Sera Vole, the midwife — the same midwife Rell had interviewed in the cells beneath the mint, the one who had spoken of cold things in birthing chambers while he wrote it all down in a report he knew would kill her. She had survived only because Bettina had smuggled her out, and because the Hollow had died before the warrant against her could be executed.

Her accuser was a neighbor, a widow named Grett Ashby, who had told the hunters that Sera marked newborns with sigils. When Grett stood in the well and admitted it — admitted it freely, tearfully, saying she'd been frightened and the silver had seemed like protection — Sera rose from her seat.

"You came to my door in winter," Sera said. "When your granddaughter was born blue and failing. I worked until dawn to pull her back. And six months later you sold my name for twelve marks."

"I did." Grett's voice broke. "I've hated myself every hour since—"

"Hating yourself costs nothing," Sera said, and the hall went very quiet. "You'll pay in the currency that matters. You'll come to every birth I attend for the next year and hold the mother's hand and boil the water and carry the buckets. You'll see what my hands do, up close, and you'll remember whose name you tried to put in the fire."

Grett nodded so hard her cap fell off. "Yes. Yes, anything—"

"It isn't anything," Sera said. "It's a year of mornings. That's what it costs."

Rell wrote none of it down. Some things should not go in reports.

* * *

His own testimony came on the sixth day, at the trial of the boy who whistled wind.

His name was Perrin Dole. Fourteen years old. He had been arrested for whistling a gale off the harbor masts during a storm, saving three fishing boats, and had spent nineteen days in the cells waiting to learn whether the crown considered a saved boat worth more than an unsanctioned gift. Rell had interviewed him twice. The second time, Rell had sat with him long after the questions ran out, unable to decide what duty meant anymore, and had finally left without filing the transcript — the one small treason of mercy in a career of filed cruelties.

Now Perrin sat at the defendant's table, taller than Rell remembered, or perhaps just sitting straighter. His accuser was a harbormaster's assistant named Quill, who had traded the boy's name for eight marks and a promotion that had, in the end, never come through.

Quill confessed. Quill wept. Perrin listened with his chin on his fists, the way boys listen to weather.

And then the panel chair — Ilse, the fishwife, who had drawn this trial too — said, "Captain Rell. You're called to testify regarding the conduct of the hunt. Take the stand."

The hall turned to look at him. He walked down from the gallery and took the oath with his palm flat on the old assay stone, and told the truth in order, beginning to end: how many he had interviewed, how many transcripts he had forwarded knowing what forwarding meant, how he had measured his words sometimes to spare a prisoner and other times had not spared anyone, and how the arithmetic of those choices lived in him now and would live in him always.

"There were forty-one denunciations," Rell said. "Eleven executions carried out before the king died. I processed every file. In four cases I believe I could have slowed the machinery and did not. I was a good soldier. I am here to tell you what a good soldier did, so that no one can ever again claim the orders excused it."

Ilse studied him. "The charter you wrote gives the panel authority to sentence any officer of the former hunt. Do you accept that?"

"I do."

"Even if the sentence is severe."

"The sentence should be proportionate to the harm," Rell said. "Mine was considerable."

Perrin spoke up from the table, surprising everyone. "He sat with me," the boy said. "In the cells. When he didn't have to. He didn't write down half of what I told him, and what he didn't write down is the reason I'm alive to complain about the food in here, which is terrible, by the way."

A ripple of laughter moved through the hall — startled, disbelieving laughter, the first Rell had heard in the building. He did not smile. This was not a day for smiling.

Ilse conferred with her fellow judges for less time than Rell expected. Then she stood.

"Here is our finding," she said. "The captain's crimes are real, and we do not launder them with good intentions. But a city that punishes only the men who confess will teach its officers to lie. So: Captain Rell is sentenced to service. He will preside over every remaining trial. He will hear every confession and every refusal of forgiveness, every debt named and every debt paid, and when the trials are done, he will stand before the city in the market square and account for himself publicly, and the city will decide then what more he owes." She paused. "We do not appeal to mercy. We appeal to usefulness. Ashvale needs men who know exactly how the fire was built, so they can make sure it's never built again."

Rell inclined his head. "I accept the sentence. Without appeal."

"That's not—" Perrin started, but Ilse raised a hand, and the gavel fell, and it was done.

* * *

The trials ran for nine weeks.

Some days were clean and some were ugly. A tanner who had denounced three families refused to confess at all, and the panel gave him to the families to name his penalty, and the families — this was the part Rell carried home each night — chose labor, not blood. Not always. One widow asked for nothing except that the man who named her husband never speak her family's name again, in any room, forever, and the panel enforced it, and Rell thought it the harshest sentence he had witnessed: erasure, chosen freely, the very crime the Hollow had committed on kings now meted out by grief.

Some of the denounced forgave entirely, which unsettled Rell more than the refusals. A cooper's apprentice embraced the man who had sold him and called him brother, and the hall wept, and Rell sat with his hands flat on the rail thinking: *they are better than the century they were given.*

Word came, in the seventh week, that the regency council wanted the trials concluded faster — that trade was suffering, that the guilds grumbled, that justice was expensive. Rell sent back a single line: *Justice at speed is just the hunt wearing different clothes.* The council did not raise it again. He suspected Maren Voss had a hand in that; she had taken over the archive with terrifying efficiency and had begun sending him documents he had no idea how she obtained, annotated in a small precise hand, as if she were still grading his homework.

* * *

On the final day, the hall was full past capacity. People stood along the walls and out into the corridor. The last case was the smallest: a flower-seller named Annet Prynne who had been denounced for tying ribbons into knots that allegedly held wishes.

Her accuser had died in the plague month — there was no one to confront, no confession to extract, no debt to name. The panel could simply have dismissed it. Instead Ilse turned to the gallery.

"Is there anyone who will speak for Annet Prynne?" she asked. "Anyone harmed? Anyone owed?"

Silence. And then, from the back, voices began — one, then five, then twenty. Customers. Neighbors. A girl who swore the luck-knot Annet tied into her hair had gotten her through her mother's dying. A boatwright who kept a wish-ribbon nailed above his bench. They spoke not to accuse or forgive but simply to witness: *she made things, and the things were good, and we loved them, and we were afraid to say so.*

Annet Prynne cried into her apron. Ilse declared the charge dissolved and the practice lawful, and outside the hall someone had already chalked on the mint wall, in letters a yard high: *THE KNOTS ARE LEGAL.* By evening the whole wall was covered in chalk drawings of knotted ribbons, layer upon layer, the way ivy climbs.

* * *

That night, Rell stood in the market square as his sentence required.

He had prepared remarks. He abandoned them. He simply stood on the steps of the old assize house, in front of several hundred citizens holding torches and lanterns, and told them again what he had told the panels: the files, the numbers, the four names he might have slowed. He named the four aloud. Their families were in the crowd. Two of them turned their backs. One spat. One — an old man, father of a girl Rell had failed — walked slowly up the steps and stood beside him and said, "Tell them the rest. Tell them all of it. If my Nella has to be a number, let the number buy something."

So Rell stayed another hour, answering questions until his voice cracked, and when it was over the old man gripped his arm once, hard, and walked away into the crowd, and Rell understood that this too was a sentence — the lifelong kind, the kind you serve by getting up every morning and being useful to the city that knows exactly what you did.

He walked home the long way, past the market district, past the stalls being rebuilt under Odric Hale's sentenced hands. At the corner of Charm Row — people were already calling it that again, defiantly, the way you reclaim a wound by renaming it — he saw lamplight in a familiar window. Elodie Fairweather's old rooms, newly leased. There were rumors the taster was coming back to the trade now that magic was to be decriminalized, that Maren Voss was drafting the statutes herself.

Rell paused at the corner. He had arrested that man on a charge invented to fit a body that wouldn't sicken. He had asked him one question in an archive corridor — *is the king still in there?* — and the honest answer had undone something in Rell that had never knit back correctly, and he was grateful for it.

Tomorrow he would send word to the tower: the trials' full records were ready for the archive. He suspected the archivist would want them. He suspected, too, that she was writing something larger than records — that the ledger she kept had grown into a history, and histories, in Ashvale, had a way of becoming law.

He turned up his collar against the harbor wind and walked on. Behind him, the chalk ribbons on the mint wall caught the moonlight, hundreds of them, holding their small illegal-now-legal wishes up against the dark.

* * *

At dawn, a runner arrived at the regency office with a letter sealed in grey wax — the archivist's seal, but heavier paper than usual. Inside, in that small precise hand, a single page:

*Captain — the trials' records arrive not a moment too soon. I have finished the Fairweather ledger. What it contains concerns every soul who testified in your hall, and one soul in particular who does not yet know what he lost. Come to the tower today. Bring nothing. Read everything. — V*

Rell read it twice, set it beside the charter he had written in bloodless ink on the morning everything changed, and reached for his coat.
