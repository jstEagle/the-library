## 39. A New Charm for Old Ashvale

The stall smelled of beeswax and river-stone, and Elodie had built it himself, which was either a triumph or a small miracle, depending on whether you'd watched him do it.

Three Saturdays of splinters and crooked nails, Wren holding the frame steady while he swore at the joints. The wood came from a dismantled fishing boat — the *Kindly Tide*, its name still ghosted under fresh paint on one plank — and when the morning sun hit the counter it gave off a faint echo of tar and herring that made children wrinkle their noses and old fishermen go quiet.

"It remembers being useful," Elodie told them. "That's why it feels good to lean on."

He had a sign now. He'd argued against it for weeks — signs invited attention, attention invited denunciations, denunciations invited ash — but Maren had put her foot down with archivist finality: *If you hide, they win twice.* So the sign hung from the awning beam on a leather cord, painted by Bettina in her blunt, unornamental hand:

**FAIRWEATHER CHARMS & REMEMBRANCES — NOTHING FORBIDDEN SOLD HERE**

Bettina had wanted to add *everything honest*, but there hadn't been room, and Elodie suspected she'd left it off deliberately so he'd have something unfinished to live up to.

* * *

The first customer of the day was always Old Tam, who wasn't buying anything but liked to be first anyway.

"Wind's from the south," Tam announced, leaning on the counter as though it were a bar. "South wind means my knees talk. You got anything for talking knees?"

"Charms don't work on knees," Elodie said. "Charms work on the part of you that's tired of your knees."

Tam considered this. "Same thing."

"Not even slightly." But Elodie took out a flat gray pebble from the river below the mint — a stone that had spent two hundred years listening to coins being struck, and had soaked up the sound of patience — and pressed it into Tam's palm. "Hold this when they ache. Not for the ache. For remembering that everything passes. Even pain. Especially pain."

Tam grunted, paid three coppers he didn't need to pay, and shuffled off toward the fish market, and Elodie watched him go and felt the specific warmth that had replaced most of his other feelings these days — the warmth of doing small good in full daylight.

Because that was the thing no one had warned him about, in all those years of selling luck-knots under floorboards: how much energy hiding took. How much of his craft had gone into looking over his shoulder instead of into the work itself. Now his charms weren't better, exactly — his hands were slower than they used to be, his memory-reading shallower, a candle beside the furnace he'd once been — but they were *whole*. Made by a man standing in the open.

Magic was legal now. Legal was too small a word. The Regency Edict of the third month — Rell's first act after the trials, before his own trial, because he insisted the law come before his reckoning — had not merely decriminalized the gift. It had apologized. In writing. Posted on every bakery door where the hunt proclamations had once been nailed.

Elodie had stood in front of Bettina's bakery reading it for a long time. *The Crown acknowledges grievous wrongs...* Bettina had come out with flour on her hands and read it over his shoulder and said nothing, and then gone back inside and burned bread, because her hands were shaking, and they'd eaten the burnt bread together without comment, because some things you didn't waste.

* * *

The children arrived mid-morning, as they always did, in a noise like a flock of starlings deciding collectively to land.

They were Maren's students, mostly — sent down from the tower school on alternating days for what the curriculum officially called *practical resonance studies* and what everyone actually called *Elodie's lessons*. There were nine today. Pip, the smallest, who could hear echoes in metal and complained constantly that coins were boring. Sefa, whose grandmother had been a midwife and who carried that inheritance like a lit lantern she wasn't sure she was allowed to hold. The Hollis twins, who finished each other's sentences and each other's readings. And Doss, eleven years old and already wearing the wary look of a boy who'd been told his gift was shameful by someone he loved, and who came to the stall every session hoping to be told otherwise.

Elodie set a chipped clay cup on the counter.

"All right. Who can tell me what this cup remembers?"

Hands shot up. He waited. That was the first lesson, always: *wait*. An echo rushed at you if you grabbed for it; it came to you if you made room.

"Pip," he said finally.

Pip pressed both palms flat against the cup, screwed his eyes shut, and went still with the effortful concentration of a child lifting something heavy. "Milk," he said. "A lot of milk. And — burning? It was near a fire." His face crumpled with worry. "Was somebody hurt?"

"No," Elodie said gently. "It sat on a shelf above the hearth for thirty years. It never burned. But it listened to fires every winter night, and things soak up what they listen to." He turned the cup so the children could see the glaze, cracked like dry mud. "This is the second rule, and it's the important one. What you read isn't always what happened. Echoes are like smoke — they drift, they thin, they cling to wrong corners. A reader who forgets that starts seeing ghosts everywhere and telling people lies about their own lives."

Sefa raised her hand. "What's the first rule?"

"You tell me."

She recited it with the flat certainty of scripture: "Never read a person without asking."

"Never read a *anyone* without asking," Elodie corrected. "Not a person, not a place, not a cup. Asking costs nothing. Not asking costs—" he stopped, because the end of that sentence lived in his own empty rooms, in the hollowed corridors beneath the palace, in a king's grave on the hill. "Costs more than you'll ever be able to pay back."

Doss spoke without raising his hand, which was how Doss asked his real questions. "My uncle says readers get hollow. He says it eats you up from inside."

The other children went quiet. They'd heard versions of the story — all Ashvale had — but they looked at Elodie now the way you look at the scar rather than the knife.

"He's not wrong," Elodie said. "I knew something that ate memories for a living. It wore a crown while it did it." He let that sit. "But here's what your uncle doesn't know. It didn't take my memories because I was a reader. It took them because I *gave* them — freely, on purpose, to save someone else. Reading doesn't hollow you. Hiding does. Lying does. Spending years pretending the biggest true thing about you isn't true — that eats you up from inside, and I've got three years of receipts to prove it."

Doss absorbed this with the suspicious slowness of a boy checking a coin for clipping. Then: "Can you still read?"

"A little." Elodie picked up the cup again. Where once the whole history would have flooded him — the potter's hands, the kiln's heat, the girl who'd bought it, the winters — now he caught only the brightest threads, like hearing a song through a wall. "Warm milk. A kitchen. Somebody singing badly." He smiled. "That's enough. Most days, that's more than enough."

He passed the cup down the line of children, and each one touched it and reported, and half of what they reported was wrong and he corrected them kindly, and the morning sun climbed, and Wren sorted charm-stock behind him with the easy economy of someone who'd been doing it for months, and Elodie thought — not for the first time, but with the freshness that never quite dulled — *this is what I almost traded away without knowing it existed.*

* * *

At noon, Wren brought out the food.

She ate lunch at the stall every day now. She had a stool behind the counter that was hers, and a knife she used to cut apples and cheese with terrifying competence, and a way of sitting with her shoulder just touching his that managed to be both casual and deliberate, the way everything about Wren was deliberate when it mattered.

Today she cut the apple into slices and handed him the first one, and he ate it, and it tasted like — an apple. Crisp, sweet-tart, wet. Just an apple.

Before, it would have been an orchard. His mother's hands. The ladder against the tree, the wasps drunk on fallen fruit, the exact gold-green of a late-summer afternoon thirty years gone. Before, every bite was a library.

Now it was an apple. And here was the strange arithmetic he'd stopped fighting: an apple was enough. An apple, fully tasted, with no echo attached, no past crowding in — an apple tasted *new*, every time, was its own small mercy.

"Wren," he said.

"Mm."

"Tell me about the luck-knot again. The first one. The day we met."

She didn't sigh. She never sighed at this request, though he must have made it a hundred times. She wiped the knife, folded it away, and gave him the story with the same care she gave everything — not performing patience, just paying out thread.

"You came to the harbor stalls on a grey Tuesday. I was mending nets because Da wouldn't let me touch the till. You sold me a knot for luck — green cord, three loops, and you said—" she paused, savoring it, "—you said, 'Luck isn't fortune, it's attention. This just reminds you to pay it.' And I thought, that's the first honest thing anyone's sold me at this market. And then a year later my father—"

"I know the rest," Elodie said quietly.

"You always stop me there." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb moved along the counter's edge, over the plank from the *Kindly Tide*, back and forth. "You always stop me right there."

"Because I want to remember choosing to forgive you," he said. "Not just having done it. If I hear it too many times it becomes furniture. I want it to stay a decision."

She looked at him for a moment — brown eyes, salt-frizzed hair escaping its braid, a small scar on her chin he'd learned yesterday and would learn again tomorrow — and then she nodded, once, and handed him the cheese.

Here was the truth he'd stopped explaining to people who pitied him: yes, he couldn't remember her face from before. Couldn't remember his mother's kitchen except as words Maren had written down. Couldn't remember the taste of his own name spoken by his grandmother, couldn't remember three years of flavors that had once been his whole hoarded treasury. The Hollow had gorged and died and left him a man with shallow water where an ocean used to be.

But Wren had understood something in those first terrible weeks that Maren had needed ledgers to understand and Rell had needed a war to understand: memory lost is grief, but memory rebuilt is *choice*. Every morning he woke and did not know her face, and every morning she walked through the door of whatever room he slept in and introduced herself — "Wren. Fisherman's daughter. You forgave me; I'm making sure you don't regret it" — and every morning he learned her again. The way she squinted before laughing. The way she counted money twice. The way she talked to cats as equals.

Other men had wives they remembered. He had a wife — not yet, not formally, but they'd both stopped pretending otherwise — whom he *discovered*, daily. No marriage in Ashvale was renewed with such diligence.

"You're staring," Wren said.

"Learning," he corrected.

"That too."

* * *

The afternoon brought the hard case, because there was one nearly every day now.

A woman came to the stall with a boy of maybe seven, holding his hand too tightly. She didn't look at Elodie directly — the newly-gifted and the families of the newly-gifted had a particular way of approaching, sideways, like people testing ice.

"They say you teach," she said. "Safe. They say you teach safe."

"I try."

"My son hears things. From walls, from — from graves, we live near the old quarter, and he wakes up crying with names that aren't ours in his mouth." Her voice dropped. "The tower has a waiting list. The priests say it's possession. I don't — I can't afford the waiting list, and I don't believe the priests."

Elodie crouched to the boy's level. Up close he could see the exhaustion in the small face, the flinch of a child who'd learned that his own head was enemy territory.

"What's your name?" Elodie asked.

The boy looked at his mother first. Permission. Then: "Cael."

"Cael. Can I show you a trick?" He held up the gray pebble — a fresh one, river-washed, patient. "Everything old is listening all the time. Walls, stones, cups. And they play it all back, all mixed up, and it pours into you because nobody ever taught you a door." He closed the boy's fingers around the stone. "This is a door. When the voices come, you hold this and you say — inside your head, not out loud — '*not mine, not now.'* Not angry. Just like closing a shutter. The stone helps because stones are very good at keeping things out. It's the whole reason they're stones."

The boy's brow furrowed. "Will it work forever?"

"For now. And in autumn the tower school opens its doors to everyone, no fees — Maren Voss made the regent put it in the edict. You'll learn properly then. Doors, windows, locks. All of it." He glanced up at the woman. "Tell the tower I sent you. Tell them Fairweather's word."

Something loosened in her shoulders. She paid — he waved it off, she insisted, he took one copper for dignity's sake — and they went, the boy clutching the pebble like a talisman, which, Elodie reflected, it was. Everything he sold was a talisman. The magic was mostly attention. It had always been mostly attention.

Wren appeared at his elbow. "You give away half your stock."

"And the half I sell pays for the half I give away. Rent is rent. That was true when it was illegal and it's true now."

"You're quoting yourself."

"Somebody has to. I can't remember who else said it."

She laughed — the squint first, then the sound — and the sound landed in his chest the way it did every time, unmemoried and therefore undiminished, and he thought: *let the Hollow keep its ocean. I have this, new every morning.*

* * *

Near closing, Bettina came by with bread still warm enough to steam through the cloth, and stood surveying the stall with her arms crossed like a general reviewing fortifications.

"Had a customer ask me last week if I was jealous," she said. "'Fairweather sells charms openly now,' she says. 'Must sting.'" Bettina snorted. "I told her: I burned my stock to keep my neck. He fed his blood to a god-eating worm. We are not in the same trade anymore, and thank every saint for it."

"How's the bakery?"

"Full. Half of Ashvale wants to buy bread from a woman who stood in the street and burned her own work rather than lie. They think it makes the loaves taste braver." She set the cloth bundle on the counter. "Maybe it does."

When she'd gone, Elodie unwrapped the bread and broke it, and gave Wren the heel, because she liked the heel, another fact relearned, another entry in the ledger he kept in Maren's careful hand — *Wren: prefers crusts. Laughs sideways first. Afraid of deep water since her brother drowned; loves it anyway.*

He retaught himself bread daily too. Today it tasted like: warm. Grain. Bettina's stubbornness, baked in.

* * *

He closed the stall at dusk. Wren stacked the unsold charms into their box while he doused the lamp, and together they folded the awning, and their hands overlapped on the same rope at the same moment, an accident that happened every single evening and surprised him every single evening.

"One more thing," Wren said, when the stall was shuttered and the market square was going blue with evening. She reached into her coat and brought out a letter — heavy paper, iron-gall ink, sealed with the archive's mark. "Maren sent it by runner. Said you'd want it tonight, not tomorrow."

Elodie turned it over. His name on the front, in Maren's precise hand. His stomach did something complicated.

"Bad news comes in this kind of envelope," he said.

"Good news comes in this kind of envelope when it's Maren," Wren said. "Open it."

He broke the seal. The lamplight from Bettina's window caught the page. Maren's handwriting, usually ruler-straight, hurried:

*Elodie —*

*The restoration work proceeds faster than projected. I have recovered more of your grandmother's cipher from the binding-ledger — pages we believed destroyed were misfiled in the Mint archive for sixty years, and among them, a personal section she wrote in plain script, not cipher. She wrote it for whoever came after. She wrote it, I believe, for you.*

*There are things in it about the rite. About what she intended the sacrifice to leave behind. About what she thinks — thought — a binder might keep, if the offering were shaped differently. It may be nothing. It may be a dead woman's wishful mathematics. But Elodie: if she is right, then what you lost may not be entirely lost. Some of it may be recoverable. Your mother's kitchen. The tastes. Perhaps — I say perhaps, and I will deny saying it if you quote me — perhaps even the three years.*

*I have booked the archive reading room for tomorrow, first light. Bring Wren. Bring the bread.*

*Come see what your grandmother kept for you.*

*— M.V.*

Elodie read it twice. The square went on around him — shutters closing, gulls arguing over the fish-market leavings, children being called home by names — ordinary Ashvale, alive and loud and free, and he stood in the middle of it holding a dead woman's letter like a live coal.

Wren read it over his arm. He felt her breath catch at the same place his had.

"Your mother's kitchen," she said softly.

"The taste of my own name." He folded the letter with hands that were not quite steady. Three years ago — or was it a lifetime, he genuinely could not say — he had walked into the dark beneath the palace knowing he would lose everything, and had chosen it anyway, and had made peace with the loss, had built this whole bright small life on top of the loss like a house on foundations.

And now the foundations might be dug up. Might be *fillable*.

"Are you afraid?" Wren asked.

He considered honestly. "Terrified. If she's wrong, I grieve it all again. If she's right—" he laughed, unsteadily, "—if she's right, I become a man who remembers, and I don't know if he gets to keep this. Keep the stall. Keep—" he looked at her face, tonight's version, tomorrow to be learned again, "—keep learning you."

Wren took his hand. Her grip was a fisherman's grip; it had hauled nets and lines and, once, him, out of worse water than any sea.

"Then we find out together," she said. "First light. Bring the bread."

Above the rooftops, the evening star came out over old Ashvale — the city of ash-burnings, the city of luck-knots sold under floorboards, the city that had burned and was learning, slowly, clumsily, to bloom in its own ashes — and Elodie Fairweather, taster, binder's grandson, man of shallow waters and chosen joys, pocketed his grandmother's letter and walked home into the dark with everything, suddenly, to remember.
