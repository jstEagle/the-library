## 40. The Hollow of Ashvale, Revisited

The road up to the royal necropolis was paved with stones that had forgotten they were ever anything else. Elodie noticed things like that now — the way memory clung or didn't — because he had become a student of absence. He climbed the hill slowly, his basket balanced on one hip, and let Ashvale fall away beneath him in its autumn colors: slate roofs going gold at the edges where the morning caught them, the market square a scatter of tiny bright awnings, the river running silver toward the sea it had always been running toward.

He could not remember the first time he'd made this climb. Maren said there hadn't been one — that he'd never visited the old king's tomb before today, in all these years — but Maren's memory of him was secondhand too, assembled from letters she'd written to a man who no longer existed to read them. The truth was that nobody alive remembered Elodie Fairweather whole. That was the arithmetic of what he'd done. He had fed himself to an ancient hunger so that a king could die free, and what came back was this: a man of forty-odd years with a flat tongue, a kind face he didn't recognize in mirrors, and a small knot of braided cord and hair that he carried everywhere like a coal banked against the cold.

The luck-knot sat in his coat pocket now, as it always did. He touched it once, lightly, the way you'd touch a doorframe passing through — not for luck, exactly. For orientation. It was the one warm thread left of whoever he had been, and some mornings it was all that told him he'd been anyone at all.

* * *

The tomb of King Aldric the Last was not grand, which would have surprised the courtiers who'd served him in the hollow years. When Rell — regent, then king-consort by acclamation, then simply Rell after he'd refused a crown twice and accepted a third term as something between a magistrate and a penitent — had overseen the funeral, he'd ordered the crypt plain. Pale stone. A slab with a name and two dates. No crown carved above it, because the crown had been melted down in the first spring of the reckoning, along with every other relic the Hollow had touched, and the metal had come out of the forge wrong somehow, gray and dead, and they'd buried it under the orchard Aldric's daughter had loved.

Elodie knew all this from Maren's letters. He knew almost everything about those years from Maren's letters, which was a strange way to know your own life: reading it like a history of someone adjacent to yourself. *You were afraid,* she'd written, in the account of the night before the unbinding. *You asked me twice if I thought it would hurt, and both times I lied and said no.* He had read that passage many times, trying to feel the fear the words described, and had gotten only as far as recognizing that a man ought to have felt it. It was like reading about weather in a country he'd never visited.

But he did not need the letters for what he remembered of Aldric's last hour. That memory the Hollow had left him — or perhaps Aldric had left *him*, pressed into his hand like a coin at a border crossing, payment for the lie Elodie told him at the end. *Would your daughter have known you?* Elodie had said yes. She would not have. They had both known it. And still the dying king had smiled, and gripped his wrist with a strength that surprised them both, and said, *Then we are even, taster. We have each spared the other the truth.*

That memory had survived intact. Elodie had never understood why. Maren theorized that it wasn't memory at all anymore but something else — a gift, a bequest, a stone passed hand to hand across a chasm. Whatever it was, he kept it carefully, unwrapped it only on certain days, and today was one of them.

* * *

There was a keeper at the necropolis gate now, an old woman with ink-stained fingers — a graduate of Maren's school, Elodie guessed, by the way she catalogued him at a glance and then deliberately stopped, having filed him correctly. Visitors to Aldric's grave were rare and tended to fall into two kinds: historians and pilgrims, and neither much liked being looked at.

"Fairweather," she said anyway, soft, when he gave his name at the ledger. Her pen paused. "The taster."

"That's what they call me." It was not quite his name anymore. His name was a word he recognized the way you recognize a word in a language you studied young and lost — shape without substance. Wren said it every morning across the breakfast table, patient as rain, and every morning it fit a little better and meant a little more, and it would never mean what it had meant before. "Is he alone up there? I'd rather not intrude."

"The last visitor was a week ago. A woman from the midwives' guild. She left bread." The keeper hesitated. "People leave strange things at his grave. Bread, mostly. Once a whistle carved from alder wood. Nobody leaves flowers."

"No," Elodie said. "They wouldn't." A king who forgot his own name had taught a city what forgetting cost. People brought him things to remember *by*. "I'll not be long."

The path wound up through birches gone the color of candle flames. Somewhere below, faintly, a bell rang the hour — the mint tower bell, recast after the collapse, because the old bell had cracked the night the palace tore at its own seams and corridors forgot themselves into childhoods. Elodie had been there for that. He had no memory of it. Maren's letter described her dragging his blank-eyed weight through rooms that had never existed and would never exist again, past doors opening onto other people's first days of school and first kisses and first griefs, and she had written: *You weighed nothing. I thought for three steps that I was carrying an empty coat. Then you said my name — just my name, nothing else, no reason — and I decided we would both survive out of spite.*

He loved her for that line. He was fairly sure he had always loved her, in whatever configuration love had taken in the life he couldn't recall, and he was certain he loved her now, in this one: the archivist turned schoolmistress who was teaching him his own biography one careful page at a time, always asking permission before she told him the hard parts.

* * *

The grave stood in a small clearing where the birches opened onto sky. Plain pale stone. A name. Two dates. And, as promised, offerings: a heel of dark bread going stale under a saucer someone had thoughtfully weighted with pebbles; the little alder whistle; a child's clay bird painted blue, its wings spread as though the painter had never seen a bird land and didn't believe in such a thing.

Elodie set down his basket and knelt, joints announcing themselves the way they did now. From the basket he lifted the dish.

It was simple ware, market-made, glazed the green of the river in March — Wren had thrown it, actually; her hands had gotten good over the years, steadier than her father's ever were before the trials. Inside the dish, arranged with a care that had taken him most of the previous evening, was a tasting portion. Thin slices of pear. A curl of salted butter. Honey bread, torn, not cut. And a single spoonful of the plum conserve that the palace kitchens had served at the king's midnight meals in the years of the Hollow — the recipe preserved, of all places, in Maren's archives, in a household ledger nobody had thought historically significant until a king died and everything he'd eaten became evidence.

Safe, this time. Every item tested twice, once by the guild assay and once by Elodie's own tongue, which tasted nothing at all and therefore could certify nothing except that he had put it in his mouth and lived. There was an irony there he had learned to hold gently rather than sharpen. The finest poison-detector in the history of Ashvale could no longer detect so much as a burnt crust. These days he cooked by trust, by recipe, by the faces people made — and it turned out that feeding people by faith was its own old magic, older than charms, and no edict had ever managed to burn it out.

"I don't know if you liked plums," he told the stone. His voice sounded odd in the clearing, too loud, then too small. "Maren says you did. She says it's in the steward's accounts — extra conserves ordered in your fourth year, which she thinks is when the Hollow started needing richer feed, sweeter, closer to the bone. So maybe you liked plums and maybe it liked plums and I honestly can't tell you which of you I'm feeding."

A jay scolded him from the birches. Wind moved through the high branches with a sound like pages turning.

"But here it is," he said. "All of it safe. All of it given."

* * *

He sat with the dish between his knees for a long while, and let himself do the thing he had come up the hill to do, which was not mourning exactly and not prayer either, but something in the country between them that had no name he'd found yet.

Every choice since had been made by the man the sacrifice left behind. He had written that sentence once, or thought it, or Maren had thought it and he'd borrowed it — authorship itself was uncertain ground these days — and it was true the way a map is true: accurate, incomplete, useful. The man who walked down out of the palace in Maren's arms, empty as a scraped bowl, could have become anything. Hollows make room. What filled him instead was choice upon choice upon choice: staying in Maren's tower instead of drifting off to some coast town where nobody knew his name, because his name needed relearning and relearning takes witnesses. Standing up at the first open trial to speak for the fisherman who had sold him — not to forgive him publicly, that wasn't his to give, but to say aloud that a man desperate enough to feed his children will sell almost anything, and a city that makes desperation profitable should look at its own ledgers before it looks at its poor. Opening the charm-stall again when magic was decriminalized, teaching the neighborhood children to read memory-echoes safely, patiently, the way you teach children to swim — not because the water is safe but because someday they'll fall in, and better they know how it holds you.

And marrying Wren. Or whatever the word was for what he and Wren had built, since neither of them cared much for ceremonies after the years of ash. She had come to him first with a returned luck-knot and a forgiveness begged, and he had given it, and giving it had cost more than anger would have — that much survived in him as knowledge without feeling, the way a scar survives its wound. And then she had come again, and again, every day, introducing herself like dawn introduces itself: without apology, without hurry. *Wren. My father denounced you. You forgave him. You don't remember any of it. I brought bread.* And one morning, years in now, she had said, "You keep learning my face. Can't you just — keep it?" And he had said, "I keep it every day. It's just that keeping isn't the same as having." And she had laughed, and he had known, in the deep place below memory where knowing lives, that this was the truest thing he'd ever been part of.

The luck-knot sat against his ribs. Her hair was braided into it, gray-threaded now — she'd added strands over the years, renewing it, practical defiance renewed annually like a lease — and through it ran the one warm thread of him that the Hollow's appetite had failed to swallow. Some nights, when the flatness of the world pressed close, when he woke unable to conjure the taste of bread or the sound of his mother's voice or the color of his own childhood kitchen, he held the knot and let it tell him what it knew: *someone loved you enough to anchor you. Someone decided you were worth hauling back across the dark. Start from there.*

So he started from there. Every morning. It was enough. It was, he had come to believe, the whole of what anyone gets.

* * *

"You'd have liked him," Elodie said to the grave, eventually. "Rell, I mean. Or maybe you'd have hated him — he tried you, after. Well. He tried everyone, including himself. First honest trials this city ever saw. Informers face to face with the denounced. Some forgive, some don't, and he let both stand, and then he took his own sentence without appeal and spent six years doing clerical work in the archive basement like the world's most dangerous filing clerk." He smiled. "Maren says you called him *the dull blade* behind his back. She says it affectionately. Mostly."

Bettina had planted a real charm openly in the market square the dawn after the king died, and it was still there — grown now into a small twisted tree of knotted rowan and river-clay that mothers touched for safe births and that the city had quietly agreed, without any law requiring it, never to burn again. The school Maren opened taught cipher and countercharm and the ethics of inheritance to children whose grandparents had informed on their neighbors, and Maren began every first lesson the same way: *"Magic was never the crime. Fear was the crime. We are here to study the difference."*

And the Fairweather ledger — his grandmother's ledger, ciphered and decoded, the binding-notes that had mapped his fate before he was born — closed with an entry in Maren's hand: *The Hollow unbound. The binder diminished but alive. The curse of total loss ends here.* He had read that entry a hundred times. He had written none of it. Both facts seemed right to him.

The plum conserve gleamed in its dish. A bee had found the honey bread and was working it with the seriousness of a small official.

"I used to taste everything," Elodie said. "Three years of poison, and I could name arsenic from nightshade blindfolded, tell you which vintage had been cellared too near the cheese. It was the only thing I was ever good at, and I hid it inside another secret, and the two secrets together nearly ate me alive." He turned the dish slightly, so the light caught the glaze. "Now I can't taste anything, and I cook for a living anyway, and last Tuesday a woman cried over my soup. Not because it was good — well, partly because it was good — but because her mother used to make it that way, and she'd forgotten, and the soup reminded her. That's the trade I got in the end. I gave away my own remembering and became a man who hands other people theirs back." He shrugged. "There are worse economies."

The wind moved. Somewhere far below, the city went on being the city — arguing, haggling, burning nothing.

"I don't remember you," he told the king. "Not really. I have one hour of you, at the end, and I spend it carefully. But I think — the man who carved FAIRWEATHER under the armrest with his last coin, who spent his daughter's laugh to buy one true act — I think that man would understand this dish. I think he'd understand why it matters that it's safe. Everything you ate for twenty years was a theft. Somebody eating from your grave freely given, tested twice, offered with both hands — that's the apology the whole city owes you, in miniature. So. Here it is. In miniature."

He rose, stiffly, and stood a moment with his hat in his hands.

* * *

He was halfway down the path when he heard footsteps coming up — quick, light, familiar as his own pulse — and Wren came around the bend with her shawl pinned wrong and her cheeks pink from the climb, carrying a second basket, because of course she'd followed him; she always gave him the grave alone and then refused, in practice, to give him the walk home alone.

"You took the good bread," she accused, breathless.

"I left half."

"You left the heel. You always leave the heel. One of these days the keeper's going to think you hate crusts." She fell in beside him, took his arm without asking, the way you take hold of a stair rail. "Well? How was he?"

"At peace, I think. The bee approved of the honey bread. I told him about the soup woman." Elodie glanced back once. From here the tomb was just pale stone in candle-colored trees, small and human-scaled, a thing you could carry in your mind entire. "Wren. Do I do this every year?"

She considered him with the expression she wore when deciding how much truth a morning could hold. "Every year. Same dish. Same speech — Maren has notes. You always tell him about the latest person the food made cry." She squeezed his arm. "You always leave the heel."

"Then I suppose I'm consistent."

"You're *you*," she said, with the particular firmness she reserved for correcting errors he didn't know he'd made. "Whatever's missing, what's left is you. I check daily. It's in my notes too."

They went down together through the birches, toward the smell of the city — woodsmoke, river mud, bread baking in the low-town ovens, none of which he could taste and all of which he knew by heart — and behind them, on the pale stone in the clearing, a dish of safe food sat offered to a king finally allowed to keep what he loved, while a bee worked the honey, and the wind turned the pages of the trees, and the story, for once, asked nothing of anyone.

Except, perhaps, breakfast tomorrow. There was always breakfast tomorrow. He intended to be there for every one of them — relearning, retasting, re-becoming — for as long as the man the sacrifice left behind had mornings left to choose.

**THE END**
