## 1. The Weight of Entries

The counting room of the Gildhall smelled of tallow smoke and old paper and, beneath it, something sweeter—the faint green scent of rain that had been traded in a jar three winters past and never fully decanted from the air. Hazel Sterling had stopped noticing it years ago, the way a fish stops noticing water, but on certain mornings, when the light came through the high windows at an angle that turned the dust to drifting gold, she noticed it again, and it always meant the same thing: another day of entries.

She sat at her desk with her sleeves pinned back and her ink-stained fingers moving down a column of figures that no honest accountant in any other city would have believed. The ledger before her was bound in gray calfskin, its spine cracked from use, and it recorded trades that would have made a bishop weep or reach for his purse, depending on the bishop.

*Item the fourteenth,* she wrote in her small, precise hand. *Received of Maren Ostler, widow, one grief entire, weighed and found true. Paid in return: forty days of rain upon the eastern fields, timed to the barley. Interest: none. Note: the widow wept only once during weighing, which is rare, and asked twice if she might keep a little of it back. Told her no. She signed anyway.*

Hazel paused over that last line. She had not been required to record the weeping or the asking. The ledger wanted figures—weight, measure, terms, interest—but she had long ago developed the habit of writing the margins full of things the ledger didn't ask for. The way the widow's hands had shaken. The way she'd said, *I don't want to forget him, I just want it to stop hurting when I remember.* Master Quillon called these marginalia sentimental clutter. He let her do it anyway, perhaps because he understood what Hazel herself only half-understood: that the Gildhall's ledgers were not really about money at all, and never had been.

She turned the page.

*Item the fifteenth. Received of Corporal Denn Vasey, retired, payment rendered over six months: his fear, bought back at eight percent per annum. Original trade: sold his fear to us eleven years past so he could hold the bridge at Corren's Ford. Now wishes it returned. Terms agreed. Note: he did not ask what happened to the fear while it sat in our vaults. We do not tell them. It grows. Everything kept grows.*

That was the truth of the trade that no crier ever shouted in the square: nothing held in the Gildhall's vaults stayed as it was. Luck fermented. Second chances soured into third ones, which were never as clean. A memory stored too long came out smelling faintly of someone else's life. Hazel had learned to account for the drift, to build it into the interest like any other cost of keeping, and she was very good at it—good enough that traders from two rivers away came to Ashvale specifically to have their impossible accounts balanced by the girl with the steady hand and the unblinking eyes.

*Good* was perhaps the wrong word. She was precise. She was thorough. She was, by every measure the guild cared about, excellent. And every morning when she tied back her hair and lit the lamp beside her desk before dawn, she felt the excellence settle around her like a well-tailored coat that belonged to someone else.

Below the counting room, through three floors of stone and timber, the vaults hummed. She could almost hear them on quiet mornings—not a sound exactly, more a pressure behind the ears, like the moment before a storm. Forty days of rain in a copper vessel shaped like a cloud. A soldier's fear, grown fat and strange in its jar. The grief of Maren Ostler, already beginning to change into something that some future buyer would purchase for purposes Hazel preferred not to imagine. Grief, aged properly, made a powerful base for courage. That was one of the first lessons Quillon had taught her, back when she was nine years old and newly arrived at the Gildhall with her mother's debt hanging around her neck like a millstone carved with her mother's name.

She did not think about her mother before noon. It was a discipline, like keeping the columns straight.

But the morning had a way of pulling thoughts where they shouldn't go, and today the light through the high windows was the exact color it had been the day she'd arrived, and so, despite herself, she thought it: *Eleven years. Eleven years of entries, and the principal has barely moved.*

Her mother's debt was not recorded in any ledger Hazel could touch. That was the first cruelty of it. It lived in a sealed folio in the master's own strongroom, a debt so old and so large that even Quillon spoke of it in hushed terms, and he was a man who spoke of selling fear without blinking. Hazel knew the shape of it, though—the total, the terms, the terrible arithmetic of interest compounding against a dead woman's name. Her mother had traded something enormous to save Ashvale, everyone said so. What exactly, no one would say. And the debt of it had come down to Hazel like an inheritance no one wanted, binding her to the Gildhall until it was paid.

She had done the sums a thousand times. At her wage, at the interest, at the rate the principal crawled downward—she would be an old woman before the last entry closed. Some nights she lay awake doing the arithmetic again as though the numbers might have changed overnight, as though mercy were a thing that accrued silently in the dark.

They did not change. Numbers never did. That was why she loved them, and why she hated them, and why she had become so very good at them: numbers were the only honest things in a building that trafficked in everything else.

"Hazel."

Master Quillon stood in the doorway of the counting room, and Hazel set down her pen with the smooth economy of long practice. He was a narrow man, dry as August grass, with silver at his temples and a chain of office that he wore the way a magistrate wears one—as though it had been riveted to him at birth. His eyes went first, as they always did, to the open ledger, then to her margins, and his mouth thinned a fraction at whatever he read there.

"You're recording feelings again," he said.

"I'm recording context," Hazel said. "Context protects the guild. When Maren Ostler's heirs come in twenty years demanding to know why their mother died content and poor, the entry will explain itself."

Quillon considered this. He had a merchant's patience, which was different from a kind man's patience; it simply lasted longer before giving out. "And the soldier?"

"Vasey's fear comes out of the vault Thursday. I've scheduled the weighing for low tide, when the vaults run quiet. It'll be easier on him." She paused. "It's grown, master. More than the standard drift. Eleven years is a long time. We should tell him what to expect."

"We should tell him what the contract says," Quillon said, "and nothing else." But there was no edge in it. This was an old argument between them, worn smooth as a river stone, and they both knew how it ended: Hazel told the clients anyway, quietly, at the door, in words that could not be entered into any ledger because they were spoken rather than written. Kindness, Quillon had once observed, was the one commodity the Gildhall gave away for free, and it cost more than anything else in the building.

He crossed the room and laid a folded paper beside her inkwell. "A buyer this afternoon. High-value inquiry. You'll take the meeting."

Hazel looked up. Buyers of a certain tier went to Quillon himself, or to the factors on the floor above. The counting room handled widows and soldiers and farmers trading a bad harvest's worth of luck. "What tier?"

"The kind you don't discuss in open rooms." His voice dropped. "The client wants a memory. Not to buy—a viewing only. A memory of the king's coronation, from the crowd's side of the rope. We hold three. They wish to experience one."

Hazel frowned. Memory viewings were common enough. The rich paid to feel young again through borrowed recollections; the grieving paid to stand one more hour inside a dead man's head. But coronation memories were odd stock—public events, felt by ten thousand people at once, worth little individually. "Why that one? There are cheaper ways to remember a parade."

"Because," Quillon said, "the client is paying with mint coin. Royal mint." He tapped the folded paper once, and Hazel understood that inside it was the sum—and that a sum drawn from the royal treasury, for a memory of the crown itself, was either a curiosity or a warning, and Quillon had not yet decided which.

"Terms?"

"Standard viewing. One hour inside the memory, no extraction, no copies. You'll conduct it. Your hand is the steadiest in the hall, and I want the entry written the same day." He was already turning toward the door. "And Hazel—" he stopped, half-turned, the light catching the silver chain, "—the margins on this one stay empty. Some entries want to be nothing but figures."

Then he was gone, and Hazel sat alone in the gold-dusted light with her heart beating faster than any entry warranted.

She looked at the ledger, at the widow's grief and the soldier's fear, at all the careful columns of impossible things, and she felt the old hunger rise in her—the one she kept fed and trained and mostly starved. It was not ambition, exactly, though the factors sometimes mistook it for that and smiled at her condescendingly. It was simpler and worse than ambition. It was the feeling of standing outside a lit window on a winter street, hands in pockets, watching people she did not know sit down to supper together.

The Gildhall was her window. She had worked in it since childhood; she knew its every stair and secret, its smells and its cruelties, and she was good here, useful here, necessary here. But necessary was not the same as chosen. She had never chosen the Gildhall. It had taken her the way a current takes a leaf—through her mother's debt, through the plague year, through circumstances written long before she could write her own name. Every morning she tied back her hair and lit her lamp and performed her competence flawlessly, and every morning, underneath, the same small voice: *You are excellent at a life you never picked.*

She wanted a belonging with her own signature on it. That was all. She wanted to walk into a room someday and be there because she had decided to be, not because a debt held her. She wanted margins she was allowed to fill.

The bell in the tower above the counting room struck the ninth hour, and downstairs a door opened—the front door, heavy oak banded in iron—and footsteps crossed the marble of the entry hall. Slow steps. Measured. The steps of someone accustomed to rooms falling quiet when they entered.

Hazel closed the ledger, squared it on the desk, and pulled the folded paper toward her. Royal coin. A memory of the king. Margins ordered empty.

She broke the seal, and the first thing she saw was not the sum at all but the wax that had sealed it—the impression pressed into it, fine and deep and unmistakable even to a girl who had never been within a mile of the palace: the crowned profile of King Aldric, rendered in a style she recognized from the coins in her own locked cashbox, except that this impression had been struck recently, and struck hard, and somewhere in the making of it something had slipped, so that the king's face in the wax seemed less crowned than swallowed, the crown pressing down into the flesh like a lid forced onto a jar.

Hazel stared at it for a long moment. Then she dipped her pen, opened the ledger to a fresh page, and began, in her small, precise hand, to prepare the entry for a buyer whose coin smelled, faintly and impossibly, of ash.

* * *

The buyer arrived at the fourth hour wearing a mask.

It was not the festival kind, painted and feathered, nor the physician's kind, beaked and grim. It was plain white porcelain, featureless except for eye-holes, and it covered the face entirely, so that when the figure swept into the counting room trailing the scent of cold stone and incense, Hazel found herself addressing a smooth blank oval that reflected the lamplight back at her like the surface of still water.

"Master Quillon receives me?" the figure asked. The voice was modulated, neither male nor female, pitched to be forgotten.

"The master sends his regrets and his trust," Hazel said, using the words Quillon had given her. "The viewing will be conducted under my hand. I am Hazel Sterling, keeper of entries."

A pause. The blank face tilted slightly, the way a bird tilts when it decides whether a thing is food or threat. "Young," the figure said. "The guild sends a child to handle the crown's business."

"I'm twenty-two," Hazel said, "and the guild sends whoever balances best." She gestured to the table prepared at the room's center: a single chair, a shallow basin of clear water, and, resting on a stand above it, a small leaded-glass vessel no larger than a sparrow's egg, within which something pale and slow moved like smoke that had learned patience.

Inside the vessel swam the memory.

Hazel had conducted forty-one viewings in her six years at the desk. She knew the ritual down to her breathing: the client seated, hands flat on the table; her own fingertips resting on the vessel's glass, bridging; the spoken words that were less magic than bookkeeping—a declaration of terms, a limiting clause, an accounting of duration. *One hour. No extraction. No copy. The memory returns to the vault whole.* The guild's neutrality lived in those clauses, Quillon always said. The Gildhall took no sides. It only kept accounts.

"State your purpose," she said now, "for the record."

There was a silence long enough that she glanced up from the ledger page. The mask regarded her. Behind the eye-holes, shadow.

"To see the king crowned," the figure said at last. "As he truly was. Before."

"*Before* what?" Hazel asked.

Another silence, and this one had a texture to it, a weight, like the pause of a person deciding how much of the truth a clerk can be trusted to carry. "Before the crowning," the figure said smoothly, "there was only a man. Afterward, there was a reign. I wish to witness the difference. Proceed, keeper."

Hazel hesitated. In eleven years of entries she had learned to hear the sound of a lie the way sailors hear weather in the rigging—not the words themselves but the space around them. There was a great deal of space around this answer.

But the coin was royal, the appointment was Quillon's, and the clauses were the clauses. She set her fingertips against the cool glass. The pale thing inside stirred toward her warmth like a plant toward sun.

"One hour," she said formally. "No extraction. No copy. The memory returns whole. Do you accept the terms?"

"I accept."

"Then look," Hazel said, and spoke the opening word of the viewing, the old word her teachers had taught her, the word that unlatched the vessel—

And the memory poured upward through the water and the air and into the masked figure across the table, and for one heartbeat—one single heartbeat before the client's consciousness sank into it—Hazel felt the edges of it brush her own mind, the way spilled wine touches the lip of a glass it wasn't poured for. Just a taste. Just the rim.

She saw a square packed with cheering thousands. Saw banners the color of new blood. Saw, rising above the crowd on a scaffold draped in white, a young man in a plain doublet waiting bareheaded for the crown—

—and she saw the crown coming down toward him carried not by a priest but by something else, something whose robes did not move quite right, something that bent over the young king's bowed head with the tenderness of a spider wrapping a fly, and the last thing the memory showed, in its final golden instant before the client drowned in it, was the young man's face looking up with an expression Hazel would spend the rest of this story trying to forget:

not joy.

Fear.

The vessel emptied. The masked figure sat motionless in the chair, breathing slowly, an hour passing in the counting room like water through fingers. Hazel sat with her pen poised over the entry and did not write. Her hand, for the first time in eleven years, was not steady.

When the hour ended, the figure rose without a word, set a small purse of mint-coin on the table—heavy, heavier than gold should be—and swept toward the door. At the threshold it paused.

"Keeper," it said, in that forgetting voice. "Your master will burn this entry. He will tell you the transaction never occurred. He will be right to, and you will be wise to let him." The blank porcelain caught the lamplight one last time. "But you copied the terms down before I arrived. I watched you do it. Consider what it means, that you couldn't help yourself."

The door closed. The footsteps faded. The counting room was silent except for the tick of cooling lamps and the sound of Hazel Sterling's own heart.

She looked down at the ledger. The entry page was blank, waiting for figures. And in her personal book—the thin, private volume she kept hidden beneath the false bottom of her desk drawer, the one no master had ever seen, where she copied things the ledger wasn't allowed to hold—she had already, sometime in the last hour without quite noticing her own hand doing it, written everything.

*Buyer unknown, masked, paid in royal mint. Purchased viewing of coronation memory, vault item 3-C. Warned that Quillon will order the entry burned.*

She looked at the words. Then, below them, in smaller letters, she added the thing she had actually seen in the spill of the memory, the image already fading at its edges like a dream at waking, but not gone yet, not yet gone:

*The king was afraid of the crown.*

Somewhere below her feet, in the humming dark of the vaults, forty days of rain waited in its copper cloud, and a soldier's fear grew fat in its jar, and the grief of a widow named Maren Ostler slowly turned into courage for someone else's war. Above her, in the tower, the bell rang the fifth hour. And in her chest, in the place where her hunger to belong had always lived, Hazel felt something shift and crack, small as ice on a pond in early spring—

because she had just realized that the first thing she had ever chosen in her life was a secret.
