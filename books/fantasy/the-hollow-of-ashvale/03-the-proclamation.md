## 3. The Proclamation

The bell in the Gildhall's tower rang the wrong hour.

Hazel noticed it first as a wrongness in her hands—her pen stuttering mid-figure, a blot of ink spreading like a bruise across the column of rain-contracts she had been balancing since dawn. The bell rang the hours for the city, and it rang them true; that was its whole purpose, the one trade the tower made freely. But now it was striking eleven when the sun stood barely past nine, and beneath its bronze voice came another sound, thinner and more terrible: voices. Many voices, raised in unison, moving through the streets like water finding every low place.

She set down her pen. Around her, the counting room had gone still in the particular way of rooms full of people pretending to work. Old Fenwick's abacus had stopped clicking. The two junior scribes by the window were pressed to the glass like children at a fair.

"Hazel." Master Quillon's voice from the doorway, quiet enough that only she heard it, which meant he wanted only her to hear it. "With me."

She rose. Her chair scraped, loud in the silence, and forty pairs of eyes tracked her across the counting room—past the shelves of ledgers bound in grey calfskin, past the iron cage where the luck-deposits slept in their little drawers, whispering faintly to anyone with ears attuned, which Hazel's were, always, whether she wished it or not.

Quillon did not take her to his office. He took her up the narrow stair to the gallery above the Gildhall's doors, where the tall windows looked out over Ledger Square, and pulled back the curtain just far enough for both of them to see.

The criers were coming up Copperlane in a line of six, spaced apart, each flanked by soldiers in the king's livery—the deep ash-grey that Ashvale had worn with pride for three hundred years, because ash was what remained when everything else burned away, and what remained was what endured. The soldiers did not look proud today. They looked like men walking beside something they'd rather not touch.

The nearest crier stopped at the fountain. Unrolled a scroll. And his voice, trained to carry across markets and mutinies alike, cracked out against the morning:

"By decree of His Majesty, sovereign of Ashvale and all its rivers and roads—" the pause, the breath, the words that would not stop being true once spoken "—the practice of unnatural trades is hereby declared heresy against the crown and gods. Bearers of the gift, dealers in borrowed fortune, sellers of memory and years: you are anathema. You will be surrendered to the crown's officers, taken to the pyres of Ashvale, and cleansed by fire, that the kingdom may be made whole."

He said it again. And again, further down the square, another crier said it, so that the edict arrived twice, overlapping, the words snagging on each other like torn cloth.

Hazel found she could not move.

It was not surprise. That was the strange thing she would remember afterward—not surprise but recognition, the dull click of a lock she had been hearing someone fumble with for months without letting herself name it. The decrees tightening over the winter. The tax levied on guilds dealing in *nonmaterial goods*, a phrase that had made Quillon laugh into his wine. The disappearance of the memory-broker on Tanner's Row, then the charm-seller on Bell Street, then the woman who sold safe childbirth in the Weft, none of them arrested so much as *absented*, their shops shuttered, their names no longer spoken.

She had recorded those absences herself. In the ledger, under *Accounts Closed*. She had written *closed* the way one writes *died*, and told herself it was the same neutral word.

"Master," she said. Her voice sounded far away. Below, a woman in the crowd—a washerwoman, by her basket—had begun to shout something back at the crier, and the soldiers were moving toward her, and the crowd around her were stepping away from her in a slow ripple, like water making room for a stone. "This can't stand. The trades are licensed. We hold charter—"

"The charter is parchment." Quillon let the curtain fall. In the dimness of the gallery his face was unreadable, which it usually was, but today the unreadability felt less like mastery and more like a door held shut. "Parchment burns faster than people, if it comes to that. Come downstairs. There is work."

---

There was work. There was, it turned out, a mountain of work, and Quillon set it before the counting room in the flat tone of a man assigning quarterly inventories, which somehow made it worse.

"All ledgers scrubbed," he said. "Every entry bearing a bearer's true name to be struck and replaced with the trade-mark only. No names, no addresses, no descriptions. You will work in pairs and you will finish by nightfall. The Gildhall has survived kings before. It will survive this one by being very boring."

"And the clients?" Fenwick asked. He was the oldest of them; his hands shook on his abacus beads, and he did not try to hide it. "The widow who sold her grief for the rains last spring. When the crown's officers come asking after her—"

"Then we will have no record of her, and neither will they." Quillon's gaze swept the room. "That is the point of scrubbing. We do not lie. We simply cease to know. A ledger that never knew cannot be tortured into confessing."

It was elegant. It was even, in its cold way, merciful—Hazel could see that, see the lives folded inside the strategy like seeds in a closed fist. But she had spent seven years learning to read this man the way she read ledgers, and there was an entry missing. A figure that should be there and wasn't.

Because scrubbing protected the clients. It did nothing for the bearers themselves—the gifted, whose names lived elsewhere than in the ledgers. Whose names lived in the undercroft rolls, in the guild's registry of bonded talent, in the great master index that Quillon alone controlled.

"Master Quillon." She kept her voice level, scribe's-flat, the voice that carried no interest and therefore invited none. "The registry. If the ledgers are struck, the registry remains. It contains every bearer's name and binding-house in Ashvale. Four hundred souls, give or take."

For a moment—one breath, no longer—something moved behind Quillon's eyes. Not guilt. Hazel had seen guilt on him once, years ago, over a mispriced second chance, and it had a specific weather. This was different. This was calculation arriving somewhere it didn't want to be.

"The registry will be dealt with separately," he said.

"Dealt with how?"

"With, Sterling." He turned away. "Scrub your section. Begin with the rain contracts."

---

She began with the rain contracts. She was, whatever else was true, good at this, and the work took her hands while her mind ran elsewhere, which was perhaps why masters liked having her.

But her mind kept returning to the square. To the washerwoman shouting, and the crowd stepping away from her. That was the thing about an edict—it didn't need to convince anyone. It only needed to make standing near a condemned person feel like standing in rising water. By evening, half of Ashvale would have rehearsed the step backward. By week's end it would be reflex.

And the bell. She kept hearing the bell ringing the wrong hour, and understanding, slowly, what she should have understood at once: the Gildhall rang that bell. The tower was theirs, leased from the city, rung by a guild servant every hour of every day. Someone had rung it wrong on purpose—or been made to ring it wrong—and she did not know which possibility frightened her more. That the guild was warning its own. Or that someone outside the guild had already reached inside far enough to bend its bells.

By midafternoon the junior scribes were crying quietly over their columns and pretending not to be, and Fenwick had taken to mutting old trade-prayers under his breath, the ones from before the guild went respectable. *What is given freely returns freely. What is written may be unwritten.* Hazel wrote *struck, struck, struck* through name after name and felt each stroke like a small amputation.

At dusk Quillon sent for her again.

---

His office sat at the heart of the Gildhall, walled in ledger-shelves the way a heart is walled in bone. He was standing at the window when she entered, watching the first smoke rise from somewhere beyond the Weft—not a pyre yet, probably, probably just a chimney—but watching it anyway, too long, the way a man watches a wound he expects to reopen.

"You'll compile the list," he said, without turning.

The words took a moment to find meaning. "The list?"

"Of bearers." Now he turned, and his face was the ledger-face, balanced, neutral, every figure accounted for. "The crown has requested, through channels, an accounting of bonded talent within the city. Names, trades, residences. They will conduct their own... verifications. We needn't involve ourselves beyond the compilation."

"You're giving them the registry."

"I'm giving them *a* list." He crossed to his desk and laid a single sheet upon it, blank except for the guild's seal at the head. "The registry itself stays here. What leaves is a copy, in a trusted hand, vetted. Yours."

The trust of it landed like a blow, and she understood a heartbeat later that it was meant to—that trust was the leash. *You will write the list yourself,* said the gesture, *so that whatever happens next belongs to your hand and not mine.*

"Master." She made herself look at the blank page instead of him. "Those are people. The candle-seller on Weft Lane who sells light that keeps nightmares honest. The ferryman who takes coin in remembered summers. I've recorded their trades. Some of them I've—met. They come to this building. They trust this building."

"They trusted the crown once, too, and look how that aged." Quillon sat, folding his hands, and for a long moment studied her with the patience of a man reading a difficult contract. "Hazel. I will say this once, and then we will speak of it no more. The Gildhall has stood four hundred years because it understands what things cost. Empires burn their witches and then, ten years on, quietly need a blessing for the queen's childbirth, and the wise guild is the one that still exists to sell it. Fire passes. Ledgers endure. Our task is to be the ledger."

"And the people on the list?"

"Are entries."

The word hung between them. She watched him watch her take it, and she thought—he must have thought she was swallowing it, because something in his shoulders eased, a fractional settling, the relief of a creditor seeing payment accepted.

"One more matter," he said. "Your mother's debt."

Her breath stopped. Of course he would use it. He had held it over her for seven years—the standing obligation, her mother's final trade gone bad, the sum that bound Hazel to the Gildhall until paid, unable to leave, unable to refuse work. It had always been abstract before. A figure in a ledger. Tonight it was a hand on the back of her neck.

"She left you that obligation, and obligations are honored in this house," Quillon said gently, which was the cruelest register he owned. "You will have the list complete in three days. Clean hand, accurate figures, no sentiment. Do this, and I'll strike a fifth of the principal. Consider it hazard pay."

He slid the blank sheet across the desk toward her.

Hazel picked it up. Her hands were steady—she noted this with a strange detachment, the scribe in her observing the instrument performing well under load. She bowed, because one bowed in this house. She walked to the door.

"Sterling." She paused. He was looking at her again with that ledger-face, and now, finally, she could read the missing entry, the one that hadn't appeared in the gallery or the counting room or this office. Quillon was afraid. Not of the crown. Of her. Of what she might do with a list and three days and a steady hand. "You understand what I'm asking."

"Yes, master," she said. "You're asking me to write the fire's shopping order in my own hand."

She closed the door before he could answer, and stood a moment in the corridor with her heart slamming and the blank page trembling slightly now, as if the paper itself had understood before she had what it was for.

Three days. A fifth off the debt. Her mother's chain shortened by exactly the length of four hundred lives.

Hazel walked back down to the counting room, where the lamps were being lit and the scrubbing continued entry by entry, name by name, the guild erasing itself into safety. She sat at her desk. She squared the blank page. She dipped her pen.

And she began—to the empty air, to the shelf of grey ledgers, to whoever might someday read the true account—to draft the list of names in her own hand.

* * *

The first name she wrote was real. She needed to know what it cost to write one.

*Maren Duskwell, charm-bearer, Weft Lane, sells safe passage over ice.*

She knew Maren. Everyone in the counting room knew of Maren; her charms came through the guild twice a year, traded honestly, priced fairly, a small bright thread in the great fabric. Writing her name felt like pressing a coal into wet paper—a hiss, a scorch, a smell of burning that existed nowhere but in Hazel's chest.

She looked at the name for a long time. Then she drew a line through it—not a scrub-stroke, not the official double-struck of cancellation, but a single line, quiet, deniable—and beneath it wrote a name from the guild's death-rolls, a charm-bearer dead eight winters, whose trade-mark was close enough that a clerk in a hurry might confuse them.

The substitution took four seconds. Checking the rolls to confirm the dead woman's mark, six minutes. Returning to the page with her heart going like a hammer in a bell-tower, and writing the second false name, and the third, and understanding as she worked that she had crossed a line that did not go back—perhaps thirty seconds total, thirty seconds between the person she had been that morning and the person sharpening a quill at midnight—but that the crossing had been almost painless, which was the most frightening part. Sin, she had always assumed, would feel heavier.

Outside, the wrong-hour bell rang midnight, and no one corrected it.

She worked until the candles drowned. By the third day she had compiled, in a beautiful clean hand, a list of two hundred and eleven names—every one of them belonging to a bearer already dead, already fled, or already erased from the world so thoroughly that even the crown's inquisitors would find nothing but dust and confusion. Two hundred and eleven ghosts marching to a pyre that couldn't touch them. And threaded invisibly through the false list, known only to her, the true names of the living—four hundred souls, give or take—nowhere written at all.

On the morning of the third day she carried the finished pages up to Quillon's office and laid them on his desk, and watched him leaf through them with the satisfaction of a man reviewing honest work.

"Good," he said. "Accurate. You've done your mother credit."

Hazel bowed. And as she turned to go, her eye caught the corner of his desk, where a letter lay open under a paperweight of river-glass—crown wax, broken seal, and visible even upside-down, in a fine bureaucratic hand, a line she would carry for the rest of her life:

*...the crown thanks the Gildhall for its cooperation, and reminds the guildmaster that the verification of names will commence with the guild's own servants...*

The hunt was not coming for Ashvale's bearers first.

It was coming for the Gildhall.
