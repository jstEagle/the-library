## 4. Names in Her Own Hand

The list was to be written in triplicate.

Quillon said it the way he said everything—flatly, precisely, as though the words had been weighed on his scales and found to balance exactly. *Three copies, Hazel. One for the Gildhall's archive, one for the magistrate, one for the crown's inquisitor when he arrives.* He set a fresh quire of paper on her desk, the good paper, the heavy stock reserved for contracts that could not be allowed to smudge or fade. Contracts that were meant to last.

"Names," he said. "Bearers within the city walls and the river wards. Trades performed in the last ten years, where known. Addresses where you have them."

Hazel looked at the paper. It was very white. "You want me to write down who they are."

"I want you to write down what we know." Quillon adjusted the chain of office at his throat, a small habitual motion, gold links whispering against each other. "The guild has always kept accurate records. That accuracy is why we will survive this edict. The crown does not burn institutions that are useful to it."

"Useful," Hazel repeated.

"Compliant," Quillon corrected, without a flicker of shame. "Useful is what compliant looks like from the inside." He paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and did not turn all the way around when he spoke again. "Your mother wrote lists too, once. During the fever years. She understood that a ledger is not a weapon unless someone makes it one. Keep it a ledger, Hazel. Keep it clean."

Then he was gone, and the counting room settled into its familiar hush—the tick of the great clock, the murmur of clerks beyond the partition, the smell of ink and tallow and old vellum. Everything as it always was. Everything changed.

Hazel sat before the blank page for a long time.

She knew these names. That was the thing Quillon either did not understand or did not care about: she did not know them as entries. She knew them as people. Marta Voss, the ferrywoman who traded three hours of her wedding day for calm water every spring flood, so the crossing would hold. Old Tam Reeve, who sold his stutter to a merchant's son and had been fluent ever since, and lonely ever since, because the stutter had been half his voice and all his humor. The sisters at the dye-works who pooled their small gift to color cloth the blue of a summer sky no one in Ashvale had seen in years. She had recorded their trades. She had watched them sign their names with the particular careful pride of people doing something wondrous and getting paid fairly for it.

And now her hand, her trained, obedient, excellent hand, was supposed to feed them to the pyres.

She thought of the masked buyer. Of the coin stamped with the royal mint, sitting in Quillon's strongbox like a spider in a jar. Of the coronation memory burned out of the ledger rather than recorded—and she had copied it anyway, that night, into her own private book, hidden behind the loose brick by the hearth. She had told herself then that it was an accident of conscience. A scribe's compulsion toward completeness.

It had not been an accident. It had been the first stone of a wall she was only now realizing she was building.

*Keep it a ledger,* Quillon had said. *Keep it clean.*

Hazel dipped her pen.

She began with the dead.

There were plenty of them. Ten years of entries meant ten years of bearers whose trades had run their course, whose bargains had closed, whose names already carried the quiet black line of completion in the guild's master rolls. Tam Reeve had died two winters back—a bad chest, the apothecary said, nothing uncanny. Marta Voss had drowned the year before that, in ordinary water, on an ordinary day, having sold nothing but her own grief to buy the rain that ended the drought. The dye-sisters had gone north. Or had died. The entry was ambiguous; ambiguity was a door, and Hazel walked through it.

She wrote the dead men's names in her beautiful, unimpeachable hand. She attached them to addresses where no one lived, or where living people had never borne the gift at all—the widow Corren, who was eighty and blind and would be terrified if soldiers came, but who was also, Hazel noted with grim satisfaction, the niece of a magistrate. She dated trades that had never happened. She invented middle initials. She built, stroke by careful stroke, a list that was a masterpiece of accuracy in form and a lie in every line that mattered.

Her heart hammered so hard she could hear it in her ears. This was forgery. This was treason under the new edict, and worse than treason under the old law, because the guild's ledgers were binding things—words written here had weight, had consequence, had a way of becoming true. If she was caught, they would not simply hang her. They would unmake whatever bargain had made her, and there was always a bargain. Everyone in the Gildhall was bound to something.

But oh—the thrill of it. She had balanced other people's debts for eleven years, had made the impossible arithmetic come out even, had never once moved a number to suit herself. And now her pen flew across the page and every stroke was hers, chosen, defiant, alive. It felt like the moment on the ice when the river cracks and you understand that you are standing on something that moves, that has always been moving, and that the stillness was the illusion all along.

By the time the evening bell rang, the list was done. Three copies. Flawless. Dead men marched down every page in her mother's handwriting—because it was her mother's hand, everyone said so, the same slant, the same severe little crosses on the t's—and somehow that made it feel less like betrayal and more like inheritance.

She blotted the pages. She squared their edges. She delivered two to Quillon's desk and locked the third in the archive herself, and her hands did not shake until afterward, alone in the stairwell, when they shook so badly she had to grip the rail.

* * *

The candle-seller worked the lane behind the tanneries, where the light failed early and the smells covered everything.

His name—he gave it reluctantly, watching the alley mouth the whole while—was Bram. He was perhaps twenty-five, thin in the way of men who ate last, with ink-stained fingers and a candle-seller's cart that held more candles than any honest trade required, because half of them were not candles at all. They were vessels. Little wax houses for borrowed light: an hour of courage, a night of dreamless sleep, the warmth of a hearth you'd left behind, sold in stubs to those who could pay almost nothing.

He had bought fear off a soldier once, the way you'd buy it off anyone—at interest, secondhand, through a broker—and the soldier had slept well for the first time in a year, and Bram had spent six months learning what terror felt like from the inside before he could sell it off in small doses. He talked about it matter-of-factly, the way Hazel recorded trades, and she recognized the tone immediately. It was her own voice. The voice of people who handled impossible things carefully so the impossibility wouldn't spill.

She had sought him out deliberately. The falsified list would hold—she was sure of it, mostly—but the list was only what the guild knew, and the guild did not know everyone. There were bearers outside the books. Bearers like Bram, trading in alleys, beneath notice, which had always meant beneath protection and now meant beneath warning.

"You're the Gildhall scribe," he said, not quite a question. He didn't offer her a candle. He didn't offer her anything. His eyes kept going to the alley mouth.

"I'm the woman who writes down what the guild knows," Hazel said. "Which is why I'm telling you what I'm about to tell you, and why you should tell everyone like you."

"The proclamation." He spat, delicately, into the gutter. "Half the lane heard it by noon. *Anathema, abomination, cleansed by holy fire.* The criers got the good dramatic pauses in. Someone threw a fish at the second one."

"They'll come with lists of their own eventually. Inquisitors make their own lists—it's how they justify their budgets. But mine buys time. Weeks, maybe a month." She kept her voice low and level, a clerk's voice, delivering figures. "If anyone asks you to sign anything, don't. If anyone asks where you learned your trade, you learned it from a dead man named Tam Reeve, who lived on Candlemaker's Row and died of a bad chest two winters ago. You learned it from Marta Voss, drowned. From a dozen others I can name for you, all of them dead or gone, none of them findable. Do you understand?"

Bram stared at her. For the first time he stopped watching the alley mouth and looked at her instead, really looked, the way you look at a card you suspect has been switched.

"You falsified it," he said slowly. "The list. The Gildhall's own scribe handed the crown a list of corpses."

"I handed the crown an accurate record of the guild's completed accounts," Hazel said. "Every name is genuine. Every death is genuine. The dates are... interpretive."

Something happened to Bram's face then—not quite a smile, more the place a smile would live if it could afford the rent. "Interpretive," he repeated, tasting the word. "Gods. Eleven years I've sold light in this city and that's the bravest sentence I've ever heard."

"It wasn't brave. It was—" She stopped. What was it? Necessary. Obvious. The only entry that balanced. "It was bookkeeping," she finished.

"No," Bram said quietly. "Bookkeeping is what Quillon did. What you did was spend yourself. I know the difference. I've sold enough courage to know what it costs the seller." He reached into the cart and drew out a candle—not a vessel, a plain tallow thing, cheap and honest—and lit it from the lantern hanging off the cart's hook, and held it out to her. "For the walk home. The lanes are dark, and dark lanes are about to get darker."

Hazel took it. The flame wavered between them, throwing both their shadows long against the tannery wall, two shapes leaning toward each other like the halves of a broken thing deciding to stand.

"Why warn me?" she asked. "You don't know me. I'm the guild's hand. By rights you should hate me."

"I did hate you. All of you, a little. Guild keeps its books, takes its cut, stays neutral while the rest of us drown." He shrugged. "Neutral's over. The crown made sure of that. So now there's just people who feed the fire and people who don't." He nodded at the candle in her hand. "You don't."

* * *

*They came for the candle-cart on the fourth night.*

*Bram had been expecting it the way you expect rain—you can't say when, but you know the sky. Two of the watch, and behind them a man in grey with a white armband, one of the new inquisitor's readers, the kind who could taste a bargain on the air like salt before a storm. Bram saw them turn into the lane and his hands went cold around the cart handles, and the stupidest thought went through his head: *I owe rent on the stall through month's end.*

*"Candle-seller," the reader said. Not a question. Readers never asked questions; they pronounced findings. "You carry more than wax."*

*Bram's whole life narrowed to the space between the cart and the wall. Run and they'd chase, and a chased man runs straight to wherever his gift shows brightest. Stand and be taken, and the pyres at Duskfield had already been stacked—he'd seen the wood going up that morning, neat as cordwood, the workmen laughing about something. There was no good door. There was only the least bad one.*

*He chose the wall. He ducked low, grabbed the false-bottomed drawer where he kept the raw stock—the fear, the courage, the little stolen comforts—and hurled the whole tray at the cobbles. Wax shattered. And everything in it woke up.*

*You couldn't do that, normally. You weren't supposed to break vessels; the contents went everywhere, undirected, hungry. An hour of courage hit the lead watchman like a wave and he bellowed and charged his own partner. A night of dreamless sleep caught the second man mid-shout and folded him gently to the ground, snoring. The reader staggered under a wash of somebody's dead grandmother's kitchen-warmth and stood blinking, tears running down his face, smelling bread he hadn't smelled in thirty years.*

*Bram ran.*

*He ran the way the lane bent, past the tannery, over the fence he'd climbed a hundred times, into the yard behind the chandler's shop—and the yard was not empty. A figure stepped out of the shadow of the rain barrels and caught his arm, and he nearly broke something else in panic before the lamplight found her face.*

*The scribe. The Gildhall woman. With a hood up and a second hood bundled under her arm and her eyes absolutely steady.*

*"There's a cellar door on the far side," she said. "I checked it yesterday. Move."*

*Later—much later, in the dark of a safehouse that smelled of onions and lamp oil, with his hands finally warm again—Bram would ask her why. Why risk it, why come at all, why burn her safe gray life for a candle-man she'd spoken to twice. And she would think about it honestly, the way she thought about everything, and say: "Because I wrote your name down somewhere in my head, and I've decided my head is the only ledger I trust." But that was later. That night she just pulled him through the cellar door and barred it behind them, and in the darkness she was shaking, and so was he, and neither of them pretended otherwise.*

*"That was insane," he whispered.*

*"Yes," she whispered back. There was a pause. Then, with the ghost of something that might someday become laughter: "I find I prefer it to compliance."*

*Above them, boots crossed the yard. The reader's voice, hoarse and wondering: "...smelled like her kitchen. Gods. Whose kitchen was that?"*

*And below, in the onion-smelling dark, the guild's scribe and the candle-seller sat shoulder to shoulder against the cold stone, two names that existed on no list anywhere, and listened to the hunt pass over their heads like weather.*

*Somewhere across the city, Duskfield's pyres waited for their first burning. Somewhere in the Gildhall, Quillon read her flawless list of dead men and said nothing, and Hazel would give a great deal, in the weeks to come, to know what passed behind his eyes in that silence. But that night there was only the cellar, and the shared dark, and the strange fierce discovery—new to her, older than cities to him—that a person you chose was worth more than every name you'd ever been ordered to write.*
