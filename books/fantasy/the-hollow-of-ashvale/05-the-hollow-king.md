## 5. The Hollow King

The candle-seller's shop sat where the Lantern Row bent toward the river, wedged between a chandler's warehouse and a house that had been leaning for so long the neighbors had stopped noticing. From the outside it was nothing—a crooked door, a window fogged with wax-smell and tallow dust, a hand-painted sign whose letters had faded to ghosts: *BRAMWELL'S CANDLES, LIGHT FOR EVERY PURPOSE.*

Inside, there were candles for everything.

Hazel had never imagined light could be catalogued like debt. Candles in rows along every wall, on shelves that climbed to the rafters, each bundle tagged with a scrap of paper in a cramped, cheerful hand. *For grief.* *For courage before a hard conversation.* *For remembering what you chose to forget.* *For forgetting what you cannot bear to remember.* The air was thick with the smell of beeswax and something underneath it—river water, ash, old paper—the smell of Ashvale itself rendered into fat and wick.

"You keep an inventory," Hazel said, because her hands needed something to do and her mouth needed somewhere to hide.

"Everyone keeps an inventory." Bram—she'd finally gotten the name out of him on the walk over; he'd introduced himself twice, flustered, as if he couldn't quite believe she wanted it—"lit the lamp above his workbench. "Mine just happens to be made of things people burned." He glanced at her sidelong. "Yours is made of things people lost. I don't know which of us should feel worse."

"I've been asking myself that since I met you."

He laughed—a small, startled sound, like he hadn't expected to make it—and turned back to the bench. His fingers were quick there, surer than they'd been in the street. This was his counting room, she realized. This cluttered little shop with its sagging shelves was his Gildhall, and here he was not the frightened man who had pressed a stub of candle into her hand behind a shuttered stall. Here he knew exactly what he was holding.

She still didn't. She'd carried the candle-stub home two nights ago and told herself it was nothing—a street vendor's gift, a superstition, a piece of tallow shaped like a promise. But she hadn't thrown it away. It sat on her desk beside her ledgers, and every time she looked up from her columns she found her eyes going to it, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"The list," Bram said quietly. He didn't look at her when he said it. He trimmed a wick with tiny silver scissors, snip by careful snip. "The names Quillon had you write for the criers' rolls. The ones they're reading from now, all down the Processional, while the pyre-crews stack wood on Coronation Green."

"What about them?"

"They're dead men."

The scissors paused. "You know that."

"I know that better than you do, maybe. Old Tam Fletcher was my neighbor for eleven years. Died of the lung-rot three winters back. If Tam Fletcher's name is being read from a crier's roll, then either the crown has learned to burn corpses, or someone wrote those names knowing they'd buy time for the living ones that weren't written." He set the scissors down and finally looked at her. His eyes were brown and ordinary and utterly unblinking. "Someone falsified a hunt-roll. In the Gildhall. With the guildmaster's seal on it."

Hazel said nothing. There was no version of silence that would help her, and no version of speech that wouldn't hang her, so she stood among the candles and let the quiet stretch.

"You did it," Bram said. Not accusing. Wondering, almost. As if she were a trade too strange to appear even in his inventory. "A Gildhall scribe. Debt-bound since you were what, twelve? You took your master's order, and you threw it in the fire."

"I wrote different names," Hazel said. "That's all. Anyone can copy words wrong."

"No," Bram said. "Anyone can copy words wrong. Almost no one copies them wrong *on purpose*, with the seal wet under their hand, knowing the rope that waits for scribes who falsify rolls." He shook his head slowly. "I've been trying to figure out why. Why you'd risk it for strangers. Then I remembered where I'd seen you before."

"Where?"

"The night of the coronation anniversary, two years past. You came down Lantern Row after dark, alone, buying nothing, looking at everything. And there was a boy selling roasted chestnuts near my stall—young, maybe fourteen—and a pair of the king's wardens decided he smelled of the gift." Bram's voice went flat and careful. "They were going to take him. And a woman in a gray clerk's coat stepped out of the crowd and asked them, very politely, whether their writ covered chestnut-sellers or only registered bearers, and whether they could produce the registry entry, because she happened to be from the Gildhall and would be obliged to file a discrepancy report, and discrepancies took *weeks* to resolve, and meanwhile the boy would have to be held in the warden-house, which the city pays for by the day."

"And they let him go," Hazel said. She remembered. She remembered the wardens' faces, the particular anger of men who had been inconvenienced by paperwork. She remembered walking home with her heart slamming against her ribs and telling herself she'd done it because a discrepancy report was easier than watching a child be dragged into a cart. That was the story she'd filed in her own ledger. She'd believed it until recently.

"They let him go," Bram agreed. "And I thought: there's a woman who knows exactly how much a word costs and how much a life costs, and she's doing arithmetic in public." He reached beneath the bench and drew out a box. Long, shallow, cedar, older than the shop around it—the wood gone dark and soft at the corners from a century of handling. "So. You saved him. Maybe you meant it as a kindness. Maybe it was bookkeeping. Either way, I think you deserve to see what you're actually keeping accounts for."

He opened the box. Inside, nested in rotted velvet, lay a single candle.

It was unlike any other in the shop. It wasn't white or cream or the honey-gold of good beeswax but a deep, bruised purple-black, like the sky ten minutes after sunset, and it seemed to drink the lamplight rather than receive it. The wick was silver. Actual silver, fine as thread.

"What is it?" Hazel asked. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, smaller than usual.

"A memory-candle," Bram said. "The oldest kind. Before the Gildhall standardized the trade—before luck and second chances got stamped and shelved—bearers used to pour memories straight into wax. What burns comes back. Whoever holds the flame sees what the maker saw." He lifted it out with both hands, reverent as a priest. "This one was poured ninety years ago. By a woman who worked in the palace. A laundress, they say. She saw something she wasn't supposed to see, and she couldn't carry it alone, so she put it in wax and gave it away, and it's been passed hand to hand ever since, bearer to bearer, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone who reads ledgers." Bram set it on the bench between them. "Because this isn't a memory of a birthday or a first kiss, Hazel Sterling. It's a record. And records want readers."

The shop felt suddenly very small, and very warm, and full of light that wasn't light. Hazel looked at the dark candle and felt the same pull she felt toward an unopened ledger—that itch beneath the sternum, the hunger to know what was owed and to whom. She had spent her whole life reading other people's entries. Grief sold for rain. Fear bought back at interest. Years traded away by people who could not afford to keep them.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

Bram struck the flame himself.

The wick caught without sound, and the light that bloomed from it was wrong—not gold but deep violet, the color of dusk underwater—and the shop walls, the shelves, the river-smell, all of it fell away like a sheet pulled off furniture.

Hazel was standing in a corridor.

No—not standing. She was *seeing* through another's eyes, low to the ground the way a servant's eyes go, moving quick and quiet along a passage paneled in pale birchwood, carrying a basket of linens. Torchlight through lattice windows striped the floor. Somewhere ahead, doors stood open onto a great hall, and from the hall came music—trumpets, drums, the slow processional grind of a coronation.

*Ninety years,* she reminded herself. *This is ninety years ago.* But the corridor smelled real. The basket handle bit into the laundress's palms. Hazel could feel the woman's heart, quick with borrowed fear, and beneath the fear something worse: awe. The awful, animal awe of the small in the presence of the crowned.

The laundress edged closer to the hall doors. Peered through.

And Hazel saw the king.

He knelt on the coronation steps in cloth-of-gold, head bowed, and the Archbishop of Ashvale lowered the crown toward him—and Hazel's first thought, absurdly, was that the crown was *heavy*. Wrong-heavy. It hung in the archbishop's hands like a stone sinks in water, like it wanted to descend, and when it settled onto the young king's brow the man's whole body jerked as if a hook had caught him behind the eyes—

—and then the vision *turned*, the way a key turns, and Hazel saw what the laundress could not have seen, what the candle itself had been made to hold, the thing the laundress's gift had shown her though she begged it to stop:

The king was empty.

Not dead. Not sleeping. *Empty.* When Hazel looked at him now—truly looked, with the candle's sight—there was nothing behind his face. No fear, no hope, no flicker of the man who must once have been a boy learning swordwork and statecraft. He was a shape wearing skin the way a glove wears a hand. His eyes were open and lit with a soft amber glow that came from *behind* them, from inside the skull, like windows of a house at night where every room has been emptied and one lamp left burning in the hall.

And the crown—

The crown was not resting on his head. It was *rooted* there. Fine dark filaments, hair-thin, ran from the circlet's inner band down into his temples, into the sockets of his eyes, down the column of his throat, spreading through him like frost through glass. And along those filaments, Hazel could see it moving: light. A thin golden stream, drawn upward, drop by drop, year by year, out of the hollow king and *up into the crown*, where it gathered and coiled and waited like water in a cupped palm.

The vision shifted. The laundress fled down the corridor, and time stuttered forward the way memory does—days folding into weeks—and she was back, months later, hiding behind a pillar in the throne room, watching petitioners approach the king. And Hazel watched a petitioner kneel: a young woman, plain-dressed, nervous, and as she stammered her request, something in her *flickered*. A faint golden shimmer rose off her shoulders like heat off summer stone, thin as breath, and streamed invisibly across the marble floor and up into the seated king, into the crown, and the girl swayed slightly, blinked, and could not afterward remember why she had come.

*The hunt,* Hazel thought, and the understanding arrived not as a thought but as a cold detonation behind her ribs. *The hunt for the gifted. The pyres. The edict.*

It wasn't piety. It wasn't policy. It was *feeding*. The crown consumed the years of the gifted—their luck, their small miracles, the strange bright surplus they carried—and it had been drinking from the kingdom drop by drop for ninety years. And now, with the gifted hunted down and burned, the crown needn't sip. It could *feast*. Every bearer dragged to the pyres was a cup brought brimming to the royal table. Sereth's inquisition, the criers, the stacks of kindling on Coronation Green—all of it, all of it was the crown feeding itself, and calling the meal righteousness.

The vision lurched again. The laundress, older now, grayer, standing in a cellar with three others, whispering. Hazel caught fragments—*the bargain, someone signed for us, someone bound the king, find the original entry*—and then the woman was pouring wax by candlelight, weeping, murmuring into the cooling tallow words that hardened into the vision Hazel stood inside now, and the last image was the laundress's own face, close and terrified and resolute, mouthing: *Tell whoever holds this. Tell them it isn't the king. Tell them it never was.*

The violet light guttered.

Hazel came back to the shop all at once, gasping, gripping the edge of the workbench hard enough to whiten her knuckles. The candle had burned down perhaps a finger's width. Bram steadied her elbow without a word, and she let him, because her knees had opinions of their own.

"It's not the king," she said. Her voice came out scraped raw. "The man on the throne. There's nothing—it's like a house where they've carried out all the furniture and left one lamp burning. The crown is—" She stopped. Started again. "The crown is drinking him. It drank him dry ninety years ago and it's been drinking everyone else since. Through the bargain. Whatever bargain keeps him alive keeps the crown fed, and now the edict, the pyres—they're not hunting heretics, they're *harvesting*—"

"Yes," Bram said simply.

"All those names." Hazel's stomach turned over. She thought of her own falsified roll, the dead men's names that had bought a few weeks of grace, and felt the small pride of it curdle into something colder. She hadn't stopped anything. She'd slowed a conveyor. "All those people on the Green, the ones already taken—"

"Fuel," Bram said. He said it gently, the way you tell someone a patient is dying. "That's what the old bearers called them, before anyone dared say it aloud. Fuel for the crown." He pinched out the candle's flame with wet fingers, and the shop snapped back to ordinary lamplight, ordinary shadows, the ordinary world—which was somehow worse now, because Hazel knew what moved underneath it. "The laundress tried to warn people. No one listened. The Gildhall certainly didn't listen."

"Why not?" The question came out sharp, and she didn't soften it. "If the guild knew—if Quillon knew—the guild trades in *memories*. Someone in that building must have seen what I just saw. Someone must have held a candle like yours."

"Oh, someone held it." Something complicated crossed Bram's face—anger banked so long it had gone to coal. "The Gildhall bought the laundress's testimony forty years ago. Paid her three silver for it. Filed it under *curiosities, unverifiable*." He tapped the cedar box. "This candle is the copy she kept for herself. The original entry sits in your master's sealed archive, gathering dust, while the guild collects its charter fees and looks the other way."

Hazel stood very still.

Forty years. The Gildhall had held the truth of the hollow king in its own vaults for forty years, stamped and shelved and declared unverifiable, and Master Quillon—he had been guildmaster for twenty-two of those years—Quillon had signed the edict's compliance orders with a steady hand, had ordered the ledgers scrubbed, had put the hunt-roll into *her* hands and told her to compile the names. Had he known? Had he sat in his office above the counting room, weighing charter against conscience, and chosen charter?

Or had he chosen something else entirely? Because a thought was assembling itself in her mind now, unbidden, unwelcome, taking shape the way a figure takes shape in fog: Quillon had not merely complied with the edict. He had been *efficient* with it. He'd scrubbed the books before the criers finished announcing. He'd known which servants bore the gift without consulting any register. He'd ordered the lists compiled by the one scribe in the Gildhall who was debt-bound, isolated, and too obedient to ask questions—or so he'd believed.

You didn't get that efficient overnight. You got that efficient through *practice*.

"Hazel?" Bram was watching her. "You've gone the color of your ledgers."

"How long," she said slowly, "has the Gildhall been supplying the crown with bearers?"

Bram didn't answer right away. He picked up the dark candle, wrapped it in cloth with the tenderness of a man burying a friend, and when he spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"Long enough that when I heard a Gildhall scribe had falsified a hunt-roll, I didn't wonder whether she'd survive it. I wondered how long it would take the guildmaster to notice the arithmetic was wrong." He looked up at her. "He'll notice, Hazel. Men like Quillon always notice the ledger. And when he does, he won't come asking questions. He'll come with the wardens, because that's the trade he knows best."

Outside, far off across the rooftops toward Coronation Green, a bell began to toll—the dusk bell, marking the hour when the pyres were lit. Hazel stood in the small warm shop full of candles for every purpose and understood, with terrible clarity, that there was no candle on any shelf for what she needed next.

"We have to move," she said. "Before he notices the arithmetic. There's something in the sealed archives I have to find—my mother's last entry. And I think—" her voice caught, steadied, "—I think Quillon has known it was there all along."
