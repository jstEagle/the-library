## 6. Quillon's Bargain

The charter hung behind Quillon's desk in a frame of blackened silver, and every morning for thirty-one years he had looked at it before he looked at anything else.

It was the first thing he saw when he came up from his rooms below the Gildhall, and the last thing he saw at night, when he doused the lamps and stood a moment in the dark of his office listening to the building settle around him like an old animal. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea. The royal seal—wax and gold leaf, pressed by a chancellor three kings dead—still caught the light from the river windows. *The Guild of Ashvale is licensed to keep and trade in those things which lie outside the common coin,* it read, in script so ornate it was nearly a drawing. *So long as the Guild serves the peace of the realm.*

*So long as the Guild serves.*

Quillon poured himself a measure of plum brandy—it was not yet noon, but the arithmetic of his life had long since stopped caring what hour it was—and sat down at the desk where he had spent more years than he had spent anywhere else on earth, including the house where he'd been born.

Below him, through the floor, he could hear the counting room going about its morning. The scratch of pens. The soft chime of the trade-bell. Hazel Sterling's voice, low and even, correcting some junior scribe's entry with that particular patience she had—the patience of a woman who had learned early that the world did not forgive errors twice.

He had watched her grow up in this building. He had signed the indenture papers herself her mother had left, along with the debt, along with the girl. He had taught her double-entry bookkeeping and the seven signs of a forged memory and the rule above all rules: *the ledger records; the ledger does not judge.*

She believed in that rule. That was the trouble with Hazel. She believed in it the way other people believed in gods—completely, without ever asking who wrote the scripture, or why, or what the writer got out of it.

Quillon drank, and looked at the charter, and thought about the list.

* * *

He had known about the crown's hunger for eleven years, ever since the night a man wearing the face of Chancellor Vess had come to the Gildhall after closing and asked to see him privately.

Vess had been dead eight months by then. Quillon had attended the funeral. He remembered the coffin, the rain, the widow's dry-eyed dignity. So when the man sat down across from him wearing that same face, older by nothing, gray at the temples in exactly the right places, Quillon did not scream or reach for a blade. He reached for the good bottle instead, because he had been guildmaster long enough to know that the extraordinary always arrived wanting something ordinary, and it went better for everyone if you offered it a drink first.

"You keep records," the dead chancellor had said, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "Records of trades. Of gifts. Of the people who carry them."

"We keep records of everything," Quillon said. "That's the business."

"The crown requires a service." The thing smiled with Vess's mouth, and the smile fit the face the way a glove fits a hand turned wrong-way-out. "Names. Bearer names, and the nature of their gifts, and where they sleep. In exchange, the charter stands. The Gildhall continues. The city keeps its... conveniences."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the charter burns, and everything it protects burns with it, and we will find our names ourselves. We are patient. But we prefer not to be inefficient."

Quillon had looked at the man for a long time. At the face of a colleague he had liked moderately and trusted not at all. At the eyes, which were the color of a ledger page left blank too long in the sun.

"What happens to them?" he asked. "The ones whose names I give."

"They are gathered," said the thing wearing Vess, "and they are spent."

It did not say how. It did not need to. Quillon was a man who had spent his whole life reading the fine print of other people's bargains, and he knew evasion when he heard it, and he knew what was being purchased with the years of the gifted even then, though he would not see the hollow king's face until much later, and understand, until later still.

He said yes.

He told himself it was for the guild. For the two hundred souls under its roof—the scribes, the appraisers, the vault-keepers, the old men who swept the memory-dust from the archive stairs. For the widows who sold grief for bread and the soldiers who bought back their fear at interest and the children born with small bright talents who would otherwise have had nowhere in the world to be anything but afraid. The Gildhall was a roof, and a roof over two hundred people was worth more than a principle over none.

He told himself that for eleven years. Repetition is the mother of belief. He had written that in the front of his first ledger as a young man, and he had never once considered that it cut both ways.

* * *

The lists went out quarterly, sealed, carried by crown couriers who never showed their faces twice. Names, gifts, addresses. Quillon kept no copy—he was careful that way; the only record of his treason was the treason itself—but he remembered every name. That was the price of doing business honestly with dishonesty: you had to hold it all in your own head, unblurred, because a man who forgets what he has sold is a man who will sell anything.

Forty-one names, over eleven years. Forty-one bearers gathered and spent.

He had met some of them. Shaken their hands across this very desk. A luck-smith named Odell who could put a fair wind into a sail. Twin sisters who shared one gift of mending between them, splitting it like a loaf. A boy of nineteen who could make candles burn blue when someone lied nearby—a party trick, nothing more, the kind of gift that should have earned him a comfortable living and a wife and a long dull life.

Quillon had put the boy's name on a list because the boy's gift was small enough that the crown's tithe-collectors might otherwise have overlooked him, and overlooked bearers made the crown suspicious of the list-maker. He had reasoned it out carefully, the way he reasoned out everything, and the reasoning had been flawless, and the boy had been taken in the spring, and Quillon had drunk the good brandy that night and slept soundly, because that was the worst of it—that he slept soundly. Conscience, he had learned, was like any other muscle. Starve it and it stops complaining.

And now there was Hazel.

* * *

He had her list on his desk. The bearer census, compiled under crown order, due to the courier by week's end. She had worked on it for four days behind the closed door of the records annex, and she had brought it up to him yesterday evening with ink still wet on the final signature line, and she had set it down and looked at him with those clear gray eyes and said, "Every name I could verify. Some of the older entries were ambiguous—I erred toward omission rather than guesswork."

Erred toward omission. He had heard the phrase and felt, for one cold instant, the sensation of standing on ice and hearing it sing.

Because Quillon had built the Gildhall's records himself, over thirty years, and he knew them the way a blind man knows his own house. He knew roughly how many active bearers traded through the hall—had known the number to within three or four for a decade, because knowing it was part of knowing what he was selling. The true count was sixty-two. Sixty-two living bearers with registered gifts, plus perhaps another dozen quiet ones the guild tolerated off-book.

Hazel's list held fifty-eight names.

Fifty-eight names of the dead.

He had checked nine of them at random last night, alone, lamplight low, the way he did everything that shamed him. Old entries. Settled accounts. Men and women whose trades had closed decades ago, whose deaths were recorded in the very ledgers Hazel kept balanced to the copper. She had not made an error. Errors scattered; they did not march in formation. Every name on that list belonged to a person already beyond the crown's reach, and every name that *should* have been there was absent, and the omissions were not random either—they clustered, when he mapped them against the city wards, around the tallow-row and the candlemarket and the tenements south of the tanneries. Around the poor quarters. Around, if his instinct served, the places where a frightened person with a small useless gift might go to ground among people who asked no questions.

Someone had tipped her. Or she had found something. Or—

Or she had simply done the arithmetic herself. That was the thing about Hazel that both made her invaluable and made her dangerous: she read ledgers the way priests read scripture, looking for the sin underneath the sum. Give her forty columns of numbers and she would find the column that didn't reconcile, and she would not rest until she knew why. Eleven years of quarterly lists, eleven years of bearers entering the hall and never trading again, eleven years of gaps in the books that he had papered over with transfers and write-offs and the slow bureaucratic fog that hides most sins from most eyes.

But not from hers. Not forever.

He turned the list over. Blank on the back. Her handwriting on the front, neat as prayer. *Compiled and verified by H. Sterling, Keeper of Entries.* She had signed it. She had given it to him with her chin level and her voice steady, and only now, replaying it, did he hear what had been underneath the steadiness: a question. She had handed him a lie and watched his face while she did it, and whatever she saw there, she had seen *something*, because Hazel missed nothing, and now she would go looking, and God help them both when she found it.

He should have refused the list. Should have told her the truth, or a piece of it, or locked the annex and burned the census order and taken whatever came. Instead he had accepted it, thanked her, and begun composing the letter he would send with it—the letter confirming the count, attesting the guild's full cooperation, buying another season of the charter with fifty-eight corpses' names as currency.

The brandy in his cup had gone warm. He drank it anyway.

* * *

There was a knock. Two soft raps, the rhythm Maren used, the under-vault keeper. Quillon slid the list into the drawer before he said enter, an old reflex, smooth as breathing.

Maren came in carrying a ledger under her arm and an expression under that which she clearly hoped passed for neutral. She was sixty, sharp-faced, loyal to the hall the way stones are loyal to a wall. "The courier's man came early," she said. "Wants the census tomorrow instead of week's end. And—" she hesitated, which Maren never did, "—he asked after your scribe. By name. Asked whether the Sterling woman keeps regular hours, whether she lodges in the hall, whether she has family in the city."

The room seemed to tilt a degree and settle. Quillon kept his hands flat on the desk. "What did you tell him?"

"That our staff's comings and goings are guild business, not the crown's. He wrote something down and left." Maren shifted the ledger in her arms. "Master Quillon. The census. Fifty-eight names. I've been keeper of the under-vault for twenty years, and I'll thank you not to insult us both, but the count's wrong, isn't it. Wrong on purpose."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a barge horn sounded on the river, long and low, the sound Ashvale made in its sleep.

"Say nothing," he said finally. "To anyone. About the count, about the courier, about anything you think you've counted. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly," said Maren, in a tone that meant she understood him entirely and agreed with none of it, and left.

Quillon sat alone with the charter glowing faintly gold in the afternoon light and understood, with the clarity that comes only after the decision is already made and all that remains is admitting it, what he had to do.

If the crown's man was asking after Hazel by name, then someone above him already suspected her—or would soon, once the false census crossed a desk that knew the true count. And when that happened, the question would come back to the Gildhall: *who falsified your list?* And Quillon would stand in whatever room they took him to, wearing Vess's dead face watching him from across the table, and he would answer, because he had answered for eleven years and a man who has answered once will always answer again, and the answer was already forming in his mouth, smooth and practiced and vile:

*I don't know. But I will find out.*

He opened the drawer. He looked at her name on the signature line—*H. Sterling*—in handwriting as clean and upright as the girl herself, the handwriting he had praised a hundred times, the handwriting her mother must have taught her before the plague took her, before the bargain, before everything.

*You were supposed to stay a bookkeeper,* he thought. *You were supposed to keep your head down and your sums straight and grow old in a warm room counting other people's miracles.*

Instead she had committed her first act of rebellion at twenty-six, quietly, competently, with perfect penmanship, and she had saved—how many? Twenty? Thirty? Thirty lives hidden inside fifty-eight deaths, smuggled past the pyres in her own neat hand—and she had handed the evidence to the man best positioned to destroy her, because she still believed the ledger does not judge, and she did not yet know that the guildmaster judged constantly, and had for eleven years, and always in favor of the charter.

Quillon took out a fresh sheet of paper. He dipped his pen. He began the letter to the crown, the one that would confirm the census, attest the guild's cooperation—and mention, in a postscript phrased with exquisite carelessness, that the compiling scribe had recently shown irregular curiosity in the archives, and that the guild would of course monitor its own staff, and report.

His hand did not shake. That was the thing he would remember afterward, in whatever remained of his life: that his hand did not shake at all.

Below him, the trade-bell chimed, and Hazel Sterling's voice rose faintly through the floorboards, correcting some junior scribe's entry, patient and clear, still believing in the rule above all rules.

*The ledger records,* Quillon thought, sealing the letter. *The ledger does not judge.*

*But the crown does. And I have fed it for eleven years, and tomorrow I will feed it again.*
