## 8. The Pyres at Dusk

They came for Wren on a Tuesday, in the hour when the Gildhall smelled of ink and cooling tallow and nothing yet of smoke.

Hazel heard the knock first—not the knocker's fist but the quality of it. Three measured strikes, patient as an executioner sharpening his blade. The counting room went still around her. Quillon's pen stopped mid-stroke. Somewhere above them, the great brass scales that weighed nothing and everything gave a faint, sympathetic shiver.

"Archives," Quillon said quietly, not looking up. "See who it is."

But Hazel already knew. She had known since dawn, when the criers' voices had come hoarse from three days of shouting and the city's bells had begun to ring wrong—too fast, too bright, the way they rang for festivals no one had ordered. She had known when she passed the candle-seller's stall on Cordwainer Row and found it empty, its awning folded, a scorch mark on the cobbles where something had been burned in haste: papers, perhaps. Names.

She descended the archive stairs two at a time and arrived in the entry hall in time to see six men in the king's gray-and-gold standing over Wren Ashby, who was holding a folio of restoration records against her chest like a shield made of paper.

Wren was nineteen. She had come to the Gildhall four years ago with chilblained hands and a head full of dead languages, and she had a gift so small and so gentle that Hazel doubted she'd ever traded on it once: flowers did not wilt in rooms where Wren sat too long. That was all. Cut stems stayed fresh. A wilting rose would lift its head again if she hummed to it. The kind of miracle that wouldn't fill a thimble.

The kind of miracle that burned just as well as any other.

"There's been a mistake," Hazel said, stepping between them. Her voice came out level. She was distantly proud of that. "Wren Ashby is a scribe. She has no entries. I keep the ledgers—I would know."

The officer in front—the one with the silver chain of office and the bored eyes of a man doing arithmetic with other people's lives—consulted a paper. "Denunciation received under the edict. Signed and witnessed." He held it out, and Hazel saw the name at the bottom in a hand she almost recognized, written in the careful clerk's script the Gildhall taught every apprentice.

*Witnessed by order of the Guildmaster's office.*

Her stomach turned to lead. Not Quillon's own hand—he was too careful for that—but his seal. His authority. Someone in this building had fed Wren to the fire, and the building had signed off on it.

"She catalogs marginalia," Hazel said. "She can't read half of it. Her gift is—" She stopped herself. Saying *flowers* aloud would be a confession.

"Then she has nothing to fear," the officer said pleasantly, "if the charge is false. The tribunal sits at dusk. Come and speak for her, mistress ledger-keeper, if you like. The crown welcomes witnesses." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who knew exactly how few witnesses ever came.

They took her. Wren looked back once from the doorway, and her eyes found Hazel's with terrible, uncomplaining clarity—as if she understood that no one in this hall would speak, that the whole weight of the Gildhall's stone and law and careful neutrality had settled onto her small shoulders and would carry her all the way to the pyre.

"I watered your fern," Wren said. Absurdly. To Hazel, alone, while the officers pulled her into the street. "The one on the third-floor sill. Don't let it die."

Then the door closed, and the Gildhall resumed its silence like a man pulling a blanket over his head.

* * *

Hazel went up to Quillon's study without knocking, which in eleven years of service she had never done.

"You signed the denunciation."

Quillon sat behind his desk with the afternoon light falling across him in bars, through the shutters he kept closed even in summer. He looked older than he had a week ago—or perhaps Hazel was only now seeing his age clearly, the way one sees a painting differently after learning what the painter did.

"The tribunal required a complainant of standing," he said. "I provided the office, not my hand. It is a distinction the law respects."

"It's a distinction *you* respect. Wren doesn't get to make it. She'll burn either way."

"Wren will be questioned first." He said it carefully, each word placed like a coin on a scale. "If she is innocent, she will be released."

"They don't release anyone. You've read the same reports I have. The tribunals in Marrowford and Cael Cross—they took forty people last month and released none. They're not looking for guilt, Master Quillon. Guilt is incidental. They're looking for *years*." She leaned forward over the desk, and for once she did not care about protocol, about the eleven years of deference that had kept her safe and small. "You know what the fires are for. Don't tell me you don't."

Something moved behind Quillon's eyes—something old and tired and frightened—and then it was gone, shuttered away behind the guildmaster's face, the face he wore the way Hazel wore her ledgers: as armor, as identity, as a place to hide.

"The Gildhall survives," he said, "because it is useful to the crown and harmless to the church. Every charter we hold, every vault, every right to trade in things the bishops call impossible—that survives because we are seen to cooperate. If I shielded every bearer in this building, there would be no Gildhall by autumn, and no one left to protect at all. You balance accounts, Mistress Sterling. Balance this one."

"I do balance them," Hazel said. "That's how I know this one doesn't. You're spending people to buy stone. And the stone won't remember anyone."

She left before he could answer. In the corridor outside, her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against the wall until the stones stopped seeming to tilt. Above her, on the third-floor sill, someone had left a clay pot with a fern in it, its fronds still green, stubbornly, impossibly green.

She carried it down to the counting room and set it beside her ledger, and she worked until dusk with her eyes burning and her pen scratching, recording trades she no longer believed in, and the fern stayed green, and it was the worst thing in the world.

* * *

They built the pyre in Cornmarket Square, because Cornmarket was big enough for a crowd and flat enough for the scaffolds, and because the city fathers had decided—Hazel heard it said in the queue of onlookers, in the flat way people say such things now—that dusk was the merciful hour. The light would be soft. The families could go home and sleep.

The square filled anyway. Half of Ashvale came, Hazel among them, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the crowd with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird. She told herself she came as witness. She told herself someone should stand where Wren could see a friendly face, if Wren could see anything at all through the smoke. But underneath those reasons, honest and ugly, was another: she needed to see it. She needed the fire to become real to her, because as long as it remained a rumor—a thing that happened in Marrowford, in Cael Cross, in places that were names on shipping manifests—it remained possible to sit in the counting room and balance columns and tell herself the guild's neutrality was a kind of virtue.

The condemned were brought out at sundown. There were five of them.

Five. Hazel counted twice, her lips moving. Five, when the list she had falsified should have spared dozens. Five, when the dead men's names she'd substituted should have closed the hunt's book on the low-city bearers for weeks.

A fishwife with rope burns on her wrists. An old man who walked with a stick and refused help climbing the scaffold, out of pride or defiance, Hazel couldn't tell. Two brothers from the tanneries who held hands the entire time. And Wren.

Wren walked upright. That was the thing Hazel would carry afterward, the thing that would wake her for years: Wren Ashby, nineteen years old, walked up the steps of the scaffold with her spine straight and her chin level, and when the priest in white began the litany of abomination, Wren looked out over the crowd—not pleading, not praying, just *looking*, the way she looked at a damaged folio, assessing what could be saved—and her gaze crossed Hazel's and did not stop, did not linger, did not accuse. That was worse than accusation. Wren had already forgiven her. Or Wren had never blamed her at all. Either way, Hazel felt the forgiveness land on her like a stone.

The priest spoke of corruption and contagion, of the gift as a wound in the world's flesh, of cleansing. The crowd was quiet in the way crowds are quiet when they are ashamed and hungry both. Somewhere near Hazel, a woman was crying without sound, her fists pressed to her mouth. Near her, a man ate roasted chestnuts and watched with mild interest, the way he might watch a bear-baiting.

*This is what neutrality buys,* Hazel thought. *A good seat.*

They lit the pyres at the moment the sun touched the rooftops, all five at once, because waiting between executions was bad for order. The kindling had been treated with something—pitch, resin, the crown's efficiency—and the flames went up fast and loud, drowning whatever sounds the five of them made, which Hazel was grateful for and hated herself for being grateful for.

And then the light changed.

It was subtle at first. The crowd murmured; heads tilted. The firelight coming off the central pyre—Wren's pyre—was the wrong color. Not orange, not gold, but a pale luminous white-gold, like light through honey, like the glow Hazel had once seen in the deep vaults when a particularly potent memory-trade was being sealed. It rose off the pyre in a slow column, visible against the darkening sky, and drifted—not with the wind, which blew toward the river, but *against* it, streaming east, streaming toward the palace hill, thinning as it went until it was a thread, until it was nothing.

The crowd saw it. Hazel watched them see it. Some crossed themselves. Some looked away quickly, the way people look away from a beggar's wound. One child pointed and asked her mother what it was, and the mother said *hush* in a voice like a slammed door.

But Hazel stood in the press of bodies with cold running down her spine, and she understood.

The years. The stolen years, drawn up out of the burning like sap, funneled east to feed whatever sat beneath the crown. This was why the fires. Not heresy. Not cleansing. *Harvest.* The hunt was not the crown's fear; the hunt was the crown's appetite, walking. Every bearer taken was a measure of grain. Every pyre was a threshing floor. And the thing on the throne—whatever it truly was—was drinking.

She thought of the ledger entries she'd recorded these past weeks: payments routed to the Office of Tribunals, requisitions for pitch and timber, stipends for the gray-and-gold officers. She had entered them in neat columns under *Crown Services, Misc.* She had balanced them. She had made the harvest legible, orderly, *accountable*—and had never once asked what account it served.

And Wren. Sweet, quiet Wren, whose gift couldn't fill a thimble, whose years were being drunk through the smoke right now, and whose name was on a denunciation bearing the Gildhall's seal. Hazel had falsified one list and told herself she had struck a blow. She had delayed five deaths by a week and called it defiance. The fire had simply waited, patient as interest accruing, and found another door.

The list she didn't write hadn't stopped anything. It had only taught her the shape of her own cowardice: that she would risk ink but not blood, that she would save strangers on paper and stand mute in a hallway while soldiers walked a girl with chilblained hands past her into the street.

The flames roared. The column of pale light thinned and vanished over the palace hill. Around Hazel the crowd began, horribly, to disperse—to drift home through the blue dusk, talking in low voices about the price of coal and whether the rains would come, because the world insisted on continuing, because horror was a thing the body could metabolize if given enough bread and enough habit.

Hazel did not move. She stood until the scaffolds were embers and the embers were ash, until the square emptied and the lamplighters came with their long poles, until her legs ached from standing and her eyes ached from not weeping, because weeping was not enough and she refused to spend her grief on something so cheap.

When the last of the crowd was gone, she knelt in the trampled mud of Cornmarket Square, in sight of the watchmen on the scaffold—who did not stop her, because a kneeling woman in mourning gray was unremarkable—and she made a vow the way she made all her serious entries: precisely, in full, with no margin for ambiguity.

*I will not delay the fire. I will end what feeds it.*

She didn't yet know how. But she knew where to start looking. Her mother had written bargains for the crown once—had helped bind whatever wore it. And somewhere in the sealed archives, in the ledgers no one but the guildmaster could open, the original terms were waiting, and Hazel Sterling had spent eleven years learning every lock, every key, every blind spot in the Gildhall's accounting.

Tomorrow, Quillon would notice the fern missing from the third-floor sill. Tomorrow, he would find his youngest archivist's desk cleared and wonder. But tonight—

Tonight, as Hazel rose from her knees, she saw a figure detach itself from the shadow of the corn exchange across the square. Small, quick, wrapped in a patched coat, moving with the particular caution of a person who has learned that streets have ears.

The candle-seller. The one whose stall had burned, whose name she didn't yet know, who had escaped the lists she'd falsified by pure chance and luck that was not borrowed.

He looked at her across the empty square. Then he raised one hand—not a wave, not quite a summons—and beckoned, once, toward the dark mouth of the alley behind the granary.

Hazel followed him into the dark.
