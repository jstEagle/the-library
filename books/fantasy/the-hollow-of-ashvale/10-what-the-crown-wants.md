## 10. What the Crown Wants

Bram had sold candles in the upper city for eleven years, and he knew the smell of every street by heart. Tallow and river mud near the Gildhall. Woodsmoke and wet wool in the weavers' quarter. The sweet rot of the tanneries drifting down from the hill like a rumor no one wanted confirmed.

The undercroft smelled of none of those things. It smelled of wax and old stone and, faintly, of the small miracles the gifted worked to keep themselves alive—warm bread that had never seen an oven, clean water wrung out of damp air, a fever cooled with nothing but a whispered word and a cupped hand. It was the smell of survival, thin and stubborn as a candle flame in a draft.

He was trimming wicks when the word came down: *gray riders at the Floodgate stair.*

His hands did not shake. Eleven years of selling candles in a city that had begun to burn people had taught him that much—shake later, when it's safe, when there's someone to hold the candle for you. He set down his knife, closed his stall's shutters, and went to find Hazel.

She was in the undercroft's common room, bent over a table covered in loose pages, doing what she had done every night since she'd come down to them: erasing debts. Not with magic—with arithmetic. She crossed out entries in her careful clerk's hand, struck through interest, voided penalties, and beside each struck line she wrote a single word in the margin: *forgiven.* The gifted of Ashvale had spent years owing the Gildhall for miracles they'd needed to live, and now a runaway guild scribe was unmaking those debts one page at a time, as if a pen could be a key.

"It's not a raid," Bram said, crouching beside her chair so he could speak low. "Marta's boy saw them at the Floodgate. Six riders, gray cloaks, no chains on their saddles."

Hazel looked up. She had ink on her cheekbone and shadows under her eyes, and Bram thought—not for the first time—that she looked like someone carrying a weight she refused to set down even to sleep. "No chains," she repeated. "Sereth doesn't need chains."

"You know her?"

"I know of her." Hazel set down her pen with the deliberate care of someone who wanted very badly to slam it instead. "Inquisitor Sereth. She ran the purges in Corrow two years ago. They say she never raised her voice. They say half the gifted of Corrow walked into the pyres holding her hand." A pause. "They also say three of them didn't. Three were spared. No one knows why."

Bram felt the cold arrive before he could name it. In the undercroft, fear traveled faster than light—it came through the walls, through the water pipes, through the way people stopped talking when a stranger passed. By the time the six gray riders dismounted at the top of the Floodgate stair, everyone below already knew they were coming, and everyone had reached the same conclusion at once:

*This is how it ends.*

* * *

But Sereth did not come with fire.

Bram watched her descend the last of the stair into the lamplight of the undercroft market, and the first thing he thought was that she looked wrong for the stories. He'd imagined a tall woman, hard-edged, armored in black and ash-gray. Instead she was slight, plain-faced, somewhere past forty, dressed in traveling clothes that had been good once and were mended now with visible care. Her hair was bound back plainly. Her boots were scuffed. She might have been a merchant's widow, a midwife, anyone.

Except for her eyes. Her eyes moved over the undercroft market the way Hazel's moved over a ledger—reading, totaling, finding the errors. And when they settled on Bram, standing at the front of the crowd the way he always did because someone had to be at the front, they stayed there a moment too long.

"Candle-seller," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way voices do in rooms built for whispers. "You're the one who pulled a fever out of the cooper's daughter on Tanners' Row last spring. Out of the air, with your hands."

No one breathed. Denying it would be useless; she'd have witnesses, records, Quillon's own ledgers if she'd bought her way into the Gildhall's archives, which of course she had. Lying to Sereth would be like lying to a scale about your own weight.

"That was me," Bram said.

"Good." Something flickered across her face—not a smile, nothing so warm, but a loosening, like a knot eased half a turn. "Then you'll understand what I've come to say. I'll say it once, to all of you, and then I'll answer questions until dawn if you want them. I'm not here to arrest anyone."

A woman near the back began to cry quietly. Bram heard it and hated how much it sounded like hope.

"The king is dying," Sereth said. "Has been dying for longer than any of you have been alive, though none of us knew it. What sits on the throne wears his face and speaks with his voice, but the man himself—" she stopped, chose her words with visible care, "—the man himself has been hollowed out. Fed upon. Year by year, gift by gift, for a generation. The pyres are not piety. They are harvest."

Murmurs. Someone spat. An old glassblower whose granddaughter had been taken in the first week of the hunts shouted something ugly, and Sereth took it without flinching, the way a wall takes thrown stones.

"I know what you think of me," she said when the shouting died. "I've earned it. I burned people. I will burn more before this is done, because the alternative is worse, and I will tell you what the alternative is." She let the silence stretch until even the crying stopped. "The binding that hollows the king can be renewed. Broadened. There is a rite—the crown's clerics found it in the old archives—and if it is performed, it will not feed on one bearer at a time anymore. It will take the years of every gifted soul in Ashvale at once. Every one of you. Your children. All of it, in a single night, to keep the thing on the throne alive another century."

The cold in Bram's chest spread outward until his fingers were numb. Around him, faces turned gray in the lamplight. This was the thing they'd all feared in the dark hours and never said aloud: that the hunts were not madness but appetite, and appetites grow.

"So here is what the crown wants," Sereth said. "It wants the original bargain. The founding document, sealed beneath the coronation hall, written decades ago by someone clever enough to bind a king and foolish enough to think the price would stay small. Find it, bring it to me, and the renewal cannot happen—because a bargain cannot be renewed twice from the same text while the original stands elsewhere. You would starve the harvest at its root." Her gaze swept the room. "And the crown offers this: any bearer who aids in finding it will be spared. Named, registered, protected. A life. A real one, above ground, in the sun."

Silence. Then, from somewhere behind Bram, a young voice—Tally, the ferryman's son, sixteen years old and terrified since the day his gift showed itself: "And if we don't?"

"Then I do my duty," Sereth said simply. "All of it."

* * *

They argued until the candles burned down to pools.

That was the trap of it, Bram thought, watching them argue. That was the genius. She hadn't offered them a lie. She'd offered them arithmetic—the one language desperate people trust more than hope. One betrayal against forty lives. One document against every child in the undercroft. She'd made collaboration look like courage and loyalty look like suicide, and she'd done it without raising her voice, and Bram understood suddenly why three bearers in Corrow had been spared, and what it must have cost them afterward to live in the sun with that arithmetic written on their hands forever.

Hazel stood apart through most of it, arms folded, saying nothing, and Bram drifted to her side because that was where his feet went now without asking him.

"You're quiet," he murmured.

"I'm listening to which of my neighbors is going to sell us." Her voice was flat, but her knuckles were white where her arms crossed. "Look at Marta. Look at Old Fen. They're not evil, Bram. They're tired. That's all it takes. That's all it's ever taken."

"And you? Are you tempted?"

That earned him a glance, sharp and almost amused. "I'm the one who falsified the hunt list. If I wanted safety I'd have stayed behind Quillon's desk, counting other people's sins." But then her eyes went distant, and he saw the truth move underneath: she was thinking of her mother, of the sealed archive, of whatever she'd found there that she hadn't yet told him. Temptation wasn't always about safety. Sometimes it was about a debt you'd carry to your grave.

"She knows something," Bram said quietly, nodding toward Sereth, who stood at the edge of the crowd answering questions one by one, patient as a physician. "Watch her. She's not reading the room to find traitors. She's reading it to find—"

"—who's already looking for the bargain," Hazel finished. "Because someone is. Because she's heard rumors." Hazel's breath caught, and Bram watched the color drain from her face as the arithmetic completed itself behind her eyes. "Bram. She's not recruiting informants. She's hunting a trail. And the only person in this room who's touched the original bargain—"

"—is you."

Across the room, as if the words had carried on some frequency only inquisitors could hear, Sereth looked up from her conversation and found Hazel across the crowded market. Held her gaze. And inclined her head—a small, precise, courteous gesture, the gesture of one professional acknowledging another.

Bram stepped slightly in front of Hazel, an old habit from days of selling candles in streets where boys threw stones, though he knew it was useless. You couldn't stand between a woman and her ledger.

"We leave tonight," Hazel whispered. "Both of us. Before she works out what I am."

"Hazel—"

"My mother wrote that bargain. My bloodline can unbind it. Which means I'm not just on her trail, Bram—I'm the trail." She was already moving, gathering her loose pages, striking one last line through one last debt with a hand that did not tremble, and Bram loved her a little for that and feared for her a great deal more. "Get your coat. Get your candles. Whatever you can carry."

Behind them, Sereth's voice rose again over the crowd, calm and reasonable, offering salvation at a price only other people would pay: *"Find the document. Save yourselves. The crown asks nothing else."*

And Bram, pulling on his coat in the wax-smelling dark, understood at last what frightened him most—not the pyres, not the hollow king, not even the woman with the patient eyes.

What frightened him was how many hands around him were already halfway raised.
