## 14. Prisoner of the Inquisitor

They took her boots first.

Hazel did not understand why until the third day, when she was led—barefoot, the flagstones cold as riverbed stone—from her cell down a corridor that smelled of tallow and old iron. The absence of boots made every step a small confession: *I am here, I am here, I am not going anywhere.* The inquisitor's tower had been built for sound as much as for stone. Every footfall reported itself. Even silence had to be paid for in shivers.

The cell itself was not cruel. That frightened her more than cruelty would have. A cot with a wool blanket. A stool. A window slit too high to reach, through which she could see one wedge of Ashvale sky and, on clear mornings, the smoke of the city's chimneys rising straight and ordinary into the blue. Somewhere down there was Bram. Somewhere down there was the undercroft, holding its breath. Somewhere, if he still lived, Quillon sat behind his desk with a fresh charter and clean hands, having sold her the way he'd sold everyone else—one signature at a time.

She had counted the days by meals. Fourteen meals meant seven days, or near enough. On the seventh day they came for her properly.

* * *

The room at the top of the tower was round, and its walls were lined not with instruments but with shelves. Books, mostly—some of them Gildhall editions; she recognized the binding house of Corvess from the gilt spine-stamps—and jars of ink, and candles by the dozen, all burning at once, which should have been extravagant and instead felt like an accusation. Light everywhere. Nowhere to sit in shadow.

Sereth stood at the window side of the room, dressed not in the black Hazel remembered from the pyres but in plain gray, her hair bound back, her hands bare. She looked less like an inquisitor than like a woman interrupted at her own accounting.

"You're left-handed," Sereth said, without turning. "I read the list you falsified. Dead men's names, all of them—I checked every one against the parish rolls. Eleven names, eleven graves. You wrote them beautifully. Whoever taught you penmanship loved you."

"My mother taught me."

"Then your mother had excellent taste in letters and catastrophic taste in bargains." Sereth turned then, and up close her face was younger than her voice—thirty perhaps, with the flat, well-rested look of someone who slept because she had arranged the world so that sleep was possible. She gestured to the stool across the table. "Sit. This is not an interrogation. Interrogations happen downstairs, where the floor drains. This is a negotiation."

Hazel sat. Her bare feet found the cold stone and she let it hold her, anchor her. "You have nothing I want."

"I have everything you want. I simply haven't told you what it is yet." Sereth sat opposite her and drew a ledger toward herself—not a Gildhall ledger; the cover was plain calfskin, government issue—and opened it to a page already dense with entries in two hands. "But first, since we are being honest with each other, I'll show you mine." She turned the book around.

It was a trade ledger. Hazel knew ledgers the way priests know psalters—the weight of them, the grammar of columns, the particular humility of a page waiting to be written true. And this one was written true. *Received: one fear of drowning, whole. Paid: service rendered, term indefinite.* *Received: the memory of a sister's face, kept in trust. Paid: as above.* Page after page, years of them, and every entry balanced against the same payment.

*Service rendered.*

Hazel looked up slowly. "You're a bearer."

"I'm a survivor who happened to be born with a gift and clever enough to spend it before someone else spent it for me." Sereth said it without shame, without heat, as though reciting a column total. "I can take fear out of a man. Did you know that? Not courage—I can't put anything in—but I can reach into a soldier on the eve of battle and lift his terror out like a splinter. It's a small gift. Useless for miracles. Worthless on any market you'd recognize." She closed the ledger gently. "Except to the crown. To the crown, it is worth exactly one life, renewable annually, in exchange for which I empty frightened men so they can do terrible things calmly. There are forty-one of us in the king's service. Gifted, all of us. Every bearer the hunt has ever taken was offered this choice first."

The candles hissed. Hazel thought of the pyres at dusk, of the young archivist—Tam, his name had been Tam, sixteen, who alphabetized everything twice—and the way the crowd had been made to watch. "No," she said. "They weren't offered anything. I've seen—"

"You've seen what we're instructed to show. Yes." Sereth folded her hands. "The fire is real. But the choice comes before the fire, and almost no one takes it, because almost no one believes the offer is real. They think it's a trick, or they think refusing is nobler, or—" her voice did something very small and controlled, "—or they have someone they won't leave behind, and the crown counts on that. Do you know how many bearers have refused in nine years? Two hundred and six. Do you know how many accepted? Forty-two." She smiled without warmth. "I was the second."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you are about to be offered the same arithmetic, and I want you to hear it from someone who has lived inside it. Because you think I'm your enemy, and I need you to stop thinking that long enough to save your own life. And because—" for the first time, something moved behind the flatness, something tired and old, "—because you wrote eleven dead men's names to save strangers, and I have waited nine years to meet anyone else in this kingdom who would do that."

* * *

They brought tea. Hazel drank it because refusing food was a currency too, and she was learning to count hers.

"Your mother," Sereth began.

"Don't."

"She wrote the original bargain. You know that now—I know you know, because you took the archive copy and ran, and people only run from truths they believe." Sereth turned pages in the government ledger, unhurried. "Maren Sterling, senior scrivener of the Gildhall, year of the gray fever. The city was dying. Nine hundred dead by midsummer, and the physicians burning their own clothes. Your mother went to the crown with a solution, and the crown, being desperate, signed what she brought them. And it worked. The plague ended in a month. No one asked why, or at what cost, because no one was dying anymore."

"It fed on the gifted," Hazel said. Her voice came out level and that surprised her. "Her bargain fed the crown on gifted lives. Years, taken from bearers, poured into—to keep the king alive, or whatever wears him."

"To keep *the binding* alive," Sereth corrected softly. "And there is a distinction you need to understand, because everything depends on it. The thing beneath the coronation hall is not a demon wearing the king. It is a contract wearing the king. Bargains, in your guild's own theology, need a vessel—they pool, they settle, they look for the deepest container in the room. When your mother sealed it, the deepest container in Ashvale was the newly crowned king. So it settled into him. It hollowed him the way water hollows stone—slowly, lawfully, according to terms. He is not possessed, Hazel. He is *foreclosed upon*."

Hazel set down the cup before her hand could betray her. "You speak about it like a creditor."

"I am a creditor. So were you, until eight days ago." Sereth leaned forward. "Here is what you don't know, and why I asked for this meeting rather than letting the Privy Council handle you, which would have gone much worse. The binding is failing. Has been for years—you've seen the king at the window of the white tower, the lucid moments getting shorter, the fits getting longer. The bargain needs renewal, the way any long debt needs restructuring. And renewal requires—" she tapped the ledger, "—principal. New collateral. Your mother bound the gift-bearers of her generation. The next renewal was always meant to bind the next generation. Slowly. Over decades. A tithe."

"But?"

"But the king is nearly empty now, and the council is frightened, and frightened men do not tithe. They harvest." Sereth held her gaze. "Three weeks from now, at the autumn equinox, the crown will perform the renewal rite in the coronation hall. And the principal will not be a tithe of the gifted. It will be all of them. Every bearer in Ashvale, every hidden gift in the undercroft, every smuggled miracle and candle-seller's charm—all of it, all of them, taken at once. Their years, their memories, their gifts themselves, poured into the binding in a single night. The king will live another century. The gift will end in this kingdom forever. And no one will ever have to burn anyone again, because there will be nothing left to burn."

The room seemed to tilt. Hazel gripped the edge of the table. She thought of the undercroft—the low warm dark of it, the stalls trading small mercies, a woman selling borrowed luck by the pinch, children born with light in their palms learning early to keep their fists closed. All of it. All of them. One night.

"That's not a renewal," she said. "That's an extinction."

"That is what the council calls *solvency*," Sereth said. "I call it what it is. But my naming it changes nothing, and yours won't either. Unless."

* * *

"Unless." Hazel repeated the word like tasting a coin for clipping.

"Unless the bargain is dissolved rather than renewed. Which brings me to the negotiation." Sereth rose and walked the shelf line, trailing one finger along the book spines, and stopped before a gap—a single missing volume, its absence dust-outlined like a body on a floor. "Two nights ago, an old man in my cells traded me a secret for a blanket. Turnkeys are cheap that way; kindness confuses them more than cruelty. He told me a scribe passed through the lower stacks the night of the raid. He told me what she carried out. And he told me that when we caught her, she fought like someone carrying something that mattered more than her life."

Hazel said nothing. Her heart was a fist against her ribs.

"The original bargain," Sereth said. "Your mother's own hand, with her marginalia. The archive copy you stole is worthless—it's a fair copy, stripped of everything dangerous. But the original, with the drafting notes, contains the unbinding clause. You've found it. I know you've found it, because I've read Maren Sterling's marginalia, and once you've seen how that woman thought, you know she wouldn't have written the cage without writing the key somewhere nearby. Debtor's bloodline. One true memory, freely given. Am I close?"

The cold of the stones climbed Hazel's spine. "If you know the terms, why do you need me?"

"Because knowing terms and paying them are different trades entirely." Sereth turned from the shelf. "The council wants the original document so they can destroy the unbinding clause and renew the binding safely forever. I want the original document so that *you* can use it. Do you see the difference? I am offering to put the key in your hand."

"And the price?"

"The price is what it always is." Sereth sat again, and now she did look tired, terribly tired, the tiredness of a woman who had been holding a door shut for nine years with her shoulder. "The rite happens in three weeks whether we act or not. If you go to the vault alone, you die, and the council finds the document, and the undercroft burns in a different sense of the word. If you give me the document and trust the crown, you die more slowly, and so does everyone you love. But if you let me move you—if you work *with* the forty-one of us who are already inside the machine—there is a window during the rite itself. The binding will be open. Vulnerable. For the space of one ritual hour, the contract is being rewritten, and a rewriting contract can be *ended*. You would walk into the coronation hall under our protection, at the moment of maximum danger, and unmake it from inside."

"And why," Hazel said quietly, "would the crown's own bearers help me destroy the binding that feeds them? Renewal keeps the king alive. Keeps the charter. Keeps *you* alive. Dissolve it, and the forty-one of you are just bearers again—in a kingdom that burns bearers. You'd be trading your survival for ashes."

For a long moment Sereth didn't answer. She looked at her own hands—bare, unadorned, steady—and when she spoke, her voice had dropped its official register entirely, and what was underneath was younger and far more frightening.

"Because I emptied a soldier's fear last winter so he could hold a door while his comrades burned a family behind it. He thanked me. He shook my hand. I stood in that courtyard and felt *nothing*, because I had spent my gift making sure I could feel nothing, and I understood that the crown hasn't spared the forty-two of us, Hazel. It has *spent* us. We are not survivors of the hunt. We are part of it." She met Hazel's eyes. "You look at me and you see what you might have become if you'd said yes to the wrong offer at the right time. Well. I look at you, and I see what I might have become if I'd said no. Between us we make one whole person who might still do the right thing. That is the negotiation. That is the entire offer."

Hazel sat with it. The candles burned; the wedge of sky beyond the window had gone amber with afternoon; somewhere below, muffled by stone, a bell marked the hour, indifferent as a ledger line.

Every instinct she owned said trap. Every instinct she owned had also said *trust the Gildhall*, once, and *trust Master Quillon*, and those instincts had been purchased long ago and never audited. She thought of her mother's marginalia, the cramped patient script: *a debt freely paid ends; a debt enforced only deepens.* She thought of Bram's face when the turnkeys took him, the small defiant miracle he'd pressed into her palm as they dragged him back—warm as a coin held in sunlight. *Get it done,* he'd mouthed. Not *save yourself*. *Get it done.*

"If I say yes," Hazel said, "I want three things. Bram released to me, alive. Word sent to the undercroft warning them of the date. And the original document in my hands—not yours, mine—at least one day before the rite."

"Two of the three are easy. Bram I can release; his cell is mine to administer, and no one counts candle-sellers. The warning—there are couriers among the forty-one who still remember which side of the wall they were born on; it can be arranged." Sereth paused, and Hazel watched her weigh the third like a moneylender weighing a doubtful note. "The document stays in crown custody until the night of the rite. That is the one thing I cannot bend, because if the council learns I gave it to you, everything ends tonight instead of in three weeks. You'll have it at the vault door. In your hands. An hour before you must use it."

"An hour isn't time to study it."

"You studied it for seven days in the archives before they caught you. Don't insult us both." Sereth extended her hand across the table—an inquisitor's hand, a survivor's hand, offered palm-up like something surrendered. "Well, scribe? Do we have terms?"

Hazel looked at that open hand and understood, finally, the shape of the mirror between them: two women who had both learned that institutions eat their servants, one from inside the cage and one from inside the whip-hand, both now standing in the same narrow place where the only choices left were terrible and few. She could refuse, and be noble, and watch the undercroft pour into the binding like wine into a wound. Or she could shake the hand of the crown's own instrument and gamble everything on the possibility that somewhere beneath nine years of service, the girl who'd once been offered the fire and lived anyway was still in there, keeping her own ledger, waiting for a debt she could rightly pay.

She reached across the table.

As their hands met, Sereth leaned close, and her voice fell to almost nothing, and what she said was not in any negotiation:

"They've moved the date. The rite is not in three weeks. It's tomorrow night."

Hazel's blood went to ice water. "*Tomorrow*—"

"Equinox tide, and the king is failing faster than the council dared admit. Which means whatever you're going to be, scribe—key, weapon, martyr, or the person who finally balances this account—you have one night to become it." Sereth released her hand and rose, already the gray official again, already moving toward the door. "Rest if you can. They're bringing you a dress for the ceremony. Wear it. Walk in like you belong there."

She stopped at the door, back turned, and added, so quietly Hazel nearly missed it:

"And when you stand in front of that thing in the crown—whatever it offers you—remember that I took an offer once, and this is what the interest looks like."

The door closed. The lock turned. Hazel sat alone in the ring of candlelight, her bare feet on the cold stone, doing the only arithmetic left to her, and it came out the same every way she ran it: one night, one memory, one chance—and beneath the coronation hall, a contract opening like a mouth.

Somewhere in the cells below, if Sereth had told the truth, Bram was already walking free with a message that was a day too late.

She put her head in her hands and began, silently, to practice the words of the unbinding—over and over, like a prayer, like a payment—against the morning.
