## 15. Bram's Ledger

The cell smelled of tallow and old stone, and Bram had learned in three days to count the drips.

They came at intervals he could not quite fix—seven, then nine, then six again—as though the water itself were uncertain of its debts. He sat with his back against the wall where the damp was thinnest, knees drawn up, and counted anyway. Counting was what he had. It kept his hands from shaking, or told him honestly when they were, which amounted to nearly the same thing.

His candle-seller's coat had been taken from him. His boots too. They had left him a rough grey smock that smelled of someone else's sweat and fear, which in Ashvale's undercroft would have been considered a trade made in bad faith, but the crown did not deal in good faith. The crown dealt in years.

*Three days.* Long enough for the bruises on his wrists to go from purple to a sickly yellow-green. Long enough for him to learn the rhythm of boots on the stair outside: the heavy pair that belonged to the sergeant who never spoke to him, the quick light pair that belonged to the boy who brought the water and always looked away, and—the one that mattered—a slow, dragging tread that came twice a day and belonged to a man named Odo.

Turnkeys were not inquisitors. That was the whole of Bram's hope, and it was a small hope, the size of a stub of candle, but he had sold smaller things for better prices.

He heard the tread now.

* * *

Odo was a broad, grey-faced man with a jaw like a drawer that stuck, and he carried the keys on a ring so heavy it had worn a groove into his belt. He unlocked the grate, slid the evening bowl through—barley porridge gone cold at the edges—and began the ritual of looking without seeing. Turnkeys learned that early. Seeing was dangerous. Seeing meant knowing what you fed and where it slept, and knowing was how a man ended up on the wrong side of a pyre himself.

"Evening, master turnkey," Bram said.

Odo grunted.

"Cold tonight," Bram said. "Colder down here than up there, I'd wager. Cold gets into the joints at my age." He was four-and-thirty and had been telling people he was older than that since he was nineteen; it loosened their purses and lowered their guard, both at once. "You've the look of a man whose knees talk to him."

"They talk," Odo admitted, because a man will admit anything about his knees.

"My mother swore by a salve." Bram shifted forward, letting his voice drop into the register he used over market stalls, warm and confiding, the register that said *this is between us and no one else*. "Mutton fat, comfrey, and something she never told me. I've made it my whole life. Sold it at the Drowned Bell market for three coppers a twist. Soldiers bought it. Guardsmen bought it. Your own sergeant has a twist of it in his kit right now, if he hasn't used it all—I marked the paper blue, you can't miss it."

Odo's eyes flicked up for the first time. Suspicion, but the ordinary kind, the kind that wanted to be talked out of itself. "You're a prisoner."

"I'm a candle-seller," Bram said. "I was arrested for being born wrong, not for selling bad wax. And I'm going to burn on the Ashvale pyre in—" he counted on his fingers theatrically, though he knew exactly how many days it was, nine, ten at the outside, before Sereth's renewal rite made the question of small heresies moot— "well. Before long. A dead man doesn't need a recipe."

The dripping went on behind him. Seven. Nine. Six.

"What do you want for it?" Odo asked, and Bram could have kissed the sticking drawer of his jaw.

* * *

Here is what they did not understand about men like Bram, the crown and its inquisitors, the guildmasters and their scrubbed ledgers: they thought the gift was the miracle. They thought the gift-bearer's power lived in the impossible trades, the borrowed luck, the grief sold for rain. But any trader worth his salt knew the truth. The gift was only the coin. The real trade happened in the space between two people when one of them wanted something and the other knew it.

Bram had spent twenty years selling candles in a city that feared him. He had learned to read want the way Hazel read ledgers—quickly, completely, down to the last copper of interest.

Odo wanted three things. He wanted his knees quiet. He wanted, beneath that, in the place a man keeps wants he doesn't name even to himself, to be the sort of person who wasn't feeding children to a fire. Bram had watched him carry the water-boy's bucket up the stair on the second day, unasked. Small mercies. A man who performed them was a man at war with himself, and a man at war with himself paid almost anything for a flag to rally to.

And third—he wanted to be clever. Wanted to be the one who got something for nothing off a condemned witch, and told the story afterward in the turnkeys' room over cheap wine.

So Bram gave him a story to tell.

"The recipe's mine to give," he said. "But my mother taught me gifts aren't given free. Not because she was hard. Because she said a thing given free is a thing forgotten by morning, and she wanted her son remembered." He held up his hands, palms out, empty as an honest man's. "I don't want coin. What would I buy? I want a kindness done while I'm still alive to know about it."

Odo's eyes narrowed. "Inquisitor said no one comes near you. No letters. Nothing passes."

"Nothing passes," Bram agreed solemnly. "I wouldn't ask it. I'm not asking you to smuggle anything, master turnkey. I'm not even asking you to speak to anyone." He leaned closer to the grate, and the torchlight from the passage caught the lines of his face and deepened them, made him look every year of the age he claimed and a few he didn't. "There's a woman named Marta who sells chestnuts outside the prison gate. Grey shawl, chilblains on her knuckles, laughs like a cart on cobbles. She's no kin of mine. No one could say she is. But when I was a boy and hungry, and my mother was working nights, Marta used to give me chestnuts off the coals and never once asked me for a copper." He let that sit. Let Odo fill in the rest himself, because a story a man builds himself is a story he'll defend. "She'll be out there tonight. She's always out there. And I thought—if some kind soul bought her whole basket and told her it was from an old friend, well. That's not passing anything. That's just a purchase."

"That's all?"

"That's all." Bram smiled, gap-toothed and guileless. "And the salve. Half now, half after."

Odo stared at him a long moment, working sums behind his eyes—not of coin but of risk, weighing a basket of chestnuts against a dead man's gratitude, finding the scale tipped comfortably toward safety. "Half now," he said finally, and spat in his palm, and pressed it flat to the bars.

Bram pressed his own palm to meet it through the iron.

It was the oldest form of bargain in Ashvale, older than ledgers, older than the Gildhall. Skin to skin, spit to seal. Somewhere in the city above, Hazel Sterling was probably writing down a trade in careful columns, and Bram hoped she'd forgive him for making this one the old way, unwritten, unwitnessed, resting entirely on the honor of a jailer and the desperation of a dying man.

Some trades, his mother had said, are too important to write down. A written debt can be burned. An owed heart cannot.

* * *

The salve worked.

That was the beauty and the horror of the small miracles, Bram thought, sitting alone again in the dark, listening to Odo's footsteps recede up the stair with something almost like a spring in them. The gift didn't care whether you used it for grand things. It worked just as faithfully in mutton fat and comfrey as it ever had in crowns and coronations. He had pushed a little of his own warmth into the mixture—nothing anyone could prove, nothing that would show, just the memory of his mother's kitchen on a winter morning, the smell of the render, the particular comfort of being somewhere safe. Anyone who rubbed it into aching knees would feel that comfort settle into the joint like a coal wrapped in wool.

It cost him. Everything cost. He could feel the price already, a faint thinning at the edges of himself, the way a candle thins below its flame. Two days ago he'd have sworn he remembered the exact tune his mother hummed while she stirred the pot. Now he remembered that she had hummed, and that it had been a tune, and nothing more. The melody was gone, traded away into a jar of grease for a jailer's knees.

Small and costly. That was the whole ledger of his life, and he was beginning to suspect it would be the whole epitaph.

But Odo would come back tomorrow, and the day after, and each time he would come back warmer, softer, more inclined to see the prisoner as a person rather than a parcel awaiting collection. And a man who saw you as a person could be moved. Bram had nine days—no, eight now—to move him the length of a prison and the width of his conscience.

Because that was the true trade underneath the false one. Not the salve. Not the chestnuts.

The message.

* * *

Sereth had made a mistake in the interrogation, on the first day, and Bram had been turning it over like a coin suspected of being clipped ever since.

She had been good—cold, patient, methodical, asking her questions in circles so he couldn't tell which answers she already had—but she had wanted one thing too much. *Where does Hazel Sterling keep the original bargain.* Over and over, in different clothes, different words. And once, tired or careless, she had said something else. Something that slipped out sideways, the way truth does when it's been pressed too long:

*"When the renewal is sealed, none of it will matter."*

Renewal. Not capture. Not trial. Renewal.

Bram had sat with that word through two nights of dripping water and understood it the way a candle understands a draft. The hollow king was failing. Whatever wore the crown needed feeding, needed it soon and needed it vast, and the entire undercroft—all the gifted hiding beneath Ashvale, the smugglers and hedge-witches and small-miracle sellers, every soul Hazel had tried to shield with her erased debts—was a larder waiting to be opened all at once.

They weren't hunting the gifted to destroy them. They were hunting them to harvest them.

And the renewal would happen at the coronation hall, at the vault, at the binding her mother wrote—it had to, everything circled back to that stone throat beneath the city—and if the undercroft knew, if they knew the hour and the place, then the hunted would know exactly where the hunters would be standing, and when, and why.

Information was the only currency Bram had left. He had no gift strong enough to break bars. He had no allies inside these walls. He had a turnkey with bad knees and a soft spot for old women, and a chestnut-seller who might be real and might be invented, and eight days.

It would have to be enough.

* * *

Odo came at dawn with the water, and again at dusk with the porridge, and on the third visit he lingered.

"She cried," he said gruffly, staring at the wall as though the stones had accused him of something. "Your chestnut woman. Cried and laughed at once, carried on like a fool. Bought her a new basket too, since hers was burnt through at the bottom." He shifted. The groove in his belt creaked. "Never seen a woman so pleased over a basket."

"She's earned a hundred baskets," Bram said softly. "Thank you, master turnkey. You've made an old man's last week kinder than his whole year."

Odo grunted, embarrassed, and thrust the bowl through harder than necessary, and left.

But that night he came back a fourth time, off-schedule, and stood at the bars with his lantern held high so the light fell full on Bram's face, studying him. And Bram, who had been waiting three days for exactly this, looked up from counting drips with the expression of a man surprised out of a nap.

"You're not what they say you are," Odo said.

"No one is what they say we are," Bram replied. "That's rather the crown's whole business, isn't it? Telling everyone what we are."

Silence. The lantern swayed. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the city bells rang the ninth hour, and Bram felt the sound go through him like a blade finding a gap in armor—nine days gone, seven remaining.

"If a man wanted," Odo said at last, very carefully, the words coming out one at a time like coins counted onto a table, "to leave something behind. After. For folk who deserved it. A man couldn't pass anything out of here. But a man could say things. Out loud. To the air. Talking to yourself isn't a crime yet." A pause. "Is it?"

"Not yet," Bram said, and his voice nearly broke on it, and he was grateful for the dark. "Not yet it isn't."

* * *

They built the habit slowly, the way you build any fire—one spark, sheltered, fed.

Each night Odo descended with his lantern, ostensibly to check the locks, and each night Bram talked to the air. He talked about the weather in the undercroft, about the smell of the river at low tide, about a woman named Sefa who could mend broken pottery with a touch and charged nothing for cups but a song. About a boy called Finch who traded lucky pennies and had a laugh like a kettle. About the Drowned Bell tavern and which cellar door stuck and which stair creaked, about the old chalk marks that meant *safe* and the newer ones that meant *moved on*, about the names of the hidden and the shape of the tunnels beneath the Tanner's Row.

None of it written. All of it spoken, plainly, to a man who was only checking locks, and who had a memory like most men have—leaky, but holding the important things.

"Why me?" Odo asked on the fifth night. "Why not the water-boy? Why not anyone?"

"Because the water-boy would be asked," Bram said simply. "No one asks the turnkey. You're furniture, master turnkey. It's the finest disguise in Ashvale, and you wear it without trying."

Odo looked at him for a long time. Then he said, quietly, "My knees haven't hurt in five days," and Bram understood that whatever else passed between them after that would pass freely.

* * *

On the sixth night, Odo brought more than porridge.

He brought it in the bottom of the bowl, wrapped in oilcloth: a stub of pencil no longer than a thumb, and a scrap of paper torn from a duty roster, blank on its back. Bram stared at them through the bars and felt his heart hammer against his ribs like a bird against a window.

"I can't take anything out," Odo said. "If they search me—"

"You won't be searched. You're furniture." Bram took the paper with fingers that trembled and made them stop trembling by force of will. "And I won't write anything worth hanging you for. Names I'll keep in my head. This is only—" he turned the scrap over, thinking fast, thinking in the old market cadence, offer and counteroffer, weight and balance— "this is for the woman with the chestnuts. If I fall ill, or if they move me, or if your nerve fails, she should know the shape of what's coming. So she can warn the others."

He wrote small, tight, in the cramped hand of a man who had priced goods on slate since childhood:

*THE RENEWAL COMES AT THE CORONATION VAULT. ALL THE HIDDEN AT ONCE. SEVEN DAYS OR FEWER. TELL THEM THE HUNT IS A HARVEST. TELL THEM HAZEL STERLING WENT TO THE VAULT AND NEEDS EVERY SOUL IN THE UNDERCROFT TO FOLLOW.*

He hesitated over the last line, pencil hovering. Then he added, because it was true and because truth was the only coin he had never counterfeited:

*BRAM IS PROUD OF HER. — B.*

He folded the scrap into a square the size of a thumbnail and passed it through the bars, and watched Odo's fist close around it, and felt the strangest sensation rising in his chest—hope, raw and unpracticed, painful as a new tooth.

"Now listen to me," Bram said, leaning close, his voice dropping to the register beneath the market register, the one reserved for truths. "Tomorrow you buy Marta's whole basket again. You tell her the same friend sends it. Then you walk away, and you let her do the rest. You never ask what became of that paper. You never speak of it again, not to me, not to your God, not over wine. Can you do that?"

"I can do that," Odo said.

"And one more thing." Bram gripped the bars, and the iron was cold enough to bite. "Seven days. When the bells ring the coronation hour, whatever happens in this city—whatever fires, whatever fighting, whatever falls—you stay down here with your keys and your porridge and you keep every door locked behind you. Do you hear me? You're furniture. Furniture survives."

Odo's jaw worked. The stuck-drawer jaw, opening at last. "And you?"

Bram smiled his gap-toothed smile, and it cost him more than the melody of his mother's humming had, more than anything he had ever sold, because it was the first honest smile he had given in six days and it showed everything.

"I'm a candle-seller, master turnkey," he said. "We're made to burn."

Odo stood a moment longer, the folded paper vanishing into his palm, into his sleeve, into the groove of his belt where the keys rode. Then he took up his lantern and went up the stairs, and his tread was slow and heavy as ever, and Bram listened to it fade and thought: *furniture walking out of a burning house with a message in its sleeve.*

Behind him, the drip counted on. Seven. Nine. Six.

Six days, now. Maybe fewer.

Bram closed his eyes and began, silently, to recite the names of the undercroft, one by one, like a prayer or a stocktake—Sefa, Finch, Old Maren, the twins at Tanner's Row—sealing them into his memory the way his mother had sealed warmth into mutton fat, trusting them to a jailer's leaky heart, trusting a basket of chestnuts to carry a war through the gates of the enemy.

Above him, muffled by fathoms of stone, the city bells rang out the hour.

Somewhere in the dark beneath the coronation hall, he was certain, Hazel Sterling was already moving toward the vault. Hold on, candle-girl, he thought. Help is coming down the stairs on the legs of a man with quiet knees.

The drips went on. He counted them, and waited for morning, and for the first time since the chains, the waiting did not feel like dying.

It felt like a ledger, balanced, awaiting payment.

And far above, through the prison's high slit of a window, the first grey light found the towers of the coronation hall standing black against the dawn—six days from the hour when every bell in Ashvale would ring, and the hunted would rise.
