## 21. What She Cannot Recall

The morning after the crown fell, Hazel woke in the undercroft and reached, as she had every morning of her life, for the memory that was no longer there.

It was not a dramatic thing. There was no tearing, no scream lodged in her throat. It was worse than that. It was a room she walked into every day and found empty—not stripped bare, not ransacked, simply *empty*, swept and dustless and waiting for furniture that would never come. She knew the shape of what should have been there because everyone had told her: a mother's face. The oldest memory of being loved, freely given at the ledger of the original bargain so that a kingdom could be unbound from its cage.

She had known the cost when she paid it. She had chosen it with open eyes and a steady voice, and she did not regret it—she wanted to be clear about that, even if only to herself, lying on a straw pallet in a smugglers' cellar while rain drummed on stone above her. She did not regret it.

But grief, it turned out, did not care what she wanted to be clear about.

She could not picture Maren Sterling's face.

She could remember *facts* about her mother the way one remembers facts about a queen long dead: dark hair, ink-stained fingers, a habit of humming when she worked the ledgers. She knew these things the way she knew the capital's population or the exchange rate of luck against years—as entries, as data, as lines in a book written by other hands. What she could not do was close her eyes and see. Every time she tried, the space behind her eyelids filled with something like smoke, something like a portrait painted by someone who had only ever heard the woman described.

*She had your chin,* people said. Hazel touched her own chin and felt like a stranger wearing it.

* * *

Three days after the fall, she went back up into the city.

Ashvale was rebuilding the way a wound rebuilds—messily, painfully, from the edges inward. The coronation hall stood with its roof half-open to the sky, and no one seemed in any hurry to close it. Scaffolding climbed the Gildhall's facade like ivy, and through the tall windows Hazel could see citizens moving among the counting tables, reading ledgers aloud in voices that shook. Debts were being struck through in public now, line by line, the way sins were once confessed. A fishwife stood at the head table with the great ledger of borrowed luck open before her, reading her own name and the price she'd paid for a son's fever to break, and the room murmured its assent, and a clerk dipped his pen, and the line came out.

Hazel watched from the doorway until her chest hurt, and then she walked on.

They knew her here. That was the strange part—the part she hadn't prepared for. People turned as she passed. Some bowed their heads. An old dyer pressed a bolt of saffron cloth into her arms and refused to take it back, saying, "For the shroud, when your mother needs one," and Hazel stood in the street holding cloth she had not asked for and could not explain, and realized she had no idea whether her mother was dead or alive, buried or ash or scattered, because the memory that held that answer had been the price of everything.

She had traded away the last true memory of belonging, and belonging was the one thing she could not buy back. Not with the guild dissolved. Not with the ledgers open. Some trades ran only one direction, and she had made hers at the bottom of the world with a dying king watching.

* * *

Bram found her at dusk on the river stairs, where she sat watching the water carry candle-lanterns downstream—one for each name read from the lists she hadn't written, and then, as the days went on, one for each name she had. Hundreds of small lights going out toward the sea.

He sat down beside her without asking, which was one of the thousand things she loved about him. He smelled of wax and river damp. His hands were still bandaged from the turnkey's irons; he flexed them absently, wincing.

"You've been up here three evenings," he said.

"Have I?"

"You have. I counted." He smiled a little. "Counting's catching."

The lanterns slid past below them, gold on black water. Hazel hugged her knees. "I can't see her, Bram."

He didn't pretend to misunderstand. That was another of the thousand things.

"I try every morning," she went on, and her voice came out flat and strange, like a ledger entry read aloud. "I sit down inside my own head and I walk to the place where she should be, and it's just—empty. Clean. Swept." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I paid one memory. One. And it turns out it wasn't one memory, it was the door all the memories hung on. Everything I know about her is secondhand now. Someone told me she hummed. Someone told me she had ink on her fingers. I'm a stranger reading my own mother's file."

Bram said nothing for a while. Below them a child's lantern caught an eddy and spun, stubbornly refusing the current, before the river took it anyway.

"When I was small," he said finally, "my mother sold her wedding day."

Hazel looked at him.

"To the old Gildhall, before your time. We were starving, and it bought a winter's bread." He turned his bandaged hands over. "I used to ask her what it was like, and she'd say: it's like a house you lived in where they've taken the doors off. You remember the rooms. You can't get into any of them." He shrugged. "She got by. But she said the worst part wasn't the missing. The worst part was that everyone else remembered it *for* her, wrong. Her sisters told it back to her with her smiling in places she didn't think she'd smiled, and after enough tellings she couldn't tell anymore what had happened and what they'd handed her."

"So I'll become whatever people say she was," Hazel said quietly.

"No," Bram said. "You'll become what people *show* you she was. There's a difference. My mother taught me that too, near the end. She said memory isn't a possession, Hazel. It's a trade. Always was. The Gildhall just put a price tag on it." He looked at her then, and his eyes in the lantern-light were very steady. "The undercroft owes you more debts than we know how to write down. Let us pay one properly."

"I don't want charity."

"It isn't charity. It's interest. Twenty years of it, compounded." He stood, and offered her his bandaged hand. "Tomorrow night. The undercroft. Everyone who knew her—and there are more of us than you'd believe. Your mother spent thirty years writing our names in books. The least we can do is spend one evening saying them back."

* * *

They gathered in the long chamber beneath the tannery row, the one where the gifted had hidden through two generations of hunts, and they brought candles—of course they did; half of Ashvale's candle-sellers' guild owed its existence to Bram's smuggling—and they lit so many that the low ceiling glowed like the underside of a sunset.

There must have been sixty of them. Old dyers and young scribes, the fishwife from the ledger-reading, a ferryman with river-mud still on his boots, Sereth standing awkwardly at the back with her inquisitor's coat exchanged for plain wool and her hands clasped like a penitent's, as though she still wasn't sure she was allowed to be there. Tam the archivist, the boy Hazel had failed to save from the pyres—no. Not Tam. Tam was ash outside the walls. But Tam's sister stood where he might have, holding one of his candles, and when Hazel saw her she had to press her hand flat against her sternum until the pain passed.

A chair waited at the center of the room. They had draped it in the saffron cloth from the dyer, and set upon it a single unlit candle and a blank page.

"Hazel Sterling," Bram announced, in the voice he used at market, the one that carried, "keeper of accounts. Tonight the account runs the other way."

And they began.

An old woman with knuckles like tree roots went first. She said: "Maren Sterling sat with me the whole night my daughter was born wrong-footed, and she never once opened a ledger. In the morning she wrote the birth in her own book and charged me nothing, and when I protested she said, *the first debt anyone owes is to be witnessed, and that one's already paid.*"

Hazel's breath caught. Not because she remembered—she would never remember—but because the words fit somewhere in her like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known she carried.

A ferryman next. "She crossed with me every flood season, free of charge, to take down the names of the drowned before the river took the bodies. Said the dead deserved accurate books. She'd stand in the rain writing on oiled paper with ink she kept warm against her body."

The fishwife: "My son's fever. Everyone thinks the rain-trade broke it. It wasn't the rain. It was Maren Sterling sitting by his bed for three days telling him stories about a city where nobody owed anybody anything, and him fighting to live long enough to see it."

Sereth, from the back, in a voice roughened by disuse: "She caught me stealing bread from the Gildhall kitchens when I was eleven. Instead of the watch, she gave me a job copying entries, and she never once asked where I'd learned to hold a pen like a knife." A pause. "I have hated myself for a long time for what I did with the gifts she gave me. This is the first evening that has felt like repayment."

One by one. A tanner who remembered her laughing—*laughing*, Hazel thought, *she laughed*—at a mispriced shipment of sorrow. Bram himself, last but one, describing how Maren Sterling had once smuggled a candle-seller's orphaned daughter out of a raid by writing the girl into a ledger as a shipment of beeswax, valuation: negligible.

"She winked at me over the girl's head," Bram said. "Your mother winked at me, Hazel. I've been on the wrong side of every law since, and I swear that wink was the most criminal thing I ever participated in."

Laughter moved through the room like warmth through stone.

And then Tam's sister rose, and the laughter died gently, the way a candle gutters when cupped.

"She wrote my brother's name," the girl said. "Not the false list. Before that. Years before. He was eight, and he'd shown the gift, and my mother was terrified, and Maren Sterling took his name into her private book and said, *as long as I keep this ledger, no fire touches him.*" Her voice broke. "She died before she could keep that promise. But Hazel—he was *in the book*. Somebody wrote him down. Somebody decided he mattered enough for ink. And when the pyres came, the person who wouldn't write the list—" she looked straight at Hazel, eyes streaming, unashamed— "that was you. Same hand. Same ink. I've watched you all week and it's the same hand."

Hazel stood up because her legs would not let her stay seated.

"I can't see her face," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle like the crown had. "I paid it. I'd pay it again—I need you all to know I'd pay it again—but I wake up every morning and she's gone and I am grieving a woman I cannot picture, and I don't know how to do that, I don't know how to mourn someone who is only facts—"

"That's why we're here," Bram said softly.

And then the old dyer began to sing—a low, work-song kind of melody, a tanning song, and one by one the others joined it, and Hazel understood, with a jolt that went through her like the moment the bargain broke, that they weren't singing a song.

They were singing a *description*. Each verse was a detail: the ink on her fingers, the hum, the wink, the warm ink in the rain. And as they sang, the others closed their eyes, and Hazel saw them doing it—saw sixty faces softening as they each conjured the same woman from their separate storehouses of her—and she closed her eyes too, and for the length of the song, assembled out of borrowed light and other people's keeping, a figure stood in the dark behind Hazel's eyelids.

Not her memory. Never her memory again.

But *held*. Held the way a bridge is held—no single stone bearing the weight, the whole span resting on all of them at once.

When the song ended, Tam's sister came forward and lit the candle on the draped chair, and Bram pressed the blank page into Hazel's hands along with a pen.

"For the new ledger," he said. "First entry. Freely given, never owed."

Hazel looked down at the white page. Her hand was shaking.

Then she wrote, in her mother's hand—which was, she was only now allowing herself to understand, also her own:

*Maren Sterling, keeper of the true list. Remembered by sixty witnesses, this night, in the undercroft of Ashvale. Debt: none. Balance: carried forward.*

She signed it.

And as she lifted the pen, the door at the top of the undercroft stairs opened, and cold river air poured down into the candlelight, and a young runner the undercroft used for messages stood silhouetted against it, breathing hard, holding out a folded paper sealed with a smudge of tallow instead of wax.

"From the north road," the runner gasped. "It's marked for Hazel Sterling. Says—it says the sender is her mother's sister."
