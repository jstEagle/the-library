## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The rain came to Caerlum the way it always did: politely, and on schedule.

Emrys Marchetti stood in the doorway of their grandmother's house with a chipped clay mug cooling in their hands and watched the water fall in straight, obedient lines. No wind worried it sideways. No thunder rolled beneath it like a promise. It was good farming rain, honest rain, the kind that asked nothing of anyone—and every morning for thirty-one years, Emrys had looked at it and felt the same small, private grief they had learned to mistake for peace.

*Magic cannot follow you here.* Grandmother Sera had said it once, when Emrys was small enough to still ask questions worth answering. She had been kneeling at the hearth, feeding the fire, and she hadn't turned around. *That is not a punishment, little storm. That is a mercy.*

Emrys had believed her. For a long time, they had needed to believe her.

They stepped off the doorstep into the wet morning and crossed the yard toward the vegetable rows, boots sinking into soil that smelled of iron and rot and green things growing stubbornly toward a sun they could not see behind the cloud cover. The valley of Caerlum lay cupped between two ridgelines like water held in two hands—twelve miles end to end, maybe less, stitched with barley fields and sheep pasture and one road that wandered in from the north pass and seemed, after a while, to forget where it had come from. Travelers passed through sometimes. They never stayed long. They said afterward, if anyone asked, that Caerlum was pleasant enough but forgettable, a valley shaped like any other valley, and they could never quite say why they'd hurried through it.

That was the covenant working. Emrys had understood this by the time they were twelve, though no one had ever told them outright. The valley forgot itself so that the world would forget the valley. And inside its borders, magic forgot them too.

It was a strange thing to live without something you were made of. Emrys had been born a storm-caller—the last of a line that went back further than the temple records in Wrenmere, further than names, back to when people still spoke to the sky and the sky answered. As a child in the lowlands, before Grandmother had carried them over the pass in the dead of night with everything they owned wrapped in a blanket, Emrys's moods had moved weather. A tantrum brought hail in high summer. Grief had once called down lightning onto the family orchard, splitting the oldest pear tree from crown to root. They remembered the smell of it—burnt sweetness, scorched sap—and they remembered their mother's face, which was worse than the smell.

Here, all of that was quiet. Here, Emrys was a farmer with strong forearms and a limp that ached before rain, who traded eggs for salt and salt for lamp oil, whose most remarkable talent was knowing exactly when the barley wanted cutting. The storms lived somewhere far away in their chest, behind a door that would not open, and some mornings Emrys pressed their palm flat against their sternum and felt nothing at all, and told themselves that nothing was better than what the power had cost them.

Some mornings, they almost managed it.

* * *

The shrine stood at the edge of the eastern fields where the land began its slow climb toward the ridge, a circle of seven standing stones furred with moss, around a central altar worn smooth as a riverbed stone. Once—it must have been once, because someone had built it, and building took faith—the stones had marked a place where prayers rose like smoke. Now the moss had taken even the carvings. If gods had ever lived here, they had left no forwarding address.

Grandmother had walked out to the shrine every seventh day for as long as Emrys could remember. She never prayed aloud. She would stand among the stones with her hands folded and her eyes closed, and after a few minutes she would open her eyes, nod once—as if confirming an appointment kept, or a debt paid—and walk home. When Emrys finally asked, at fifteen, what she said to the silent stones, she had smiled with only half her mouth.

*I don't speak to them,* she said. *I keep watch that no one else does.*

She had died four winters ago, in her sleep, with her hands folded, and Emrys had buried her under the pear tree they'd planted the year they arrived—a cutting from the split orchard tree, grown crooked but alive—and inherited the house, the fields, the limp-when-raining ache in their hip, and the weekly walk to the dead shrine.

Emrys walked it now, as they did every seventh day, mud pulling at their boots, the rain thinning to mist around them. Routine was armor. They had learned that early. Do the same things at the same times, greet the same neighbors with the same words, be unremarkable in every particular, and the years will slide past you like water off oiled wool. In thirty-one years, Emrys Marchetti had become so good at being invisible that sometimes they frightened themselves with how completely it worked.

They stopped at the boundary of the stones. Habit put their hands together. Habit closed their eyes.

And then—because habit was armor, but they were alone, and the mist was thick, and no one living had ever heard them say it—they broke their own rule.

"Hello," Emrys whispered. "Is anyone there?"

It was not really a prayer. Prayers asked for things. This was just a greeting, tossed into the silence the way a child tosses a pebble down a well—not hoping for an answer, only wanting to hear the splash, to confirm the depth of the dark.

For thirty-one years, the well had swallowed everything.

This time, the dark answered back.

It was not a voice. Emrys would spend the rest of the day trying to make it into one and failing. It was more like pressure—a soft, vast attention settling over the shrine the way fog settles over water, intimate and enormous at once. The moss on the stones brightened, just slightly, the way coals brighten when breathed upon. Somewhere behind Emrys's ribs, behind the sealed door where the storms lived, something turned over in its sleep.

*Who keeps the Marchetti watch?*

The words arrived whole inside Emrys's mind, the way a memory arrives, and they were old—old the way bedrock is old—with a weariness in them that made Emrys's throat close.

"I—" Their voice cracked. They tried again. "My name is Emrys Marchetti. My grandmother was Sera. She kept this shrine until she died."

A pause. The attention drew closer, and Emrys felt it like a hand hovering an inch from their face, not touching, asking permission to exist.

*Sera's grandchild.* Something that might have been wonder, or might have been alarm. *Then the covenant holds. Then why can I reach you?*

The question dropped through Emrys like a stone through still water. "I don't know what that means," they whispered. "There's no magic here. There hasn't been—not since I was a child. Nothing answers. Nothing *can* answer. That's the whole—"

*That is what we were promised,* the presence said, and now the weariness sharpened into something colder. Fear, Emrys realized. An immortal thing, afraid. *The realms sealed apart. The gods beyond the veil, and the mortals safe within it, and the price paid in full. But the seal is thinning, Marchetti. I should not be able to touch your mind. I should not be able to count your heartbeats. And yet I have been listening to this valley for six days, and I can tell you how many sheep breathe on the western slope.*

The mist had gone strangely still. Not calm—*held*, the way breath is held. Emrys became aware that their hands were shaking, and that the shaking had nothing to do with cold.

"Six days," they repeated. Their voice sounded very small against the stones. "What happened six days ago?"

Silence. Long enough that Emrys thought the presence had withdrawn. Then:

*Something burned,* it said. *Far away, past the edge of your sky. Something old gave up the last of its fire, and the seal drank it like a dying man drinks, and it was not enough. It will not be enough again. The covenant is failing, keeper, and I do not know what happens to a world when the wall between it and us comes down—but I remember what happened the last time the wall was weak, and I have spent three thousand years trying to forget.*

The pressure withdrew. Not all at once—it thinned like fog burning off, reluctant, and the last thread of it brushed across Emrys's mind almost gently, almost apologetically.

*Keep your watch, Marchetti. And when someone comes asking about the fire—and someone will come—remember that your grandmother did not build this silence. She inherited it. Ask yourself what else she buried along with it.*

Then it was gone, and the mist resumed falling, ordinary and indifferent, and Emrys stood alone among the seven stones with their heart slamming against the locked door in their chest.

* * *

They did not go home. Not immediately. They walked the perimeter of the shrine instead, three slow circuits, the way Grandmother used to, touching each stone as they passed. Moss. Lichen. Cold rock underneath. Everything exactly as it had been for as long as Emrys could remember, which was precisely the problem—everything in Caerlum was exactly as it had been, forever, and Emrys had mistaken that permanence for safety rather than questioning what it cost to maintain.

*Your grandmother did not build this silence. She inherited it.*

Inherited. The word sat wrong, like a key that almost fit a lock. People inherited houses and debts and bad hips. What did it mean to inherit a silence? Who had Sera inherited it *from*? And from whom had *they*—

Emrys stopped walking.

The Marchetti name. The presence had known it instantly, had reached for it like a hand reaching for a familiar tool: *Who keeps the Marchetti watch?* Not *who tends this shrine*. Not *who walks these fields*. The watch had a family attached to it. Which meant the family was attached to whatever the watch actually guarded, and Emrys had spent thirty-one years watering leeks and counting sheep on top of something enormous, the way a person might build a garden over a grave without ever digging deep enough to find it.

They thought about Grandmother's hands folded at this altar. Her single nod, once a week, appointment kept. They thought about the night she'd carried them over the pass—how she'd never once looked back at the lowlands, not even when young Emrys had cried and begged to see their mother again. *We don't look back, little storm. Looking back is how they find you.*

They had assumed *they* meant the people who feared storm-callers. The neighbors with torches, the temple wardens, the mob that had come for the orchard. Thirty-one years of assuming, and not once had it occurred to Emrys that *they* might mean something older than mobs. Something that had just spoken to them through moss and mist and admitted, in a voice like grinding continents, that it was afraid.

The rain picked up again. Emrys turned toward home.

The house looked the same as it always did—grey stone, slate roof, smoke curling from the chimney where the morning's banked fire still smoldered. Grandmother's house. Grandmother's hearth. Grandmother's garden, fenced with her own hands, planted in rows too straight for any farmer's vanity. Emrys had lived inside her routines for so long that they had become his own, and he had never once asked himself why a woman who claimed to want nothing but quiet would arrange her life with such ferocious precision. Why the fence posts were set exactly four feet apart. Why she checked the hearthstones every spring, prying them up one by one, inspecting the packed earth beneath with a lantern, then setting them back exactly as they'd been.

Exactly as they'd been.

Emrys stood in the yard, rain running down their face, and looked at the chimney, and felt the first true crack run through the careful invisible architecture of their life. Not fear, exactly—or not only fear. Underneath the fear was something hotter and more dangerous, something that felt disturbingly like the old pressure behind their sternum, the sleeping storms shifting in their sleep.

Because if the silence was failing—if the wall between the worlds was thinning—then the door in Emrys's chest might not stay shut either. And they did not know, could not know, whether what came through it would feel like relief or like ruin.

They went inside. They hung their coat. They knelt at the hearth and fed the fire the way Grandmother had taught them, log by log, and when they were done they did not get up. They stayed kneeling, staring at the grey fieldstone around the firebox, at the seventh stone from the left—the one Grandmother had replaced twice in Emrys's memory, always in early spring, always alone, always telling Emrys to go see to the fences while she worked.

Four feet apart, the fence posts. Seven stones, the shrine. Seven hearthstones, and the seventh always renewed.

Emrys reached out and laid their palm flat against the cool stone.

Underneath, faint as a heartbeat, faint as a coal breathed upon, something answered the warmth of their hand.

Outside, the polite scheduled rain kept falling. Inside, Emrys Marchetti knelt in front of their grandmother's hearth and understood, with terrible clarity, that the life they had built on top of her secrets was about to be dug up—stone by stone, lie by lie—and that whatever lay buried beneath had been waiting for them all along.

The stone shifted, just slightly, under their palm. As if something below had heard them thinking.

As if it had been waiting for exactly this.
