## 4. The God Who Answers

### Wren

Wren had not dreamed properly in three years.

Shrine-keepers learned early to sleep shallow. The temple taught it as discipline—*a keeper's mind is a lamp kept trimmed and lit; do not let the wick drown*—but Wren had come to understand it differently, later, after everything: shallow sleep was a door held half-shut. You slept with your shoulder against it, always, because you never knew what might want to come through.

So when the dream took her that first night in Caerlum, she knew immediately that something had gotten past her shoulder. That alone would have been enough to frighten her. What frightened her more was how *quietly* it happened.

She was standing in the temple courtyard at Vespers-Half, except the courtyard was wrong. The flagstones were the same gray as memory made them, but they stretched away into a fog that had no business existing in a place she could have walked blindfolded. The bell tower rose behind her, and when she looked up at it, the bell had no rope. It hung silent in an arch of black stone, and beneath it, carved where the temple's sunburst sigil should have been, was a mark she did not know: a circle crossed by a single vertical line, like a door standing closed.

"You kept your lamp trimmed," said a voice from everywhere. "I will grant you that. Three years of running, and still you sleep like a keeper."

Wren turned in a slow circle. The fog did not move when she moved. That was the detail her body seized on—the wrongness of it, air that did not stir—and her heart began to hammer against her ribs the way it had the night she'd climbed the wall of the reliquary garden with the censer burning a hole through her pack strap.

"Show yourself," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Small mercies.

The fog *considered*. She felt it considering, the way one feels weather deciding whether to break. Then it parted—not all at once but in a long slow peel, like skin off fruit—and what stood revealed in the courtyard's center was small. That was her first absurd thought: *it's so small.* Barely taller than a child. A figure robed in dove-gray, faceless beneath its hood, hands folded with terrible patience.

"I am called by forty names," it said, "and answered to by none of them anymore." The voice was neither deep nor high. It sounded like a bell heard through water. "You may call me Anselm. I wore that name longest, in the years when the valley folk still left bread on my threshold for safe roads. Do you remember me, Wren of the Ninth Cloister? They taught you my feast day. You sang to me when you were six."

"I don't remember you," Wren said.

"Liar," the god said gently. "But a kind liar. That is a rare thing. I will take it."

The courtyard flickered. For half a heartbeat Wren saw it whole and true—banners snapping, incense smoke climbing in gold ribbons, dozens of white-robed keepers kneeling in the frost—and then it guttered back to gray emptiness, like a fire fed damp wood. Her stomach dropped. She had spent her whole life in that courtyard and had never once thought of it as *alive* until she saw it dead.

"What is this?" she asked. "What do you want?"

"What any beggar wants." The small god unfolded its hands. Beneath the robe, where fingers should have been, there was only a soft blur, like a figure painted by someone who had never seen hands. "I want to be carried. I am too worn thin to walk even this far"—the blurred hands gestured at the fog—"and thinner every hour. But you, keeper. You have strong legs and a stronger stubbornness. I watched you climb out of our garden with stolen fire in your pack and no plan beyond *away*. Stubbornness like that can move mountains, or so we used to tell our faithful. We lied about many things. Not that."

Wren's hand went instinctively to her collarbone, where the brand sat beneath her shirt—the keeper's mark, pressed into her skin at ordination, hot iron and holy oil. It ached now. It had been aching since Caerlum, since the ash in her censer had begun to glow brighter each night instead of dimmer, which was impossible, which she had told herself every evening was impossible, and which glowed anyway.

"The fire," she said slowly. "The dragon-fire. Is that why I'm dreaming this?"

Anselm went very still. Even faceless, even blurred at the edges, the god managed stillness like a held breath.

"You carry it," the god said. "Of course you carry it. Of course it came to *you*." Something in the voice cracked—not anger, Wren realized. Grief. Old, water-worn grief, like a riverbed stone. "Then you already know more than most. Tell me, child of the Ninth Cloister: what did they teach you of the Quiet Valley? Caerlum, they called it in the old maps. The Hollow. Did they teach you why the gods do not answer there?"

"They taught us the gods withdrew from everywhere," Wren said. "That faith must stand on its own legs. That anyone claiming to hear the divine is—" she recited it flatly, the way she'd recited it a thousand times in a thousand lessons— "*either deceived or deceiving.*"

"Yes. And yet here I am, deceiving or deceived, standing in your borrowed courtyard asking favors." Anselm laughed, and the sound made the dead bell tower shiver. "They built good walls around their lie. I will say that for them. But walls are not silence, Wren, whatever your teachers believed. Silence has an author. And the author is dying."

The fog pulsed once, darkly, and Wren felt it in her chest—a pressure, a coldness, as if the whole gray world were a lung forgetting how to fill.

"There is a dragon," the god said. "Vaelith. Last of the fire-drakes, last of the old witnesses. When the realms warred—before your temple, before my names, before nearly everything—it was Vaelith who ended it. She gave her own flame to seal the bargain between the divine and the mortal. She burned her fire into law. And the price of that law was a silence between worlds: gods shut on one side, mortals on the other, and nothing crossing over. Nothing answering prayers. Nothing *listening*."

"And Caerlum—"

"Was the keystone." Anselm's hood tilted. "A valley where the silence was made flesh. Held, all these generations, by mortal guarantors. Blood sworn to keep the door shut. There is such a bloodline living in this valley right now, keeper. They do not know what they inherited. They think their grandmother was merely strange."

Wren thought of the mark she'd glimpsed on Emrys Marchetti's wrist that morning, when the stranger had reached across the market stall to steady a falling jar of honey. A coiled line, old ink faded to silver, shaped like nothing she'd ever seen in temple iconography—but the records in the restricted archive had described it precisely, and she'd spent three nights of her flight comparing memory against memory, heart pounding, before daring to follow the road down into the valley.

"A storm-caller," she said aloud. "Living where magic cannot follow."

"A storm-caller living where magic cannot follow," Anselm agreed, "is a candle burning inside a locked box. The box does not extinguish the candle. It only keeps the light from touching anything. But the box is cracking, keeper. The silence is thinning everywhere, and thinnest here, at its keystone. Can you not feel it? Your little censer glowing brighter instead of darker. My voice reaching you across a boundary that should have stopped me cold. The door is not merely opening. It is *failing*. And when a door fails rather than opens, what comes through does not come gently."

The pressure in Wren's chest tightened. She wrapped her arms around herself and hated how young it made her feel, how much the gesture belonged to the six-year-old who had sung to a nameless god on his feast day.

"What comes through?" she whispered.

"That depends entirely," said Anselm, "on whether someone reaches Vaelith before her flame goes out. If the fire dies unbound, the covenant dies with it—and every prayer ever unanswered, every god ever shut out, every hunger ever sealed away comes through at once, with sixty generations of silence behind it. But if someone carries word to her—if someone helps reforge what was broken—then perhaps the door is opened instead of broken. Perhaps what crosses over comes as guest, not flood." The blurred hands spread wide. "I am asking you to carry word. To a dying dragon, through a land that has forgotten how to listen. I know what I am asking. I was there when the price was set."

"Why me?" The words burst out of her harder than she meant them. "I ran away. I stole from the temple. I'm not—I broke my vows before I ever spoke them, everyone says so, my mentor *said* so—"

"Wren." The god said her name and the whole courtyard went quiet in a new way, a listening way. "Do you know why the temple teaches keepers to sleep shallow? Why they train you to hold that door?"

"To keep things out."

"To keep *you* in," Anselm said. "Every keeper is a door too. Every one of you. The temple found that out long ago and decided the safest thing was to make you all into locks. But you were never a lock, child. You were a hinge pretending to be one. That is why the censer chose you. That is why I chose you. Doors fail. Hinges *turn*."

The fog was beginning to thin at its edges, and through it Wren could see—faintly, unbearably—the real world leaking in: the low rafters of the Marchetti house, the shape of the hearth banked for the night, the gray beginning of dawn.

"Wait," she said. "Wait—how do I find her? Where is the hollow? What do I—"

"Follow the ash," Anselm said. His voice was fading with the fog, going thin as paper. "It remembers its mother. And keeper—one thing more. One thing I beg of you, though begging shames us both. The blood-guarantor. The storm-caller. Whatever you decide, whatever road you choose—" the blurred hands pressed together, a gesture that might have been prayer or plea— "*do not leave without them.* The covenant knows their name. If it is to be reforged, it must be reforged by the name it was forged with. Take the Marchetti with you, or all of this is ash."

And then the courtyard folded up like a letter being put away, and Wren woke with her cheek against cold floorboards, the censer glowing in her open pack like a small furious sunrise, and her own brand burning beneath her collarbone as though someone had just taken fresh iron to it.

She lay still for a long time, listening to the house breathe, and tried to decide which frightened her more: that a god had begged her a favor, or that he'd been right about the hinge.

### Emrys

Emrys woke to thunder that wasn't there.

It came the way it always had, before the valley—first as a pressure behind the eyes, then a taste like coins on the tongue, then the deep sub-audible roll that lived in the bones rather than the ears. They lay rigid beneath their blanket in the dark of the loft, counting heartbeats, waiting for it to pass the way it had passed ten thousand times over thirty-odd years. *Storms happen elsewhere,* Grandmother used to say, her hand cool on Emrys's hair. *Here, the sky keeps its opinions to itself.*

But the pressure didn't pass. It gathered. And beneath it, faint and rhythmic, came a sound from below stairs: humming. Wren was awake, and she was humming the Vespers cadence—the one every temple-raised child learned before they learned letters—and she was humming it *wrong*, dragging the third note, the way country congregations did it, the way people did it who had learned the song from grandmothers rather than cloisters. From people who'd sung it hoping someone was listening.

Emrys sat up slowly. Through the floorboards, lamplight bled upward in a thin gold seam. The house smelled of woodsmoke and rain-that-hadn't-fallen-yet, and every hair on their arms stood at attention.

They climbed down the ladder more quietly than a person their size should have managed, a skill honed across decades of wanting not to be noticed, and paused at the edge of the kitchen light.

Wren sat at the table with the censer open before her. The ash inside was glowing—that was wrong enough, ash did not glow, ash was the *end* of burning—but that wasn't what stopped Emrys's breath. What stopped it was that the glow pulsed. Slowly. Steadily. Like something breathing. Like a heartbeat keeping time with the phantom thunder rolling through Emrys's skull.

"You feel it too," Wren said, without looking up.

"Don't know what you mean." The old deflection, smooth as a well-worn shutter.

"The pressure. Behind the eyes." Now she looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and ferociously awake. "Like a storm that won't commit. I've felt it since I crossed the boundary. I thought it was me—withdrawal, maybe, or guilt. But it's louder tonight. And Emrys—" her voice dropped, went careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something fragile across a room— "it wasn't loud at all until I met you."

Emrys leaned against the doorframe and crossed their arms, and was annoyed to discover the pose was less refusal than self-holding. "You've been here four days. You've told me stories about glowing ash and temple archives and a birthmark you seem to think means something. I've told you nothing, because there's nothing to tell. I'm a beekeeper's granddaughter. I mend fences. Magic doesn't work here—any fool with a kettle knows that."

"Then explain the ash."

"It's a trick of the light."

"At midnight."

"Persistent light."

Wren exhaled through her nose. And then, instead of arguing, she did something worse: she slid the censer across the table toward Emrys, into the space between the lamp and the edge, an offering or a dare.

"Touch it," she said.

"No."

"Please."

"Absolutely not." But even as Emrys said it, the pressure behind their eyes swelled, and the taste of coins flooded their mouth so suddenly and so completely that they gagged, and the lamp flame bent sideways though there was no draft, bending *toward* them, toward their hands, like a plant toward sun.

Wren watched the flame bend. Watched their face. And Emrys watched her watching, and understood with sick clarity that the conversation had just passed some border that couldn't be uncrossed.

"You dreamed something," Emrys said quietly. "Last night. Tonight. Both. That's why you're awake humming to the dark like a woman at a deathbed."

Wren's jaw tightened. "How did you—"

"My grandmother hummed like that. Whenever the sky did something she didn't like." The admission escaped before Emrys could catch it, and once out, it pulled others after it, the way pulling one thread ruins the cloth. They crossed the kitchen—when had they decided to cross it?—and sat down heavily across from her, and looked at the breathing ash, and heard themselves say: "Tell me what your god showed you."

For a moment Wren simply stared. Then, in a low and halting voice, she told it: the courtyard, the fog, the small gray figure with blurred hands. The dragon named Vaelith. The covenant. The silence with an author. *The author is dying.* The door failing rather than opening. And last, delivered with her eyes fixed firmly on the table, the plea: *do not leave without them.*

When she finished, the kitchen was so quiet Emrys could hear the honey jars settling on their shelf, and the ash pulsing, and underneath both, patient as tide, the thunder that wasn't there.

"My grandmother," Emrys said at last, "had a saying. She said this valley was *asleep*, and that we should all be grateful, and that anyone who tried to wake it would answer for it." Their hands had begun, without their permission, to tremble. "I always thought it was a figure of speech. She had hundreds of them. *The sky keeps its opinions to itself.* *Don't sing on the ridge, the wind carries.* *We don't pray in this house.*" A laugh came out of them, cracked down the middle. "Gods. We didn't pray in this house. I thought she was just—irreligious. Everyone here is. It's practically a civic virtue. Nobody prays in Caerlum. I never once wondered *why* nobody prays in Caerlum."

"Because it's not a custom," Wren said softly. "It's a term of a contract. And I think your family signed it."

The trembling got worse. Emrys pressed their palms flat against the tabletop, feeling the grain, the honest ordinary wood of it, trying to anchor themselves in the mundane. But the mundane had developed a crack down its center, and through the crack poured everything they had spent a lifetime not-feeling: the ache of power with nowhere to go, the itch under the skin on stormy days that they'd told themselves was arthritis at thirty, the way birds went silent when they walked past, the way fires leaned. All the small wrongnesses of a lifetime, suddenly arranging themselves into a single legible sentence.

*You are the candle in the box,* the gray god had apparently said, according to Wren. *The box does not extinguish the candle. It only keeps the light from touching anything.*

"I need to show you something," Emrys heard themself say.

They rose and went to the hearth. The fire had burned down to a bed of embers, breathing its own slow light, and above it the mantel held the usual clutter of a life: chipped cups, a tin of matches, a wooden horse Grandfather had carved, and the framed portrait of Grandmother that Emrys dusted weekly and avoided looking at daily. Beneath the mantel, the third hearthstone from the left—the one that sat a finger's width proud of its neighbors, the one Grandmother had scolded seven-year-old Emrys for prying at with a spoon—waited as it had always waited.

Emrys knelt. The stone came loose easier than stone had any right to, as if it had been loosening for decades in anticipation, and beneath it, nested in a bed of ancient wool, lay a flat slab of dark rock the size of a hymnal.

The moment their fingers touched it, every candle in the room flared white.

Wren made a small strangled sound. The ash in her censer blazed so bright it threw shadows *upward*. And the thunder that wasn't there rolled through the house at last like a verdict, rattling the cups on their shelf, and outside, far off over the ridge, real lightning answered it—a single silent flicker, there and gone, the first lightning Caerlum's sky had shown in living memory.

On the slab, in lines cut deep and worn shallow by time, characters glittered faintly. Wren leaned close, lips moving, reading the old script the way only a keeper could, and her face went through recognition, disbelief, and finally something like mourning.

"*By the blood of Marchetti, the door is kept,*" she read aloud, voice shaking. "*By the fire of Vaelith, the door is sealed. Let none within pray outward, let none without answer inward, until the guarantor speaks release.*" She looked up. "There's more. A date. And a name repeated three times—your grandmother's name, Emrys. She's listed as *second guarantor*. There's a first name above hers, struck through. And below her—" Wren stopped.

"Below her what?"

Wren swallowed. "Below her, the stone lists a third guarantor. The line's blank. But the groove for carving is there. It was always meant to be filled." She met Emrys's eyes, and the blazing ash lit her face from below like something out of an illuminated page. "The covenant expects an heir."

Outside, the thunder rolled again, closer now, and the pressure behind Emrys's eyes crested into something that was almost pain, and they understood—with the terrible clarity that arrives only once, at the exact moment a life splits into before and after—that the sky had stopped keeping its opinions to itself because the sky had realized, before they had, that Emrys Marchetti had just picked up the contract.

"We leave tomorrow," they said, before they could talk themselves out of it. "Before I change my mind. Pack light."

Wren nodded once, keeper-calm, and began banking the censer with hands that weren't quite steady either.

Above the house, unseen, the clouds over Caerlum gathered like an audience taking their seats.
