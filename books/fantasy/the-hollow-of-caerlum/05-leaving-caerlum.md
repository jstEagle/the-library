## 5. Leaving Caerlum

They left at dawn, because Wren said the boundary would be easier to cross in the grey half-light, when the world was still deciding what it wanted to be.

Emrys did not argue. They had not argued since the night they'd opened the hearth and found the covenant-stone with the Marchetti name cut into it like a scar that had never been allowed to heal. Arguing required a position, and Emrys had spent three days without one, moving through their grandmother's house like a guest at a funeral for someone they'd hated.

The house was locked now. The key was under the third flagstone, where it had always been, and Emrys had set it back exactly as they'd found it, though there was no one left to find it. Some habits outlive their reasons. That was practically the motto of Caerlum.

Wren waited at the gate with her censer swinging from its chain, the ash inside glowing a faint, stubborn orange — the last dragon-fire in the world, or close enough to it that Emrys's chest tightened every time they looked at it. She had braided her hair back and wrapped her feet in strips of blanket, and she looked, in the pre-dawn murk, like exactly what she was: a girl who had run a very long way and hadn't stopped running yet.

"You don't have to come," Emrys said, for the fourth time.

"And you don't have to say that," Wren said, "for the fifth time." She turned east, toward the hills that cupped the valley's edge like a hand around water. "Come on. I want to be over the line before the sun's fully up. The god said dawn is when the veil's thinnest."

"The god," Emrys repeated flatly. "You're taking scheduling advice from the god who visits your dreams."

"He seemed very sure of himself."

"They usually are."

Wren glanced at them sideways. "You've met gods?"

"I've met *silence*," Emrys said. "For forty years. It's possible I've forgotten the difference."

That wasn't entirely fair, and they both knew it. Emrys had been born before the silence finished settling — old enough to remember, in fragments, what it was to feel the storm in their teeth. Their grandmother had carried them up this same path when they were six years old, feverish with a power that kept leaking out of them in little crackles and sparks, and had stood at the valley's edge and said, *Once we cross, it will stop hurting. Once we cross, you'll be safe.* And Emrys, six years old and burning alive from the inside, had believed her, because six-year-olds believe grandmothers, and because the pain *had* stopped. Like a limb going numb. Like mercy.

It had taken thirty-four years to understand that numbness is not the same as healing.

The path climbed through birch scrub gone gold with autumn, and neither of them spoke much. Emrys noticed things with an attention that felt almost violent — the exact weight of dew on a spiderweb, the particular grey of Wren's eyes when she thought no one was looking, the way the censer's glow pulsed slightly faster as they gained altitude, like a heartbeat quickening on approach to something it recognized. Forty years of dullness were peeling off the world, and Emrys didn't know if it was the covenant failing or simply the fact that they were leaving it, but everything looked like it might be the last time.

Because it might be. That was the other thing they weren't saying. Beyond the boundary, magic could follow them again — all of it, all at once, after four decades of drought. Emrys had tried to imagine what that would feel like and had produced only images of floodwaters and broken dams. Their grandmother had crossed back and forth her whole life, apparently, carrying the covenant's weight in both directions, and she had never once told them what the crossing felt like.

Maybe she'd been protecting them. Maybe she'd been ashamed. With Grandmother Marchetti, those had always been the same gesture viewed from different angles.

"Here." Wren stopped so suddenly that Emrys nearly walked into her back.

There was nothing to see. That was the unsettling part. A fold of hillside, a stand of birches, a stream chuckling down over stones — ordinary valley country, the kind Emrys had walked a thousand times. But the air had a texture to it here, a faint resistance, like the skin on warm milk. And the censer's ash had gone from orange to a deep, urgent red, throwing Wren's shadow long and sharp across the grass.

"This is it?" Emrys asked.

"This is what the dream showed." Wren's voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was frightened and managing it. "There was a stone. There was supposed to be a stone."

There was. Half-buried in the turf, moss-eaten, no bigger than a loaf of bread — and carved, when Emrys knelt and brushed the growth away, with the same spiral-and-line sigil that marked the covenant-stone under the hearth. The Marchetti mark. Their grandmother must have stood right here, on some morning decades ago, and pressed her palm against this stone and said whatever words sealed the valley shut.

"She did this alone," Emrys heard themselves say. "She came up here alone and she closed the door on the whole world, and then she went home and made dinner and never told anyone."

"Maybe telling people wasn't allowed."

"Maybe." Emrys stood. Their legs felt strange, light and heavy at once. "Or maybe she knew that if she said it out loud, someone would have talked her out of it. Someone would have said, *Ilsa, you can't seal your granddaughter away from half of what she is just because it frightens you.*"

"Do you think someone should have?"

Emrys looked at the stone. At the thin, milk-skin shimmer in the air beyond it. At Wren, whose whole life had also been rearranged by other people's covenants — temple vows she'd never chosen, a name in a ledger she'd fled.

"Yes," Emrys said. "And no. And I'm tired of both answers. Let's go."

They stepped over the stone together.

* * *

For three steps, nothing happened.

On the fourth step, the world remembered Emrys Marchetti.

It began as sound — a low hum in the bones of their skull, like standing too near a struck bell. Then heat, pouring up through the soles of their feet as if the ground itself had been waiting years to give something back. Then light: not real light, but the memory of it, the ghost of every storm they'd ever called flickering behind their eyes, and beneath all of it, rising like water into a cracked riverbed, the *power* —

Emrys went to their knees.

It hurt. That was the first true thing. All the stories made magic coming home sound like joy, like wings unfolding, and there *was* joy in it, somewhere, buried deep — but mostly it was pain, vast and specific, the pain of a limb waking after decades of sleep, every nerve firing at once, blood hammering back into tissue that had forgotten how to hold it. Emrys felt forty years of stored pressure break loose inside their chest like a keg giving way. The storm-calling — theirs, theirs, *theirs*, the thing they had been named for and exiled for and had learned to pretend was only a story — roared awake and demanded *out*.

"Emrys?" Wren's voice, very far away. "Emrys, your eyes—"

Lightning crawled along their arms. Not summoned. Leaking. Blue-white threads dancing over their knuckles, jumping to the wet grass, and the hair on their forearms stood up, and above them the dawn sky — clear grey, mild, an ordinary Caerlum-adjacent morning — began to churn.

*Stop,* Emrys told themselves. *Contain it. You know how, you knew how even as a child—*

But knowing how to hold a cup and holding one are different things, and their hands had been empty for forty years. The power poured through them like the stream through a burst millrace, and every instinct they'd built in the valley — dampen, dim, disappear, be small — was exactly wrong here, was a dam built backwards, was holding the flood *in* until the banks gave.

The sky split.

Not rain — not yet — but wind, sudden and screaming, flattening the birches, tearing Wren's braid loose, ripping the censer from her grip so it spun away trailing red light. The clouds above had gone the color of a bruise, rotating slowly, and the first thunderclap hit like a physical blow, close enough that Emrys felt it in their fillings, their ribs, the roots of their teeth.

"EMRYS!" Wren had thrown herself flat, fingers hooked in the turf. "Whatever's happening — make it STOP!"

"I'm TRYING—" The words tore out of them half-swallowed by wind. They clawed for control the way a drowning person claws for the surface, and found — nothing. No handle. No shape. Just the raw torrent and their own heartbeat, which had picked up the storm's rhythm, which was becoming the storm's rhythm, which meant that when the lightning finally discharged it would discharge through *them*, all of it, forty years of it, and anything standing nearby—

Anything standing nearby.

Wren.

The realization cut through the roar like cold iron. Emrys looked down at the girl clinging to the hillside — small, stubborn, ash-marked, the first person in decades who had looked at Emrys's bloodline and seen a person instead of a problem — and understood with terrible clarity that they were about to kill her. Not out of malice. Out of arithmetic. Forty years of held breath, released all at once, in a radius wide enough to flatten the hillside.

*No.*

The word had no magic in it. It was just a word, just a human refusal, and perhaps that was why it worked — because underneath the storm-caller, underneath the guarantor's blood, there was still just a person, and the person said *no*.

Emrys reached for the storm the way you reach for a panicked animal. Not to cage it. To *hold* it. To be the thing it trusts.

And for one endless second, they hung between — the power streaming through them, vast and delighted and utterly indifferent to the small soft world below, and Emrys's own will, frayed and terrified and absolutely unwilling to let go of one specific girl on one specific hillside.

Then they turned it *up*.

It was the opposite of every instinct. Instead of clamping down, Emrys opened — gave the storm somewhere to go, shaped their own body into a channel, a lightning rod, a throat. The discharge ripped through them and out through their raised arm and into the sky, a single blinding column that punched through the cloud-base and lit the whole valley rim white. The thunder that followed rolled away eastward, echoing off hills that had not heard thunder in forty years, and the pressure broke, and the wind died, and Emrys collapsed face-first into the wet grass.

* * *

Rain came later — gentle, apologetic rain, the kind that follows a tantrum. Emrys lay on their back in it and watched the bruised clouds tear open into ordinary grey, and shook, and shook, and shook.

Every nerve ending sang. That was the horrifying miracle of it: it *felt incredible*. Forty years of muffled grey and now the world arrived all at once — the taste of ozone and crushed grass, the individual cool points of raindrops, the distant boom of their own thunder still wandering the hills like a dog that had slipped its lead. Power pooled in their chest, quiet now but *present*, a banked fire where there had been only ash. Emrys had forgotten they were this large inside. They had genuinely forgotten.

They started laughing. It came out ragged and half-hysterical, and they didn't try to stop it.

"Are you *hurt*?" Wren's face appeared above them, upside down, dripping, furious. There was a streak of mud across one cheekbone and her eyes were enormous. "Are you — you're laughing. You nearly killed us both and you're laughing."

"I nearly killed us both," Emrys agreed, "and I'm laughing." They pushed themselves up on shaking arms. "I'm sorry. I'm — Wren, I'm so sorry, I thought I had more control than—"

"You didn't know." She sat down hard beside them, wringing out her braid. "You couldn't have. Nobody crosses that line after forty years and knows." A pause. Rain pattered on the turf between them. "My mentor used to say the first prayer after a long silence always comes out wrong. Too loud. Asking for the wrong thing."

"That's reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be *true*." She looked at them sidelong. "Does it always feel like that? Like being set on fire in a good way?"

"I don't remember it feeling like anything," Emrys said. "When I was a child it just hurt. This is the first time it's ever been—" They searched for the word and didn't like any of the ones they found. "*Mine*. Which frightens me more than the hurting did, honestly."

"Why?"

"Because hurting things, you want to put down. Mine, you want to keep." Emrys flexed their fingers. Faint blue threads answered, dancing between fingertips like tame fish, and they closed their fist and snuffed them. Already easier. Already the channel widening, learning its new-old shape. "Forty years ago my grandmother brought me over that line and the power went quiet, and I built a whole life in the quiet. A good life. Small, but good. And now I'm sitting in the rain feeling more awake than I've felt since I was six years old, and some traitorous part of me is thinking, *why did we ever leave? Why did we stay numb for one single day?*"

"And the rest of you?"

"The rest of me remembers that I nearly flattened a hillside because I was happy." Emrys looked east, past the boundary stone, past the birches, to where the land fell away into country they had never seen — real country, unsealed country, country where prayers got answered and gods walked loudly and a dying dragon waited with the last fire in the world. Somewhere out there, the covenant was failing, and every day it failed, the world outside grew louder and stranger and less survivable for people who'd forgotten how to listen.

"We keep walking," they said. "But we do it differently. I tell you what I'm feeling — all of it, even when it's ugly — and you watch me. If the storm starts rising and I don't feel it coming, you say so. Loudly. You have my permission to be rude about it."

Wren considered this. "Can I throw things?"

"...Within reason."

"Then yes." She scrambled up, retrieved the censer from where it had landed in a gorse bush, still glowing, still stubbornly alive. When she turned back, her expression had shifted into something Emrys hadn't seen on her before — not wariness, not curiosity, but the beginning of trust, offered carefully, like something that had been dropped before. "For what it's worth — the way you turned it. At the end. You didn't fight it, you *aimed* it. My mentor said that was the hardest lesson in shrine-craft and most people never learn it. You learned it in about ten seconds, while screaming."

"I had excellent motivation."

"Yeah." She looked east too, and her jaw tightened. "So did I."

They walked. The land beyond the boundary was the same land, geologically speaking — same hills, same gorse, same rain — but Emrys felt it differently now, felt *everything* differently, the way a deafened person who regains hearing finds the world almost too loud to bear and cannot stop listening anyway. Every stream whispered. Every wind carried voices at its edges, half-formed, wanting things. The old gods, waking. The covenant, fraying thread by thread. A whole realm stirring from the same long silence Emrys had just escaped, and none of it gentle, all of it hungry for attention after forty years of neglect.

By midday they could see it: far to the southeast, a smudge on the horizon that was not cloud. Dark, and getting darker, and arranged in a way clouds were not.

Wren saw it too. She stopped, and the censer's glow reflected in her eyes, and her voice when it came was very small.

"Emrys. That's the direction of the dragon-hollow."

"I know."

"It's *moving*."

Emrys watched the dark smudge crawl slowly across the horizon, and felt the storm wake again in their chest — not raging now, but alert, attentive, like a hound lifting its head.

"Then we walk faster," they said.
