## 6. Road of Small Prayers

The first village beyond the valley was called Merrow's Ford, and it smelled of tallow and fear.

Wren noticed the smell before anything else—she'd spent nine years in a temple where incense covered everything, and she had learned to read what burned beneath it. Tallow meant cheap candles, burned in quantity. Fear meant people praying hard enough to wear through wax like water through stone.

"Keep your hood up," Emrys murmured beside her. They walked with their shoulders drawn in, hands buried in their sleeves, doing their best impression of an ordinary traveler. It wasn't a good impression. Emrys moved like someone perpetually listening for thunder.

"I know how to walk into a village," Wren said. "I did it for a living."

"You walked into temples. This is different."

She wanted to argue, but the evidence was all around them. The shrine at Merrow's Ford stood at the crossroads where the road bent toward the river—a squat stone thing, older than any of the houses, its niche empty of any god's image. But it was *drowned* in offerings. Candle stubs stacked like cordwood. Ribbons tied so thickly to the iron rail that they'd fused into one matted pelt. A wall of small clay tablets, each pressed with a thumbprint and a wish, some cracked from being kissed too many times.

And every single offering was *fresh*. None of it grayed by weather. Someone came here daily, or hourly, or perhaps never left.

"They're answered," Wren said quietly. She stopped at the rail without meaning to. Her hand rose the way it always rose, the way nine years of training had carved into her body, reaching to straighten a crooked ribbon—and then she caught herself and pulled it back down.

Emrys watched her do it. Said nothing. That was worse than commentary.

"The prayers," Wren clarified, because silence was a habit she was trying to break. "In Caerlum, nothing answers. Here—" She touched one of the clay tablets. The wish scratched into it was simple: *let my son's fever break.* Below it, in a different, shakier hand: *it broke. he is well. i owe everything.*

"That's good, isn't it?" Emrys said. "A boy who didn't die."

"Yes." Wren turned the tablet over. On the back, smaller, nearly illegible: *i owe everything. i owe everything. i owe everything.* "It depends on who collects."

They found the collector at the inn, or rather the inn found them. The common room was full, but the noise dropped when they entered—not hostile, not yet, just that particular hush of a room taking inventory of strangers. Wren counted exits out of habit. Two: the door behind them and a kitchen passage half-hidden by a hanging curtain of dried herbs.

A woman approached before they'd even reached the bar. She was perhaps fifty, dressed in undyed wool, her hair bound in a white cord—the kind of plainness that cost more money than finery. Around her neck hung a censer on a chain, and Wren's whole body went still at the sight of it, because she knew that shape, knew the weight of it in the hand, knew the way the lid's hinges were supposed to sing when it swung.

It didn't sing. The hinges had been soldered shut.

"Pilgrims?" the woman asked warmly. Too warmly. Her eyes went to Wren's hood, to the way she stood, to something in her posture that Wren realized too late must have screamed *temple-trained* to anyone who knew what to look for.

"Travelers," Emrys said smoothly. "Looking for beds."

"Everyone's looking for beds. Everyone's looking for everything, now." The woman smiled. "I'm Mother Sarelle. I keep the Answering here. You'll have heard of us—the fever children, the mended wells, the harvest that came twice last year. The gods are *listening* again, friends. First time in living memory. Some say first time in a hundred years." She leaned closer, and beneath the warmth Wren caught the other smell under the tallow: ash. Old ash, banked ash, ash kept alive the way you keep a coal alive overnight. "You'll want to give thanks properly while you're here. Gratitude keeps the channel open."

"We don't have coin for gratitude," Emrys said.

Sarelle's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a shutter drawing back from a window. "Gratitude isn't coin. Yet." She laughed, and the room laughed with her, a beat too quickly, a note too high. "Beds are two coppers. Supper's included if you attend evening devotion."

Wren paid the four coppers for two beds and did not say a word until they were alone in the narrow room above the common room, the ceiling low enough that Emrys had to stoop.

"Soldered hinges," Wren said.

"What?"

"On her censer. Temple censers are built to swing free—the sound is part of the rite, the little bell-note that tells the god you're coming. Hers has been sealed shut." Wren sat on the bed, which was less a bed than a pallet with ambitions. "That's not devotion. That's control. You seal a censer when you don't trust what your people might hear in the ringing."

Emrys sat across from her, knees nearly touching hers in the small room. "You think she's faking it? The answered prayers?"

"No." Wren rubbed her eyes. "That's what frightens me. The prayers are real. Something out there really is answering. She's just... standing in front of the door and charging admission."

Outside, the evening bell rang—or rather, a hammer struck a bar of iron, three dull notes with no music in them at all. Devotion, presumably. Through the floorboards they heard the common room empty itself, everyone flowing obediently toward the crossroads shrine.

Wren lay back and stared at the beams. Nine years she had kept shrines. Swept them, dressed them, tended the flame, memorized the litanies, learned forty-one ways to fold a prayer-cloth depending on the season and the god addressed. And in all those years, not once—not *once*—had anything answered. She had told herself that was the nature of faith: the seed goes into the dark and you water it anyway. Now the seeds were sprouting everywhere she looked, and every green shoot made her feel sick, because she could see the shape of the garden being built around them.

*Gratitude keeps the channel open.* And whoever stood at the channel decided what gratitude cost.

* * *

They left Merrow's Ford at dawn, before devotion, and Wren felt the village's eyes follow them out like burrs picked up on wool.

The second village was worse.

Its name was Harrowgate, and they knew something was wrong a mile out, because the fields were fallow. Not poor—fallow, deliberately, weeds waist-high in soil that should have been under autumn wheat. At the village edge, a sign had been nailed to a post, the letters painted fresh:

**THE GODS GAVE. WE GIVE BACK. STRANGERS WHO TAKE WITHOUT GIVING WILL BE GIVEN TO THE FLAME.**

"Well," Emrys said. "That's welcoming."

"We can turn around."

"We can't. The road east is the only road east." Emrys pulled their hood lower. "We go through. Quietly. We buy bread, we don't pray, we leave."

Buying bread was the mistake. Or rather, buying bread *without the right words* was.

The baker was a broad, tired man whose forearms were scarred with old burns. He sold them a loaf and a wedge of hard cheese and asked no questions, and Wren was almost relaxed again when a woman in the queue behind them saw Emrys's face—saw, Wren would realize later, the particular stillness of someone who had grown up untouched by answered prayer, the look of a person the gods had never fed—and began to scream.

"*Ungrateful.*"

The word went through the market square like a spark through dry flax.

"Ungrateful! They walk through Harrowgate and they don't kneel, they don't thank, they *buy bread like it's theirs*—" The woman's voice climbed. Others turned. Wren saw the arithmetic happen in their faces, the same arithmetic she'd seen in temple records of the old purges: stranger plus impiety equals permission.

The baker set his hand flat on his counter. "Marta. They're travelers. Maybe they come from somewhere the gods haven't reached yet."

"There's nowhere the gods haven't reached." Marta's voice dropped to something worse than screaming: certainty. "There's only places that haven't *thanked* yet."

The crowd was forming the way crowds form, not deciding to be a mob but discovering it already had. Wren counted: twelve, fifteen, more drifting in from side lanes. Iron tools in some hands. Torches unlit—it was midday—but pitch pots sitting by doorways, ready.

Emrys's breath had gone strange and shallow. Wren recognized it: they were reaching for magic that wasn't there anymore, or wasn't there *here*, and the absence was panicking them. "Wren," they said, very quietly. "I can't—"

"I know." She stepped forward, out of Emrys's shadow, and did the last thing either of them had planned.

She knelt.

Not to the crowd—to the little roadside shrine at the square's edge, the one she'd clocked on arrival, its niche holding a smooth river-stone dressed in a strip of red cloth. She knelt the way she'd been trained to kneel, spine straight, palms open on her thighs, head bowed at exactly the angle that said *this person knows what they're doing.* Then she rose, took the red cloth, refolded it in the third of the forty-one folds—the one for gods of hearth and grain, the one that said *your gift is received and returned*—and retied it with a knot that only shrine-keepers used, a knot that had no purpose except to prove the tier had been taught by someone legitimate.

The square went quiet.

"She's ordained," someone whispered. "Look at the fold. My grandmother folded like that. Before."

Wren kept her face grave and her heart hammering and lied with her whole body. "I am a keeper of the old rites," she said, pitching her voice the way Mentor Ilsa had taught her, low enough to require leaning in, certain enough to require no proof. "I have walked far, and I will tell you what I have seen: the gods are answering everywhere now. And in every place, they answer most where gratitude is *freely given*, not demanded." She let her gaze pass over Marta, gentle and pitiless. "Fear is not thanks. A gift taken by threat is theft wearing a gift's clothes. You have been blessed. Do not let the blessing curdle."

Silence. Long enough that Wren heard her own pulse.

Then the baker laughed—one short, exhausted bark—and said, "Well. There's more sense in that than in anything shouted here this month." He pushed a second loaf across the counter. "On the house. For the fold."

The crowd dissolved as slowly as it had formed, reluctantly, each person needing to be seen choosing peace. Marta stayed longest, staring at Wren with an expression that wasn't hatred anymore but something more painful: hunger. "Will you bless the well?" she asked. "Before you go? Our well went bitter two winters back and the god fixed it, but it tastes of iron sometimes, and I'm afraid, and—"

And there it was. Underneath the mob, underneath the certainty: a woman afraid her child would drink bad water. Wren's anger didn't vanish. It just made room.

"I'll look at it," Wren said.

* * *

The well needed nothing. The water was clean; the iron taste was the stones, and Wren said so, and showed Marta's daughter how to drop a pinch of crushed oak-gall into a cup to test, a trick from the temple kitchens. It took ten minutes. Marta wept anyway, grasping Wren's hands, pressing a copper into them that Wren tried to refuse and couldn't.

They camped that night in a hay barn outside the village, because leaving immediately after blessing the well would have undone whatever the well had bought them. Emrys sat against a hay bale turning the copper over in their fingers.

"You lied beautifully," they said.

"It wasn't a lie."

"Forty-one folds?"

"Forty-one folds." Wren pulled her knees up. "Third one's real. Hearth and grain. Everything else was theater."

"It saved our lives."

"I know." She stared at the barn's dark rafters. "That's what I can't stop thinking about. Nine years of keeping shrines and the first time my training actually *matters*, it matters as a stage trick. Fold the cloth right and the mob loves you. Say the words right and they'll let you eat." Her voice came out sharper than she intended. "Do you understand what these villages are becoming? In Merrow's Ford, that woman has sealed her own censer shut so her congregation only hears *her*. In Harrowgate, they'll burn a stranger for walking past a shrine. The prayers are being answered, and every answer is making people *smaller*. More frightened. More certain. Faith was supposed to be the thing you carried into the dark. Instead they've built walls out of it and posted guards."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was careful, the voice of someone handling something fragile. "My grandmother prayed at a dead shrine her whole life. Every morning. Nothing ever answered her, and she kept doing it. I used to think it was habit. Now I wonder if she knew something we don't—that maybe the silence was the kindest thing the gods ever gave us, and she was saying thank you for it."

"And now the silence is breaking."

"And now the silence is breaking." Emrys tossed the copper gently, caught it. "Vaelith said the covenant is failing. I'm starting to think failing means exactly this. Not fire from the sky. Just... doors opening everywhere, and every fool with a censer standing in one, charging toll."

Wren lay back in the hay. Above the smell of dust and dried stalks, faint but unmistakable, she caught it again: ash. Banked ash, kept alive. She sat up so fast her neck cracked.

"Do you smell that?"

Emrys lifted their head. "Hay?"

"No." Wren was on her feet, moving toward the barn's far end, following the scent the way a hound follows blood. It led to a corner where the boards gapped, and through the gap she saw, down the slope, a light in the dark field. Not a torch. Torchlight flickered orange. This light was the deep, patient red of a coal that refuses to die, and it was moving—no, *many* lights were moving, dozens of them, a procession winding along the river road toward Harrowgate, and every one of them burned the color of banked dragon-fire.

At the procession's head, borne on a pole like a standard, swung a great censer. Even at this distance, even with the wind wrong, Wren heard it ring.

Free-hinged. Singing.

"Emrys," she whispered. "Someone's collecting."
