## 7. The Hollow Choir

The rain found us two days out of the saltmarsh villages, and by then we had learned to read the weather like a letter from an enemy. Wren counted the pauses between thunderclaps under her breath; I felt each one in my teeth, where the storm-calling sat coiled and half-awake, a limb remembering it had fingers.

"We should find shelter," she said, hunching her shoulders against the downpour. The censer of dragon-ash hung at her hip beneath her cloak, and I had begun to notice that when storms gathered near us, its glow deepened—like a coal stirred by breath. "Real shelter. Not another barn with a leaky roof and suspicious chickens."

"You've made peace with the chickens, then."

"Never." She wiped wet hair from her eyes. "But I'd make peace with almost anything that has four walls right now."

I was about to answer—I don't remember what I meant to say, something dry, something that kept the distance between us at its usual careful arm's length—when I saw the lights through the trees. Not hearth-light, which flickers amber and honest. These were pale, steady, arranged in a ring, and they did not waver even when the wind came through and bent every tree on the hillside.

Wren saw them too. Her hand went to the censer, not to hide it but to touch it, the way a child touches a talisman. "Emrys," she said quietly. "Those aren't lanterns."

"I know."

"They're *still*."

"I know," I said again, and started walking toward them, because we were soaked to the bone, because the road behind us had grown teeth, and because—though I would not admit this to Wren for a long time—something in those unmoving lights pulled at me like a hand closing around an old scar. Magic had come back into me all at once at the valley's edge, violent as blood returning to a numb limb. But there were parts of me it hadn't reached. Places still gray and silent. And whatever waited on that hillside was silent too. I could feel it from fifty paces: a hush so complete it rang.

The settlement had no name that anyone offered us. It stood in a bowl of hills where three dead rivers met, and it was built of stone taken apart and put back together wrong—walls without corners, roofs that sloped into courtyards instead of away from them, everything shaped to hold quiet the way other buildings held warmth. The pale lights turned out to be lamps filled with something that wasn't oil and didn't smoke. They burned with a light like moonlight that had been convinced to stay.

They were waiting for us at the gate. That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't understand how wrong until later. A dozen figures in undyed wool, faces open and kind, and none of them flinched when thunder rolled over the hills. They simply stood in it, unhurried, as if weather were a rumor they had declined to believe.

"You're the storm-caller," said the woman at their center. Not a question.

"I'm nobody," I said, which had been true for thirty years and had stopped being true the day I crossed the boundary, but habit is a stubborn animal.

Something moved across her face—not unkindness. Recognition, maybe. Pity. "No one is nobody here," she said. "That's rather the point. Come in out of the rain. We've been expecting you since the ash began to glow."

Beside me, Wren went very still.

* * *

They fed us barley stew and dark bread and asked nothing—or seemed to ask nothing, though I learned quickly that silence itself was their interrogation. The commune called itself the Hollow Choir, and its members were exactly what they appeared: exiles. Mages who had fled guilds or temples or families, hedge-witches burned out of their villages, a former battle-priestess with a scarred throat who communicated in chalk and gestures, a boy no older than sixteen whose shadow moved a half-second behind him and who would not say why. Every one of them had been broken somewhere else and had carried the pieces here, to the one place in the world where magic could not follow.

Except it could follow now. I watched them feel it. At supper, when a pot boiled over and the youngest acolyte flinched and the water *stopped*, hanging for one impossible heartbeat before collapsing back into the pot—the whole room went rigid. The woman with the scarred throat set down her chalk. The leader, whose name was Sera Voss, closed her eyes as if listening to a distant bell.

"It's getting louder," someone whispered.

"The covenant is failing," Sera said, and opened her eyes, and looked directly at me. "And you are walking proof."

Afterward, she walked me through the settlement while Wren was shown to a bunkroom by two women who were friendly in the way of people following orders. Sera was tall and gray-streaked and moved like water finding its level, and she talked the way the lamps burned—steady, smokeless, without flicker.

"You think we worship nothing," she said. "Everyone does, at first. Silence looks like absence if you've never lived inside it."

"And you have."

"Thirty years. I was a flame-speaker before I came here. I could pull fire out of stone." She held up her hands, ordinary hands, work-worn. "Do you know what it's like to have that taken from you? To cross a boundary and feel half your soul go deaf?"

"Yes," I said, before I could stop myself.

She smiled, and it transformed her face into something younger, something wounded and warm. "Yes. You do. So you understand. This place isn't a prison, Emrys Marchetti. It's the only mercy left in the world. Here, the fire in your blood can't hurt anyone. Here, you can't burn a village because you dreamed of lightning. Here—" she gestured at the crooked buildings, the pale lamps, the people drifting between them with their quiet faces— "here, you can just be a person. Don't tell me you haven't dreamed of that."

I had. For thirty years, I had told myself Caerlum was exile, but there had been nights—many nights—when the silence felt like rest. When the dormant storm in my chest felt less like a cage than like sleep. I hated her a little for naming it.

"The covenant is failing," I said. "Your gods are waking up. Answered prayers, desperate villages, cults burning shrines. Whatever mercy this place holds, it won't hold long."

"Then let it fail gently," she said. "Or better—let us end it cleanly, ourselves, on our own terms. There is a way, you know. There has always been a way. The last guarantor chose silence over sacrifice, and the silence has held for sixty years. Another hand could close it forever." She stopped walking and turned to face me fully, and the lamp-light did not waver on her face, and I understood suddenly why the buildings were built wrong: they were built to trap the quiet, and so was she. "We know what you carry, Emrys. The ash. The ghost-memory. You're going to the hollow to reforge the old covenant—to chain the realms together again, the way your grandmother chained them, the way that killed the last dragon-fire."

"She didn't kill it. She—"

"She spent it," Sera said softly. "Every year of your life, a little more of it, buying the quiet you slept so peacefully inside. And now there's almost nothing left, and the price of renewal is memory, and you're going to pay it, aren't you? Because you're brave. Brave people always pay." She touched my arm, gentle as snowfall. "You don't have to. Stay here. Bring the ash inside our walls, where the fire can't reach it, where it will gutter out slowly and painlessly, like a candle in a jar. The realms will part. The gods will go back to sleep. The villages will forget their answered prayers within a generation—people forget anything that costs them comfort. And you—" her voice dropped, and it was the kindest voice I had heard in years, and that was precisely what frightened me— "you could finally rest. No storm. No debt. No dead grandmother's name carved on a stone under a cold hearth. Just a room of your own. A table. People who understand what it cost you to be what you are."

Behind her, the commune had drifted near, drawn by some signal I couldn't see. Faces in the pale light. The boy with the lagging shadow. The priestess with the ruined throat. People who had been broken by magic the way I had been broken by magic, standing in a bowl of hills where magic couldn't touch them, offering me the one thing I had never once had:

A family that required nothing.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in the bunk they gave me—clean straw, wool blanket, walls thick enough that the thunder outside arrived muffled and far away, a storm translated into another language—and I turned Sera's offer over and over like a stone in the pocket.

Here was the truth I didn't want: part of me wanted to say yes. Not the coward part. The tired part. The part that had spent a lifetime making itself small, that had learned invisibility so well it had begun to disappear even to itself. In Caerlum I had been tolerated—a strange, solitary figure who fixed fences and never aged quite right and never spoke of the past. Nobody had ever chosen me. My grandmother had died leaving me a house and a secret and no explanation. And here was a hundred people—no, forty, but it felt like hundreds—who looked at me and saw not a threat, not a freak, but a *returning*. Who had lit lamps that didn't flicker and built walls to hold the quiet and waited, they said, since the ash began to glow.

Waiting for whom? I'd wondered earlier. Now I knew. Waiting for a guarantor. Waiting for the Marchetti name to walk through their gate and give their silence a foundation again.

Even my belonging here would be a transaction. Even this.

I got up and went looking for Wren.

I found her in the shrine—or what passed for one. A low stone room at the heart of the settlement, windowless, lampless, lit by nothing except the censer sitting open on the floor. The dragon-ash glowed in the darkness like a banked heart, and Wren knelt beside it with her sleeves pushed back and her hands hovering over the warmth, not touching, the way she always did. Guarding it and grieving it at once.

"They offered you something," she said without turning around. "Sera doesn't talk that long unless she's offering something."

"How do you know?"

"Because she talked to me for ten minutes and offered me nothing at all." Now she turned, and in the ash-light her face was young and old at the same time, the way it always was. "Shrine-keepers are useful for tending relics. We're not useful for deciding things. So." She folded her arms. "What did they offer you?"

"A home," I said. It came out flat and ugly. "Belonging. Forty exiled mages who worship the silence and want to keep it. A room of my own. An end to the storm, the debt, all of it. All I have to do is give them the ash and abandon you."

The words hung there. I watched her take them, watched her weigh them, and I braced for the hurt—but what crossed her face wasn't hurt. It was recognition. She nodded slowly, like someone confirming a diagnosis.

"That's how they get you," she said. "That's how the temple got me, in the beginning. Not with threats. With *fitting*. They measure the hole in you and offer exactly the shape that fills it." She looked down at the ash. "Vaelith showed me a little of herself, back on the road. Through the censer. Did she ever show you what the original covenant felt like?"

"No."

"Love," Wren said simply. "It felt like love. A dragon loved the world enough to spend her fire on it. That's what a covenant is, Emrys—it's not law, it's love with structure. And what they're offering you is the opposite. Belonging without love. A family that only wants you for your name." She stood, and her jaw was set, and there was ash-dust on her knees. "You can take it, if you want. I mean that. I've run from worse places than this. But don't call it home. Home is the thing you build with people who'd still want you if your name were mud."

I laughed—short, startled, rusty, like a door opening after years. "When did you become the wise one?"

"Somewhere around the third mob," she said. "Wisdom comes cheap after that."

But her hands weren't steady, and I realized, watching her kneel back down beside the censer, that Sera had offered her nothing at all—that Wren had already measured her own worth against this place and found it lacking, and had decided, quietly, in advance, that if I stayed she would leave alone. She had rehearsed it. I could see the rehearsal in the set of her shoulders. This girl who had fled her temple with a dying god's ashes and no plan beyond *away* had already planned her next flight, and the destination was nowhere, and she'd packed for it anyway.

That decided me more surely than any argument could have.

* * *

At dawn, Sera came to our door with bread and honey and the same gentle voice, and found us both awake, packs assembled, the censer wrapped and slung.

"You're leaving," she said. Not surprised. Perhaps she had known from the first; perhaps the whole evening had been ritual, the commune's way of witnessing a refusal they'd seen coming. Or perhaps—this thought came later, on the road, and chilled me—perhaps she had simply wanted to see the ash up close. To learn what guarded it. To learn what *we* guarded.

"The covenant fails either way, you know," she said, standing in the doorway with the morning fog rolling past her. "If you reforge it, the price will break you. If you fail, the realms tear open and everything my people built here drowns in divine noise. There is no version of your quest that ends well for this valley." She handed me the bread anyway. Kindness as a blade's edge. "But I'll tell you a secret, Emrys Marchetti, guarantor's blood, storm-caller, ghost-bearer. The Hollow Choir is not the only order that worships the quiet. We are merely the gentlest. There are others further along your road who do not offer bread. They offer graves. When you reach the river crossing at Saltmarsh Bend, walk wide of the ferryman. He sings to himself. Listen to what he sings."

"What does he sing?"

"Nothing yet," Sera said, and stepped back, and the fog took her the way the pale lamps took everything—steadily, completely, without flicker. "He hasn't been told yet. But he will be. Word travels faster than storms, these days."

We walked out through the crooked gates into the gray morning. Behind us, the settlement sat in its bowl of hills, silent as a held breath, and I did not look back—except once, at the crest of the ridge, and saw the forty pale lamps still burning in the daylight, unwavering, and understood at last what they reminded me of.

Stars. Stars seen from underwater.

Wren walked beside me, the censer glowing faintly at her hip, and neither of us spoke for a long time. Then she said, "Saltmarsh Bend is three days east."

"I know."

"And the ferryman sings."

"I heard."

She glanced up at me, and for the first time since the valley boundary, since the storm that had nearly killed us both, she smiled—small, tired, real. "Good," she said. "Because whatever's waiting for us at that river, I'd rather meet it with you than without you."

Three days later, from the top of the ridge above Saltmarsh Bend, we heard the ferryman singing—and the song was my name.
