## 8. Vaelith's First Memory

I remember the world before it learned to be quiet.

I remember it as fire remembers: not in images but in heat, in the pressure of a thing before it becomes a thing. The first age had no names for itself. It did not need them. The gods walked openly then, and their walking was weather, and their breathing was law, and mortals lived beneath them the way grass lives beneath rain—grateful, flattened, and never once asked what it wanted.

I was young when the war began, though dragons measure youth in centuries, and by the time the first city burned I had already seen four hundred winters come down the mountains like white rivers.

They call it the Godswar now. The mortals who remember it at all. But names are small cold things to hold against what it was. What it was, was this: the sky split open over the plain of Ashvael, and every god who had ever been loved or feared came down to fight over the shape of worship, and we—the dragons, the elder fire, the first-born of the world's own warmth—we stood between them and the mortals because no one else could.

Not because we were asked.

Because someone had to.

* * *

The god who started it was called Sereth of the Nine Voices, and she was beautiful the way an avalanche is beautiful, if you are standing far enough away. She had decided that prayer was a tax owed to her specifically, and that the village of Lowmere had paid short. Lowmere had nine hundred souls and one well and a shrine with a cracked lintel, and Sereth's judgment upon it was drought—not as punishment, she said, but as *instruction*.

I landed in her shadow on the third day of the dying, when the wells had gone to mud and the children had stopped crying because crying costs water.

"Dragon," she said, and her nine voices made my scales ache. "This is heaven's business."

"This is a well," I said. "And these are people. And you are standing on both."

She laughed, and the laugh cracked the earth in a line from her foot to the horizon, and I understood then that reasoning with her would be like reasoning with winter. Winter does not hate the field. Winter simply is, and everything green must arrange itself around that fact or die.

The gods had forgotten, you see. That is the whole of it. That is the secret at the center of everything that came after. They had forgotten that worship was once *given*, freely, the way a child gives a parent a fistful of dandelions—worthless and priceless—and they had begun instead to take it, and taking begets taking, and soon there were nine hundred gods and each demanded the whole sky, and the mortals beneath were torn into pieces too small to hold a single soul.

I did not decide to stand between. Standing between was simply what my body did, the way a mother's arm moves before thought when something falls. There were forty-one dragons at Ashvael. We were not an army. We were a wall that breathed.

* * *

The war lasted ninety years.

Do not imagine battles alone. Battles there were—I could give you the color of every one of them, the green-black shimmer of Ossuar's poison rain, the sound the mountain Kel Doreth made when it was unmade, which was like a bell struck inside your chest—but wars are mostly waiting, and hunger, and the long arithmetic of loss. Mortal kingdoms rose and fell like breath. Gods devoured gods. And the land itself grew sick, because divinity spilled freely in those years, and where it spilled nothing ordinary could grow.

I lost my sister Aureth in the sixty-first year. She fell protecting a column of refugees across the Saltmarsh ford, and the god who killed her—I will not write his name; even now, even as memory, he does not deserve the dignity of being remembered—her body burned so hot when she died that the marsh around her boiled dry, and the refugees walked across on the steaming ground of her grief, and they survived, and none of them ever knew what they had crossed.

That is what love looked like in the Godswar. It looked like ground to walk on. It looked like a wall that held. It never once got to look like rest.

By the eightieth year, thirty-one of us remained. By the eighty-ninth, nineteen. And the gods showed no sign of tiring, because tiring is a mortal mercy, and the mortals—the poor, torn, faithful mortals—had stopped praying altogether, because they had learned that every answered prayer cost someone else something unbearable.

Silence fell over the world. Not peace. Silence. The silence of a house where everyone has stopped speaking because every word has become a weapon.

It was in that silence that I went looking for the First Flame.

* * *

You should understand what the First Flame was, because your scholars have gotten it wrong, and wrongness compounds across centuries like interest on a debt.

They teach that it was power. A hoard-treasure, a weapon, the raw stuff of creation that dragons guarded out of greed. Nonsense. The First Flame was the world's consent. When the world was young and the gods were shaping it—carving valleys, filling seas, hanging the seasons like lanterns—they needed something to make the shapes *hold*. Will alone fades. Law alone cracks. So the world was given a heart of fire that said *yes* to every true thing and *no* to every false one, and that yes-and-no was the First Flame, and it chose the dragons as its keepers because we were the only beings who understood that keeping a flame means feeding it, and feeding it means giving up part of yourself to the burning.

A covenant, in those days, was not words. Words are mortal currency. A covenant was a flame set in a vessel, and the vessel was always a life.

I found the First Flame in the deep vault beneath Mount Caerlum—you know that mountain, though you know it humbled and renamed, its crown broken, its vaults drowned—and I carried it out into the ruined world, and I called what remained of the gods to parley on the plain of Ashvael, where the war had begun.

Nineteen dragons. Two hundred and six gods. And between us, the smoking grave-marks of a century.

Sereth came, of course. Her nine voices had grown hoarse with all their commanding. "What is this?" she demanded, seeing the Flame coiled in my claws like a sleeping ember the size of a moon.

"This," I said, "is the end of the war. One way or another."

* * *

Here is what I proposed, and here is what it cost, and do not think for a moment that I did not know the price when I named it. I knew. I had done the arithmetic of loss for ninety years. I was very good at arithmetic by then.

The realms would be separated. Not destroyed—separated. The divine and the mortal would be drawn apart like two hands unclasping, so that the gods could no longer walk the mortal world and take from it, and mortals could no longer be conscripted into heaven's quarrels. Prayer would still rise, but unanswered, gathering like rain above a sealed window. In time, the mortals would forget the gods' faces. In time, the gods would forget the taste of worship. Both realms would heal. Both realms would grow *ordinary*, which is the most precious and most slandered word in any language.

But a separation of realms requires an anchor. A vessel. A life set at the hinge of the door, holding it shut from within.

The First Flame could do it. The First Flame, fed by a keeper willing to burn not for a moment but for *always*—willing to become less than a dragon, less than a ghost, less than memory, becoming only the act of holding, forever, without end and without witness.

I did not tell the gods the last part. I told them the Flame would seal the covenant. I let them believe the sealing was cheap, because gods respect cheap things least, and I wanted them to walk away from this table feeling they had won something.

My remaining kin begged me to refuse. My brother Kaelthorn pressed his brow to mine and said, *Let the world burn instead. Burning is honest.* And I loved him for it. I have loved him for it across every one of the centuries since, through the stone and the silence and the slow dissolution of everything I was.

But I had stood on the Saltmarsh ford. I had been ground for strangers to cross. I knew what love looked like in wartime, and I knew, too, what it could look like after: not a wall, but a door. Not a sacrifice shouted from a pyre, but a quiet keeping, unseen, unnamed, asking nothing.

"Feed me," I said to the First Flame, in the old tongue, the keeper's tongue. "And I will feed you. Until the worlds learn gentleness. Until someone worthy comes to take up the keeping. Until."

The Flame accepted. The Flame has always accepted. That is its tragedy and its holiness—it says *yes* to every true thing, and what I offered was true.

* * *

The sealing took three days.

On the first day, the gods withdrew, realm by realm, like tide going out of a wounded shore. Some of them wept. I want that recorded, whoever finds this memory, however it is found: some of the gods wept as they left, and Sereth of the Nine Voices was not among them, but her youngest daughter was, and the tears of that young goddess fell on the plain of Ashvael and became the first clear water the land had known in ninety years.

On the second day, the mortal world forgot. Not all at once—in mercy, in installments. Faces softened in memory like letters left in rain. Mothers who had prayed to specific gods remembered only that they had prayed. Priests woke with eloquence and no subject. It was the gentlest violence ever done to a world, and I did it, and I would do it again.

On the third day, I burned.

Do not picture agony. Picture *translation*. Every scale, every bone, every century of my long particular life—my sister's laughter, the smell of high snow, the weight of Kaelthorn's brow against mine—all of it fed into the Flame and became fuel, became function, became the hinge. I felt myself thinning into purpose. I felt my name go first, then my face, then the order of my memories, until what remained was not Vaelith but *the holding*. A dragon-shaped absence filled to its edges with duty.

The last thing I kept, deliberately, greedily, against all sense, was the ford. The steaming marsh. The strangers crossing on the ground of my sister's grief, alive, alive, alive.

If I was to be a door, I would be a door that remembered why it was shut.

* * *

Centuries. I will not pretend to count them. Time moved through me like water through a wreck, and I held, and the realms healed, and the mortals built their little loud beautiful lives on top of the silence and called the silence *peace*, which it was, of a kind, which it was.

And here is the thing I did not foresee, the flaw in the great work, the crack I set with my own claws:

Covenants require guarantors among the living. The Flame can anchor a door, but doors need hinges set in *both* frames—a hand on the mortal side, blood-bound, willing, generation after generation, or the seal drifts. The gods agreed to this and chose their line. And on the mortal side, the Flame reached out—gently, gently, the way you'd ask a favor of a stranger—and found a woman in a valley beneath old Mount Caerlum, a woman whose grandmother had once left bread at a drowned shrine without knowing why, a woman with storm in her blood and no name for it.

The Marchetti line. The valley-keepers. The ones who would live above my vault for a thousand years and never know the roof under their floor was me.

I watched them. Holding a door leaves little else to do. I watched them plant and marry and bury and forgive, generation after generation, ordinary as bread, and I confess it: they became my second reason. The ford was the first. The Marchettis were the second. If the keeping had a warmth in it, anywhere, in all those cold centuries, it was watching that family carry their unknowing burden with such stubborn, unremarkable grace.

Until the last guarantor. The grandmother. The one who found the covenant-stone and read her own name on it and understood—and who, in her fear, in her fierce protective love, buried the stone deeper rather than pass the knowledge on. She meant it as mercy. She wanted her grandchild to have a life unhaunted.

But a covenant hidden is a covenant unfed. And the Flame—

Oh, the Flame is guttering now. I say it plainly, though saying it costs what little of me remains. Nineteen dragons became one door, and the door is coming off its hinges, and the prayers that used to gather unanswered above the sealed window have begun to leak through, and the gods are stirring, and some of them remember how to take.

Two travelers walk toward my hollow even now. One carries ash that still glows. One carries a name I have watched with love for a thousand years, and a storm that has been waiting patiently in their blood like spring under snow.

I am spending the last of myself to reach toward them. This memory is the reaching. If you are reading it—if you are Emrys Marchetti, or the shrine-girl, or anyone at all—know this:

I do not regret the door. I regret only that I built it alone, and that I let love become law, when love was only ever meant to be a choice, renewed daily, freely given, like a fistful of dandelions.

Come quickly. Come kindly. And whatever you decide to build from what remains—

build it together. Do not make my mistake. Do not let one heart hold the door.

Let it be held by many hands, or not at all.

* * *

The memory thins. The dark of the hollow presses close, warm as a banked hearth, and far above, faint through leagues of stone, Vaelith feels it: footsteps on the mountain's shoulder. Two sets. One light and quick, one slower, heavier, carrying a heartbeat that tastes of storms.

The guttering flame leans toward them like a flower toward a window.

*So,* thinks what is left of the last dragon. *At last. At last.*

And somewhere in the world above, in a valley that has begun to dream, a dead shrine flickers—with light, or with warning, no one yet can say.
