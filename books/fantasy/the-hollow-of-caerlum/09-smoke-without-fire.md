## 9. Smoke Without Fire

The dragon-hollow did not look like a place where anything vast had ever lived.

That was Wren's first thought, and she was ashamed of it. She had spent eleven years tending shrines, and the first lesson every shrine-keeper learned was that holiness never announced itself. It hid in the chipped bowl of a offering-cup, in the moss-eaten foot of a waystone, in the small grey god who asked for nothing but company. Holiness was always smaller than you expected, because bigness was a human appetite, and the sacred had no use for it.

Still. She had imagined a mountain carved by breath. A valley of glass where fire had fallen. Something.

What they found instead was a fold in the hills north of the saltmarsh, green and ordinary, where the grass grew long and the wind moved through it in slow silver waves. At its center stood a hill with a hollow at its crown like a cupped hand, and from that hollow rose smoke — not black, not white, but the color of old parchment, thin as breath on a winter morning.

Wren stopped walking. The censer at her hip had been warm for three days now, ever since they'd crossed out of Caerlum, ever since Emrys's storm had torn the sky open and left them both shaking in the wet grass. But here, at the edge of the hollow, it went hot enough to sting through her coat.

"Emrys," she said.

Emrys was already staring up at the hill. Their face — Wren had learned to read it, in the weeks since the valley, in the weeks since the storm — wore the expression of someone hearing their own name called from very far away.

"I know," they said. "I feel it too."

"It's so *quiet*." Wren meant it literally. Since leaving Caerlum, the world had grown loud in ways she couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't kept a shrine — prayers rising off villages like steam, gods muttering behind the treeline, the whole landscape humming with half-woken attention. But here the noise fell away entirely, and what replaced it wasn't silence. It was *listening*. The hollow was listening to them.

Emrys started up the slope. Wren followed, one hand pressed flat against the censer's lid, feeling the ash inside shift and glow like something breathing.

* * *

The hollow at the crown of the hill was perhaps forty paces across, ringed by stones worn smooth as river pebbles, and it was full of smoke.

Not obscuring smoke. It hung in slow coils at knee height, waist height, parting around them as they stepped down into it, and through it Wren saw the dragon.

Or what had been a dragon.

She lay coiled in the center of the hollow, and at first glance she seemed whole — the great wedge of her skull, the ridged spine descending into a body longer than the shrine-house Wren had grown up in, wings folded along her flanks like a cathedral deciding to be patient. Her scales were the deep red of banked coals, shot through with veins of ember-light that pulsed, faintly, in a rhythm Wren recognized before she understood why.

It was the rhythm of the censer. Of the ash. Of the last dragon-fire, keeping time.

But when the light pulsed brighter, Wren saw what the light was hiding. Where the dragon's hindquarters should have tapered into a tail, there was only smoke — dense, slow-turning, holding the shape of a tail the way a memory holds the shape of a face. One of her forelegs ended at the wrist in drifting grey. Along her left flank, scales thinned to translucence, and beneath them Wren could see not flesh but *images*: a burning city seen from above, a field of kneeling soldiers, a woman's upturned face lit by firelight. Memories, wearing the dragon's body like a garment that no longer quite fit.

"She's coming apart," Emrys said quietly. They had gone very still, the way people go still around the dying. "Wren. She's made of memories now. That's all that's holding her."

"Then we'd better give her some new ones to hold onto," Wren said, and hated how small her voice came out.

She knelt at the edge of the coil, close enough to feel the heat rolling off the dragon's hide — gentle heat, hearth-heat, nothing like the violence she'd expected. Up close the ember-veins were beautiful and terrible, flickering unevenly, guttering like a candle in a draft. Between pulses, whole sections of the great body went dark and translucent, and Wren could see straight through her to the grass beyond.

"Vaelith," Wren said. "My name is Wren. I was a keeper of the Ashen Shrine, in Corvane. I carried your fire out of the temple when they tried to snuff it. I've carried it ever since."

Nothing. The smoke-coils turned. Somewhere in the deep of the dragon, something ticked and settled like a house at night.

Then the dragon's eye opened.

It was the size of a cartwheel and it did not open all at once — the lid dragged, sluggish, and the eye beneath was not the molten gold Wren had braced herself for. It was grey. Smoke-grey, memory-grey, and in its depths images drifted past like clouds reflected in water: faces, places, centuries.

When Vaelith spoke, her voice did not come from her throat. It came from everywhere — from the smoke, from the stones, from the censer at Wren's hip, which flared so hot Wren cried out and fumbled it open. The ash inside rose into the air in a slow spiral, glowing, and hung there between them like a constellation.

And the voice spoke *through* it, in borrowed fragments:

*"—and the king said, what will it cost? And she said—"*

A different voice, deeper, older, a woman's voice worn smooth by ages: *"Everything you love, eventually. That is what covenants are."*

*"—mother, mother, the fields are burning—"*

*"—swear it, little sister, swear it on your own fire—"*

The fragments braided together, voices that could not have belonged to the dragon, pouring out of her the way water pours from a cracked vessel. Wren realized, with a lurch, what she was hearing. Not Vaelith's words. Other people's words. Words the dragon had carried so long they had become part of her body, surfacing now because there was less and less of *her* left to hold them down.

"She can't speak for herself anymore," Wren whispered. "She can only speak in what she remembers."

The great grey eye found her. Held her. And in it, briefly, Wren saw herself — a small figure kneeling in smoke, a girl with a censer and ash-burned hands — and the image wavered, threatened to dissolve, held.

*"—the keeper,"* said the woman's voice, the old one, and this time it sounded almost present, almost *hers*. *"The keeper comes. I remember waiting for the keeper."*

"I'm here," Wren said. Her eyes were stinging. "I'm sorry I'm late."

*"Late,"* the dragon repeated, and the word came out in Wren's own voice — her voice, from years ago, from the night she'd fled the temple, saying *it's too late, it's too late* to a mentor who hadn't listened. Vaelith had caught that memory somehow, plucked it from the air or from the ash, and now she wore it, trying it on like a coat. *"Too late. Too late is a mortal word. I have been dying for nine hundred years. What is late, to me?"*

A sound escaped Emrys behind her — half laugh, half sob.

"You're dying faster now," Emrys said. "Since the stone broke. Since the Choir—"

*"I know what broke."* The smoke stirred, coiling tighter around the failing body. Fragments surfaced and sank: *"—hold the line—"* in a soldier's shout; *"—she gave everything, do you understand, EVERYTHING—"* in a grief-cracked voice; a child singing a song about rain. *"Every crack in the covenant, I feel it. Every prayer answered wrong. Every god waking hungry. Your world is leaking into mine and mine into yours, and the wall between was my body, once."*

Wren looked at the translucent flank, the dissolving tail, the memories showing through the skin. "You *were* the wall."

*"I am what's left of it."* The eye dimmed, brightened. The borrowed voices rose again, unbidden, a tide of other lives: *"—by root and rain and rendered flame—"* (a priest's cadence, formal, ancient); *"don't make me do this alone"* (young, furious, familiar in a way that made Emrys inhale sharply); *"—and the dragon said yes, though none of them asked if she wanted to—"* (a scholar, dry, distant).

"That last one," Emrys said slowly. "Who said that?"

*"A historian. Four hundred years dead. He was nearly right."* The ember-veins pulsed, steadier for a moment, as if speaking fed her. Or as if being *heard* did. *"None of them asked. So I answered anyway. That is what sacrifice means, keeper. It means no one asks."*

Wren thought of the temple. Of the high keepers deciding, in panelled rooms, whose prayers mattered. Of herself, eleven years old, told she had been chosen and never asked. "I know what it means," she said.

The grey eye regarded her for a long moment. Then, softly, in a voice that was almost — almost — Vaelith's own, and not anyone else's:

*"Yes. I think you do."*

* * *

They made camp in the hollow that night, because neither of them could bear to leave.

It was not a decision they discussed. Emrys built a small fire near the ring-stones — an absurd gesture, cooking supper beside a creature made of fire — and Wren noticed the dragon's smoke leaning toward it, gently, the way a cat leans toward a warm lap. When Wren ladled out barley stew, the ash-spiral that was Vaelith's voice drifted low over the pot and lingered there.

*"—smells like the valley kitchens,"* said a voice Wren didn't know, young and homesick. *"—more salt, grandmother, more salt—"* said another, laughing.

"She tastes our memories too?" Emrys asked quietly.

*"No."* The word came in the old woman's voice again, the covenant-voice, and Wren was beginning to understand that this one was closest to the dragon's true self — or had been, once, whoever *she* had been before she became a wall. *"I taste yours. You are thinking of a kitchen. Stone floor. Someone humming."* The smoke curled toward Emrys, almost tender. *"Whose kitchen, storm-child?"*

Emrys's spoon stopped halfway to their mouth. "I don't—" They set the spoon down. "I don't remember. There was a kitchen. I know there was. Grandmother's house had one. But when I try to see it—" Their face did something complicated and closed. "It's just a shape where a room should be."

Wren went cold. "Emrys. Have you lost memories? Before any of this?"

"I assumed it was the valley. Magic doesn't work there; maybe remembering doesn't either." Emrys picked the spoon back up, stared into the stew. "Or maybe I gave things away a long time ago and didn't notice the holes until now."

The dragon said nothing, but the ash-spiral turned slowly, and in the grey of her eye Wren saw images drift past that she was sure were not hers to see: a younger Emrys, lightning-lit, screaming; a stone under a hearth; a name being spoken over a cradle like a spell or a sentence.

*"Memories are not lost,"* Vaelith said at last, in the historian's dry voice, then shifted to her own: *"They are *held*. Everything given up is held somewhere. Remember that, when the asking starts."*

"The asking?" Wren said. "What asking?"

But the dragon's eye had drifted shut, and the ember-veins along her flank slowed, guttered, steadied. In her sleep — if a creature like this slept — fragments leaked from her like heat from a cooling stove: a battle-hymn, a lullaby, a man crying *forgive me*, a bell ringing across water.

Wren sat watch through the middle hours, as she had on a thousand shrine-nights, and found the habit held even here. She fed the small fire. She checked the censer. And she talked, quietly, because she had spent eleven years talking to gods who never answered, and it seemed suddenly important to talk to something that might.

"My mentor used to say the fire chose its keepers," she told the dark bulk of the dragon. "That carrying it was an honor. She never said it would burn. She never said the temple would decide the fire mattered more than the people carrying it." She turned her burned palms upward, the old scars silver in the firelight. "I ran. Eleven years I told myself I was done with sacred things. And then I walked into a valley where nothing answers, and the first thing I did was start keeping a dead shrine anyway. Because I didn't know how to stop."

The ash-spiral stirred. *"—she keeps faith like breathing,"* said a voice, warm, amused — and Wren froze, because she knew that voice. It was her own. From the temple courtyard, years ago, saying it to another novice, certain no one holy was listening.

"You *do* take memories," Wren breathed. "From the air. From the ash."

*"Only what is offered,"* said Vaelith, in the old woman's voice, and there was something in it that Wren, with a keeper's ear, identified as gentleness. *"You offered. Long ago. I only now had strength to catch it."* A pause. The embers pulsed. *"Keeper. When I am gone — and I am going, soon, sooner than your road allows — the fire must pass through willing hands. That is the first law. Before the covenant, before the gods, before the war. Fire does not obey. Fire consents."*

"And if it doesn't consent?"

*"Then it goes out,"* said the dragon simply, *"and both realms go dark, and everything any of us loved becomes a story with no one left to tell it."*

Above the hollow, the stars were coming out, sharp and cold. Wren pulled her coat tighter and looked at Emrys, asleep sitting up against a ring-stone, exhaustion finally having won, their face slack and young in the firelight. She thought about kitchens with shapes instead of rooms. About a name spoken like a sentence.

She thought: *this is going to cost them something. This is going to cost all of us.*

* * *

Near dawn, the dragon woke them with a memory that was not a memory.

It began as weather — a pressure change, a hush — and then the ash-spiral flared white-hot and every borrowed voice in the hollow spoke at once, layered, harmonized, a choir of everyone Vaelith had ever known:

*"THEY ARE COMING."*

Wren was on her feet with a knife she didn't remember drawing. "Who's coming? The Choir? How far?"

*"Not the Choir."* The voices separated, settled, became the old woman's alone. *"Worse. Older. The gods have smelled my failing, and three of them are coming to discuss terms while there is still something of me to bargain with."* The grey eye opened, and for the first time Wren saw fear in it — real fear, vast and ancient, the fear of a wall watching the flood rise. *"Storm-child. Keeper. Whatever they offer you — and they will offer you beautiful things — remember what I told the king, at the forging."*

"What did you tell him?" Emrys asked.

The ember-veins flared. The smoke rose. And Vaelith, dying, dissolving, made of other people's words, reached down through nine hundred years and found her own:

*"Everything you love, eventually. That is what covenants are. Ask what you are loving *for*."*

Far to the south, across the dark hills, the horizon flickered — not lightning, not dawn, but a slow pulsing gold, like something enormous opening its eyes.
