## 10. The Price of Reforging

The dragon-hollow breathed.

Emrys felt it before they understood it—a slow tidal pull in the air, as though the whole cavern inhaled and exhaled on some vast, wounded rhythm. The walls were ribbed with pale stone that might once have been scale. Pools of standing water caught a light that came from nowhere, from everywhere, the way light behaves in dreams. And at the center, coiled around a broken altar older than any nation Emrys could name, lay what remained of Vaelith.

It was hard to look at her directly. The eye wanted to slide away, the way one's gaze skitters off the moon. What Emrys could see was enormous and incomplete: a body like a hillside given intention, ribs rising like cliffs, wings folded into ruins of membrane gone translucent as old ice. Half of her was simply *absent*—not destroyed, not rotted, but unwritten, the way a word fades from a page left too long in the sun. Where her tail should have swept the far wall there was only a suggestion of tail, an outline, an apology.

And threaded through all of it, guttering, was fire. Not flame in any honest sense—more a glow beneath the skin of her, deep in the chest, pulsing like a heart deciding whether to keep its appointment.

Wren stood very close to Emrys's shoulder. They had not spoken since entering. Even Wren, who talked to shrines and scolded gods and argued with crows, had nothing.

"You came." The voice did not come from the dragon's mouth. It arrived inside them, the way a memory arrives—sudden, complete, carrying its own weather. It was a woman's voice and something vaster than a woman's, layered like thunder heard through stone. "I wondered if anyone still carried the Marchetti name. I wondered if the name still carried anyone."

"You know me." Emrys's voice came out smaller than they intended. "You know my family."

"I knew your grandmother when she was younger than you are now." Something moved in the ruined face—not quite a smile; dragons' smiles were probably terrible things, and perhaps this was Vaelith being gentle. "She stood where you stand. She asked me the same question you are about to ask."

"Then you know why we're here." Emrys stepped forward, gravel clicking under their boots, echoing strangely—the sound fell flat, swallowed, as if the hollow ate noise. "The covenant is failing. Prayers are being answered again. Gods are leaking back into places that spent sixty years forgetting them. Villages are tearing themselves apart over it. My grandmother sealed something under her hearth, and whatever she sealed, it's coming undone."

"It is undone," Vaelith said. "What remains is the wreckage of it, still standing because no one has pushed it over. You felt the boundary break when you crossed out of Caerlum. You will feel worse things before this ends."

Behind them, Wren made a small sound. When Emrys glanced back, the girl had gone white to the lips, her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if something inside her had knocked.

"She's speaking through me too," Wren whispered. "Not words. Just—weight."

"I apologize," said Vaelith, and the apology landed in Emrys's chest like a warm stone. "My body is mostly memory now. I speak the only way I can: by being remembered. It is rude, and I am sorry. I was never good at dying quietly."

Emrys looked up at the vast dim shape of her, at the ember-light stuttering in her chest, and made themselves say it plainly, because plainness was armor. "Then tell me how to fix it. Tell me how to remake the covenant. Whatever it takes."

The silence that followed lasted three of the hollow's slow breaths.

"You do not know what you are offering," Vaelith said at last.

"No," Emrys admitted. "So tell me."

* * *

Vaelith told it the way old things tell stories: without hurry, as though time were a courtesy she extended rather than obeyed. She spoke partly in voice and partly in image, pressing recollections directly against the inside of Emrys's skull, and so Emrys saw it even while hearing it—and seeing it was worse.

"I was young, for my kind. Younger than your valley is old. In those days the gods walked openly, and mortals loved them, and were loved, and both sides mistook love for permission." The images came hot and chaotic: skies full of descending radiance, temples rising overnight, fields bending toward prayers like wheat toward sun. Then other images—smoke. A city burning under a god's indifferent gaze because its people had prayed to a rival. A river god drowning a village that had stopped leaving offerings, not out of cruelty but out of *hurt*, which is more dangerous. Armies marching behind banners bearing living light.

"The war between gods and mortals was not a war of armies," Vaelith said. "Armies end. This was a war of attention. Every answered prayer fed them; every desperate prayer gave them another handhold in the world. Mortals could not stop praying—it is what mortals *are*, the reaching-out. And the gods could not stop answering—it is what they *are*, the reaching-back. Two hands grasping each other until both crushed."

Emrys saw the councils then. Saw mortals and divinities seated across from one another in a hall whose architecture hurt to remember, negotiating terms neither side fully understood. Saw the terms fail. Saw the war resume. Saw centuries of it.

"And then?" Emrys asked, though the images were already showing them.

"And then the dragons decided to be the wall between two burning houses." A pause, long enough that the pools rippled. "There were many of us once. We proposed a third covenant: not silence imposed from outside, but silence *held*—a seal maintained by a willing anchor, a guarantee written in flesh and kept by choice. Someone had to hold the door shut from within, forever, so that neither side could force it open again. Someone who belonged to both realms enough to bind both."

"And the price?"

"The anchor does not merely sign," Vaelith said. "The anchor *becomes* the covenant. Their memory is the ink. To seal the realms apart, the guarantor must give their remembering—must pour their own past into the seal so that the seal has substance, has *weight*, has a life woven through it that both realms must honor. The gods cannot break what is made of mortal memory; mortals cannot break what a dragon's death has sanctified. But the guarantor—" the ember-light flickered, dimmed, steadied— "the guarantor remembers nothing afterward. Not the war. Not the sealing. Not their own name, unless others keep saying it for them."

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. "That's—"

"That is why your grandmother did not know what she had done," Vaelith finished gently. "Yes."

The images shifted. Emrys saw her now—younger than in any portrait, dark-haired, furious, standing in a hall of smoke with the last surviving negotiators while dragons died one by one around them, each death feeding strength into a seal that needed a final anchor. She saw the list of volunteers thin to nothing. She saw her grandmother argue, plead, rage—and then, when no one else would or could, step forward alone.

"She paid half," Vaelith said. "Half was possible because she was not the anchor herself. She was the *guarantor*—she wrote her bloodline into the contract as surety, binding her descendants to maintain the silence, generation upon generation, each inheriting the duty without knowing it. She gave her memories of the sealing itself—all of us, all of this, torn out and pressed into the stone. That is why she hid the covenant-stone under her hearth and never spoke of it. She literally could not remember why she had done it. She only knew, in the bone-deep way one knows grief without a cause, that the house was heavy and the hearth must never be disturbed."

"But the seal needed a true anchor eventually," Emrys said. The understanding was arriving cold, piece by piece, like frost climbing a window. "A blood-guarantor. One of us. Willing."

"One of you. Willing." Vaelith let the word sit. "Your grandmother bought time, not victory. She hoped—I believe she hoped—that a descendant would come to understand, and choose freely what she had been forced to improvise. Or she hoped the need would pass entirely. Hope makes poor drafting. The clause was always there, waiting. *A Marchetti, knowing, may reforge the covenant by becoming its anchor.*"

"And the anchor pays with everything." Emrys heard their own voice as if from far away. "Every memory. Everyone they've ever—"

"Yes."

The word dropped into Emrys like a stone into a well, and the well went down much further than they'd thought.

They thought of Caerlum. Of the smell of bread in Grandmother's kitchen, of the particular creak of the third stair, of rain on the roof that they'd listened to for thirty years without ever being able to call down themselves. Of Wren—of ten days of Wren, of arguments about prayer and shared fires and the girl's terrible singing, of the moment on the boundary road when Wren had grabbed their sleeve as the magic flooded back and *held on*.

All of it. Poured out as ink. Written into a wall so that strangers' grandchildren could live in peace, and never know whose life the peace was built on.

"That's not sacrifice," Emrys said, and their voice shook. "Sacrifice is giving something up. This is being *erased*. You're asking someone to unmake themselves and call it duty. My grandmother refused—"

"Your grandmother refused to pay *alone*," Vaelith corrected softly. "There is the difference you must hear. She would have given everything—but an anchor who gives everything must be *witnessed*, must be held in the memory of others, or the erasure is total. Nothing of them persists, not even the fact of their gift. She had no one she trusted to carry her. The other negotiators were dying or dead. So she invented the bloodline instead—a chain of partial debts instead of one whole one. She spread the cost across generations because she had no one to share it with *now*."

Emrys looked at Wren.

Wren was already looking at them. The girl's eyes were wet, and her jaw was set in that stubborn line Emrys had come to know in ten short days, and she said, before Emrys could speak: "No. Don't you dare decide anything yet."

"There's nothing to decide," Emrys said. "Someone has to—"

"Don't." Wren's voice cracked. "*Don't.*"

* * *

Later—or what Emrys's body insisted was later, though the hollow kept its own hours—they sat at the edge of one of the lightless pools while Vaelith rested, if a thing like her could rest, her breathing slowing until the cavern's tide nearly stilled.

Wren had gone to check the entrance, muttering about wards and sightlines and other practical things, because Wren coped with unbearable truths by organizing them. Emrys stayed.

"Ask me the real question," Vaelith murmured. "The one sitting behind your teeth."

Emrys watched the faint reflection of the dragon's ember-heart shiver on the pool's black surface. "Is there another way. Truly. Some other anchor, some other ink. Anything."

"No." No softening, no mercy in it. "The covenant must be remade before my fire dies, and my fire dies soon. It must be anchored by a guarantor's blood and a guarantor's choice. You are the last of the bloodline who knows. There is no ritual clever enough to cheat this. Believe me—we tried, in the years before the sealing. Cleverness was how the first covenant broke."

Emrys wrapped their arms around their knees. "And if I refuse? If I just—walk away? Let it all come down?"

"Then the realms crash together unbound. The gods return with no law between them and the world, and the world, which has spent sixty years learning helplessness, will be devoured by its own prayers. Some will worship. Some will be worshipped. Both will be ruined." A pause. "Or perhaps humanity surprises everyone. I am old, not omniscient. But I would not bet the world on it."

"And if I don't refuse—if I do this—what survives?"

"Whatever others keep of you." The ember-light pulsed, and Emrys realized the dragon was looking at them, truly looking, with the whole ruined weight of her attention. "Memory is not only held in the skull, little storm-caller. It is held in other people. Your grandmother lost every memory of the sealing—and yet here I am, telling you what she did, because I *am* her memory of it, kept alive in me. What is witnessed cannot wholly vanish. That is the flaw in the design, and it is also the mercy."

"So I'd need witnesses," Emrys said slowly. "People who'd carry me."

"You would."

Emrys thought of Wren's hand gripping their sleeve at the boundary. Thought of the way the girl had said *don't*—not *don't do it*, Emrys realized now, but *don't do it alone, don't disappear, don't you dare make yourself a stranger to me.*

"My grandmother had no one," Emrys said. "That was her tragedy. Not the cost. The aloneness."

"Perhaps," Vaelith said. "Or perhaps her tragedy was a world that taught her to pay alone. Yours need not be the same world. That is rather the point of reforging, if you ask me. Contracts can be rewritten. That is what I have been trying to tell anyone who would listen for sixty years, and no one listens to dying dragons."

Something almost like laughter moved through Emrys—brittle, terrified, real. "You're saying I should write better terms."

"I'm saying," Vaelith rumbled, and the whole hollow resonated with it, dust sifting from the cliff-high ribs of her, "that the old covenant was forged in desperation by the last survivors of a war, and it has held longer than it deserved, and it is failing, and what comes next does not have to be a copy. An anchor who is *loved* is a different kind of anchor, little storm-caller. An anchor who chooses, with witnesses, with names said aloud—that is not the same contract at all. The old seal was a wall. Yours could be a door."

Emrys sat with that. The horror was still there—cold and total, the vision of themselves poured out like water into sand, every remembered morning in Grandmother's kitchen gone, Wren's laugh gone, their own name gone. It did not shrink. But beside it now, something else had settled: resolve, quiet and stubborn, the same thing that had carried them across the boundary into a world of returning magic with nothing but a runaway shrine-keeper and a dead god's errand.

"I'll do it," Emrys said. "But not tonight. And not as a copy of hers. If I'm going to be ink, I'm going to write."

Above them, deep in the hollow's throat, the air changed. A draft slid down from the entrance, cold and wrong, carrying a smell Emrys had learned to dread on the road: old incense, burnt rope, ash that had never known fire.

Wren appeared at the mouth of the inner passage, silhouetted against the grey outside light, and her face told him everything before she spoke.

"They're coming," she said. "Torches. Hundreds of them. And they're singing the silence."
