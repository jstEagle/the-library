## 11. Hollow Men Marching

The Hollow Choir did not sing.

That was the first thing strangers noticed, the thing that made them bar their doors and pull their children from the road: a hundred pilgrims walking in perfect silence, gray-robed, ash-marked, their mouths moving in prayers that made no sound at all. They had taken vows of quiet so complete that even their footsteps seemed swallowed, boots pressing into mud without a splash, crossing streams without a ripple. Birds went still when they passed. Dogs lay down and would not rise until the last of them was over the hill.

At the head of them walked Mother Serris, and she alone was permitted a voice.

She used it sparingly, the way a miser spends coin. Tonight she stood on the crest of a ridge above the village of Lowmere, watching lamplight gutter in the windows below, and she spent three sentences like a woman laying cards on a table.

"They have found the dragon," she said.

Behind her, a hundred throats tightened around a hundred silent screams.

"And they mean to wake it."

The wind came up off the fields carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet—a wrongness in the air that Serris had learned to read the way sailors read water. The gods were stirring. Everyone could feel it now; it was no longer a secret kept by mages and shrine-keepers. The old powers were leaning against the walls of the world like prisoners hearing keys in distant corridors, and every day the walls rang thinner.

"We march tonight," Serris said. "We walk until we reach the hollow. And when we stand before that dying beast, we will do what our grandmothers' grandmothers should have done a century ago."

She turned to face them. In the dark her eyes caught what little light there was and gave none back, and a hundred ash-gray faces looked upon her with the particular devotion of people who have surrendered the thing they most feared losing.

"We will let it die *finished*."

* * *

Brother Callow had not always been hollow.

He had been a farmer's son in the wheat country east of Caerlum, a boy who sang off-key at festivals and wept openly at weddings, and he could remember—dimly, the way you remember a room you lived in before a fire—the sound of his own laughter. Then the drought came, and his mother prayed to Saint Aldera of the Wells, as her mother had, as her grandmother had, and for eleven days nothing happened, because nothing had ever happened, because the gods of the valley-lands beyond Caerlum were locked behind an old silence like flies in amber.

On the twelfth day his mother prayed again, and this time something answered.

It answered through the well.

What came up out of the water wore his mother's face and spoke with a voice like wet gravel, and it offered rain in exchange for things Callow did not learn about until after the funeral. He had run then. He had run until he found the Hollow Choir camped in a burned chapel, and Mother Serris had listened to his story without interrupting, and at the end she had said only:

"You see why the silence must be kept."

He saw it still. He saw it every night behind his eyelids.

So it was Callow whom Serris sent ahead into Lowmere, because he knew how villagers thought before the fear took them, and because his voice—rusted from disuse but functional—could pass for an ordinary man's.

He walked into the village alone, hood back, hands open, and bought bread at the bakery with honest coin and made small talk about weather, and it was almost like being a person again. The baker's wife remembered him from years past, when the Choir had passed through as mere mendicants. She gave him the end crusts free, browned with butter.

"The road's gone strange," she said, wrapping the loaves. "You'll want to be careful. Folk coming through from the north talk about lights in the sky. And the miller's daughter—" she dropped her voice, though the kitchen was empty, "—she prayed at the waystone last week. Just a girl's prayer, mind, for her sweetheart to write. And the stone answered her. Spoke right out loud, in a voice like her dead father's. She hasn't slept since. Won't eat. Keeps saying the stone knows her name."

Callow felt the cold settle into his chest, familiar as an old coat. "And what does your priest say?"

"Priest left." The baker's wife laughed, but there was no humor in it. "Left a month ago. Said if the gods were waking, he'd rather meet them on his knees somewhere far from here. Can you imagine? A shepherd running from his own flock?"

*I can imagine,* Callow thought. *I've seen what the wolves look like.*

He thanked her, took his bread, and paused at the door. "If the Choir comes through," he said carefully, "you'd do well to keep your shutters closed and your daughters indoors. We're peaceful folk. But there are others on the roads who aren't."

"Oh, we know," she said, and her face did something complicated. "Everyone knows the Hollow Men are marching. My brother says they're the only ones standing between us and—that's what he says. Others say different."

"What do you say?"

She looked at him for a long moment, flour on her hands, butter-browned crusts cooling on the sill between them, and whatever she said next she said quietly, the way people say true things.

"I say my daughter's sweetheart is stationed at Saltmarsh Crossing, and yesterday morning every bell in the garrison rang at once with nobody pulling the ropes. So whichever side you hollow men are on, God help the other one."

* * *

They crossed through Lowmere at dawn without stopping, a hundred silent shapes flowing along the rutted road like smoke given direction, and Callow watched the shutters close ahead of them window by window, street by street, a wave of small wooden surrenders. No one threw stones. No one spat. That was worse. Fear that has decided to wait is more dangerous than fear that acts.

By midday the first god found them.

It did not come as a shape. It came as a pressure, a change in the light, and suddenly the noon sun had the color of candleflame seen through amber wine, and every member of the Choir stopped walking at once, a hundred bodies halting in unison like a single creature deciding.

In the barley field beside the road, the grain began to move against the wind.

*Children of the hush,* said a voice from everywhere and nowhere, warm as bread and vast as weather. It was not cruel. That was the horror of it—it was *kind*, the way a landslide might be kind, the way gravity is kind to everything equally. *You walk to snuff a little flame. How diligent. How dutiful. Tell me, little grays—when the fire is out and the walls are thick again, will you visit me? Will you bring me songs? I have been so hungry for songs.*

Mother Serris stepped forward, and where her shadow fell across the barley, the stalks went gray and stiff, frost-killed in high summer.

"You are not owed songs," she said. Her voice was quiet and it carried anyway, the way it always did, as if the world itself leaned in to listen. "You are owed nothing. That was the covenant. That was the mercy."

*Mercy,* the god said, and laughed, and the laugh rippled through the field in waves of bent gold. *A dragon burns its own heart to build your prison, and you call the prison mercy. Sweet children. When your walls crack—and they are cracking, can't you hear them singing?—remember that I asked politely. Remember that I was kind.*

The light snapped back to ordinary noon. The barley stilled. But as the Choir resumed marching, Callow noticed that the grain nearest the road, where Serris's shadow had lain, stood bleached and dead in a long pale swath, and that the villagers who crept out to watch them go fell to their knees—not toward the Choir, but toward the field.

Toward the god.

That night, camped in a hollow of hills, Serris gathered the inner circle around a low fire—one of the few fires they permitted themselves, small and screened, because fire was a language the enemy spoke fluently.

"It grows bolder," said Brother Anselm, the eldest of them, his ash-marks faded nearly to skin. "When I took my vows, twenty years ago, the powers couldn't touch the waking world. Now this one speaks through barley in broad daylight. The covenant is failing faster than the records predicted."

"The records assumed no one would seek the dragon," Serris said. She was feeding the fire splinter by splinter, each piece placed with surgical care. "The records assumed the guarantor line understood its duty. Instead, some Marchetti whelp has gone wandering with a runaway keeper and a head full of temple stories, and every step they take toward the hollow loosens another stone."

"You're certain it's a Marchetti?" Anselm asked. "The bloodline was supposed to be extinct outside the valley."

"I am certain of what the censer told me." Serris held her palms to the flame. On the back of her right hand, her own ash-mark—the double spiral all Choir members wore—had begun, in recent weeks, to darken and sharpen, as if the silence itself were concentrating in her flesh, gathering itself for the work ahead. "I am certain that two travelers passed through Thornhollow asking after the dragon-road, and that the innkeeper described a storm-caller's brand on the older one's wrist. Three spirals and a bolt. The mark of the oath-line. I burned that innkeeper's memory of it myself, but memories burn unevenly, and word travels faster than we do."

"Then we must travel faster than word," Callow said.

Serris smiled at him, and it was the smile that had saved his life once, years ago, on the worst night of it, and he loved her the way survivors love the hand that pulled them from the water—without examination, without limit, and with a private terror of ever being set down.

"Yes," she said. "Tomorrow we leave the roads entirely. We cut across the fells and come down on the hollow from the north. Four days, if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?" Anselm asked.

Serris looked up at the sky, where clouds were assembling on the southern horizon in formations no honest wind had ever built—towering, slow-turning, patient. Somewhere far away, she knew, a storm-caller was learning to use muscles grown monstrous from decades of disuse.

"If it doesn't," she said, "then we will finally see what we are marching against. Sleep. All of you. Tomorrow we become the silence the world remembers."

* * *

Wren could not sleep.

She sat up on the second watch, knees drawn to her chest, watching Emrys sleep fitfully by the banked fire—their brow furrowed even in dreams now, fingers twitching in small gestures that raised static sparks along the blanket's edge. Since the marsh, Emrys hadn't slept a whole night through. Power like that didn't rest easy in a body that had spent thirty years pretending it wasn't there.

Beyond the firelight, the dark felt occupied.

Wren had grown up in temples; she knew the grammar of the sacred, the way a holy place hummed just below hearing. But this was different. For three nights running, the darkness around their camp had felt *attentive*, the way a crowded room feels when everyone has stopped talking to listen to you. And twice now she had woken to find the censer of dragon-ash glowing brighter than it should, pulsing softly like a heart under cloth, and both times the pulse had synced, horribly, to footsteps she could not hear.

Tonight the censer flared again, and this time she heard the footsteps.

Not near. Far. A great many of them, miles south, marching in unison—and yet she heard them clearly, each footfall distinct, as if the ground itself were carrying the sound to her like a cupped hand offering water. A hundred silent walkers whose steps the earth refused to swallow, broadcasting their approach across the fells.

She woke Emrys with a hand over their mouth, because she had learned that much already.

"Marching," she breathed. "South of us. A lot of someone. And Emrys—" she lifted the censer, its glow staining both their faces ember-orange, "—it knows. Whatever's in here, it knows they're coming, and it's *scared*."

Emrys stared at the pulsing ash, and Wren watched something resolve in their face—the same expression they'd worn at the marsh, terror and purpose fused into a single hard thing.

"How many?" Emrys whispered.

"A hundred. Maybe more." Wren swallowed. "Emrys, they'll reach the hollow before we do. At this pace, they'll reach it *first*."

Emrys was quiet for a moment. Then, in the smallest voice Wren had ever heard them use—smaller than any prayer, and just as desperate—they said:

"Then we stop sleeping, and we start running. Because whatever else happens, whatever I have to burn of myself to pay for it—" their hand closed gently around the censer, around the tiny frightened heat of the last dragon-fire, "—we do not let them arrive alone."

Far to the south, beneath a sky where the stars had begun, very faintly, to flicker like guttering candles, the Hollow Choir marched on through the dark—and the dark, for the first time in a hundred years, marched back.
