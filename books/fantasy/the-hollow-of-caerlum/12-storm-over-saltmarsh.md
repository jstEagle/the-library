## 12. Storm Over Saltmarsh

The river came out of nowhere.

That was the lie I told myself for the first hour of seeing it — that it had appeared, sudden and malicious, like everything else in our lives since we'd left Caerlum. In truth, Wren had been warning me about it since dawn, reading the land the way she read temple script: *the ground's gone soft, look how the reeds lean, there will be water soon.* But I had spent forty years in a valley where rivers were polite things, fordable, named on no map worth keeping, and some stubborn part of me refused to believe the world outside could simply open its throat and swallow a road.

The saltmarsh opened around us like a held breath. Grey water sheeted over grey mud to the horizon, stitched with channels that gleamed like veins under skin. The air tasted of brine and rot and something older underneath — the same mineral ghost I'd felt at every boundary since leaving home, the taste of magic pressing against the world's skin from beneath. The road became a suggestion, then a rumor, then a line of stones sinking one by one into black water.

"The crossing is ahead," Wren said. She stood in the cart bed — we'd traded my grandmother's silver for the cart and a mule with opinions — and shaded her eyes against a sun that couldn't decide whether to exist. "There. The ferryman's post."

I saw it: a leaning pole wrapped in faded prayer-ribbons, and beside it a flat-bottomed boat hauled half onto a mudbank. And I saw what Wren saw a heartbeat later, because her hand closed hard around my shoulder and her breath went thin.

Men. A dozen of them, maybe more, spread along the near bank in the reeds. Not fishermen. Fishermen don't wear iron nails hammered through their boot-heels — I'd learned that much on the road, learned it from villages that had stopped answering prayers and started answering fear. Hollow Men, the villagers called them, though they weren't the Choir itself, not yet. Just men who had decided the silence was a wall worth defending with their bodies, and who had heard — everyone had heard, by now — that a storm-caller was walking east with a runaway shrine-keeper and a box of dragon ash.

"They've seen us," Wren said quietly.

"Yes."

"Do we run?"

I looked at the marsh behind us, the miles of drowning ground we'd crossed since morning, and at the channel ahead, wide as a street and moving with the tide. Running would mean the mud. The mud would mean the reeds, and the reeds were already full.

"No," I said, and heard my grandmother's voice in mine, and hated it. "We cross."

* * *

The ferryman was a woman with a face like driftwood and a boy — her grandson, I guessed — poling the flat. She looked at us, at the cart, at the mule, and named a price that was robbery twice over. I paid it. Wren counted the coins into her palm and said, softly, in the cadence of shrine-greeting, *"May what watches you watch kindly."*

The ferrywoman flinched. That told me everything. These were not people who wanted watching, kindly or otherwise. These were people who had learned that being watched was how it started — answered prayers first, then demands, then gods standing in your fields asking to be fed.

"Load quick," she said. "Water's wrong today."

"What does that mean?" Wren asked, hauling the ash-box up the plank.

"It means the tide's coming in against the wind." The woman spat over the side. "It means something big is pushing the water. It means get in the boat."

We got in the boat. The mule swam behind, roped, furious. The boy poled us out into the channel, and the current took us sideways immediately, dragging us downriver toward the open marsh, and the woman cursed and threw her whole weight against the sweep-oar, and for a few minutes there was nothing in the world but water and work.

That was when the arrows came.

Not many. Three, loosed from the reeds on the far bank, falling short with little kisses of spray. Then five more, better aimed, and one of them took the boy through the shoulder and put him down in the bottom of the boat, screaming the small, astonished scream of someone who has never been hurt before and cannot believe the world would begin now.

Everything after that happened slowly, the way disasters do — each second stretched and lit like a page I was forced to read.

Wren reaching the boy, tearing her sleeve for bandage, her hands steady because shrine-keepers learn wounds before they learn prayers. The ferrywoman roaring words I didn't know, hauling the oar, taking us toward the far bank anyway because there was nowhere else. The reeds on that bank shifting, men rising out of them with iron-tipped pikes, and among them — I knew him even at that distance, because you don't forget a voice once it has offered you a family — Brother Callow, the Hollow Choir's envoy, the man who had smiled at me across a commune table and said *you could belong here* while his eyes measured me for a grave.

He raised one hand, and the marsh went silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The way Caerlum was silent. The wind died mid-breath. The water flattened. Even the boy's screaming cut off, swallowed, and I felt the absence close around my skull like a hand — the stolen silence-magic, the thing they'd torn out of broken covenant-stones, wielded now like a net thrown over the world.

And in that silence, I heard my own blood.

That is the only way I can describe what happened next. For forty years I had been a house with the furniture covered in sheets. Now, inside the Choir's dead-air, every sheet blew off at once. I felt the storm that had been waiting in me since the day I crossed the valley boundary — the storm I'd barely survived, the one that had cracked three teeth and burned the nerves of my left arm numb for a week — and I understood, finally, that it had never been wild. It had been patient. It had been waiting for me to stop being afraid of it, or failing that, for something I loved more than I feared it.

Wren looked up from the boy's wound. Our eyes met across six feet of rocking boat, and she didn't say *don't*. She didn't say *be careful*. She said, aloud, in a silence that should have made speech impossible but somehow carried anyway, the way prayer carries:

"Emrys. It's all right. I'm right here."

* * *

I have tried, since, to remember choosing it. I can't. What I remember is the sky.

The sky came down.

There was no gathering, no darkening, none of the theater storms are supposed to perform. One moment the air was grey and held; the next, the clouds above the saltmarsh tore open along a seam a mile long, and the light that fell through was not sunlight. It was the color of the inside of a bell. And the sound — the sound arrived before the lightning did, a pressure, a fist closing around the whole marsh, and every reed lay flat at once as if the land itself had been ordered to kneel.

I called the lightning down on the far bank.

I called it down on the water between, on the near bank, on the reeds where the archers crouched, on the iron nails in their boots that they thought would keep the magic out — iron keeps out small magics, hedge-craft, charms; it does not keep out weather. Weather is not a spell. Weather is the world being asked a question it cannot refuse.

The first strike hit the channel and turned the water white. The second hit the mudbank and glassed it. After that I stopped counting, because counting requires a self doing the counting, and I was no longer entirely a self. I was a conduit with a human memory attached, and the memory was screaming, and the storm poured through the screaming like water through a broken dam, and somewhere very far away I understood that I was leveling the marsh. Not striking it. Leveling it. The channels boiled. The reeds burned green and vanished. A stand of alders that had stood in that mud for two hundred years went up in a single flash and came down as smoke. The thunder did not echo off the horizon because there was no horizon left unbroken to echo off of.

And the men.

I want to be able to say I missed them. Some I did — the storm is vast and blind and most of those men lived, thrown down, deafened, their iron nails fused, crawling away through burning mud. But the storm found the ones who kept coming. It found the archer who loosed again through the fire-rain, and it found the pike-line that advanced on the ferry post through a marsh that was dying around them, and it found Brother Callow standing untouched in a bubble of stolen silence, shouting orders I could not hear, and it *hated* him — I hated him, the distinction had dissolved — and the bolt I sent at him split the silence-bubble like an eggshell and threw him sixty feet into the deep channel, and I watched the water close over him and I did not know if he rose.

I still don't know if he rose.

What stopped me wasn't mercy. Mercy takes a self. What stopped me was Wren's hand on my wrist.

She had crawled to me through the chaos of the boat — the ferrywoman holding the sweep, the boy grey and breathing, the mule somehow alive and standing chest-deep in shallows — and she had put both hands around my forearm, the numb one, the scarred one, and she was saying my name. Not shouting it. Saying it. Over and over, the way you'd speak to someone walking in their sleep at the top of a stair.

"Emrys. Emrys. Emrys. Come back. Come back. You're here. You're with me. Come back."

The storm let go.

Not gently. Storms don't know gentle. It tore free of me the way a hook tears free, and I came back into my body all at once — the pain of it, gods, the pain, every nerve I owned ringing like struck bronze — and I collapsed against the gunwale and was sick into the salt water, and above us the torn sky began, slowly, grudgingly, to knit itself shut.

The marsh steamed. Smoke stood up off the flats in a hundred columns. Where the alder stand had been there was a crater of grey glass, and rain was falling on it now, soft ordinary rain, hissing, and the sound of that rain was the loudest thing in the world because everything else had been erased.

I had done that.

*I* had done that.

* * *

We made the far bank because the far bank no longer had anyone on it willing to be there. The ferrywoman poled us in without a word, took her grandson — Wren had packed the shoulder wound with marsh-moss and it had held, the bleeding stopped, the boy conscious and staring at me with the particular blankness of someone revising every story he'd ever been told about what people are — and pushed off into the smoking water without looking back. I couldn't blame her. I wouldn't have looked back either.

The mule stood on the mudbank, dripping, alive, regarding me with an expression I chose to interpret as forgiveness.

I sat down in the mud. It seemed appropriate. Everything else had been brought low; I might as well join it.

"I killed people," I said.

Wren crouched in front of me. Her sleeves were soaked red to the elbow. There was soot on her face and her braid had come half loose and she looked, in that moment, more like herself than I had ever seen her — the runaway who had walked out of a temple with a censer of dying fire because someone had to carry it, because carrying things was what she did.

"Some," she said. "Maybe. We don't know."

"We don't know isn't the same as no."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She sat down beside me in the mud, which I hadn't asked her to do, which ruined the whole architecture of my despair, because despair wants an audience at a respectful distance. "Do you want the version where I tell you they came to kill us? Do you want the version where I remind you the boy would be dead, and I'd be dead, and the ferrywoman too? Or do you want the true one?"

"Which is the true one?"

"The one where both things are real." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "You saved us. And you scared yourself worse than you scared them. Both. At the same time. That's what power is, Emrys. Nobody ever tells you that part. They tell you it feels like glory. It doesn't. It feels like finding out the room you've lived in your whole life was missing a wall, and outside the wall is the sea."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, closer to the other thing. "My grandmother sealed a valley rather than feel this."

"Your grandmother sealed a valley rather than *choose* this," Wren said. "There's a difference. She inherited a covenant and paid for it with silence. You just — " she gestured at the smoking marsh, the cratered glass, the rain still falling soft and ordinary on the ruin — "you just paid for ten seconds. With a piece of yourself. And you're sitting here feeling the bill."

"And you're sitting next to me."

"I'm sitting next to you," she said, "because when the sky opened, do you know what I thought? Everyone on that bank thought they were going to die. I thought: *there it is.* The thing they wrote about in the temple records. The Marchetti bloodline, the storm in the family, the reason a whole valley had to be locked away from the world. I grew up on those records, Emrys. I used to think they were describing a monster." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes were steady and brown and completely unafraid, and that undid me more thoroughly than the lightning had undone the marsh. "They were describing a person. A frightened person with too much sky in them. That's all."

The rain thickened. Somewhere out on the flats, a bird started up again — the first sound to return to the world, some marsh-wader crying its two-note question across the water, and I clung to that sound like a rope. The world was already beginning again. The world is shameless that way.

"There were men in that reed-line," I said finally. "Running. I saw them running and I — the storm saw them running, and it didn't care, and I couldn't make it care. Wren, I couldn't *find* the part of me that cared. It was like — like being a bell. Someone else decides when you ring."

"Then we teach you to hold the rope yourself." She said it simply, the way she said everything, as if it were a chore like mending a wick. "Vaelith will know how. Dragon-fire and storm-blood — the old records pair them constantly. That's why the covenant needed your family. Not to lock the door." She touched the ash-box between us, still faintly warm even through the wood, still glowing its ember-red through the seams. "To *hold* it. There's a difference, Emrys. There's always been a difference. The Choir just never learned it."

I looked out at the marsh. The rain was laying the smoke down. In the grey distance, the deep channel where Callow had fallen glinted flat and innocent, keeping whatever it kept. And beyond it, east, past the drowned flats and the hostile villages and the communes of the silence-worshippers, the land climbed toward high moor, and above the moor, faint as a memory of heat, the air shimmered the way air shimmers over a forge.

The hollow. Vaelith's hollow. Two days' walk, if the world let us have two days.

"He'll rally them," I said. "Callow, if he lived. And if he didn't, whoever's behind him. They've seen what I am now. So has every village for fifty miles — you don't hide a storm, Wren. You don't hide a storm."

"No," she said. She got to her feet, mud-streaked, exhausted, twenty years old and steadier than the ground, and held out her hand to me. "So we stop trying. Come on. The mule's judging us."

I took her hand. My arm ached where the lightning had passed through it, and my ears rang with a silence deeper than the Choir's, and somewhere inside me the storm lay coiled and satisfied and utterly indifferent to my grief, and I stood up anyway, because she was pulling, and because the ember in the box pulsed once as I rose — quick, bright, eager — like a heart answering a name.

Two days to the hollow. And behind us, on the far horizon, I could have sworn I saw dust rising. Not weather-dust.

Marching-dust.
