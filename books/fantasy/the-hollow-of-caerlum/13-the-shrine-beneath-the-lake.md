## 13. The Shrine Beneath the Lake

The lake had a name once, before the temple took it. Now everyone called it simply the Shrine's Water, as if the lake existed only to hold up the building that stood upon it — and in a way, it did.

Wren saw it first from the ridge road at dusk: a black disc of water cupped in the hills, and rising from its center on stilts of pale stone, the Sunken Temple of Aurelith, its copper roof gone green with centuries, its lanterns burning gold against the coming dark. The whole structure seemed to float, though Wren knew better. It was anchored. Everything here was anchored — by chains, by doctrine, by the slow drowning of anyone who tried to swim away.

She stopped walking. Emrys stopped beside her, and after a moment, so did Vaelith's ember-glow in the lantern at their hip, dimming politely, like a guest lowering their voice.

"That's it," Wren said. Her voice came out flat. She'd been rehearsing this for three days of travel and none of the rehearsals survived the sight of the place. "That's where I grew up."

Emrys said nothing. They were good at that — saying nothing in a way that made room. The wind off the lake moved through the reeds below the road with a sound like whispered counting, and somewhere across the water, the evening bell began to toll. Seven strokes. Vespers. Her body knew the count before her mind did; her hands nearly folded themselves into the old prayer-shape, thumb to thumb, fingers laced like a cage.

She forced them open.

"The relic's beneath the high altar," she said. "The First Flame's cradle. A shard of the original vessel — they call it the Undying Wick. I carried it in procession every Feast of Ashes. I know exactly which stone it sleeps under." She paused. "I also know exactly who will be standing between us and it."

"Your mentor," Emrys said.

"Mother Sarelle." The name tasted like lake water: cold, mineral, faintly rotten underneath. "She raised me from the age of six. Taught me to keep shrine-fire, to read the old liturgies, to walk the reflection-path without looking down." Wren's jaw tightened. "And when I found out what the temple actually does with answered prayers — whose prayers get answered, and what it costs the ones who ask — she was the one who signed the order to have my memory scoured. Not because I was wrong. Because I was *loud*."

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. In the last light, their storm-grey eyes held something Wren hadn't seen in them before — recognition, maybe. Two people who understood that the worst betrayals wear kind faces.

"You don't have to go in," Emrys said. "We could find another way to—"

"There is no other way. Vaelith needs a vessel that held true fire, and there are four in the world. Three are lost or guarded by things we can't fight. This one is guarded by women in grey robes who think silence is holy." She shouldered her pack. "Besides. I've been running from this place for two years. I'm tired of running past it too."

* * *

They crossed at low bell, when the causeway emerged from the shallows like the spine of some drowned animal — a mile of wet flagstone winding between reed-beds, wide enough for two pilgrims abreast, lined with votive posts where travelers tied ribbons for prayers the temple had promised to forward upward. Hundreds of ribbons. Thousands. They snapped and fluttered in the evening wind, and Wren thought of everything each one represented: a sick mother, a lost son, a failing harvest. Prayers the temple collected like tithes and sorted like grain — the cheap ones granted to build faith, the expensive ones shelved, the dangerous ones drowned.

Two acolytes met them at the water-gate, young faces under grey hoods, lanterns swinging.

"Pilgrims," one said. Not a question. Pilgrims were the only kind of person the temple recognized; everything else was weather.

"We seek the vigil of the Undying Flame," Wren said, using the ritual cadence, letting the old words come up out of her like water from a well she'd poisoned but never drained. "For healing of the soul."

It was a lie shaped like a truth, which was the temple's own native language, and the acolytes bowed and led them in.

Inside, the Sunken Temple was exactly as she remembered, and that was the cruelest part. The great hall built over open water, floor grates revealing the lake's green depths below, so that worshippers knelt literally above the abyss while incense coiled upward toward a ceiling painted with a god's indifferent golden face. The smell of beeswax and cold stone and under it, always, the mineral breath of the deep. Novices gliding along the walls with downcast eyes. And everywhere the sound — the soft endless lap of water beneath the floor, the temple's heartbeat, the sound Wren had fallen asleep to for fourteen years and woken from screaming for two.

They were given cells. They were given bread and lentils. They were told the vigil would be granted at dawn if their hearts were clean.

Wren sat on the narrow cot in her old cell — they'd put her within twenty feet of the room she'd grown up in, whether by chance or design — and listened to the water breathe beneath the floor, and did not sleep.

Near midnight, someone knocked.

She knew the knock before she opened the door. Three raps, pause, one more. *Ready yourself, child.* Mother Sarelle had knocked that way across fifteen years of mornings.

The woman in the corridor was smaller than memory kept her. Grey-robed, silver-streaked hair bound tight, hands folded in that eternal posture of patient authority. Her face had more lines now, and fewer illusions, but the eyes were the same: pale, clear, and utterly without warmth, like winter light on snow. Eyes that could look at you with what felt like love and register, underneath, only your usefulness.

"Wren," Sarelle said softly. "Or whatever you're calling yourself now."

"You knew I'd come."

"I knew *someone* would come asking after the Wick. The lake has been restless for a week. The reflections show things they shouldn't." Sarelle's gaze flicked past her, down the dark corridor, toward the cell where Emrys slept — or didn't. "You travel with a Marchetti. With an exile. With—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—a piece of the old fire. Do you have any idea what you're carrying into my house?"

"*Your* house." Wren stepped out and closed her cell door behind her. "Funny. When I lived here, it was the god's house. Then it was the covenant's house. It seems to change ownership whenever it's convenient."

Sarelle's expression didn't move, but something behind it did — a flinch, small and fast, like a fish under ice. "You were a child. You understood nothing of what the temple does, of what it *must* do. The covenant is failing, girl. The gods are waking up hungry, and prayers are being answered by things wearing familiar faces. The temple's discipline is the only dam holding back—"

"I know." Wren's voice stayed level, which surprised her. "I know exactly what the temple does. That's why I left. That's why you tried to hollow out my head with the scouring-rite — not because I was wrong about any of it, but because I wouldn't stop *saying* it." She took a step closer. In the lantern-light, Mother Sarelle looked suddenly, terribly mortal. "I found the ledger, Mother. The prayer-tithe ledger. I found the name of the miller's daughter from Fenlow, the one who prayed for her father's life, and I found what the temple charged her family for the miracle, and I found what happened when they couldn't pay. Don't stand in this corridor and lecture me about necessity. I learned necessity at your knee."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Beneath the floor, the lake breathed.

"You were the best of them," Sarelle said finally, and her voice cracked along a fault line Wren had never heard before. "The best novice I ever taught. Do you think it gave me no pain, signing that order? Do you think—"

"No." Wren said it simply. "I think it pained you the way amputation pains a surgeon. Cleanly. Professionally. With an eye to the health of the body and none at all for the limb." She watched the older woman absorb that, watch it land, watch it fail to change anything — and that was the thing she had come here to learn, really. That it would fail. That there would be no hidden wound, no secret repentance, no moment where Mother Sarelle broke open and became someone who deserved forgiveness. Some people are not locked doors waiting for the right key. Some people are walls, load-bearing, and you do not forgive a wall. You walk around it, or you tear it down, or you build your house elsewhere.

"I didn't come for an apology," Wren said. "I want you to know that. I came for the Wick. And I'm going to take it."

Something shifted in Sarelle's stance — the teacher becoming the guardian, the guardian becoming the soldier. "The vigil begins at dawn. The relic is sealed beneath the altar until then, and the seal answers to my voice and the god's. You'll find I've warned the sisters. The water-gate is closed. Whatever you're planning—"

"Nothing yet," Wren admitted. "But you should know something, Mother, since you taught me to always report accurately." She leaned close, and let her voice drop into the register Sarelle herself had used for terrible truths, the intimate whisper of confessional and command. "The dragon is dying. The last one. The fire that holds both realms apart is guttering in her chest like a candle in a doorway, and when it goes out, the silence ends — not gently, not by treaty, but the way a dam ends. Every prayer in the world will be answered at once, by whatever's listening, and your disciplined little dam won't even slow the flood. The Wick isn't a relic anymore. It's a seed. And I'm going to plant it before the ground burns."

She straightened. Sarelle stared at her, and for one unguarded instant, Wren saw it — real fear, and beneath the fear, worse, the ghost of belief. The old woman believed her. That was somehow the most painful thing the night produced.

"The refectory stores oil for the lamps," Sarelle said quietly. "Third stair down from the novices' gallery is loose. It's been loose since you were nine; I never had it fixed." She turned away, robes whispering, and paused at the edge of the lantern-light. "I am not helping you, Wren. Understand that. If you're caught, I will scourge you myself. But the lake has been showing me the same reflection for seven nights, and in it, the temple stands empty, and the water is full of light." Her voice frayed. "I am an old woman who has spent her life keeping a flame she was never allowed to look at directly. Go. Before I remember my duty."

She walked away into the dark, and Wren stood alone in the corridor, listening to the water breathe, feeling something in her chest that was not forgiveness and not quite its opposite — just the strange lightness of setting down a weight she'd mistaken for part of her own skeleton.

* * *

They went down at the hour of deepest quiet, when even the lake seemed to hold its breath.

The loose stair opened onto the undercroft, and the undercroft opened onto the place Wren had been walking toward in her dreams for two years: the Reliquary of the First Flame, a round chamber sunk below the waterline, its walls of green glass through which the lake pressed like a vast slow heart. Fish moved overhead, shadows among shadows. Roots of the temple's own pilings descended through the water like the legs of drowned giants. And in the center, beneath a canopy of hammered gold, on an altar of black stone worn smooth by ten thousand kisses of pilgrim foreheads —

The Undying Wick.

It was small. That was the shock of it, every time. A shard of dark glass no longer than a finger, veined with a thread of amber light that pulsed — slowly, patiently, with a rhythm Wren recognized now, having spent weeks listening to a dying dragon breathe. Fire-memory. The oldest kind. A splinter of the vessel that had carried Vaelith's sacrifice, back when the world was young enough to break cleanly.

"It's real," Emrys breathed beside her. Their storm-sense made the air around them shiver; frost feathered and melted on the glass walls. "I can feel it pulling. Like a low pressure front. Wren — the whole lake is leaning toward that thing."

"Then let's not keep it waiting."

The seal was a ring of liturgical script around the altar's base, and it woke as Wren approached, letters flaring white. *Who claims the flame?* the old words demanded, in the god's borrowed voice.

Wren set her palms flat on the black stone. She thought about lying, about using the ritual phrases, about the clever answers she'd prepared. Then she thought about Vaelith, vast and fading, speaking through borrowed memories because she had nothing left of her own. About Emrys, trading pieces of their mind for a world's survival. About the ribbons on the votive posts, thousands of them, snapping in the wind.

"A thief," she said clearly. "A runaway. A keeper who keeps for the people now, not the god. I claim the flame, and I claim it *out loud*, and if your god objects, he knows where to find me."

The seal considered. Somewhere far above, the temple bells rang once, unprompted, a single bronze note that rolled down through the water like a verdict.

Then the letters went dark, and the Wick rose from its cradle of its own accord, drifting into Wren's cupped hands, warm as a sleeping bird.

No alarm sounded. No sisters came running. Either Sarelle's warning had been thorough, or the temple's god, waking hungry in a thinning world, had decided to let this particular theft proceed — and Wren honestly didn't know which possibility frightened her more.

They were halfway back up the loose stair when the water outside the glass walls changed.

It began at the far shore — a line of torchlight, dozens of torches, descending the ridge road in perfect silent order. No singing. No shouting. Processional discipline, grey and absolute. Wren pressed her palm to the cold glass and watched the torches resolve into ranks, and at their head a figure in white who walked without a lantern, because the darkness made way for her.

"The Hollow Choir," Emrys whispered. "Here? They were days behind us—"

"They didn't follow us." Wren's mouth had gone dry. "They came for the same thing we did. And they're not stopping at the shore."

Above them, muffled by fathoms of stone and water, the temple bells began to ring — not vespers, not vigil, but the alarm-pattern, frantic and arrhythmic, and through the green glass the lake itself shuddered, as if something enormous on the far side of the world had turned over in its sleep.

Wren closed her hand around the Wick, felt its small fierce heartbeat answer her own, and started to run.
