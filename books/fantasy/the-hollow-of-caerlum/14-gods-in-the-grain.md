## 14. Gods in the Grain

I remember when the sky had a thousand voices.

Not metaphor—memory. Before the covenant, before I poured my fire into the seal and let it be ground into law, the air itself was crowded. Gods moved through grain and riverlight the way weather moves: everywhere at once, touching everything, answerable to nothing. Mortals prayed into that vastness and were heard, or were not, and either way the world turned on.

Now there is only me, half-dissolved in the dark of my own hollow, listening to the silence I helped build begin to crack like river ice in spring.

The humans are coming. I feel them the way I once felt storms gathering over the saltmarshes—a pressure behind the ribs of the world. Two small warm lives walking toward me with a relic and a death-wish, and behind them, farther off but closing, a column of people who worship emptiness and mean to make it permanent.

But they are not what wakes me tonight.

Tonight, the gods are stirring.

* * *

It begins as a taste. That is how the old ones announce themselves to me now—I who am no longer wholly dragon, no longer wholly ghost, but a memory wearing the shape of one. Ash and honey on the tongue means Serevanth, the Harvest-King, he who was worshipped with scythes and first-fruits and whose altars ran red more often than any mortal likes to admit. I have not tasted him in two hundred years.

"You're awake," says the darkness at the mouth of my hollow, and then it is not darkness at all. He steps out of it the way harvest steps out of summer—all at once, inevitable, golden and terrible. He wears the shape of a broad-shouldered man crowned with wheat gone to seed, and his eyes are the brown of turned earth, and beneath the warmth of him is the thing mortals always forgot until the knife came down: gods do not love. Gods *take account.*

"Serevanth." My voice comes out as smoke. It costs me more than it used to form even this much of myself. "You shouldn't be able to find this place."

"Nothing should be able to die, either, little dragon, and yet here you are." He crouches before me, and I see that his golden light gutters the way mine does. The failing covenant starves us all—but it starves the bound ones worst. "The seal is thinning. Can you feel it? Every answered prayer tears another seam. Your Marchetti child has been very busy."

"They are not *mine.*"

"No?" He smiles, and wheat husks fall from his hair like snow. "You bled your fire into their grandmother's stone. You made her blood the hinge of heaven. If that isn't ownership, Vaelith, I don't know what the word means anymore."

"I made her blood a *promise,*" I say. "There is a difference. It was the whole point."

He laughs, low and rolling, thunder in a granary. Then the laughter stops as if cut with a sickle, and what remains is older and colder.

"The others are waking," he says. "The thinning has reached even the deep prisons. And not all of us dream of freedom, dragon. Some dream of thrones. I have come to know which side of that line you'll find me on—before someone less patient finds you first."

"And which side is that?"

He spreads his hands, and for a moment I see through the man-shape to what he truly is: an appetite as old as the first planted field, the understanding that if you control what feeds people, you control them entire.

"I want *in,*" he says simply. "Not dominion. Not the old war. A door. A lawful door, cut by the guarantor's own hand, so that we return as guests rather than conquerors. Give me that, and my strength is yours against whatever crawls up from the deeper dark."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will watch what happens," he says gently, "and I will weep beautifully at the funeral."

* * *

I give him nothing. Not yet. But I let him go without unmaking him, which in my current state is nearly the same as agreement, and we both know it.

That is the arithmetic of dying gods: every bargain struck from weakness is a debt written in someone else's blood. My grandmother-of-the-covenant—the first Marchetti, the woman who knelt in this hollow while I burned my own heart into the seal—she understood this. *Never trade with hunger,* she told me, her hands still wet with the clay of the covenant-stone. *Hunger only learns where you keep the food.*

But I am so tired. And the seams are tearing faster now.

* * *

The second one does not announce herself at all.

I simply become aware, deep in the night of the hollow, that the water pooling at my feet has gone mirror-still, and in its surface stands a woman made of river-current and drowned things. Neth-Isara. The Drowned Choir. In the old days she took the shape of whatever the grieving needed most—a mother, a lover, a child come home—and by the time the bereaved realized the shape was hollow, they had already followed her under.

She does not bother with a shape now. Perhaps she knows I cannot be fooled by one. Perhaps she simply no longer has the strength for courtesy.

*"Vaelith."* Her voice arrives inside my skull like water filling lungs. *"Serevanth came to you. I felt his passage. What did the Harvest-King offer?"*

"A lawful door. His strength against the deep ones."

*"And you believed him?"*

"I believed that he believes himself sincere. It amounts to the same danger."

The water shivers. If she had a face, I think it would be smiling. *"You always were the honest one. It's why they chose you for the sealing—you were the only one of us who could be trusted to mean it."*

"They didn't choose me. I volunteered."

*"Yes."* Something moves through her voice that might, in a mortal, have been grief. *"Yes, you did. That was the tragedy of it."*

She drifts closer, and the pool rises with her, and I understand suddenly that I am speaking with something desperate. Even predators drown when the flood takes them too.

*"Listen to me, memory-of-a-dragon. The deep ones are coming up. The ones the first war never killed, only buried—Ur-Golomach who eats names, the Mother-of-Thorns who plants herself in sleeping minds and grows. The thinning seal is a door left unlatched, and they have had two hundred years to learn patience. When they rise, your human children and their little relic will not stop them. Nothing stops them except us. All of us. Standing together on the threshold."*

"So you also want a door."

*"I want an army,"* she says, with the honesty of the drowning. *"And you are going to need one far more than I will."*

* * *

She leaves—or recedes; with Neth-Isara it is hard to say—and I lie in the dark of my hollow doing the arithmetic of ruin.

Three parties, if I count the humans as a party, and I do; Emrys Marchetti walks toward me carrying my last hope in a censer and a stranger's hands, and I would count them twice before I counted any god. Against them: the Hollow Choir, marching with stolen silence in their fists. Beneath all of us: the deep ones, stirring in their long graves. And around the edges, waking like sleepers after a fever: the old pantheon, divided between those who want a world and those who want a crown.

Two hundred years ago, I solved a problem like this with fire. I burned myself into law and called it peace, and it worked, and it cost everything, and everyone I saved has forgotten my name.

Fire is not available to me anymore. What remains is wit, and time, and the stubbornness of a creature who once volunteered for her own immolation.

So I begin to bargain.

* * *

Serevanth returns at moonrise, because of course he does; patience was never the Harvest-King's virtue, only his costume.

"Well?" he asks.

"You will have your door," I tell him, "when the new covenant is forged—not the old one mended, but a new one, built on witness instead of silence. The Marchetti child means to write it that way. I have seen it in the shape of their coming."

His eyes narrow. Earth-brown, calculating. "A covenant that lets mortals look upon us unblinded. Do you know what that means, dragon? No more mystery. No more terror to keep the altars full. We would be *known.*"

"Yes," I say. "That is the price. That is always the price. You of all of them should understand—what is a harvest but a seed that agreed to be seen, and taken, and trusted to return?"

For a long moment he says nothing. The wheat in his crown stirs though there is no wind.

"If the deep ones rise before your new covenant is written," he says at last, "all bargains are void, and I will save myself as gods do."

"Noted," I say. "And in exchange for standing on the threshold until then?"

He considers. Then he extends one broad hand, palm up, and in it lies a single grain of wheat, glowing faintly gold. "My strength, lent to the guarantor's hand. One season's worth. Enough to hold a threshold, not enough to win a war." His smile is sharp as a sickle. "Hope bought on credit, little dragon. I know you can afford the interest. You've been paying it for two hundred years."

I take the grain. It burns going down, sweet as first bread, and somewhere in my dissolving body a candle that had guttered to a thread stands up straight again.

One.

* * *

Neth-Isara's price is stranger.

She comes back with the dawn—or with what passes for dawn in a hollow that has not seen true sun since I sealed myself inside it—and she brings with her the sound of singing. Hundreds of voices, layered and looping, the drowned choir of every prayer ever whispered over open water.

*"I want a name,"* she says.

I wait.

*"When the new covenant is written, the mortals will know us. Truly know us. And what they know, they name, and what they name, they hold power over."* The water trembles. *"I have been the thing in the deep for so long, Vaelith. The thing mothers warn children about. I want to be named kindly. Once. By the guarantor's own voice. Let Emrys Marchetti call me something gentle, and speak it aloud, and mean it—and my choir will stand your threshold until the stars burn out."*

"And if they name you cruelly?"

*"Then I will be the thing they fear,"* she says simply, *"and act accordingly."*

It is a terrible bargain. It puts the shape of a god's soul in the mouth of a frightened mortal who does not yet know she is a guarantor of anything. But I have watched Emrys Marchetti since the day their grandmother's stone cracked—watched them kneel at a dead shrine and whisper a prayer into decades of silence, watched them take a runaway stranger's hands in the dark. Frightened people, given the chance, often choose gentleness. It is the only wager I have left.

"Done," I say.

The singing swells, and for one moment the hollow fills with the sound of every drowned prayer finally answered, and I weep smoke, and do not know for whom.

Two.

* * *

The third does not come to me. I must go to it—which is to say, I stretch what remains of myself along the thinning seams of the covenant, out beyond the valley, into the high cold places where the mountain-god Ossuar sleeps standing up, as he has slept since the first war, a peak that dreams it is a man.

He was the strongest of us once. He was the one the others expected to volunteer for the sealing, and when I did it instead, he never forgave me—for the sacrifice, or for the shame of being spared it. I do not know which.

I find him dreaming, and in his dream he is still whole, and mountains do not wake easily.

*Ossuar,* I say into the dream. *Brother-of-stone. The seal is failing.*

The dream shifts. Glaciers calve. Somewhere far below, magma turns over like a sleeper deciding whether to rise.

*I know,* he says, and his voice is rockfall. *I have known for a century. I hoped to sleep through the ending.*

*There may be no ending. There may be a rewriting. The Marchetti blood walks toward me now with fire and relic and a plan I did not teach them.*

*Plans fail.*

*Yes. Which is why I need you awake.*

Silence, long as an age. Then, grudging as spring thaw: *What do you offer, ghost-of-Vaelith? You have nothing left. I have watched you spend yourself for two hundred years. You are a candle counting its own drips.*

*I offer you what you actually want,* I say. *Not worship. Not dominion. An end to watching.*

The dream goes very still.

*You have hated the silence as much as any of us,* I continue, pressing into the crack the way roots press into stone. *But you are too proud to admit it, because admitting it means admitting the sealing was a wound and not a victory. So here is my offer, brother: when the new covenant is written, you will not return to rule or to be ruled. You will return to *participate.* To be part of the world again instead of a monument above it. To feel rain on your shoulders and call it yours. I offer you an ending to your exile, Ossuar. The same one I am buying for myself with the last of my name.*

*And your price?* he rumbles. *Everything has a price. You taught us that with your burning.*

*Time,* I say. *Stand the threshold. Wake now, walk slowly, arrive before the deep ones do. Lend your weight to the guarantor's cause—not your strength, just your presence. Sometimes a mountain need only stand in the right place to change the shape of a war.*

Another age of silence. Snow falls in the dream. And then, so quietly I almost miss it beneath the grinding of stone:

*The girl with the censer. The shrine-keeper. She prayed to me once, when she was small. At a wayshrine on the pilgrim road. Did you know that?*

*I did not.*

*She asked for nothing. She only said thank you—that someone had made the mountains beautiful. First prayer in two hundred years anyone offered me that wasn't a demand.* The dream-mountain shifts, and I feel the vast attention of him turn, slow as sunrise, toward the road where two small figures walk. *Very well, ghost. For the one who said thank you. I will stand your threshold.*

Three.

* * *

I return to my hollow as the false dawn greys, and I take stock of myself the way a miser counts coins.

One season of harvest-strength, held in trust. A choir of drowned voices, bound by the hope of a kind name. A mountain, walking. Against them: the Hollow Choir and their stolen silence, the deep ones rising, and a pantheon full of gods whose bargains I have not yet struck, some of whom want crowns and none of whom want to be known.

And at the center of it all, two mortals a week's walk away, carrying my resurrection in a brass censer—one of them marked by a covenant she never chose, the other by a temple she barely escaped.

Hope bought on credit. The interest is paid in memories, and I am running low on collateral.

Far off, at the edge of my fading senses, I feel the Hollow Choir break camp. They are moving faster than I thought they could. The leader has learned to drink the silence directly, and it makes them strong, and strong things travel quickly.

They will reach the hollow in three days.

The humans, walking wounded and weary, will need four.

I lie back into the dark that is eating me, and I begin, with the last of my fire, to pray—the first prayer I have offered in two hundred years, addressed to no god at all, only to the small stubborn creatures on the road:

*Hurry.*
