## 15. What We Keep

The fire was Wren's idea.

She built it the way she built everything—carefully, with a shrine-keeper's economy of motion, stacking the wet driftwood they'd gathered along the saltmarsh's edge in a lattice that caught flame on the first strike of her flint. She'd learned to make fire without magic before she'd learned her letters, she told me once. Every keeper did. The gods were never obligated to answer, and a temple that couldn't warm itself was just a cold room full of disappointment.

I hadn't thought about it until now, watching her feed the flames with the patience of someone tending something alive: how much of what I knew about Wren had come to me sideways, in fragments, offered like coins dropped into a beggar's bowl rather than given face to face.

We had been traveling together for weeks. We had nearly died together twice. And I could not have told you, if you'd asked, what her mother's voice sounded like, or whether she had any siblings, or what she'd been doing the night she decided to run.

The saltmarsh stretched dark around us, breathing its slow tidal breath. Somewhere out in the reeds, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling. Above us, the sky was wrong in the way it had been wrong for days now—too many stars where there should be few, constellations shifting like restless sleepers, the old gods pressing against the thinning membrane of the world. Vaelith's ember, carried in the iron lantern at my hip, pulsed faintly with each of my heartbeats, as if the dying dragon kept time with me now instead of the other way around.

Wren sat cross-legged across the fire from me, turning a strip of dried fish over the coals with a stick. Her hair had grown out enough to tie back, and she'd done it badly, wisps escaping at her temples. There was ash ground into the creases of her knuckles that no amount of river water would lift—the ash of the censer she'd carried out of her temple, the ash that had led her to me.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous habit. It'll get you killed." She handed me the fish without ceremony and took the second one for herself. "You've been quiet since the marsh."

Since the marsh. Since I had pulled the storm down out of a clear sky and unmade half a mile of wetland to keep a slaver's net off her shoulders. Since I'd stood ankle-deep in the wreckage of it, shaking, hearing my own power in my ears like a language I'd been forbidden to speak for thirty years and discovered I was fluent in after all.

"I've been trying to decide," I said slowly, "what I'm going to lose."

The stick paused in her hands. The fire popped. Out in the dark, the tide turned, a vast soft exhalation.

"Vaelith said the price," Wren said. "A blood-guarantor anchors the new covenant with their own memory. Their own—" She stopped. Started again. "You heard what she said. You know what it costs."

"I know what it cost my grandmother to refuse to pay it alone." I turned the fish over. It was burning on one side; I didn't care. "She let the covenant rot instead. Let the valley go silent, let the dragon gutter, because she couldn't bear to give up whatever memory it was that mattered most to her. And look what her love bought us. Thirty years of nothing. A valley that forgets. A family that—" I stopped too.

"A family that forgot you," Wren finished quietly.

My grandmother had sealed Caerlum when I was nine years old. I remembered the day the magic left—not as an event but as an absence, a door closing somewhere behind the world. I remembered my grandmother's face afterward: relief and grief braided so tightly together you couldn't tell which was which. I remembered growing up in a house where no one spoke of why we lived in a place that made us small.

And I remembered, though I had spent decades pretending otherwise, that the silence had taken things from me even inside the covenant's protection. Not memories exactly. Edges of them. The way a photograph fades at the borders first.

"Wren." I set the fish down. My hands weren't steady, and I decided to let them be unsteady where she could see. "What would you hate to lose?"

* * *

She didn't answer right away. She fed another piece of driftwood to the fire and watched it catch, and I recognized the gesture for what it was—a shrine-keeper's pause, the sacred interval between question and answer that she'd been trained to honor. Some habits outlive the temples that teach them.

"When I was seven," she said at last, "there was a flood. In the river town where I was born, before the temple took me. My mother put me on the roof of our house and went back down for my brother." The words came evenly, recitation-smooth, and I understood that she had said this to herself many times, in the dark, in the same cadence. "The water took the house while she was inside. I don't remember her face anymore. I remember her hands—she pushed me up onto the tiles, and her hands were rough, and there was mud under the nails—and I remember the color of her shawl. But the face is gone. I've tried for twenty years to build it back and all I get is hands and shawl."

"Wren—"

"So that's the answer." She looked up, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "That's the one I'd hate to lose. The hands. Because they're the last true thing. Everything else I've rebuilt is just scaffolding around the hole where she used to be." A breath. "If your grandmother refused to pay the price because of one memory, one thing she couldn't give up—I understand her. That's the terrible part, Emrys. I understand her completely. And I still think she was wrong."

The honesty of it landed in my chest like a stone dropped into deep water. I thought of my grandmother in her last years, sitting by the dead hearth, sometimes speaking to people who weren't there—or who were there, once, long ago, on the other side of a silence she had built herself. I had assumed she was senile. Now I wondered if she'd been keeping company with the only version of the past she could still afford.

"My turn," I said, because if I didn't say something I was going to come apart, and I had promised myself, somewhere between the marsh and here, that I would not do that in front of her again. "You asked what I'd hate to lose. Everyone assumes it'll be the storms. The calling. That I'd sooner die than live without the power."

"Wouldn't you?"

"No." I laughed, and it came out strange. "That's the joke of it. The storms are the easy thing to lose. I lost them once already—for thirty years I was just a person, and I survived it. No. What I'd hate to lose is smaller than that." I stared into the fire. "There was a morning. I was maybe six. Before everything. My grandmother took me up onto the ridge above the valley to watch a thunderhead come in over the western hills, and she put my hand flat against a boulder and told me to feel it before the lightning came—the charge in the stone, the way the air goes metallic. And I felt it. I felt the whole sky getting ready. And she said, *that's you, little storm. That's what you are.*"

The fire cracked. Vaelith's ember pulsed against my hip, slow and warm.

"It wasn't the power she gave me that day," I said. "It was the naming. Someone looked at what I was—all of it, the dangerous, unhideable truth of me—and called it good. Called it *me*. I've spent my whole life since either hiding that or grieving it. If the price takes that morning—" My throat closed. I worked past it. "Then whoever wakes up on the other side of this covenant won't be me. They'll be someone with my hands and my name and a hole shaped like a boulder on a ridge."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Show me."

"What?"

"The ridge. The boulder. Show me the memory—all of it, however much detail you have. Right now, tonight, out loud." She leaned forward, and the firelight carved her face into planes and shadow. "Because here's what I know from the temple records, Emrys, the thing they taught us about covenants and no one ever believes until it's too late: a memory spoken aloud doesn't stay only in the speaker anymore. It lives in the listener too. Imperfectly. Secondhand. But it lives." Her voice dropped. "If you pay the price—if you give up the morning on the ridge—then I will carry what I can of it. Badly. In my own words. But I'll carry it. And someday, when you ask me why you flinch at the smell of rain on hot stone, I'll tell you a story about a boulder, and you won't know why it makes you cry, but you'll cry anyway, and it will be yours and mine both."

I stared at her. The tide breathed. Somewhere far away, thunder muttered—not mine, not anyone's, just the sky remembering itself.

"That's not how memory works," I said weakly.

"How would you know? You've lived in a valley that forgets your whole life." But she smiled when she said it, tired and crooked, and I laughed despite myself, despite everything.

So I told her. All of it—the smell of the heather on the ridge, the weight of my grandmother's hand on my shoulder, the exact gray-green of the thunderhead, the tingle in my palm against the boulder, the words themselves, *that's you, little storm*, in my grandmother's low smoky voice. I talked until the fire burned low and the fish was forgotten and my voice went hoarse. And Wren listened the way she did everything, completely, her whole attention a held breath, and when I finished she repeated it back to me in her own words, clumsily, getting some details wrong, and the wrongness made it somehow more real—as if the story, passing through her, had become a living thing instead of a preserved one.

"There," she said. "Now it's anchored twice."

* * *

We traded through the rest of the night.

Her brother's name—Tomas, drowned at four, and she said it aloud for the first time since she'd run from the temple, and her voice didn't break until the second syllable, and I said it back to her, *Tomas*, and she nodded like I'd handed her something heavy and precious.

My father's laugh—he'd left Caerlum when I was twelve, gone over the boundary into a world that could hold him, and I'd hated him for years for choosing exile over me, and only recently understood he'd chosen it because staying meant watching his child be erased by inches. His laugh was a bark, too loud, always slightly before the rest of the room found the joke funny. Wren made me demonstrate it. I did. It was awful. She laughed her own quiet laugh at it, and then she had it, and neither of us said what we were both thinking—that every memory traded was another coin banked against the fire to come.

By the time the sky began to pale in the east, we had catalogued ourselves like priests taking inventory of a reliquary. The scar on Wren's knee from falling out of a pear tree at eight. The taste of the honey-cakes sold outside my grandmother's chapel on festival days. The hymn Wren's mentor used to hum while copying scripture, the one whose melody she could no longer separate from the betrayal that came after. The name of my first storm, the one I'd called at nine years old, wild and joyous, before I knew what calling them would cost the world—Vaelith had named it for me, in the hollow, in her voice like wind through a canyon: *the green one, the laughing one.*

"We've already lost so much," Wren said finally. She was lying on her back now, watching the fading stars, the wrong constellations drifting like ships. "Before any of this. Before the covenant, before the temples, before any price was asked of us. Life just—it takes. Incrementally. A face here, a melody there. And we call it growing up and we don't even notice we're being robbed." She rolled her head toward me. "So maybe that's the real rite, isn't it? Not the fire, not the stone. This. Deciding what matters while we still can. Saying it out loud while there's still someone to hear it."

"Found family as witnesses," I murmured. It was something Vaelith had said, or almost said, in the hollow—something about how the old covenant had failed because it had only one guarantor and no witnesses, law without love. I hadn't understood it yet. Lying by a dying fire with Wren's inventory of precious things stored in my head alongside my own, I was beginning to.

The ember at my hip pulsed. Then pulsed again—faster.

I sat up. Wren sat up with me. The lantern's iron was warm through my coat, warmer than it should have been, and through the vent-slits I could see the light inside stuttering, flaring, dimming—like a candle in a draft, like a heart missing beats.

"Emrys," Wren whispered. "What's happening?"

I didn't know. But as I fumbled the lantern open, the ember's glow threw shadows against the saltmarsh grass, and the shadows were moving wrong—stretching eastward, all of them, every shadow in the firelight leaning the same direction, toward the road we'd come by, toward the low hills beyond it.

Toward the sound, faint but unmistakable, rolling across the dark water: footsteps. Many. In rhythm.

Someone was singing as they marched.
