## 17. Guttering

The hollow had become a wound in the world.

Emrys felt it before they saw it—the pressure of the Choir's approach like a storm front with no rain in it, a silence so total it pressed against the eardrums and made the teeth ache. They stood on the ridge above the dragon-hollow with Wren beside them, the relic from the lake-shrine wrapped in oilcloth against Wren's chest, and below them Vaelith's flame burned low and blue in the dark of her own ribs.

"They're not running," Wren said. Her voice was flat with disbelief. "They're *walking*."

She was right. The Hollow Choir came up the valley road in ranks, unhurried, their white-grey robes catching the last of the daylight. At their head moved a figure Emrys knew now as well as their own heartbeat's dread—Sera Ilvane, once a hedge-witch of the northern fens, once an exile like any of them, now carrying stolen silence the way other people carried knives. She walked as though the ground were grateful for her. Behind her, three hundred voices sang nothing at all, and the nothing spread ahead of them like frost across a window.

The defenders were already dying.

Vaelith's last allies—a scattering of god-touched pilgrims, two storm-priests who had answered the gods' growing noise, a band of saltmarsh folk with iron-tipped staves—held the mouth of the hollow in a thin, desperate line. Emrys watched a woman go down under three grey robes and did not see her rise. The battle made no sound where the Choir touched it. Screams were swallowed mid-throat. Steel rang once and then fell mute, as though the world had been dipped in wool.

"We should be down there," Wren said.

"We'd be dead in thirty seconds." Emrys's hands were shaking. Not with fear—or not only with fear. With the other thing, the thing that had been building in them since the saltmarsh, since they had torn a river from its banks and flattened a mile of reeds to save one person. The power sat in their chest like a caged animal that had learned the cage was made of paper. "We hold the ridge until we have to move. That's what Vaelith asked."

"She asked us to be ready," Wren said. "Not to watch."

"I know what she asked."

But neither of them moved, because moving meant descending into that soundless slaughter, and every instinct Emrys had spent forty years sharpening screamed at them to wait, to watch, to survive. Old habits. Valley habits. The habits of a person who had made invisibility into a religion and called it safety.

Below, the line broke.

It happened all at once, the way lines do—one moment a ragged wall of defiance, the next a scatter of running shapes and still ones. The Choir poured through the gap without hurrying, without triumph, filling the hollow's mouth like water finding a crack. And Sera Ilvane stopped at the threshold of the dragon-hollow itself, tilted her head back, and looked directly up at the ridge where Emrys stood.

Even at that distance, Emrys heard her voice. It arrived inside their skull rather than their ears, soft and reasonable and terrible.

*"You've come all this way to watch her die. How honest of you."*

"Emrys." Wren's hand closed on their arm. "Your eyes."

"What about my eyes?"

"They're doing the storm thing."

Emrys dragged in a breath. Grounded themselves the way they'd taught themselves decades ago: name five things you can see. Ridge-grass. Oilcloth. Wren's chapped knuckles. The blue glow rising from the hollow. The sky going the wrong color overhead, green-black and bruised, clouds gathering that Emrys had not called but could no longer entirely disown.

"Stay behind me when it starts," they said.

"When what starts?"

"Everything."

* * *

They went down into the hollow together anyway, because watching was worse than dying, and because Vaelith was calling them—not in words anymore, her voice too thin for words, but in pulses of heat and memory that washed over Emrys like waves over a breakwater. *Come. Come quickly, little storm. I am almost out of time, and time is the only thing I ever owned.*

Inside, the hollow opened into the vast chamber where the dragon lay. Emrys had seen her twice before and both times had needed a long moment to understand what they were seeing: not a body, exactly, not anymore. A shape remembered by the air. Scales that were sometimes scale and sometimes the idea of scale, translucent as old glass. Wings folded around a chest cavity where a flame burned—the last dragon-fire in either realm—and even now, even knowing what they knew, some animal part of Emrys wanted to fall on their knees before it.

The flame was guttering.

That was the only word for it. It rose and shrank and rose again, each cycle smaller than the last, and where its light failed, Vaelith's body failed with it. A section of her flank simply wasn't there for a moment—just hollow air shaped like absence—and then the light surged back and she was solid again, gasping, if dragons could be said to gasp.

"You brought it," she said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls, from Emrys's own bones. "The relic. Good. Good. There may yet be—"

"There will be," Emrys said. "There has to be."

"Careful, little storm. *Has to* is the language of gods, and look what it made of them."

Then the light changed, and Emrys turned, and Sera Ilvane was standing in the entrance of the chamber.

She had come alone. That was somehow worse than an army. Three hundred of her followers waited beyond the threshold in perfect silence, and she stepped into the firelight with her hands folded like a woman entering a chapel, and Emrys saw what the stolen silence had made of her. Her shadow didn't move when she did. Her outline kept smearing slightly, as though the world couldn't quite decide where she ended. Around her feet, the ground was grey—not dusty, not ash, just *drained*, colorless, a circle of stone that had forgotten it was anything.

"Emrys Marchetti," she said aloud this time, and her voice was kind, and that was the worst thing yet. "Grandchild of Alba Marchetti. Guarantor's blood. Do you know how long I've looked for you? Do you know how many of us prayed to the silence for someone like you?"

"I'm not looking for converts."

"No. You're looking for a miracle." She gestured gently at Vaelith's failing flame. "And there it is, guttering out while you stand here planning some desperate rite. Let me tell you what I see, Emrys. I see a creature that traded the world's wholeness for its own survival. I see a covenant written in a dragon's blood that stole the gods from every praying child in every village for three generations. I see your grandmother's name carved on a stone that bought peace with amputation." Her head tilted. "I'm not your enemy. I'm the only person here telling you the truth."

"The truth," Wren said sharply, stepping forward, "is that you're wearing stolen silence like a crown, and everything it touches dies."

"Everything it touches goes *quiet*," Ilvane corrected. "There's a difference. You wouldn't know. You kept shrines. You fed the machine." Her gaze slid to Wren, and something cold passed through it. "I know your temple, little keeper. I know what they do to children who ask questions."

"Don't," Wren said, very quietly. "Don't you dare speak about them."

"See? Even your anger is borrowed from them."

Emrys moved then—stepped between them, arms half-raised, feeling the storm press up against the inside of their skin like floodwater against a door. "Whatever you came to say, say it. The flame doesn't have long."

Ilvane smiled. It transformed her face into something almost gentle, almost wounded, and Emrys understood suddenly why three hundred exiles had followed her into the dark. She looked like forgiveness. She looked like the end of loneliness.

"I came to offer you what I offered before," she said. "Belonging. Real belonging, not the rite you're planning, not a covenant patched with your memories and held together with prayer. Come with me. Help me keep the realms apart forever—permanently, cleanly, no more guttering flame, no more gods bleeding through into dreams and driving farmers mad. We'll build the silence into law. No more exile for people like us, because there'll be nothing left to be exiled *from*. A world where magic cannot follow anyone anywhere." She extended her hand. "A home that holds everyone. Isn't that what you want? Isn't that what you've wanted your whole life?"

And the horror of it—the true horror, the one Emrys would carry even after losing pieces of themselves to the new covenant—was that for one full breath, they wanted to say yes.

One breath of a world with no storms to lose control of. No power cracking their bones open. No gods whispering in dreams. Just a valley that went on forever, quiet and safe and gray, and hands to hold, and no one ever lost to something vast again.

Then they heard Vaelith's flame stutter, and heard—in the stutter—a sound like a mother trying to sing through a closed throat, and the breath passed.

"No," Emrys said. "You're offering a tomb and calling it a house."

Ilvane's hand lowered slowly. The gentleness drained out of her face the way color drains from a bruise, and what remained underneath was older and tireder and sadder than Emrys had expected.

"So be it," she said. "I did try."

She reached into her robes and drew out the covenant-stone.

Emrys's whole body went cold. It was impossible—it should have been buried under their grandmother's hearth, half a valley away—but of course it wasn't really stone at all, not anymore, not since the night Emrys had pulled it into the light and spoken their name over it. Since then it had traveled. It lived partly in Emrys's pocket, partly in their memory, partly in the space between realms, and Ilvane's stolen silence had simply *reached* into that space and taken hold.

It was small in her palm. A grey oval shot through with veins of gold, the Marchetti name still legible across its face. Three generations of silence. One family's whole inheritance.

"This belonged to your grandmother," Ilvane said. "And her mother before her. And it ends tonight—one way or another. Either the flame dies and the covenant fails on its own, or—" she turned the stone over, examining it like a jeweler, "—we stop pretending the old world can be saved."

"*Don't*—" Vaelith's voice cracked across the chamber like a whip, and for one instant the flame flared bright and furious and the dragon's ghost-body blazed into terrible solidity, wings unfurling, eyes like twin eclipses. "*Mortal, you do not know what you hold—*"

"I know exactly what I hold." Ilvane's voice didn't waver. "I've held silence my whole life. This is just more of it."

She raised the covenant-stone above her head.

Time did something strange then. Emrys would remember it afterward in fragments, the way you remember lightning: the stone arcing downward. Wren lunging and being too slow. Emrys reaching for the storm with everything they had and feeling it answer—feeling it come *roaring* up through them, too much, far too much, a lifetime of dammed weather breaking loose all at once—

And the stone shattered on the chamber floor.

* * *

The silence broke first.

Three hundred throats found their voices simultaneously, and the sound they made was not a scream but a *release*, centuries of swallowed prayer erupting out of the Choir like water from a burst dam. People fell to their knees. People laughed. People wept and tore at their robes and called out names of gods they hadn't been allowed to name, and the names *answered*.

Because the covenant was broken, and the seal between the realms tore open along every seam at once.

Emrys felt it happen inside their own chest. The wall that had stood between the mortal world and the divine for three generations—that their grandmother had maintained, that her mother had maintained, that had cost the last dragon her fire—came apart like rotted rope, and through the gap poured *everything*. Raw divinity, undiluted, unshaped by covenant or courtesy. It hit the hollow like a tidal wave hits a sandcastle.

The chamber walls rippled. Stone flowed like water and hardened into shapes that hurt to look at—faces, mostly, enormous and serene and *awake*. Grass sprang up through the floor and bloomed and withered and bloomed again in seconds. Somewhere above, the sky split along a seam of gold, and through it came sounds: bells, birdsong, laughter, weeping, a thousand prayers being answered at once by powers that had gone decades without practice and had forgotten gentleness.

In the villages beyond the valley, Emrys knew without knowing how, wells were speaking. Crops were growing wild. Statues were turning their heads. Every minor god that had ever been squeezed out of the world was pouring back through the breach, and they were not coming gently, because no one had taught them in eighty years what mortal fragility required.

And Vaelith—

Vaelith was screaming.

The broken covenant had been holding her together as much as it had held the realms apart; her sacrifice was the covenant's keystone, and with the stone shattered, the memory that was all she was began to come apart at the seams. Her body flickered wildly—dragon, ember, girl, dragon, smoke—each shape lasting less than a heartbeat. The flame in her chest guttered down to a spark no bigger than a candle-flame, and the darkness rushed in around it like a tide.

"*Emrys.*" Her voice was barely there now, fraying with every word. "*The rite. Now. While there is still—I cannot—there is so little—*"

"It's too soon!" Wren had the relic unwrapped, clutching it, tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face. "We're not ready, the alignment isn't until dawn, we don't have the second focus, we don't—"

"*Now,* little keeper. Or never. Choose faster than fear."

Emrys stood in the middle of the ruin of everything—the shattered stone, the screaming heavens, the dying dragon, the Choir on their knees in the wreckage of their own silence—and felt rock bottom open beneath them like a second hollow. Their grandmother's inheritance: rubble. The old covenant: rubble. Eighty years of careful, costly, imperfect peace, and every soul who had suffered under it and every soul it had protected, all of it broken open at once by a woman who had only ever wanted the hurting to stop.

There was nothing left to lose. That was the terrible gift of it. Rock bottom, Emrys thought dizzily, is the one place where you can finally push off.

"Wren." They turned, and took her face in both hands, ash and tears and the golden light of the torn sky reflected in her eyes. "Whatever happens in the next few minutes—whatever I forget, whatever I have to give up—you were the best thing that ever happened to me. I need you to know that *before*, not after."

Wren's breath caught. Then she set her jaw in the way Emrys had learned meant she had decided something no one would talk her out of, and pressed the relic into their hands.

"Then let's write something worth remembering," she said.

Behind them, Vaelith's flame sank to a single blue point in the dark—and held.

Barely.

For now.
