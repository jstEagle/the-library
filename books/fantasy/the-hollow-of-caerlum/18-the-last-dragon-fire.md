## 18. The Last Dragon-Fire

The covenant-stone lay in pieces at the Hollow Choir's feet, and the world was screaming.

Not with sound—with *presence*. Every prayer that had gone unanswered for a hundred years was answering at once, all across the valley's rim and beyond it, a tide of voices pouring through the wound the leader had torn in the fabric between realms. I could feel them the way I felt storms before my grandmother taught me to be quiet: pressure, static, the taste of copper on the tongue of the sky.

I knelt in the ash of the hollow with Wren's hand locked around my wrist, and I understood, finally and completely, what my grandmother had known when she buried that stone beneath her hearth.

Someone has to pay. Someone always pays.

"Emrys." Vaelith's voice came from everywhere now—smoke, ember, the dying heat pressed into the walls of her own hollow. Her body was less than a suggestion: a vast outline of ribs rendered in drifting grey, a skull like weathered limestone through which a single point of light still pulsed. Dimming. "The stone is broken. The old anchor is dead. What comes through that breach will not be gods as your prayers imagined them. It will be hunger wearing their names."

Above us, the sky was opening. Not clouds—a seam, luminous and wrong, widening over the shattered hollow like an eye being forced awake. Through it came shapes too large to look at directly, presences pressing down against the thin place where the silence used to be, testing it, tasting the absence left behind by a century of enforced quiet.

The Choir's leader stood amid her people on the ridge above, arms raised, face lit with ecstasy. She had wanted this—or some version of this. Freedom for the exiled mages, an end to the silence that had made them pariahs. She had not understood that silence was not a cage. It was a dam.

And we were standing in the floodplain.

"There's another way," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, small against the roar of arriving divinity. "Vaelith. The rite. Tell me again what it needs."

"You know what it needs." The dragon's light guttered. "A guarantor of Marchetti blood, willing to bind themselves into the covenant's heart. Your grandmother refused to pay it alone, so she paid nothing—and bought a century for the world at the cost of everything after. You are not obligated to be braver than she was, child."

"I'm not being brave." I got to my feet. My knees were shaking. I let them. "I'm being *specific*. Tell me exactly what the anchor gives."

Wren's grip tightened on my wrist. "Emrys, no. Whatever you're about to—"

"A memory," Vaelith said softly. "One memory, freely surrendered, for every strand of the new binding. The old covenant demanded one thing: silence. Absolute. A whole self erased to hold two realms apart. But you said—" and there was something almost wondering in the vast ruined voice— "*witness*. Not silence. If you build it that way, the price changes shape. It will not take all of you. It will take only what you choose to feed it, thread by thread, until the weaving holds."

"And if I run out of threads?"

"Then it takes what remains."

* * *

I turned to Wren.

They were kneeling in the ash, censer still cradled against their chest—the little bronze vessel they'd carried out of a burning temple half a world away, packed with dragon-fire ash that should have gone cold decades ago and hadn't. Their face was streaked with soot and blood from the fighting, from holding the hollow's eastern arch while the Choir broke against it, and their eyes were wide and furious and terrified, and I loved them with a completeness that frightened me more than the sky did.

"Don't," Wren said. "Don't do that math. Don't stand there deciding which parts of yourself you can live without, because I've watched people do that math and it always ends with them giving away something they didn't know mattered until it was gone."

"Wren—"

"The relic." They thrust the censer toward me, and I saw their hands were trembling. "The flame goes through this—it was made to carry fire, that's what shrine-keepers are *for*, we're the vessels, we're the ones who hold the sacred so ordinary hands don't have to touch it. Give me the flame. Give me the burning part. You don't have to give it anything else."

"The flame is dying." Vaelith's light pulsed once, weakly, agreement or apology—I couldn't tell. "It cannot cross without an anchor. Fire needs a hearth, little keeper. That is what a covenant is. That is all any covenant has ever been—a hearth, and someone willing to sit beside it forever."

The sky tore wider. Through the seam, something spoke—not in words but in *pressure*, a name spoken so loudly that the stones around us sang with it, and half the surviving defenders on the western wall dropped to their knees clutching their ears. One of the Hollow Choir began to laugh, high and unhinged, pointing up at the light.

I looked at Wren, and I thought about Chapter One of my life—the one written by a grandmother who never explained herself, who died with a secret under her hearth and left me a valley instead of a home. I thought about every year I'd spent invisible, storm-calling smothered in my chest like a coal wrapped in wet cloth, safe and numb and *less*. I thought about the road, and the saltmarsh, and a hundred small evenings where Wren had hummed off-key over the campfire and I had thought, *this, keep this, whatever happens, keep this.*

"I know what to give," I said quietly. "That's how I know it'll work."

"Emrys—"

"Vaelith said the covenant is witness. So it needs witnessed things. Real things. Things seen and kept." I took the censer from Wren's hands. The ash inside was glowing brighter now, responding to the breach, to the nearness of its dying source. "It doesn't need me emptied. It needs me *authored*. And an author chooses what goes in the book."

"That is the correct understanding," Vaelith murmured, and there was grief in it, and pride, and something older than both. "Few who stand where you're standing ever understand it correctly. They think sacrifice means giving everything. It doesn't. Sacrifice means knowing exactly what you can spare and offering it *anyway*, because the offer itself is the binding. Willingness is the mortar, child. Always has been."

I knelt in the ash beside the last fragment of the covenant-stone—the piece with my family's name carved into it, MARCHETTI, worn nearly smooth by a hundred years of buried dark. I set the censer on the flat face of the stone.

"How does it start?" I asked.

"Speak the memory," Vaelith said. "Give it words. Witnessed things must be *spoken* to be given. Speak it, and let go, and I will carry it into the fire."

* * *

The first one was easy, because it was supposed to be easy. That's what no one tells you about sacrifice—that it begins gently, like a kindness, like a door held open.

"My seventh winter," I said. "The ice on the millpond. Grandmother teaching me to skate, and falling, and laughing—she *laughed*, she almost never laughed, and the sound of it echoed off the hills, and I thought the whole valley had stopped to listen."

I spoke it fully—the cold, the ache in my palms, the way her breath had fogged silver in the air. Then I opened my hands, metaphorically and otherwise, and let it go.

It left like warmth leaving a cup. No pain. Just a softening, a place in me going smooth and featureless, like snow filling a footprint. I knew I had lost something. I could no longer say what.

The ash in the censer flared. A single tongue of true flame rose from it, red-gold, impossibly alive, and curled upward toward the tearing sky like a finger pointed in accusation—or greeting.

"One thread laid," Vaelith breathed. "Continue."

"The bitter year." This one cost more. "The year after she died. The year I hated her, and hated the valley, and sat alone in her house with her secrets pressing down on me, and swore I would leave and never did. The loneliness of it. The specific flavor of it—how it tasted like unwashed dishes and closed shutters."

Gone. A whole year of my interior life, smoothed to blank. I felt its absence the way you feel a missing tooth—continuously, a small wrongness where weight used to be. But underneath the wrongness, something else was happening: the flame in the censer grew taller, steadier, and the seam in the sky stopped widening. Held. The hungry shapes beyond it pressed, and the flame pressed back, and the boundary between them was no longer a dam straining to break.

It was becoming a *window*. Lit from both sides.

"Two threads," Vaelith said. "The weave holds. Emrys—the realms are listening. Both of them. Whatever else you give, give it *loudly*. This is not a burial. This is a reading aloud."

So I read aloud.

"My mother's face." I didn't have much of her—a few years, a few impressions—but I gave all of it. Gone. The flame burned higher.

"The smell of rain on Caerlum stone in summer, the particular green-dark smell of it, the way I used to stand in the doorway just breathing." Gone. Higher.

"The name of my childhood friend—the boy from the east farms whose name I've carried for forty years like a smooth stone in my pocket." Gone. And with it went the boy himself, his face dissolving even as I reached for it, and I grieved him in the same breath that I fed him to the fire, and the fire *accepted* the grief too, took the mourning and wove it in, and I understood then that the covenant would take anything witnessed honestly—even the loss itself became part of the record.

Higher. Brighter. The flame stood now a full arm's length tall above the censer, defying every law of ash and fuel, and through it I could see—*through* it—the other side. Not the hungry shapes. Beyond them. Fields of impossible color, mountains that moved like sleeping animals, a god's silhouette watching from very far away, hands folded, patient. The realm that had been sealed away from mine since before my grandmother was born, looking back at me through the window I was building with the rooms of my own life.

"They see you," Vaelith whispered. "Emrys. They *see* you. Do you understand what you're making? Not a wall. Not a door that locks. A pane of glass, and both realms keeping watch through it, each responsible to the other, each *known* to the other. No one has built such a thing since the First Age. The gods warred rather than risk it. And you're building it out of skating ponds and grief and the smell of summer rain."

"I'm building it out of what I can spare," I said. My voice was fraying. So many rooms empty now. I was becoming a house with open windows and fewer furnishings every minute, and still I searched myself, room by room, asking the terrible question: *what else?*

And then Wren was beside me. On their knees in the ash, close enough that our shoulders touched, and they put their hand flat on the covenant-stone fragment next to mine.

"Take some of mine," Wren said.

"You can't—"

"It's not a Marchetti covenant anymore, is it?" Their eyes were fierce, wet, absolutely certain. "You said witness. Well, I witnessed you. Every mile. That makes me party to this. Shrine-keepers are *vessels*—it's the whole point of us, Emrys, we exist to hold what's sacred so it can survive the crossing. Let me hold some of you. Let me be a thread."

I wanted to refuse. I want to tell you I considered refusing nobly. But the truth is simpler and worse and better: I looked at them—at this runaway who'd fled a temple that used them and a god that lied to them, who'd crossed the valley line with a stolen censer and a stolen name and rebuilt themselves from scratch in the space of one impossible season—and I understood that refusing their gift would be the same sin my grandmother committed. Deciding alone. Carrying alone. Sealing others out of the covenant of my own life.

So I nodded.

Wren spoke their offering aloud: "The night on the road when he fell asleep mid-sentence talking about cloud formations, sitting straight up against a fencepost, mouth open, and I sat there for an hour just watching the stars come out over his stupid sleeping head." Spoken. Released. I felt the memory leave them and enter the weave—I felt it *from the other side now*, felt it arrive in the covenant like a lamp being lit in a window, and the flame leapt so bright that the Hollow Choir on the ridge cried out and shielded their eyes.

More. Wren gave the taste of the bread we'd shared in the village of Small Prayers; the sound of my laughter, which they claimed sounded like a door hinge but which they'd clearly grown fond of anyway; the moment in the saltmarsh when I'd leveled the marsh to save them and they'd looked at me afterward not with fear but with *recognition*—that one burned going in, and the flame turned briefly white, and somewhere overhead one of the hungry shapes recoiled from the purity of it.

We gave and gave. Childhoods, grievances, small joys, the texture of particular days. The covenant drank them down and grew, and the window in the sky widened—not dangerously now, but *deliberately*, its edges smoothing into a clean round frame, and through it both realms regarded one another across the flame-lit glass, and I felt the moment the far side understood what was being offered. Felt the shift in the pressure. Hunger becoming attention. Attention becoming—cautiously, immortally—*reciprocation*.

On the ridge, the Choir's leader screamed something. I heard it distantly, the word "betrayal" maybe, or "lies"—it didn't matter. Her stolen silence-magic lashed down at the window and broke against it like a wave on harbor-stones, because you cannot shatter a covenant made of freely given witness. There is no force in either realm that can unmake a gift already given. She had spent her whole life learning how to break things that were built to hold by force.

She had never learned what to do with something built to hold by love.

"The final thread," Vaelith said, and her voice was very faint now, and very full. "The keystone. One more, Emrys. The one you saved for last. I can feel it waiting behind your teeth."

I knew the one. I'd known from the beginning. It was why I'd been able to stand up in the ash and do the math.

"The night Wren arrived in Caerlum," I said, "with ash still glowing in a censer, and looked at me like I was a question they'd been carrying across the world. The exact moment I stopped being invisible. The moment someone *saw* me—my bloodline mark, my history, my inheritance—and stayed anyway. Stayed knowing what I was, what my family had done, what it might cost them. The first moment of my life that I belonged somewhere on purpose."

I spoke every detail of it. The wind. The lantern-light. The exact angle of Wren's wary, hopeful face.

And I let it go.

* * *

The flame went up like a sunrise.

It rose out of the censer in a column that touched the window-frame in the sky and spread along its edges, sealing them, and where it passed, the glass became something better than glass—a living membrane, warm on both sides, through which each realm could see and be seen and be *answerable*. The hungry shapes withdrew, not banished but *invited to approach properly*, to knock, to introduce themselves, to be guests rather than floods. Somewhere very far away, a god folded its hands and bowed.

And Vaelith—

Oh, Vaelith.

The flame poured back down into the hollow, into the grey ruin of her, and for one blazing moment the dragon stood whole again in the eye of my mind: wings like storm-fronts, scales like a sky full of embers, vast and ancient and *grateful*. Her voice filled the valley one last time at full strength, and it was not sorrow anymore.

"*Witnessed,*" she thundered, to both realms at once. "*Sealed in mutual sight. Kept not by silence but by recognition, anchored in the freely given memories of Emrys Marchetti and Wren of the Broken Shrine, and renewed for as long as anyone on either side chooses to look through the glass and remember what they saw.* So witnessed. SO SEALED."

The light collapsed inward, gentle as a closing book, and the sky healed over into honest evening—the first clean sunset I had seen since the stone broke, gold and violet and utterly ordinary, utterly miraculous.

In the ash where Vaelith had lain, something small glowed.

I crawled toward it on hands that no longer worked quite right, past Wren, who was crying and laughing and doing both into my shoulder, and I found it: a single ember, red-gold, pulsing slowly. Breathing.

An egg. No bigger than my fist, warm as a hearthstone, and faintly—so faintly I might have imagined it—I heard a voice like smoke settling:

*Clever child. You gave away your bitter year. I felt it go. I will keep it for you, if you like... in case you ever want it back.*

I laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt. Whole rooms of me were empty, smooth-walled, unfurnished, and I could not have said what had stood in them, only that the house felt lighter and stranger and somehow more *mine* than it ever had when it was full.

Wren pulled me upright. Around us, the hollow was silent—truly silent, the good kind, the kind that follows a held breath—and on the ridge, the Hollow Choir stood motionless among their fallen, staring at the healed sky, at the ember-egg in my hands, at the two of us kneeling in ash with soot-black faces and nothing left to give except each other.

Their leader's arms had lowered. Her face was doing something complicated. Behind her, her people were beginning—one by one—to kneel.

And the egg in my palms pulsed again, twice, quick as a heartbeat, quick as a knock at a door—

—and cracked.
