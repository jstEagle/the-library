## 20. The Memory I Chose to Lose

Emrys woke to the smell of rain on warm stone and did not know, for three long breaths, where they were.

Not Caerlum. The air was wrong for Caerlum—too wide, too open, carrying salt and smoke and something older underneath, a mineral sweetness like lightning that had struck the same place twice. They were lying on a slope of granite above the dragon-hollow, wrapped in someone's cloak, and the sky above them was the pale washed blue of morning after a storm that had emptied itself completely.

They remembered the storm. That was the first mercy: they remembered it whole. The Choir's leader shattering the old covenant-stone, the raw chaos pouring through the crack like water through a dam's failure, Wren screaming their name across the burning hollow, Vaelith's flame guttering down to a coal no bigger than a thumbnail. And then the choice—the terrible arithmetic of it, laying out their memories like coins on a table and deciding which ones they could live without.

Emrys sat up slowly, the way you rise from a sickbed, testing each part of yourself before trusting it.

Their hands were theirs. Their name was theirs—*Emrys Marchetti*, and even the surname carried weight now instead of shame, because it was written in fire on a new covenant, witnessed by a runaway shrine-keeper and a dragon made of memory. Their mother's voice was theirs; they could hear it still, humming off-key in a kitchen that smelled of cardamom. Their grandmother's face—

Emrys reached for their grandmother's face and found a smooth place where it should have been.

It was not emptiness, exactly. It was like touching a wall where a door used to be: they knew there had been a door, knew its shape from the frame left behind, but the door itself was gone, planed away, sanded into the grain of them. They knew a woman had raised them in the valley of Caerlum. They knew she had loved them badly and well, in the way of people who carry secrets too heavy to set down. They knew her hands had buried a covenant-stone beneath the hearth and her silence had cost a realm its gods. But her face—her particular face, the one they had looked at across sixty years of breakfasts and silences—that was ash now. They had fed it to the fire along with a bitter year they could no longer name, some season when everything had gone wrong in ways that had once seemed permanent.

They tried to grieve and found the grief strangely clean. That was the trick of it, they realized. The price had been paid not in pain but in *particularity*. What remained was true but general: love without a face, forgiveness without a date. They had chosen which memories to lose, and the choosing had been done by someone who understood, better than anyone alive, what a self was made of—and what it could spare.

"You're awake."

Wren came up the slope with a clay cup steaming in both hands, hair loose, soot still shadowed in the creases of her knuckles. She moved carefully, the way people do around the recently resurrected, as though loud noises might undo them.

"How long?"

"Two days." She crouched, offered the cup. Willow-bark and honey. "You slept like the dead. I checked your breathing more times than I'll admit."

Emrys drank. The warmth spread down through their chest and pooled somewhere behind the sternum, where a new thing lived now—a small steady pressure, like a second heartbeat that wasn't quite a heartbeat. They pressed their palm flat against it.

"It worked," they said. Not a question. They could feel it working, feel the covenant humming in the world the way you feel a floor under your feet. Not silence anymore. *Witness.* The realms bound in mutual recognition, each one able to see the other without either being devoured. Somewhere beyond the valley's old borders, the gods were stirring—but stirring like sleepers in an adjacent room, not prisoners breaking down a door.

"It worked," Wren said, and laughed—a cracked, exhausted sound, half disbelief. "You should see it. The hollow. The grass is coming back green where it burned, and there are—" She stopped herself, eyes going bright. "There's something you need to see first. Or someone."

She reached into the front of her shirt and brought out a fold of cloth, unwrapping it with the tenderness of a priest handling relics.

The ember-wyrm was no longer than Emrys's forearm. Its body was a coil of dark scales shot through with veins of light, orange and gold, pulsing slowly in rhythm with—Emrys's hand flew again to their own chest. In rhythm with the new covenant. With them. Its wings were translucent membranes, still wet-looking, folded tight against its back like a fledgling's. When it lifted its head, its eyes were ancient and new at once: the deep amber of banked coals, holding a patience that no creature hours old should possess.

"Vaelith," Emrys breathed.

"She wouldn't say it at first," Wren said. "Wouldn't say anything. Just kept looking at you while you slept. But last night—" Her voice caught. "Last night she spoke. One word. She said, *'Little guarantor.'*" A wet laugh escaped her. "I think it's affectionate. I think. Dragon humor is hard to calibrate."

The ember-wyrm uncoiled from the cloth with grave deliberation, stretched wings that would never again blot out a sky, and walked—walked, on tiny taloned feet, with the unhurried dignity of a queen in exile—across the blanket and up onto Emrys's knee. The weight of her was almost nothing. The warmth of her was enormous.

"I remember you," Emrys said softly. "All of you. The mountain of you. The first flight over the war-fields. The day you gave your fire to seal the covenant because you loved the world too much to watch it tear itself apart." Their throat tightened. "I'm sorry. For what we asked of you. For what my family let you carry alone."

The wyrm regarded them for a long moment. Then she pressed her small hot brow against Emrys's wrist, and into their mind—not words, not yet, or not many—came a feeling like sunlight falling through the doorway of a house where everyone has come home safe.

*I chose too,* the feeling said. *We are a company of choosers now.*

Emrys wept, briefly and without shame, and Wren sat down beside them in the wet grass and put her arm through theirs, and the little dragon warmed them both like a hearth with opinions.

* * *

The days that followed were strange and gentle, and Emrys learned to live inside their own altered head the way you learn a house after the furniture has been rearranged.

The missing pieces announced themselves by their absences. A childhood face, gone—the grandmother-shaped hole that ached only when Emrys reached for it deliberately, like probing a tooth socket with the tongue. A bitter year, gone—they had chosen it from the table of their life almost greedily, that year whose name they'd whispered to the fire with the last of their courage, and now they could not even miss it properly, because missing requires knowing what you lost. There were smaller losses too, discovered by accident: the name of a village they'd once passed through, the taste of a specific meal, the melody of a song they could hum but not source. Gaps like missing teeth in a smile. You learned to talk around them.

But what remained—what *remained*—was staggering in its abundance. Every storm they had ever called, every night under their grandmother's roof (faceless woman, beloved stranger, they mourned her anew each morning), every moment of Wren: the first sight of her in the market square clutching a glowing censer like a stolen sun, her voice cracking as she read temple records aloud, her hands steady on the relic beneath the lake while everything else fell apart. The campfire night when they had traded their most precious memories like children trading treasures, and Emrys had learned that Wren kept a list of every prayer she'd ever failed to answer, and that she recited it not in bitterness but as a promise. All of it intact. All of it *theirs*, guarded jealously through the fire.

"You look like a man counting his money," Wren observed on the fourth morning, finding Emrys sitting cross-legged outside the rebuilt shrine-house, eyes closed, inventorying themselves.

"I am." Emrys opened their eyes. "I keep expecting to find something else gone. Instead I keep finding things I forgot I had. Did you know I can still recite the entire litany of the western winds? I haven't thought about it in thirty years and it's all just—*there*. Sitting in my head like a tenant who never missed rent."

Wren dropped down beside them. She was thinner than when they'd met, harder in some places and softer in others, and there was a new scar along her jaw from the battle that she touched absently, the way you touch a line of writing to make sure it's real. Around her neck, on a cord, hung the relic—the shard of the first altar, dull now, its work done, kept anyway because some things earn the right to be carried.

"The Choir survivors are asking about you," she said. "The ones who broke when the leader did. They want to know if the new covenant has room for people who marched against it."

"What do you tell them?"

"That it's not mine to answer alone." She glanced sideways. "That's rather the point of it, isn't it? Witness. Mutual recognition. Even of the ones who got it wrong." She paused. "Most of them are just tired, Emrys. Tired people follow loud voices. It doesn't make them monsters."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It makes them us, on our worst days." They thought of the Hollow Choir's commune, the seductive warmth of belonging offered at the price of abandoning Wren, and how close they had come—how *humanly* close—to saying yes. "We'll hear them. All of them. That doesn't mean trusting all of them. But hearing comes first. That's the covenant."

From the eaves of the shrine-house, the ember-wyrm chirped—a sound like a kettle agreeing—and glided down to land on Wren's shoulder, where she had taken to perching with proprietary regularity. Wren had named her, over Emrys's mild objections, *Ashcake*. Vaelith tolerated the indignity with the weary grace of the very old among the very young.

"She wants food," Wren translated. "Charcoal, specifically. Burnt, specifically. She's developed opinions."

"She's always had opinions. She once held a grudge against a mountain range for four hundred years."

"Ashcake holds grudges against the cookfire daily. It's very on-brand."

And Emrys laughed—really laughed, from the belly, the sound startling birds out of the shrine-house eaves—and thought: *this is what I bought with a face and a year. This. This exact stupid joy.*

* * *

On the sixth day, Emrys climbed to the crest above the hollow alone, because there was a thing they needed to do and some things cannot be witnessed, even by those who love you.

They built a small cairn of stones at the summit, in the old style, the way travelers mark a grave or a threshold. Then they sat beside it and spoke aloud to the grandmother they could no longer see.

"I don't know your face anymore," they said. The wind took the words east, toward Caerlum, toward the valley that had forgotten them so they could forget nothing. "I chose to give it up. Your face, and one bad year—I still don't know which year, which I suppose was the point. The fire needed fuel and I decided what to burn, and I chose the parts of me that were keeping me small." They set a flat stone atop the cairn. "You sealed the realms apart to keep them safe, and you paid for it with silence, and you made me inherit the silence without telling me the price. I used to think that was betrayal. Maybe it was. But I understand it differently now. You were trying to protect the world with the only tool you had, and you were afraid, and fear makes us hoarders. I was afraid too, right up until the moment I opened my hands."

The wind moved through the grass in a long silver wave.

"So this is what I've built on top of what you buried," Emrys went on. "A covenant of witness instead of silence. Doors instead of walls. I kept your name in the stone—Marchetti, guarantor. Third generation now. I hope wherever you are, whatever's left of you, you can feel the difference. I hope it feels less like a chain and more like a hand."

They stayed until the sun cleared the ridge and the light came down the slope like something poured. Then they rose, brushed off their knees, and turned back toward the hollow—and stopped.

Because the ember-wyrm was there, sitting on the cairn's topmost stone, having followed them up in silence. Ashcake. Vaelith. Both and neither. She cocked her head at Emrys with one ancient amber eye, and into their mind drifted an image, delicate as smoke: a kitchen. Cardamom. A woman humming off-key. A child at the table learning the names of winds.

Emrys went very still. "You can't—you weren't there. That was mine."

The image shifted, deepened, gained a detail Emrys had lost: the woman's face, seen from the child's low angle, laughing at something the child had said. Laughing, and young-seeming, and *known*—known the way you know a face you have loved across decades.

*I am memory,* the wyrm said simply, in the wordless way she said things. *What is given to fire is not destroyed. It is changed. I held yours while you slept, little guarantor, so that the covenant would have something kind to build with. I do not have to give it back. But I am allowed to show you the shape of what you paid.*

Emrys sank down onto the cold grass. The face hovered in their mind's eye, bright and impossible, already beginning to fade at the edges like breath off glass. They memorized it frantically—the laugh-lines, the crooked tooth, the way her eyes crinkled—and knew even as they did that this copy would soften and blur with time, a painting of a photograph of a person. But it was more than they'd woken with. It was a gift from a creature who understood, better than any god, what memories are worth and what they cost.

"Thank you," Emrys whispered.

The wyrm flowed down from the cairn and into their arms, warm as a loaf fresh from the oven, and for a while neither of them said anything at all, and the wind sang over the hollow where a dragon had died and a dragon had been born and the world had been rewitnessed into something gentler.

When Emrys finally stood, the wyrm curled into their breast pocket—her preferred traveling arrangement now, an ember-wyrm-sized kingdom of wool and body heat—and Emrys started down the hill toward the smoke of the morning cookfire, toward Wren's voice calling their name, toward the road.

Toward home. Because that was the next thing, wasn't it? The valley. Caerlum, which had forgotten them, which magic could not enter, which had been a sanctuary and a prison and a covenant's cornerstone. Except the covenant was new now, and the borders were doors, and Emrys did not know—could not know—what they would find when they crossed back over the line their grandmother had drawn in blood and silence.

They only knew that the road bent east, and that Wren was waiting with two cups of willow-bark tea, and that somewhere ahead a valley waited to learn whether it remembered them.

"Ready?" Wren called up the slope.

Emrys touched the pocket where the small fire slept, felt the new covenant hum beneath their ribs like a second heart, and answered, "Almost. Ask me again at the border."
