## 21. Homecoming, Unhidden

**Emrys**

The valley appeared on the third morning the way a face appears in fog—slowly, and then all at once.

Emrys had been bracing for it since the river. Every mile of road behind them had carried its own small miracle: wheat standing in fields that should have drowned, a chapel bell ringing without hands, a well that sang when you leaned over it. The world outside Caerlum was awake now, loud with the new covenant's pulse, and every mile had also carried the same unspoken question between them: *what will home do when we return?*

Wren walked ahead on the ridge road, the ember-wyrm riding her shoulder like a brooch made of living coal. Vaelith—small now, no larger than a cat, her scales still more memory than matter—had taken to the girl with an ease that Emrys tried not to find unfair. She had known them both as legends once. Apparently legends were easier to love at this size.

"You're dragging your feet," Wren said, not turning around.

"I'm savoring."

"You're dragging your feet."

They were right. Emrys could feel it in their own knees, in the way each step toward the valley rim grew heavier than the last. For forty years, Caerlum had been the shape of their hiding. Its silence had been their mother tongue; its forgetting, their armor. They had crossed out of it carrying a covenant-stone and a dead woman's name, and they were crossing back into it carrying something far more dangerous: themselves, whole, remembered, *known*.

The last rise came up beneath their boots, and there it was.

Caerlum lay cupped in its hills like water in a hand—the terraced barley, the slate roofs going gold with morning, the shrine at the valley's heart where Emrys had whispered their first answered prayer into forty years of nothing. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, ordinary columns. A dog barked somewhere. It looked exactly as it always had.

It did not look exactly as it always had.

The light was wrong—or rather, it was *right*, in a way Emrys had never seen here. Sunlight fell through the valley air and seemed to arrive somewhere, seemed to be received. The barley turned its heads as they descended, not with wind but with attention. Even from the ridge, Emrys could feel the valley noticing them, the way a sleeping person notices a familiar hand on their shoulder.

"It remembers," Wren said softly. She had stopped walking. Her hand hovered near the ember-wyrm, who had lifted his small head, nostrils flaring at some scent older than any of them. "The covenant changed it. It's not sealed anymore—it's *listening*."

Emrys swallowed. "Listening to what?"

"To us." Wren glanced back, and there was something in her face Emrys had learned to read over the long road: fear worn openly instead of hidden, which she considered the braver form. "Everything here used to forget so it wouldn't have to choose. Now it has to choose again. Like everyone else."

They came down off the ridge as the sun cleared the eastern hills, and that was when Emrys saw the people.

They stood along the road where the barley gave way to the first farmyards—not a crowd, not yet, but dozens. The miller and his sons. Old Serafina from the dyehouse, whose hands were still stained blue from yesterday's work. The children who had thrown stones at Emrys's windows as a dare, years ago, because everyone dared each other eventually to provoke the quiet Marchetti who kept to themselves. Farmers with scythes leaning against fences, unused. No torches. No iron. No pitchforks held like weapons.

Faces turned as they approached. Nobody spoke.

Emrys's storm-heart stuttered against their ribs. Forty years of practiced invisibility screamed at them to lower their eyes, to make their body small and forgettable, to become weather that no one remarks upon. But their feet kept moving, because Wren was moving, and because the ember-wyrm had risen from her shoulder to hover at Emrys's ear, and his warmth against their cheek felt absurdly like courage.

It was Serafina who broke the silence. She stepped forward, blue-handed, squinting, and said in the tone of a woman confirming an account:

"Emrys Marchetti."

Not a question. Not quite an accusation either. Just the name, spoken aloud in the valley air, where names had weight now.

"Serafina," Emrys managed.

"My grandmother used to say your family kept the rain off us." The old woman's head tilted. "I thought she meant it the way farmers mean things. Superstition. But the night the sky over the pass went green—" she gestured vaguely eastward, toward the mountains, toward everything that had happened, "—my grandson swore he saw lightning walk uphill like a man. And I thought: *the Marchettis have come home.*"

"We're not—" Emrys began, and found they didn't know how to finish it. We're not dangerous. We're not what you think. We're not staying. All of it might be true. None of it was what mattered.

What mattered was that the miller's youngest boy, maybe nine years old, had worked free of the crowd and was standing three feet away, staring up at Emrys with the naked, terrible frankness of children.

"Did you really fight a dragon?" he asked.

"He *is* half a dragon," Wren said gravely, nodding at the ember-wyrm. "The other half is worse."

The boy's eyes went enormous. And then—Emrys would replay this moment for the rest of their life—he laughed. A bright, unguarded sound, the laugh of a child who has decided the world is more interesting than it is frightening, and something in the crowd loosened like a knot coming undone. Questions began to spill. Was it true the gods could be heard again? Was it true the river at Saltmarsh had run upward? Was it true—

"Enough," said a voice, and the crowd parted, and Emrys's chest clenched.

Tam Vess. Headman of Caerlum for as long as Emrys had lived under his jurisdiction, a broad, gray-bearded man who had signed the papers of Emrys's grandfather's exile with a steady hand and had never once looked at Emrys directly since. He had been the face of the valley's careful forgetting. He had looked through them for decades, politely, thoroughly, the way one looks through a wall.

He was looking at them now. Directly. His eyes were wet.

"The shrine keeper told us you'd come," he said. "Brother Aldous sent a rider three days ago—a letter, saying the covenant was remade, saying the silence was lifted, saying—" His voice caught. He steadied it with visible effort, the way men of his generation did, treating grief like a load of grain to be shifted shoulder-high. "Saying that whatever the Marchettis had carried for us, it was finished. That they might come home."

"They might," Emrys said carefully.

"That's what the letter said too." Tam almost smiled. "Aldous writes like a lawyer even when the world ends." He looked around at the gathered valley, then back, and what he said next he said loudly enough for everyone to hear, which Emrys understood was deliberate. "We've spent forty years not asking questions. It turns out not-asking is a kind of work, and we're tired of it. So." He spread his hands. "Ask us anything. Or don't. There's bread in my kitchen and a fire lit, and neither of those things depends on your answers."

Behind him, Serafina laughed her dry laugh. "The bread does depend on him, mind. Tam burns everything."

The crowd murmured—not hostility, never hostility, but the warm rumble of a thing deciding to be alive—and Emrys stood in the middle of it feeling like a man who has been holding his breath for forty years and has just been given permission to exhale.

Wren's hand found theirs. Small, calloused from the road, steady.

"Well?" she whispered. "Are you going to cry in front of the entire valley, or shall we do bread first?"

"Bread first," Emrys said, and their voice cracked only slightly, and nobody minded.

* * *

**Wren**

The bread was burnt, as promised. Wren ate three pieces of it anyway.

She sat on Tam Vess's doorstep in the late afternoon with the ember-wyrm asleep in her lap—heavy now, heavier every day, his memory thickening into substance as though the world itself were feeding him—and watched the village move around her. That was the strangest part, she decided. Not the gods returning, not the dragon reborn, not even Emrys. The strangest part was watching a place practice being unafraid.

For six years Wren had belonged to a temple whose holiness was built on control—who got to pray, who got to answer, who got burned for asking the wrong question. She had fled it with a censer of stolen ash and a conviction that faith was a cage with very good singing inside it. And here was a valley full of farmers and millers and blue-handed old women, none of whom knew a single rite properly, sitting in doorways asking real questions with their mouths open and their doors open too, and somehow that was more sacred than anything the temple had ever managed.

"You're doing the thinking face," Emrys said, dropping onto the step beside her. Their shoulder pressed hers. In the weeks since the hollow, since the flame and the shattering and the terrible bright remaking of everything, they had developed a habit of touching her lightly, often, as if confirming she was still there. She had decided to let it become permanent.

"One of us has to."

"Vaelith says you snore when you think. She says it carries across planes."

"She's the size of a loaf of bread. What does she know." But Wren scratched the wyrm's chin ridge gently, and he purred like a banked fire, and neither of them moved.

Down the lane, the children had organized themselves into something between a game and a pilgrimage. They were following Vaelith's earlier path across the yard, reenacting—Wren gathered from the shouted dialogue—the battle at the hollow, with a broom as the shattered covenant-stone and the miller's daughter as the Choir leader, dying theatrically every thirty seconds before rising to demand a rematch. One of the smaller boys kept breaking character to ask if the dragon had *really* talked through borrowed memories, and whether she could talk through his memories, and whether she wanted to see his rock collection.

"She'll ruin him," Wren said fondly. "He'll believe everything is possible now."

"Terrible," Emrys agreed. "Imagine."

They sat in the gold light a while. Then Emrys said, quietly, in the voice they used for the true things: "I keep waiting for it to close again. The valley. I keep waiting to wake up and feel the silence settle back over everything, like snow."

"Do you?"

"Yes. And then I remember it can't. Because it isn't grandmother's silence anymore, or the old gods' silence. It's—" They searched for it, frowning at the middle distance. "It's *witnessed*. There's a difference. A seal is just a wall. But what we made in the hollow—" their hand tightened on hers, "—it holds because people hold it. Because they *choose* it daily, the way you choose a fire in winter. And people are unreliable, Wren. People change their minds. Which means it could fail."

"Or," Wren said, "it means it's alive."

Emrys looked at her.

"A covenant that can't fail isn't a covenant," she said. "It's a grave. I lived in one for six years. The walls held perfectly." She turned to face them fully. "You didn't trade your exile for safety. You traded it for a home that has to keep choosing you. That's scarier. That's the whole point."

The ember-wyrm opened one eye, regarded them both with ancient amber patience, and said, in his voice like coals settling: "She is wiser than the both of us combined, and I include my former self, who was very large and very foolish."

"Don't flatter her," Emrys muttered, "she'll be insufferable."

But they were smiling. And then the smile changed—deepened into something Wren recognized, having seen it once before, on a ridge above a dead shrine, on a night when a prayer got answered after forty years of nothing. The look of a person about to do something just for the joy of it.

"What?" she asked.

"Watch," said Emrys, and stood up, and walked out into the open yard.

* * *

**Emrys**

The villagers noticed, of course. That was the point. That was the terrifying, glorious point.

Emrys stood in the middle of Tam Vess's yard with the whole of Caerlum drifting toward stillness around them—children pausing mid-battle, Serafina setting down her dye-pots, the miller leaning on his gate—and felt the valley's attention settle over them like a second skin. Once, attention had been the enemy. Once, the whole art of their life had been the avoidance of exactly this: eyes, names, the risk of being seen as what they were.

They raised their face to the sky.

The clouds were high and white and indifferent, the ordinary clouds of an ordinary afternoon. Emrys reached for them the way they had once reached for storms across a hundred miles of sea—except gentler, so much gentler, the way one reaches for a bird rather than a weapon. Power woke in them gladly. It had been waking gladly ever since the hollow, ever since they had poured their memory into the new covenant and found that what flowed back was not loss but *room*. The storm-calling no longer felt like a wild animal barely leashed. It felt like a limb, fully returned, flexing in sunlight.

*Come down,* they asked the sky. *Softly. Only softly.*

The white clouds darkened at their edges, gathered, drew together overhead like a congregation. A hush went through the yard. Somewhere a child gasped. And then the rain began—

—not the hammering summer deluge Caerlum knew, but a fine, warm, silver drift of it, falling straight down without wind, catching the late sun so that every drop burned briefly gold. It fell on the barley and the slate roofs and the upturned faces of the valley, and it smelled the way the world smelled when it was newly made.

Emrys stood in the center of it with their arms loose at their sides and their eyes closed, and simply *felt* it. Each drop landing on their skin like a word in a language only they had ever spoken fluently. Rain had been their first tongue, before exile, before silence, before the long numb decades. To stand inside it, seen by everyone, wanting to be seen—to call the water down not to save or destroy or prove anything, but only because it was beautiful and they were allowed—was the closest thing to prayer Emrys had done since the shrine.

When they opened their eyes, the whole valley was standing in the golden rain with them.

Nobody had gone indoors. Nobody was sheltering the bread or the dye-works or the hay. Serafina had her face tipped up and her blue hands open. The miller's daughter had abandoned her role as defeated tyrant entirely and was spinning with her arms out. Tam Vess stood at his door with rain running into his beard, and he wasn't wiping it away, and he was looking at Emrys the way a man looks at a country he thought he'd lost.

And the boy—the nine-year-old, the one with the questions—came and stood directly in front of Emrys in the falling gold light, and tugged their sleeve, and shouted over the soft drumming:

"Can you teach me?"

Emrys crouched to his level. Around them, the yard had gone quiet again, but it was a different quiet now—attentive, leaning in, the quiet of a room full of people who realize a question has been asked that belongs to all of them.

"Not today," Emrys said. "Magic isn't a trick. It starts with listening—really listening—to what the world is already saying. And that takes years, and teachers, and a place where it's safe to get it wrong."

The boy's face began to fall.

"But," Emrys went on, and felt Wren's presence at their shoulder like a struck match, and the ember-wyrm's warmth perched on their arm like a small sun, "there used to be a shrine at the heart of this valley where nobody was taught anything, because the gods weren't talking and the magic couldn't follow. That's over now. The shrine's empty. The valley's listening. And I know a shrine-keeper who owes me for saving her life at least twice—"

"Once," Wren said. "Maybe once."

"—who has been threatening to start teaching anyone who'll listen since the day I met her. And I know a dragon, however small she currently is, who knows more about the old days than any book left burning. So." Emrys looked around the yard, at the rain-gold faces, at the doors standing open all down the lane. "If Caerlum wants it—we'll open a school."

The silence lasted three heartbeats.

Then Serafina laughed her dry laugh and said, "About time someone put that shrine to use," and the valley broke into noise—voices overlapping, questions, offers, the miller shouting something about lumber, Tam Vess already listing which families had spare rooms—as though the decision had been waiting in all of them for years, needing only to be spoken aloud to become inevitable.

Emrys stood up slowly in the rain and found Wren beside them, soaked and laughing and trying to look exasperated and failing completely.

"You owe me twice," she said.

"I absolutely do."

On Emrys's arm, the ember-wyrm lifted his head, and his amber eyes had gone distant, fixed on the eastern hills where the light was failing into evening—and when he spoke, his coal-voice had an edge in it that had nothing to do with the cold.

"There is a rider coming up the pass," he said quietly, so that only they could hear. "Fast. Flying temple colors." His gaze slid to Wren, and something old and sorrowful moved through it. "Child—they are coming for *you*."
