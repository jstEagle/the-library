## 22. A New Covenant Written

**Emrys**

The shrine had been dead longer than I had been alive, and in three months of autumn we taught it to speak again.

It was Wren's idea first—though she would deny it, the way she denied everything generous about herself. We had come back to Caerlum with ash under our fingernails and a wyrm the size of a loaf of bread sleeping in my coat pocket, and for two weeks we simply existed. We slept until the light through the shutters woke us. We ate bread that Maren the baker left on our doorstep without a note. I called a small rain over the vegetable gardens and no one ran screaming into their houses; old Tam Braddock stood in it with his arms out like a boy, laughing, his cataract-clouded eyes turned up to a sky that had finally remembered him.

Then one evening Wren stood at the door of the ruined shrine above the millpond—the one where, a lifetime ago, I had whispered a prayer into silence and been answered by nothing—and said, "This building is going to fall down on somebody's head eventually. Might as well be on students."

"Students," I repeated.

"You know things." She shrugged, that sharp-shouldered shrug she used to armor her softness. "I know things. The valley's full of people who've spent their whole lives being afraid of something they never understood. Seems wasteful to let all that fear just sit there when it could be a curriculum."

The ember-wyrm, curled in the crook of my arm, opened one eye. It was the color of the last coal in a hearth at midnight, and when it breathed—which was rarely—a smell of hot stone and juniper filled the room. Vaelith, reborn small, with perhaps a tenth of what she had been and, if you asked me, twice the opinions.

"She agrees," I said.

"The wyrm is not a vote."

"She's older than every government in existence. That makes her an institution. Institutions vote."

Wren threw a rag at my head. It was, I would later insist, the moment I knew we were going to be all right—not because of the rag, but because throwing it required her to turn her back to the door of a building full of strangers, and she did it without flinching. Three months ago she had slept sitting up with a knife in her boot. Now she threw laundry at me like a person with a future.

We rebuilt through the cold months. The villagers helped, at first out of curiosity, then out of something sturdier. Tam Braddock re-laid the roof tiles. Maren's daughters hauled timber. The Hollis twins, who were nine and feral, dug out the foundation stones and uncovered, buried beneath the eastern wall, a shard of the old covenant-stone—the one I had shattered in another life, or so it felt, though it had only been a spring and a summer ago. The shard still hummed faintly when touched. Wren wanted to bury it deeper. I said no.

"That piece of stone kept us apart from everything for sixty years," I told her. "It doesn't get to hide anymore either. We'll set it in the doorway. Let everyone walk over it coming in. Let it learn what it was for."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she knelt and set the shard into fresh mortar with her own hands, pressing it flat, its inscription facing up: *witness*, worn nearly smooth by centuries of feet that had never known they walked on it.

That became the school's first lesson, before there were even benches. Everyone who entered stepped on the word.

* * *

**Wren**

The first student was a girl named Isolde whose prayers had started getting answered in the spring and who had not slept properly since.

She came to the shrine in the second week, hovering by the door the way people hover when they want something but are afraid the wanting shows. Emrys saw her—they always see them, they have an eye for the haunted, being haunted themselves—but it was me who went out and stood next to her in the cold and said nothing, because sometimes the best thing a former shrine-keeper can do is stand beside someone and not demand their confession.

Finally she said, "I prayed for my father's cough to get better. And it did. And then I prayed for rain for the fields, and it rained, and now—" Her voice cracked. "Now everyone looks at me. And I don't know what I am. And the god won't stop *talking* to me."

"What does it say?"

"It says thank you." She was crying now, angry tears. "It says *thank you* over and over, like I did something. Like I'm supposed to do something. I'm twelve."

I knew that terror. I had carried it across half the world in a censer of glowing ash. So I took her inside, sat her down on a bench that still smelled of cut pine, and told her the truest thing I knew:

"The gods got loud because the walls came down. That wasn't your fault, and it isn't your job to hold them up again. But someone has to teach people how to live in a world where the divine answers back. Might as well be us. First rule: you are allowed to say *not now*. They waited sixty years behind a covenant. They can wait while you eat supper."

Isolde blinked at me. "Can you really tell a god no?"

"You can tell anyone no." I glanced at Emrys, who was pretending not to listen while repairing a shutter with unnecessary force. "Some of us took rather a long time to learn it, but yes."

She came back the next day. Then she brought her cousin. By midwinter we had eleven students crammed into a shrine built for quiet contemplation, and by spring we had knocked down the lean-to and raised a proper schoolhouse with the covenant-shard in the threshold, and I stood in the doorway on opening morning watching children step deliberately on the word *witness*—some of them hopping on it, because they were children—and felt something in my chest unclench that I had not realized was still gripping.

I had fled a temple because it taught faith as obedience. Here we taught it as conversation. Here the gods were not masters to be placated but neighbors to be negotiated with, honored, occasionally told to mind their business. Here memory was not a burden hoarded by guarantors but a skill taught openly: how to keep a memory safe, how to offer one freely, how to know the difference between a sacrifice and a theft.

Emrys taught storm-calling and covenant-law. I taught rites and wards and the sacred art of saying no. The ember-wyrm taught nothing officially, but she would drape herself across the desks during lessons and radiate heat in winter, and when a child asked her a question she would answer in images—flashes of mountain fire, of a war older than language, of two mortals standing in a dragon-hollow holding a flame between them. The children learned more history from those dreams than from any book.

And some evenings, when the students had gone home, Emrys and I would sit on the steps with the wyrm between us and watch the sun go down over the valley that had forgotten itself back into remembering, and neither of us would say anything, because we had both learned that silence, chosen together, is a different thing entirely from silence imposed.

* * *

**Vaelith**

*I remember* — I choose to remember — *a hollow in a mountain where a dragon gave away her fire to make a law.*

That was the old covenant. Law written in loss. Silence purchased with burning. My grandmother-self believed it was the only shape peace could take: realms sealed apart, gods muzzled, mortals safe in their ignorance, and one dragon diminished to a ghost-memory keeping the ledger of what it cost.

I was wrong. Or rather—I was right for that age, and that age ended, and I am glad beyond the measure of my ashes that it ended the way it did. Not with a shattering. With a rewriting.

I have watched covenants forged since the world was young. They are always made of the same materials: power, fear, and the willingness of someone to pay. This new one is different. This one was drafted at a kitchen table, argued over for six nights, amended seventeen times at the insistence of a baker who thought the gods should be required to give notice before manifesting in anyone's flour. This one has witnesses—thirty-one names carved into a new stone, mortal names, none of them asked to pay anything except attention. Attention, freely given, renewed each season, revocable at will.

Do you understand what a revolution that is? A covenant held together not by sacrifice but by *attendance*. Not by one bleeding guarantor but by a village that keeps showing up. The Marchetti blood anchors it still—Emrys's name is in the heart of it, and the price they paid sits in them like a missing tooth, a childhood face they can no longer summon, a bitter year gone smooth as river glass. But around that anchor, hundreds of small hands hold the rope.

When the realms touch now, it is gentle. Rain remembers how to fall in patterns. Gods knock. Dragons—

Dragons grow. I am the size of a large cat now. By midsummer I may manage a dog. My flame, when I breathe it, is the size of a candle, and Emrys swears it smells like juniper and home, and I permit this sentimentality because I am, after all, reborn, and rebirth entitles one to sentiment.

But I remember the hollow. I remember dying. And I remember the two of them kneeling in the ruin of everything, offering memories like coins, refusing to let the other pay alone—and I think: *this*. This is what the old covenant should have been. Not one dragon's fire sealing the doors shut, but many small flames holding the doors open.

I am teaching the children to tend fires now. Real ones, in the shrine's restored hearth. Small hands, small flames. It is enough. It is more than enough.

It is, at last, the right shape.

* * *

**Emrys**

A year, almost to the day, since I knelt at this shrine and whispered into dead air.

I didn't plan the anniversary. Wren noticed it—she keeps track of things, dates and debts and the names of everyone's mothers—and she said nothing, which is how I knew she'd noticed. But on that evening, after lessons, after the students had scattered down the hill with their satchels and their noisy, unafraid lives, I found myself walking down to the millpond alone, the way I had walked that first night, and standing where I had stood, and looking at the water where the shrine's reflection used to be a wound and is now just a reflection.

The old prayer rose in me unbidden. I hadn't spoken it aloud in over a year. It was a small prayer. That was always what struck people about it afterward, when I finally told the story—that after everything, the prayer that cracked open the world had been so small. *Let there be an answer. Even a hard one. Even one I don't understand. Just—let the silence break.*

Back then, the valley swallowed it whole. Back then, the silence was a law with my family's name on it, and the law said *no*.

I stood at the water's edge and felt the wind move through the valley—real wind, wind I could feel the edges of, wind that carried the smell of bread and woodsmoke and pond-water and, faintly, from far off, the sea—and I spoke the prayer again. Out loud. The same words, worn smooth by repetition in my head over decades of not-daring.

And the answer came.

Not thunder. Not a god unfolding out of the sky. Just footsteps on the path behind me—Wren's boots, unhurried, and the click-click-click of Vaelith's claws on the flagstones, and then Wren's shoulder against mine, warm, solid, choosing to be there, and the ember-wyrm climbing up to my other shoulder with the heavy dignity of a creature who remembers being vast.

"Well?" Wren said. "Did the silence break?"

I laughed. It startled a heron off the reeds.

"No," I said. "Better. Somebody answered."

Because that was the difference, and it took me a year and a shattered covenant and a dragon's death and rebirth to learn to say it plainly: the old miracle was a silence breaking. The new one was smaller and infinitely larger. A prayer whispered into the evening air, and human hands answering it—Wren's hand finding mine, calloused from rebuilding; the wyrm's warmth soaking through my coat; somewhere up the hill a lamp lit in the schoolhouse window because Isolde had come back for her forgotten arithmetic book and was leaving it burning for whoever came next.

Anyone can break a silence. Storms break silences. Wars do.

But an answer—*an answer* takes two.

We stood there a long time, the three of us, in the blue hour, while the valley went on living around us: lamps lighting, suppers cooking, prayers rising from a dozen houses to a dozen patient ears, and everywhere, everywhere, the sound of hands opening doors.

The covenant holds. We wrote it ourselves.
