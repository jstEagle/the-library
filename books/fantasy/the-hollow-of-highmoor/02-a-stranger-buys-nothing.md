## 2. A Stranger Buys Nothing

The bell above the door had not rung all day, and Ada had begun to count that as a mercy.

By the seventh hour of the evening she had sold exactly three things: a warming stone to a lamplighter with chilblains, a sachet of dreamless sleep to a widow who paid in buttons, and—she winced at the memory—a luck-knot to a fisherman who would be lucky if it kept his boots dry. The guild's stock had thinned to this: small comforts for small coin, magic diluted until even an inspector would have to squint to see it. Her grandmother used to say a charm-shop's shelves should hum when you walked past. These days Ada's shelves only creaked, and not from power.

She was sweeping grit from behind the counter when the bell finally spoke.

Not rang—spoke. That was the only way she could describe it afterward: a low, deliberate toll, as though the brass itself had paused before deciding to announce him. She looked up with her broom still moving and found a man standing just inside the threshold, half in shadow, letting the door swing shut behind him without touching it.

He was ordinary in the way that fog is ordinary—present everywhere, remarkable nowhere, and impossible to hold in your eyes for long. Grey coat, grey scarf, dark hair going silver at the temples though he could not have been past forty. His face was the sort you'd struggle to describe five minutes after looking away. And yet something about it snagged her, like a splinter under the skin. She had seen him before. She was almost certain she had seen him before, and equally certain she could not say where.

"We're closing," she said. It came out more tired than firm.

"I know." His voice was quiet, roughened at the edges, like paper that had been folded and unfolded many years. "I waited until you were."

Ada set the broom against the counter. In Highmoor, people who waited until closing were usually people who wanted something they couldn't ask for in daylight. "Then you'd better buy quickly."

He crossed the shop slowly. She watched his hands as he moved—not reaching for anything, just hovering near the shelves, close enough to feel but never touching. He passed the warming stones. He passed the luck-knots and the fever-charms and the little pressed-glass bottles of courage that Ada sold mostly to young men on their way to job interviews. None of it seemed to interest him, or rather, none of it seemed capable of interesting him, the way a menu cannot interest a man who has already decided there is nothing on it he can eat.

At last he stopped at the counter across from her, and up close she saw his eyes properly for the first time. They were the pale, washed-out grey of things left too long in weather—and they were fixed on her with an attention so total it felt almost like being held.

"You're Ada Sterling," he said.

"Everyone in Highmoor is someone," she said. "What do you need?"

"A charm." He said it carefully, testing each syllable. "Against being remembered."

The sweepings in the dustpan behind her seemed suddenly loud. Ada kept her face still. Ten years behind this counter had taught her that the strange requests were never really strange; they were just honest. A woman asking for a love-charm wanted to stop being lonely. A man asking for a protection sigil wanted to stop being afraid. So she asked the question she always asked.

"Against being remembered by whom?"

He blinked. For a moment something moved through his face—surprise, maybe, or its exhausted cousin, recognition. "I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "That's the thing. I don't know if it's anyone. I don't know if it's everyone." His hand came up and rubbed at his sternum, an unconscious gesture, as though something ached there in a place no charm could reach. "People forget me, Miss Sterling. Not rudely. Not on purpose. They forget me the way water forgets a stone. I'll speak to a man at the market on Tuesday, and on Thursday he'll look straight through me, and by Friday he'll swear we've never met. Landlords. Clerks. Priests." A thin, terrible smile. "My own name feels borrowed most days. Corin Ash. Say it to yourself and see how long it stays."

"Corin Ash," Ada repeated—and then went cold, because she realized, saying it, that the name already fit in her mouth. As if she'd said it before. As if she'd known it the way you know a word from a childhood song, without knowing where you learned it.

His expression didn't change, but something in the room did. The lamps guttered, both of them, in perfect unison, and the fire in the grate drew down low, and outside the window the fog pressed closer to the glass as though leaning in to listen.

"Do that again," he whispered.

"Say your name?"

"No. Don't." He stepped back from the counter, one hand raised, and for a heartbeat he looked genuinely frightened—not of her, she understood, but of whatever happened when his name lived briefly in another person's mouth. "It doesn't matter. If it doesn't stay, it only hurts more when it goes."

Ada's hands had gone still on the countertop. She thought of the ledger beneath the floorboards—the forbidden one, the one with the iron clasp and the cipher her grandmother had never taught her, the one Ada had promised on her deathbed promise to leave sealed. She thought of nothing else, which was itself suspicious. When a mind fixes on one locked thing, it is usually because something nearby has rattled it.

"I can make what you're asking for," she heard herself say. "A forgetting reversed. A charm of remembrance—it would anchor you. Make you stick, in other people's minds. It's old craft. Hard craft." She hesitated. "It's also the kind of thing that gets shops marked. You understand why I'd need to know more."

"There isn't more." He was already retreating toward the door, and she saw now that he had been retreating since he entered—that the whole conversation had been a man walking backward away from hope. "There's never more. I've asked in four cities before this one. There's always a charm, and it's always expensive, and it never works, because the forgetting isn't in me. It's—" His hand found the door handle. "It's somewhere else. Somewhere I can't pay."

"The price," Ada said, "isn't coin."

It wasn't a question, and he knew it wasn't a question. He stood with the door half open, fog curling gray around his shoulders like a coat being offered to him by the city itself, and looked at her for a long moment with those washed-out eyes.

"No," he said softly. "I don't suppose it is."

And then Corin Ash was gone, and the bell spoke once behind him, low and reluctant, and the shop settled into its usual silence like a dog lying back down by the fire.

* * *

Ada did not light the back-room lamp until she had bolted the front door twice and stood listening to the building breathe.

Every old house in Highmoor breathed. The guild had built its shops on foundations of worked stone older than the city charter, and the stones kept their own hours, expanding in the damp and settling in the cold with sounds like slow speech. Tonight the house sounded agitated. Or perhaps that was only her.

She told herself she was going to the back room for tea. She poured the kettle over the leaves and let it steep untouched while her eye traveled, as it had traveled every night for ten years, to the third floorboard from the hearth—the one with the iron ring set flush into the wood, worn smooth by a century of nervous thumbs. Grandmother Sterling's board. Grandmother Sterling's seal. *Leave it shut, Ada-love,* she had said, the last winter, her hands like paper around Ada's wrists. *Some ledgers are debts. Some are doors. This one is both, and you are not ready to read either.*

*I'm not ready* had felt like wisdom when Ada was twenty-two and grieving. At thirty-one, standing over the board with a stranger's name still warm in her mouth, it felt like the thing it had probably always been: a lock someone else had installed to keep her out.

She knelt. The floor was cold through her skirt. The iron ring came up with a sound like a held breath released.

Beneath it lay a box of black cedar, unvarnished, banded in copper gone green at the seams. Inside the box, wrapped in oilcloth that crackled like frost, lay the Hollow Prophecies—or what the guild had always called them, though Ada had never known why a book of prophecy should smell, faintly and terribly, of iron and old pennies. The ledger was thick as a paving stone, bound in leather so dark it drank the lamplight, and its pages were written in her grandmother's cipher: a lattice of knots and strokes that Ada had studied secretly as a girl and never cracked, because cracking it required a key, and the key—

The key was a name.

She had discovered this much at sixteen, eavesdropping outside the guildhall: that the cipher turned on names, that each entry was keyed to the soul it described, and that reading an entry aloud with the right cadence opened it like a lock opening. She had assumed as a child that this was mere cleverness. Kneeling here now, she understood it was worse. The book didn't record names. The book *held* them.

Her hands shook as she untied the oilcloth.

The first page fell open to a drawing—a face sketched in graphite so fine it might have been blown onto the paper in ash. An old man, hollow-cheeked, patient-eyed. Beneath the portrait, rows of knotted script, and beneath the script, a date. Ada recognized the date the way you recognize your own birthday: 14th of Embermonth, sixty-two years ago.

The old man had no name that Ada could find. But at the bottom of the page, in her grandmother's plain hand—unencrypted, as if she had needed it legible even to herself—were the words:

*Paid in full. Guild stands. God forgive me.*

Ada sat back on her heels. The kettle whistled thinly in the next room, and she let it whistle, because her legs would not yet agree to carry her anywhere.

Sixty-two years ago. One generation. Her grandmother had been guildhead for forty of them, and in all those forty years the shop had never once gone hungry, the guild had never once begged the Assembly for relief, and Highmoor's magistrates had mysteriously, consistently, looked the other way. Ada had grown up believing this was excellence. Diligence. The natural reward of good craft honestly practiced.

*Paid in full.*

She turned the page.

Another face. A woman this time, young, frightened, drawn with the same fine graphite, dated fifty-four years ago. Then another, forty-three years. Another, thirty-one. Each portrait followed by the knotted cipher, each cipher followed by her grandmother's plain, terrible marginalia—shorter each time, as though the words cost more with every purchase. *Forgive me. Forgive me. Forgive me.*

Five souls. Five faces. Five dates spaced down a century like fence posts.

Ada's mouth had gone dry. She was turning the sixth page before she fully understood that she was looking for something, and turning the seventh before she admitted what.

Corin Ash.

The face looked up at her off the page and she made a sound she hadn't made since childhood—a small involuntary noise, half gasp, half prayer. It was him. Not approximately him, not the way a sketch is approximately anyone. It was *him*: the tired line of the mouth, the washed-out eyes rendered in soft graphite shading, the hair going silver at the temple. Drawn with a precision no living artist could manage from imagination alone. Drawn as if the artist had seen the face every day for years.

Drawn, Ada realized with a lurch, the way you draw someone you have met.

Below the portrait, the cipher ran longer than any other entry in the book—dense rows of knots and strokes, a paragraph where the others had held sentences. And below the cipher, in her grandmother's hand, the margin note. Not *forgive me*. Something else. Something Ada read three times because her eyes refused to accept it the first two.

*Bought the same year as the others. Never delivered. Entry stands open. Thirteen moons remain.*

And beside that, smaller, the letters cramped as though squeezed out against her will:

*Tomorrow.*

Tomorrow. The word sat on the page like a coal.

Ada looked at the date column and did the arithmetic with a numbness that felt almost peaceful, the calm that comes in the moment after a fall, before the pain arrives. Tomorrow was the date. Not the anniversary of the sale. The date of something else—an ending, a reckoning, a clause coming due. Thirteen moons remained, her grandmother had written, and tomorrow the count began.

She looked at the portrait again. At the tired, patient, unforgettable-unrememberable face.

"Who are you?" she whispered, and the house breathed around her, and somewhere beneath the floorboards—deeper than the box, deeper than the foundation stones, deeper than any cellar her family had ever dug—something answered. Not in words. In weight. A pressure against the underside of the floor, gentle as a hand laid palm-down on the far side of a door.

Outside, the fog had come up against the windows so thick that the streetlamp across the lane was only a smudge, a ghost of light, a lamp that had forgotten itself. Ada wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth with hands that would not quite obey her, slid it into the box, and reached for the lid.

She stopped.

Because there, pressed into the dust on the inside of the lid—a print, fine and clear, the print of a thumb. And she knew whose. She had seen that hand hover over every shelf in her shop not two hours ago, close enough to feel, never touching.

Corin Ash had touched this box. Years ago, by the depth of the print. Long before tonight. Long before he ever walked through her door and asked her, politely, quietly, for a charm against being remembered.

Which meant he had known about the ledger.

Which meant, some cold and rising part of her understood, that he had not come to her shop tonight by accident at all.

The house exhaled. Down in the dark beneath the boards, the pressure against the door grew very slightly heavier—as if whatever waited there had felt her notice it, and was waiting, patiently, thirteen moons deep in patience, to see what she would do next.
