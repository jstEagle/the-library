## 3. The Ledger Under the Floorboards

I did not sleep.

That was not unusual — sleep and I had been negotiating terms for years, and usually the shop won — but tonight the losing felt different. Tonight I lay on my cot above the workroom listening to the rain write its endless signature on the slates, and beneath me, under the floorboards where I had hidden it at nineteen and told no one, the ledger waited like a held breath.

Grandmother's book. The one she had pressed into my hands three days before she died, with her fingers cold as coin and her voice stripped down to wire: *When you find his face in it, Ada — when you finally look — remember that I loved you more than I feared anything.*

I had thought she meant the guild's enemies. The inspectors, the magistrates of the Ministry of Ordinary Affairs, the neighbors whose loyalty was a candle guttering in fog. I had put the ledger under the floorboards and told myself it was a relic, an account book of a dead trade, full of ciphered inventory and old prices for old wonders.

Then a man named Corin Ash had stood in my doorway at closing time and asked for a charm against being remembered, and this morning I had opened my grandmother's forbidden pages to tomorrow's date and found his face drawn there in pencil so fine it might have been breathed onto the paper rather than drawn.

So: no sleep. Instead, lamplight, cold tea gone bitter in its cup, and the ledger open on my knees like something with a pulse.

* * *

The cipher was Grandmother's own — she'd invented it during the Outlawry years, when even a shopping list could hang you if the wrong eyes read it. She called it knotwork, because that was how it looked: letters tied together, doubled back on themselves, every word wearing a disguise over its true face. She taught it to me as a game when I was small. *A charm against prying eyes,* she'd say, tapping my nose, *the only kind we can sell openly.*

She never taught me the second layer. I hadn't known there was a second layer until last night, when I held Corin Ash's sketched face up to the lamp and noticed what I had taken for shading along his jaw was not shading at all. It was writing. Microscopic, threaded through the pencil strokes themselves, a script inside the image the way marrow lives inside bone.

Knotwork within knotwork. A letter inside its own disguise.

It took me four hours to crack, and most of those hours were spent fighting my own hands. They shook. I kept telling myself it was the cold, but the stove was lit and my tea had long since gone lukewarm and my hands went on shaking anyway, because some part of me — the part that had spent thirty years reading customers instead of books, the part that knew what people looked like just before bad news — already understood that whatever was written inside that face would change the shape of everything I owned, everything I owed, everything I was.

The inner script read:

*Entry the ninth. Given freely by the hand of Maren Sterling, guildhead, in the year the frost came late. Name: Corin Ash, of Ashfall village. Term: thirteen turns of the moon, counted from first crossing of the threshold below. Upon the thirteenth turn, the soul passes wholly to the Hollow Court, and the bargain stands fulfilled unto the next generation.*

Below that, in a different hand — older, spikier, pressed so hard into the paper the pen had nearly torn through — a single line:

*God forgive us. He was only nine years old.*

I sat very still for a long time.

The rain kept on. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, the milk-cart probably, old Brammel's horse coughing its way through the fog. Highmoor waking up. Bakers lighting ovens, inspectors buttoning their grey coats, children running errands through streets where magic had been outlawed for a generation and everyone pretended that was simply weather.

And underneath all of it — under the city, under the mountain, under the floorboards of my own shop — a court. A court that bought souls. That had bought one soul, once, from my grandmother's hand, and set a clock on him that had been ticking for longer than I'd been alive.

Thirteen turns of the moon.

I did the arithmetic the way you do terrible arithmetic, slowly, twice, hoping to be wrong both times. If the entry dated from the year the frost came late — and I knew that year, every child in Highmoor knew it, the winter that wouldn't end, the year the guild's fortunes turned overnight from struggling to golden — then the count had begun decades ago. Which meant either the count was wrong, or the count was not counted the way I counted, or—

Or the thirteenth turn was coming now. This year. This season.

His face had been sketched beside *tomorrow's date.* Not the date of the sale. Not the date of the signing. Tomorrow.

As if the ledger expected him.

* * *

I should have stopped there. Any sensible woman would have stopped there, closed the book, buried it deeper than the floorboards, maybe buried it in the river, gone upstairs and made tea and opened the shutters and sold a warming-stone to Mrs. Pell at ten o'clock sharp.

But the line about the nine-year-old had hooked into me like a fishhook into soft flesh, and I turned the page.

And then the next. And then back to the beginning, because once you can read a thing you cannot unlearn it, and the whole ledger opened to me now like a body opening under a surgeon's knife.

It was not an inventory book. It was a register.

*Entry the first.* A name I didn't know — Wren, of nowhere, no village given, no age given — sold by a guildhead whose name had been scraped away so thoroughly the parchment had thinned to lace. No term listed. Just two words in the margin: *never returned.*

*Entry the second.* A boy from Tanner's Row. Sold by a guildhead named Aldous Crane, whose portrait still hung in the Guildhall antechamber, gilded frame and benevolent smile, the man credited with saving Highmoor's charm-trade during the worst of the Outlawry purges. The margin note, in that same spiky desperate hand that would later be Grandmother's: *the guild eats well this winter. God forgive us.*

*Entry the third.* A girl of eleven. Entry the fourth, a ferryman. Entry the fifth — I had to stop after the fifth, put the book down flat on the table, press my palms against my eyes until I saw red constellations — the fifth was a name I recognized. Tam Vael. Sold forty years ago by guildhead Odile Marchmont. I knew the surname because Inspector Vael wore it like a uniform two sizes too small. I'd assumed he had family somewhere in the administrative quarter. Brothers, perhaps. A mother who baked.

One soul per generation. That was the pattern, once the entries settled into rhythm — one per generation, each sold by the sitting guildhead, each *given freely*, which the ledger repeated with a lawyer's insistence, as though repetition could make it true. And beside every sale, in columns as neat as any merchant's book, the payment:

*Frost broken. Crops spared.*
*Purge passed over our street. Three shops saved.*
*Guild charter renewed by ministerial decree.*
*Prosperity, twenty years' standing.*
*Prosperity, continued.*

There it was. The source of it all. Every warm stone I'd ever sold, every lucky knot, every season the shop survived when the other trades around us folded like wet paper — bought and paid for, generation upon generation, with the freedom of someone who could not afford to be missed.

Because that was the other column. The one without headers, the one I understood only when I cross-checked the names against the census rolls I kept for tax purposes, the way you keep such things, resentfully and accurately. None of them appeared. Not in the rolls, not in parish registers, not in the memory of any record the city kept. Corin Ash, of Ashfall village — I checked Ashfall too, in an old atlas; the village wasn't there anymore, wasn't anywhere, a blank space in the valley where a settlement had been — Corin Ash existed in exactly one place in all the world's paperwork.

In my grandmother's handwriting. In a book under my floorboards.

No wonder he asked for a charm against being remembered. No wonder the door had opened for him twice when it never opened twice for anyone else. The court had not merely taken his future. It had reached backward through his life and eaten the evidence of him out of the world — his village first, then his records, then his place in every mind that ever held it — until the only copy of Corin Ash left standing was the one the guild itself had needed to keep. The receipt. You keep the receipt. That's all he was to us. All he had ever been to the Sterlings and the Cranes and the Marchmonts and whoever came before them.

A receipt that walked and breathed and knocked on doors.

* * *

Midmorning light came through the workroom window the color of dishwater, and I was still on the floor, the ledger across my knees, when I heard footsteps on the stair.

My body knew the rhythm before my mind caught up: the heavy tread, the pause, the little cough. Master Fenwick. Of course. The last other member of the Charmwrights' Guild — ninety-one years old, hands like knotted roots, a man who came by twice a week to drink my tea and tell me the guild was dying and thank the saints it was dying slowly, on my watch, in honorable decline.

He knocked. I slid the ledger under the loose floorboard with a speed born of thirty years' practice at hiding things, dropped the rug over it, and opened the door with a face I hoped looked like a face.

"Ada." Fenwick beamed at me from under the awning, rain dripping off his hat brim. "You look dreadful, child. Up all night?"

"Inventory," I said.

"Mm." He came in, hung his coat, accepted tea, sat in his chair by the stove — the whole ritual worn smooth as riverstone from decades of repetition. It took him until the second sip to notice. Whatever it was he noticed, I couldn't have said — the tremor in my pouring, maybe, or the way I kept my eyes on the window, or simply the animal knowledge of one old craftsman for another — but he set the cup down carefully, the way you set things down when your hands have begun to disagree with you, and said, quietly:

"You've been reading her book."

The stove ticked. Rain ran down the glass in long silver threads.

"You knew," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice in my own mouth. "You knew what it was."

Fenwick looked at his hands. For a moment he seemed to grow older in front of me, the way ice grows thinner in spring — nothing moving, everything giving way.

"I signed the witness line on your grandmother's entry," he said. "Forty-one years ago. There were three of us in the room. Your grandmother. Me. And the magistrate who came up from below wearing a coat of ordinary grey, the way they always do." He lifted his eyes to mine, and they were wet and steady at once, which is the worst combination eyes can manage. "Ask me what you're going to ask me, Ada. But first let me tell you that she wept. She wept for three days before and three days after, and she never slept a whole night again as long as she lived, and she built this guild into the finest thing in Highmoor with the sound of that child's name in her ears. Ask me. I'll answer. But you will not stand there and think she did it lightly, because if you think that, I'll have failed her twice."

"The child," I said. "Corin Ash. He was nine."

"He was nine."

"And we—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "We bought everything. The charter, the prosperity, the shop, my whole life — we bought it with him. One soul per generation, and the guild has been living off the interest for a century."

Fenwick said nothing. He didn't have to. His silence was a signature on the witness line, again, still, always.

"He came into my shop yesterday," I whispered. "He's real. He walks around out there and nobody sees him twice and the ledger says he has thirteen moons left and I think— Fenwick, I think the moons are almost done."

Something moved behind the old man's face — fear, yes, but under the fear something worse, something that looked terribly like resignation. Like a man who had watched this exact storm approach from a great distance his entire life and had hoped, without quite believing it, to die before landfall.

"Listen to me," he said, leaning forward, gripping his cane with both hands. "Whatever you're thinking of doing — and I know that look, child, I saw your grandmother wear it, God rest her, the night before she signed — you let it stand. Do you hear me? The bargain is sealed. The court holds the contract, and the court does not renegotiate, and the men and women who tried are in that ledger too, in the back pages, in the margins, in ink that isn't ink anymore. You let it stand. You grieve for him if you must, but you let it *stand.*"

"Why?" I asked. "What happens if I don't?"

Fenwick's mouth opened. Closed. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass like something listening.

And then, from under the floorboards — faint, muffled, unmistakable — the ledger began to hum.

Not loudly. The way a kettle hums just before it sings. The way a struck bell hums after the sound seems finished. A thin, patient vibration rising through the boards, through the rug, into the soles of my feet, and with it — I swear this on my grandmother's grave — warmth. A gentle, coaxing, shop-warming-charm sort of warmth, spreading out from the book like a hand opening.

Like an invitation.

Fenwick had gone white as flour. "Ada," he said hoarsely. "Ada, what did you do?"

"I read it," I said.

The humming swelled, and beneath it, threading up through the floorboards like smoke through a keyhole, I heard — or dreamed I heard, though I have never once managed to convince myself it was a dream — a voice. Dry as paper. Patient as debt.

*The buyer's heir is summoned. The account comes due.*

Down in the street, a bell began to ring — not a church bell, not the market bell. The deep, deliberate tolling of the Ministry of Ordinary Affairs, rung only when an inspector carried warrants.

Fenwick closed his eyes. "Too late," he murmured. "Oh, child. It was always going to be too late."
