## 4. Corin Ash, Unremembered

The first thing you learn, when the world stops holding your shape, is that doors are honest in a way people are not.

Doors open or they don't. They don't pretend they've never seen you before. They don't smile with that particular Highmoor politeness—the one that means *I am certain we have spoken, and I am equally certain I cannot say when*—and then slide shut on whatever conversation was beginning to root.

I had counted my doors over the years. It was not a habit I chose; it was simply what remained of a life when everything else had been sanded away. The baker's door on Coriander Lane opened for me exactly once. The second time I stood before it, the baker looked through me as though I were weather—a gray drizzle shaped like a man—and turned back to his ovens. The librarian at the Salt Archive let me return a book I'd borrowed, frowned at it as if it had walked in on its own legs, and shelved it without checking it out again. The ferryman at the Tidewalk didn't even look up. He charged me nothing, which should have been a kindness, but charging nothing is its own kind of forgetting: no coin changes hands, so no memory has anything to hold onto.

Seven years, give or take a season. That's how long I had been drifting through Highmoor like smoke looking for a chimney.

I did not remember arriving. That was the first wrongness, though it took me years to name it. Everyone arrives somewhere. Even the born-and-buried locals could tell you the street of their first steps, the church where their mothers wept over them, the winter their father lost his trade. I had none of it. My past began, as far as I could reconstruct it, on a Tuesday morning outside a chandler's shop, with rain coming down and three coppers in my pocket and no idea whose coppers they had been.

I tried, in those early months, to be remembered on purpose. You would be surprised how much of a person can be built out of sheer insistence. I made myself loud. I learned jokes and told them twice to the same faces—except there were no same faces, that was the trouble. I bought rounds of drinks in taverns where the laughter came warm and immediate and died the moment I left the table. I once carried an old woman's market basket half a mile through the Spindle District while she told me her whole life story, every grandchild's name, the scar her husband earned at the rope-works, and when we reached her door she thanked me kindly, called me "young man," and asked if we'd met before, because I seemed familiar somehow.

*Familiar.* That word became my whole biography. Familiar the way a draft is familiar. Familiar the way a song is familiar when you can't place it and stop trying.

By the third year I stopped insisting. That was the year I understood something worse than being forgotten: being forgotten leaves no room for grief, because grief requires someone to hold it, and no one held mine. Not even me, some days. There were mornings I woke and had to check my own hands to confirm they were still attached to a body, still casting a shadow, still leaving footprints in the frost along the Tidewalk. Some mornings the frost kept my prints only until the next passerby, and I chose to believe that was the cold and not the truth.

You want to know how a man stays sane inside that? I'll tell you the secret, and it's a small and shabby secret: he keeps lists. I kept them in a cheap notebook with a cracked spine, bought from a stationer who forgot me before I reached the door. Every person I'd met more than once—which was to say, every person who had ever failed to remember me—went in the book with the date and the circumstance. It wasn't much of a life, written down. It fit in a coat pocket. But it was proof. If the world wouldn't carry me, I would carry myself, page by page, in ink that faded slower than I did.

And then there was the charm-shop.

* * *

I had passed Sterling & Daughters perhaps forty times before I went in. It sat on Mirefoot Row like an old woman at a funeral—dignified, out of fashion, pretending not to notice the cold. The sign hung by one chain. The window displayed the sad inventory of a dying trade: luck-knots in faded red cord, dried lavender bundles, little tin stars that claimed to ward off nightmares and probably did nothing but catch the light. Above the door, painted letters that had once been gold: *CHARMS, WARMTH, SMALL MERCIES.*

Magic shops in Highmoor were legal the way a candle is legal indoors. The Edicts had gutted the guilds a generation ago—everyone knew the story, everyone repeated it in the careful voice people use for the dead—but a few licensed trinket-sellers had been permitted to keep their doors open, selling warmth and luck to a city too frightened to call it what it was. Most of those doors had closed since. This one hadn't. I noticed things like that. A man nobody remembers becomes a scholar of persistence.

But here is why I finally went in, and I have turned this over a hundred times since, looking for the seam in it: the door opened for me twice.

The first time was a gray afternoon in late autumn. I stepped inside on impulse—I couldn't tell you the impulse; impulses were all I had—and a bell above the frame rang out clear and silver, and a woman behind the counter looked up, and she *saw* me.

I don't mean she perceived me. Everyone perceived me; perception is cheap. I mean she saw me the way you see a person: her eyes caught and stayed, her brow moved a fraction, her mouth began the small mechanics of greeting. She had dark hair pinned up with what I later learned was a pencil, because she could never find her combs. She had ink on her fingers and shadows under her eyes and the particular posture of someone carrying a weight she'd stopped noticing years ago. And when our eyes met, something crossed her face—not recognition, not yet, but interest. Attention. The rarest coin in Highmoor, and she spent it on me without hesitation.

"Looking for anything particular?" she asked.

"No," I said, which was true in ways she couldn't have imagined. "Just looking."

"Well," she said, "look thoroughly. Things in here reward attention."

I remember thinking that was a strange thing for a shopkeeper to say. I remember the smell of the place—rain, mostly, and under it something older, like a cellar full of paper and iron. I remember a child's mitten on the counter, red cord wound around it, and the woman's hands moving over the knot with a tenderness that didn't match her tiredness. And I remember walking out, and the bell ringing behind me, and standing on Mirefoot Row feeling—for the first time in seven years—like a man who had been *somewhere.*

The second time was four days later. I told myself I was going back for practical reasons. I needed a charm against damp, I told myself; my lodgings leaked. I needed a luck-knot; my luck was famously bad. These were lies and I knew they were lies, because what I actually needed was to find out whether the bell would ring again, whether her eyes would catch again, whether the door would do the honest thing doors do—or whether it would treat me like the baker's door, like the library, like every threshold in this city that had quietly agreed I was nobody's business.

I put my hand on the latch and pushed.

The bell rang. Silver, clear, unembarrassed.

And she looked up from her ledger, and her face did the most extraordinary thing: it flickered. Confusion, effort, a reaching—like someone groping for a word on the tip of the tongue. Then her eyes widened, and she said:

"You. Four days ago. You said you were just looking."

I stood in the doorway with rain dripping off my coat and felt something crack open in my chest that I had thought was sealed for good. Because she remembered. Not perfectly—there was strain in it, like a muscle lifting something heavy—but she remembered, and more than that, she was *surprised to have forgotten*, which meant forgetting was abnormal to her, which meant—

"I'm sorry," I managed. "Most people don't."

"Don't what?"

"Remember me. Twice." I heard how it sounded the moment it left my mouth. Mad. Sad. Both. I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "That was a strange thing to say. Forget I said it."

But she was already leaning forward over the counter, studying me with those ink-stained fingers laced together, and I watched her decide something. Watched her choose, deliberately, to take me seriously. "No," she said slowly. "I don't think I will forget you said it. In fact—" and here she smiled, tired and crooked and real, "—I suspect it's the most interesting thing anyone's said in this shop all year."

Her name was Ada. Ada Sterling. She told me it plainly, the way people hand you a key.

* * *

I started coming to the shop the way water finds a low place. Not every day—that would have been suspicious even to me—but often enough that the bell's ring became a sound I trusted. Often enough that Ada began keeping a stool behind the counter, and then a cup for my tea, and then, without ceremony, opinions about my coat, my posture, my habit of hovering near the window display as if the tin stars might bolt.

She never asked directly why no one remembered me. She was too proud for pity and too clever for clumsy questions. Instead she tested, in her quiet way. She sent me on errands—to the apothecary, to the records office, to old Master Fenwick's house with a parcel of charms—and each time I came back she'd ask, casually, how it went, and I'd report the same small apocalypse: blank looks, half-greetings, a world that received me like a letter addressed to no one. Each time, her frown deepened. Each time, she wrote something down after I left. I know because I saw the ledger once, open on the counter, my name in her cramped hand surrounded by notes and dates and small desperate symbols I couldn't read.

My name. That was another strangeness worth recording. People forgot my face, my errands, my existence—but my name stuck in odd corners. The stationer's ledger showed purchases by "C. Ash" going back years, though the stationer himself, shown the entries, had no idea who that was. A landlady I'd paid monthly for two winters kept my rent receipts in a drawer and swore she'd never had a tenant by that name. It was as if the world had agreed to erase the man but couldn't quite bring itself to burn the paperwork. Names are stubborn things. They're cut deeper than faces.

Ada understood that before I said it aloud. One evening, near closing, with fog pressing against the windows like something listening, she looked up from her ledger and said, "Corin Ash." Just that. Testing the weight of it.

"What about it?"

"It doesn't feel forgotten," she said. "It feels *filed*. Like something put away rather than erased. There's a difference." She tapped the counter with one ink-stained finger. "Erasure is violence. Filing is procedure. Somebody did this to you with paperwork, Corin Ash. I'd bet the shop on it."

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to tell her she'd been breathing lamp-oil fumes too long. But standing there in the smell of rain and old paper, with the fog leaning in and her eyes steady on mine, I felt the truth of it settle over me like the coat I wore: somebody had done this. Deliberately. With procedure. Which meant it could be undone, or at least explained, and either of those was more than I'd dared hope for in seven years.

"Why does it matter to you?" I asked her. It came out rougher than I intended. "You run a shop. You sell warmth to people who've forgotten warmth exists. Why spend your evenings on a man nobody else can see?"

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, a city inspector's boots rang past on the cobbles, and I watched her whole body go still until the sound faded. When she spoke again, her voice was lower.

"Because I know what it is to keep a door open that everyone wants closed," she said. "Because this shop is invisible too, if you squint. Half this city walks past it and sees nothing but clutter. And because—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide, again, to take me seriously, "—because when you walked in that first day, you looked at my shelves like they mattered. Like the things in them were real. Nobody looks at this shop like that anymore, Corin. Not even the people who buy from it. So maybe we're both in the business of being unseen. Maybe that makes us colleagues."

Colleagues. I turned the word over all the way home, through streets that ignored me, past windows that reflected a smudge where a man should be. Colleagues. It was such a small thing to be given. It was the largest thing I'd been given in seven years.

That night I took out my cracked notebook and, for the first time, wrote something other than a record of being forgotten. I wrote: *Sterling & Daughters, Mirefoot Row. The door opens twice. The bell rings. She remembers.*

Underneath, after a long pause, I added: *Find out why.*

* * *

I wish I could say I slept well that night. Instead I dreamed—properly dreamed, in color, which almost never happened to me—of a stairway going down beneath the city, of stones carved with names, of a great ledger lying open in the dark with pages turning on their own. In the dream, a woman's voice read my name aloud from those pages, and the reading of it felt less like introduction and more like sentencing.

I woke before dawn with the notebook clutched to my chest and one sentence sitting in my mind like a stone dropped in still water, rippling outward, disturbing everything:

*If my name is written somewhere, then someone wrote it—and whoever holds the pen decides what happens next.*

I dressed in the dark. I told myself I was going to the shop early just to help her open up, just to see her face in the gray morning light, just to sit on my stool and drink tea from my cup and be, for one hour, a man in a room where he existed.

All of that was true. None of it was the whole truth. The whole truth was that I had seen the ledger on her counter—her grandmother's ledger, she'd called it, the forbidden one she kept beneath the floorboards—and I had seen my own name in her eyes when she looked at me that last evening, and it had not been only kindness there. It had been fear.

When I reached Mirefoot Row, the fog was thick enough to drink, and the shop's single lit window burned through it like a held breath. The door was unlocked. The bell rang.

Ada was standing behind the counter with the floorboard pried up beside her feet and an old book open in her hands, and when she looked up at me, her face was white as the fog outside.

"Corin," she said, and my name in her mouth sounded nothing like a greeting. It sounded like an apology. "There's something you need to see. It's your face. In my grandmother's handwriting. Beside tomorrow's date."
