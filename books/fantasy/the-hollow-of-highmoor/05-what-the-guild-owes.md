## 5. What the Guild Owes

Fenwick's house sat at the end of Candlemaker's Row like a tooth that had outlived its neighbors. Once the whole street had belonged to the Guild of Small Warmths—six shops with brass signs and blue-painted doors, each one humming faintly with the kind of magic that didn't frighten anyone: charms against chilblains, blessings for sourdough, knots that kept nightmares from the doors of children. Now five of the six were ordinary businesses or empty shells, and only Fenwick's remained, kept by a man who had stopped practicing forty years ago and never quite explained why.

Ada stood on his step in the gray light of morning, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat, pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She had not slept.

She had tried. She had sat up with a candle burning low and Corin Ash's name swimming in her vision until she could no longer tell whether the letters said *Corin* or *sinner*, and then she had blown out the flame and sat in the dark instead, listening to the shop settle around her the way old animals settle—creaking, sighing, keeping watch. Somewhere past midnight she had understood that she would not sleep again until she heard it from someone who'd been there when it was done.

There was exactly one such person left.

The door opened before she knocked twice. Master Fenwick peered out through spectacles thick as bottle-bottoms, and Ada watched the recognition cross his face—and watched something else cross behind it, quick as a fish under ice. Fear.

"Miss Sterling." He stepped back to let her in, which he would not have done if she were any other visitor. "You look like your grandmother."

"People keep telling me that."

"They mean it kindly." He closed the door and shot three bolts, top to bottom, an old habit from the years when inspectors came without appointments. "Mostly."

* * *

The inside of the house smelled of dust and camphor and, beneath both, of something older—the dry mineral scent of a place where nothing living had been welcome for a long time. Fenwick led her through a hall lined with portraits of dead guildmasters, their gilt frames gone dull, their painted eyes following her with the particular patience of people who already knew how every story ended. Her grandmother hung fourth from the stairs. In the portrait she looked severe, unyielding, a woman carved from the same stone as Highmoor itself.

Ada remembered her differently: laughing over a pot of tea, showing a seven-year-old Ada how to coax warmth into a copper coin, saying *the guild survives because we are small, little wren, because we ask for nothing and give warmth away*. She had believed it with her whole child's heart.

"Sit," Fenwick said, in a parlor where the furniture wore sheets except for two chairs, as though the room were a corpse being kept decent. He did not offer tea. That, more than anything, told her he knew why she'd come.

She unwrapped the ledger and set it on the table between them.

Fenwick looked at it the way men look at snakes. He made no move toward it.

"You went under the floorboards," he said.

"I've been going under the floorboards since I inherited the shop. I just never learned to read what was down there until now." Her voice held steady. She was almost proud of it. "One soul per generation, Master Fenwick. Sold below. Freely given—by the guildhead. That's the cipher, isn't it? All those years of entries, all those names in my grandmother's hand. And the newest one—" She turned the pages with fingers that finally, now, trembled. "Corin Ash. Entered eleven moons ago. Thirteen moons to the turn."

The clock in the corner ticked into the silence like someone tapping a fingernail on glass.

"Say it's not true," Ada said. "Lie to me. You're old enough to be good at it."

* * *

For a long moment Fenwick simply breathed. Then he rose, crossed to the sideboard, and poured two glasses of plum brandy from a bottle that must have been laid down before the Purge. His hands shook only slightly. He set one glass in front of her and drank half of his own in a single swallow, and Ada thought: *he has been waiting thirty years for someone to make him do this.*

"It's true," he said.

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well—one, then another, then another, each striking deeper water.

"The guildhead gives freely," he went on, as though reciting a catechism he'd memorized so long ago the meaning had worn smooth. "That is the terms. It must be given, not taken. The court below will accept nothing coerced—that's not mercy on their part, mind you, it's bookkeeping. A soul sold unwilling sours the ledger. Sours the whole contract." He refilled his glass. "Your grandmother gave. As did her mother before her, and hers before that. Six generations. One soul each, at the guildhead's choosing, in exchange for—"

"In exchange for what, exactly?" Ada's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it. "I've read the entries. Names, dates, payments received. But there's no line that says what we got. There's no invoice, Fenwick. What did we buy with a human being?"

He flinched at her use of his name without the honorific. Good, she thought viciously. Feel it.

"Warmth," he said quietly. "We bought warmth."

* * *

"When the Purge came," Fenwick said, settling back into his chair with the groan of an old man and the weariness of something much heavier, "they burned the great houses first. The Weatherwrights. The Deepbinders. Anyone whose craft could topple walls or boil rivers. The Court of Inspection called it the Cull, and they were thorough—they took nine hundred practitioners in the first year alone. Nine hundred, Miss Sterling. Do you know what happened to them?"

"Everyone knows. The pyres on Gallow Hill."

"The pyres were the merciful ones." He smiled without humor. "Most were sent below. To the Hollow Court, as tribute—because the magistrates below had struck their own bargain with the new government above. The court would take the gifted, and in return the court would stay buried, quiet, and let the city pretend magic had died. And the guilds that cooperated—" he spread his hands, "—were permitted to continue. Small crafts. Trinkets. Nothing that could frighten anyone. Provided we paid our tithe."

"One soul per generation."

"Freely given by the guildhead. It was written into the Charter of Continuance. Your grandmother signed it with the others—all six surviving guildheads, in the cellar of the Assay Office, while the smoke from Gallow Hill still darkened the sky." His eyes went distant. "I was her apprentice. Nineteen years old. I stood outside that door and waited, and when she came out she was white as paper, and she never spoke of it. Not once, in forty years. But she paid. Every generation, she paid, and the guild survived, and the shop stayed warm, and no inspector ever touched us because we were worth more to everyone intact than burned."

Ada stared at him. The parlor seemed to tilt, very slowly, the way a ship tilts when it begins to take water somewhere below the waterline.

"She chose them," Ada said. "The ones who were sent below. She *chose* them."

"The guildhead chooses. Those are the terms."

"Who? Who did she choose, Fenwick? Tell me the names."

He looked at the ledger, and then away from it, and his throat worked.

"Read them yourself. You have the cipher."

"I'm asking *you*. I want to hear it from a mouth, not a page."

* * *

And he told her.

He named them one by one, and each name fell like a clod of earth onto a coffin. A fiddler called Tam Broadstairs, who played at guild festivals and had a laugh you could warm your hands on. Two sisters from the Rivergate, hedge-witches, who mended nets and broken hearts and asked nothing for either. An old man named Hollis who taught letters to slum children and had once, rumor said, been able to speak with birds. And others—a dozen across the decades, each entry in the ledger matching a face that Ada realized, with dawning horror, she had grown up hearing about as *gone away*, *moved on*, *lost to the fever year*.

Her grandmother had lied to her. Kindly, gently, consistently, for the whole of Ada's childhood—with stories and shrugs and the particular softness adults use when they believe a child cannot bear the truth. *They moved on, little wren.* *Highmoor is hard on people.* And all the while the ledger lay beneath the floorboards of the very room where they drank tea together, recording exactly where they had gone and who had put them on the cart.

"The last one," Fenwick said, and his voice had gone very thin. "Eleven months ago. Your grandmother was dying—her heart, the doctors said, though we both know guildmasters don't die of anything so simple. She knew the tithe was due within the year. She knew that if she died unpaid, the contract lapsed, and a lapsed contract means the court collects however it likes. No choosing. No fairness. They'd have come up through the cellars of every guildhouse and taken whoever they pleased, and taken the guild's charter with them, and there would have been inspections, and fires, and—" He stopped. Drank. "So she chose quickly. A young man. Unattached, she wrote in her notes. Unremembered by design—he'd made a bargain of his own years before, as a boy, and the court already had a claim on his name. She reasoned—she *reasoned*, Miss Sterling, God forgive us all—that taking a soul already half-claimed was a kindness. Less stolen than borrowed."

"Corin Ash," Ada said.

"Corin Ash." Fenwick set down his glass with exaggerated care, as if afraid that any sudden movement might shatter more than glass. "Your grandmother died four days after signing him over. Peaceful. Comfortable. In her bed, in a warm house, with the guild's future secured." He met Ada's eyes at last, and behind the cataract-fog of his spectacles she saw something that looked unbearably like relief—as though confessing had finally let him set down a weight he'd carried so long it had grown into his spine. "Now you know. Now you carry it too. That's what inheritance *is*, girl. Not the shop. Not the sign over the door. The debt."

* * *

Ada stood. Her knees felt strange, unjointed, as though her body had been reassembled slightly wrong while she wasn't looking.

"Let it stand," Fenwick said, and now he leaned forward, urgent, both hands flat on the table. "Listen to me. You are the guildhead now—the last guildhead, the only one, God help us both. The contract passes through you. If you pay nothing, the court comes collecting on its own terms, and I promise you, I have seen what that means—I saw it in 'forty-one, when the Chandlers' Guild defaulted. Three days later their whole row was condemned for 'structural failure,' and fourteen people were never seen again, and the inspectors filed reports full of lies that everyone signed because signing was safer than asking. Is that what you want? Is that what you want for your shop, your street, your city?"

"What I want," Ada said slowly, "is for Corin Ash to walk out of whatever hole they keep him in, alive, free, and whole."

"He can't. There's no *out*. That's the entire architecture of the bargain—it only works because it's final. Your grandmother knew that. She wept over it, if you want the truth; I found her once, late at night, sitting on the floor of the shop with the ledger open on her knees, crying like the world was ending. And then she dried her face and went back to work the next morning, because that is what guildmasters *do*. We carry what must be carried." His voice dropped to something almost tender, which was somehow worse than everything else he'd said. "Let it stand, Ada. Mourn him, if you must—mourn him properly, put a candle in the window for him—but let it stand. Thirteen moons is long enough to learn to live beside a thing like this. I've done it for forty years. You're younger. You'll manage faster."

"You've managed," Ada repeated.

"I have."

"And what does managing feel like?"

For the first time since she'd arrived, Fenwick had nothing prepared. His mouth opened and closed. Behind his spectacles his eyes swam, and Ada understood suddenly that she was looking at a man who had spent four decades performing survival so convincingly that he'd fooled even himself—and that some locked room inside him had been waiting all this time for someone to ask exactly this question and hold the door shut behind it.

"It feels," he whispered, "like being warm in a cold house and knowing which room the firewood came from."

* * *

Ada picked up the ledger. Wrapped it in its oilcloth, slow and deliberate, giving her hands something honest to do while the rest of her decided what it believed.

"Fenwick," she said. "When the moon turns—when the thirteenth turn comes—what happens to him? Exactly. What does the court do with a soul it owns outright?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him go gray.

"You're going down there," he said. Not a question. "Oh, girl. Oh, you foolish, brave, *doomed* girl. Nobody goes down there. The stair doesn't open for the living. The ferryman doesn't carry the willing. The magistrates eat yesterday, Ada—they consume memory like bread, and what walks out of that court, if anything ever has, walks out hollowed. You'd trade yourself for him and gain nothing but a second name in the ledger."

"That's not what I asked."

He sank back. The clock ticked. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past on Candlemaker's Row, an ordinary sound in an ordinary morning, and Ada marveled distantly that the world could keep making sounds like that while underneath it all this other machinery turned.

"On the thirteenth turn," Fenwick said at last, so quietly she had to lean in to catch it, "the claim completes. The soul becomes court property entire—not servant, not prisoner, *property*. Assimilated. They'll render him down into evidence. Memory, name, face, all of it filed away in their archive to be weighed and spent as the magistrates see fit. What remains after that—" He made a small, helpless gesture. "There is no *after* that. That's the end of the entry. Every name in that book ends the same way, Ada. Thirteenth turn. Assimilation. Closure."

"Closure," she echoed.

"It's the legal term."

"Of course it is." She tucked the ledger under her arm and walked to the door. At the threshold she paused, her hand on the bolt, and looked back at the old man sitting alone among his sheeted furniture with his brandy and his forty managed years.

"Thank you for telling me the truth," she said. "It cost you something. I know it did."

"Don't do this," Fenwick said. "Ada. Don't go down there. Let it stand. Please. I am begging you as the last friend your grandmother had—let it stand, and in twenty years you'll thank me, and in forty you'll be me."

She looked at him—at the fear and the shame and the terrible, threadbare respectability of him—and she thought of her grandmother weeping on the floor of the shop, drying her face, going back to work. Carrying what must be carried.

"No," Ada said. "In forty years I intend to be something else entirely."

She opened the door onto the fog-bound street, and behind her Fenwick said one more thing, so softly the fog nearly took it before it reached her:

"There's a record of the original sale in the court's archive. Your grandmother's copy of the contract. If you truly mean to do this madness, find it—it's the only document down there with a binding clause loose enough to drive a knife through. She hid it well, but she always meant—" His voice caught. "She always meant for the last guildmaster to know everything."

Ada stepped out into the gray morning, the ledger heavy under her arm, thirteen moons minus one day ticking away beneath the mountain, and began walking home the long way—past the aqueduct, where the fog pooled thickest, and where, she noticed for the first time, an old sealed archway sat half-drowned in ivy at the base of the eastern pier.

Someone had scratched a mark into its stone. Small, recent, deliberate.

It was the guild's mark: the knot of seven warmths.

And it was fresh.
