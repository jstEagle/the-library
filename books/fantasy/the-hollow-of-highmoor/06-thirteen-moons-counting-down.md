## 6. Thirteen Moons, Counting Down

*Ada*

Three nights after the ledger told her the truth, Ada still hadn't slept.

She sat in the back room of Sterling Charms with every lamp lit, which was itself a confession—she never burned more than two wicks, because oil cost coin and coin kept the roof patched and the guild's name on its last surviving charter. But tonight the shadows under the shelves seemed to lean in and listen, so she drowned them in light.

The Hollow Prophecies lay open on the table before her, weighted at both corners with candlesticks. Her grandmother's cipher had come apart easier than she'd expected, once she understood the key. That was the cruelest part. All those years of squinting at the cramped script, believing it was inventory records, supplier lists, prices paid for moonwort and grave-iron—and it had been nothing but arithmetic. One soul per generation. Dates received. Dates due. A column of names written in a hand that trembled only once.

Corin Ash. Due: the thirteenth turn of the hunter's moon.

She'd counted on her fingers like a child learning sums. Eleven weeks. Perhaps ten, depending on how the almanac fell. She had ten weeks to break a contract older than she was, signed by a woman who had been dead fourteen years and could not be asked why.

The shop bell chimed.

Ada's hand went to the ledger before her hand went to anything else—an instinct she hated herself for, slamming the cover as though Corin couldn't have read it anyway, couldn't have read his own name in a stranger's careful columns. He stood in the doorway between shop and back room, hat in his hands, rain still beading on his shoulders. He always looked faintly wet, she was learning. As if the city never quite let him dry.

"You left your shutter half-latched," he said. "Anyone could see the lamps."

"Let them see."

"That's not what you've spent your life doing." He said it without judgment, which made it worse. He set something on the table beside the candlestick: a folded paper, soft with handling. "I went to the Hall of Records today. The public wing."

"And?"

"And they've never heard of me." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man reporting weather. "No birth entry. No tax roll. I checked forty years back. There's a gap in the census where my street should be—the whole street, Ada. Not just me. Everyone who lived there, gone from the record, as if the ink dried wrong and someone scraped it clean."

Ada stared at him. "An entire street."

"Rookery Lane." He unfolded the paper. It was a rubbing he'd taken from a memorial stone, charcoal on cheap stock, showing names of plague dead from sixty years ago. Halfway down, the surface went smooth. Unengraved. A blank stretch of stone exactly wide enough for nine names. "The Hollow Court doesn't take people," he said quietly. "It takes the fact that they were ever here."

The lamps guttered, all six at once, though no door had opened. Ada pressed her palms flat against the Prophecies and felt the pages hum against the wood like a struck string.

"The sealed archives," she said. "The Guild kept keys to them, back when we were more than a charm-shop. Grandmother's ring has an iron key I never found a lock for."

"I know a lock," Corin said. "Under the Hall of Records. I've walked past it every winter for eleven years. I never knew why I stopped. Now I think maybe part of me remembered."

Ada looked at him—at this man the world kept forgetting, standing in her lamplight because hers was the only door that opened twice—and felt something in her chest go hard and bright, like a coal blown clear of ash.

"Tomorrow night," she said. "Bring the rubbing. And Corin—" she closed the ledger gently now, the way you'd close a coffin, "—whatever we find down there, you should know I'm not doing this out of guilt alone. Guilt is heavy but it doesn't steer. I'm doing it because it's *right*."

He nodded. But as he turned to go, he paused at the threshold, silhouetted against the dark shopfront, and said, "The thirteenth turn. What happens then? You read further than I did."

Ada had read further. She wished she hadn't.

"It says," she said, "that on the turn, the debt *matures*. The soul stops being borrowed." She swallowed. "It becomes court property. Forever. No bargain after. No appeal. The magistrates keep what they own."

The bell over the front door stirred, though no one had touched it. Corin stood very still in the doorway, and for the first time since she'd known him, Ada saw fear move through him like water through cloth—and then saw him fold it up small and put it away, the way men do when they've had long practice having nothing to lose.

"Then we have ten weeks," he said, "to steal a man from a court of law."

"To steal him from *ownership*," Ada corrected. "Law is just what they call their ledgers."

He smiled again—different, this time—and let himself out into the fog.

*

*Corin*

The Hall of Records at midnight was a cathedral built to paperwork.

Corin had been inside once before, years ago, in daylight, trying to find himself among the rolls. He'd been escorted out within the hour—not thrown out, simply *forgotten out*, the clerks losing track of him mid-sentence until he wandered the aisles unseen. Tonight, with Ada's iron key warm in his pocket and Ada herself a shadow at his shoulder, the building felt different. Attended. Awake.

The lock was set into a floor panel behind the genealogy stacks, iron gone green with age, shaped like a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail. Ada knelt and fit the key, and Corin heard something below the floor shift its weight—the sound of a very old mechanism, or a very old thing pretending to be one.

"You're certain about the inspector?" Ada murmured. "Vael walks this district at night. I've seen his lamp."

"He walks where people are. Nobody's down here but us and the dead files." Corin glanced at her. "You're afraid of him."

"I'm afraid of what he represents. There's a difference." The lock clicked. "Vael isn't cruel. Cruel men are simple. Vael is *thorough*, and thorough men eventually arrive at the truth, and his truth ends with my shop boarded and me in the stocks. We have ten weeks, and one of his constables already marked my door."

The panel swung down onto darkness and cold air that smelled of stone dust and, faintly, of rain—the same rain-smell as the shop, which made the hairs on Corin's arms lift. Some magics were kin to each other across centuries.

They descended by Ada's lamp. The stair spiraled deeper than any cellar had a right to, past shelves carved into living rock, and the archive opened around them like the inside of a lung: vaulted caverns, corridors of pigeonholes, and documents everywhere—hanging in bundles from hooks, stacked in drifts, bound in chains. Sealed archives. The Guild's inheritance, abandoned when magic was outlawed, and no one had thought to burn it because no one alive could read most of it.

Except, apparently, grandmothers.

"Here." Ada's lamp found a shelf near the stair's foot: *COURT OF THE HOLLOW — TREATIES AND TERM SHEETS.* She pulled a folio thick as a brick and laid it open on a reading stand furred with dust. "We want the terms of sale. What the contract actually does."

Corin read over her shoulder. The script was old Highmoor cursive, but the language was plain enough—plainness being the most frightening register a document can speak.

*"Item: one soul, freely rendered, per generation, in perpetuity of arrangement. The rendered soul remains in circulation among the living until maturity of term. Upon the thirteenth turning of the appointed moon, ownership vests absolutely in the Court. Thereafter the soul shall serve as record, evidence, and memory of the Court, in perpetuity, without remainder."*

"In perpetuity, without remainder," Corin repeated. The words tasted like well water—cold and mineral and old. "Do you understand what that means?"

"I think so." Ada's voice had gone carefully flat, the voice she used with inspectors. "You don't die. You become part of the court's *archive*. They eat memories down there. Evidence, Wren called it—or no, that hasn't happened yet." She caught herself; he watched her do the arithmetic of things not yet said. "The point is, they don't keep souls in cages. They keep them the way we keep paper. Filed. Consulted. Worn smooth by handling."

"Then burning the contract—"

"—would free you, yes. If we can. Keep reading. There's a schedule of moons."

Corin turned pages until he found it: a table of lunar reckonings going back a hundred and twenty years, each sold soul given a row. Names he didn't know, dates of rendering, dates of maturity. And then, in the current century's section, fresh ink:

*Rookery Lane renderings (9): matured or maturing.*
*Current outstanding: C. ASH. Maturity: 13th turn, Hunter's Moon.*

Nine names above his. An entire street's worth of souls, all matured, all filed away somewhere beneath the mountain, worn smooth by handling. He thought of the blank space on the memorial stone and had to put a hand on the reading stand to steady himself.

"Ada. The plague bargain. My village—I remembered it wrong, or partially. Read the marginal note."

She bent closer. Her lips moved through the faded script, and Corin watched the color leave her face by lamplight.

"'Note,'" she read slowly. "'The Rookery renderings were collective—nine souls pledged jointly against the plague's lifting. Eight matured and vest. The ninth, being youngest, was granted extended term: thirteen turns from the date of this writing, that the pledge might be renewed by the guild of the next generation rather than lapse.'" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "'Renewal requires a freely-giving seller of the guild line. Failing renewal, the outstanding soul vests at maturity.'"

"So either they sell someone new," Corin said, "or they get me anyway."

"There's a third option. It lapses. The whole arrangement dies unfulfilled." Ada looked up, and her eyes were fierce in the lamp glow. "Which is what I intend. But Corin—there's more. Underneath. In her hand. Grandmother's."

She turned the folio to the last leaf, and there it was, in the same trembling-steady script as the Prophecies upstairs—a letter, unsigned, wedged between the treaty pages as if she'd hidden it from her own descendants and her own conscience at once.

*To whoever inherits this weight:*

*I have done a terrible arithmetic and I pray you never check my sums. The Court came to me in the famine year and offered terms. Our stores were empty. The inspectors were at our backs. I gave them a name from the list of the plague-orphaned, a boy with no family to mourn him, and I told myself it was mercy twice over—that he would live comfortable somewhere below, and the guild would live at all.*

*I have since learned there is no comfort below. Only filing.*

*The Court requires a successor. When the moon turns, they will come to collect not the boy but the office. The seller's office. It passes to the head of the guild, and the head of the guild is you.*

*I am sorry. I chose the guild over a child, and I would choose it again, and that is the most damnable sentence I have ever written.*

*Burn this after reading. Or better—burn the book I couldn't burn. Find the master prophecy. Everything else is only copies.*

Ada's hands shook. The lamp flame leaned toward the page as if it too wanted to read.

"She named the office," Corin said softly. "Not just me. *You.* The prophecy upstairs—it wasn't recording your future, Ada. It was invoicing it."

"The next seller." Ada laughed, a single dry note with no humor anywhere in it. "All these years I thought I inherited a shop. A legacy. Turns out I inherited a *debt position*. Grandmother signed us into perpetuity and the interest comes due on the hunter's moon—with me as signatory whether I've signed anything or not."

"They'll hold you to it?"

"They held *her* to it. She believed she saved the guild. Belief is all the consent they need—that's what 'freely rendered' means, I'd wager. Not freely chosen. Freely *believed*." She straightened, and Corin saw the transformation he'd already begun to expect from her: fear gathering itself into purpose, the way fog gathers into rain. "Then we don't renew, and we don't let it vest. We make it lapse. And if the only way to make a hundred-year contract lapse is to burn the original—"

"Then we find the master prophecy." Corin closed the folio. Somewhere far above them, muffled by stone, a bell rang the second hour. "Down there. With the magistrates. On their ground, under their law, ten weeks from tonight."

"Yes." Ada took the letter, folded it into her bodice over her heart like armor. "And Corin—we tell no one. Not Fenwick, not the remnant cells, no one. Every person who knows becomes a witness the Court can reach. From tonight it's just us and the clock."

"The clock," Corin agreed.

They climbed back toward the world, and at the top of the stair, before the panel closed, Corin looked back down into the dark archive—at the drifting dust, the chained folios, the nine smooth names on a plague stone waiting somewhere in the black to be remembered.

Ten weeks.

For the first time in eleven years of being forgotten by everyone, Corin Ash had a deadline. It felt, strangely, like being alive.
