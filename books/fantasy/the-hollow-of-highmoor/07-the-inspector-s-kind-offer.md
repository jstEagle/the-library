## 7. The Inspector's Kind Offer

Inspector Corvus Vael had been watching the charm-shop on Lantern Row for six years, and in all that time he had never once seen anything worth arresting anyone for.

That was the trouble with it.

He sat across the street in the alcove of a shuttered cobbler's stall, his coat buttoned to the throat against the fog, and watched the shop the way other men watched a river—patiently, knowing that eventually it would give up what it carried. The sign above the door read *Sterling Charms & Small Wonders* in paint so faded it looked like a scar. In the window, arranged with deliberate artlessness, sat the inventory: luck-knots braided from river grass, warming stones the color of hearth-ash, little glass bells that were supposed to hold off nightmares. All of it, by law, inert. All of it, by law, nothing but craft and superstition and the sentimental residue of a magic Highmoor had outlawed a generation ago.

And yet.

Vael had spent twenty-two years as an inspector of the Bureau of Unlicensed Practices, and he had learned that outlawed things did not vanish when outlawed. They went quiet. They learned to breathe shallowly. They moved into shops on narrow streets and put on the costume of trinkets, and they waited for the world above them to forget why they had ever been feared.

He watched a woman hurry out of the shop with her collar up—a laundress, by the look of her hands—and he watched her stop three doors down, unfold something she'd bought, press it briefly to her lips like a prayer, and walk on with her shoulders a half-inch straighter than they had been before.

Small wonders. That was what the girl called them.

Vael did not believe in small wonders. He believed in ledgers. And somewhere beneath that shop, behind some wall or under some floorboard, there was a ledger, and every entry in it was a violation of the Edict of Severance, and Ada Sterling knew it, and he knew she knew it, and between those two facts stretched six years of nothing happening at all.

He checked his pocket watch. Half past four. The inspector who would relieve him wouldn't come until dark; Vael had long ago stopped waiting for relief. This watch was his own appetite. His superiors thought his fixation on one small shop was diligence. Let them think it. Diligence was a respectable word for what he actually felt, which was closer to hunger, though he would never have said so aloud, because Vael was a man who kept his hungers where he kept everything else: filed, cross-referenced, and locked away.

The bell above the shop door chimed. A boy came out—the frightened kind, maybe ten, sleeves too short, the particular hollow-eyed look of a child carrying something home that he couldn't show anyone. Vael had seen a hundred such children over the years. They came to Lantern Row because their mothers had told them about luck-knots the way you tell a child about saints, quietly, against the law, and the children grew up believing there was a warmth in the world that the city could not confiscate.

In another life, Vael might have been one of them.

He pushed that thought into its drawer and crossed the street.

* * *

The bell chimed again as he entered, and Ada Sterling looked up from her counter, and he watched the exact moment she recognized him. It was a good performance, if a performance it was—no flinch, no freeze, just the mild wariness any shopkeeper shows a uniformed official. Her hands kept sorting charms. Only her eyes changed, and only slightly: the way a deer's eyes change when the wind shifts.

"Inspector." She said it evenly. "We're nearly closed."

"I can see that." He let the door swing shut behind him. The shop smelled of rain and dried herbs and something faintly sweet underneath, like honey left too long near a candle. "I won't keep you. I have a few questions. Routine."

"You said that last time. And the time before."

"And it was true both times." He took off his hat, which was not required, and set it on the counter, which was definitely not required, and watched her notice both. "Miss Sterling. May I call you Miss Sterling?"

"You may call me whatever you like. You will anyway."

Something flickered across his face that in a less disciplined man would have been a smile. "Your grandmother ran this shop for thirty years. Did you know I visited it twice while she was alive? Twice, and found nothing. Then she died, and the ledger of licensed goods passed to you, and I've come"—he consulted a notebook he didn't need to consult—"eleven times since."

"That does sound routine."

"The Bureau has a new directive." He turned the hat brim slowly under one finger. "There are remnant guilds in the lower quarters. Working circles. Nothing charming about them—they're doing real workings, unlicensed ones, and two nights ago a house on Cindervane Street caught fire from the inside out. No flame damage on anything within a foot of the walls. You understand what that means?"

"It means someone was careless."

"It means someone was *practicing*, Miss Sterling. Badly. And people died." He leaned forward. "The Bureau is offering immunity. Full immunity, backdated five years, for any guild-affiliated party willing to name names. Addresses. Meeting times. The old hand-signs. We're going through the remnants one by one, and yours is the oldest line still standing in Highmoor. Which means your grandmother taught you things. Names. Where the others went to ground after the Severance."

Ada set down the charm she was holding. For a moment she looked at him—not at the inspector, but at him, at the tired man under the coat—and Vael felt, uncomfortably, seen.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to hear the terms. You're asking me to sell people." She folded her hands on the counter, and her voice stayed level, but he could hear the steel coming up under it like a blade rising through water. "People who trusted my grandmother, whose grandmothers trusted hers. You're asking me to trade their doors for mine. No."

"They'll be found anyway," Vael said quietly. "That's not a threat, it's arithmetic. The directive is funded. There are more inspectors now. The fire on Cindervane made certain people upstairs very frightened, and frightened people fund things." He paused. "You could be safe. Genuinely safe. Licensed, even, eventually, if the political weather turns. Your shop could become what it pretends to be."

"My shop is already what it is."

"Then we understand each other poorly, because from where I stand, your shop is an open question I have been asking for six years, and tonight I offered to close it gently, and you refused."

"From where I stand," Ada said, "you just asked me to burn down every warm thing left in this city, and dressed it as kindness. So yes. We understand each other poorly."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere above the ceiling a beam ticked, settling. Vael became aware of the smell again—that sweetness under the herbs—and of the way the light from the window seemed to pool around the counter rather than simply fall on it. Six years of watching, and the shop had never once done anything in front of him. But standing inside it, he felt the way he imagined a diver feels at the moment the surface closes overhead: pressure, and depth, and the certainty that the bottom was farther down than anyone admitted.

He picked up his hat.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it more than he intended to.

"So am I," said Ada.

He stepped out onto the step, turned, and from his coat pocket drew a small tin of marking chalk—Bureau issue, gray-white, the color of official attention—and drew on the doorframe, at eye height, a single vertical stroke.

One mark. First warning. Every inspector in Highmoor would know it. Every neighbor would too.

Behind him, through the glass, he heard Ada Sterling exhale, slow and steady, the breath of someone setting her jaw.

* * *

He should have gone home. Home was two rooms above a countinghouse on Wren Street, cold, tidy, and full of files. Instead Vael walked the long way, down through the lantern district, past the aqueduct with its dry stone arches, past the mouth of the old stair that the city had sealed a decade ago and everyone pretended not to see anymore. The fog came up thick off the low streets, and the lamps burned halos, and Highmoor looked the way it always looked at dusk: like a city holding its breath over something buried.

His brother's face surfaced, the way it did at dusk, uninvited.

Tobias. Gone forty years this autumn, taken in the last sweep before the Edict hardened into habit—taken, their mother was told, for "questioning regarding unlicensed practices," and never returned, and written into no record anywhere, because the men who took him worked for the Bureau's first director, and the first director believed records were how rebellions survived. Their mother had gone to her grave still setting a place at the table. Vael had joined the Bureau at nineteen with a single private purpose, and twenty-two years later he had searched every archive, every sealed vault, every confiscated ledger, and had never found so much as a line in Tobias's handwriting.

Some nights he told himself his brother was dead and the not-knowing was mercy. Other nights—the foggy ones—he wondered if the Bureau hadn't simply eaten him the way institutions eat people: completely, and then forgot the meal.

He stopped at the corner of Lantern Row, looking back at the shop. One gray mark on the doorframe. In a week, a second inspector would add a second stroke. In a month, a third. Three strokes and the Bureau could seize the premises without further process. It was clean. It was lawful. It was, by every measure he served, the correct action.

So why did his chest feel like the space where a name used to be?

Because the girl had looked at him like a person, that was why. Because when she'd said *you're asking me to sell people*, something in him had stood up and agreed with her, loudly, in a voice he'd spent two decades locking away. Because he had spent twenty-two years hunting remnant magicians for the crime of keeping warm in a cold city, and somewhere in all that hunting he had stopped being able to say whether he was avenging his brother or becoming the men who took him.

Vael turned up his collar and walked on into the fog.

Behind him, on Lantern Row, the lamp above the charm-shop guttered once and steadied, and if he had looked back—which he deliberately did not—he might have seen, in the window among the trinkets, a small glass bell turning slowly on its string with no wind at all, ringing nothing, warning of everything.

He didn't look. But he marked the date in his notebook anyway, and beside it, without quite deciding to, he wrote two words in his tight inspector's hand:

*She refused.*

And below that, smaller, almost hidden:

*Why do I want her to be right?*

Three days later, the second stroke would go on the door, and the clock would begin in earnest. But that night, Inspector Corvus Vael stood alone in the fog on Wren Street, outside his own cold rooms, and understood—with the clarity that comes only to men standing on the wrong side of their own lives—that the Bureau had sent him to break a shopkeeper, and instead he had just met the only honest person in Highmoor.

And that the moon overhead, fat and pale through the murk, was already counting.
