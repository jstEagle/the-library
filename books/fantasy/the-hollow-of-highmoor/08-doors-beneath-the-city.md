## 8. Doors Beneath the City

I have dreamed of water running through stone for eleven years.

Not rain. Rain is honest—it falls on everyone the same, and it never asks your name. What I dreamed was older than rain: water moving in a channel cut by hands, sliding beneath the city like a vein beneath skin, and somewhere along that vein a door that knew me before I knew myself.

In the dream I always stood at the same place. A stair going down into blackness that wasn't empty but *waiting*. And a voice—not a voice so much as a pressure behind my ears, like depth in water—saying a word over and over until I understood it was my own name being read back to me by something patient as geology.

*Corin Ash.*

*Corin Ash.*

*Corin Ash.*

Ada didn't believe me at first. Or rather—she believed me too quickly, which frightened her more. She'd spent three days with the ledger open on her worktable, her grandmother's cipher half-broken, and every time she looked up at me there was arithmetic happening behind her eyes that she hated. Thirteen moons. One soul. Her guild's warm hearth paid for with my cold wandering.

"We go to the aqueduct," she said finally, closing the book. "The old one, north of Tanner's Row. The Prophecies mention 'the vein' twice, and if the Hollow Court takes its tithe from below, there has to be a way down that isn't through the front door of a magistrate."

"There's a front door?"

"Everything has a front door," Ada said grimly. "That doesn't mean anyone living should use it."

So we went at dusk, when Highmoor's fog comes down thickest off the mountain and the gas-lamps burn halos instead of circles, when even the inspectors stay indoors and drink tea they've charmed to stay hot because the law frowns on warming spells but says nothing about warm cups.

* * *

The old aqueduct runs out of the foothills like a spine laid across the city's northern shoulder. Three arches still stand aboveground where it crosses the ravine at Gildersleeve's Cut; the rest of it burrows under Tanner's Row and disappears, swallowed a century ago when the tanneries were built on top of it and then abandoned when the tanneries failed. The city forgot it. The city is very good at forgetting—that's practically its trade now, though only two people in it know how good.

Ada carried a lantern with a flame she'd coaxed into burning green, low and smokeless. "Inspector-proof," she said, catching my look. "It reads as marsh-gas to any charm-sniffer within fifty feet. My grandmother invented it for exactly this kind of errand, which tells you something about what kind of woman my grandmother was."

"It tells you she expected you to need it."

"She expected *someone* to need it." Ada's jaw tightened. The guilt had been sitting on her all week like a second coat. "Careful here—the stones are slick."

We walked the channel itself, the dry bed of the aqueduct, our boots crunching on silt that hadn't seen water since before either of us was born. Above us the arches marched away into fog, each one furred with dead ivy. It should have felt like walking through a grave. Instead it felt like walking down a throat—and I don't mean that as poetry. Every few steps the sensation from my dreams grew stronger, that deep-water pressure behind my ears, and each time it came, the word arrived with it.

*Corin Ash.*

"You're doing the face again," Ada said quietly.

"What face?"

"The one where you're listening to something I can't hear." She stopped, lifted the lantern. Green light climbed the curved wall beside us and found something that made us both go still.

Letters. Cut into the stone, old enough that lichen had filled the grooves and made them soft-edged, but unmistakable under the lantern's close light. Not Highmoor script. Something angular, deliberate, the letters of people who wrote to last.

Ada brushed the lichen away with her thumb. Her breath went out of her slowly.

"It's a name," she said. "Or part of one. *...ORIN ASH.*"

The fog seemed to lean closer.

"That's not possible," I said, and heard how thin my voice came out. "This aqueduct predates me. Predates my grandmother. These stones were cut before my family ever came to Highmoor."

"I know." Ada ran her fingers along the carved letters without quite touching them. "And yet someone stood right here, however long ago, and chiseled your name into the foundation of this city." She looked up at me, and the green light made her eyes strange. "Corin. The ledger didn't record your sale. It recorded your *return*. Grandmother's cipher—the entry doesn't say 'sold.' It says 'delivered back.' As if you'd been here before. As if the court had been expecting you."

The pressure behind my ears swelled until my teeth ached.

*Corin Ash. Corin Ash. Corin Ash.*

"The dream starts here," I heard myself say. "Every time. This exact stretch of channel. And the voice—" I pressed my palm flat against the stone. It was cold, but not dead-cold. Cold like held breath. "The voice comes from below. Down and left. Always down and left."

Ada turned the lantern that way. Twenty paces on, where the aqueduct bent toward the ravine, the channel floor changed—rougher stones, darker, fitted together with none of the mason's care visible elsewhere. And set into the wall at the bend, half-hidden behind a curtain of dead ivy, was a slab.

A door, though it had no handle, no hinges, no seam except the hairline crack where it met the wall. Carved across its face in the same angular letters was a single word, and this time the lichen hadn't touched it. Someone—or something—kept it clean.

Ada translated slowly, tracing each character. "*ASH.* Just—your name. Again." She laughed once, without any humor in it. "Your name is written on this city more times than mine is, and you've been here four years less than I've been alive."

"Maybe that's why no one remembers me," I said. "Maybe I'm not forgotten. Maybe I'm *reserved*."

Neither of us liked how true that sounded.

* * *

We cleared the ivy together, working in silence, and the silence was its own kind of conversation. I had learned to read Ada Sterling the way you learn to read weather—the set of her shoulders telling you whether the day would hold or break. Tonight her shoulders said *storm coming, stand clear*, but her hands were steady, and her hands were the truer report. Whatever she feared about what waited below, she had already decided to walk into it. She'd decided the night she found my name in her grandmother's ledger, I think. Everything since was just her catching her conscience up to her courage.

When the last tendril of ivy fell away, the whole slab stood bare, and I saw what the lichen had hidden: the carved letters weren't decorative. They were worn. Worn smooth in the center, as if a thousand hands had rested against them.

Or as if the stone itself had been saying my name for a century, wearing its own voice down.

"Touch it," Ada said softly. "It opened for you in the dream, didn't it? The stones recognized you."

"In the dream the door wasn't stone at all. In the dream it was—" I searched for it. "Warm."

"Then stop stalling."

I put both palms flat against the slab.

For a moment, nothing. The cold crept up my arms, and I thought: *a door is a door is a door*, and the whole journey had been a madman's map drawn from bad sleep. Then—

The warmth came up through the stone like blood returning to a numb limb, painful and sweet, and with it came memory—not mine, or not only mine. A corridor of green light. A table of dark wood. A pen scratching. A child's hand holding the pen. My hand. Smaller. Steadier than I remembered my childhood hands ever being, because I couldn't remember them at all, and now suddenly I could, one flash of it, ink on skin—

I staggered back. The slab groaned.

Not metal-on-metal. Stone-against-stone, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and the hairline seam widened, widened, swung inward on hinges I still couldn't see, exhaling air from below that smelled of rain.

Not the city's coal-and-fog rain. Old rain. Rain that had fallen on forests, on mountains, on the world before Highmoor put its lid on everything. The shop smelled of that rain—I'd told Ada that the first night, told her it was the only doorway that had opened for me twice, and she'd gone white because she'd known then what I was only learning now. The shop smelled the way it did because it was built over the same vein. Everything in this city sat on top of the Hollow like a lid on a pot, and the pot had been simmering for a hundred years.

Beyond the open door: stairs. Narrow, steep, cut into living rock, descending beyond the reach of the lantern's green glow. And on the threshold stone, freshly kept, another inscription—but this one Ada read aloud in a whisper, because it was in plain Highmoor script after all:

*ALL WHO DESCEND ARE ALREADY KNOWN.*

"Well," Ada said. "That's welcoming."

"It's a warning."

"It's both. That's what makes it welcoming." She lifted the lantern and looked at me, and for a moment the storm in her shoulders settled into something steadier. "Once we go down, Corin, I don't know that we come back up the same. People who go into courts come out changed or they don't come out. You understand that."

"I've been changing for thirteen years whether I descend or not." I looked past her into the dark. "At least down there, whatever happens, happens *to* me. Up here nothing does. Up here I'm a draft through an unlocked window."

Ada reached out and took my hand. Her fingers were cold from the channel air, but her grip was certain. "Then let's go be somebody," she said. "Together."

We stepped through.

The stairs took us in a slow spiral, and with every turn the sounds of Highmoor died away—the far traffic of Tanner's Row, the fog-bells on the harbor cranes, even the wind—until there was only our footsteps and the drip of water somewhere ahead and the faint, ceaseless murmur that I finally understood was the source of the voice in my dreams. Not one voice. Hundreds. Thousands. Layered like sediment, murmuring names.

"Do you hear that?" I whispered.

Ada nodded, and her face in the green light was pale. "It sounds like the ledger."

"It *is* the ledger. The book was just the surface. The reading room." The realization settled over me with terrible calm. "They've been writing down souls down here since before the guild existed. Before the law banned magic. Maybe before Highmoor had a name."

The stairs ended.

We stood at the edge of a cavern so vast the lantern couldn't find its ceiling or its far wall, only hints: the glint of wet stone rising into darkness overhead, the suggestion of architecture far below—a colonnade, maybe, or the ribs of some enormous sunken thing. A river ran through the bottom of the hollow, black and slow and silent, carrying lights. Small lights, drifting like lantern-loosed candles, hundreds of them, flowing toward a distant archway that glowed with a warmth no fire had ever produced.

"What are they?" Ada breathed.

I knew. God help me, I knew the way you know your own name.

"Memories," I said. "The ones they take. They don't destroy them. They *spend* them." And as I spoke, one of the drifting lights rose from the river, drifted up the cavern wall, and came to rest directly in front of me—close enough that its glow lit my hands.

Inside the light, moving like figures behind frosted glass: a kitchen. A woman singing. A small boy at a table, watching steam rise off a bowl.

My mother. My mother's kitchen. My mother's song.

The fragment hovered there a moment, as if recognizing me, as if remembering me better than I remembered myself—and then it sank back into the black river and flowed on toward the archway, toward whatever waited in the court below, and the ache it left in my chest was so precise and so total that I understood, finally, exactly what had been stolen from me and how much of me had been spent keeping the lights on in a charm-shop above.

Ada's hand found my arm. "Corin—"

"Thirteen moons," I said. My voice came out level. That surprised me. "That's what's left before the rest of me ends up in that river. So we'd best hurry, hadn't we? Because I intend to get my mother's song back. And then—" I looked down the long dark toward the glowing arch, where the magistrates sat eating yesterday, "—then I intend to make them choke on it."

Far below, in the court whose walls we could not yet see, a bell began to toll.

Ada counted the strokes under her breath, and I watched her face change with each one.

"Corin," she whispered, when the last echo died. "That bell isn't counting hours."

"No?"

"No. It's counting *us*. It rang twelve times." She swallowed. "One for every moon that's already passed."
