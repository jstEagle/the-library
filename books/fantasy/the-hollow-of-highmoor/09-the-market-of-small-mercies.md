## 9. The Market of Small Mercies

The stair did not end so much as dissolve.

One moment we were descending through stone that smelled of river-slime and old iron; the next, the walls fell away on either side like a held breath finally released, and I stood blinking in a light that came from nowhere and everywhere at once—a pale, pearlescent glow that seemed to rise out of the ground itself, as though the earth beneath Highmoor had swallowed a moon long ago and never quite digested it.

"Stay close," Corin murmured.

He didn't need to say it. The place pulled at you. Not physically—my feet stayed where I put them—but something in me leaned forward, the way a sleeper leans toward a window left open in winter. I had the sudden, irrational certainty that if I stopped paying attention, even for a heartbeat, some part of me would stay behind when the rest walked on.

We had come out into a cavern so vast its ceiling was lost in haze. And filling that cavern—not sparsely, not modestly, but *generously*, the way water fills a bowl—was a market.

Stalls of black wood and silvered bone lined a street that curved away into the glow. Lanterns hung from wires strung between impossible heights, each one burning with a different color of flame: blue-white, ember-orange, a green so pale it looked like the ghost of green. Vendors called out in voices that echoed strangely, arriving at my ears a half-second after their mouths moved. And the customers—the customers were the thing that made my skin prickle and my guild-trained eyes go wide.

They were not all alive.

Oh, some were. I saw living faces, hollow-eyed men and women in city clothes who had clearly come down the way we had, clutching bundles to their chests the way you'd clutch a child. But moving among them, browsing the same stalls, haggling over the same goods, were shapes made of that same pearlescent light—translucent figures wearing the fashions of a century ago, a century before that. A woman in a hoop skirt examined a jar of something glowing amber. A soldier whose uniform style I only knew from history books turned a coin over and over in fingers that had no substance, his face slack with an attention that never quite landed.

"They can't see us," Corin said quietly. "The dead ones. They're echoes. Leftovers." He said it with the flatness of a man describing weather he'd lived through too many times. "This is the border-hollow. Between the city above and the court below. Everything that falls between ends up here."

"And everything here is for sale," I said.

I hadn't meant it as a question, but he answered anyway. "Everything here is *payment*. Look closer."

I did, and wished I hadn't. The stalls weren't selling trinkets. They were selling pieces of people. Jars of laughter—you could hear it fizzing faintly against the glass. Bottles of first kisses. Spools of thread wound from someone's grief, each strand humming a different note of sorrow. One vendor spread out a cloth covered in what looked like ordinary buttons, but when I glanced at them directly, each button showed me a scene: a kitchen, a funeral, a wedding, a hand letting go of another hand. Memories. Loose, unmoored, harvested from whoever had needed passage or bread or simply forgotten how to hold on.

A child's voice rose from a stall near us—"Warm hands! Warm hands, cheap, warm hands!"—and my heart lurched before I understood: the vendor was selling the *memory* of warmth, not warmth itself. A gaunt man pressed three coins into the vendor's palm, drank something from a stoppered vial, and sighed with a pleasure that lasted exactly as long as the swallow. Then his face went gray again, and he moved on, hunting for the next small mercy.

I had spent six years selling charms that did almost nothing, warming cold hands with just enough power to be honest about being nearly nothing. I knew poverty's arithmetic down to the copper. But this—

"This is what happens," I said slowly, "when magic is outlawed and need doesn't die with it. It goes underground and gets *hungry*."

"That's one word for it." Corin's jaw was tight. He was looking at the soldier-echo, still turning his coin. "Wren said to find her at the far landing. We shouldn't linger."

"Wren." I tested the name. "Your dream-ferryman."

"The only person down here who ever remembered me twice." His mouth twisted. "Though she claims she doesn't know why. She remembers everyone. Everyone except herself."

* * *

The crowd thickened as we followed the curve of the street downward, toward the sound of water. I kept my hood up and my eyes moving. There were rules in places like this—I could feel them the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm—and I did not yet know what they were. My grandmother's ledgers had taught me one lesson above all others: *never sign anything you haven't read to the last line.* Down here, I suspected, the signing happened with less ink and more self.

A vendor caught my sleeve as I passed—a woman with kind eyes and a smile that didn't reach anywhere near them. "Charms, dearie? You've the look of a charm-maker. I can smell the guild-wax on you." She held up a bracelet of tiny braided cords. "Luck-knots. Fresh. Only cost you the name of your first friend."

"I don't trade names," I said, and felt something in my chest go hard and bright. "And those knots are crooked. Whoever braided them was rushing."

Her smile flickered—genuine offense, briefly, before the mask slid back. "Guild-trained," she said, almost admiringly. "They don't make many of you anymore."

"No," I agreed. "They don't."

Corin drew me onward. At the street's lowest point, where the pearlescent glow gathered thickest, the market opened onto a black river so still it looked poured rather than flowing. A dock of dark timber stretched out over it, and moored alongside was a boat—long, low, and old, its hull carved all over with names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Some sharp and recent, some worn to whispers, some so ancient they'd been carved over by newer letters until the hull was a palimpsest of the forgotten.

At the dock's end stood a woman.

She was small and wiry, wrapped in a coat that might have been fashionable forty years ago and had since given up pretending. Her hair was iron-gray and cropped short. Her face was unremarkable in the way that faces become unremarkable when they have seen everything there is to see—every feature worn smooth, every expression available and none of them fixed. But her eyes—

Her eyes were the busiest things I had ever seen. They moved constantly, cataloguing, filing, *keeping*. When they found Corin, they widened with something that looked almost like relief.

"Copper-top," she said. "You actually came back. Third time's the charm, isn't that what they say up there?" She snorted. "Charm. Hah."

"You remember him," I said.

"I remember everyone." Wren's gaze swung to me, and I felt it like a lamp being shone into a room I thought was dark. "Ada Sterling. Granddaughter of Maren Sterling, guildhead of the Charmwrights' Guild of Highmoor, keeper of the shop on Candlemaker's Row, maker of luck-knots that are honestly braided and warming-charms that do exactly what they promise and not a whit more." She paused. "You bite your thumbnail when you're frightened. You've been doing it since you were four. Your mother used to pull your hand away from your mouth and kiss the fingertip."

My breath went out of me. "How—"

"Because I ferried your mother down this river once, years ago, when she came looking for answers about the bargain. And because I remember everyone." Wren's voice dropped, and for the first time something moved behind the busy eyes—something that might have been envy, or grief, or both wearing the same coat. "Everyone except myself. I look in a mirror, I see a stranger. Someone says 'Wren,' and the word lands on me like rain on glass. It runs off." She shrugged, briskly, as if shrugging off the weight of a century. "First soul ever sold to the Hollow Court. They kept my memories as evidence and gave back the rest of me incomplete. Been ferrying bargainers ever since, waiting for one of them to finish the job."

Corin went very still beside me. "Finish it how?"

"That's a conversation for the boat." Wren jerked her chin toward the gangplank. "Passage isn't free. Never has been, never will be. The river takes toll, and the toll is *you*—a piece of you, true and weighty. Small mercies for small crossings. What you carry across is what you'll owe on the return, doubled, if you come back at all." Her eyes glittered. "So choose carefully, Charmwright. What will you give?"

* * *

The boat moved without oars or current, gliding over water that gave back our reflections blurred, as though the river couldn't quite be bothered to render us accurately. The market lights receded behind us. Ahead, the cavern narrowed into a channel walled with stone that grew gradually more finished, more deliberate—worked blocks, then carvings, then columns, until it was clear we were entering architecture rather than geology. The court was making itself known.

Wren poled nothing and steered everything, standing at the stern with her hands loose at her sides, and talked while she worked, in the manner of someone who has been alone with her thoughts for a hundred years and intends to spend none of them in silence now that company has arrived.

"The court eats memory. That's the whole of it, whatever fancy robes they wear. The magistrates take memories as evidence, weigh souls like ledger entries, and call it justice because justice is the prettiest word for appetite ever invented." She spat elegantly over the side. "Every contract they hold is written in what the signer can't afford to lose. That's why they last. You can break a contract written in blood—blood regrows. You can't break one written in forgetting, because the person who'd want to break it can't remember why."

"But it *can* be broken," Corin said. It wasn't a question. He'd asked it in his sleep for years, I suspected, and was asking it awake now with both hands gripping the gunwale.

Wren was quiet a moment. The channel darkened around us.

"There's a book," she said at last. "The master prophecy. The original. Every contract the court has ever cut is copied into it, and every copy is anchored to it. Burn the master, and every page it anchors turns to ash in the holding. Every soul it binds walks free—including the ones they've already eaten, if any of them are left to walk." Her voice roughened. "It sits in the Chief Magistrate's own archive, under guard, behind wards that taste intention. You cannot steal it. You cannot trick your way to it. You can only reach it in the chaos of the thirteenth turn, when the court spends itself consuming what it's waited a generation to collect."

"Me," Corin said.

"You." Wren looked at him with those busy, hoarding eyes, and I realized she was memorizing his face. Adding it to the collection she kept and could never be part of. "Thirteen moons, and the turn completes. The court will open its throat. And in that minute—maybe two—everything it owns is busy swallowing, and nothing it owns is watching."

* * *

The channel opened into a wide, slow basin, and Wren brought the boat alongside a second dock—smaller, quieter, lit by a single lantern burning the blue-white color of the flame I'd liked best in the market. Beyond it, stairs descended into a darkness that had texture, weight, *attention*. The Hollow Court proper. I could feel it down there, the way you feel a large animal in a dark room: not seeing it, but knowing precisely where it was.

"We walk from here," Wren said. "But first." She held out her palm. "Toll. Both of you. Now, while the water's calm. Pay it on the water or pay it in the court, and the court charges interest."

Corin paid first. He reached into himself the way a man reaches into a pocket, and what he drew out was small and gray and shapeless—a wisp, a scrap. "It's not much," he said apologetically. "Most of me is already gone."

"It's a memory of rain," Wren said softly, taking it. "Rain on a window, and someone's hand pointing out the drops. It's worth more than you think. It's the oldest kind of memory there is." She set it adrift on the black water, where it sank without a ripple, accepted.

Then she looked at me.

I had known this was coming since the market. I had rehearsed on the walk down, sorting through myself like a merchant sorting stock, deciding what I could bear to lose. A bad winter. The smell of the inspector's office. My tenth birthday, which had been disappointing anyway. There were rooms in me I could board up and live without.

But Wren's busy eyes moved over my face, and she said, gently, terribly: "Not the junk, Charmwright. The river knows junk. It sinks junk and sends it back. Toll has to be *true*."

My hand drifted to my thumbnail. I made myself stop.

There was one thing. There had only ever been one thing, and I had known it since the dock, and I had hoped with a coward's hope that I wouldn't have to say it aloud.

"My mother sang to me," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Every night. Even after the guild started failing, even after father left, even when we were eating bread and onions for a month straight. She had a lullaby—her mother's mother's, maybe older, maybe it came down the same channels the magic did. I don't know the words anymore. Isn't that strange? I know the melody perfectly, every rise and fall of it, but the words went somewhere I can't follow, years ago. So I hum it. When the shop's empty. When the inspector's footsteps fade. When I can't sleep." I swallowed. "It's the last thing I have of her that isn't a photograph or a debt."

Wren's hand stayed open. Patient. The river waited.

"If I give it up," I said, "I won't miss it. That's the cruelty of it, isn't it? I won't lie awake wondering what's gone, because I won't know anything's gone. I'll just be a woman who sleeps badly and doesn't know why."

"Yes," Wren said. "That's the cruelty. That's the whole design." And then, softer: "You can refuse. Ferry's free if you swim."

I looked at Corin. He was watching me with an expression I was beginning to know well—the look of a man who had lost everything piecewise and would give it all back piecewise to keep one person from paying for his sake. His mouth opened.

"Don't," I said. "Don't offer to split it. This one's mine."

I closed my eyes. I found the lullaby where it always lived, curled behind my sternum, warmer than any charm I'd ever sold. Three rising notes, a fall, a turn, a long sweet resolve—her voice underneath it, low and a little smoky, the way it got at the end of the day. I let myself have it one full time through, humming it silently, start to finish, the way you read a letter you know you're going to burn.

Then I reached in and lifted it out.

It came away glowing—a small warm coil of gold light, turning slowly in my palm, and for one last moment I could feel it, could feel her, could feel the kitchen and the candle and the blanket tucked under my chin. I loved it. That was the toll. That was the whole terrible price of justice outside the law: you paid in love, in the exact currency of the thing, and the payment was that afterward you would not know what you had paid.

I set it on Wren's palm.

She closed her fingers around it with a care that undid me more than the losing did. "Maren's daughter sang this to you," she whispered. "I heard it through the hull, the night I ferried her. She was singing it to herself, going down. Trying to remember what she was fighting for." Her busy eyes shone. "It's safe with me. Whatever else happens—whatever the court takes—it's safe."

I nodded. I could not speak. Something was already smoothing over inside me, soft as snowfall, filling in the space where the lullaby had been, and I could feel myself beginning to forget that there had ever been a space at all.

"Ada." Corin's hand found mine. His grip was the only warm thing left in the world, and I held on.

"Come on, then," Wren said, and her voice was all business again, brisk as a deck swabbed clean. "The court doesn't wait, and neither does the moon. Thirteenth turn's close enough now that the magistrates can taste it." She stepped onto the dock, lantern in hand, and paused at the head of the stairs that went down into the attentive dark.

"One more thing you should know before we knock on the devil's door," she said. "The Chief Magistrate keeps a room of preserved memories. Evidence, they call it. Yours truly is filed there. So is your grandmother, Charmwright." She looked back at me, and the lantern-light carved her face into planes and shadow. "And yesterday evening, while you two were picking locks in the sealed archives, the court took delivery of a new acquisition. A fresh memory, recently harvested, still warm."

"Whose?" Corin asked.

Wren's busy eyes went flat and careful, the way eyes go when they are deciding how much truth a person can carry and still climb a staircase.

"An inspector's," she said. "Vael. He's coming down after you. And what he's carrying in his head, the court wants very badly indeed."
