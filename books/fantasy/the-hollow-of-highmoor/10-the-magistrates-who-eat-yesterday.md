## 10. The Magistrates Who Eat Yesterday

The ferry did not rock.

That was the first wrong thing Corin noticed about the crossing—that the black water beneath them lay flat as poured glass, and the boat moved without wake, without sound, as though the river itself were holding its breath and letting them pass through the held part. Above, Highmoor's fog had thinned to a ceiling of grey gauze, and through it he could see the underside of the city: cellar doors, drain mouths, the roots of bridges hanging down like teeth. He had lived under that city for eleven years and had never once thought of it as having an underside. Now he knew better. Everything with a top had a bottom, and everything buried in the bottom was still owed something.

Wren poled them onward with a long oar of pale wood that did not drip when she lifted it. She had not spoken since telling them about Vael. Her silence had a texture to it—attentive, like a hand hovering over a wound.

Ada sat across from him in the bow, her knees drawn up, her grandmother's ledger open on her thighs even though there was hardly light enough to read by. She read anyway. He watched her lips move over the ciphered lines, translating grief into grammar. Every few strokes of the oar she looked up—not at him, exactly, but at the space where his face was, the way people look at a candle they are afraid will go out.

"I can feel you watching me," she said, without looking up.

"You can?"

"No." She turned a page. "But I'd bet a whole shop's worth of luck-knots that you were."

He almost smiled. It was strange, how quickly she had learned the shape of his humor—or perhaps not strange at all. Perhaps this was what happened between two people whom the world refused to remember: they memorized each other instead, carefully, redundantly, the way scribes copied out a single precious book.

The river bent, and the Hollow Court appeared.

Corin had expected a palace. What he saw was a courthouse grown vast and wrong, the way a tooth grows wrong in a small jaw. Columns rose from the water in ranks, each one carved from a different material—some granite, some salt-white bone, one that seemed to be made entirely of stacked letters, characters pressed together so tightly they had fused into stone. Between the columns hung doors. Hundreds of doors, free-standing, leading nowhere, their frames crusted with frost or moss or rust depending on which decade they had been pried from. Some stood ajar. From the dark inside them came sounds: a woman humming, rain on a tin roof, a child counting to ten and starting over, delighted, forever.

"What are those?" Ada whispered.

"Memories," Wren said. "Evidence. The court keeps everything it has ever been paid." She shipped the oar and let the boat drift the last stretch on its own. "Every soul sold here brought its whole life as surety. They store the life and keep the person. That's the trade. You get to keep walking around, and they get to keep *you*."

The boat touched stone without a bump. There was no dock, only a stair rising out of the water, each step worn concave by feet that had climbed it unwillingly.

Corin stepped onto the first stair and felt the court notice him.

There was no other word for it. A pressure passed over his skin like a searchlight, slow and thorough, and somewhere far above—or below; direction meant nothing here—a voice said, in a tone of mild clerical interest:

*Entry logged. Ash, Corin. Status: pending. Thirteenth turn in nine days.*

Nine days. Hearing it spoken aloud in that dry ledger-voice made it real in a way the prophecies never had. His knees wanted to soften. He locked them.

"Say nothing," Wren murmured, taking up a position behind them both like a servant attending masters she pitied. "Answer direct questions with direct answers. And whatever they weigh—" her busy eyes flicked to his chest, as if she could see his heart through it, "—don't let them weigh what matters most. Once it's in the scale, it stays in the scale."

* * *

The great hall of the Hollow Court was a library that had eaten a courtroom, or possibly the reverse.

Shelves rose in cliffs along every wall, and the shelves were full—not of books, Corin realized as they were led down the central aisle, but of jars, bottles, lanterns, loaves of bread gone stale but unspoiled, wedding rings, a child's shoe, a glass eye that rolled slowly to follow him as he passed. Each vessel held a memory, and the memories were alive in the way embers are alive. Warmth came off the shelves in waves. He caught fragments as they walked: the smell of a specific kitchen on a specific morning; the exact weight of a sleeping cat; someone's last sight of their mother, waving from a doorway, getting smaller.

At the hall's far end, on a dais of black basalt, sat the magistrates.

There were seven of them, arranged in a crescent behind a desk of green stone, and they were robed in grey so fine it seemed woven from fog itself—the same fog, Corin thought with a lurch, that hung over Highmoor. Perhaps it was. Perhaps the city's famous weather was just the court's laundry, drifting up through the cracks.

Their faces were the worst part. They had faces—he could see eyes, mouths, the architecture of noses—but the features would not hold still. They drifted, subtly, endlessly, like reflections in disturbed water. When one magistrate turned to look at him, Corin understood with cold certainty that the face he was seeing had been borrowed. Taken as evidence. Worn because wearing things was simply what the court did.

The center magistrate spoke. Its voice was the ledger-voice, amplified, patient as arithmetic.

"Corin Ash. You have come to the court of your own accord. This is irregular."

"He's come to contest his contract," Ada said, stepping forward before Corin could find his tongue. Her chin was up. Her hands, at her sides, were shaking. "Under provision—"

"The contract is not contested by the sold," the magistrate said. "It is honored by them. That is what makes it a contract rather than a theft." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You are not the party under discussion, Ada Sterling. Though your name does appear elsewhere in our records. We will return to that."

Corin felt Ada go very still beside him.

"But," the magistrate continued, and something in its borrowed face shifted—amusement? hunger? the two wore the same expression here—"you have walked a long way down, and the court is not ungenerous to guests. We will show you the evidence in your own case, Corin Ash. It is only fair that a man see what he is worth before he is collected."

A clerk detached itself from the shadows behind the dais—a thin figure whose face was smooth as an egg, featureless except where, faintly, like a watermark, the ghost of a previous face showed through—and went to the shelves.

It returned carrying a jar.

Inside the jar was a sheet of paper. Ordinary paper, cream-colored, foxed at the edges with age. Corin stared at it and felt the hall tilt slightly, because he recognized the paper. Not the document—himself. He had carried that cream color, that foxing, all his life without knowing why certain textures felt like home.

"The sale contract of Corin Ash," the magistrate announced, "executed one generation past. Entered into willingly, by the named party, in exchange for consideration rendered to the guild of charm-wrights of Highmoor." The clerk set the jar on the green desk and unstopped it, and lifted the paper out with two careful fingers, and unfolded it, and turned it to face the hall.

"Read it," the magistrate said. "The court insists. We insist on reading, always. Reading is how memory becomes evidence, and evidence becomes ours."

Corin stepped forward. His legs carried him the way legs carry men in dreams, obedient and distant.

The contract was written in a fine, formal hand, ink gone brown with age. He read the clauses without absorbing them—*the undersigned, of sound mind and free will, conveys unto the Hollow Court all right, title, and interest in the soul herein described*—and then he reached the signature line, and stopped.

The name written there was his own.

Not the way he wrote it now—his adult hand, cramped and hurried from years of signing for lodgings no innkeeper would remember him renting. This signature was round. Careful. The letters leaned slightly left, the way children lean when they are concentrating too hard on formation. Beneath it, in smaller script, the same child-hand had added, unprompted, the way children add things:

*I promise this is really me.*

The hall was silent. Somewhere in the shelves, faintly, a memory of applause played and faded.

"That's not my signature," Corin heard himself say. His voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing further away than he was. "I never signed anything. I never—I didn't know any of this existed until three weeks ago."

"The signature is authenticated," the magistrate said gently, horribly gently. "Ink age, hand analysis, and—" it gestured, and the clerk produced a second vessel, a small lantern of smoked glass, "—corroborating memory. Yours. Would you like to verify?"

"I don't remember signing anything."

"No," the magistrate agreed. "You wouldn't. The memory of the signing was remitted to us at the time of execution. Standard practice. The sold retain everything except the moment of sale—it makes for more cooperative souls, and less messy appeals." It tilted its drifting face, considering him with eyes that might once have belonged to a schoolteacher, or a hangman. "But the memory exists, Corin Ash. It has been in evidence for twenty-six years. Would you care to see it? The court permits parties to review their own files. We find it... settles them."

Every instinct Corin owned told him to refuse. To step back, to take Ada's hand, to walk back up the stairs that recognized his name and never learn what the child who wrote *I promise this is really me* had been promised in return.

"Yes," he said.

Because he had spent eleven years being nobody, and a nobody who refuses to look is still a nobody. He would rather be somebody who knew the worst thing about himself than nobody at all.

The magistrate smiled with its borrowed mouth. It was the kindest expression Corin had ever seen and it made his stomach drop like a stone down a well.

"Witnesses may observe," it said, "at the risk of the observed memory becoming partially theirs. Court rules." Its eyes moved to Ada. "Do you wish to share your friend's file, Miss Sterling? Couples often do. It builds intimacy. Intimacy is, after all, merely shared evidence."

"I'm watching," Ada said, and her hand found Corin's, and gripped it hard enough to hurt, which was the point. Pain was proof of body. Body was proof of now.

The clerk raised the smoked-glass lantern, and broke its seal, and opened it toward them the way you open a window onto a garden.

* * *

The memory took them like water takes a dropped coin.

He was seven years old. He knew it the way you know things in memories, absolutely and without source. He was sitting at a kitchen table in a house that smelled of woodsmoke and fever-salve, and the table was too tall for him, and his feet did not reach the floor. Outside the window, snow. Inside the window, everywhere else, sickness. He could hear it in the next room—a wet, rattling breathing, multiplied by four, by six, by however many of the village lay in that room beyond his counting. His mother's voice came from somewhere above and behind him, frayed down to threads:

"It's a good bargain, love. It's the best bargain anyone ever made. You'll save them all. Do you understand? Everyone. The whole village. Everyone gets to stay."

"And I go away," the child said. Him. His voice, small and serious, already old in the way children in hard houses go old early.

"For a little while. And then—" his mother's voice did something complicated and broke off, and started again, steadier, lying beautifully, "—and then we'll see. But listen. Whatever the gentleman asks you, you say yes. Can you do that? Say yes, and sign your name, and then everyone lives. That's the whole spell. That's the whole magic. Your name is the magic."

A man came into view then—grey-robed, his face doing the drifting-water thing, and even through the memory's soft focus Corin felt the recognition crawl up his arms: the same magistrate, or one of its kindred, unchanged across the decades while everything human in the room decayed around it.

"Do you give yourself freely, little one?" the grey man asked. "Freely given, freely taken. Those are the terms."

The child looked at the door of the sickroom. Through it, the rattling breaths. One of them stuttered, caught, resumed—someone fighting, someone not ready.

"I promise this is really me," the child said, which was his way of saying yes, which was his way of saying *I am brave enough for this*, and he took the pen in his fist the way children hold pens, all fingers, and he wrote his name leaning left, careful as prayer.

And the grey man leaned down, close, and said words that the memory kept with terrible fidelity, words that had been excised from the boy's waking mind but preserved here, catalogued, shelved, warming the shelf for twenty-six years:

"A freely given soul is the finest coin, child. And the court always gives change."

The memory ended.

* * *

Corin came back to himself on his knees on the basalt floor. He did not remember falling. Ada was crouched over him, both her hands on his face, saying his name—his actual name, the one almost no one in Highmoor had ever said twice—and he clung to the sound of it like a man clinging to a rail in a flood.

"There was a plague," he said. His voice came out shredded. "My village. My mother sold—no. No, she didn't sell me. *I* signed. She asked me to and I signed. I was seven years old and I signed my own soul away to save a village I can't even remember the name of."

"Corin—"

"They gave change." He started laughing, or something adjacent to laughing, and it hurt his ribs. "That's what he said. The court always gives change. Eleven years of nobody remembering my face—that was the change. That was my *change*, Ada. I saved everyone and the court gave me back to the world as nothing, as a draft blowing through doors, and I thought—" his voice cracked clean through, "—I thought I was just broken. I thought I was the kind of person people naturally forgot. I built my whole life on being the kind of person people naturally forget."

Ada pulled his forehead down against hers. Her hands were shaking against his jaw.

"Listen to me," she said. "You were seven. You were seven, and someone put a pen in your hand and told you your name was a spell, and you used it to save everyone you loved. That's not consent. That's not a contract. That's a child being spent like currency by adults who should have burned the courthouse down first."

"The signature stands," said the magistrate above them, mild as milk. "The law does not inquire into the heart's age, only the hand's."

"Then the law," Ada said, rising, turning to face the crescent of drifting faces with the ledger still clutched in one white-knuckled fist, "is going to have a very bad nine days."

The magistrates regarded her. All seven faces rippled at once, and Corin, still on his knees, saw something pass across them that he would think about for the rest of his life—however long that turned out to be. It was not anger. It was appetite recognizing appetite. The look of a thing that has eaten yesterday for a century and has just been offered tomorrow.

"Nine days," the center magistrate agreed pleasantly. "Do enjoy your visit, Miss Sterling. The archive is open to guests. So is the wing where we keep the memories of guildheads." A pause, silk over stone. "Your grandmother's is particularly well preserved. She visited it often, near the end. Guilt, we find, is the longest-lasting preservative."

Behind them, unnoticed until now, the egg-faced clerk had begun sorting papers on the green desk—and atop the stack, face-up, lay a fresh summons bearing a date nine days hence and two names.

One was Corin Ash.

The other, newly inked, still shining wet, was hers.
