## 11. A Bargain Overheard

The archive of the Hollow Court did not smell like dust. That was the first thing Ada noticed, and the last comfort she took from it.

Dust would have meant neglect. Dust would have meant that somewhere in this vast dark beneath Highmoor, there were things the magistrates had forgotten to care about. But the air here was clean and cold and faintly sweet, like the inside of a locket, like paper kept pressed between clean linen for a hundred years. Everything in this room was cared about. Everything was *kept*.

Ada crouched in the gap between two shelving units so tall she could not see their tops, even by the pale greenish light that seeped from the shelves themselves—light with no source, the way the glow came off her grandmother's ledger when it wanted to be read. The shelves were not wood. She had touched one, briefly, before Corin's hand closed over her wrist and stopped her. The material had been warm. It had given, very slightly, under her fingertip, the way skin gives.

She had not touched them again.

*They're not shelves,* Corin had whispered, an hour ago or a year ago; time moved strangely down here, pooling in corners. *Look at what's on them.*

And she had looked, and wished she hadn't. Not books. Not scrolls. Each shelf held rows of small glass vessels, each no bigger than a teacup, each glowing faintly from within—and inside each one, something moved. A curl of smoke that shaped itself, now and then, into a doorway. A flicker of candlelight that guttered at some private wind. Once, horribly, a snatch of laughter, thin as a hair, rising out of the glass and dying before it finished.

Memories. Thousands upon thousands of them, bottled and shelved and indexed, the evidence vault of a court that tried souls by what they remembered.

*Don't touch anything,* Wren had told them at the ferry. *Down there, remembering is ownership. If you hold a memory, the court can argue you're holding stolen property.* Her face had done something complicated when she said it—the face of a woman who had been arguing about her own property for a hundred years. *And don't let them hear your names. Names are how they file people.*

So they had come into the archive nameless, moving through the aisles like thieves in a temple, and now Ada crouched in the dark between two walls of other people's yesterdays, and her legs had gone numb hours ago, and she did not dare shift her weight because three aisles away, voices had begun.

* * *

There were four of them. She counted their footsteps first, before she ever heard words: the soft irregular tread of someone pacing, the stillness of two who stood, and a third whose steps made no sound at all, which frightened her more than the pacing.

"—the guild is a husk," said the pacer. His voice was dry and precise, the voice of a man reading aloud from a document he had written himself. "One shop. One keeper. The old woman's granddaughter, and she sells luck-knots to children." A pause, and then something that might have been amusement, if amusement could be taxidermied. "Though I understand the knots do work."

"The knots work," said a second voice, older, slower, worn smooth like a stone in a stream. This one, Ada thought, was the Chief Magistrate. There was a weight to it, a patience, the way there is weight in deep water. "Everything the guild makes works. That is precisely the problem, Magistrate Halloway, and precisely the opportunity."

"Opportunity." Halloway's pacing stopped. "You mean the girl."

"I mean the line."

Silence. In it, Ada heard the faint sound of the shelves—the glass vessels humming to themselves, almost inaudibly, the way a full house hums when everyone in it is asleep.

"The bargain was struck fairly," Halloway said. "One soul per generation, freely given by the guildhead. The Ash boy was given. His term runs out on the thirteenth turn, and the court takes possession entire. Clean. Final. Why complicate a century of clean law?"

"Because a soul is not a harvest, Halloway. You do not reap it and begin again. A soul, once ours, ripens." The Chief Magistrate's voice lowered, and Ada leaned forward despite herself, her shoulder brushing a vessel on the shelf beside her. Inside it, something flinched. She froze, heart hammering, but the voice went on below, unhurried. "The Ash boy has spent nine years among the living without being remembered. Do you know what that does? Memory is what wears a soul into a shape. Take away everyone else's memory of him, and the shape stays soft. Pliable. He has become—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—excellent raw material."

"You want to keep him past his term."

"I want to do better than keep him. I intend to bind him to the court permanently, and through him, bind the guild. Permanently." Paper rustled. "Fetch me the master prophecy."

Footsteps—two sets, receding. The soundless walker stayed. Ada pressed her back against the shelf and closed her eyes and breathed through her mouth, slow and silent, the way her grandmother had taught her to breathe when hiding contraband charms from inspectors: *stillness isn't holding yourself still, love, it's forgetting you have edges.*

When the footsteps returned, they brought with them a sound Ada knew in her bones. A heavy book being set down. A cover opening. And underneath it all, a low murmur, like a crowd heard through a wall—the sound of the Hollow Prophecies, the ledger beneath the mountain, reading itself.

"Here," the Chief Magistrate said softly. "The Sterling entry. Listen to how neatly it was written. *For the survival of the Guild of Charmwrights of Highmoor, Maren Sterling gives freely that which the court shall name.* Freely given. Witnessed. Sealed in wax and grief—I have the wax here, you can smell the lavender she burned while she signed." He turned a page. "And note the clause the old woman never read to the end. *That which the court shall name*—not the Ash boy. The court named the Ash boy, yes, but the naming clause survives every sale. When his term ends, the court may name anew."

"And you will name the girl."

"I will name the girl." A pause, savoring. "Maren Sterling sold one soul to save her guild. Her granddaughter will sell every soul the guild will ever have. Every charmwright born under that roof, in perpetuity, theirs by clause. The guild becomes a farm, Halloway. A quiet little farm at the top of the stairs, sending us one soul a generation forever, and grateful for the arrangement, because the alternative is the ash-heap the other guilds got."

Halloway was quiet a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—something almost like unease in it. "The girl doesn't know. About the clause."

"She knows she owes a debt. Debts make people honest. And when the moon turns and we name her, she'll learn what honesty costs." The Chief Magistrate chuckled, low and dry. "Besides. She's already here."

Ada's blood stopped.

"She came down the aqueduct stair with the Ash boy. My silent friend here watched them pass the border-hollow. They paid the ferryman." A pause. "Which means the girl has seen the market. Which means she intends to contest the sale."

"Then stop her."

"No." The word fell like a coin into a well. "Let her contest. Let her climb every stair and open every door and stand in our chamber and argue until her voice cracks. Because every argument she makes, she makes *as a guildhead*. And the moment she stands in our court and speaks for the guild—claims its debts, disputes its contracts, wields its name—" the pages turned, gently, lovingly, "—she activates the succession. She becomes the seller in law as well as blood. She'll bind herself trying to unbind him. They always do. It's the oldest trap in the ledger, and it has never once failed."

The silence that followed had a texture. Ada sat in it, numb from the hips down and from the heart up, and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had walked into the trap days ago—that she had walked into it the moment she'd said *my guild* to Wren on the ferry, the moment she'd claimed the debt as hers to pay. Every step she had taken to save Corin Ash had been a step deeper into the clause.

*Grandmother,* she thought, and the name ached. *Did you know? Did you read to the end after all?*

But no. She had seen the memory of the signing—or she would see it; Wren had told them where it was kept, in this very archive, sealed as evidence. She had to believe the old woman had signed in ignorance, weeping, lavender smoke curling around her hands. Desperate women first. Villains only afterward, if at all.

The real villain was patient, and he was three aisles away, and he was turning the pages of her life.

"There is one complication," Halloway said.

"Name it."

"The Ash boy's contract bears his own signature. Childhood mark, but valid—he consented, however ignorantly. Consent cuts both ways in contract law. If he finds the original, if he reads the terms he agreed to..." Halloway hesitated. "A soul that understands its own sale can petition for review. It's ancient law. Older than the court."

Another silence, longer this one. When the Chief Magistrate spoke again, all the patience had gone out of his voice, and what remained was colder and much older.

"Then the boy must not reach his contract. Move it. Deep vault, west range, under seal. And the Sterling memory with it—if the girl sees her grandmother's signing, she may find whatever thread leads to the boy's consent. Weave them together where no one will look. Bury the consent under the crime." Papers slid across a surface. "And post watchers at the archive doors. Nothing walks out of this room that the court hasn't finished with."

"As you will."

Footsteps, receding. The soundless walker went last, and though Ada stared into the dark aisle behind her, she never saw him pass. Only felt, for one cold instant, a pressure against her mind like a thumb testing fruit—checking whether she was ripe—before it moved on.

* * *

She didn't move for a long time after the voices faded. Neither did Corin.

He had been crouched on the far side of the aisle, close enough that she could hear his breathing—shallow, controlled, the breathing of a man who had spent nine years practicing invisibility and hated every hour of the lesson. Now, in the dark, his hand found hers. His fingers were cold.

"You heard," he whispered.

"All of it."

"They're moving my contract." His voice cracked on the word *contract*, and she squeezed his fingers. "My signature. Ada, I signed it. I was a child, I don't even remember—but I signed it, and that means—"

"It means nothing yet." She kept her voice flat and steady, the voice she used with inspectors. "Listen to me. They think the trap is sprung. They think I'm going to walk into their chamber and claim the guild and bind myself forever." She turned toward him in the dark. Even half-blind, she could feel the shape of his attention on her, total and unguarded, the way he looked at everything—as if it might be the last thing anyone ever showed him. "So we're not going to give them a trial. We're going to give them a theft."

"The vaults are sealed. West range, under seal, and watchers on every door."

"Yes." Ada's mind was moving fast now, the old shopkeeper's arithmetic running behind her eyes—not coins this time but minutes, guards, distances, the exchange rate of risk. "But they're moving two things into the same place. My grandmother's memory and your contract. Buried together, where no one will look." She smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "Where no one will look except the two people those things are *about*."

Corin was quiet. Then, slowly: "Wren said the vessels answer to need. That memories want to be found—it's why the court keeps them lit."

"Wren also said she'd been waiting a hundred years for someone to finish the job." Ada rose, stiffly, her joints cracking like green wood, and peered down the aisle toward the distant green glow of the archive doors. Two shapes stood there now, motionless, where none had stood before. Watchers. "West range," she murmured. "Under seal. Past two door-guards who listen for names."

Corin stood beside her. For a moment neither moved. Somewhere above them, impossibly far above, the moon was beginning its thirteenth turn, and every hour they spent in the dark was an hour off Corin's life.

"Ada," he said quietly. "If we're caught, they take you. Not me—they already own my term. They'll name you early. Tonight. Bind the whole line tonight and be done with it."

"Then we won't be caught."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only plan I've ever had that worked." She looked at him, and let him see all of it—the fear, the fury, the stubborn banked heat that had kept a dying guild alive one warm charm at a time. "Corin. They wrote you into a book when you were a child and called it fate. They wrote my whole family into the same book and called it legacy. Tomorrow the moon turns and they mean to write the ending." Her hand closed around the small iron charm at her throat—her grandmother's, the only piece of true craft she carried, warm against her palm. "I have spent my whole life selling warmth in a city that's afraid of wonder. I am *done* being afraid of it myself."

She stepped out of the shelter of the shelves, into the long green light of the aisle, and the nearest vessels brightened as she passed—just slightly, just enough—like a thousand small yesterdays turning in their sleep to watch her go.

Behind the watchers' backs, at the far end of the archive, a door marked with a wax seal began, very softly, to glow.

It had heard its name coming.
