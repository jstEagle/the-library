## 12. The Room Where Grandmother Waited

The archive of the Hollow Court did not creak the way the shop's floorboards did. It hummed.

Ada felt it through her boots as she moved between the shelves—a low, patient vibration, like a hive of sleeping bees, or a heart that had forgotten how to stop. Every shelf held not books but vessels: jars of smoked glass, boxes banded in iron, lanterns with their wicks drowned in something darker than oil. Memories. The magistrates' evidence. A century of Highmoor's stolen yesterdays, catalogued and shelved and waiting to be eaten.

She had been hiding in the archive's upper gallery for what felt like hours, wedged behind a case of memory-lanterns from some decade she couldn't name, listening to the Chief Magistrate's footsteps fade below. Corin was somewhere on the far side of the court, doing whatever half-remembered instinct told him to do. Wren had gone back to the water. And Ada—Ada had stayed, because staying near danger had become the only way she knew how to breathe.

That was when she saw the label.

It was on a shelf at eye level, in the gallery's quietest corner, where the light from the hollow-glow came thin and gray through slits in the stone. The labels here were all written in the same spidery court-hand: *name, date, nature of evidence*. She had been reading them idly for an hour, the way one reads gravestones. *Merrit Low, 61 years past, debt of voice. Sable Quince, 40 years past, pledge of firstborn laughter.*

And then: *Elara Sterling. 33 years past. Contract of sale. Original witness-memory.*

Ada's hand went to her mouth before she understood why.

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's *date*—thirty-three years ago, two years before Ada was born, three years before the guild's fortunes turned and the charm-shops of Highmoor went from starving to merely poor while the Sterling ledger stayed fat.

The vessel beside the label was small. A jar of green glass, no bigger than a fist, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. Through the glass she could see nothing—not fog, not light, just a darkness that seemed to move if she didn't look directly at it, the way deep water moves.

*Contract of sale. Original witness-memory.*

Her grandmother had been there. Not as the seller—the seller's memory would be filed elsewhere, or perhaps worn on a magistrate's tongue by now—but as the *witness*. The court kept witnesses. Of course it did. What was a contract without proof? What was a soul sold without someone left behind who remembered the price?

Ada looked over the edge of the gallery. Below, the archive stretched in long aisles toward the central chamber where the master prophecy sat chained to its lectern of black stone. Two magistrates drifted between the shelves like moths that had learned to walk, their gray robes whispering, their faces tilted toward the vessels they passed the way a man tilts his face toward bread.

They hadn't seen her. They never saw her, quite—she was not yet theirs, not yet entered in any book, and the court's attention slid off the unclaimed the way rain slid off oiled cloth. But that grace was expiring. The moon above Highmoor was swelling toward its thirteenth turn, and every hour it grew, the court's gaze would sharpen, until she stood out in this dimness like a candle in a cellar.

*Now,* said the part of her mind that sounded like Corin. *Whatever you're going to do, do it now.*

She lifted the jar from its shelf.

It was warm. That was the first cruelty of it—warm, like a hand, like a cheek, like the little luck-knots she sold to frightened children. Someone's yesterday, sealed in glass, and still holding heat after thirty-three years because her grandmother had felt things too large to cool.

There was no ceremony to opening it. The wax crumbled under her thumbnail like old bread, and the stopper came free with a sound like a sigh, and the darkness inside rose up around her face—

—and the archive was gone.

* * *

Rain. Rain on the guildhall roof, the old roof, before the leaks, before the rot, when the guildhall still smelled of beeswax and cut herbs instead of damp and surrender. Ada knew the smell instantly, helplessly; it was the smell of her childhood, which had been the smell of her grandmother's workroom, which had been the smell of everything safe.

She was seeing through her grandmother's eyes.

Elara Sterling stood at the guildhall window, watching the rain come down on Highmoor, and her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against the sill to make them stop.

Below, in the great room, the guild sat in session—all of them, all eleven masters, though only seven still mattered. Fenwick was there, young then, his beard still brown, his voice still carrying that note of reasonable despair he had made into an art form. Ada recognized the others from portraits and from Fenwick's endless, bitter anecdotes: Master Ivo with his burned hands. Mistress Calla, who had taught weather-charms until weather-charms were illegal. Old Mother Quill, who was older then than anyone was now.

They were arguing about closing.

*"Three shops shuttered this season,"* Fenwick was saying, in a voice Ada almost couldn't reconcile with the man who had begged her to let the bargain stand. *"Four last season. The inspectors took Ivo's whole stock on Tuesday—Ivo, who charmed the mayor's own mother's arthritis for twenty years. We are not a guild anymore, Elara. We are a list of arrests waiting to happen."*

*"Then we arrest ourselves quietly and go home,"* said Mistress Calla, *"and in ten years no child in Highmoor will know what a charm is."*

*"Better than the alternative."*

*"Is it?"* Mother Quill's voice, dry as paper. *"I've seen the alternative. I remember the famine courts. I remember what the city does to people with empty hands and hungry children. Magic isn't the only thing they've outlawed. Hunger is next, mark me, and we are the ones with the stores."*

Ada watched her grandmother turn from the window. Elara Sterling was fifty-one years old and looked seventy; the last five years had carved her down. But her eyes—Ada's eyes, Ada's exact eyes—were still burning.

*"There is another way,"* Elara said.

The room went quiet the way rooms go quiet when everyone has already guessed.

*"Don't,"* Fenwick said softly. *"Elara. Don't say it."*

*"You know what's under the city. You've always known. Our founders knew—they built the aqueduct over the stair for a reason. The Hollow Court takes contracts. It has always taken contracts. One soul, freely given, per generation—that is the old law, the deep law, older than the prohibition, older than the inspectors. And in exchange—"*

*"In exchange it eats you,"* Calla said. *"Body, name, and memory. No one comes back. No one is even remembered to have gone. Is that the trade? Say it plainly, Elara, since you'll say it at all."*

*"No,"* Elara said. *"Not my soul."*

And here the memory did something strange—it shuddered, the way a candle gutters, because thirty-three years later the woman remembering it was flinching even inside her own recollection. Ada felt the flinch pass through her like cold water.

*"A soul freely given,"* Elara said, and her voice cracked on *freely*, and steadied. *"It must be given freely. That is the law. I cannot give mine because I am needed—someone must hold the guild together, someone must sign, someone must keep the accounts. So it will be a soul given on the guild's behalf. A soul with no ties. A foundling, if we can find one. A drifter. Someone the city has already forgotten."*

*"You mean someone no one will miss,"* Calla said, with terrible precision.

*"I mean someone whose absence will cause no grief,"* Elara said, and Ada heard, beneath the words, the sentence her grandmother could not finish: *whose absence will cause no grief, because I cannot survive grief, because I have buried a husband and a daughter is coming and I have nothing left to bury with.*

The argument went on for hours. Ada lived all of it in the space between two heartbeats. She heard Fenwick argue mercy and lose to arithmetic. She heard Mother Quill recite the names of the guild's dependents—widows, apprentices, the orphaned children of arrested charm-wrights—as though a list could balance a life. She heard Calla storm out and come back, because where else was there to go.

And then she heard the door open, and saw him.

He was young. Younger than Corin was now—younger than Ada, barely more than a boy, with rain in his dark hair and the particular stillness of someone who has learned that stillness keeps him safe. He stood in the doorway of the guildhall with his cap in his hands, and he said, in a voice that was trying very hard to be steady:

*"Begging pardon. The tinker at the market said the guild pays for odd work. I'll do odd work. Anything. I'm strong, and I don't eat much."*

Ada's heart stopped, because she knew him. Not his face—his face was rounder then, softer, a boy's face. But she knew the way he stood. She knew the careful hands. She had spent weeks watching those same hands turn pages in a forbidden ledger, learning the shape of a name that appeared beside tomorrow's date.

Corin Ash. Or whoever Corin Ash had been, thirty-three years ago, before the name was ash.

*"What's your name, lad?"* Elara asked, and her voice was gentle, and Ada hated how gentle it was, because gentleness was the knife's wrapping.

*"Ash,"* the boy said. *"Just Ash. I was a foundling. The ash-wife raised me till she died."*

*A foundling.* The word landed in the memory like a coin in a well. *A soul with no ties. Someone the city has already forgotten.*

Ada wanted to scream. She was screaming, somewhere, in the part of herself that was still Ada Sterling standing in a cold archive holding a warm jar—she was screaming *don't, run, they are going to feed you to the dark and call it bookkeeping*—but the memory carried her forward without her, merciless as a river.

They fed him. That was the worst part. They fed him first—bread and stew and honey-cakes, the first honey-cakes he'd had since the ash-wife died, and he ate slowly, carefully, saving half of each cake the way hungry children save food, and Elara watched him save them and wept silently into her sleeve where no one could see, and *still did not stop.*

The contract was drawn that night. Ada saw it—saw the actual document, the one she had read a copy of in the court's records, the one bearing a signature in a child's careful hand. She watched the boy bend over it, frowning, sounding out the long words. She watched Elara kneel beside him and explain, in a voice that shook only once:

*"This says you'll go below and serve the court there. It says you'll be... changed. It says you won't be able to come back, or be remembered, and that's the hard part, Ash, that's the part I need you to understand most of all. Do you understand?"*

The boy was quiet a long time. Then he said: *"Will it hurt the village?"*

*"The village?"*

*"Fenwick's Row. Where I sleep. There's sickness there—the coughing kind. If the guild prospers, the tinker said, the guild buys medicine for the row. He said that's part of the bargain. That's why I came."*

And Ada felt her grandmother's heart break in real time, thirty-three years late, because the boy had not been lured at all. He had *come*. He had walked through the rain because a tinker—one of Fenwick's people, sent out deliberately, Ada understood now, sent out like a fisherman casting into shallow water for exactly this kind of fish—had told him the guild would buy medicine for the sick row if the guild prospered. He was selling himself to buy cough syrup for neighbors who would forget his name within the month.

*"Yes,"* Elara whispered. *"The row will be cared for. The guild will see to it. I swear it on my name."*

*"Then it's all right,"* the boy said, and signed.

He wrote *Ash*—just *Ash*, because that was all the name he had—and the letters were careful and slightly crooked, and Ada recognized the crookedness, and something in her chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

The rest happened fast, the way terrible things do. The stair beneath the aqueduct. The stones that opened. The descent—Elara insisted on walking him down, insisted on it as penance or as witness, the court required a witness, and so the guildhead herself led the lamb to the altar with her hand on his shoulder. Gray robes at the bottom. Voices like dry leaves. A magistrate reading the contract aloud in a language that made the walls sweat.

And at the very end, when the boy turned back once and smiled at her—*smiled*, because he believed he had saved his village, because he believed he was going to be a hero in a story no one would ever tell—Elara Sterling reached out and touched his cheek and said, so quietly the magistrates could not hear:

*"Forgive me. Whatever you become, wherever this puts you—forgive me."*

And the dark took him, gently, the way the dark takes everything it's paid for, and the memory ended.

* * *

Ada came back to herself on the archive floor with the empty jar rolling against her knee and tears freezing on her face in the hollow air.

She had expected rage. She had come to this place carrying rage like a lamp, ready to burn her grandmother's name out of her heart, ready to add *betrayer* to the list of truths she'd uncovered about the woman whose portrait hung above the shop's hearth. *Villains,* she had thought. *We were villains. Prosperous on a purchased boy.*

But the rage wouldn't light.

Because she had seen it now. Not the crime—the crime was real, the crime was enormous, and no amount of weeping un-weighed a soul. But she had seen the woman inside it: exhausted, terrified, drowning in a dying guild and a outlawed craft and a city that wanted her people erased, reaching for the one terrible door that was still open, telling herself the oldest lie the desperate ever tell: *no one will miss him. It will cost no one anything I love.*

Villains were desperate women first. That was the lesson of the green jar, and it was worse than villainy. Villainy she could fight. This—this was a mirror with thirty-three years of dust wiped off it, and Ada Sterling looking into it and seeing her own face wearing her grandmother's expression.

*Someone the city has already forgotten.* Wasn't that what she'd thought, the night Corin Ash walked into her shop and bought nothing? Hadn't she felt the pull of him precisely because he was untethered, unclaimed, a soul with no ties?

The apple hadn't fallen. It had been planted.

She knelt there a long moment, breathing, letting the archive's hum fill the silence where the memory had been. Then she set the empty jar upright on the shelf, in its place, beneath its label. Evidence returned. Let the court keep its proof; it would need it soon enough, when there was no court left to keep it.

She was rising, wiping her face, when she heard the footsteps.

Not a magistrate's drift. These were deliberate, booted, human—boots on stone, coming up the archive stairs two at a time, and beneath the sound of them, faintly, the scrape of something being dragged.

Ada pressed herself into the shadow of the memory-case as Inspector Vael rounded the gallery rail, his lantern held high, his coat dark with the wet of the aqueduct stair. His face was gray and set, and behind him, wrists bound with regulation cord, stumbling but upright, came Corin—alive, unharmed as far as she could see, his eyes finding hers across the dark for one blazing instant before Vael shoved him forward.

"Search the gallery," Vael said to the magistrates drifting up behind him. "She's here. The Sterling girl. And when you find her—" he paused, and his voice dropped into something quieter and more broken than duty, "—remind her that I also lost someone to this place."

The lantern light swept toward her shelf. Toward the empty jar. Toward the crumbled wax still scattered on the floor at her feet like breadcrumbs leading straight to her door.
