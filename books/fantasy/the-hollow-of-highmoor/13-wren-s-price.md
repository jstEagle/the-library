## 13. Wren's Price

The river in the border-hollow does not flow so much as remember. Wren had learned that a hundred years ago, in the first hour of her service, when she dipped an oar into water the color of spilled ink and watched it carry her boat not downstream but backward, toward every place the water had ever been. The Hollow's river ran on memory the way other rivers ran on rain.

She poled the ferry through the mist now, and the mist parted for her the way it never parted for the living. That was the courtesy the Hollow paid its own. Not kindness—courtesy. There was a difference, and Wren had a century of nights to learn it.

The two of them were waiting at the third landing, the girl and the ghost. Ada Sterling, guild-blood, smelling of lamp oil and stubbornness, with her grandmother's jaw set against the dark. And beside her the unremembered one, Corin Ash, whose name had begun to fray at the edges like a hem worked too many times. Wren could see it. Down here, where everything was written somewhere, she could see the loose threads.

They did not see her at first. The living rarely did. They stood on the wet stone landing arguing in whispers—she caught fragments: *the magistrates*, *the archive*, *we can't go back*—and Wren let them argue. It was a kindness, letting people finish the last argument they'd have as people who didn't know her. After this, everyone knew her, or thought they did. That was the trouble with being the only thing in the Hollow that remembered everything.

Except herself.

She brought the boat alongside and knocked the oar twice against the hull, the old signal, older than the court, older than the guild. The girl spun, hand going to her pocket where Wren knew she kept a warming-charm and a knife and, if she was anything like her grandmother, a third thing she hadn't decided about yet.

"You're the ferryman," Ada said.

"I'm the ferry," Wren said. "The boat and I came to an arrangement long ago." She gestured with the pole. "In, both of you. The mist doesn't hold still for conversation, and the magistrates eat on a schedule. You do not want to be on the water when they feed. The river remembers what they take, and it screams."

Corin Ash stepped in first, careful, the way the forgotten learn to be careful of everything—doors, thresholds, other people's eyes. Ada followed. The boat took their weight and something more; it always did. It took a little of their history, just to know them, and Wren felt it pass through her like cold water: a shop that smelled of rain. A ledger under floorboards. A child's hand signing something it shouldn't have been able to reach.

She poled them out into the dark.

* * *

The crossing to the court took as long as it took, which was never the same twice. Time in the border-hollow was a bargain like everything else, and the river spent it as it pleased. Wren used the hours the way she used all her hours: watching.

"You knew my grandmother," Ada said. It wasn't a question. It had the particular flatness of a question someone had already answered for themselves and was only checking the arithmetic.

"I've ferried every soul the guild ever sold," Wren said. "Your grandmother I ferried twice. Once when she came down to make the bargain, weeping into her gloves so the ink wouldn't smear. Once—" she let the pole rest, let the boat drift, "—once when she came down to look at what she'd bought. She stood on that landing where you're sitting and asked me if the man she'd sold could still see the sun."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the truth, which is the one currency I've never been able to stop spending." Wren turned her face toward the mist. "I told him he could see it. He just couldn't hold on to it. Nothing sticks to the forgotten, girl. Not sunlight. Not grief. Not other people." She felt Corin Ash go very still beside her, the stillness of a man being described accurately for the first time. "Your grandmother asked me if he suffered. I said no. That was the cruelty of it. He couldn't even keep his suffering. It slid off him like rain off oiled cloth."

Ada's voice came out low and rough. "And you? You remember everyone. The ferryman who remembers everyone. That's not a job. That's a sentence."

Wren laughed. It had been a long time since the sound had surprised her, but it did now, a little—a dry rustle, like paper turning.

"Sharp. Guild-blood, all the way down." She poled them around a bend where the mist thickened into something like walls. "You want to know what I am? Then look at me properly, Ada Sterling, and tell me what you see."

The girl looked. Wren watched her do it—the way her eyes went first to the face, then faltered. Wren's face was a thing that didn't hold still. A century of ferrying had worn it smooth the way water wears stone, and when people looked too long they saw, without meaning to, everyone they had ever lost. It made the living uneasy. It made the dead weep.

"You're—" Ada began, and stopped.

"Older than the court," Wren said. "That's the answer you're circling. The magistrates will tell you they built the Hollow, that they wrote the first prophecy, that the ledger under your mountain is their book. They believe it. Memory-eaters always believe they invented what they ate." She leaned on the pole. "But someone had to carry the first soul down. Someone had to hold the boat. And when the bargain was struck—*one soul per generation, freely given, and the guild prospers*—someone had to be the example. The first coin into the purse."

The mist pressed close. Corin Ash spoke for the first time, his voice quiet as a draft under a door. "You were sold."

"I was *given*," Wren corrected, and hated how the old word still fit her mouth like a ring worn so long the flesh grew around it. "That's the legal term. The magistrates are very fond of legal terms. It's how they sleep, if they sleep. I was given, freely, by a guildhead who believed she was saving her people from the famine years. And when the contract was fulfilled—when the court had taken what it took—they released me." She smiled, and felt her face do something complicated with it. "Incomplete."

"Released," Ada repeated. "You're free?"

"I'm *loose*," Wren said. "There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is the one thing in this whole Hollow I cannot remember." She held up her hand, and in the grey light they could see it properly: the fingers fading at the tips, not wounded, just *unfinished*, like a sketch the artist abandoned. "They took my name when they took my service. A soul isn't a whole thing, girl. It's pieces, and the name is the piece that holds all the others together. Take it, and everything else stays—every face I've ferried, every bargain, every weeping guildhead—but there's nothing to pin it to. I am a book with every page but the cover. I remember everyone." Her voice did not shake. She had practiced that for a hundred years. "Except who I was."

The boat drifted. Somewhere in the mist, water dripped in a rhythm almost like counting.

* * *

"They'll do the same to him," Ada said finally. "When the thirteenth moon turns. They'll take him and keep him, whole. That's what the ledger says. Thirteen turns and the soul becomes court property."

"Property," Wren agreed. "And then they'll begin to eat him properly. Slowly, over years, the way they eat everything—evidence, they call it. A memory is only evidence of having lived. They consume the past and call it justice." She watched the girl's hands close into fists, watched the guild-blood rise in her like heat. Good. Anger was fuel, and they would need every scrap of it. "You've seen the magistrates by now. You know what they are."

"We hid in their archive," Corin said. "We saw the contracts. Mine has—" his voice caught on it, the way it must catch every time, "—my own signature. From when I was a child."

"Every contract does," Wren said. "That's the court's oldest trick, and its cruelest. They don't take the desperate adult's signature. They go back along the thread of a life and find the child who would have signed anything to save his village, his mother, his guild—and they have him sign. Then the adult stands in the archive reading his own name in a child's hand and learns that his ruin was his own doing." She turned to look at him directly, and let him see her unfinished face. "You were six, Corin Ash. Whatever you signed, you signed because someone you loved was dying and a stranger in the mist said there was a price. A child's consent. That's the whole foundation of the Hollow Court. A hundred years of bargains, every one of them resting on signatures gathered the way a spider gathers flies."

"Then how," Ada said, and her voice had gone quiet in the dangerous way, the way her grandmother's must have gone quiet on this same water, "does it end?"

Wren had waited a century for that question. She had ferried forty bargainers down this river—guildheads and thieves, mothers and magistrates' own clerks—and every one of them had asked it, and every one of them had asked it *wrong*. They asked *how do I get him back*, or *how do I get out*, or *how do I keep what I have and lose only what I can spare*. Forty bargainers, and not one of them had asked the question the way this girl asked it: not *how do I win* but *how does it end*.

She poled the boat into a side channel, where the mist was thick enough to chew, and spoke with her back to them, because some truths were easier to say to a wall of fog than to a face.

"The contracts aren't the court," she said. "Understand that. The magistrates aren't the court either. They're *staff*. The court is the book. The master prophecy—the first ledger, the one your guild's little copy was made from. It sits in the heart of the Hollow, in the Chamber of Record, and every contract ever struck is a line in it, and every line is a chain, and the magistrates are only the hands that pull the chains." She turned. "Burn the contracts, and the magistrates re-ink them by morning. They've done it before. Burn the *book*—"

"The book is the court," Corin said slowly.

"The book is the court," Wren agreed. "The magistrates eat memories. The book *is* their memory. It's the only thing down here older than me. Unmake it, and every contract written in it unwrites itself. Every soul it holds goes free. Every name it ate comes home." She let that sit in the mist between them, and watched hope bloom on the girl's face the way she'd watched it bloom and die on forty faces before.

Then she took the hope and put her teeth in it, because hope without teeth was just another thing the Hollow ate.

"Here is the price, Ada Sterling, and I'll not soften it. The book can't be burned by force. Fire doesn't touch it—the magistrates made sure of that, or it made sure of itself, the two have blurred over the centuries. It can only be burned by *consent*. The same way every contract in it was made. Freely given. It has to be carried into the Chamber of Record by someone the book has a claim on, and it has to be given to the flame willingly, knowing what it costs." She held up her unfinished hand. "The book holds your line, guild-blood. Your grandmother's bargain, your mother's silence, and—" she watched the girl flinch, "—yes. You've read the thirteenth turn. The prophecy names the next seller, and the next seller is you. The book has a claim on you. You can carry it to the fire. But the book will bargain. It will offer. It will show you your guild's legacy, every stolen wonder, every charm your people sold before the outlawing, everything that was taken when magic was driven underground—and it will say: *burn me, and lose all of it forever*. The guild's whole inheritance, ash. Or keep the book, and keep the legacy, and keep Corin Ash in a cage of forgetting until the sun burns out."

Ada was silent a long moment. The river dripped its counting.

"And if I burn it," she said. "What happens to the guild?"

"It ends," Wren said simply. "Not with a death. With an ending. No more guild, no more legacy, no more inheritance. Everything your grandmother saved, spent. You'd walk out of the Hollow with a man who owes you everything and a shop that's only a shop." She smiled her complicated smile. "The magistrates know that's the price. It's why they're not afraid of bargainers. Everyone who comes down here wants to save the soul *and* keep the gold. Forty of them, girl. Forty. And every one chose the gold, or told themselves they'd find a third way, and the third way was a door the book had built for them."

"And you?" Corin asked. "You've ferried us down. You're telling us how to unmake the court. Why? What does Wren the ferryman get?"

The question landed where it always landed, in the hollow where her name should have been.

"I get my name back," she said. "Or I get nothing, which is what I have. The book holds it. First line, first entry, first coin. When it burns, everything it holds comes home—including the piece of me that's missing." She looked at her fading fingers. "I have ferried a hundred years of other people's bargains hoping one of them would be brave enough and foolish enough to finish the job. Forty failures. And then, three landings ago, I saw a guild-blood girl arguing with a ghost about whether to go forward or back, and I thought: *maybe*. Maybe the forty-first is the one."

The mist ahead was thinning. Through it, faint as a watermark, the lights of the Hollow Court had begun to show—cold, orderly, patient, a city of lamps arranged like a ledger's columns.

"One more thing," Wren said, and now she did turn to face them fully, and let them see all of her unfinished face at once, which was a cruelty and a gift and the only honest thing she had left to give. "The Chamber of Record is not unguarded. The Chief Magistrate keeps it as his own heart keeps its blood. And he has been expecting a guild-blood bargainer for a hundred years. He *wants* you to come, Ada Sterling. He wants it the way a fisherman wants the river to rise. Because there is a second entry in the master prophecy, newer than mine, and it doesn't just name you as the next seller." Her voice dropped. "It names you as the last. The one who binds the guild's line to the court *forever*. He doesn't mean to stop you. He means to *use* you. Every step you take toward that book, you're walking down a road he drew."

Ada's face in the grey light was her grandmother's face, and her own, and something newer than both.

"Then he'd better draw faster," she said.

Wren laughed again, and this time it didn't surprise her, and she poled the boat toward the lights of the court, and behind them, far up the dark channel, she heard—or thought she heard, and the river never lied to her—a third set of footsteps on the landing stones. Heavy. Booted. A man's tread, the tread of someone who had never learned to be careful of thresholds.

Someone from the sunlit world had followed them down.

"Sit low in the boat," Wren said quietly, and put her back into the pole. "We're not the only ones paying the ferry tonight."
