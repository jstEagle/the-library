## 14. What Corin Remembers

The archive of the Hollow Court smelled of cold candlewax and old paper and, beneath both, something faintly sweet—like fruit going over in a locked room. Corin had been breathing it for hours, kneeling in the aisle between the shelf-cases, and he had stopped noticing. That was the trick of this place, he was learning. It made you stop noticing things. That was its whole trade.

The shelves rose out of sight into a dark that the lamps refused to reach. Each case held sheaves, folios, boxes of grey vellum tied with black thread, and each one hummed very faintly against his fingertips when he touched it—the way a struck bell hums after the sound has gone, holding the shape of the noise inside itself. Memory did that here. Memory had weight. Memory had a sound it kept making even when no one was left to hear it.

He had come in with Ada's list of names—her grandmother's cipher, decoded by lamplight in the border-hollow, copied out in Ada's quick slanted hand—and he had worked through the night while Wren kept watch at the water-door and Ada slept badly on a bench in the reading alcove, her coat balled under her head, her hand curled near his as if even asleep she were checking that he still cast a shadow.

*Corin Ash,* her list said. *Sold under seal, year of the Long Frost. Buyer: the Office of Records. Seller: unnamed.*

Somewhere on these shelves was the box that would tell him who he had been before the city forgot him. He felt about it the way a man feels about a diagnosis. He wanted to know. He was afraid to know. He opened the cases anyway, one by one, because a man who has spent eleven years being nobody will crawl toward any scrap of himself the way a drowning man crawls toward driftwood—even when the driftwood is on fire.

* * *

He found the box an hour past the middle of the night, in a case marked only with a sigil like a closed eye.

Inside: a contract on vellum gone soft as cloth with handling. A ledger page, torn from some larger book, bearing his name in a clerk's copperplate. And a small stoppered phial, no bigger than a thumb, filled with a light that wasn't light—a slow grey shimmer, like fog remembering being a cloud. The label on the phial read, in a hand he somehow knew before he knew why: *ASH, C. — ACQUISITION, AGE ELEVEN — VILLAGE OF MARLOW BEND — PLAGUE-SEAL CONTRACT — SEE ADDENDUM.*

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He had expected them to shake.

He unstoppered the phial, and the court took him back.

* * *

Rain on a thatched roof. He was small. He knew he was small the way you know things in dreams—not by looking down but by the height of the table, the weight of the spoon, the way his mother's apron strings hung level with his eyes. Marlow Bend smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath, since midsummer, of lye and vinegar, because half the village was sick and the other half was scrubbing everything they owned twice a day.

The strangers came at dusk, three of them, riding grey horses that made no sound on the mud. They wore coats the color of the sky before snow. The villagers watched them from doorways with the particular silence of people who have already decided what they will deny later.

They sat at his family's table because his father offered, because you offer, because refusing the grey coats was how villages stopped appearing on maps.

"The plague is not natural," said the tall one, and his voice was pleasant the way ice is smooth. "It was sold here. Someone in your valley bought a sickness from below, and now the sickness is eating your children, and there is only one way to close the account."

Corin's mother's hand found his shoulder and gripped.

"A bargain," said the second stranger, laying a document flat on the table. "A soul, freely given, seals the plague-gate forever. One life against every life in the valley. That is the mercy of the Court, and we are its clerks."

"And if we refuse?" his father asked. His voice was very quiet.

"Then the gate stays open," the tall one said, "and in a season there will be no valley."

The candle flame leaned. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a child was coughing—that deep wrong cough, the one that had emptied three houses already—and everyone at the table heard it and no one moved.

"We have two sons," his mother said. "You're asking us to choose."

"We're asking no one to choose," said the clerk, and for just a moment the pleasantness fell off his voice like a mask slipping, and underneath was something tired and old and horribly kind. "The bargain must be freely given. Not by parents. Parents give out of love, and love is a debt, and debts bind crookedly." He turned his head, slowly, deliberately, and looked at Corin across the table. "It must be given by the soul itself. Willingly. Knowing the price."

The silence went on so long that Corin could hear the rain finding every gap in the roof.

He was eleven years old. He had watched his sister's friend Nell go into the ground a fortnight past; he had helped carry the little coffin because there weren't enough grown men left with strength enough to lift it alone. He had stood outside his brother's door for six nights listening to that cough and praying to gods whose names he only half knew.

And here was the thing he had never told anyone, the thing that lived in him all these years without his knowing it was there: he understood the arithmetic immediately. Completely. Without flinching. One against all. It was not even a hard sum. What was hard was watching the grown-ups fail at it—watching his father open his mouth and close it, watching his mother's grip tighten on his shoulder as though love were a rope that could hold him against arithmetic.

"I'll do it," Corin said.

His own voice sounded enormous in the room. His mother made a sound like something tearing.

"Corin—" his father began.

"Everyone keeps saying I'm too young to decide things," Corin said, "and then deciding everything for me. I can do sums." He looked at the clerk. "What happens to me?"

"You will be taken below," the clerk said gently. "Your name will be entered in the master ledger, and the ledger will be sealed, and the plague-gate with it. You will serve the Court as property until the term runs."

"How long is the term?"

"Thirteen turns of the moon," said the clerk, and Corin, who was eleven and could do sums, nodded, because thirteen moons was a year and a month, and a year and a month was survivable. Anyone could survive a year and a month.

He signed his name in the book they gave him. He wrote carefully, tongue between his teeth, because he had only learned cursive that winter and he wanted it to look like a man had written it. The letters came out wobbly but whole: *Corin Ash.* The ink drank into the vellum and glowed briefly, faintly gold, like an ember settling.

"There is one further condition," the clerk said, as the tall stranger rose to take Corin's hand. "The gate is sealed by forgetting as much as by giving. If the valley remembers the bargain, the gate remembers it too, and grief will pry it open again. So: the village will forget you were ever born. Your mother will wake tomorrow with one son and no ache where the other should be. This is the mercy. Do not fight it."

And his mother—this was the part that broke him even now, even in memory, even held at arm's length behind glass—his mother knelt down and took his face in her flour-dusted hands and kissed his forehead and said, "Thank you," and meant it, and that was the worst thing anyone ever said to him, and he carried it below.

* * *

Corin came back to himself on the floor of the archive with his cheek against cold stone and the empty phial rolling slowly beside his hand, and he lay there a while, because a man who has just remembered dying a little should be allowed to lie down.

So that was it. That was the whole of it.

He had thought—he had spent eleven years thinking, in the wordless way of the forgotten, the way a stone thinks about water wearing it down—that he had been stolen. Sold by someone else's greed, cheated, robbed. When Ada had shown him his name in her grandmother's ledger, the shame of it had lodged in him like a fishhook: *your guild bought its comfort with me.* He had let her carry the guilt of that. He had needed someone to carry it, because the alternative was carrying nothing at all, which is heavier.

But there was no theft. There had never been a theft. There was a boy at a table doing sums, signing his own name in wobbling cursive, choosing.

The anger didn't vanish. Anger is loyal; it stays. But it changed shape. Because you cannot be robbed of what you gave, and he had given it, freely, exactly as the clerk demanded—freely, knowingly, at eleven years old, more freely than any guildhead had ever given anything. Whatever the Hollow Court owned of him, they owned a gift, not a victim. The difference mattered. The difference was the whole architecture of his self, rebuilt in a single night on a cold floor.

And the term. Thirteen turns of the moon. He had signed for thirteen moons and served eleven years, because contracts below, Wren had told them, roll forward unless someone breaks the book that holds them. The Court hadn't merely kept him past his term. It had quietly rewritten the sum. Even his sacrifice had been skimmed, taxed, extended. The fury at that was clean and useful, and he folded it away like a tool he'd need soon.

* * *

"Corin?"

Ada's voice, sleep-roughened, from the alcove. He heard her cross the aisle, heard her kneel, felt her hand hover over his shoulder blade without landing—she had learned that about him, that being touched unannounced was like being grabbed by strangers, and everyone was strangers to him.

"I'm all right," he said. It came out hoarse. "I remember my mother's hands. Flour on the right one, always, from the morning kneading. She never got it all off, not once in my life." He sat up. The lamp put her face in amber and shadow. "There was a plague. In my village. Someone bought it—from below, from the Court's markets—and the only way to seal it was a soul, freely given. So I gave mine." He laughed, and it came out strange, and he let it. "I was eleven. I thought thirteen moons was survivable."

Ada said nothing for a moment. Then: "The ledger entry. My grandmother's cipher. It said *sold under seal.*"

"It was. By me." He watched that land in her—the guilt rearranging itself, the debt she'd been carrying shifting its weight. "Whatever your grandmother did, Ada, whatever the guild owes—it isn't owed to a stolen man. It's owed to a volunteer who got cheated on the change."

"That's not better," she said quietly.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. It's worse, actually. It means they took a child's best day and made it into their inventory." He reached for the torn ledger page, the clerk's copperplate. *ASH, C. — ACQUISITION — TERM: THIRTEEN TURNS.* "Look at this. Thirteen turns. I've been down here eleven years, Ada. Eleven years above, drifting through Highmoor, knocking on doors that never opened twice. The term ran out a decade ago, and nobody told me, because telling me would mean admitting the ledger could be read against them."

Ada took the page. Her thumb moved along the copperplate, stopped. "There's an addendum," she said. "The label mentioned an addendum."

She found it folded inside the box lid—a slip of vellum in a different hand, cramped, hurried, the writing of a clerk working late and ashamed:

*Addendum re: ASH, C. Acquisition lawful; consent genuine (see attestation). Term expired Yr. 12 of the Long Frost count. Renewal declined by subject per clause of free consent — subject cannot be lawfully retained. Note: Chief Magistrate has ordered retention continued regardless, citing "administrative necessity." Recommend the matter never reach the surface archives. The boy chose to save his valley. Letting him leave would set a precedent.*

Corin read it twice. The words stayed the same both times, which felt miraculous, given everything else words had done to him.

"They know," he said. His voice was very even. He noticed it was even and decided to keep it that way; it was the one thing he owned outright. "They've known for ten years that they're holding me illegally. There's a clerk who wrote that—who *knew*, and filed it, and lit a candle over it, and went home." He looked up at Ada, and found her eyes bright and fixed on him, and realized she wasn't looking at the paper anymore. She was looking at him the way you look at something you'd misjudged the size of.

"You were never nothing," she said. "I think—I think I half believed you were a blank. A hole where a man should be. I'm sorry."

"You shouldn't be sorry," Corin said. "You should be angry. I've got plenty; I'll share."

That surprised a laugh out of her, short and wet, and in the archive of the Hollow Court, surrounded by ten thousand shelved and humming memories, the laugh rang out into the dark and came back changed, and somewhere far off among the shelves something shifted, as if the building itself were turning over in its sleep.

* * *

They worked the rest of the night side by side.

It went easier now. Now that he knew what he was looking for—evidence, proof, precedent—the archive stopped being a mausoleum and became an armory. Corin pulled the attestation of his own consent, signed in his eleven-year-old hand, and laid it beside the addendum. Ada, sharp-eyed and relentless, dug out the plague-seal record for Marlow Bend: the gate closed, the deaths ended within the week, the valley saved entire—one life against every life, the sum done correctly, by a child, when the adults at the table couldn't.

"He did the arithmetic," Corin murmured, touching the record. "Somebody should say it. Somebody should say it out loud, once, after all these years." He cleared his throat, and in the empty archive, in a voice that shook only slightly, he said: "It was a good bargain. I'd make it again." And the shelves around them hummed, softly, all together, like a congregation agreeing.

Near dawn they found the others. Of course there were others. Once you knew the shape of the fraud you saw it everywhere: souls retained past term, renewals "declined by subject" and overridden, addendum upon addendum, a century of clerks filing their consciences in triplicate. Wren's name appeared four times. And once—Ada went white as bone when she found it—an entry marked *STERLING, next acquisition pending,* with the seller's line left blank and a date written in: the thirteenth turn.

"They're not keeping me past my term out of habit," Corin said. "They're keeping the machinery running. Me until the moon turns, and then you, and then whoever comes after. Forever. A guild-line bound to the court in perpetuity." He stacked the papers with hands that had finally, now, begun to shake. "We don't burn a contract, Ada. We burn the book."

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the great bell of the Hollow Court began to toll the hour before dawn—and under its voice, threading down through the archive's high dark window, came a sound Corin knew from the sunlit world and had hoped never to hear again down here: boot-heels on marble. Measured. Patient. Getting closer.

Ada blew out the lamp.
