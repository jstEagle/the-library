## 15. Vael Follows the Stair

The door of the charm-shop had been marked for three days before Vael understood what the mark meant.

He had made it himself, of course—chalk and ash, the sigil of open investigation that every inspector in Highmoor learned before they learned to shave. A circle, bisected. *Watched.* He had pressed it into the paint above the lintel with his own thumb, and Ada Sterling had watched him do it with that flat, tired look of hers, the look of a woman who had been watched her whole life and had stopped finding it interesting.

But three days later, the mark had *moved*. It sat lower on the frame, tilted, as if the door itself had shrugged it off and someone had pressed it back on out of politeness. And beneath it, in a hand that was not his, someone had written a single word in the old guild cipher—the cipher Vael had spent eleven years learning to read, letter by stolen letter, from confiscated books he was supposed to burn:

*Gone below.*

He stood in the fog at the mouth of Copperlane with his hand flat against the door and felt something in his chest turn over, slow and heavy, like a boat coming about.

They had gone down. Sterling and the ghost-man, the one no record could hold, the one whose name Vael had chased through four registries and found only once, in a ledger that predated the prohibition itself. They had found the stair.

Vael had spent years telling himself that if they ever did, he would simply write his report. *Subjects fled the city. Pursuit impossible.* A clean lie, filed in a clean hand, and the whole rotten business would be someone else's problem—someone senior, someone with a pension and no curiosity left.

He told himself that now, standing in the cold, and then he went home and took the lantern down from the shelf where it had gathered dust for thirty years, and he did not ask himself why.

* * *

His brother's name had been Tam.

Vael had been six when Tam went out to buy bread and did not come home. He remembered the shape of that day the way you remember the shape of a room you grew up in—every corner of it, worn smooth by handling. His mother's coat still on the hook. The loaf that never arrived. The way his father went to the magistrate's office every week for a year, and then every month, and then stopped going, and the way the family's photographs of Tam grew fewer, and then fewer, until one day Vael had counted and there were none, and when he asked his mother where they had gone she had looked at him with a face like a shut door and said, *What brother?*

He was eight when his family forgot his brother on purpose. Or something had made them forget, and they had let it, because a forgotten son hurt less than a lost one.

Vael had not forgotten. That was the whole of his secret, the thing he had carried through the academy and the inspectorate and eleven years of raids on charm-shops where frightened people sold warmth to cold children. He had not forgotten, and so he had become the man he became: precise, patient, unsmiling, the inspector who read confiscated grimoires cover to cover before burning them, who memorized the guild ciphers, who kept a list of every soul in Highmoor whose records had gaps in them the way a body keeps a list of its pains.

Tam Vael. Disappeared 14th of Harvest Month, age nineteen. There had been a ledger, once. Vael had found a fragment of it in a raid on the Ferrow Street cellars, a single torn page in a cipher he could not then read, and on it, half a line: *...the Vael boy, second of the harvest lots, freely given by—*

Freely given by whom? His father had been a dock clerk. His mother mended sails. What could they have possibly been given? What had they been *promised*?

He had never learned. But he had learned this, over years of listening in the gray corners of the city: that the guilds of the old days had paid their debts to the Hollow Court in souls. One per generation. Freely given by the guildhead, the old books said, and Vael had always understood *freely* the way you understand the word on a contract—as a legal fiction, a word that meant the opposite of what it said.

And now Ada Sterling, last daughter of the last guild, had gone down the stair with a man who did not exist, thirteen days before the moon finished its turn.

Vael did his arithmetic on the walk to the aqueduct. The thirteenth moon. The ledger fragment. *Second of the harvest lots.* Tam had been taken in Harvest Month. The moon's turn ran thirteen months, the old books said, and the turn that had begun the year Tam vanished would end—

He stopped walking.

It would end now. In thirteen days. Which meant that somewhere beneath the mountain, in a court that ate yesterday for breakfast, there was a record with his brother's name in it, and that record was about to come due.

He had told himself he was going down to arrest two fugitives. He was a good liar. He had been lying to himself for thirty years, and he was very practiced at it, and it had never once worked.

* * *

The aqueduct was older than the prohibition, older than the city charter, a spine of black stone running out of the mountain's shoulder and into the fog. Vael had walked its length twice on routine inspection and found nothing, because routine inspection looks with the eyes you are issued and no others.

Now he walked it at dusk with the lantern unshaded, and he looked the way his brother's ghost would have looked, and he found it in twenty minutes.

The stair was behind a wall of fitted stone beneath the third arch, and the wall was not hidden so much as *uninterested*—it had the quality of a thing that had decided not to be noticed, and Vael felt its indifference press against him like deep water. His lantern flame leaned toward it. That was the old sign, the one from the confiscated books: flame leans toward working stone.

There were boot prints in the silt at the base of the arch. Two sets. One light and quick—Sterling, who walked like a woman used to counting her steps against rent. One set that troubled him more, because it was faint, half-erased, as though the stone could not quite agree that the man who made it had weight.

The ghost-man. Corin Ash. The name that appeared in a century-old ledger beside a date that had not yet come, when Vael had first read it, and had since come.

Vael stood at the sealed wall for a long time. He was not a brave man; he had never thought of himself as one. Bravery was for people who had something to lose that they valued more than the thing they wanted. Vael had spent thirty years with exactly one possession—a name, a shape, a boy of nineteen with flour on his sleeves—and he had guarded it so jealously that he had built his whole life into a lockbox around it.

He put his hand on the stone and said, in the old cipher-tongue, the words he had learned from books he should have burned:

"The Vael boy, second of the harvest lots."

The stone thought about it.

He had expected to be refused. He had expected, honestly, to be arrested by the wall itself, or dropped through the mountain's gullet, or simply ignored until he froze. What he had not expected was for the stones to *recognize* him—to feel, through his palm, a slow attention like the attention of something very old turning over in its sleep, and then a warmth, and then the seam of the wall opening like a book to a marked page.

The stones knew his name. Not Vael's—*Tam's.* The debt was still on the books. The court remembered his brother, even if no one else did. He was not a man walking into the dark. He was a creditor, walking into a debtor's house.

He took the lantern and went down.

* * *

The stair went on longer than any stair under any city had a right to. Vael counted four hundred steps and then stopped counting, because the numbers began to feel like a rope he was holding onto and he did not want to know what happened when the rope ran out. The air changed as he descended—colder, then warmer, then neither, an air with no weather in it at all, air that had been indoors so long it had forgotten the sky.

And his memories began to loosen.

He noticed it first as a slip: he was halfway down, thinking of the report he would never write, and he could not recall the color of his mother's kitchen. He knew it had been a color. He knew he had eaten ten thousand meals in it. The fact remained; the color was gone, scooped out, like a word on the tip of the tongue that never arrives.

Then, three hundred steps further, he reached for the memory of Tam's face and found it *soft*—not gone, never gone, he would not allow it gone, but blurred at the edges, the way a photograph blurs when you handle it too much. He stood on the stairs in the dark with his lantern trembling and did the only thing he knew how to do: he said the name. Out loud. Into the stone.

"Tam."

The stone said it back to him, in a whisper that was not an echo. And the face came sharp again, for a moment—flour on the sleeves, that crooked grin, the way he'd whistled through his teeth when he was thinking—and Vael understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the temperature, what this place was for.

It was a place where memory was currency. And he had just paid his first coin at the door, and the toll would only get heavier, and he had thirty years of hoarded memories and one that mattered, and he would spend every other one he had before he spent that.

He gripped the lantern and went on down, saying the name at every hundred steps, like a man counting beads.

* * *

He caught them in the Market of Small Mercies.

He had meant to catch them on the stair, but the stair had beaten him down—had taken the color of his mother's kitchen, and the smell of his father's pipe, and the name of the street he'd grown up on, and by the time he reached the bottom he was a man walking on a floor made of missing things, careful not to look down. So he had hesitated, and hesitated again, and the hesitation cost him the stair and gave him the border-hollow instead: a gray market strung with lanterns that burned without heat, stalls selling nothing he could name, and a ferryman's boat rocking at a pier of black water.

They stood at the ferryman's stall. Sterling had her back to him. The ghost-man was facing him, and even across the market, even in that lightless light, the man's eyes found Vael's and held them, and Vael saw the moment he was recognized—not as an inspector, but as a face. The ghost-man's mouth moved. Vael read the shape of it across the distance: *You came down too.*

Then Sterling turned, and Vael moved, because he had been moving toward this for thirty years and his body knew the choreography even if his reasons had rotted through. He came out of the stall-shadow with his hand already closing on her wrist, and he turned her, and put her back against the pier-post, and said the words he had rehearsed in a hundred imagined versions of this moment:

"Ada Sterling. By the authority of the Prohibition Act, you are—"

"You're Tam's brother."

The words went through him like a blade through wet paper.

He had never said the name aloud in Highmoor. Not once, not in thirty years. It existed nowhere in any file, any report, any record—the inspectors' registry listed him as an only child, because the registry had been corrected the same week the photographs vanished, and Vael had let it stand, because a man with no brother had nothing to grieve and grief was not a quality the academy rewarded.

"How," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "How do you know that name."

"The ferryman." Sterling's wrist was thin in his grip; she did not pull. Her eyes—gray, exhausted, furious with a fury that had nothing to do with him—went past his shoulder to the ferryman, Wren, a small gray woman who was watching Vael with an expression he could not read and did not want to. "She remembers everyone. She said—she said there was a boy from the harvest lots, thirty years back, who had a little brother who used to wait for him at the dock stairs. She said the little brother grew up and became a *lawman*." Sterling's mouth twisted. "She said it like it was the saddest part."

The market seemed to lean in. The heatless lanterns guttered. Somewhere in the gray stalls, something laughed softly, or maybe it was only the water.

Vael became aware, with terrible clarity, of what he was. A grown man in an inspector's coat, holding a woman's wrist at the edge of the world, having descended a haunted stair on the strength of a torn page, and the first person he'd met down here had already told her his whole story—the story he had told no one, the story he had kept locked so long he'd forgotten the lock was made of his own ribs.

"I'm here to take you up," he said. "Both of you. Out. Before the moon turns."

"You're here to trade me." Ada Sterling said it without heat, the way you state a price. "For your brother's record. Wren told me that too. She said the lawman would come, and he'd want a trade, and I should decide now what I'm worth to myself."

The ferryman's boat knocked gently against the pier. Knock, knock. Like a door. Like a door that had been knocking for thirty years.

Vael's hand opened. He hadn't decided to open it. His fingers simply let go, one by one, the way a fist lets go in sleep.

"I don't want a trade," he said, and heard, as he said it, that it was both a lie and the truth, which was the only kind of sentence this city allowed him anymore. "I want my brother back."

"He's not your brother anymore," Sterling said gently, and it was the gentleness that broke him. "He's a line in a ledger. And in thirteen days the ledger comes due, and the line gets *collected*. If you take me up now, you save me and you lose him forever, and you'll spend the rest of your life writing reports about charm-shops with his name in your mouth that no one can hear."

The lantern in Vael's hand shook. He was aware of the ghost-man watching, and of the ferryman watching, and of the gray water, and of four hundred steps behind him that had eaten his mother's kitchen and his father's pipe and the color of a morning in a year he could no longer name, and he understood that he was standing at the exact center of the choice he had been walking toward since he was six years old, and that every road out of it went through the court above the black water.

"The magistrates will put you on trial," he said. "If I bring you in. They'll feed you to the court, memory by memory. I've read the old procedures. I know what a trial by memory is."

"I know," said Ada Sterling. "I'm going anyway."

He looked at her then—really looked, inspector's eyes, the eyes that had been trained to find the guilt in any face—and what he found was the thing he had never once found in thirty years of looking: a person with nothing left to protect except the thing she'd come to do.

Vael closed his eyes. When he opened them, he took the inspector's seal from his coat—the brass, the weight of the law itself, the thing he had polished every night for eleven years—and he set it down on the pier-post between them, and left it there.

"Then I'm not taking you in," he said. "I'm going with you. And when we find the ledger, I'm reading my brother's line first."

Behind him, soft as a tide, the ferryman began to unmoor the boat.
