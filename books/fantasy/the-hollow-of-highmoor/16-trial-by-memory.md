## 16. Trial by Memory

They did not chain her. That was the first cruelty.

Ada had expected irons—cold iron, the kind the city inspectors carried in their belt-pouches for the worst offenders, the kind that bit through a charm like teeth through bread. Instead, two magistrates in robes the color of wet slate simply walked on either side of her down the long gallery of the Hollow Court, and their presence alone was enough. Their nearness pressed against her mind like thumbs against her eyes. She could feel them *reading* her with every step, tasting the edges of her thoughts the way a man might run his tongue along his teeth to find the loose one.

The gallery itself was impossible. It had been carved—or grown; she could not tell which—from pale stone that seemed to remember light it had never seen. Lamps burned without flame along its length, each one a small silver sphere that pulsed faintly, and as they passed, Ada understood what fed them. Memories. Thousands of them, distilled and burning slow. She caught fragments as she went: a wedding dance, a drowning, a woman laughing over spilled flour, a boy's hand around a wooden horse. Each lamp had been someone's whole afternoon once. Now they were lighting.

*Don't look at them,* she told herself. *Don't look at anything you can't afford to lose.*

Corin was somewhere behind her. She could hear his footsteps, uneven, and Wren's low voice murmuring something that might have been a warning or a prayer. Vael walked ahead of the magistrates with his hands bound in a cord of grey silk—the court did not trust him, but it wanted what he knew—and every few paces he glanced back at Ada with an expression she had never seen on an inspector's face before. Not triumph. Not even duty. Something closer to apology.

Then the gallery opened into the chamber of judgment, and there was no more time for looking anywhere at all.

* * *

The courtroom was a bowl of black stone, and it was full of yesterday.

Ada felt it before she saw it—a pressure in the air like the moment before rain, thick with other people's lives. The benches rising in tiers were occupied by shapes she hesitated to call people. The magistrates of the Hollow Court wore faces the way other creatures wore borrowed coats: loosely, and only when convenient. Most of the time their features were smooth as river stones, and where eyes should have been there were shallow pools that reflected not the room but moments from it—flashes of memory surfacing and sinking like fish beneath ice.

At the center, on a dais of veined marble, stood the Chief Magistrate. He had chosen a face for this occasion—an old man's face, dignified, lined with the suggestion of wisdom—but the face sat slightly wrong, like a portrait hung a half-degree off true, and behind it Ada could see the stone-smooth blankness waiting to take it back.

"Ada Sterling," the Chief Magistrate said, and his voice was many voices braided together, some of them hers. "Of the Guild of Charmwrights. Daughter of Marigold. Granddaughter of Edith Sterling, who signed."

The name landed in Ada's chest like a dropped stone.

"You stand accused of no crime," he continued, spreading hands that were almost hands. "Understand this. The Court does not punish. The Court *weighs*. Your guild entered covenant with us a generation past. One soul, freely given, per turn of thirteen moons, against the continuance of your charter and the protection of your members. That covenant is written." He gestured, and above the dais the air unfolded—a ledger vast as a sail, pages turning without wind, ink crawling across them like living things. "But covenants are only as strong as the memory that sustains them. And so we must establish, before all assembled, whether you understood what was given, and what is owed."

"And if I don't?" Ada said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

The Chief Magistrate smiled with the old man's mouth. "Then we will help you remember. That is what we are for."

He raised one hand—not quite a hand, more the idea of one—and the trial began.

* * *

It started gently. That was the trap, and Ada saw it too late.

A magistrate descended from the first tier, robes whispering, and laid fingers cool as well-water against her temple. There was no pain. There was only a soft *tug*, like a thread being drawn out of cloth, and then—

—her mother's kitchen, steam on the window, cinnamon and burnt sugar, her mother humming off-key while Ada, six years old, stirred batter with both hands because one wasn't strong enough—

—the memory lifted out of her whole and glowing, and the Chief Magistrate held it up to the light like a jeweler assessing a stone.

"The subject's earliest recollection of domestic warmth," he announced, and the pools of the magistrates' eyes flickered with steam and cinnamon. "Accepted into evidence. The subject demonstrates capacity for attachment. Note it."

And the memory was gone. Not dimmed—*gone*, plucked clean, leaving behind a hole shaped exactly like that morning. Ada groped for it and found only a smooth absence, a word missing from the middle of a sentence. She knew she had loved her mother's kitchen. She could not recall why, or how it smelled, or the sound of the humming. She knew it the way you know a town you've never visited: by report, by inference, by the shape of its absence.

"That's mine," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word.

"It is evidence now," the Chief Magistrate said. "Everything given freely to the Court belongs to the record. As your grandmother understood."

*Don't let them see it shake you.* Wren's voice in her head, or maybe just her own courage wearing Wren's voice. *They want the fear. Fear is a flavor.*

The second magistrate took her first day at the shop—the bell over the door, the smell of beeswax and rain, her grandmother's hand heavy on her shoulder saying *this will be yours someday, all of it, everything under these boards.* Gone. The third took her mother's funeral, and Ada would have screamed except that halfway into the scream she realized she could no longer fully remember her mother's face, only that she had once known it, and the scream turned inward and became something quieter and much worse.

They worked methodically. They were thorough. They were, in their terrible way, respectful—each memory handled with care, examined, named, entered into the great turning ledger above the dais. *The subject's grief at age nineteen. Accepted. The subject's first successful charm. Accepted. The subject's shame upon deceiving Inspector Vael. Accepted.*

Piece by piece, Ada Sterling was disassembled and filed.

She learned quickly which memories they wanted most. Not the big ones—they took those almost carelessly, the way a farmer takes a fencepost. What made the magistrates lean forward, what made the pools of their eyes churn, were the small ones: the exact weight of a luck-knot in a child's palm; the particular grey of the fog on the morning Corin Ash first opened her door; the taste of the tea she'd drunk the night she found the ledger. Specificity. Texture. The Court was starving for texture. It ate centuries old, coarsened memories, and fine-grained human moments were delicacies, and Ada understood with a cold sliding horror that her ordinary life was a feast.

*Hold on to something,* she told herself, as the fourth magistrate approached. *Pick one thing and hold on to it until your fingers bleed.*

She chose his face.

* * *

They came for Corin in the ninth hour, and that was when the trial stopped being survivable and started being a war.

By then Ada had lost, by her count, forty-some memories. She had lost the name of her childhood street. She had lost every birthday after her twelfth. She had lost the feel of rain—actual rain, on skin—which in Highmoor was like losing a language. She sat in the witness hollow at the center of the bowl, and around her the lamps of the gallery burned a little brighter with each withdrawal, fed on her, and the great ledger overhead grew fat with her handwriting, her life transcribed and archived and taken.

But she still had his face. She had built a wall around it in the rubble of herself, and behind that wall she kept it the way you keep a coal alive through a long night: cupped, breathed on, fed.

Grey-green eyes. A jaw that looked like it had been drawn quickly by someone who'd been interrupted. The small scar through the left eyebrow he'd never explained. The way he looked at doors before opening them, always, as if asking permission. She rehearsed it hour after hour while the magistrates stripped everything else, and when they reached for the wall she threw other things at them—old grudges, dull afternoons, the memory of stubbing her toe in urchin-dark alleys—to buy the coal another minute.

Then the Chief Magistrate said, "Bring the asset," and Corin Ash was led into the bowl, and Ada understood what the trial had actually been about.

"This," the Chief Magistrate announced to the tiers, "is the soul in question. Sold by Edith Sterling, freely given, twelve turns past, with the thirteenth due at the coming of the full moon. You will observe—" and here he turned the old man's face toward Ada with something that in a human would have been tenderness, and was infinitely worse, "—that the seller's line has developed an attachment to the property. Sentiment. We must determine whether this sentiment compromises the covenant, or completes it."

"They're going to make me watch," Ada said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else's. "That's the point, isn't it. You don't need my memories to weigh the contract. You need me to *feel* it."

"The law is feeling, properly administered," said the Chief Magistrate.

They put her memory of the shop door opening—his first entrance, the fog rolling in at his heels, the words *I'm looking for something against being remembered*—and they held it up between them, the three of them arranged in the hollow like figures on a card, and they began to unmake it in front of her.

Not all at once. In pieces. First the fog went, drawn out of the scene like dye from water, and the doorway stood flat and dry and wrong. Then the sound of the bell. Then the smell of the tea going cold on the counter. Then his voice, so that the memory played on as silent theater, his mouth shaping words she could no longer hear. And last—slowly, luxuriantly, the way a gourmand saves the best bite—they began to draw out his face.

"No," Ada said.

The grey-green eyes wavered like reflections on disturbed water.

"No!" She was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. The nearest magistrate turned toward her with the mild interest of a thing watching weather, and she heard Wren shout something, and Vael strain against the silk cord, and none of it mattered, none of it existed, because the face behind her wall was dissolving and she was the only one in the world who had it and she was losing it—

*Think,* she screamed at herself, inside, in the cellar of her skull where they hadn't yet dug. *Think of something they haven't found. Something they can't find, because it never happened yet.*

Because that was the flaw in their hunger. The Court ate *yesterday*. It said so in its very bones—*the Magistrates Who Eat Yesterday*. Every lamp in the gallery, every page in the ledger, every pool in every eye was the past. They had archives older than the mountain. They had everything that had ever been done.

They had nothing that had not.

So Ada, standing in the witness hollow with her life stacked in evidence around her, did the only magic left to her. She closed her eyes, and behind the wall, beside the fading coal, she built a memory that had never occurred.

She built tomorrow. She built the shop after everything—the sign repainted, the bell mended, fog outside the glass and warmth inside it, and Corin Ash sitting on the stool by the counter doing nothing, nothing at all, just existing in her shop in plain daylight, remembered by everyone who passed. She built the ordinary miracle of it: him handing her a cup of tea, him complaining about the damp, him *being bored*, gloriously, safely bored, because boredom was a luxury only the remembered could afford. She built it in detail finer than any memory she'd owned—finer than anything the Court had ever tasted—and she held it up inside herself like a lamp.

And the magistrates' heads turned. All of them. All at once.

"What is that?" the Chief Magistrate said, and for the first time the braided voices were not in unison.

"It's not evidence," Ada said. Her eyes were still shut. Tears ran down her face but her voice was iron, iron forged in the last warm place she owned. "You can't have it. It hasn't happened yet. It's not yours to eat—it's *mine to make*—and I am telling you, I am swearing it into whatever passes for law down here: it *will* happen. He will sit in my shop. He will be remembered. And no book, no bargain, no court under this mountain is going to stop it, because I have decided, and my decisions are the one thing you cannot file."

Silence in the bowl of black stone. The lamps pulsed. Somewhere in the great ledger overhead, a page turned of its own accord, slowly, as if listening.

Then the Chief Magistrate laughed—a sound like a library falling—and said:

"How touching. How very *guild* of you. But the moon does not consult your decisions, charm-witch." He raised the not-quite-hand, and high above, through a shaft in the vaulted stone, Ada saw the first thin edge of moonlight slide down the chamber wall—pale, precise, inevitable. "The thirteenth turn begins tonight. And when it ends, the property reverts, sentiment notwithstanding. Enjoy your tomorrow while you still have a self left to imagine it with."

The moonlight touched the floor.

And Corin Ash began, gently, softly, at the edges, to come apart.
