## 17. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The moon did not rise over the Hollow Court. It *arrived*—a slow silver pressure bleeding down through the vaulted dark above the chamber, as though the sky itself had been peeled back to let it in. Corin felt it before he saw it: a tugging behind his sternum, gentle at first, like a hook set in wool.

He was standing at the edge of the great weighing floor, pressed into the shadow of a pillar of black basalt that sweated cold, with Ada's hand locked around his wrist and Wren's ferry-pole braced across the doorway behind them. The magistrates sat in their crescent of thrones, hooded and patient, their faces nothing but smooth grey where faces should have been. They did not need eyes. They read him the way a clerk reads ink.

"The turn begins," said the Chief Magistrate, and its voice was the sound of a page turning in an empty room.

"I know," Corin said. His own voice sounded far away to him, thin as smoke. "I've felt it for thirteen moons. I just didn't know what I was feeling."

He had felt it the morning he woke in a boarding house whose landlady looked through him. He had felt it every time a door closed a half-second too soon behind him, every time a name he'd given slid off a stranger's ear like rain off glass. Thirteen small turnings, each one a little tighter, all of them leading here—to this chamber where the air tasted of old paper and colder iron, where his whole life lay stacked in shelves around him like evidence awaiting verdict.

Ada's grip tightened. "We're leaving. Now. Before it finishes."

"There is no *before*, seller's-heir." The Chief Magistrate rose from its throne, and its robes moved like poured ash. "There is only the reading. The contract has come due. The soul named Corin Ash passes to the Court entire."

"He isn't yours." Ada stepped forward, out of the shadow, and Corin's heart lurched—not from the moon's pull but from her courage, which had always been the most reckless thing about her. "He was sold under false weight. A child signed that contract. Children can't consent to be eaten."

"Consent," the magistrate said, tasting the word, "is a formality. Memory is the substance. And we have his memory in abundance." It gestured, and somewhere in the towering archive walls, a shelf sighed open. "Come. Let us begin with something small. A winter morning. Bread. A mother humming."

"No—" Corin reached for the shelf in his mind where that memory lived, and found the door already standing open.

It started gently, the way drowning starts. A warmth left him—the smell of bread baking on a frost-bright morning, the specific gold of light through a kitchen window when he was six. He felt it go the way you feel a word slip your tongue: there, then not there, then not even missed. In its place the moon pressed closer, filling the hollow it left, sweet and cold as well-water.

"Corin." Ada's face swam in front of his. Her hands were on his cheeks now, her thumbs cold against his skin. "Look at me. Stay with me. You know my name. Say it."

"Ada." It came out easily. That surprised him. Everything else was sliding, but she held fast, like a nail driven deep into old wood. "Ada Sterling. Your shop smells of rain. You sell luck-knots for two coppers and charge children nothing."

Something broke in her expression—relief and terror in the same breath—and she pulled him back against the pillar as the magistrates began to move.

They did not walk so much as *pour*, robes trailing, three of them gliding toward the center of the floor where a basin of liquid silverlight waited: the Reading Pool, where souls were weighed by what they could recall. The moon overhead brightened another degree. Corin felt a larger memory loosen—a whole year of it, some boyhood summer—and grabbed for it and caught only its edges, like trying to hold water in a fist.

"Wren!" Ada shouted. "Whatever you were going to do—do it now!"

* * *

Wren had been still so long that Corin had almost forgotten she was there. She stood in the doorway with her ferry-pole planted, her blind white eyes turned upward toward the moon, and there was something terrible in her face—not fear, but the particular fury of someone who has waited a hundred years for permission.

"They took mine first," she said quietly. Not to the magistrates. To the room. To the moon. "My name. I stood where he stands, and they took my name first, because a soul without a name cannot argue. I have ferried a century of bargainers past this door and watched every one of them come up short. No more."

She drove the ferry-pole down against the stone.

The sound it made was not wood on rock. It was a bell-note, deep and wrong, and the whole chamber answered it. Every shelf in the archive walls shuddered. And then the shelves began to *open*—all of them, hundreds, thousands, the preserved memories of every soul the court had ever consumed or held as evidence—and the memories came out.

They came out like weather.

Corin saw it happen and would spend the rest of his life failing to describe it. Light poured from the shelves in ribbons and rivers, and each ribbon was a life: a wedding seen from the back of a crowd, a fever broken at dawn, a dog waiting at a gate, a first snow, a last goodbye, ten thousand mornings of bread and rain and lamplight. The chamber filled with them, swirling, a blizzard of everything the Hollow Court had ever taken and hoarded, and the magistrates stopped mid-glide and turned their blank faces upward, and for the first time in a hundred years, they seemed—Corin would have sworn it—*afraid*.

"You keep us as evidence," Wren said, wading forward into the flood of light, her voice cracking like ice on a river. "You weigh us and file us and call it law. Then drown in your own ledger."

"What is she doing?" Ada had Corin's arm over her shoulders, hauling him toward the side passage they'd scouted two nights past, the one that wound up toward the archive levels. "Can she stop it? Can she put them back?"

"She's not putting anything back." Corin understood suddenly, with a clarity that cut through even the moon's pull. "She's spending them. Every memory she releases is one the court can't use as leverage anymore—one less brick in the wall. She's buying time the only currency this place respects." His throat closed. "And it's costing her whatever she has left."

Because he could see it. With every shelf Wren cracked open—with every burst of stolen lives she loosed into the air—she grew fainter. Not smaller. Fainter. The edges of her went soft, then translucent, then translucent again, like a figure painted too many times on the same pane of glass. The pole in her hands flickered between wood and mist.

"Wren!" he shouted. "You'll unravel!"

"Boy," she called back, and she was smiling, and it was the worst thing he had ever seen, "I unraveled a hundred years ago. This is just making it official. *Run.*"

* * *

They ran.

The side passage was narrow and spiraled upward through the basalt, lit by seams of moonlight that shouldn't have existed underground. Ada half-carried him, which should have been impossible—she was half his size—but grief and fury lend strength, and she had both in plenty. Behind them the chamber roared with released lives. He heard a magistrate speak for the first time in something other than ceremony: a high, thin sound, like a kettle complaining, like a thing that had never been interrupted before and did not know how to bear it.

"Seal the shelves. Seal the—there are too many—*where are they all going*—"

Everywhere. That was the answer. The memories weren't staying in the chamber; they were pouring out through every crack and vent, flooding downward into the border-hollows, upward into the aqueduct tunnels, outward toward Highmoor itself. Corin imagined them arriving: a baker on Tallow Street suddenly remembering a grandmother she'd never known she'd lost. A city inspector waking at his desk with his brother's laugh in his ears. The whole fog-bound city, seeded overnight with everything that had been stolen from it.

"It's beautiful," Ada panted. "It's the most beautiful thing I've ever—Corin, your hand."

He looked down. His fingers were going silver at the tips, faint as moonlit water, and the change was creeping up his wrist like frost climbing a windowpane. The moon had found him anyway. Wren's flood had bought minutes, not freedom. The turn did not need the magistrates' machinery; it needed only the date, and the date had arrived.

"How much of you is left?" Ada demanded, stopping on a landing, gripping his silvering hand in both of hers. "Tell me honestly. What do you still have?"

He took inventory, the way you take inventory of a house before the bailiffs come.

"My name. Yours. The shop. The smell of rain. Fenwick's terrible tea. The stair beneath the aqueduct opening for me like it knew me. My mother's face—I still have my mother's face, mostly, though the humming is gone now, the humming went first." His voice shook. "I have the feeling of being seven years old and choosing something brave, even if I can't remember what I chose. Does that count?"

"That counts," Ada said fiercely. "That counts more than anything. Hold onto it."

"The rest is going, Ada. I can feel it going like water through—" He stopped. Blinked. "What was I saying?"

Her face went white. "You were telling me what you remember. What else do you remember?"

"...We came down a stair. There was a ferryman. She had kind eyes, I think. Or no eyes. One of those." He laughed, and the laugh frightened him, because it was easy, unburdened, the laugh of a man with nothing heavy in his pockets. "Why am I laughing? Why does this feel like relief?"

"Because it was designed to," Ada whispered. "The sale. It was designed to feel like relief. That's how they get children to sign." She hauled him onward, up the spiral, faster now, and he let himself be hauled, because some deep surviving grain of him trusted her absolutely even as everything else drained away. "The archive's ahead. Grandmother's memory is stored there—Wren said the original sale is recorded inside it, and if the master prophecy is chained to it—"

"Chained to what?" Corin asked pleasantly.

Ada didn't answer. She couldn't. He watched her swallow it, watch her decide not to correct him, and he understood dimly that he had become a man with holes in him, and that she was memorizing the shape of those holes even as they widened, and that this—this grief she was carrying silently while dragging him uphill—was a heavier burden than any he'd been asked to bear.

Behind and below, the chamber erupted again. Wren's voice rang off the stone, thinner now, stretched nearly to transparency, and yet triumphant:

"*Take it back, then! Come and gather it, magistrates—come and pick through the flood—every minute you spend gathering is a minute they climb!*"

A roar of kettles. A crash of shelving. Then, cutting through everything, clear and cold and close, the Chief Magistrate's voice, no longer ceremonial, no longer patient:

"The flood changes nothing. The contract stands. The moon turns whether our shelves are full or empty. Seller's-heir—we know where you climb. Bring the soul to the pool yourself, and we will spare the shop. Refuse, and when the turn completes, we will come up the stair and read *your* city instead."

Ada's step faltered—just once, just a stutter of one boot on stone—and Corin felt it through her grip.

"Don't listen," he said.

"They have my shop. They have everyone in it."

"They have *nothing*." And the words came out of him with a force that surprised them both, rising from that deep surviving grain, from the seven-year-old who had chosen something brave. "Ada. Listen to me while I still know enough to say it. Whatever they offer, whatever they threaten—they only have power over things written down. So don't write anything down. Don't sign. Don't trade. Don't give them a single line of ink." His silvering hand found hers in the dark and gripped it, and for a moment the frost receded, pushed back by sheer stubbornness. "Promise me. While I can still hear you promise."

"I promise," Ada said. "No ink. No trades. No lines."

"Good girl." He smiled. "Whoever you are. Good girl."

Above them, through a seam in the ceiling, the thirteenth moon reached the top of its turn and hung there, poised, a coin balanced on its edge—and somewhere below, Wren spent the last of herself buying them one final minute, and somewhere ahead, in the archive where his childhood slept on a shelf, the master prophecy waited with its pages already beginning, very softly, to glow.
