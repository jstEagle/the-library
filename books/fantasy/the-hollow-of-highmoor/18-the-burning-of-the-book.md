## 18. The Burning of the Book

The flood came the way grief comes—suddenly, and from everywhere at once.

One moment I was pressed against the cold basalt of the trial dais, my wrists bound in ribbon-light that drank warmth from my skin, watching the magistrates lean over Corin like scholars over a dying text. The next, the chamber's eastern wall burst inward with a tide of light that was not water, though it moved like water, though it roared like water, though it soaked through me to the bone and left me shivering with other people's lives.

Memories. A century of them, stolen and stored and hoarded by the Hollow Court, all of it loosed in a single drowning wave.

Wren stood on the crest of it.

I had never seen her stand before. In the border-hollow she was always hunched over her ferry-pole, always small, always apologizing for taking up space in a world that had taken everything from her first. Now she filled the whole archway, her gray hair streaming behind her like smoke, her eyes blazing with a hundred years of borrowed fire.

"You kept *everything*," she said, and her voice cracked the vaulted ceiling. "Every memory you ate. Every soul you weighed. You couldn't help yourselves—you're magistrates, you *archive*. Well." She spread her arms wide, and the flood spread with her. "Archive *this*."

The wave broke over the court.

I saw what it did to the magistrates and I will not be able to describe it properly for as long as I live, but I will try. They were creatures made of accumulated evidence—centuries of contracts, confessions, testimonies, the raw stuff of other people's pasts packed into robes of shadow and bone-white masks. And when Wren's flood hit them, all of that evidence woke up at once. The memories they had eaten remembered themselves. The stolen lives surged inside their stolen vessels, demanding to be felt again, to be *owned* again, to go home.

Magistrate Ossuary—the one who had signed Corin's contract with a flourish of ink that moved like living things—clutched his mask as faces bloomed across its surface. A farmer weeping over blighted fields. A girl learning her grandmother's name. A soldier's last morning. They surfaced and sank and surfaced, and beneath the mask something howled.

"SEAL THE VAULTS," the Chief Magistrate thundered, and its voice shook dust from rafters older than the city above. "SEAL THE FLOOD OUT. THE CONTRACT CANNOT—"

"The contract," Wren said, wading toward the dais through light up to her knees, "*can drown.*"

The ribbon-light binding my wrists frayed as the flood washed through it. I tore free and ran—not away from the chaos, but into it, because I had seen where the Chief Magistrate had turned, and I knew what it was trying to protect.

Behind the judgment dais, set into the wall like a heart set into a chest, stood the master prophecy.

I had glimpsed it only once before, during my desperate hours hiding in the court's archive: a book the size of a door, chained shut with links of frozen moonlight, its cover stitched from leather I had refused then to think too hard about. The Hollow Prophecies. Not the copy in my grandmother's ciphered ledger beneath the floorboards of the shop—that had been a merchant's record, a bookkeeper's tally. This was the original. Every contract ever struck between the guild above and the court below flowed out of these pages like blood from a wound. Grandmother's bargain. Fenwick's signature. Corin's sale. My own name, waiting in the margins for the thirteenth turn to make it true.

Every soul sold. Every freedom traded. All of it written here, and while it was written, it was *law*.

The Chief Magistrate reached it before I did. Of course it did—it had been standing beside this wall for longer than Highmoor had streets. Its long fingers closed around the chain of moonlight, and the frozen links began to glow, drinking the flood's light the way a drain drinks rain.

"You cannot unmake what is written," it said. It wasn't speaking to me. Magistrates don't speak to people; they speak to *evidence*, and I was, in their ledger, merely an entry not yet filed. "The words are older than your anger, seller's daughter. The words are the bones of this place. Burn the book and you burn the city's foundations."

"Then the city," I said, "was built on the wrong thing."

It turned its mask toward me then—slowly, the way glaciers turn—and for the first time since I had descended into the Hollow Court, I understood that it saw me. Not as inventory. As opposition.

"Ada Sterling," it said, tasting my name. "Granddaughter of Maren, who wept so beautifully when she signed. We keep her tears in a jar. Would you like to see them? We could show you exactly how much your legacy cost, line by line, until you understand that destroying us destroys *you*—your shop, your guild, every charm you ever warmed a child's hands with. Without our contracts, your guild has no history."

"My guild," I said, "has no *future* while you hold its debts. I've done the arithmetic, magistrate. I learned it at my grandmother's counter. Everything in that book was bought with someone else. And I am done spending people."

The Chief Magistrate studied me for a long moment. Around us, the flood raged; magistrates were coming apart at the seams, unraveling into streams of returned memory, their masks cracking like river ice in spring. Somewhere in the white noise of a thousand restored lives, I heard Corin shouting my name, and Wren laughing—a sound like bells underwater—and none of it mattered as much as the space between me and the book, which was ten feet of flooded stone and a hundred years of fear.

"I could offer you terms," the Chief Magistrate said. "Your guild restored. Your legacy intact. Your entry in this book... amended. Deferred. You would die wealthy, honored, remembered. Is that not what your grandmother wanted? Is that not what you want, deep down, under all that righteous exhaustion? To matter?"

And here is the truth I will carry to whatever grave the fire leaves me: for one heartbeat, I wanted it. One whole heartbeat, warm as a luck-knot in winter. To matter. To restore what was taken. To be the guildhead who brought the Sterling name back from the ash heap of outlawed magic.

Then I thought of Corin Ash, drifting through Highmoor for years, knocking on doors that would not open twice. I thought of him asking me for a charm against being remembered, because being forgotten was the only life he'd been left. I thought of a child signing his name in a contract he couldn't read, trading himself away to save a village that never knew his sacrifice.

Fate wrote him down. Fate can learn to write differently.

"No," I said.

I moved.

The Chief Magistrate was fast—faster than anything that old should be—but it was also, in that moment, holding a chain. Its fingers were full of moonlight links, and I was full of nothing but desperation and a striker-tube from my own shop's counter, the kind I used to light the warming-lamps each grey morning, tucked in my sleeve since the day Vael marked my door. I had carried it down the aqueduct stair like a talisman. Like a promise.

I wrenched the chain free of the wall. Moonlight links snapped and scattered, hissing where the flood touched them. The Chief Magistrate spun, robes flaring, one hand rising wreathed in the dark weight of a century of judgments—

—and Wren hit it sideways like a ship hitting rocks.

"*GO,*" she screamed, pinned beneath it, her ancient arms locked around its waist, her face pressed against its mask. "Ada—*girl*—the pages, BURN THE PAGES—" The magistrate's shadow poured over her like oil, and I saw her begin to fade at the edges, giving herself to the dark the way she had given everything else, and still she held on. Still she laughed. "A hundred years I ferried your kind! Finish it! FINISH IT!"

I got the book open.

The chain had been the lock; without it the covers fell apart like a mouth opening, and the smell that rose out of those pages was the smell of every bad bargain ever struck—copper and candle-smoke and the particular staleness of air sealed too long over something ashamed of itself. Page after page after page, columns of names in ink that writhed when I looked at it directly. Souls sold, generation after generation, the guild's prosperity tallied in the currency of other people's tomorrows. And there, near the end, fresh as yesterday: *Corin Ash*, and beneath it, in the margin, in handwriting I knew better than my own—grandmother's slanted script—*the thirteenth turn completes the account.*

And beneath that, waiting like a hook waiting for a fish:

*Ada Sterling.*

My own name. My own future, pre-written, pre-sold, filed and waiting. The court hadn't just planned to claim me at the moon's turning. It had already recorded me. I had been property since before I walked down that stair. Since perhaps before I was born.

The Chief Magistrate threw Wren aside and rose to its full height, and the whole chamber seemed to rise with it, shadows gathering, the surviving magistrates crawling toward us across the flooded stone with their masks split and leaking light.

"Burn it," it said, and there was something new in its voice now, something I hadn't expected, something almost like *pleading*. "Seller's daughter. Listen to me. That book is the only reason any of us exist. Unmake the contracts and you unmake the court, yes—but the court *is* the memory of every bargain ever struck beneath this mountain. Burn it, and no one will remember what was done here. No one will remember the crimes. No one will remember the ones who were sold. Their sacrifices will never have happened. Is that justice? To erase the very proof of what was suffered?"

And there it was. The last trap. The good trap, the clever one, the one built for someone like me—for a woman who had spent her whole life believing that remembering was the holiest work there was. If I burned the book, Corin's sacrifice would vanish. His choice to save his village. Wren's century of service. Grandmother's tears. All of it, unwritten.

I looked at the page. At his name. At mine.

"No," I said again, softer this time. "You've got it backwards. You didn't preserve their stories. You *imprisoned* them. A story locked in a ledger isn't remembered, magistrate. It's held hostage. Their sacrifices were real before you wrote them down, and they'll be real after. Because the people who lived them are going to walk out of here"—I struck the flint, and sparks bloomed against the page like tiny defiant stars—"*and tell them themselves.*"

The flame caught.

Ink screams. I want to say that plainly, because it's true: the ink screamed. A hundred years of contracts ignited at once, and the sound they made was the sound of every lie in Highmoor catching fire—my grandmother's voice crying out from the page, Fenwick's younger voice, names I never knew, names going back and back beyond the guild's founding, all of it burning upward in a column of gold-and-black light that touched the vaulted ceiling and *kept going*, as if the fire had found a chimney that led all the way to the sky.

The Chief Magistrate lunged for the burning book, and I did the bravest and simplest thing I have ever done in my life.

I held on.

Heat licked my sleeves. Light poured through my fingers. The magistrate's shadow closed around both of us, cold as a cellar, whispering terms, amendments, offers, my name in my grandmother's voice, my name in Corin's voice, my name in my own—

"Let. *Go,*" I told it, through my teeth, through the smoke. "The account is closed."

The book burned through.

And the Hollow Court came undone.

Not all at once—in layers, like fog lifting off the moor at dawn. The walls went first, dissolving into drifts of pale light that might once have been testimony. Then the dais, the chains, the archive shelves with their jars of preserved tears and laughter. The magistrates unraveled gently, almost gratefully, their masks falling away to reveal nothing underneath but released memory streaming homeward—up, I realized. Up, through the mountain, through the aqueduct stones, back toward the city that had forgotten them. Every soul the court had ever consumed was going home.

The Chief Magistrate lasted longest. It stood in the center of its own dissolution, mask cracked clean down the middle, and in the last instant before the light took it, I swear I saw something human look out through the fracture—something tired, something relieved.

"Who will remember *us*?" it asked.

"Nobody," I said. "That's what you chose, when you chose to become this. But I'll remember what you taught me: that ledgers aren't justice. People are. Somebody has to start over."

Then it was gone, and the fire was gone, and the flood was gone, and I was kneeling on wet stone in a vast empty dark, holding a handful of ash that smelled, impossibly, like rain.

* * *

Corin found me there. Or maybe I found him—we were both stumbling blind through the collapsing dark, calling each other's names, and collided somewhere near where the judgment dais had been. He gripped my shoulders hard enough to bruise, and I gripped his, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because some things are too large for mouths.

"It's done," he whispered. "I felt it. Ada, I *felt* it—the contract letting go of me, like a hand opening. I'm—" His voice broke. "I don't know what I am yet. But I'm not theirs."

"You're yours," I said. "You always were. That was the whole point."

Wren limped out of the settling light, singed, diminished, half-transparent at the edges where the magistrate's shadow had drunk deep of her—but grinning. Grinning like a woman who had waited a hundred years to grin.

"Well," she said. "That's that."

"That's that," I agreed.

She looked at the ash in my hands, and then at me, and her grin gentled into something older and sadder and warmer. "You know what it cost," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

I looked down at the ash. Tried to remember my mother's face, and found I could—her eyes, her hands, the way she hummed. Tried to remember the lullaby itself, the one I had given to the ferryman at the border-hollow, and found a smooth blank place where it should have been, a missing tooth in the row of my memories. That price had been paid days ago, and I had known it would never come back.

But there were other blanks now. New ones. Small ones, scattered through me like holes in cloth. The name of my first teacher. The color of the dress I wore to my mother's funeral. The taste of the tea Grandmother made on winter mornings. The fire had taken its tithe as it burned—not everything, but enough. Enough to feel, forever, like a draft through a shuttered window.

And Corin—I looked at him, really looked, searching his face for the man who had sat across my counter and asked for a charm against being remembered, and something flickered in his expression. Something uncertain. He was looking at me the way you look at a stranger whose face feels important.

"Ada," he said slowly. Testing the name. "Your name is Ada."

"Yes," I said, and my chest tightened around something sharp.

"You knew me. Before. Didn't you." It wasn't a question either. "I can't—I remember my village. I remember choosing. I remember *everything* except—" He pressed his palm flat against his chest, as if checking whether his heart was still there. "Except the parts with you in them."

The chamber was nearly empty now, nearly quiet, the last of the freed memories streaming upward through cracks in the stone like light through shutters. Wren had turned away, giving us privacy the way ferrymen do—professionally, completely.

"We'll get to that," I said, and my voice held steady, which I count among my life's small miracles. "There's time. There's a whole world up there with no court in it. We'll get to all of it."

He nodded. And then, because he was Corin Ash—even unremembering, even remade—he did the Corin thing. He took my ash-covered hand in his ash-covered hand, and held it, and said, "Then let's go up and find out who we are."

We climbed.

The stair beneath the aqueduct had changed. The stones that had recognized Corin's name and ground open for us now glowed faintly as we passed, shedding their centuries like a snake shedding skin, and by the time we reached the top rung, they were just stones—old, damp, ordinary, free. Above us, through the grate, Highmoor glittered under a sky scrubbed clean by wind. The moon hung there, fat and silver, beginning—just beginning—to complete its thirteenth turn.

It finished its arc over an empty court. Over burnt paper. Over no one.

I pushed the grate open and breathed in the city—coal smoke and river mist and bread from the bakery on Tanner's Lane—and it smelled like the first day of a new ledger, blank and waiting.

Behind me, Corin paused at the lip of the stair and looked back once at the darkness we'd climbed out of. When he turned around, his brow was furrowed, and he said the words that would follow me home like a second shadow:

"There's a shop," he said. "Isn't there? With a crooked green door. I keep seeing a crooked green door, and someone standing in it, lighting lamps." He frowned harder. "Why do I keep seeing that?"

I smiled at him through the ache, and the ache, and the ache.

"Come on," I said. "I'll show you."
