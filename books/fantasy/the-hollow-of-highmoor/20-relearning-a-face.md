## 20. Relearning a Face

The shop smelled of rain again.

Ada stood in the doorway with the key in her hand and let herself feel it—the damp wool-and-copper scent of Highmoor after a wet night, the particular smell of her own threshold, which she had feared for three weeks she would never smell again. The door swung on its hinge with the same tired creak. The bell above it gave the same cracked half-note. Inside, everything was exactly where she had left it on the morning they went down under the aqueduct: the luck-knots in their glass jar, the warming-stones ranked on their slate shelf, the little brass scale for weighing grief that had never once been used because no one in Highmoor wanted to know the weight of what they carried.

Except now she knew. She knew to the ounce.

She crossed the floor slowly, trailing her fingers along the counter's edge, and stopped at the place where the third floorboard—loose, always loose, Grandmother never fixed anything she could hide things beneath—had been pried up and not quite set back down. Beneath it, the Hollow Prophecies were gone. Burned. Ashed into the dark of the court where the magistrates had unraveled like smoke given a sudden opinion about direction. She could still see the fire if she closed her eyes: the ledger curling, the ink lifting off the pages like startled birds, every contract in a century of quiet crimes going up together.

Every contract. Including hers. Including his.

That was the arithmetic she could not make come out even. She had burned the book and saved him, and the fire had taken its fee from both of them, and now Corin Ash walked the fog-bound streets of Highmoor whole and free and remembering almost nothing—and what he did not remember was her.

* * *

He had come back once already. Three days ago.

She had been restocking the warming-stones when the bell gave its cracked half-note and she looked up and there he was in the doorway, dripping fog, looking around the shop with the polite blankness of a stranger—which was, of course, precisely what he was. What he had been made to be. He'd said, "Sorry—is this the charm-shop? Someone told me—" and then frowned, because no one *had* told him; no one ever told him anything twice; that was the shape of the curse the court had left in him even after the contracts burned. He'd bought a luck-knot. He'd paid with coins he counted out carefully, as though money were a language he was relearning. He'd said, "Have we met? You look like someone I should know," and Ada had gripped the edge of the counter until her knuckles went white and said, "No. I don't think so."

It was the hardest sentence she had ever spoken, and she had once stood in front of the Chief Magistrate's unraveling face and lied about the location of a door.

He'd paused at the threshold on his way out. Turned back. Looked at her a moment too long, the way you look at a room you're sure you've dreamed. Then the fog took him, and Ada sat down on the floor behind the counter among the dust and the loose floorboards and cried the way she hadn't cried at the trial, or in the flood-chamber, or when Wren spent a century of stolen memories buying them minutes. She cried then because then there had been something to do. Now there was only this: a man walking free through a city that couldn't hold his name, carrying a hole where she used to be.

So she made a decision, kneeling on the floor of her own shop with her sleeves over her hands, and the decision was this: she would not tell him.

Not because she didn't want to. Her hands shook with wanting to. But she had watched what happened when other people wrote his story for him—a guildhead grandmother with a ledger and a desperate heart, a court of magistrates with their appetite for evidence, a prophecy that had held his name before he could hold a pen. Everyone who had ever decided who Corin Ash was had taken something from him by deciding. His childhood signature sat on a contract he couldn't have understood. His village had traded his memory for mercy he hadn't asked to give. Even she—even Ada, who loved him, who had burned a century of fate to free him—if she walked up to him on the street and said *I know you, here is your history, here is what we were*, she would be doing it again. Writing him into a book he hadn't opened.

Let him choose, she thought. Let him walk past this door a hundred times if he needs to. If he comes back, let it be because something in him wants to, not because something in him remembers being told to.

It cost her everything to think it and more to mean it. But she meant it.

* * *

The days after that were strange in a way she hadn't anticipated: quieter than the years before the descent, and louder too. Because the shop was different now. She had taken down the shutters entirely. She had moved the warming-stones to the front window where anyone passing could see them faintly, stubbornly glowing, small suns in jars. She had written a sign in her own hand—no cipher, no code, no inspector-dodging ambiguity—and hung it above the door:

**CHARMS. SMALL MAGIC, HONESTLY PRICED. ALL WONDERS WELCOME.**

Inspector Vael had walked past it twice without stopping. The second time, he had looked at the sign for a long moment, and then at her through the glass, and something in his face had been—not approval, nothing so warm—but recognition. One lawman acknowledging another kind of law. He touched two fingers to his hat brim and kept walking, and Ada understood that the watching was over, or at least transformed into something she could live beside.

The neighborhood noticed within a week. Children came first, as children always do when a door stops being afraid of itself. A girl named Tansy with soot on her knuckles asked if the luck-knots really worked, and Ada said, "They work the way a kind word works—not reliably, but enough," and Tansy had laughed like that was the best answer in the world. An old woman brought in a dead songbird and asked, hesitantly, if anything could be done, and Ada held the small cold body and found one thread of warmth still knotted in it, one hour's worth, and sang it back for an hour while the old woman wept into her apron. Word went down the terraces and up the stairways: the Sterling shop is open. Really open. The magic isn't hiding anymore.

And every day, Ada caught herself listening for the bell.

* * *

On the ninth day, he came back.

She knew his step before the bell rang—she hated that she knew it, loved that she knew it, some part of her hearing tuned to him the way warming-stones are tuned to cold hands. The bell gave its cracked half-note. The fog came in wearing a coat.

"Hello again," Corin Ash said.

"Hello," Ada said. Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that.

He drifted along the shelves the way he had the first time, touching nothing, reading labels. She watched him sidelong while pretending to sort knotwork. He looked better than he had three weeks ago—filled out, steadier, the gray undertone gone from his skin. Whatever the fire had returned to him, it had returned it generously. Everything except—

"You're the charm-woman," he said. "From before."

"I'm the charm-woman," she agreed.

"The one with the luck-knots." He picked one up from the jar, turned it over. "I've been wearing mine. I don't know why. It doesn't seem to do anything."

"It does the way a kind word does," Ada said. "Not reliably. But enough."

He smiled at that, and the smile went through her like a blade wrapped in cloth, because she had seen that exact smile across a ferry-boat in the border-hollow, across a candle in the archive, across the last inch of space between them before the moon turned and the court began to eat him alive. He didn't remember any of it. He was smiling at a stranger. And the stranger was the truest thing he'd ever been to her.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"People usually do, in a shop."

"Do you ever get the feeling"—he set the luck-knot down carefully, aligning it with the jar's rim, a precise, deliberate gesture she remembered from a hundred small moments that existed now only in her—"that you've lost something? Not an object. Something bigger. Like a room in a house you can't find the door to anymore. You know the house has the room. You can almost hear it. But every time you reach for the handle—"

"Yes," Ada said. Quietly. "I know that feeling."

He looked at her then, fully, and for a moment neither of them breathed. The lamp hissed. Outside, the fog pressed against the window like a listener.

"That's the strange thing," he said. "Everyone I talk to, I forget them. Or they forget me. It's been that way for years—it got worse, I think, recently, or maybe I just notice it now. But this shop—" He gestured at the shelves, the stones, the hanging bundles of dried rowan. "This is the only door in Highmoor that's ever opened for me twice. Do you understand what that means? I walked past a hundred doors today and none of them were going to remember me tomorrow. And then I remembered this one. I don't know why. There's no reason. There's just—" He pressed his hand flat against his chest, over the sternum, the way you press a wound. "There's a warmth here. Like a stone that's been sitting near a fire. The fire's gone. Nobody's tended it. But the stone stayed warm anyway."

Ada's eyes stung. She busied them with the ledger, opening it, closing it, needing somewhere to put her hands.

"We sell warming-stones," she managed. "Maybe you sat near the stock."

"Maybe," he said, in the tone of a man humoring a lie he finds charming.

* * *

He came back the next day. And the day after.

He never bought much. A spool of red thread once, because he said it seemed like the kind of shop that would have good thread. A candle stub. Once, nothing at all—he just stood by the window watching the fog move down the terrace, and Ada worked beside him in a silence that ached in the specific way of a silence that used to be full.

She learned the discipline of it, the way you learn any craft. She learned to hand him tea without letting her fingers linger. She learned to laugh at his jokes at the pitch of politeness instead of the pitch of intimacy. She learned to say *Corin* only rarely, and lightly, as though the name were new to her mouth—as though she hadn't shouted it across a flooding chamber while Wren's stolen memories raged around them like a sea given permission.

Once, he caught her looking. Just for a second. She had been watching him examine the brass scale—the grief-scale, the one that had never been used—and her face must have done something unguarded, because he looked up sharply and said, "What?"

"Nothing," she said. "You look like someone who's about to ask me what that scale is for."

"Isn't it obvious?" He turned it in his hands. "It weighs things that matter. Things people carry. Grief, probably. Regret." He set it down. "Who brings something like that to a shop? Who *needs* it weighed?"

"People who are tired of carrying it alone," Ada said.

He was quiet for a moment. "My mother used to say something like that," he said, surprised at himself. "I didn't—I thought I'd lost all of that. Before. But it keeps coming back in pieces. Little things. A kitchen. Rain on a roof. A woman singing off-key on purpose because the song sounded better wrong." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Is that normal? After—you know what happened to me. Everyone knows the story now. The court, the burning, the souls freed. They printed it in the *Highmoor Gazette* like a serial. Half the city claims they were there."

"What do you remember of it?" Ada asked, very carefully.

"Fog. A lot of fog. Water rising. Someone holding my hand." He frowned, reaching. "Someone saying my name like it mattered. Like it was worth saving." He looked at her. "I keep thinking if I could just remember the voice—"

"The Gazette will print more installments," Ada said. "Wait for those."

It was a coward's answer and she knew it. He accepted it, and she hated how easily he accepted it, how trained he was by years of erasure to take whatever explanation was offered and be grateful for the having of anything at all. The court had taught him not to ask twice. She was benefiting from the lesson, and the benefit curdled in her throat like milk left in thunder-weather.

* * *

On the seventeenth day, it rained hard, the first real storm since the burning, and the shop filled early with customers wanting warming-stones and dry corners, and by evening Ada was exhausted in the old familiar way, the good way, the way of work honestly done. She was blowing out the lamps when the bell rang again and Corin stood in the doorway soaked through, laughing at himself, holding a paper bag gone translucent with rain.

"Bread," he said, holding it up. "From the bakery on Cooper's Stair. I bought too much. I don't know why I bought too much. I just kept thinking, she'll want some—"

He stopped. Blinked. As if the sentence had run out ahead of him and he'd walked into the wall of it.

"*She*?" Ada said, very evenly.

"I don't—" He laughed again, shakier now, and came in out of the rain, dripping on her floorboards, and stood there with his ruined bread and his bewildered face. "I don't know why I said that. I don't know who I meant. I've been doing that all week. Buying two of things. Turning down streets toward places I've never been and finding them familiar. Last night I woke up with a word in my mouth." He set the bread down on the counter with exaggerated care, like a man setting down something explosive. "Do you want to know the word?"

"If you want to tell me."

"*Sterling.*" He said it softly, tasting it. "I woke up and it was just—there. Sterling. Like a coin under my tongue. And I thought, that's a name, whose name is that, and then this morning I was walking and I looked up and there was your sign, and I thought—" He passed a hand over his face. "I know this is mad. You can tell me it's mad. But I think the word is yours. I think it's been yours for a long time, and I don't know how, and I don't know why, and every time I try to remember, it's like reaching into a room where the fire's been—"

"Warm ash," Ada said. "A stone that stayed warm."

"A stone that stayed warm," he repeated, and stared at her.

The lamp guttered. Rain ran silver down the black windows. Ada stood behind her counter with her grandmother's ledger under her hands—the new ledger, the blank one, the one she'd bought to replace the burned book—and made the choice one final time, weighing it on the grief-scale that had never been used, and found that it weighed nothing at all. That was the answer. That was what the scale was for. Some things weigh nothing because they aren't a burden; they're a wing.

"Corin," she said. "Sit down. Have some bread. It's good bread, even wet."

He sat. He looked at her across the counter with the fog-light behind him and the patience of a man who has waited years outside doors.

"There's a story," Ada said, "about a woman who sold something that wasn't hers to sell, and a man who paid for it without knowing, and another woman who inherited the debt and burned the book. It's a long story. Parts of it are in the *Gazette*, but the *Gazette* gets the important parts wrong." She slid the loaf toward him, tore off the end, and handed him her half—the larger half, the way you do for someone you—well. The way you do. "If you want to hear it, I'll tell you all of it. Every piece. And if you hear it and decide you want to walk out that door and never come back, that's your right, and I'll survive it, because I've survived worse."

"And if I stay?"

Ada looked at him—at the rain in his hair, at the warmth coming off him like a banked coal, at the face she had fought a court to keep hold of while her own memories were fed to magistrates one by one.

"Then we start a new book," she said. "One that records choices. Not fates."

Corin Ash took the bread. He ate it slowly, watching her the whole time, and Ada saw it happen—the thing she had bet everything on, the thing warmer and older and truer than memory. Not recollection. Recognition. The soul knowing what the mind had lost, the way a stone knows a fire it can no longer see.

"Tell me the story," he said. "All of it. Start with the shop."

And outside, the rain eased, and the fog lifted just enough to show one star over the rooftops of Highmoor, and in the drawer beneath the counter, the new ledger lay open to its first clean page—waiting, patient as a door that opens twice, for whatever names would be written in it next.
