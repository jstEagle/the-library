## 1. Salt on the Ledger

There are three ways to close a smuggling contract in Greyfen, and I've survived all of them, which is more than most can say.

The first is the honest way: you deliver the goods, you count the coin, you shake hands. Nobody does this. The second is the dishonest way: you deliver the goods, they don't pay, and somebody bleeds. This happens often enough that we budget for it. The third — the one I prefer, the one that keeps my crew fed and my name worth what it costs — is the way where everyone walks away slightly unhappy and nobody reaches for a knife.

That was the arithmetic I was doing in the back room of the Drowned Eel, watching Odrik Fenwick sweat through his third shirt of the evening.

"The roads," he said, for the fourth time, "were not kind to you."

"The roads were fine." I slid the ledger across the table so he could see the columns. "Your drivers were drunk, your guards were greedy, and your salt was packed by someone who hates you personally. We lost eleven sacks at the ford because your man Hesk decided the crossing looked shallow. It wasn't. That's not my invoice problem."

Odrik's finger traced the numbers like he could find a kinder version of them hiding between the lines. He couldn't. I write clean ledgers. It's one of maybe four virtues I possess, and it's the only one that pays.

"Eleven sacks of white salt," he muttered. "Do you know what salt runs for now?"

"I know exactly what salt runs for now. I wrote it down." I tapped the column. "Which is why you're paying us two hundred crowns instead of two-fifty, and why you're going to stop talking before I remember the ford wasn't our fault either."

He looked up at me then — a fat, frightened merchant with rings on every finger and fear behind his eyes that had nothing to do with salt — and something in his face made me pause. Not the haggling fear. The other kind. The kind I'd been seeing all week in Greyfen, on doorposts and in lowered voices.

"You've heard," he said quietly.

"Heard what?"

He glanced at the door. At his own guard, standing by it, suddenly very interested in the ceiling. "The criers come tomorrow. From the capital." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Proclamations. About the old gift."

I kept my face still. Twenty years of mercenary work teaches you to keep your face still; the rest of me went cold in a way I didn't let show.

"What about it?" I said, bored, and reached for the wine.

"That people who carry it are to be reported. That there's a bounty." He wet his lips. "My wife's cousin in Vellmark saw a woman taken in the market square. In broad daylight. For *hearing things*, Theo. They put her in an iron cart."

"Sounds like Vellmark," I said, and drank. "Vellmark will believe anything."

But my hand wasn't as steady as my voice, and Odrik noticed, because merchants notice everything except when to stop talking.

"They say the crown has inquisitors now. Real ones. With writs." He leaned across the table, and the smell of him — pomade and panic — washed over me. "You hear things sometimes, don't you? Everyone says—"

"Everyone says a lot of things about me." I set down the cup. "Mostly that I'm expensive. Two hundred crowns, Odrik. Pay your crew, sign the page, and go home to your wife."

He signed. They always sign, eventually. Fear makes men generous, and I had just learned which flavor of fear he was carrying, and I wanted him gone before he started being generous in directions I didn't like.

* * *

The Drowned Eel's common room was doing its best impression of warmth: peat smoke, spilled ale, a fiddler who only knew six songs and played them all badly. My crew had claimed the corner table the way my crew claimed everything — early, loudly, and with their boots up.

Brannoc had the chair with its back to the wall, because Brannoc always took the chair with its back to the wall, even in towns where nobody knew his name. He was a head taller than anyone else in the room and built like something the mountains had rejected for being too stubborn. Fifteen years my captain, once. Now we were partners, which is what captains become when they get too old to pretend they're invincible and too proud to retire.

He watched me cross the room and read the whole transaction off my walk, the way he always did.

"Two hundred?" he said.

"Two hundred."

"He cried?"

"A little."

Brannoc grinned and pushed the coin purse across the table without counting it. That was the thing about Brannoc. He never counted. Fifteen years, and he'd never once counted. I don't know if that made him the best partner I ever had or the biggest fool, and some nights I still don't know.

Across from him, Wren was teaching dice to Tam, our youngest, who was losing with the particular enthusiasm of someone who hasn't yet learned that Wren doesn't lose. Wren — small, quick, a face like a closed door — caught my eye and tilted her head toward the window. Toward the street. I shook my head a fraction. Later.

Marta was there too, sharpening a knife she'd sharpened twice already tonight, which meant she was thinking about home. Marta always thought about home when contracts closed. Home was a farm in the lowlands she sent half her pay to and hadn't seen in six years, and none of us ever mentioned it, because that's what family is: people whose soft spots you know and refuse to step on.

"Well?" Brannoc said. "Do we drink or do we sulk?"

"We drink." I sat down. "And then we talk about routes south."

His grin faded a degree. "South."

"Winter's coming. Thornfell passes will close. Greyfen's picked clean. There's caravan work out of Corrow's Landing, down past the marshes."

"Corrow's Landing," Brannoc repeated. "That's a long way from anything."

"That's the appeal."

He studied me for a moment. Brannoc studies people the way other men study maps — patiently, looking for the route in. Whatever he found in my face, he decided not to press it, which was wise of him, because I'd have lied, and we both would have known it.

"To Corrow's Landing, then," he said, and raised his cup. "May the roads be dull."

We drank to dull roads. It's the only toast mercenaries mean.

* * *

I left the Eel alone near midnight, which was stupid, and I knew it was stupid, and I did it anyway, because there was one piece of business left and it wasn't the kind you do with witnesses.

Greyfen at night is a town holding its breath. Lamplight in the windows, shutters drawn early, and everywhere that feeling you get before weather turns — the pressure of something coming that everyone can smell and no one will name. A dog followed me for two streets and then thought better of it. Smart dog.

The old altar stood where it had always stood, at the crook where the mill road met the river path: a squat stone thing older than the town, older probably than the kingdom, worn smooth by centuries of hands and rain. Once it had been painted. Once there had been a priest, and incense, and a bell rung at dusk. Now it was just stone, and moss, and the dark.

Vael's altar. Or what passed for one, out here on the border where the old god's name had thinned to a whisper and then to nothing at all.

I dug in my purse and took out a silver crown. Tithe. One coin in ten, the old rule, though nobody kept the old rules anymore and hadn't for years. I set it on the stone out of habit — habit being the only prayer I had left — and stood there in the cold with my breath fogging and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that I hadn't said a hundred times before, into a silence that never once broke.

Seven years.

Seven years since Vael had last spoken to me. Seven years since the dreams stopped — mostly — and the warmth behind my sternum, the one that used to answer when I prayed, went cold and stayed cold. Seven years of setting coins on stones in every border town from here to the coast, like a woman leaving offerings for someone who moved out and didn't leave a forwarding address.

The priests had explanations. Gods withdraw. Gods test. Gods sleep through the long ages and wake hungry. The priests had a different explanation for every season and none of them helped, because none of them explained why it felt less like absence and more like *wound*. Like something had been done to Him, or to the space where He lived, and He wasn't ignoring me at all. He simply couldn't reach.

Or — and this was the thought I kept in the locked room at the back of my mind, the one I visited only on nights like this — or there had never been anything there at all, and I'd spent thirty years talking to my own reflection.

I pressed my palm flat against the altar stone. Cold. Just cold. Stone doesn't answer. It never answers.

"Wherever you are," I said quietly, "I hope it's better than Greyfen."

The coin sat on the rock, catching moonlight. Somewhere across the river, a nightbird called three times and stopped, as if interrupted.

I told myself I imagined the shiver that ran up my arm. I was good at telling myself things. It may be my finest skill.

* * *

Wren fell into step beside me two streets later, materializing out of a doorway the way she did, silent until she wanted not to be.

"You felt it too," she said.

"Felt what?"

"Don't." She kept her eyes forward, hands in her pockets, walking like any late-night drunk's shadow. "This afternoon. When the crier's horn sounded from the east gate. You went white. So did I. So did Tam, and Tam doesn't even know why yet — I watched him figure it out while he was figuring it out. It's waking up, Theo. Whatever it is. It's waking up in all of us at once, and I want to know what you're going to do about it."

I walked three steps before answering. Maybe five.

"There's nothing to do about it. There's nothing *to do about*. I'm a salt merchant with a sword. So are you."

"The woman in Vellmark was probably a salt merchant with a sword."

"The woman in Vellmark is a story Odrik Fenwick heard from his wife's cousin. Stories grow teeth in the telling."

"And if they haven't?" Wren stopped walking. In the dark, her face was all eyes. "Theo. I have dreamed about water for a month. Water rising, and a voice under it, saying words I almost understand. Last night I understood one word." She swallowed. "It was my name."

The nightbird called again, far off. Twice, this time. Then nothing.

"Pack light," I said finally. "We ride south day after tomorrow. Before the criers come. Before whatever's coming comes."

"And the contract Brannoc mentioned? The one you took aside about the Thornfell job?"

I hadn't mentioned any such job to Brannoc. Which meant Wren had been listening at doors again, which meant I needed to have a conversation about that, which meant I was tired in a way sleep wouldn't fix.

"It's one escort," I said. "One client, one case, over the mountains and gone. Triple pay."

"Nobody pays triple for an escort."

"This one does." I started walking again, faster. "And tomorrow, before we ride, I'm going to find out why."
