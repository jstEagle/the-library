## 2. The Proclamation

The morning the crown came for the gods' listeners, I was hungover and haggling over the price of dried fish, which is to say I was in no condition to save anyone, least of all myself.

Greyfen wakes ugly. The town sits where the Saltwash river flattens into marsh before giving up and joining the sea, and by dawn every street smells like a bet between rot and brine that nobody wins. The fishmongers set up their stalls in the grey half-light, gulls scream their opinions from the rooftops, and the mud — well. The mud is permanent. You don't walk through Greyfen so much as negotiate with it.

I was at Dalla's stall because Dalla owed me a favor and I was collecting it in smoked eel, which is better currency than coin some months. She was pretending the favor had slipped her mind.

"Three bundles," I said.

"One bundle and my undying gratitude."

"Dalla. Your gratitude died years ago. Everyone saw it happen."

She laughed, which meant she'd pay, which meant the morning was going fine right up until the bells started.

Not the harbor bell. Not the temple bell, either, though Greyfen's temple hadn't rung its bell in six years, not since the day old Father Aldous climbed the tower, rang it twice, stopped mid-swing, and came down looking like a man who'd been told a joke he couldn't repeat. The bell that rang that morning was the magistrate's bell — the sharp one, the one they used for fires and executions and other civic entertainments.

Dalla's hands went still on her scale. Around us, the market did that thing markets do when trouble arrives: everyone kept moving but slower, heads tilting, ears pricking, the whole square becoming one animal listening.

"Criers," someone said near the well. "They've got criers."

Crown criers in Greyfen were about as common as snowfall. We were too far east, too poor, too damp for the crown to bother remembering us except at tax time. Three of them stood up on the customs house steps in royal blue that must have cost more than the building, with a sergeant of the watch flanking them and four soldiers doing their best to look inevitable.

The middle crier unrolled a scroll with the flourish of a man who enjoyed being read to himself.

"By order of His Majesty's Grace, let it be known throughout the realm—" and here his voice took on the practiced music of proclamation, the cadence that makes even horror sound like accounting— "that the practice known as covenant-speech, being commerce with false and fallen powers, is hereby declared an act of treason against the crown and an abomination before the true faith."

The market went very quiet. Even the gulls seemed embarrassed.

"Persons found to possess the gift of hearing or answering said powers shall be taken into custody. Informants reporting such persons shall receive fifty crowns upon confirmation. One hundred crowns for the capture of a confirmed speaker alive." A pause, professionally timed. "Two hundred crowns for a speaker of the old orders."

Two hundred crowns. I did the math without wanting to. Two hundred crowns was a new boat. Two hundred crowns was a crew paid through winter and spring both. Two hundred crowns was the kind of money that turned neighbors into informants faster than you could say *I always thought something was off about him*.

"They're mad," Dalla murmured. "Half this town leaves offerings. My mother left bread out for the marsh-wardens every solstice till the day she died."

"And your mother," I said, keeping my voice light, "was careful not to be seen doing it. That's the whole trick, isn't it?"

But my stomach had gone cold, and not from the eel.

Here's the thing about proclamations like that one: nobody panics immediately. People laugh, mostly. They make jokes about who'll be reported first, and everyone looks sideways at the odd ones — the widow who talks to her garden, the ferryman who mutters thanks to the water, the children who swear there's something under the bridge that trades secrets for buttons. Laughter is how crowds test the shape of a fear before they commit to it.

Then the soldiers grabbed Wenna Cole by the arms, and the laughter stopped having anywhere to live.

Wenna lived two doors down from the room I rented above the cooper's shed. She was seventy if she was a day, walked with a stick, and had spent forty years leaving little clay boats on the riverbank — offerings to a river-god whose name nobody remembered anymore, maybe not even Wenna herself. It was habit, the way my own tithing was habit. Nobody had heard the river answer in decades. It was the emptiest kind of devotion there was.

It didn't matter. The sergeant had a list, and lists don't weigh intent.

She didn't go quietly, exactly, but she went the way old women go — loudly, furiously, hitting a soldier with her stick until he took it away, then calling him names I made a mental note of for future use. Her daughter tried to intervene and got shoved into the mud. A few people shouted. Someone threw a fish, which hit a soldier on the shoulder and accomplished nothing except making me love this town a little.

And I stood there. In the crowd. With my hood up and my mouth shut, watching a woman I'd known for eleven years get dragged toward the magistrate's cells, and I did nothing, because doing something would have been dying, and dying helps no one, least of all Wenna Cole.

That's what I told myself, anyway, standing very still while two hundred crowns' worth of fear washed through the square like the tide coming in.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I am, since apparently the crown has opinions on the subject now.

I'm not a mystic. I'm not one of those gaunt-eyed zealots you find in the mountain shrines, fasting themselves into hallucinations and calling it communion. I'm a smuggler. I move goods across borders that taxes have made stupid, I keep my crew fed and paid, and I do it with a minimum of stabbing. Faith is bad for business; faith makes people take stands, and taking stands gets you killed or broke, usually both.

But.

There was a god once. Vael — the Quiet One, the hearth-side god, the one who kept the small promises. Not thunder and glory. Vael was the warmth behind you when the fire's low, the sense of being watched over by something that wasn't hungry. My mother's people kept his altars. I grew up thinking that was just what altars were for, the way chimneys are for smoke.

When I was young — properly young, before the mercenary work, before the ledger and the salt — Vael spoke to me. Twice. Maybe three times, depending on whether you count the storm on the Thornfell pass, and I do count it, because a voice telling you *hold to the rock* in your own dead grandmother's accent is a voice, whatever the priests say about stress and altitude.

Then, six years ago, along with every other god in the world, Vael went quiet.

Not faded. Fading I could understand. Everything fades — crews, loves, the taste of a meal you loved as a child. But this was silence, deliberate and total, like a door closing in a house you're still standing in. The temples emptied. The offerings piled up unanswered. Father Aldous came down from his bell tower and never preached again, just swept the floor and drank.

Except.

Except that sometimes — not often, maybe once or twice a year, always asleep, always at the edge of a dream — I still hear him. A murmur. Half a word. Once, memorably, my own name, spoken the way you'd speak to someone through a wall, urgent and muffled, like he was shouting down a well.

I'd never told anyone. It felt private, the way grief is private, and also — because I'm honest with myself at knife-point if nowhere else — I knew exactly how it sounded. A smuggler who hears voices? There are cheaper ways to get committed somewhere unpleasant.

But standing in that market square, watching Wenna Cole's stick clatter across the cobbles, the arithmetic changed. Because the crier hadn't said *practicing* speakers. He'd said *persons found to possess the gift*. Possession, not action. You didn't have to talk to gods to hang. You only had to hear them.

And some mornings I woke with Vael's murmurs still in my ears like water behind a door.

I finished buying the eel. I thanked Dalla. I walked home through streets that already felt different — doors closing a beat sooner than usual, eyes sliding off me a shade too quickly — and I climbed to my room above the cooper's shed, barred the door, and sat down on my bed to have the conversation I'd been avoiding with myself for six years.

*You're on the list now,* I told myself. *Maybe not today's list. But lists grow.*

* * *

That evening I went to see Brannoc.

The Broken Keel was Greyfen's only tavern built on pilings out over the actual river, which meant it swayed gently in bad weather and smelled permanently of rope, tar, and the particular despair of men who drink at noon on a Tuesday. Brannoc held court at the corner table the way he always did — captain of the Brightwater Company, twelve blades strong when everyone was healthy, nine when they weren't, and currently nursing an ale he'd been nursing since noon.

He looked up when I sat down. "Theo. Heard the criers. Grim theater."

"It's not theater if they drag someone off."

"Wenna Cole?" He shrugged, and I wanted to break his nose a little, and I noted the feeling and put it away, because that's what you do with feelings when you're me. "Old woman. Sad business. But the crown does this every generation — finds a scare, milks it, moves on. Remember the coin-clipping panic? The witch-burnings down in Marle? This passes."

"This one has bounties attached."

"Bounties pass too." He slid the ale toward me, an offer of peace, or maybe just a gesture so I'd stop looking at him like that. "What do you want, Theo? You've got your negotiation face on."

"I want out. South, past the Marle border, somewhere the crown's writ goes soft. I need passage arranged, papers bought, and a route nobody in this company knows but me."

He studied me for a long moment. Brannoc's a big man going soft at the edges, with a beard he trims like he's pruning a hedge he resents, and underneath the bluster he's canny in the specific way of men who've survived thirty years of selling violence. I watched him decide how much to pretend not to understand.

"You think you're flagged," he said finally.

"I think everyone's flagged until proven otherwise. I think a hundred crowns buys a lot of testimony, and I've been having strange dreams and saying strange things after drinks for years, and somebody in that company—" I tipped my chin at the room full of our own hired blades— "is going to remember it when the money's waved under their nose. I'd rather not find out which one."

"And the company? The contracts?"

"I'll finish what's open. Then I'm done. No drama, no splitting of shares, I just — stop being a name on your roster."

Something flickered across his face. Hurt, maybe, or calculation wearing hurt's clothing. With Brannoc it was always hard to tell the stitching from the cloth.

"One last job," he said. "Then south."

"One last job. Then south."

We shook on it, which between me and Brannoc has historically meant absolutely nothing, but it felt official, and some nights you need a thing to feel official.

Walking back along the riverwalk afterward, past Wenna Cole's dark windows — someone had already laid flowers on her step, which is Greyfen's way, brave after the fact — I passed the little clay boats on the bank. Half-trodden, rain-swollen, pointless. Offerings to a god who hadn't lifted a finger to save the woman who loved him best in this whole drowned town.

"Don't look at me," I told them. "I'm not the one with the power here."

The river said nothing. The river had gotten very good at it.

* * *

Three nights later, the dream came back, and this time it wasn't a murmur.

I was on the salt flats below Greyfen, the ones that flood at high tide and become a mirror, and the sky was wrong — too close, like a lid. And Vael's voice came through it, clearer than it had been in six years, and it wasn't gentle. It was ragged. Strained. A voice like a man holding up a collapsing roof.

*"Not forgotten. Held. Tell her— "*

And then pain — I woke with it, actually woke with it, a white-hot line across my palms as if I'd gripped something burning — and the word *her* hanging in the dark of my room like a hook with nothing on it yet.

Tell her. Tell who? Tell her what?

I sat up, heart hammering, hands stinging, and looked at my palms in the moonlight. Nothing. Of course nothing. Dreams leave bruises on everything except the body.

But I couldn't shake the shape of it. Six years of half-words and muffled murmurs, and now urgency, and now a pronoun. As if the silence weren't absence but effort. As if somewhere on the other side of whatever wall had gone up between the worlds, something was pressing against it, spending itself, trying to reach through to — someone. Some *her*.

I'm not *her*, I told myself firmly. I'm nobody's message. I'm a smuggler with a hangover and a plan involving southern sunshine.

Outside, the town bell began to ring. Not the magistrate's bell — the alarm pattern, short-short-long. Fire, or riot, or another arrest. From my window I could see torchlight moving down Marsh Lane, three or four torches, moving with the unhurried confidence of men being paid by the hour.

They stopped at the ferryman Osk's house. Osk, who muttered thanks to the water. Osk, who'd ferried me and my cargo a hundred times and never asked questions.

They brought him out between two soldiers, barefoot in the mud, and this time nobody threw a fish. This time the crowd watched from behind shutters, learning the lesson the lesson-teachers had come to teach: *this could be you, this will be you, keep your gods to yourself or lose everything.*

Osk's wife stood in the doorway holding a lamp, and the light caught her face, and I turned away from the window because there's a limit to what I can watch and still sleep, and I needed to sleep, because tomorrow—

Tomorrow I had a meeting. Word had come round, the way word does in Greyfen, of a woman arrived on the north road asking specifically — by name — for a guide who knew the Thornfell passes. Offering absurd money. Triple rates, the innkeep's boy said, and sealed papers, and a case she wouldn't let out of her sight.

Normally I'd walk away from a client like that. Clients with secrets and triple rates are clients whose funerals you attend. But I had a last job to take before vanishing, and a purse to fill, and a growing conviction that the crown's reach was longer than Brannoc believed.

And besides. Call it superstition — call it the thing I officially don't have — but when the boy said *she's asking for someone who knows the mountains*, I felt that sting again across my palms, faint as an echo.

*Tell her—*

Tell who? I was starting to suspect I was about to find out.
