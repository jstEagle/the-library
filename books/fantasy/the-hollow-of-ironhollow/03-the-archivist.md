## 3. The Archivist

The rain came back on the third morning after the proclamation, as if the sky had only paused to let Greyfen read its notices properly before drowning them again.

I was sitting in the Saltcellar with my boots up on a chair nobody else wanted, nursing a cup of something that called itself tea and had probably never met an actual leaf, when the woman walked in. I remember thinking two things about her in quick succession: first, that she was going to be trouble; and second, that I was going to take her money anyway.

The second thought arrived first, if I'm honest.

She stood just inside the doorway, letting her eyes adjust, doing that thing newcomers do in border towns — cataloguing exits, weighing faces, deciding which of the dozen people pretending not to look at her was actually looking. She did it well. Better than most. But she did it, and that alone told me she wasn't from here. Greyfen locals don't catalogue anything. They've already decided nothing in this town is worth the effort.

She wore a traveling cloak of good wool gone soft at the edges, the kind of garment that had once belonged to money and now belonged to someone running fast enough to wear the money out. Under it, mud to the knee. A satchel strap crossed her chest, and beneath the satchel, flat against her side where a sword would hang on anyone sensible, rode a rectangular case the length of a forearm, bound in grey leather with brass corners worn dull. She carried it like other people carry infants. Or wounds.

"You're Brightwater." Not a question. Her voice was low and even, the kind of voice you use in archives or sickrooms, and it carried anyway, the way quiet voices sometimes refuse to.

"I'm whoever paid for the tea," I said. "If you're selling something, the answer's no. If you're buying, sit down."

She sat down. She didn't smile, which I appreciated. People who smile at mercenaries are usually about to lie to them, and at least this way the lying would be honest work.

"My name is Sera Vellin," she said. "I'm an archivist. Formerly of the Vault at Coriel."

"Formerly."

"The Vault no longer exists." She said it the way you'd report weather. "The crown burned it eleven days ago. I was the last one out."

I took my boots off the chair because suddenly I wanted both feet under me. "Eleven days ago. That would be four days after our crier friend started reading his list."

"Yes."

"And you came to Greyfen. Where every smuggler, deserter, and half-deaf fence is talking about nothing except the list."

"I came to find you specifically." She set her hands flat on the table, fingers laced, and I noticed they were ink-stained to the second knuckle, old stains, the kind that live in the skin. "Because you know Thornfell better than any living guide. Because your company has a reputation for discretion that has survived contact with reality longer than most. And because you don't pray anymore, which means you won't flinch at what I'm carrying."

Well. That was three assumptions too many, delivered in one breath, by a stranger who knew my name and my habits and my relationship — or former relationship — with the divine. Any one of those should have sent me for the door. Instead I heard myself say:

"What's the pay?"

* * *

Here is the thing about greed that no one tells you when you're young and broke and learning the trade: it isn't loud. It doesn't roar. It sits very quietly in the room while instinct is shouting, and it waits for instinct to run out of breath, and then it says, *well, we could always use the coin,* in a voice exactly like your own.

Triple pay. Sera Vellin said triple pay and meant it — coin counted onto the table in advance, half again more promised on delivery, and delivery defined, in her words, as "the far side of Thornfell, to a place I will name when we are past the snowline." No destination named now. No contents of the case disclosed. No explanation of why a scholar whose institution had been reduced to ash by royal decree needed to cross mountains infested with crown scouts hunting people precisely like, I suspected, her.

"Who's hunting you?" I asked, over the second round of tea that she bought and I drank. "Crown? Inquisition? Both?"

"Crown writs. Enforced by the Inquisition." Her jaw tightened on the word. "There's an Inquisitor working the mountain roads. Corvane. He's thorough."

"I've heard the name." Everyone on the border had heard the name by then; it traveled ahead of him like a smell. "Thorough is a kind word for what he does."

"It's an accurate one." She looked at me steadily. "That's rather the point of him. He believes what he does is necessary. Those are always the dangerous ones."

"And what he thinks is necessary is finding people who can hear the old gods."

"People who can *speak* to them," she corrected, softly. "Hearing is common. Speaking is the crime."

I kept my face still, which is the single most valuable skill I own, and thanked whatever was left of Vael that the Saltcellar was dim and my hands were around a warm cup where a tremor wouldn't show. *People who can speak.* The dreams had come back lately — Vael's voice, thin as smoke, saying words I couldn't hold onto past waking. I'd told myself it was grief dressed up as grace, an old wound dreaming itself shut. I'd told myself the criers meant other people. People with real gifts, not ghosts of them.

Sera watched me across the table with those steady archivist's eyes, and I had the distinct, unpleasant sensation of being a page she'd already read.

"Why me?" I said. "You said you came for me specifically. The border's crawling with guides cheaper than I am and uglier, which should make them easy to find."

"Because of who your mother was."

The words landed in the middle of the table between us like a dropped knife.

I hadn't spoken my mother's name aloud in years. I barely owned it. Maerin Brightwater, dead before I could walk, a woman made of gaps — no grave I'd ever found, no kin who'd claim me, no story of her that didn't end in someone spitting or crossing themselves. The sisters at the foundling shrine in Lowmarsh had fed me and taught me letters and refused, always, with a particular careful blankness, to tell me anything else. I grew up knowing my mother the way you know a locked room: by its outline against the wall.

"I don't know who my mother was," I said.

"No," Sera agreed. "You don't." And there was something in her face then — not pity, worse than pity, something like *guilt* — that lasted exactly long enough to see and exactly not long enough to name. "But I did. A little. Enough to know that if the Inquisition ever learns your lineage, Brightwater, triple pay will be the least of what anyone offers for you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind me a drunk laughed at nothing. My heart was doing something slow and cold that I decided not to examine until later.

"That's a strange thing to say to someone you want to hire," I managed.

"It's the only reason I can say it." She unlaced her fingers and pressed her palms flat to the table, and for the first time since she'd walked in, she looked tired — genuinely, bone-deep tired, the fatigue of someone who'd been awake for eleven days and running for most of them. "I need you to understand I'm not deceiving you, Theo. There's no time left for deception. The case I carry holds something the covenant left behind, and it answers to blood — specific blood — and I believe yours is the blood it needs. I won't pretend otherwise. I'd rather you hate me honestly on the road than trust me kindly and die surprised."

"Comforting sales pitch."

"I'm not selling. I'm warning." She slid the purse across the table anyway. Heavy. Honest weight. "Take it or don't. If you don't, I hire the next best guide and hope. If you do — we leave tomorrow, before dawn, through the salt-marsh road, because Corvane's riders will be checking the river gates within the week."

I looked at the purse. I looked at her. I looked, briefly, inward, at the place where instinct was still shouting, hoarse and ignored, and greed sat beside it wearing my face and counting coins in a voice exactly like mine.

One last job, I'd told myself after the proclamation. One last contract, then south, past the reach of the writs, somewhere the old gods were only stones and everyone was equally deaf. Take the money, buy silence, disappear. This job was that job. This job *was* the disappearing — straight through the wildest country on the border, in company with a fugitive the Inquisition wanted, carrying an object I already knew I didn't want to understand.

Every sane part of me said no.

"Half now," I heard myself say, "half at the snowline. And you tell me everything the moment we're past the last checkpoint. Everything, Vellin. I don't escort mysteries. I escort people who happen to have them."

Something eased in her shoulders — relief, but a controlled relief, banked like a coal. "Agreed."

"And one more thing." I pulled the purse toward me and felt its weight settle into my palm, into the old familiar arithmetic of risk and price. "Whatever your case is. Whatever it wants with my blood. If it starts costing more than coin, we renegotiate. I've paid tithes to a god who stopped answering, and I'm done making donations."

For the first time, Sera Vellin almost smiled. It flickered and went out like a moth near a lamp. "Fair terms," she said. "Better than fair." Then, quieter, almost to herself, in the tone of someone reading an inscription she'd hoped was wrong: "Maerin would have driven a harder bargain."

I stood up so fast the chair barked against the floorboards.

"Say that name again," I said, "and the deal's off."

She held my stare without flinching, and I understood, in that moment, with the certainty of cold water, that she had said it deliberately. That every word since she'd walked through the door had been deliberate. That I was not hiring on to this job so much as being fitted for it, gently, patiently, like a key being tried in a lock.

"Understood," she said simply. "Tomorrow, before dawn. The salt-marsh road." She rose, gathered her satchel, settled the grey case against her ribs with both hands — that unconscious, protective gesture — and walked out into the rain.

I sat back down. I drank her tea. It tasted like leaves after all, or maybe like something else entirely.

Across the common room, Doss caught my eye from the bar where he'd been ostentatiously not listening, which for Doss meant he'd heard every word. He raised an eyebrow. I raised one back. He came over, dropped into the abandoned chair, and helped himself to the dregs.

"Trouble?" he asked.

"Money," I said.

"Those aren't different things."

"They're going to be." I turned the purse over in my hand. Triple pay. Coin enough to vanish on, bought from a woman who knew my mother's name and carried a box that answered to blood, headed into mountains where the crown was collecting people like me the way children collect interesting beetles.

I should have said no. Every instinct I'd survived twenty-nine years on said no.

I counted the coin instead, and it counted out even, and honest, and heavy, and outside the rain kept falling on Greyfen like the sky had decided to wash the town clean of secrets and given up halfway.

"Doss," I said. "Wake the crew. We move at fourth bell."

* * *

I didn't sleep much that night, which was normal, but I dreamed, which wasn't.

In the dream there was a door of grey stone set into a hillside, and the hillside was Thornfell though I'd never seen that particular fold of it, and on the door was a seam of light like a crack in a kiln. And from behind the door, faint as breath on winter glass, a voice said my name — not Theo, the other one, the one I'd been born with and never used — and the voice was Vael's, and it was not silent, and it was afraid.

I woke with my hand closed around the hilt of a knife I don't remember drawing, and the grey case's shape burned on the inside of my eyelids, and downstairs, in the dark, someone was already packing.
