## 4. Into Thornfell

The road out of Greyfen smelled like a lie everyone had agreed to believe.

That's the thing about border towns — they run on the fiction that nothing is happening here, nothing ever happens here, and if you pay your tolls and keep your mouth shut, the fiction will hold. I'd spent eleven years making my living inside that fiction, hauling goods through gaps in the law so narrow you had to grease them with silver to fit. It was honest work, in the way that a pickpocket is an honest surgeon of purses.

Now the fiction was rotting from the inside. Criers on every corner. A neighbor dragged off in the night. And me, standing at the edge of town at dawn with a woman I barely knew, a sealed case I wasn't allowed to open, and three times my usual rate burning a hole in my conscience like a coal wrapped in wool.

Sera was waiting at the old drover's gate, exactly where I'd told her to be, wearing her fear like it was tailored. Good traveling boots, a cloak that had seen weather but not poverty, and the kind of nervous stillness that reads as either discipline or guilt. She had the case strapped across her back like a second spine.

"Ready?" she asked.

I looked at her the way you look at a horse before buying it — teeth, feet, eyes. "One question first. When this goes wrong — and jobs go wrong, that's not pessimism, that's statistics — what do I do with you? Run? Hide? Fight?"

"Hide me," she said. "Whatever else happens, hide me."

"That's not an answer, that's a preference."

"It's the only instruction that matters." Her voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. People who shake are telling you the truth about themselves. People who don't have decided something already.

Fine. I'd carried stranger cargo. I'd once hauled a caged lynx through three checkpoints because a wine merchant swore it was a very large cat and the guards were too bored to argue taxonomy. A woman with a box was easier than a woman without one, probably.

We went north while the fog still owned the valley, because fog is the poor man's wall and I've never been rich enough to waste it.

* * *

There are two ways into the Thornfells. The first is the King's Road, which is wide, graded, patrolled, and named after a king who died choking on a grape, which tells you everything about how much the road cared for him. The second is everything else: shepherd cuts, smuggler threads, game trails that exist only because goats are too stupid to know where they're going and too stubborn to admit it.

I knew the everything else.

Not from maps — maps of the Thornfells are prayers more than documents, half wishful thinking, half cartographer's apology. I knew them from eleven years of moving contraband through country that officially didn't have paths on it. There was a sheep track above the Greyfen gorge that only existed between midsummer and the first frost, when the bracken died back enough to walk. There was a ledge below Widow's Tooth where the rock overhung just enough to hide a man from anyone looking down, provided the man didn't mind that the mountain was also trying to shrug him off into four hundred feet of air. There was a dry streambed behind the old quarry that went up and up until it forgot it had ever been a stream.

I took us up the gorge track first, because it was the fastest way to put the road and its patrols out of sight, and because Sera walked it like someone who understood that mountains don't care whether you live. That earned her a point in my ledger. Not trust — points aren't trust, points are just a record of not being an idiot yet.

"You've done this before," I said somewhere around the third switchback, mostly to hear whether she'd lie.

"I've walked long distances carrying heavy things," she said. "Whether it's been *this*—" she patted the case, "—is a longer story."

"Longer stories cost extra."

She laughed, short and surprised, like the laugh had escaped custody. "Everything costs extra with you, I'm learning."

"It's not personal. It's inflation. The crown's made silence expensive lately."

Her face did something complicated at that — the look of someone who'd heard a joke land too close to a wound. I filed it away. Archivists who flinch at jokes about silence are archivists with reasons.

By noon we were above the tree line's little brother, the scrub pine that grows sideways out of spite, and the fog had burned off into a sky so clean it felt like an accusation. The Thornfells rose ahead of us in gray and white shoulders, snow still packed in the high folds even though it was late summer down in the valleys. Somewhere past those shoulders was the pass Sera needed, and past the pass, whatever a sealed case was worth triple pay to deliver.

I let myself think about the money. Triple rate, paid half up front, and the half was real coin, not company scrip or a merchant's promise shaped like a promissory note. With that much I could go south past the Salt Marches, buy passage on a caravan, and be nobody in a city so big that proclamations took a year to reach the neighborhoods that mattered. That was the plan. One job, then vanish. The dream of every mercenary, right after the dream of dying rich and slightly before the reality of dying broke.

Except.

Except there was the other thing, the thing I hadn't said out loud since the criers came through Greyfen. The dreams. Vael's voice, or the shape of a voice, arriving in sleep like water finding cracks. *Hold.* *Wait.* *Not yet.* Three words in six years, and all of them useless as instructions and terrifying as omens, because a god who speaks in fragments is either dying or deciding something, and neither option comes with a manual.

If the crown's hunters were looking for people who could hear the old gods, then I was exactly the kind of person they were looking for, and I'd known it since the neighbor was dragged away, and I'd decided to deal with it by walking toward the mountains instead of away from them, which if you asked any sane tactician would be called a mistake and if you asked me would be called a feeling I refused to name.

Sera hadn't asked. Not once, in three days of hiring and provisioning and route-arguing, had she asked why a smuggler with no ties and no faith was taking a job that ended in the exact wilderness the crown couldn't patrol. Smart people ask fewer questions when they already suspect the answers.

I noticed that. I noticed it the way you notice a draft under a door — small, persistent, meaning something bigger is open somewhere.

* * *

We made the high pasture by late afternoon, a bowl of thin grass and glacial meltwater where the smuggler thread crossed beneath a ridge the locals called the Shepherd's Rib. Water was good. Cover was better. From the Rib you could see the whole southern approach, and the whole southern approach was empty, which in my experience is the most suspicious thing a landscape can be.

Empty roads mean traffic has been warned away. Traffic gets warned away when someone with authority has decided the road belongs to them now.

"We camp short tonight," I told Sera. "Push the pass at dawn."

"You think we're followed."

"I think we're *watched*. Followed is a decision. Watched is just weather." I nodded at the ridge line. "Crown scouts have been working these hills since the proclamation. Not soldiers — soldiers march and complain audibly. Scouts sit. Scouts are patient the way spiders are patient."

"And if we meet some?"

"Then we have a conversation about alternative routes, and if the conversation goes poorly, we have a shorter conversation."

She looked at me for a long moment. "You've killed people."

It wasn't horror in her voice. It was inventory. That, more than anything, told me what kind of archivist she was.

"Eleven years moving illegal goods past armed men," I said. "What do you think the contingency plans were made of?"

"I think I'm glad the contingency planner is on my side of the argument."

"Everyone says that right up until they see the bill."

We made cold camp in the lee of the Rib — no fire, because fire at altitude is a signal flare with delusions of coziness — and I slept in shifts the way I always did, knife hand free, boots laced, one ear pointed downhill like a dog's. Sera slept badly, which I could tell from the way her breathing never quite settled into the deep rhythm, and around midnight she sat up against her pack and just watched the stars, and I pretended to be asleep and watched her watch them, because a woman who can't sleep under open sky is a woman running from something specific, and specific things have names.

She never said hers. Not that night.

* * *

Dawn came up bloodless and pale, and we hit the pass trail while the shadows were still long enough to walk in.

The pass itself was a throat of stone between two shoulders of the Rib, maybe forty yards wide at the bottom, narrowing to twenty where an old rockfall had choked it centuries ago. Smugglers called it the Needle. Everything worth moving through the southern Thornfells went through the Needle eventually, and everyone who used it knew the rule: cross it fast, cross it quiet, and never, ever stop in the middle, because the middle is where the mountain keeps its opinions.

We were thirty yards in. Twenty. Ten from the far mouth, close enough to see the trail spill down the northern side in lazy switchbacks, close enough to taste the end of the day's climb —

— and then the light changed.

Not dramatically. That's the trick of ambushes; they don't announce themselves with trumpets. The light just went slightly wrong, the way it does when something that shouldn't be blocking the sun starts blocking the sun. I glanced up at the eastern lip of the Needle, at the tumbled boulders where a careful eye might rest, and a careful eye found what it found: a boot. Just a boot, black leather, crown-issue, resting on a stone at an angle no resting foot naturally takes.

Six years of instinct went off in my chest like a struck bell.

"Down," I said, and grabbed Sera's collar, and we dropped behind the rockfall's shoulder as the first crossbow bolt went through the space where my head had been and sparked off stone behind us.

Then the Needle woke up. Voices above — two, maybe three on the east lip — and the flat clack-clack-clack of a crossbow being spanned, and a shout rolling down from the western side that meant they'd boxed the throat from both ends. Crown scouts, and not green ones. Green scouts make noise early. These had waited until we were in the choke, which meant they'd watched us enter it, which meant they'd been sitting on this pass since before dawn, which meant someone had told them exactly where a smuggler would take an archivist with a box.

Later. I'd chew on that later. Right then the arithmetic was simple: four shooters, two exits, both covered, and us pinned behind a rockfall with a client whose only instruction was *hide me*.

"Talk to me," I said to Sera, low, under the shouting. "Is there anything in that case that helps? Anything at all?"

"No."

"Anything that explodes?"

"*No.*"

"Worth asking." I risked a look around the rockfall's edge. The western exit was the problem — two scouts there, one with a bow, one with a billhook and the posture of a man who'd joined the crown's service because it came with meals. The eastern lip held the crossbows, but they were shooting down at a bad angle, arcing bolts that slammed into stone and shattered rather than skimming flat. Bad angle was a gift. Bad angles buy seconds.

Seconds were the only currency I had.

"The west pair," I breathed. "The one with the polearm is going to come down into the throat to flush us. They always do — bows hold position, blade closes distance. It's doctrine. When he commits, I take him, and we go out the west mouth under the bowman's line before he repositions."

"Take him," Sera repeated carefully.

"Incapacitate, ideally. Kill, if the arithmetic insists." I checked my knife, then the short sword on my hip, and hated how calm my hands were. Calm hands in a fight are a skill. Calm hands before a fight are a warning.

"He'll have armor."

"Crown scout kit is mail over the vitals and leather everywhere else. Mail stops blades. It doesn't stop a man falling fifteen feet onto rocks." I pointed up at the shelf of stone above the western mouth, the one the billhook man would have to descend. "He comes down that. I'll be under it."

Sera stared at me. "You're going to let him fall on you?"

"On *me* is the point. Falling men aim poorly. Falling men near me aim worse." I gave her the only comfort available. "Stay behind the rockfall. Whatever you hear, stay. Your instruction stands — I hide you, you stay hidden."

And then, because the universe despises a plan, the billhook man didn't wait for doctrine.

He came down early, alone, impatient, before the crossbows had properly suppressed us — a big man, broad through the shoulders, picking his way down the shelf with his weapon held awkwardly against the rock, and I realized with a lurch that he was younger than the kit made him look. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Some farm boy from the river valleys who'd been told covenant-speakers were a plague and hunting them was service.

He saw me the same moment I stepped out from cover.

Here is what nobody tells you about killing, the first time, when it isn't a brawl gone bad or an accident with momentum behind it. Here is what eleven years of proximity to violence had somehow never taught me. It's slow. In the stories it's a flash — steel, a cry, done. But standing ten feet from a living person who has decided you should stop existing, time goes thick as honey. You see everything. The chapped knuckles. The breath steaming. The exact moment his eyes flick from me to the rockfall behind me, doing the math on the archivist, and the moment his weight commits forward because his math says two targets, one blade, move now.

His math was fine. My math was better. He was on a downslope with his weight forward and his weapon high, and I was below him with a short sword and no illusions.

I didn't fence him. Fencing is for duels, and this wasn't a duel, it was geometry. I went inside his reach before the billhook's head could come around, took the shaft on my left forearm — wood biting bone-deep bruise, I'd feel that for a week — and put my shoulder into his chest and my foot behind his heel and let the mountain finish the argument.

He went backward down the shelf. Fifteen feet of it. The sound he made hitting the bottom was a sound I will not describe, because describing it would be a kind of dishonesty, dressing it up as something literary instead of what it was, which was a boy from a river valley stopping being alive on a gray morning for wages.

The bowman above shouted something — a name, I think, the boy's name — and loosed wild, and the bolt cracked off stone a hand's width from my ear, and I did the only thing the geometry allowed, which was to run at the western mouth screaming like something out of a badger's nightmare, sword up, giving the bowman a target that was coming at him fast and low, and he scrambled back along the lip to reset his shot and lost the angle entirely.

"GO!" I grabbed Sera's wrist as I passed the rockfall. "West mouth, NOW!"

We went through the throat of the Needle at a dead sprint, over the fallen boy — I made myself step wide, made myself look, made myself not look away, because I have decided that anyone who kills gets to keep the memory clean and unflinched, it's the only tax on the trade I refuse to dodge — and out the western mouth and down the switchbacks with bolts chasing us until the ridge swallowed the sightline and the shooting stopped.

We ran for a mile. Maybe two. Down through the scrub pine, along a meltwater cut, until my lungs filed a formal complaint and my legs seconded it, and then we stopped in a fold of hillside with the pass hidden behind us and the northern Thornfells opening ahead like a gray sea.

Sera bent double beside me, hands on knees, breathing in ragged pulls. The case had stayed strapped to her back through all of it. I remember noticing that — through the sprint, the scramble, the drop behind cover, she'd never once moved to protect herself before the case. Priorities like that are load-bearing. You build a whole person on them.

"You killed him," she said finally.

"Yes."

"You said incapacitate, ideally."

"The ideal and the actual shook hands and disagreed." I sheathed the sword with hands that had finally started shaking, three minutes too late to be useful. "He came down the slope at us with a weapon and orders. The orders were to take us alive, by the way — you heard the shouting. *Alive.* Which means someone wants you delivered, not dead, and that's a worse sentence than a grave."

She straightened slowly. Looked back the way we'd come, at the invisible pass, at the invisible boy lying at the bottom of a shelf of stone.

"I'm sorry," she said. Quietly. Like it cost her something true. "That you had to — that I'm the reason you had to."

"You're not the reason. The reason is a crown that's decided hearing voices is a crime." I rolled my left forearm, testing the bruise. "But yes. Noted. Apology accepted, entered into the ledger, payable upon delivery."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, brittle at the edges. Then her face changed, and I watched it change, and I didn't understand what I was seeing until she spoke.

"Theo," she said. "Your arm."

"What about it?"

"Look at it."

I looked. The bruise where the billhook shaft had struck was blooming dark across my forearm — ordinary, ugly, mine. Except around it, rising through the skin like heat through frost, was something else. Lines. Thin, branching, faintly luminous lines spreading out from the impact point in a pattern I knew, the way you know a face in a crowd before you know why — the same pattern that crept along the altar stones in Greyfen, the same tracery the old carvings showed around the mouths of covenant-speakers, the same veins of light that lived in the borders of every pre-Breaking map I'd ever fenced to collectors who whispered about them.

The mark of Vael. Waking. In my blood. Because I had killed, and something ancient in me had risen to the occasion like a dog hearing a whistle only it could hear.

I stood very still and did not scream, which I counted as a professional achievement.

"It fades," Sera said softly. "It fades after. I've seen it before."

"When?" I kept my eyes on my own arm like it might behave better under supervision. "*When* have you seen it before?"

She didn't answer. Of course she didn't answer. But she reached out and laid two fingers lightly on the edge of the luminous tracery, not touching the center, the way you'd steady something fragile without claiming it, and the lines dimmed, slowed, sank back beneath the skin over the course of a dozen heartbeats, leaving only the bruise and a strange residual warmth like sunburn from a sun that wasn't there.

And underneath the warmth, underneath the fear, underneath the wry narration I run over everything like ballast over a hull — underneath all of it, faint as a voice through six years of stone, something in my chest that had been silent since I was nineteen years old turned over in its sleep.

*Hold,* said the not-voice. *Wait.*

*Not yet.*

I closed my eyes. Above us the Thornfells went on being enormous and indifferent, and somewhere behind us a boy lay cooling on the rocks of the Needle, and somewhere ahead of us was a pass, and a case, and whatever truth Sera was carrying in that sealed box — and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moments, that I hadn't taken this job despite the dreams.

I'd taken it because of them.

"Sera," I said. "When we get to the wayshrine — the old one, two days north, the one with the broken bell — you're going to tell me what's in that case. All of it. Because I just bled for your cargo, and my blood is doing things blood shouldn't do, and I want to know why before the crown's next scouts find us. Or before *that*—" I tapped my forearm, "—decides to introduce itself properly."

Sera looked at me for a long moment. The wind moved her hair off her face, and what was underneath it wasn't guilt, exactly. It was relief. The relief of someone who has been carrying a secret alone up a mountain and has just been given permission to set it down.

"All right," she said. "At the wayshrine. I'll tell you about the keystone."

I hadn't said the word *keystone*.

Neither had she — not until now, not out loud, not in three days of contracts and provisions and careful questions.

The wind kept blowing. The mountain kept not caring. And I stood there in the cold morning light, holding a bruised arm and a word I'd never spoken, understanding that the job had changed shape somewhere back in the Needle, and that whatever I'd agreed to escort through the Thornfells, it had been waiting for me a great deal longer than three days.
