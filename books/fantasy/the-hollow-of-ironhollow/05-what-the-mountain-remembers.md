## 5. What the Mountain Remembers

There is an art to telling someone a truth that will rearrange their life. I have never mastered it.

I had three days on the road with Theo Brightwater to practice the opening lines. *The thing in my case is older than the crown.* Too dramatic — she'd laugh, and her laugh was not a generous one. *You should know that what you're escorting may be worth more than your own head.* Too honest about the danger; she'd renegotiate, and I'd already spent money I didn't have. *Do you ever dream of a voice?* Too close to home, and she flinched from home the way a dog flinches from the hand that fed it last.

So instead I said nothing for three days, and let the mountain do the talking, because the mountain had been trying to say something to her since the moment we crossed into Thornfell, and she wasn't listening yet.

She would. The blood always listened eventually. That was rather the problem.

* * *

The wayshrine sat in a fold of the mountainside like a cupped hand, half-swallowed by lichen and forty years of weather. It had been built before the Breaking — you could tell by the proportions, by the way the doorway framed a person standing rather than crouching, as if whoever raised it had assumed travelers would arrive upright and unafraid. Inside there was a stone bench, a hearth ring blackened by generations of fires, and a niche above the bench where a god-statue had once stood.

The niche was empty. Someone had chiseled the god out of it deliberately, leaving only the outline of shoulders and a suggestion of a face, like a shadow pressed into stone.

Theo stood in the doorway while I built a fire, and I watched her look at that hollow shape. She looked at it the way people look at graves they're not sure are occupied.

"Smugglers use this route," she said. "Why haven't they stripped the lead flashing off the roof?"

"Because it isn't lead." I held my hands to the flame. "And because things left in old shrines have a habit of being remembered by whoever takes them."

"That's superstition."

"Yes," I said. "Most true things start out sounding like superstition. That's how you know they've survived."

She grunted and came inside, which was its own small victory. Theo Brightwater negotiated with the world the way a trader negotiates with a river — constantly, warily, conceding nothing she didn't have to — but cold was cold, and the wind up here had teeth that bit through mercenary pragmatism straight to the bone beneath.

We ate. She cleaned the cut on her forearm from yesterday's ambush with the economy of someone who had stitched herself more often than anyone else had. And when the fire had burned down to a language of embers, she said, without preamble:

"All right. Keystone. You promised."

I had. Promises were currency I couldn't afford to devalue, not with this particular woman, so I set down my bowl and took the case onto my knees.

It was iron-banded ashwood, sealed with wax that predated the current dynasty, and it weighed far more than its contents should have — though "contents" was doing generous work, since what it held was less object than obligation. I broke the seal. The wax crumbled like old bread.

"You know the histories they teach," I said. "The Breaking. Forty years ago, the border between realms tore open along the whole spine of the world, and every god went silent at once."

"Everyone knows that."

"What they don't teach is that the border didn't tear." I lifted the lid. "It was broken. Deliberately. By someone standing roughly where you're sitting now."

Inside, on a bed of cloth gone grey with age, lay the first keystone.

It was unimpressive at first glance — a shard of grey-green stone the size of a fist, faceted like it had been snapped off something larger, threaded with veins of silver that moved. Not glittered. *Moved*, slow as sap, the way frost creeps across a window. Even in firelight, even to eyes that carried no gift, it did something wrong to the air around it. The fire leaned toward it. My breath leaned toward it. Somewhere under the floor of the world, something enormous turned over in its sleep and sighed.

Theo did not touch it. I'll give her that — most people reach immediately, the way children reach for knives. She went very still instead, the stillness of a woman who has spent her whole life learning which silences are dangerous.

"That's a piece of the border," she said quietly. Not a question. Her instincts were better than her education, which made her rare and useful and occasionally infuriating.

"A cornerpost of it. There were three anchoring the covenant — the treaty between the realms, the agreement that kept what sleeps below asleep and what walks above walking. Your histories call the Breaking an accident. A wound. An act of godless men." I wrapped my fingers around the stone and lifted it out, and felt it hum against my palm like a trapped bird. "This is one third of the lock. And it has been waiting thirty-nine years for one specific hand."

"The archivist's hand," Theo said.

"No."

I set it on the flat stone between us. The fire guttered, then steadied, burning slightly greener than fire should.

"The keystones answer to the line of Maerin Brightwater," I said. "They answer to *you*, Theo."

* * *

Here is what I expected: denial, anger, a drawn blade, perhaps. I had rehearsed responses to all three. What I got was worse. She laughed — one short, ugly syllable of it — and then stopped laughing almost before it started, because I think some part of her had been waiting for exactly this sentence since Greyfen, since the Needle, maybe since childhood, the way you wait for a diagnosis you already suspect.

"My mother," she said, "died when I was four days old. In a birthing house in Lowmarsh. Nobody's name. No family. The midwife sold me to a factor's household before my cord fell off. That's the whole inheritance. So either you've got the wrong Brightwater, or—"

"Or the birthing house was a fiction, arranged by women who died making sure it looked like one." I heard my own voice go gentle and hated it. Gentleness from me was always a kind of theft. "Theo. Three nights ago, crown scouts ambushed us on a road only smugglers know. You killed a man — the first in this contract — and afterward you told me the wind tasted like copper and singing. Did you?"

Her jaw worked. Outside, the wind worried the shrine's empty doorway like a dog nosing a wound.

"It tasted like—" She stopped. Started again. "When he went down. There was a second where everything went very quiet, and I thought I heard..." She shook her head hard, as if dislodging water. "A voice. Saying a word I don't know. And my blood did something. Felt like standing too close to lightning."

"That word has a name in the old tongue. So does what happened to you. Covenant-speech — the gift of speaking to what lives behind the border, and being answered. It runs in certain lines. Yours is the strongest I've encountered." I pulled back my sleeve, showing the faded scar-circle branded into my own wrist — the mark of the Quiet Order, though she didn't know it yet. "Mine is a candle. Yours, from what I saw on that road, is a bonfire someone forgot to watch."

"And Vael?" The question came out fast, stripped of armor, and just as quickly she tried to take it back — "Forget it, forget I asked" — but I didn't forget, and neither did she.

"Your god isn't silent because he abandoned you," I said. "He's silent because the border broke, and everything on the other side of it went quiet as the drowned. Whatever voice you heard on that road bled through the crack. It shouldn't have been possible. Which means the cracks are widening. Which means we're running out of time."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened next, because I have spent years pretending I understand it and I don't.

Theo reached for the keystone. Not decisively — hesitantly, the way you touch a door in a house you've dreamed of but never entered. And the moment her fingertips met the stone, the fire went flat and colorless, and the air filled with a pressure like the moment before thunder, and she made a sound I have never heard a living person make: a small, involuntary note of recognition, grief and terror braided together, the sound of someone hearing their mother's voice from across a crowded room after believing her dead for thirty years.

Then the memories came. Not hers. Not mine. They surfaced through her like bodies rising in a flooded cellar, and because I was touching her shoulder — because I made the mistake, or the choice, of steadying her — they surfaced through me too.

I will set them down as they came.

*A kitchen. Steam on glass. A woman with dark hair going grey at the temples, humming a tune with no words, and a child — not Theo, someone else, or possibly Theo, memory doesn't keep its edges clean — watching flour dust settle in lamplight.*

*A mountaintop at night. The same woman, younger, kneeling among nine others in a circle of white stones, all of them bleeding into a basin, all of them chanting a name that the memory itself refused to carry, as if the name had weight enough to break the remembering.*

*Iron. The smell of hot iron and burnt feathers. A scream that went on too long and then stopped in a way that was worse than continuing.*

*A pair of hands — strong, cracked-knuckled, a working woman's hands — pressing something small and grey-green into a wrapping of oilcloth, whispering: *not to be found, not to be opened, not until she comes for it, and gods forgive me, she'll be so angry with me, and that will mean she's alive.*

*A door closing. Snow beginning to fall. The feeling — this last fragment was pure feeling, no image at all — the feeling of having done something terrible for reasons that would never be believed, and walking away from it anyway, upright, unafraid.*

Then the fire was fire-colored again, and Theo was on her hands and knees on the shrine floor, shaking, and I was holding her shoulder hard enough to bruise, and neither of us knew which of us had been crying.

"She was real," Theo said. Her voice came out scraped raw. "She was — she hummed. She had a burn scar on her left thumb, I've seen it in dreams my whole life and I thought I'd invented it, I thought I'd invented *her*—"

"You didn't invent her." I eased the keystone away from her — gently; it clung to her skin like frost to a warm window — and wrapped it back in its cloth. "Maerin Brightwater was real. She was the strongest covenant-speaker of her generation. And forty years ago she walked into the Hollow beneath Ironhollow with eight companions and broke the border, and every history book in the realm calls it the greatest betrayal in living memory."

"Is it?"

I looked at the woman kneeling on the floor of a dead god's shrine, wearing her mother's cheekbones and her mother's gift and none of her mother's answers, and I lied to her.

"I don't know," I said. "That's why we're carrying this to Ironhollow. To find out."

It was a lie because I did know. I had known for eleven years, since the night the Quiet Order's last archive burned and I ran with this stone under my coat while our brothers and sisters died around me. I knew what Maerin had done and why, and I knew that the truth would either save Theo or destroy her, and I told myself — the way cowards have always told themselves — that she wasn't ready. That the road would prepare her. That I was protecting her.

Two women keeping secrets, sheltering from the wind in a dead god's doorway. Only one of us knew hers yet.

* * *

We didn't sleep much. Toward the coldest hour, Theo sat up against the wall with her blade across her knees and said, to the fire more than to me:

"The midwife who sold me. Do you know if she's alive?"

"I looked. Years ago." This much was true. "Dead twelve winters. But I found the factor's ledger from that year. The fee paid for you was marked *remitted — order of M.B.*"

Theo was quiet for a long time.

"So she knew where I was. All those years. She could have come for me."

"Or she was keeping the crown's eyes off you the only way she could," I said, "by staying a ghost. Theo — a woman who breaks a border doesn't get to also be a mother. She chose which failure she could live with. That's not forgiveness. It's just arithmetic."

"That's the worst sentence anyone's ever said to me," she said, and there was something in her voice that was almost a laugh, and I understood suddenly why her crew followed her, why Brannoc's people would follow her anywhere, why I had chosen her out of all the possible Brightwaters: because she could hold a broken thing and a joke in the same two hands.

"Get some sleep," I told her. "Tomorrow we cross the Saddleback, and after that the roads stop being roads."

She slept eventually, fitfully, murmuring fragments — half of them smugglers' cant, half of them, I suspected, a dead woman's lullaby in a tongue neither of us spoke. I sat watch and turned the keystone's cloth bundle over and over in my hands, and thought about the lie sitting between us like a third traveler, warming itself at our fire.

Dawn came up grey and thin. And with it, faint on the southern wind, came another sound entirely: hoofbeats. Many. Moving with the unhurried discipline of people who already knew exactly where they were going.

Theo's eyes opened before mine finished widening.

"You sold our route," she said softly. Not to me — to the wind, to the morning, to whoever was listening. "Someone sold our route."

I had not. But I knew, looking at her face, that the someone had a name she trusted, and that whatever came next through that pass, the woman who walked out of these mountains would not be the woman who had walked in.

I picked up the case. We ran.
