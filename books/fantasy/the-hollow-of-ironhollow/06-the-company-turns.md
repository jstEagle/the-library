## 6. The Company Turns

There's a smell that comes before trouble in the high passes. Not snow, not pine, not the iron tang of a storm rolling in off the peaks — though all of those were there too, layered like lies in a merchant's ledger. It's something underneath. A stillness. The birds stop arguing and start listening, and if you've spent enough years selling your sword to people you'd rather not invite to supper, you learn to listen with them.

I smelled it two hours before Brannoc found us.

Sera didn't. She'd spent the morning chattering — well, Sera's version of chattering, which is to say asking questions she already knew the answers to, watching which ones made my jaw tighten. The case rode strapped across her back like a second spine, and every time we stopped she checked its buckles with fingers that trembled only slightly less than they had in Greyfen.

"You're quiet," she said, as I crouched at the mouth of the pass and did not touch the frost on the stone.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet *differently*."

I almost smiled. Almost. "Then take note of this one. It's the quiet of a woman doing arithmetic."

"What kind?"

"The kind where the numbers keep coming up wrong." I nodded up-slope, toward the notch between the two shoulders of rock ahead — the Throat, smugglers called it, because everything went through it and nothing came back the same. "We've been followed since the river fork. One set of prints, big man, army-issue boots worn down on the outside edge. Carries his weight on his left leg. Favors it when he thinks no one's watching."

"You can tell all that from a boot print?"

"I can tell all that from knowing him." I stood, brushed frost from my knee, and let myself say the name out loud for the first time in six years. "Brannoc Vane."

Her face did something careful. "Your captain."

"My *former* captain. There's a distinction, and it matters." I checked the draw on my knife out of habit rather than need; the blade was fine. Blades usually are. It's the hands holding them that fail. "He taught me most of what I know about staying alive. He'll have sold our route by now, or he will soon. Question isn't whether he finds us. Question is who he brings."

"And the answer?"

I looked up at the grey sky pressing down on the peaks like a lid on a pot. "Whoever pays best."

---

We could have gone around the Throat. Add three days, drop into the low country, take our chances with the crown patrols there. But low country meant witnesses, and witnesses meant word traveling faster than feet, and besides — some stubborn, stupid part of me wanted to see Brannoc's face when he did it. Wanted him to look me in the eye while he collected whatever Inquisitor Corvane had put on the table.

You spend enough years telling yourself you're not sentimental, you start believing your own ledger. Then someone hands you the bill and you find whole columns you never entered.

We came through the Throat at midday, when the light slants flat through the notch and blinds anyone waiting above. Textbook. My textbook. Which is why it stung so much when the trap wasn't laid above at all.

It was laid behind us.

"Evening, Theo."

His voice came down the pass like a dropped stone. I turned slow, hand away from my knife — showing empty palms is a language too, and I wanted him fluent in it before I switched dialects.

Brannoc Vane stood thirty paces off, filling the narrow way the way he always had: a wall of a man gone soft at the edges, grey threading his beard, his left leg braced just so. Behind him, spaced along the rocks like beads on a wire, eight crossbows. Crown issue. Crown blue. Two of the faces under the hoods I knew — Dalla, who owed me a month's wages and a debt I'd never pressed, and young Piet, who used to cheat at cards so badly everyone pretended not to notice because losing to him was cheaper than losing him.

"Brannoc," I said. "You look well. Retirement suits you."

"Retirement." He laughed, and it cracked halfway through. "Aye. Something like that."

"How much?"

"Don't."

"No, genuinely. I'm curious. What does six years of— " I gestured at the space between us, at all the unspoken ledgers in it — "what does that fetch, these days? Was it a round number? Did they haggle?"

"Theo." His voice dropped, and there it was, the old warmth, the voice that had talked me out of walking into three separate ambushes because *the crew comes first, girl, always the crew first*. "Walk away. Let me have the archivist and her box. You walk south. Nobody writes your name down. That's the best deal anyone's offering in these mountains today, and I'm offering it because—" his jaw worked — "because you were the best of them. Because I don't want it to be crossbows."

Behind me, Sera had gone very still. I could hear her breathing — controlled, deliberate, the breathing of a woman doing her own arithmetic.

"The box," I said.

"The box."

"Which you haven't seen. Which you know nothing about. You sold a route and a name, and Corvane filled in the rest, didn't he? Told you what was in it. Told you what she is."

Something flickered across his face. Doubt, maybe. Or the memory of doubt, embalmed. "They told me enough."

"They told you enough to make it *clean*, you mean." I took one step forward, and eight crossbow bolts tracked me like a school of fish. "Here's what I know, Brannoc. I know you carried Tam Osterling four miles on your back after Harrow Ford with a shaft through his lung, because the crew comes first. I know you paid Widow Marsh out of your own purse when her boy died on our contract, and never told a soul. And I know a man like that doesn't sell his own for coin. So either you've changed, or somebody changed the price for you. Which is it?"

For a moment — half a heartbeat, maybe less — I thought I'd reached him. His eyes went wet and furious and lost, all at once, and his hand opened at his side like a man letting go of something heavy.

Then he said, "The leg's bad, Theo. Winters are coming on. The company's got forty mouths and no contracts since the border troubles started, because nobody hires swords when priests are buying. Forty mouths." He said it like an indictment. Like arithmetic absolves. "I chose the many over the few. You'd have done the same."

"No," I said quietly. "I really wouldn't have. That was always the difference between us, Captain. You did the sums. I did the standing."

And that's when Dalla's crossbow shifted — not at me, at Sera — and everything went to hell in the particular, intimate way that hell prefers.

---

Later, I would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory's a coward; it flinches from the moments that matter. But here are the pieces I kept:

Dalla firing wide — deliberately wide, I'd swear to it on any altar including the silent ones — and shouting "*Run, damn you*" even as she reloaded.

Piet not firing at all. Piet dropping his crossbow and stepping sideways into the gap between us and the bolts, arms spread, nineteen years old and cheating death the way he'd cheated at cards — badly, transparently, with his whole heart.

Brannoc roaring "*Hold!*" a full second too late, because a second is a very long time when the order is the wrong one.

Sera's hand closing on my wrist, hauling me back up the scree toward the notch while the world behind us tore itself open with strings and screams.

And Vael.

Vael, who had been silent for six years — silent through funerals and betrayals and every cold night I'd spoken into that silence like a fool speaking into a well — Vael stirred. Not words. Not even close to words. A heat in the blood, a pressure behind the eyes, a sense of the world going suddenly, terribly *clear*: every rock, every angle, every heartbeat on that pass laid out in silver. For one suspended instant I knew where all eight crossbows were, knew which bolt would land where, knew the exact weight and weakness of every body on that slope.

The gift. The thing the proclamations called plague and I'd called nothing at all because naming it made it real. It woke in me like a drowned man surfacing, gasping, and it wanted — gods, it wanted to be *used*.

If I'd let it, I think I could have killed them all. All eight, and maybe Brannoc too, with a word I didn't yet know I knew.

Instead I grabbed Sera and ran.

---

We gained the high notch with bolts sparking off stone around us, and then the pass geometry turned in our favor — the Throat narrowing to a crack no wider than a coffin, which cuts both ways. They couldn't bring more than two abreast. But two abreast is enough, and they had the legs, and Sera's breath was tearing ragged beside mine.

That's when Piet caught us.

Not chasing. *Catching up.* He came over the rocks like the gangly kid he was, blood sheeting down one arm where a bolt had kissed him, and he fell in beside us running without a word, without a glance, like he'd always been headed this way and the last six years had merely been a detour.

"Piet," I gasped. "What in all the hells—"

"You said once the company was the family you picked." He wasn't even breathing hard. Nineteen-year-olds never do; it's nature's cruelest joke. "Well. Picking again."

"You're dead to them now."

"Yeah." And he grinned — grinned, the absolute idiot, the grin he used to wear right before losing a hand of cards on purpose. "Worth it."

Dalla came next, alone, having loosed her last bolt into empty air and thrown her crossbow into the gorge for good measure. She didn't grin. Dalla never wasted motion on joy. She just fell in on Sera's blind side and said, "Corvane has riders. Two days out, maybe less. And Theo — the captain took their coin already. Half upfront. He has to deliver *something*."

"He has us."

"He has a story. Different thing." Her eyes cut to the crack behind us, where pursuit would come. "This notch bends twice ahead. Second bend, the trail splits — high road and the chimney. Chimney drops you onto the glacier shelf, ugly climb, but riders can't follow. High road is fast and a graveyard."

"Chimney," I said.

"Chimney," Dalla agreed. "Go. We'll hold the bend."

I stopped running.

Every instinct I owned screamed the mercenary's catechism — *don't turn your back, don't split the party, live to spend another day* — and beneath it, quieter, older, Vael's warmth pulsing in my veins like a second pulse, saying nothing and everything. I looked at Dalla, and Dalla looked back with the flat patience of a woman who had already done her arithmetic and gotten a number she liked better than I would have.

"Two of you against however many come up that crack," I said.

"Two of us against however many come up that crack," she agreed. "But they'll be strung out single file, tired, and facing downhill. And one of us cheats at cards." Behind her, Piet was already wedging a boulder loose with his good arm, whistling.

"Dalla—"

"Theo." For the first time in six years, she smiled, and it was the smile she'd worn at Harrow Ford, carrying the wounded, swearing cheerfully at the sky. "The crew comes first. Always the crew first. Somebody finally taught the captain that lesson backwards. Let us teach it forward."

I wanted to argue. I want you to understand that — I stood there in the freezing wind with betrayal burning my throat and grief sitting on my chest like a gravestone, and I *wanted* to argue, because arguing is what you do instead of admitting the sum has already been done.

Sera touched my elbow. Said nothing. Which was, from Sera, a speech.

I gripped Dalla's forearm, hard, the old way, the way that says *ledger closed, debts honored*. Then Piet's, and he squeezed back and whispered, "Tell nobody I couldn't shuffle," and I laughed — actually laughed, a horrible cracked sound with tears in it — and then I ran.

---

The chimney was everything Dalla had promised and worse. We climbed down it in the grey failing light, Sera white-knuckled and silent, the case protected between our bodies like a child passed through fire. Twice the rock gave way under me and twice the rope held, and both times I thought — distantly, absurdly — of the tithe I'd left on the silent altar in Greyfen. Salt on cold stone. Payment to a god who hadn't answered in years. Maybe this was the change coming back. Maybe this was the receipt.

From somewhere above, faint with distance and wind, I heard it: a single shout, cut short. Then a rumble — deep, tectonic, final. Piet's boulder finding its purpose.

Then nothing.

Nothing has a lot of textures. This one was the worst.

We made the glacier shelf as full dark came down, and I built a fire small enough to cup in two hands, and Sera sat across it from me with her knees drawn up and her eyes on my face like I was a text she was translating.

"You knew them," she said finally. "The two who stayed."

"Twelve years, all told." I fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch. "Dalla pulled me out of a river when I was seventeen and never once mentioned it afterward. That was her love language. Unmentioned things." The fire popped. Somewhere far below, wind moved through the pass like a long exhale. "And Piet was nineteen and terrible at cards and today he was the bravest person I've ever seen."

"I'm sorry."

"Are you?" I looked up, and I heard how it sounded, and I didn't take it back. "Because here's where we are, Sera. Your box cost me a captain, a company, and two people whose names I'll carry in place of the memories I might have had instead. Corvane knows our route because I trusted a man who taught me everything. And the crown's riders are two days behind us on roads I no longer have friends to warn me about. So forgive me if 'sorry' feels like small coin tonight."

She took it. Didn't flinch, didn't plead. Just absorbed it the way stone absorbs weather. "Fair," she said softly. "All of it's fair. And none of it changes what's in the case, or what you are, or where we're going. But Theo — for what it's worth — I didn't hire you knowing this would happen. I hired you because you were the only name on my list whose god was quiet."

I stared at her. "Say that again."

"Later." She lay back against her pack, turning her face to the wheeling stars, and her voice went thin and strange. "When you're ready to hear it."

Above us, on the ridge line, a red spark bloomed and died. Another. Camp fires, moving along the high road — not following, not yet, but pacing us. Patient as arithmetic.

And in my blood, faint as banked coals, Vael's presence pulsed once, twice — not words, not yet, but rhythm. Attention. The silent god, watching me burn my old life down, and saying, in the only language left:

*I see the ash.*

*Now walk.*

---

I sat up the rest of the night with my knife across my knees, keeping watch over a sleeping archivist, a sealed case, and the smoking ruin of everything I'd been. Come morning, I fed the fire one last twig, shouldered my pack, and did the only arithmetic that mattered anymore.

One road south. Two keystones' worth of secrets ahead. Zero lives left to lose that weren't already promised.

I walked.
