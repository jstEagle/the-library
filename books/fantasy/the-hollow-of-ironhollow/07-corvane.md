## 7. Corvane

I have hanged eleven people in the king's name, and I remember every one of their faces. This is not a boast. It is a discipline.

The Inquisition does not attract men who forget. Forgetting is how heresy spreads — a face slips from memory, a name goes unrecorded, and somewhere in a border town a woman who hears voices in the stones is remembered by her neighbors as kind, and kindness becomes permission, and permission becomes plague. So I keep ledgers. Faces in charcoal when ink is scarce. Names in triplicate. The last words of every condemned speaker, transcribed verbatim, because even a lie tells you what a liar fears.

My junior clerks think this is morbid. They are young. Morbidity is a luxury of the young; the old call it bookkeeping.

We made Greyfen by the fourth bell of the afternoon, riding out of a rain that had followed us for two days like a debt collector. The town sat where the salt road met the river — mud streets, fish-smoke, the particular gray resignation of places that exist to be passed through. My writ gave me authority over three counties and the right to commandeer lodging, horses, and silence. What it did not give me was dry socks, so my first act upon dismounting was an act of humility before a hearth, which is as close as I come to prayer these days.

"Master Corvane." The town reeve met me at the inn, wringing his cap. He had the look of a man rehearsing innocence and finding his lines insufficiently memorized. "We received no word of your coming."

"I sent word."

"We received no word."

"You received word," I said, "and burned it, or fed it to the fire out of caution, or never read it because reading crown correspondence felt like an invitation. All three are forgivable. Only one is wise." I let him decide which confession suited him. He chose none, which told me he'd done all three. "Where is your crier?"

"In the square, master, but—" He hesitated, and hesitation in a reeve is worth more than testimony in a courtroom. "There was trouble this morning. A woman taken up. Some are saying she was only ill."

"Only ill," I repeated. "What was her complaint?"

"She spoke to her loom. And it—" His voice dropped, embarrassed on behalf of the universe. "It answered."

* * *

The woman's name was Odile Vass, forty-one years, widow, weaver, mother of two living children. I record such details not from sentiment but because sentiment requires details to attach itself to, and I have learned that if I do not write them down, some part of me will invent them anyway, and invention is where mercy goes to rot into negligence.

She had been weaving since she was nine. Her husband died six years back of the lung-rot, and sometime after that — grief keeps its own calendar — the loom began answering her. Not with words, at first. With patterns. She would think of her husband's coat and find the threads arranging themselves into the weave of it. Then, in the last month, with actual speech: small comforts, mostly. *He is warm where he is. The children will be tall.* Things a lonely woman needs to hear and a dead man cannot say.

You will want to tell me this is harmless. Everyone wants to tell me it is harmless. The village elders said it, her sister said it, even the man who reported her — and there is always a man who reports her, guilt already leaking through his boots — said it, as though apologizing in advance for what he knew I must do.

Here is what I know that they do not.

Twelve years ago, in the county of Marrowdale, a girl named Liss began hearing her dead brother in the well-water. Harmless. Comforting. Within a season she was hearing other things in the water, things that were not her brother, things that wore his voice the way a crow wears a stolen ring. By winter she had stopped eating, stopped sleeping, stopped being Liss. She spoke in a language our grandmother's grandmother would have known and no one living did. She died on the feast of Saint Aldery, in my arms, seventeen years old, and the thing that had been speaking through her used her last breath to say — and I have carried this sentence for twelve years like a stone sewn behind my ribs — *"Thank you for keeping the door open."*

So no. I do not believe the gift is harmless. I believe it is a wound, and wounds admit infection, and infection kills the host and everything sleeping beside it.

Odile Vass wept when I questioned her. Not for herself — for her loom. "It'll go quiet again," she kept saying. "It always goes quiet if you don't ask it anything. I don't ask it anything anymore. I just listen sometimes. Is listening a crime?"

Listening is how doors get opened, madam. But I did not say that to her. I said, "Your children will be provided for," which was true, and "This will be quick," which I intended to make true, and I wrote her name in my ledger in my own hand, because a clerk's hand is accurate but mine is a promise.

Her sister spat at my feet in the square. I had the fine assessed and paid it myself from my own purse. Justice should cost the one who deals it something, or it stops being justice and becomes merely procedure.

That night I stood at my window over the square and watched the river take the town's lamplight apart, thread by golden thread, and I thought about doors. About the old covenant, broken — everyone agrees it was broken, though no two priests agree on when or by whom or why — and about what leaks through a broken seal. The Church says the speakers are remnants, echoes, dying sparks of a dead arrangement. The Crown pays me to say the same thing louder.

But I have read the confiscated texts. I have sat whole nights with the fragments the Quiet Order failed to burn, and there is a passage in the oldest of them — a single line, half-eaten by damp — that has never let me sleep properly since:

*The border was not broken. It was bitten.*

Bitten. By what? By whom? And what does a thing do, while it chews?

* * *

Brannoc Hale arrived at my camp two days later, escorted by his own men, wearing the expression of a merchant who has finally found a buyer for goods he is ashamed to own.

I had heard of Brannoc before I ever saw him. Captain of the Brightwater Company — mercenary work, escort work, the gray trade that keeps border towns fed and honest towns lied to. Solid reputation. The kind of man kings hire when they want something done quietly and deniable. Which is precisely the kind of man who, when the wind changes, sells information fastest, because mercenaries understand markets better than they understand loyalty, and markets at least have the decency to be honest about what they are.

He sat across my field table like a man sitting across from a surgeon. Respectful. Watchful. Hands flat on the wood where I could see them.

"You have information," I said.

"I have a route." He produced a map — hand-drawn, annotated in a cramped soldier's script, the smuggler roads through Thornfell marked in red like veins. "Two travelers. Passed through Greyfen ahead of your arrival. Took the mountain way, the one the crown doesn't know exists because the crown doesn't pay people like me to know things."

"One of them is a woman named Sera. An archivist." I laid down the description my agents had assembled: mid-thirties, dark-haired, traveling under a scholar's plainness, carrying a sealed case she would not surrender even to sleep beside. "Describe the other."

Brannoc's jaw worked once. Here it comes, I thought. The moment every informer dreads and every informer arrives at: the point where the goods turn out to be a person.

"Theo Brightwater," he said. "My second. Twelve years in my company. Best blade I ever fielded and the only one of my people who still tithes at the old altars." He laughed, short and ugly. "Out of habit, she says. Pays a silent god like settling a debt to a landlord who's moved away."

"Theo Brightwater." I wrote the name. I looked at it. Something in my chest performed a small cold maneuver that I have learned to recognize as recognition. "Brightwater," I said. "Any relation to Maerin Brightwater?"

The change in him was immediate and total. Men can hide fear in their faces and lies in their mouths, but the hands confess everything, and Brannoc's hands had gone still as a dead man's. "Why would you ask that?"

"Because Maerin Brightwater broke the covenant," I said. "Or ended it, or betrayed it, depending on which scripture survived the burning. Because every text that mentions her mentions a daughter, lost in the chaos after the Breaking. Because I have spent twelve years following a plague backward toward its source, and sources have families." I turned the ledger toward him. "Does she hear them, Captain? Does she hear the gods?"

"I don't know."

"You do know. You sold me her route; sell me the rest, it weighs less than you think."

He stood then, and his men shifted, and my guards shifted, and for a moment the tent held the particular tension of a room deciding whether to become a battlefield. Then Brannoc sat back down, because Brannoc was many things but he was not a man who mistook a moment for a future.

"She dreams," he said. "Wakes up speaking words nobody knows. Twice last year she put her hand on a dying horse and the horse got up. She thinks it's nothing. She prays to a god that doesn't answer and calls the quiet faith." His voice cracked along a seam I suspected had been forming for days, maybe years. "She pulled me out of the Siltmarsh campaign with an arrow through my lung. She carried Denna four miles with her own shoulder dislocated. Whatever blood is in her, it's hers, and she's never once used it for anything but—"

"Saving people," I finished. "Yes. They all say that. The ones who love them always say that."

"And what do you say, Inquisitor?" There it was — the anger, arriving late but arriving honestly. "You've hunted us — hunted *them*, the speakers — for how long? You've killed what, a dozen? All of them weavers and well-listeners and children talking to grandmothers' ghosts? Where in all your ledgers is the one who opened a door on purpose? Where's your great plague, Master Corvane? Show me the corpse it made."

I could have answered him with doctrine. Doctrine answers everything, which is how you know it isn't listening.

Instead I told him about Liss. My sister. Seventeen. The well-water. The last words. I told it plainly, without ornament, the way you report weather, because ornament is where grief goes to perform and I refuse to perform for a man selling me his own soldier.

When I finished, Brannoc was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his anger was gone, replaced by something worse — the flat calm of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"My sister drowned too," he said. "Fever, when we were boys. No voices, no gods. Just fever. The world kills us plenty, Inquisitor, without needing help from the other side of it." He rose, took his payment pouch, and left it untouched on the table. "Keep the coin. Buy something warm for the next one. You're going to need it."

At the tent flap he paused. "Theo will kill you," he said, almost conversationally. "Not because she's cruel. Because she's cornered, and cornered things with her blood don't die easy. I've seen her walk out of things that had no exit." He looked at me with something I was surprised to find I recognized: pity. "Whatever you think is hunting her, Inquisitor — check whether it's just you."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat with my ledgers instead, and I did a thing I have never done in twelve years of methodical faith: I reread them looking for doubt.

Eleven hangings. Eleven faces. I went through each one the way a man walks a fence line in failing light, testing posts. The miller of Harrowgate, who sang to the river and the river sang back — had he ever asked the river for anything? No. Had the river ever asked *him*? I had not thought to record that. The twin boys of Pell's Crossing, who shared dreams the way other twins share a mirror — I had separated them before questioning, standard practice, and both had wept, and neither had spoken a word of power, and I had recommended leniency upward to a tribunal that did not believe in the word. The old shepherd. The midwife. The girl who was not Liss, though for one terrible moment in the square she had turned her head at exactly Liss's angle, and I had signed faster than I needed to, and I have never written that down until now.

There. That is what the gift does, even secondhand. Even to me. It makes the dead wear familiar faces, and it makes the living sign too quickly.

But here is the thing my clerks would not understand, and Brannoc would not forgive, and I accept both costs: being wrong sometimes is not an argument for stopping. A physician buries patients. A mason loses buildings. The failure of a method is not the refutation of its necessity — it is the tuition. Liss died because no one was watching. Odile Vass died because someone was. If those are the only two options available to this world, I will spend my life choosing the second and paying for it nightly, and I will keep the ledgers, and God — whatever is left of Him, whatever is drowning on the far side of that bitten border — can weigh them when I arrive.

Before dawn, I woke my senior rider.

"Break camp. Full company, and send word to the garrison at Ironhollow — I want the fortress passes watched and the mining warrens sealed. Nothing comes out of Thornfell without passing me." I rolled Brannoc's map closed and tucked it inside my breastplate, against the sternum, where I keep the things I intend to be buried with. "The Brightwater woman is heading for the mountains. She carries something, or she *is* something, and either way she is walking toward the place where the border was bitten."

"And if she resists, Master Corvane?"

I looked out at the dark line of Thornfell rising against the stars, black teeth against blacker gum, and I thought of a girl of seventeen kneeling at a well, and a weaver asking nothing of her loom but company, and a captain who paid for a hanging he hadn't ordered and called it warmth for the next one.

"Then she resists," I said. "And I will write her name in my own hand."

We rode at first light, north, into the teeth of the mountains — and by noon, the first report came up the column from our outriders, and it was not about Theo Brightwater at all.

It was about the snowline. About how, above the village of Ashfall, a whole slope of it had come down in the night, miles from any road, burying a pass no crown map admitted existed.

As if something up there had spoken a word.

And the mountain had answered.
