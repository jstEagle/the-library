## 8. The Drowned Chapel

The lake found us before we found it.

That's the thing about Thornfell — nothing announces itself. You walk for two days through pine and scree with your collar up against the wind, telling yourself you know these mountains because you've run smuggler roads through them since you were sixteen, and then the trees simply give up, part like a curtain, and there it is: water so still it looks poured rather than gathered, lying in the bowl of the valley like something the mountain swallowed and never quite digested.

"Chapel's under there," Sera said.

I looked where she was looking. The lake's near shore had eaten half of some old building whole — you could see the roofline slanting down beneath the surface, slate tiles furred with pale weed, and above the waterline the top third of a bell tower leaned out at an angle, like a drowning man who'd gotten his head above water and then simply decided not to struggle anymore. A bell hung silent in the open arch, green with age, tilted just enough that it would have been useless even when it rang.

"A chapel," I said. "Underwater."

"The lake rose after the Breaking. Waters remember what they cover." Sera adjusted the strap of her pack — the case inside it, the one she'd guarded like a second heartbeat since Greyfen, shifted with a faint sound I'd been trying not to hear for days. Not wood. Not metal. Something between a hum and a held breath. "It's dry inside. Mostly. The water stopped rising years ago."

"And we're sheltering in a sunken chapel because...?"

"Because nothing crown-built comes here, no god still answers here, and the things that do come here don't like to be watched." She started down toward the shore without waiting for me. "Also because I said so, and you're being paid triple."

There it was. Triple pay, the great silencer of misgivings. I'd have argued more on principle, but the light was going amber and low, and the last thing I wanted was to make camp in open ground with Corvane's riders somewhere behind us and whatever had brought down that slope ahead of us. Because yes — I'd heard about the pass. Everyone in the high valleys had heard about the pass by now. A whole snowfield, moving on a windless night, burying a road only smugglers used.

Sera hadn't said a word about it. That was its own kind of loud.

* * *

Getting in was exactly as dignified as you'd expect. The chapel's doorway stood half above the waterline on the far side, which meant wading thigh-deep through water cold enough to make my teeth file formal complaints, hauling our gear over my head while Sera picked her way along a submerged wall-top like this was a stroll she'd taken a hundred times. Maybe she had. There was a lot about Sera I was beginning to suspect she'd done a hundred times.

Inside, she'd been right, damn her. Dry. Mostly. The floor sat maybe a handspan below the water level outside, and old stone holds its grudge against wet better than you'd think. Someone long ago had stacked furniture against the far wall — pews dragged into a barricade that faced inward instead of toward the door, which told me either the last congregation had feared each other or had feared something coming from the lake itself. I chose not to speculate out loud.

The interior rose into shadow. Broken rafters. An altar at the far end, black stone, dry as bone. And everywhere — everywhere — carvings worn nearly smooth but still legible if you let your fingers read them: interlaced figures, hands joined to hands joined to hands, a chain of them circling the walls at shoulder height. The covenant-work. I'd seen it on wayshrines all my life and never once thought about what it meant. Hands, joined. That was all it had ever been to me. Decoration.

"Sit," Sera said. "Eat. We're safe for one night. The keystone wants to rest too."

I paused with hardtack halfway to my mouth. "You can feel it?"

"I can feel everything about it. It's why I was chosen to carry it." She set the case down between us with a care that bordered on liturgy. "And it's restless tonight. Can't you hear it?"

I could. I had been hearing it since the lake came into view — that hum underneath my own pulse, faint, patient, like a tuning fork struck somewhere in another room. I'd assumed it was the altitude. Or hunger. Or the beginning of a headache I refused to schedule time for.

"It got louder when we crossed the tree line," I admitted.

"Yes."

"Sera. Straight answer, once, please. Why does a sealed box sing to me?"

She was quiet long enough that I counted four drips of water somewhere in the dark above us. Then she unlatched the case.

I want to say I saw it clearly. I didn't. The chapel had gone blue-dark with evening, and what lay in the case seemed to drink even that — a shard of stone roughly the length of my forearm, angular, shot through with veins of something that caught light and kept it, and around it the air did that thing heat does over summer road-stone, bending, trembling. The humming resolved as I stared at it. It wasn't a hum anymore. Up close, it was almost words. Almost a voice saying something over and over, just below the threshold where sound becomes language.

"It's been doing that since Greyfen," Sera said quietly. "Louder each day. Loudest near you." She turned the case toward me. "Your blood opened the first seal at the wayshrine. Your blood is Maerin Brightwater's blood. This stone was hers, Theo. It has been waiting a long time to be held again."

Every sensible mercenary instinct I owned stood up, collected its belongings, and headed for the door. I stayed sitting because my legs had made their own decision, and because — gods help me, gods who weren't listening — I wanted to touch it.

"You said activated. At the shrine. What does activation mean, exactly? In terms I can bill for."

"I don't fully know," Sera said, and the honesty in it was worse than a lie would have been. "Maerin's order kept records, not instructions. But the stone remembers her. And you carry her blood. Touch it, and it may... show you what it remembers."

"That sounds like a curse from a bad ballad."

"It probably is."

We looked at each other across the open case, two women in a drowned chapel, and I thought about every choice that had led me here — the tithe at Greyfen's dead altar, paid out of habit to a god who'd stopped answering; the crier's list; the neighbor dragged away by men in grey; Brannoc's face in the pass as the arrows went home; Dalla and Wren, who'd died covering my escape and whose names I'd been carrying unspoken for three days like stones in my boot. I thought about how every one of those choices had felt small and reasonable at the time.

Then I reached out and touched the keystone.

* * *

The chapel vanished.

Not faded — vanished, like a door slammed between heartbeats. And I stood somewhere else, somewhere that was also somehow the same place, except the roof was whole and the walls were white and the chain of carved hands around the room was fresh-cut and gilded, catching candlelight from a hundred flames that burned without flickering, which should have been my first warning, because fire always flickers, fire is alive, and this fire was performing.

People filled the benches. Dozens. Hundreds. They were singing — no, not singing, speaking, all together, a low rolling cadence in a language I shouldn't have known and knew anyway, each word landing in my chest like a hand pressing flat against my sternum. Covenant-speech. Massed. The air itself was thick with it, and above the altar the space bent the way the air above the keystone bent, and through that bend I could see — sky. Not the chapel ceiling. Actual sky, stars, a border of light hanging in the air like a seam in cloth, and along that seam walked figures I couldn't focus on, tall and robed and wrong in a way my mind kept sliding off of.

At the altar stood a woman with her back to me.

Dark hair, braided crown-wise. A travel-stained coat with the shoulders worn shiny from pack straps. She was raising both hands toward the seam in the world, and she was speaking alone now — the congregation had fallen silent, hundreds of people holding one breath — and her voice was the voice from the keystone's hum, and it was steady, and it was terrified.

I knew her. Not from memory. From the mirror, on bad mornings, in the line of the jaw and the set of the mouth. My hands knew her coat. My lungs knew the rhythm of her breathing.

Mother.

I tried to call out. Nothing came. Of course nothing came — this wasn't a place, it was a memory, and memories don't leave room for visitors.

The ritual climbed. I felt it climb in my own body, pressure building behind my eyes, in my teeth, in the scar on my left forearm that I suddenly understood I'd inherited rather than earned. The seam in the air widened — not breaking, opening, the way a door opens — and beyond it was not sky. Beyond it was hunger. I don't have better words than that, and I've killed men and buried friends and I'm telling you there are no better words. It looked back. It had been looking back the whole time, through the seam, patiently, the way winter looks at a green field.

And Maerin Brightwater — my mother, a phrase that until this moment had been an empty envelope with no letter in it — began to close it.

That's when the iron came in.

Sound first: boots, many, ringing on stone outside. Then the doors bursting open, and men in the crown's grey-and-gold pouring in with drawn steel, and the candles guttering — really guttering now, finally behaving like fire, like something dying — and the congregation screaming and scattering, and a voice bellowing orders I couldn't parse through the roar, and Maerin turning, and her face —

Her face was the worst part. Not fear. Recognition. She looked at the soldiers the way you look at a debt you'd always known was coming.

"Seal it!" someone shouted — one of the robed figures at the seam, voice cracking. "My lady, we cannot hold it and fight — seal it or lose everything!"

"I need three more minutes—"

"We have none!"

Steel met flesh somewhere to my left. The scream that went up wasn't one voice, it was dozens, and through it all Maerin turned back to the seam and did the only thing left, the unfinished thing, the thing that would cost what it cost — she spoke a word I felt in my bones rather than heard, a word like a mountain deciding something, and the seam began to slam shut, too fast, unevenly, like a wound being cauterized by a drunk surgeon—

—and through the closing gap, before it sealed, something reached out.

I felt it reach. Through her hands. Through my hands, because I was her and she was me and the memory didn't care about the difference. Cold. Patient. Delighted. A attention like a thumb pressed on the wet spot of your soul, finding exactly where it hurt, and pressing, and a voice that was not a voice saying, in the tone of someone noting a detail for later:

*Oh. There you are.*

Then iron. A blade, taking her mid-spell — I saw it happen, felt it happen, the shock-bloom of it, her knees hitting the altar step, blood on white stone, and the seam snapping shut above her with a crack like the world biting down, incomplete, badly sealed, leaking at the edges — and her hand, her bleeding hand, dragging itself across the altar, carving something into the black stone even as the soldiers closed in, even as the memory tore at its own edges—

—the last thing I saw was her face turned toward me, toward wherever-I-was, and her mouth shaping words I couldn't hear over the iron and the screaming, and I would spend a long time afterward wondering whether those words were a warning or a name.

* * *

I came back to myself on the chapel floor with Sera's hands on my shoulders and my own voice raw, which means I'd been making noise, which I'll be thinking about at three in the morning for the rest of my life.

Outside, full dark. Inside, the keystone in its case glowing faintly now, veins pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse, and the hum gone quiet — satisfied, spent, like a dog that finally got to finish its story.

"What did you see?" Sera asked. Her face was very close and very careful.

I took stock. My hands were shaking. The scar on my left forearm ached like the day it was cut, except I'd never been able to remember getting it, and now I knew why, and the knowing was worse than any explanation would have been. My mother hadn't given me life and vanished. My mother had been cut down mid-ritual, sealing a door on something hungry, leaving the lock broken, and the blood on the altar — her blood — had been the last thing to bind what binding was left.

My inheritance wasn't a gift. It was a crime scene. And I was the evidence.

"Theo. Talk to me."

"She was interrupted," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "The ritual. Soldiers broke it. Crown soldiers, Sera — grey and gold. Whatever she was doing, the crown stopped it, and the seal went in crooked, and something on the other side *saw her.* Saw—" I stopped, because the next word was *me*, and I wasn't ready to say it aloud in the dark.

Sera had gone very still. "Grey and gold," she repeated. "With a sunburst on the breast?"

"How did you—"

"Because that was the Order of the Unbroken Dawn. Corvane's order. Their founding charter dates from the year after the Breaking, Theo. They weren't formed to hunt your mother." Her voice dropped. "They were formed *because of* her. By the men who did it. The crown has been finishing that interruption for thirty years, and it calls the hunt an inquisition."

The lake lapped against the drowned walls. Somewhere above us, the tilted bell stirred in a wind I couldn't feel, and did not ring — and yet I heard it, one clear note, deep under the water, deep under everything, like a voice remembering how.

And in that note, in the silence after it, something answered from far away. Not Vael. Not quite. Something older and colder and patient, pressing up through thirty years of bad carpentry in the seams of the world, and it said, in the tone of someone noting a detail for later:

*Still there.*

I got up off the floor and started packing. We had a fortress to break into, an inquisitor to outrun, and apparently a family legacy of botched apocalypses to clean up.

But as I shouldered my pack, one thought rode with me, quiet and cold as the lake:

Whatever my mother had failed to finish — whatever had looked through that seam and seen her, and seen *me* —

it had been waiting thirty years for someone with my blood to come back to the door.

And now I was coming.
