## 10. The Toll Bridge

The river came out of nowhere, which is Thornfell's way of saying hello.

One moment we were picking our way down a scree slope that hated us personally, and the next there it was: a grey-green ribbon of water cutting the valley floor, moving with the patient confidence of something that had been erasing mountains since before anyone thought to name them. And straddling it — because of course straddling it — was a bridge.

I've crossed a lot of bridges in my years. Most of them were honest structures: stone, rope, timber, occasionally a fallen tree with delusions of infrastructure. This one looked honest enough at first glance. Old stone, arched twice over the water, moss eating its edges like a slow green fire. It was the kind of bridge you'd cross without thinking twice and then spend the rest of your life not thinking about again.

Except for the toll-keeper.

He stood at the near end, and I want to be precise about what I mean by *stood*, because he wasn't quite doing that. He was more *insisting upon standing* — a tall figure in a robe the color of river-fog, holding a lantern in one hand even though the afternoon sun was up and doing its job perfectly well. His face kept almost resolving into an old man's features and then losing interest, blurring back into smooth grey like water closing over a dropped coin.

Sera stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her pack.

"That's a godling," she whispered.

"It's a man in a bathrobe," I said, because denial is free and I'd been paying out everything else lately.

"He's a *godling*, Theo. A minor deity. Look at him."

I looked. The lantern flickered, and inside its glow I saw — or thought I saw, which in the Thornfells amounts to the same legal standard — small shapes drifting through the flame. Faces, maybe. Or memories wearing faces, the way a coat wears a man.

"He's clinging," Sera said softly, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before. Not fear. Grief. "Look how thin he is. When the covenant broke, most of the little gods just... went out. Like candles. But some of them refused. Some of them held on by taking payment from travelers. Memories, usually. It's how they eat now."

"Wonderful," I said. "A bridge troll with a theological upgrade."

"Theo—"

"Sera." I shifted my pack and drew a breath that tasted of river-mist and impending expense. "We have two keystones' worth of road ahead of us, one sealed case that hums when it thinks I'm not listening, and about nine days of food if we stretch it, which we will, because stretching rations is the one prayer every mercenary actually believes in. If the way across this river is a sad ghost who wants my memories, then I'll haggle. That's all this is. Haggling with extra steps."

It wasn't all this was. But I didn't know that yet, and ignorance, as my old sergeant used to say, is the only luxury a soldier gets issued for free.

* * *

The godling watched us approach with eyes that were lantern-light reflected in nothing. Up close he smelled like rain on hot stone, that petrichor smell that always makes people homesick for places that may never have existed. His robe moved in a wind I couldn't feel.

"Crossing?" he asked. His voice came from everywhere around us and slightly underwater.

"Seems the general idea," I said. "What's the toll?"

"Something true." He tilted his head, and his face flickered — an old man, a young man, a face I almost recognized and then didn't, which unsettled me more than any monster I'd met on this road. "Something held long enough to warm you. Coin is dead weight here. I cannot eat coin. The crown's silver buys bread, and bread does not remember."

"Memories," Sera murmured behind me. "I told you."

"You told me. I was hoping you were wrong. You've been right so often lately it's becoming a character flaw."

The godling raised his lantern, and the light spilled across us both, and I felt it *looking*. Not seeing — looking. The way an appraiser looks at a necklace, turning it, weighing it, checking for flaws.

"Ah," he said, and there was hunger in it, but also something worse: recognition. "You carry the old blood. Brightwater blood." His gaze slid past me to Sera's case, and whatever he saw made him sway on feet that may not have existed. "And you carry a piece of the keystone. Oh. *Oh.* Travelers, do you know what walks across my bridge today?"

"Two women who'd like to keep their memories, thanks," I said. "Name your price and let's be done with it."

"Everything has a price," he said gently. "Even refusal. Refusal costs the crossing, and the crossing costs days, and days cost—" he gestured vaguely upstream, toward the snowline, toward Ironhollow, toward everything chasing us, "—well. You know better than I what days cost, Brightwater's daughter."

I flinched. I hate that I flinched. Somewhere between the wayshrine and the drowned chapel I'd gotten used to strangers knowing my mother's name, and I resented each new instance like a bill collector at the door.

"Fine," I said. "Show me the menu."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about selling a memory: you don't get to browse your own head and pick something worthless. Worthless doesn't feed a starving god. The godling's lantern showed me my life laid out in little flames, and every flame was something I'd rather keep than lose, because that's what memory is — the stuff that stuck.

There was my first kill, which I'd happily give away, except the lantern pulsed dull red around it and the godling shook his head. "Pain alone is gristle. Too easy to come by. I need warmth."

There was Brannoc teaching me to throw knives, laughing, before Brannoc became a name I wanted to burn out of my mouth. Warm, yes. But giving it up felt like letting him win somehow. I set it aside.

There was Vael. Every scrap of Vael. The godling's lantern practically *lunged* at those flames, and I snatched them back hard enough that I felt the motion in my chest.

"No," I said.

"They are very bright," the godling said wistfully. "A whole god's worth of attention, once poured into one small girl. Do you know how rare—"

"I said no. Those aren't for sale. Those are evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"That I wasn't crazy," I said, before my brain could stop my mouth. "That something used to answer."

The godling regarded me for a long moment, and the hunger in him dimmed into something older and sadder. "Ah," he said. "You're one of the *waiting* ones. I wondered. There are fewer of you every year, and the waiting gets longer, and eventually the waiting becomes its own religion." He sighed, and the sound moved the river. "No, child. Keep your silent god. Hoarding grief is its own toll; you've been paying it for years."

I did not care for how precisely that landed.

Then the lantern swung, and found a flame I hadn't noticed among the others. Small, low-burning, tucked in a corner of myself where I don't go often. The godling leaned toward it the way a starving man leans toward a bakery window.

"*That* one," he breathed.

I looked. And my stomach dropped through the valley floor, through the bedrock, into whatever cold dark holds the world together.

The house on Lantern Row. Greyfen, south quarter, the rented rooms above the chandler's shop where I'd lived from age four to eleven. Rain on the shutters. Beeswax smell bleeding up through the floorboards. My father's boots drying by the stove. The particular creak of the third stair, the one I knew so well I could climb it blind, and did, many nights, sneaking down to sit near the warmth while the adults talked low over their cups.

My childhood home. Before the fever took him. Before the company took me. Before everything took everything.

"No," I said.

"It is very warm," the godling said. Almost apologetic. "Homes usually are. That is why they hurt so much to hold, and why the hungry reach for them first."

"Pick something else. Pick anything else. I've got a scar I don't remember earning anymore — take the mystery, I hate mysteries."

"Child." His flickering face settled, briefly, into genuine kindness, which was infinitely worse than menace. "I will not lie to you as other toll-takers might. I am dying. I have been dying since the Breaking, slowly, politely, holding open this bridge because the people on both sides of it still needed to cross and no one else would do the work. I take only what travelers can spare and still survive. But I must take something true, and yours are mostly armor, and armor does not nourish." He lifted the lantern higher, and the little flame of home burned steady, unafraid, warm. "This one you can live without. That is what makes it fair. What makes it cruel is that living without it will cost you anyway. That part is not my doing. That part is just what remembering costs."

Behind me, Sera said quietly, "Theo. We could turn back. Find another crossing. It would take—"

"Four days," I said. "Maybe five. Corvane moves faster than five days." I stared at the flame. At the rain on the shutters. At my father's boots.

You know what's funny? In the moment, standing on that bridge, I couldn't remember my father's face. I'd lost that years ago — or sold pieces of it, maybe, in ways I never signed for. Dead men's faces fade fast when you're busy surviving. But the house? The house I had. The house was *mine*. The wax smell, the stair, the boots, the rain. The last place I'd ever been loved by someone whose whole job was loving me.

And the godling was right, which was the worst part. I could live without it. I had already been living without it, in every way that mattered. I carried it around like a coin I never spent.

"Wait," Sera said suddenly, stepping forward. "Take mine instead. I have—"

"You have archives to protect and a case full of keystone and a debt to a dead woman," I said. "You're not spending your memories on my nostalgia." I turned back to the godling. "How much of it? All of it?"

"All of it, or none. A half-eaten meal helps no one."

I closed my eyes. Wry narration is easier when you're not about to do something stupid and permanent, so forgive me if the wit dries up here.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. Take it."

* * *

I won't describe the mechanics. Partly because it happened fast, partly because describing it would require me to have understood it, and understanding requires having kept it. What I can tell you is this:

The lantern flared. Something reached into me — gently, I'll grant it that, gentler than anything else this quest had done to me — and lifted the flame out.

For one heartbeat I felt the whole memory at once, blazing, complete: rain on the shutters, beeswax, the third stair, the boots, my father's laugh coming low through the floorboards, the feeling — gods, the *feeling* — of being small in a warm place where nothing was yet broken. One heartbeat of holding it all.

Then it went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

And I was standing on a stone bridge over a grey river, looking at a stranger's house in my mind's eye. I knew facts about it. I knew it had been on Lantern Row, above a chandler's shop. I knew my father had died of fever when I was eleven. I knew these things the way I know the population of towns I've sacked: accurately, uselessly, without a drop of feeling attached.

The rain-on-shutters feeling was gone. The boots. The stair. Gone, gone, gone.

"There," the godling said softly. The lantern glowed brighter than it had — not much, but noticeably, a candle that had remembered it was once a bonfire. He looked less blurred. His face resolved into an old man's, and stayed. "Thank you, Brightwater's daughter. Your house will keep me lit through winter."

"Lucky house," I said. My voice came out flat. Distant. Like hearing it through a wall.

He stepped aside from the bridge and bowed, deep and formal, the bow of an older world. "Cross freely. And traveler — a gift, since you paid fairly." His lantern-light touched my brow, feather-light. "When you grieve this — and you will grieve it, soon, in some quiet hour when you finally stop moving — you will not know what you are grieving. That is the cruelest shape of grief. But hear me: the feeling of being loved once is not stored in houses. Houses only kept it safe for you. Whatever survives the sale, that is the true inheritance. Guard it better than you guarded the rest."

Then he blurred back into fog and lamplight, and the bridge was just a bridge, and we crossed it.

* * *

Sera didn't talk for the first mile. She's learned that about me — or thinks she has — that silence after a wound is a courtesy. Mostly she's right. Mostly I want the silence.

But somewhere along the river path, watching the water run with that infuriating patience, the grief hit me exactly the way the godling promised: sideways, unnamed, enormous.

I stopped walking. Sera stopped a few paces later and waited, which I was grateful for, because I couldn't have explained it. How do you explain crying over something when the something has been scooped out of you like the pit of a fruit? My eyes were burning and my chest was caving and there was no *there* there — just a hole shaped like home, aching in the negative space.

"I don't know what I'm crying about," I managed, furious at how it sounded.

"Yes, you do," Sera said gently. "You just can't see it anymore."

"That's the same thing as not knowing."

"It really isn't."

She sat down on a boulder and let me be ridiculous at the river for a while. The keystone case hummed against her hip, faintly, the way it did sometimes — like it was listening. Like it cared. Everything on this road cared about me except the things I actually chose.

"My father died when I was eleven," I said at last, wiping my face with a sleeve. "Fever. I know that the way I know tax rates. But I can't—" I pressed my fist against my sternum, hunting for the ache that should have been attached to the fact. "There was a house. Above a chandlery, in Greyfen. I remember the address and nothing else. It's like someone handed me the receipt and kept the purchase."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "What did the toll-keeper say to you? At the end?"

"Why?"

"Because the old godlings dealt in truths, not just memories. If he gave you a gift, it matters. They don't waste gifts on people who don't matter to the pattern."

I replayed it. *The feeling of being loved once is not stored in houses.*

"He said whatever survives the sale is the real inheritance," I said. "Which sounds profound until you realize it's the sort of thing ghosts say to make theft feel like philosophy."

But even saying it, I noticed it — a small coal under my breastbone that the grief hadn't touched. Warm. Wordless. The feeling of being loved once, still there, still burning, with no house and no face and no name attached to it. Just the fact of warmth itself, orphaned and stubborn.

I put my hand over it, the way you'd cup a candle in the wind.

"Come on," I said, standing, shouldering the pack. "Ironhollow's three days east. Corvane's probably two behind us. Let's not waste daylight on theology."

We walked. The river ran beside us, going wherever rivers go, keeping nothing, carrying everything.

It was near dusk when we crested a ridge and saw it below us: a village in the valley, roofs collapsed inward like the whole settlement had exhaled and given up. No smoke. No dogs. Figures moving in the streets — slow, wrong, unhurried figures that stopped and turned to watch us as one, though no wind carried our scent down to them.

"Ashfall," Sera whispered, and her hand found the case, and her knuckles went white. "Theo. That's Ashfall Village. It died in the Breaking."

The figures in the street began, all together, to walk toward the ridge where we stood.

They weren't hurrying. They had all the time in the world.

They were coming to see her mother's face.
