## 11. Ashfall Village

I have kept a great many secrets in my life, and I will tell you the truth about them: they get heavier the longer you carry them, but not in the way you'd expect. They don't weigh on your shoulders. They weigh on your tongue. Every secret is a word you're not saying, and words, for someone like me — for an archivist, for a woman who has spent thirty years believing that the written thing is the saved thing — unspoken words are a kind of rot.

So when Theo asked me, three days out from the wayshrine, "How much further to the pass?" and I answered her honestly, I felt lighter than I had any right to feel.

"Two days," I said. "And then one more day after that, if we go through Ashfall."

Theo stopped walking. She was ahead of me on the smuggler's track, as she always was, because she didn't trust the ground behind her and I'd stopped taking it personally. She turned, and the wind coming down off the snowline pushed her dark hair across her face, and for a moment — just a moment — I saw Maerin Brightwater standing on that ridge with the ash of the world still in her hair.

I looked away first.

"Ashfall," Theo said. "That's not on any map I've seen."

"No," I agreed. "It wouldn't be."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Ashfall Village before we reached it, which is more than I wanted to know and less than I needed:

Before the Breaking, it was called Ellowmere. It sat in a high green valley where three shepherd roads met, and it was famous for two things: its wool, which was soft enough that southern lords paid triple for it, and its shrine, which was old beyond reckoning. Not a god-shrine, exactly. Older than gods, some said. A listening-place. People came from four provinces to sit in it and hear — well. To hear whatever they heard. The covenant-speech ran strong there, in the water and the stone. Children born in Ellowmere were said to hum in their sleep.

Then Maerin Brightwater broke the covenant, and the border between realms tore like wet paper, and everything that lived on the far side of that border came pouring through the wound looking for something to eat.

Ellowmere was directly beneath the tear.

The village burned for reasons no fire could explain. The lake above it fell upward. And when it was over — when Maerin and whatever remained of her order sealed the Devourer back behind the broken seam — there were survivors. That's the part the histories leave out, or rather, that's the part I left out, because I wrote those histories, or the drafts of them, and I could never make myself write this part.

There were survivors. Forty-one of them, by the last count anyone bothered to make. And they stayed. In the ash. In the valley where the silence had come down like snowfall and never lifted. They stayed because their god had died in front of them, and they had nothing else to worship except the place where it happened.

They called themselves the Quiet Congregation. They called the valley Ashfall. And they called Maerin Brightwater the Godslayer, and they taught their children to spit when they said the name.

* * *

"Why do you want to go through there?" Theo asked me. We'd made camp in the lee of a fallen cairn, and she was cleaning her blade with the methodical patience of someone who finds the ritual calming. She'd killed two crown scouts on the mountain road two days past, and she hadn't talked about it, and I hadn't asked, because I recognized the look on her. It was the same look Maerin used to wear after the rituals went badly. Grief wearing competence as a coat.

"Because the other route adds four days through open country," I said. "And Corvane's riders are in the open country."

"And the villagers? Will they report us?"

I fed a stick into our small fire and watched it catch. "The Quiet Congregation doesn't speak to outsiders. Doesn't trade, doesn't warn, doesn't help. They haven't spoken at all in years, most of them. Vowed silence. They communicate by slate and gesture." I hesitated. This was the moment to tell her. This was the moment I'd been circling since the wayshrine, since the keystone sang under her blood and I watched her face while her mother's memories moved through her like weather.

I didn't tell her. Old habits. Heavy tongue.

"They keep to themselves," is what I said instead. "We'll be through the valley in half a day. They won't trouble us if we don't trouble them."

Theo looked at me across the fire for a long moment. She has her mother's eyes — I've never told her that either — grey-green, the color of the sea over a shoal, beautiful and treacherous in exactly equal measure.

"You keep doing that," she said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Answering the question I asked instead of the question underneath it." She slid the blade home in its sheath. "One day soon, Sera, you're going to run out of questions underneath, and then I'm going to want the whole ledger. Every entry."

"I know," I said. And then, because I am a coward in small ways even when I'm brave in large ones: "Sleep. Dawn comes early in the mountains."

She lay down facing away from me, and I sat up keeping watch, and the fire burned down, and I thought about all the entries in the ledger I was going to owe her, and how many of them ended with the word *ash*.

* * *

We came down into the valley at midmorning, and I watched Theo's face as she saw it for the first time.

You have to understand: she'd grown up hearing about the Breaking the way everyone does — as history, as catastrophe, as a story with a villain in it. Everyone knows the shape of it. The covenant broke, the gods went silent, the border tore, and a woman named Maerin Brightwater did the breaking. What nobody tells you — what can't be told, only seen — is what a wound in the world looks like eleven years later.

It looks like this: a valley that should be green, ringed by peaks that should hold snow, and instead everything is grey. Not burnt-grey. Ash doesn't burn twice. It's a fine pale silt that lies over everything, drifts against the ruined walls, softens every edge until the whole valley looks like something remembered by someone who wasn't paying attention. No birds. No insects. The wind makes a sound here that wind shouldn't make — a hush, a held breath, as though the air itself had learned reverence or fear and couldn't tell them apart.

Theo reined in — we had mules by then, bought cheap from a widow outside Thornfell who hadn't asked questions, another small mercy I intended to add to my growing debt — and she just looked.

"It's quiet," she said. And then she frowned, because that wasn't the right word, and she tried again. "No. It's *silent*. There's a difference."

"There is," I said. "Out here, sound dies before it reaches you. Listen to your own footsteps."

She listened. I watched her realize. Her boots were striking packed ash, and the strikes made no sound at all. The mules' hooves fell without echo. Even our breathing seemed to happen somewhere else, somewhere the sound could escape to.

"What happened here?" she asked, and her voice came out flat and strange, stripped of its usual dry edge.

"The Breaking happened here," I said. "Directly beneath the tear. This was the first place it touched."

She was silent — truly silent, in the local fashion — for a long time. Then she said, "And people live here?"

"Forty-one of them. Or there were, nine years ago, when I last had a count." I nudged my mule forward. "Come on. The road through is straight. Half a day."

* * *

The village announced itself by its chimneys first — a forest of them, standing over collapsed roofs like the masts of sunken ships. Then the walls, ash-pale, windowless. Then the people.

They were working a field when we came down the main street. That was the detail that undid me, both times I've seen it: they farm. In soil that grows almost nothing, in a valley where the light comes down thin and grey, forty-odd men and women turn the earth and plant barley and pray — silently, always silently — over the rows. The barley comes up stunted and white-stalked, and they harvest it anyway, and they bake it into grey loaves, and they eat, because the dead do not get to stop being hungry, and that is what they are. The living dead. The congregation of a god whose corpse is the silence itself.

They stopped working as we passed. All of them, in the same moment, the way a flock of birds turns. Dozens of faces lifting toward us out of the ash-haze, hollow-eyed, ash-dusted, ageless in the way that grief makes people ageless. None of them spoke. None of them moved. They simply watched us ride the length of their street, and the watching had weight. I've been scrutinized by inquisitors and customs-men and once, memorably, by a river-godling with too many eyes, and none of it compared to being seen by the Quiet Congregation. They don't look at you. They look *through* you, at the silence behind you, checking whether you've brought it anything.

Theo rode close beside me. I felt her tension like heat off a stove.

"They're not going to—" she started, and I shook my head.

"Slate posts," I murmured, nodding at the boards set at intervals along the street, each covered in chalk writing, layered and overwritten, years of messages in a dozen hands. *Welcome, traveler. Pass through. Do not linger. Do not speak. The silence hears.* And beneath, in a shakier hand, repeated over and over like a refrain: *He is still hungry. He is still hungry. He is still hungry.*

Theo read it. I watched her read it, and I watched the color leave her face, because she had stood in the Hollow beneath Ironhollow — no, wait, she hadn't yet; forgive me, the order of things blurs when I remember forward — she had *felt* something, in the wayshrine, when the keystone woke. She knew, in the wordless way the gift knows, that the chalk was not madness.

"He," she whispered. "Who's he?"

I said nothing. The heaviest secret of all, sitting on my tongue like a stone.

And that was when the elder came out of the shrine.

* * *

The listening-shrine of Ellowmere still stands, or the bones of it do. It was built before mortar, they say — fitted stone, older than the province, older than the crown, maybe older than the gods. Its roof had fallen in during the Breaking, and the congregation had left it open to the sky, because the sky was the wound and the wound was holy now. As we drew level with it, an old woman stepped through the doorway.

She was tiny and bent and wrapped in grey wool gone greyer with ash, and she walked with a stick, and she planted herself in the middle of the street directly in our path, and she looked up at Theo Brightwater.

I knew what was going to happen approximately one second before it happened. I have replayed that second many times since, and in most versions I move faster, I wheel my mule between them, I do something, anything. In reality I did nothing. I sat there with my secrets and my heavy tongue and I let it unfold, because some part of me — the archivist part, the part that believes the true record must be kept even when it costs — understood that Theo needed to see this. Needed it the way a wound needs air.

The elder studied Theo's face for a long, long moment. The wind moved the ash. Somewhere a shutter creaked without sound.

Then the old woman's mouth twisted, and she gathered, with visible deliberation, a mouthful of the grey silt that lay over everything in that valley — the ash of her god, the ash of her village, the ash of everyone she had ever loved — and she spat it at Theo's feet.

It missed. It didn't matter. Nothing lands harder than a miss.

"You have her face," the elder said.

Her voice was shocking after all that silence — cracked, rusted, a hinge long unused. Around the field, the congregation had gone still again, every head turned toward us.

"You wear her face like it's yours," the elder said, louder, and now other voices joined hers, hoarse whispers at first, then a low rising chorus, the vow of silence breaking all along the street like ice on a thawing pond. "Maerin. Maerin Brightwater. Godslayer. *Godslayer.*"

Theo had gone very white. "I'm not— my name is Theo. I don't— who is Maerin? What are you talking about?"

And there it is. There is the sentence I will carry to my own grave alongside all the others: *Who is Maerin?* Asked by Maerin's daughter, in the valley her mother broke, while her mother's victims spat ash at her feet.

"She never told you." The elder laughed, and it was the worst sound I heard in all the years of this story, worse than the Devourer's voice, worse than the tearing of the sky — a laugh with nothing in it, a laugh like a dropped stone. "Eleven years. Eleven years of silence, and her blood walks the world wearing a stranger's name, and she never even *told you*."

"Tell me what?" Theo's voice cracked too. Her hand had drifted to her sword, not in threat but in the old mercenary reflex, fear reaching for iron. "What did she do?"

"What did she *do*?" The elder's whisper-chorus took the question up and passed it down the street, *what did she do, what did she do*, and then the old woman raised her stick and pointed it at the shrine, at the open sky, at the grey light falling through the wound in the roof. "She killed the god who listened. She tore the veil and let the hunger in. She burned Ellowmere and drowned the lake in the sky and left us alive — do you understand, girl? She left us *alive*. That was her mercy. That was her punishment for us: to live here, in the ash, in the silence, tending the grave of everything, forever."

The chorus had become a wall of sound. Ash sifted down from the rooftops. One of the mules screamed — a soundless scream, mouth wide, throat working, nothing coming out, because even terror couldn't find purchase on the air in that valley.

"She has your face," the elder said again, quieter now, and somehow that was worse. "Godslayer's daughter. Get out of our valley. Take your mother's sins off our ground. Go."

* * *

We went. There was nothing else to do. Theo didn't speak — couldn't, perhaps — and I led the mules at a fast walk through the jeering, weeping, silent-again congregation, out past the last slate post, up the far side of the valley where the ash gave way grudgingly to scree and the wind remembered how to carry sound.

Halfway up the switchbacks, Theo finally stopped, dismounted, and stood with her back to me, looking down at the grey valley. Her shoulders were shaking. I thought she was crying. When she spoke, I learned she was laughing instead, and I honestly cannot tell you which frightened me more.

"Eleven years," she said. "My whole life, Sera. My whole life I've wondered why Vael went quiet. Why the gods all went quiet. And there's a village full of people down there who *know*, and they think my mother did it, and apparently so do you, because you've known this entire time. You've known since the wayshrine. Since before the wayshrine." She turned around. Her eyes were dry and terrible. "What did she do, Sera? And don't answer the question I asked. Answer the one underneath."

The wind came up the switchbacks and pulled at our cloaks, and below us the valley lay silent and grey, and somewhere in the middle of it an old woman was sweeping ash off the shrine steps with a bundle of twigs, tending a grave that would never be finished.

I opened my mouth. I owed her the whole ledger, every entry, and I knew it, and I was so tired of carrying the weight on my tongue.

"The case," I said. "The keystone you bled on. Your blood opens it because it was locked with your mother's covenant. Maerin Brightwater was the anchor of the old covenant, Theo. And the thing she broke it to imprison—"

That was as far as I got before the mountain answered.

Far below us, at the bottom of the valley, in the listening-shrine with its wounded sky, every member of the Quiet Congregation stopped moving at once. Forty-one heads lifted. Forty-one throats opened. And from the depths of the ashfall rose a single sound — the first sound that valley had permitted in eleven years — a vast, slow, subterranean groan, like a door the size of the horizon beginning, very slowly, to open.

The elder's voice floated up to us, thin and clear and utterly calm, and I have never wished more desperately that the silence would take her back:

"*He heard you say his name.*"
