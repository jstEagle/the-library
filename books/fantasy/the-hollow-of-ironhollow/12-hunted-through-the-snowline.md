## 12. Hunted Through the Snowline

### Theo

The snow started as a rumor at the treeline and became a fact by midmorning.

I'd climbed enough mountains to know the difference between weather that means to inconvenience you and weather that means to kill you. This was the second kind — the dry, hissing kind that comes down out of a sky the color of old pewter and buries every track you've ever made, including the ones you were counting on making later.

"We should turn back," Sera said, for the fourth time since dawn. She said it the way she did everything, in that flat archivist's cadence, like she was reading aloud from a ledger of bad ideas and mine was simply the next entry.

"Back is down," I said. "Down is where Corvane's riders are."

"Then forward is up."

"Forward is always up. That's the trouble with forward." I adjusted the strap of my pack and kept climbing. The keystone case rode against her back under oilcloth, and every so often it hummed — a faint, bone-deep vibration I felt in my teeth more than heard. It had been humming louder since we crossed into the high passes, which either meant it was happy or hungry, and given everything else about this job, I was betting on hungry.

Here's what nobody tells you about fleeing for your life: it's mostly bookkeeping. You count arrows. You count hours of daylight. You count the miles between you and the last place you slept, and then you do arithmetic about whether the men hunting you are better at math than you are. I'd been doing this kind of arithmetic since Greyfen, and the numbers kept coming back worse.

Two days ago there'd been three riders on our trail. Yesterday, six. This morning, from a ridge above the tree line, I'd counted nine — and among them a pale banner with a black circle stitched on it that I recognized from a distance most people never get to recognize anything.

That was an Inquisitor's mark. That was Corvane's own company, not some provincial levy playing at manhunt.

"He's not chasing us anymore," I told Sera. "He's herding us."

"Herding us where?"

"Away from the passes he doesn't know and toward the ones he does. Toward open ground. Toward—" I gestured at the white nothing above us, the snowline itself, where the mountain gave up pretending to be hospitable. "Toward somewhere a crossbow has room to work."

Sera looked at the slope ahead of us, then back down the way we'd come, where our tracks stitched a neat, damning line through the fresh fall. For once she didn't argue. That frightened me more than the riders did. Sera arguing was a constant, like gravity. Sera going quiet meant she agreed, and Sera agreeing meant things were worse than even my arithmetic suggested.

We climbed.

* * *

### Sera

There is a particular shame in being carried by someone smaller than you.

Theo Brightwater stood perhaps a hand shorter than me and weighed less, and yet by the third hour above the snowline she was breaking trail through drifts to my chest while I floundered behind her like something newly fished out of a pond. She did it without complaint, which was somehow worse than complaining. Complaint would have been honest.

She was also talking to herself. Low, half-under-her-breath, in a rhythm I recognized because Maerin Brightwater had done exactly the same thing when she thought no one was listening: a counting cadence, four beats and a rest. Old covenant meter. A prayer-shape, worn smooth by repetition, used by people who no longer believed they were praying to anyone.

*Vael isn't listening,* I wanted to tell her. *But the mountain might be.*

I didn't say it. Some knowledge is a weight you have no right to set down on someone who hasn't asked to carry it. So instead I watched her broad back and counted her steps and tried not to think about the blood I could feel seeping warm and wrong along my left side, where the scout's bolt had caught me two days ago in the pass below. Theo had dressed it. I had lied about how much it hurt. Both of those acts of mercy had been mistakes, and now the mistake had a fever in it.

"Sera." She'd stopped. "How bad?"

"It's fine."

"You're listing to port."

"I'm conserving energy on my left side."

She turned, and I saw her face properly for the first time since morning — wind-scoured, chapped, eyes narrowed against the glare, and underneath all of it a genuine, unarmored worry that she clearly hated showing. People think mercenaries are made of leather and indifference. They aren't. They're made of people who got very good at hiding the soft parts, and Theo hid hers the way a miser hides coin: constantly, compulsively, and badly, if you knew where to look.

"Sit," she said. "On the rock. Don't argue."

I sat. She peeled back the bandage with hands that were steadier than mine would have been, and her expression went through several small changes, none of them good. The wound had gone the color of bruised plums at the edges.

"That's not fine," she said.

"That's healing. Slowly. With complications."

"With rot," she said flatly. "You need a real healer, heat, and about a week of not running across a mountain, and you're going to get none of those things, and I need you to tell me honestly how long before your leg stops answering orders."

I considered lying again. I considered that she had killed a man two days ago to keep him from putting a second bolt in me, and that she hadn't slept properly since, and that she was asking me this question while kneeling in a snowdrift with her own frostbitten fingers.

"Half a day," I said. "Maybe less on a climb."

Something closed behind her eyes. Not despair — Theo didn't seem built for despair; it slid off her the way rain off oiled canvas. Calculation. She looked up-slope, then down-slope, then at the sky, and I watched her do the arithmetic I'd been doing all day and arrive at the same sum.

"Then we don't stop," she said, "and we don't get caught, and you can be furious at me later. Deal?"

"Deal," I said, because it was easier than telling her the truth, which was that I had been carrying a secret heavier than the keystone since Greyfen, and secrets make poor ballast when you're bleeding.

We went up.

* * *

### Theo

They nearly had us at the col.

I heard the horses before I saw them — that hollow drumming of hooves on frozen scree that carries forever in thin air — and by the time I'd pulled Sera behind the shoulder of the ridge, nine riders had crested the saddle below us and fanned out across the bowl with the crisp, unhurried competence of professionals who have done this many times and enjoyed it.

And at their center, on a grey mare too fine for this altitude, sat Inquisitor Corvane himself.

I'd never seen him up close. I wish I could report disappointment. He wasn't the theatrical monster of crown propaganda — no burning eyes, no cruel smile. He was a lean, grey-templed man in a plain black coat, sitting his horse like furniture sits a floor, utterly still, watching our ridge with the patience of a man reading a document he already knows the contents of. Somehow that was worse. Monsters roar. Accountants just wait.

"Brightwater!" His voice carried up the bowl, clear and almost gentle. "You've led me a long way, and I thank you for it. Every mile, you spent something you couldn't replace. Look at your friend. Look at how she leans. You cannot carry her over the icefield, and you cannot go back down. There is no version of today in which you win. Put down the case and send down the woman, and I will let you walk south. On my writ, I swear it."

The horrible thing was that he sounded sincere. The more horrible thing was that some exhausted animal part of me wanted to believe him.

Sera's hand found my sleeve. "Don't," she breathed.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Your face does something when you want to believe people. It goes hopeful and stupid."

"Charming. Remind me to describe your face sometime."

"Brightwater." Corvane again, still patient, and now the riders were moving — spreading wider, cutting off the low route around the bowl, two of them dismounting to climb. "I will not ask twice. My writ permits me great patience and greater force. Do not make me spend the latter on a dying archivist."

And that — the casual inventory of Sera as an object, a line item to be spent — did something to the fear in me. It burned it off like frost in sun. Fear makes you small; fury reminded me I had teeth.

"Come and take her," I called down, and drew my blade mostly for theater, because theater buys seconds, and seconds buy lives, usually.

Except the mathematics were rotten and I knew it. Nine riders, one pass, a wounded woman, an afternoon dying fast. We had maybe ten minutes of lead and no direction that wasn't watched.

So we ran. Up. Because down was death and sideways was death and up was only probably death, and I have always preferred probably.

The snowline broke over us like a wave. Past a certain height the mountain stops having scenery and becomes only angle and white — a face of scree and ice tilted at forty degrees, streaked with old slide-paths, humming with that deep sub-audible groan glaciers make when they're thinking about moving. Sera's breathing went ragged beside me. Her left leg was dragging. I got her arm over my shoulders and took her weight and pretended it cost nothing, the way you pretend everything costs nothing right up until the bill arrives.

Behind us, boots on scree. Shouted orders, clipped and calm. A crossbow span crackling.

"Brightwater — down!"

I dropped, and the bolt cracked off the rock where my head had been and whined away into the white.

They were close. Too close. Twenty yards, maybe fifteen, dark shapes flowing up the slope with ropes and pitons and the terrible ease of men who train on mountains for exactly this. And I felt it happen — felt the thing in my blood that Vael had left behind when he went silent rise up like water finding its level. Heat behind my sternum. A pressure in my ears like standing too near a waterfall. Words surfacing in me that I had never learned, in no language I had ever studied, pressing at the backs of my teeth like a sneeze made of meaning.

Every instinct I had as a survivor said *don't*. You don't touch fire you don't understand. You don't speak words you can't take back. The gift — whatever it was, whatever Sera suspected I carried, whatever my mother had died holding — was not a tool. Tools obey.

But the riders were fifteen yards away and Sera couldn't run and Corvane was watching from below with patient, certain eyes, and I was out of everything except the one thing I had sworn never to use.

"*Get down,*" I told Sera, and she read my face, and for once in her insufferable life she didn't argue.

The word came up out of me whole.

I won't write it here. Partly because I can't — it doesn't sit in memory the way words do; it sits in the body, the way a scar does — and partly because I have learned since what happens to paper that carries such things. But I shaped it with everything I had, all the grief for a silent god and all the terror of the moment and all the rage at being hunted like vermin by a man with a sincere voice, and I aimed it at the slope beneath the riders' feet.

The mountain answered.

Not dramatically. That's the part that haunts me. No thunderclap, no shudder. The snowfield above the riders simply *agreed* — a vast, silent concurrence, thousands of tons of packed snow deciding, all at once, to reconsider its position. I heard a sound like a giant exhaling, and then the whole face of the slope came down in a slow-motion white flood that swallowed the two climbers first, then the horses at the base, then the bowl entire, a white tide rolling over the grey mare and the patient man on it and everything else, erasing them the way a wet cloth erases chalk.

It took maybe six seconds. Then the mountain was quiet again, and the air was full of that glittering suspended powder that hangs after an avalanche, and the silence underneath was enormous.

Sera was staring at me from the rocks. Snow had settled on her shoulders and in her hair and she hadn't brushed it off. She was looking at me the way you look at a locked door that has just opened by itself.

"Theo," she said. My name. Not Brightwater, not mercenary. Just my name, said very carefully, the way you'd say *fire* to a child standing too close to a hearth. "What did you do?"

"I don't know," I said, and it was true, and the truth of it terrified me more than the avalanche had.

Because here is what I haven't said yet, what I could barely admit to myself as the powder drifted down around us like ash: it hadn't felt like power. It had felt like *permission*. Like something vast and buried far below the mountain had heard my word and answered, *yes*, gladly, hungrily — and that whatever I had spoken to, it had been waiting a long time for someone with the blood to knock.

The keystone, in its case against Sera's back, began to hum.

Loudly. Joyfully. Like something greeting kin.

And far below us, out of the settling white, came a sound that stopped my heart: coughing. One throat, then another. Voices. Men digging themselves out of the grave I'd dug for them.

Corvane was alive. Of course he was alive. He'd held back, hadn't he? Patient. Reading his document.

"Brightwater," drifted up through the glittering air, ragged now, the gentleness gone, replaced by something that shook — and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't rage in his voice. It was grief. "*What ARE you?*"

Sera gripped my arm. "We have to move. Now. Before they regroup."

I moved. I put her arm over my shoulders and hauled her up onto the icefield, away from the voices and the humming stone and the question hanging in the cold air behind us. But I carried the question with me anyway, because there was nowhere on this earth I could put it down:

*What am I?*

And deeper, quieter, in the place where Vael's silence lived:

*What did my mother leave inside me — and what did it just say yes to?*

Above us, the icefield stretched white and endless toward Ironhollow, and behind us, faint but gaining, came the sound of survivors digging.
