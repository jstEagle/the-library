## 13. The Price of a Word

I woke to the taste of iron and someone else's grief.

That's not a figure of speech. Grief has a flavor — I learned that morning, huddled in the lee of a fallen pine with snow coming down like the sky had given up on subtlety. It tastes like coins held too long in the mouth, like blood you haven't bled yet. I knew it wasn't mine because it came attached to a scar I'd never earned: a thin white line across the back of my left hand, old and silvered, the kind you get from reaching through a fence you were told not to climb.

I looked at my hand. No scar.

I looked again. Scar.

* * *

Sera found me staring at my own knuckles like they owed me money. She crouched, set down the sledge she'd insisted on hauling despite the wound in her side, and studied my face with that archivist's patience — the look of a woman cataloguing damage for a report she hoped never to file.

"Theo."

"Someone was in here," I said. "Last night. In my head. She was crying over a fence."

Sera went very still. Stillness, I've learned, is what clever people do instead of screaming.

"Tell me," she said.

So I told her. About the dream that hadn't been a dream. A garden behind an iron fence, and a woman with my hands reaching through the bars for something — a sprig of white flowers, frost-bitten — and the fence biting her for it. The pain had been mine. The memory hadn't. And when I woke, the scar was there, faint as breath on glass, and so was the knowledge of what those flowers were called. *Winterveil.* I have never in my life known a flower's name. I don't know the names of flowers. Flowers are things that die if you look at them wrong, like contracts.

"Say it again," Sera said quietly.

"Winterveil." My mouth shaped it without consulting me. "She grew them along the north wall. They bloomed late. She liked them because—"

"Because they were stubborn," Sera finished.

The word dropped into my chest like a stone into a well. I waited for the splash. It took too long coming.

"That's hers," I said. "That knowing is hers. Not mine."

"Yes." Sera didn't dress it up. One thing I'd come to grudgingly respect about her: she lied beautifully when lying mattered, and told the truth like a surgeon when truth did. "The covenant-speech doesn't run on nothing, Theo. Words of power aren't words. They're *promises*, and every promise is paid for. When you spoke the slope down, you paid with yourself."

"With what part?"

"Whatever the mountain thought you could spare." She glanced at my left hand, then away, and I understood she'd already seen the scar. Had probably seen it before I had. "It starts small. A memory here, a preference there. Things you won't miss right away."

"And then?"

She was quiet long enough that the snow filled the silence for her.

"And then you wake up one morning and can't remember your mother's face, because your mother's memories have taken up the room where it used to be."

The wind came down off the peaks and tried its hand at flaying us both. I let it. It seemed fair, somehow, that something should hurt on the outside while everything else hurt on the inside.

"How much?" I asked. "How much did I spend on that slope?"

"I don't know."

"Sera."

"*I don't know.* That's the truth. There's no ledger for this. No tally. You find out what's gone by reaching for it and finding someone else's hand where yours used to be." She pulled her cloak tighter. Her side must have been screaming; she never mentioned it. "Which is why we get to Ironhollow before you spend anything else. Every word you speak from now on costs more than the last. The gift isn't a muscle. It's a *hoard*, and you're spending from it, and something is making change."

"Something being my dead mother."

"Something being the shape of the covenant itself." She stood, wincing, and offered me a hand up. I ignored it and got up myself, because apparently I still had my pride. Small mercies. "Come on. We need to move before Corvane's riders pick up our trail under fresh snow."

I fell into step beside her, and as we walked I did what any sensible woman does after discovering she's being colonized from the inside by her own inheritance.

I made a list.

* * *

Here's what I know about Theo Brightwater, mercenary, smuggler, occasional blasphemer:

I know Greyfen. I know the smell of the salt-flats at low tide, the particular rot-sweetness of the fish market in high summer, the way the tithe-altar to Vael used to hum under my palm before it went quiet. I know Brannoc's laugh, which sounds like a wagon breaking. I know how to gut a man efficiently and a fish inefficiently. I know that I hate olives, love rain on canvas, distrust anyone who smiles before noon, and once lost a week of my life to fever in a place called Marlow's Ferry.

I know these things the way you know your own teeth. By feel. By absence when they're missing.

Here's what else I know, and this is the problem:

I know a kitchen with copper pots hanging from hooks worn bright by decades of hands. I know a woman singing low, out of tune, some lullaby about a river going home. I know the exact weight of a sleeping child against a shoulder at three in the morning. I know a door painted blue, though the paint was peeling, and the way the floorboard by the hearth always complained on the seventh step. I know a man named Aldric who laughed too loud and died badly. I know the taste of bread baked over coals on the worst night of a siege, shared four ways among people who were about to stop existing.

None of this happened to me. I'm sure of it the way you're sure of your own name — except lately my surety has developed a stutter. Because the sleeping child against the shoulder, in the memory, has my weight. My warmth. And the woman holding that child—

I've never seen her face. That's the cruelty of it. Maerin Brightwater comes to me in pieces: hands, voice, the smell of rosemary and woodsmoke, but never the whole. As if the mountain that took my payment took her face first, deliberately, so I couldn't even compare.

Or maybe — and this thought arrived around midday and refused to leave — maybe the reason I can't see her face is that I never could. Maybe the memory of her face was the first thing I spent, years ago, without ever speaking a word. Maybe the silence of Vael and the hole where my mother's face should be are the same hole.

I stopped walking.

Sera walked three more paces before noticing. She turned. Snow gathered on her shoulders like the mountain was trying to bury her gently.

"Theo?"

"When I was a child," I said, "in Greyfen. Did I have a mother?"

The question hung between us. I watched Sera understand it — really understand, all the way down to the ugly machinery — and I hated her a little for the pity that crossed her face before she mastered it.

"You had a father," she said carefully. "Daven Brightwater. A cooper. He raised you alone."

"Where was my mother?"

"She left. Or—" Sera's jaw worked. "Or she was taken. The records are unclear. You were very young."

"And I remember her." I held up my hands, watching them shake. "I remember her kitchen. Her voice. Her *lullaby*. But I can't remember her face, Sera, and I can't remember whether I ever could, and this morning I woke up with a scar on my hand from a fence I never climbed, and I am standing here trying to work out whether I loved a woman I can't picture or whether some dead woman's love for *me* is wearing my memories like a coat that fits too well."

Silence. Snow. Somewhere above us, a rockfall ticked down the mountainside like the world clearing its throat.

"What do you want me to say?" Sera asked finally, and there was no archivist in her voice anymore. Just a tired woman on a cold mountain.

"Tell me one thing that's mine," I said. "One memory. Something only Theo Brightwater would know. Prove I'm still in here."

Sera closed the distance between us. She took my face in her cold hands, which was either comfort or triage, possibly both.

"The Marlow's Ferry fever," she said. "You told me about it on the second night out of Greyfen, when you thought I was asleep. You were delirious for six days. Your father sat with you the whole time, reading aloud from a book he didn't understand because he couldn't read well — a book of old prayers, Vael's liturgy, stumbling over every line. He read it wrong for six days. And when you finally woke, you told him his version was better than the true one, and he cried, and you swore never to tell anyone because a mercenary doesn't trade in tears." Her thumbs brushed my cheekbones. "Do you remember it?"

And I did. Oh, I did. It rose up whole and warm and *mine*, smelling of lamp oil and bad wine and my father's sweat, and I laughed — a wet, cracked sound — and pressed my forehead against hers.

"He read 'Thou who walks behind the storm' as 'Thou who walks behind the store,'" I managed. "Like a grain merchant. Six days."

"A devout grain merchant."

"My father." The name anchored me. Daven Brightwater, cooper of Greyfen, who couldn't read but read anyway. Mine. That one was mine. I held onto it the way a drowning woman holds a plank.

But here's what I didn't say, what I carried up the mountain in the hours that followed, turning it over like a coin I suspected was shaved: *the lullaby about the river going home*. If my mother left or was taken when I was very young — if I barely knew her — then where had I gotten the lullaby? Where had I gotten the kitchen? Copper pots don't imprint themselves on a stranger's dreams.

Unless they weren't strangers' dreams. Unless they were mine, old and buried, and the mountain hadn't replaced them at all. Unless it had simply *excavated* them, dug down past the mercenary, past the smuggler, past thirty years of competent cynicism, and handed me back the child who'd once known those rooms.

I couldn't tell anymore. That was the horror of it. Not the losing — losing I could price, I'd priced worse things. The horror was that I could no longer tell which memories were excavated and which were installed. My own past had become a smuggler's hold: sealed crates, no manifest, and something moving inside the cargo.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, Sera's keystone case between us like a third traveler who neither ate nor slept. I built the fire small. Old habits. Sera bandaged her side and pretended it didn't matter, and I pretended to believe her, and between us we maintained the fiction that we were two women having a hard journey rather than two women running out of time.

I slept. Or something slept in me.

I dreamed of the blue door.

Not the kitchen this time — the door itself, paint peeling, and beyond it a hallway I knew the way I knew my own sword-grip. I walked it. Seventh step, the floorboard complained, and the sound of it went through me like a bell through water, and I thought: *I know this house. I lived in this house.*

Had I?

I opened a door and found a bedroom, and in the bedroom a child's cot, and in the cot a girl of perhaps three, black-haired, fists curled, breathing in the fast shallow rhythm of a fever. And beside the cot a woman knelt, and this time — for the first time — I saw her face.

Maerin Brightwater looked like me.

Not resembled. *Looked like.* Same jaw, same crooked nose from a break poorly set, same dark brows that made every expression an accusation. She was praying — not to Vael, I understood that immediately, the words were older and harder, syllables that bent the candleflames — and her hands were shaking, and she was saying *please* to something that wasn't answering, and the terror on her face was the twin of the terror I'd felt on the slope when the word of power tore out of me unbidden.

She was terrified *of herself*. I recognized it because I'd worn it yesterday.

"No," I said in the dream. "This isn't mine. This is yours. Give it back."

Her head turned. She saw me — saw me standing in her doorway, in her memory, wearing her daughter's body — and her expression did something terrible. Not recognition. *Relief.* Like I was rescue arriving.

"Theo," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like a debt being called in.

"I'm not doing this," I told her. "I'm not your vessel. I'm not your second chance. Whatever you left in the blood, whatever the covenant needs — I'm not the payment. I'm the courier. Find another—"

"You'll have to choose soon," she said, and her voice was calm now, horribly calm, the calm of a woman who has done arithmetic she doesn't share. "They'll tell you it's simple. Restoring what was broken. It isn't. Nothing about this is what they think it is. Ask Sera what happened at the—"

And the dream tore. Not faded — *tore*, like canvas in wind, and I came awake gasping in the dark of the overhang with my heart slamming and the fire burned down to red eyes and—

Sera was sitting up across the ashes, watching me. She'd been watching me for a while, I judged by the tension in her shoulders. In her lap, wrapped in cloth, the first keystone glowed with a faint amber pulse — slow, steady, wrong. Like a heartbeat keeping time with something far away.

"It's never done that," she said.

"Then it's doing it now." I sat up, scrubbed my face with my hands. My left hand. The scar was darker this morning. Deeper. As if the fence had bitten me again while I slept. "Sera. What happens at the—"

"At the what?"

I reached for it. Reached for the end of Maerin's sentence the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't there. Nothing. Just smooth blank wall where a memory should be, and behind the wall, faintly, the smell of rosemary and woodsmoke.

"That's twice," I said quietly. "Twice now she's started to tell me something, and something cut her off. First the garden, now this." I looked at the pulsing keystone. "Is that you? Are you censoring my dead mother?"

Sera didn't answer. Which was an answer.

"You know," I said. "You know what she was going to say. You've known since the wayshrine."

"Theo—"

"Don't." I stood. The cold bit through everything; I welcomed it. "I've spent today finding out that my head is a rented room with furniture I don't recognize. I've got a dead woman's scar on my living hand and her nightmares on my sleep and I cannot tell my father's voice from a stranger's lullaby. So you will forgive me, Sera, if I've lost my appetite for being handled. What happens at the *what*? Finish her sentence. Finish it or I swear by a god who hasn't spoken to me in nine years that I will walk into Ironhollow blind and let the chips fall on your conscience instead of mine."

Snow hissed on the coals. The keystone pulsed. Amber light washed Sera's face, and in it she looked suddenly older — or maybe that was just honesty arriving late.

"The siege of Cael Duren," she said at last. "Twenty-six years ago. Before you were born. Your mother was there. So was I." She drew a breath that seemed to cost her. "It's where the Breaking began. And it's where the Devourer first wore a god's face."

Above us, far up the mountain, something answered — a sound I felt in my teeth before my ears caught it. Not thunder. Thunder moves *past* you. This moved *through*, rolling down through stone and bone alike, deep and deliberate, like the mountain itself shifting its weight. Like something vast turning over beneath us in its sleep.

The keystone pulsed faster.

Sera stared at it, and then at the dark peak looming over us, and her face did something I'd never seen on it before.

Fear without a plan behind it.

"We need to reach the sanctuary," she said. "Now. Tonight. Before it wakes fully — because if that's what I think it is, Theo, then your mother's sentence was going to end differently than either of us wants to hear."

I looked down at my left hand, at the silvered scar of a fence I'd never climbed, and flexed my fingers one by one, taking inventory of what remained.

Ten fingers. For now.

One name — *Theo Brightwater* — held, for the moment, by habit alone.

"Then we walk," I said.
