## 14. Sanctum of the Quiet Order

The monks found us before we found them, which I suppose is the point of a hidden order.

We had been stumbling down the western face of the Thornfell range for the better part of two days — Theo half-carrying me because my leg had gone from "wounded" to "architecturally unsound" somewhere above the snowline — when the mountain simply stopped being a mountain and became a door. One moment there was scree and wind and the particular grey despair of high altitude; the next, a cleft in the rock that had not been there a heartbeat earlier, breathing out air that smelled of cedar smoke and old paper.

Theo's knife was out before I could say anything. She'd had it out most of the trip, honestly. It was less a weapon at this point than an emotional support dagger.

"This is a trap," she said.

"Almost certainly," I agreed. "But it's a trap with fireplaces, and I can no longer feel my toes. Weigh your options."

She weighed them. To her credit, she only took about four seconds to do it, which for Theo counted as reckless abandon. Then she put her shoulder under my arm again and we went into the dark together.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about mute monks: they are the loudest people you will ever meet.

They communicate in a language of hands and faces so expressive it made spoken conversation feel like reading tax ledgers by comparison. Within ten minutes of our arrival — after a silent brother had looked at my leg, looked at Theo, looked at my leg again with an expression of profound editorial disappointment — we were installed in a warm cell with a fire, a basin of water that smelled of some herb whose name I have forgotten and whose effect I will never forget, and a bowl of barley soup thick enough to stand a spoon in.

I ate like something recently exhumed. Theo ate like something recently exhumed who also suspected the gravedigger might want it back. She sat with her back to the wall, facing the door, the way she did everywhere, and watched the brothers move through the corridor outside with an expression I was learning to read as *calculating the exits.*

"They're not going to hurt us," I said.

"You said that about the toll bridge."

"The toll bridge didn't hurt us. It charged us. There's a difference. That's basically civilization."

"It ate your memory of your childhood home, Sera."

"And it gave change." This was true, though the change — a smooth river stone that hummed faintly when held, which I suspected was worth more than what I'd paid — sat in my pack feeling less like payment and more like an accusation. "Anyway. These people are the Quiet Order. They've been keeping covenant lore alive since before the Breaking. If Maerin trusted anyone with the truth, it would be them."

At the name — her mother's name, spoken aloud for perhaps the fifth time since Blood Is a Map and the argument that followed it — Theo went very still, the way she did. Like a deer hearing a hunter's voice in its own sleep.

"I didn't say she trusted them," I amended quickly. "I said *if.*"

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Soften it after you've said it. You do that. You drop the rock and then you try to catch it on the way down." She set the empty bowl aside. Her hands were steady; her voice wasn't, quite. "Just let the rock land."

I wanted to tell her that letting rocks land was the one skill I had spent thirty years perfecting, and look where it had gotten me: hunted across a mountain range, carrying a sealed case full of the end of the world, having paid a memory-toll to a dying godling and lied through my teeth about how much of the truth I still held back. Instead I said nothing, because she was right, and because a brother had appeared in the doorway with his hands already moving.

*You are asked,* he signed, and then, seeing our blank faces, he sighed — a whole biography compressed into one exhale — and drew in the ash at the hearth's edge with one finger. A circle. Inside it, three lines converging on a center point. He tapped the center, then pointed at me. Then at the case. Then he folded his hands and waited.

"The Abbot wants to see us," I translated. My mouth had gone dry. "Tonight."

Theo looked at the drawing in the ash for a long moment. "Three lines," she said. "Three keystones."

"Yes."

"And he wants to talk to *you.*"

"He wants to talk to the case," I said, and hated how easy the lie came, and hated more that it wasn't entirely a lie. The case mattered. Of course it mattered. It just wasn't all that mattered, and the part that wasn't was the part shaped exactly like Theo Brightwater, and I had been carrying that particular weight since before I ever met her.

* * *

The Abbot received us in a round chamber beneath the main hall, a room that had clearly been carved out of the mountain long before the monastery above it existed. No windows. A skylight shaft instead, open to the stars, with snow drifting down through it in slow spirals that never seemed to reach the floor. The walls were covered in script — thousands upon thousands of lines of it, in hands ranging from careful to frantic, climbing toward the ceiling in columns.

"Covenant-speech," I breathed. "It's a library of it."

The Abbot stood among it like a man standing in weather. He was old in the way mountains are old — not decrepit but worn, smoothed down to essentials. When he signed, his hands moved slowly enough for me to follow, and when they didn't, he wrote on a slate with a piece of chalk worn to the size of a thumbnail.

*Welcome to the last shelf of a burned library,* he wrote, and showed it to us with the ghost of a smile.

"You know why we've come," I said.

*I know why you think you've come.* He set the slate down and gestured, and a younger brother brought forward a low table, and on it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay objects I recognized from drawings I had studied until my eyes bled: fragments. Shards of pale stone shot through with veins of gold that moved, very slightly, when you weren't looking directly at them.

Keystone pieces. Not whole ones — splinters, slivers, the shavings left over when something enormous was broken into something portable. But even the shavings sang. I could feel them against my teeth.

"There are others like these?" I asked.

The Abtor looked at me for a long time. Then he wrote: *There were others like you.*

He turned the slate toward Theo.

I watched her read it. Watched her jaw tighten, watch her hand drift toward the knife sheathed at her hip and then deliberately away, which was, I was coming to understand, Theo's version of heroic restraint.

"What do you mean, 'like me'?" she asked.

He answered in signs first, and I translated because my hands remembered more than I'd admitted to anyone, including myself — an inheritance from my own years in Maerin's service, from the order's older sisterhoods, from a life I had been fleeing in a straight line for fifteen years only to find it waiting at the bottom of every road. *The gift does not appear at random,* he signed. *It runs in lines. In blood. The crown hunts speakers as if they caught the plague from each other. Fools. You cannot catch what you were built from.*

"Built," Theo repeated flatly.

He wrote then, chalk whispering: *Before the Breaking, the covenant needed anchors. Living ones. Families bred true for generations, the way you breed hounds for scent or horses for stamina. Most lines died out or went underground. Some were destroyed.* The chalk paused. *One line survived the purge better than most, because its founder was clever, and powerful, and made certain arrangements before she disappeared.*

"Maerin Brightwater," I said.

*Maerin Brightwater,* he agreed.

And then, because the Abbot was evidently a man who believed in twisting the knife with a steady hand, he turned to Theo and signed one more sentence, slowly, so I could render it faithfully:

*Which makes you not a fugitive with a dormant gift, child. It makes you the heir to the covenant itself.*

* * *

Theo took it the way I expected: badly, quietly, and with a great deal of walking.

They gave her the cloister walk to pace, and she paced it for the better part of an hour while I sat with the Abbot over maps and argued theology by slate. From where I sat I could see her through the arches, a dark shape moving between pillars, stopping sometimes to press her palm flat against the old script on the walls as if checking whether the words were warm.

I knew what she was doing. She was trying to feel like the same person who had walked out of Greyfen three weeks ago with a contract, a crew, and a god who didn't answer. And she couldn't, because she wasn't, and every hour the mountain peeled back another layer of who she thought she was and found someone else underneath — someone chosen before birth, someone designed, someone whose mother had apparently run a breeding program for destiny.

When she finally came back, she didn't sit. She stood over the table with its sad little shards and its maps, and she said, "Tell me the actual thing. Not the history. The thing you're not saying."

The Abbot met her eyes without flinching. I liked him enormously for that; most people flinched.

He wrote: *Your case holds the first keystone.*

"Yes," I said. Because denying it now would have been obscene.

*It is damaged. Broken when the covenant broke — everything connected to the covenant broke with it, that is rather the definition. It cannot be repaired here. It cannot be repaired anywhere except where it was made.*

"Where it was made," Theo said.

*Ironhollow.* He let the word sit. *The Hollow beneath it. The forge-shrine where the keystones were cut from the border-stone itself. Your mother worked there. What remains of the second keystone is fused into the altar where she worked. The crown has built a fortress on top of it — whether by accident or design, I leave to your judgment of crowns.*

I felt the color leave my face and did not bother pretending otherwise. "Under a fortress. Under *the* fortress. The garrison town. The one with the new walls and the—"

*With the Inquisition's regional seat, yes. Chancellor Ilvane's eastern command. I am told the wine cellar alone requires three keys.* He almost smiled. *The old orders hid well, child, but we hid by becoming small. Ironhollow hid by becoming useful. Nobody searches inside the thing everyone relies upon.*

Theo was staring at the map — at the small inked square that marked the fortress, at the warren of mining tunnels the cartographer had bothered to draw beneath it, because miners always kept their own maps and cartographers always stole them. I watched her trace the tunnels with one finger, down and down, past the deepest marked gallery, to where the ink simply stopped.

"She knew," Theo said quietly. "Maerin knew. That's why Sera has the keystone. That's why the case opens to my blood. She built the whole road and she just... died before she could walk it."

*Or chose to stop walking it,* the Abbot wrote, and then, catching whatever crossed Theo's face, added: *I offer no judgment on a woman I never met. Only the observation that those who break covenants rarely do it lightly. The Breaking was not an accident, whatever the histories sold in Greyfen claim.*

"No," I said. "It wasn't."

Both of them looked at me. Theo's gaze had an edge I hadn't earned yet and knew I deserved. I had given her pieces — served her mother before the Breaking, the keystones answer to Maerin's line — and withheld the rest, and the rest was getting heavier every day, and I could feel the hour approaching when the whole weight of it would come off my shoulders whether I lifted it or dropped it.

Not tonight. Tonight there was soup and a fire and a roof that had hidden from emperors. Tonight the rocks could hang in the air a little longer.

"We'll need a guide into the warrens," I said instead. "And supplies. And—" I gestured at my leg, which had opinions of its own and all of them negative. "Time."

The Abbot wrote: *You will have shelter and silence for as long as you need both. The mountain decides who finds us; the mountain has decided to keep you a few days.* Then, after a pause, chalk moving slower: *But I will tell you what I tell every speaker who passes through, and you may believe it or not.*

He turned the slate.

*The gift is not a sword. Every bearer who treats it as one dies holding it. It is a door, and doors open from both sides, and what you speak through it hears you speaking. Learn to be quiet inside yourself before you learn to be loud. Otherwise the next thing that answers your voice will not be Vael.*

Theo went white. I saw her lips form the name — *Vael* — soundlessly.

"How do you know about Vael?" she whispered. "I haven't told anyone here about Vael."

The Abbot set down his chalk. For the first time, his composure shifted — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has heard a song he learned in childhood coming from the mouth of a stranger.

He signed, and I translated, and my hands were not quite steady either:

*Because forty years ago, a woman with your face sat in that chair, with that same hunger and that same grief, and asked us the same question. And we taught her everything we knew. And she thanked us, and she left, and she broke the world.*

*So this time,* the Abbot wrote, *we will teach you differently.*

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which was standard issue lately, so I took a lamp to the script-covered corridors and read.

Covenant-lore, preserved in a thousand hands across a hundred years of silence: prayers that were really contracts. Names that were really hinges. Warnings, dozens of warnings, all circling the same idea — that speech was power and power was debt and the old gods had kept the ledger honest until somebody tore the ledger in half.

I found Theo in the third gallery, sitting on the floor with her back against the wall, her head tipped back, looking up at columns of dead language by lamplight.

"Can't sleep either?" I asked.

"Sleeping's when she talks." She said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "The echo. Maerin's echo. It waits until I'm too tired to hold the door shut and then it says things in her voice. Things I want to believe." She glanced at me. "You knew her. Tell me something true about her that isn't beautiful. Something ugly and ordinary. So I have ammunition."

I sat down next to her. The lamp between us threw our shadows huge against the ancient script.

"She bit her nails," I said. "A woman who broke reality in half, and she bit her nails down to the quick whenever she was thinking. She was terrible with money — brilliant with cosmology, bankrupt twice. She laughed at her own jokes before she finished telling them." I hesitated. "And she talked about you. Before you were born. All the time. About what she'd teach you, and what she'd never let anyone make you carry." My throat tightened around the rest, so I set it down gently, like something breakable: "She was wrong about that last part. But she meant it. That counts for something."

Theo didn't answer for a long while. The lamp flame leaned and steadied.

"Tomorrow we start learning," she finally said. "'Quiet inside myself.' Whatever that means." She laughed — short, unamused, real. "A mercenary taking lessons in silence from monks who never speak. Brannoc would laugh himself sick."

Something cold slid down my spine at the name. I let it go. There would be time enough for Brannoc, and Corvane, and every other wolf on our trail.

What I didn't say — what I carried up to bed and lay awake holding, like a coal in cupped hands — was what the Abbot had shown me alone after Theo left, unwrapping the oilcloth on the table one final time. Among the shards, half-buried, had been a fragment of parchment, old beyond guessing, sealed with a sigil I knew: Maerin's sigil, the closed eye and the open hand.

The Abbot had written beneath it: *This arrived with a pilgrim two years ago. We were told to give it to her daughter, when her daughter came. It concerns the third keystone. It names where it has been hidden all these years.*

*And, child — it is not where. It is who.*

Two floors below, in the dark, I could hear Theo turn over in her sleep, and murmur something, and go quiet.

Tomorrow I would give her the letter. Tomorrow the last rock would land.

Tonight I just lay there and listened to the mountain breathe, and wished — not for the first time, and not with any real hope — that some doors opened from neither side.
