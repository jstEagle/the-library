## 17. Descent to Ironhollow

The monk who led us to the mouth of the warrens was called Brother Fen, which was fitting, because he moved like water finding its way downhill — patient, inevitable, and utterly uninterested in obstacles. He hadn't spoken a word since we left the Sanctum. None of them did, of course. The Quiet Order had taken vows of silence so absolute that even their prayers were conducted in hand-signs, and I'd spent three days learning enough of their finger-speech to say *thank you*, *danger*, and *I need to piss*. A complete vocabulary, really, if you thought about it.

He stopped at a cleft in the mountainside two miles east of Ironhollow's fortress walls, where a curtain of dead bracken hid a seam of black rock, and made a sign I knew: *down*.

"Wonderful," I said. "I love going down. Down is my favorite direction."

Sera gave me the look she'd perfected over weeks on the road — the one that said *your humor is a coping mechanism and I am allowing it, barely*. "Theo."

"I'm serious. Up you get wind and rain and people shooting at you. Down is quiet."

Fen's hands flickered: *Down is not quiet here.*

I should have listened harder to that.

* * *

The entrance swallowed us the way a throat swallows — reluctantly, with a muscular certainty that suggested we would not be coming back up the same way. The smuggler roads through Thornfell had been narrow, but they'd been roads. This was something older and less forgiving: a mine-shaft cut into the mountain's root before the Breaking, when Ironhollow had been famous for iron instead of infamous for what the crown built on top of it.

We went down by lamplight, single file, Fen first, then me, then Sera with the case strapped to her back like a coffin for someone small. The keystone inside hummed against her spine. I could feel it too, faintly, through the soles of my boots — a vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. It had been getting louder for days as we climbed toward Ironhollow, and I'd told myself that was good. That meant we were close. Close was good.

Close was also, I was beginning to suspect, loud.

The shaft opened into galleries. Someone had worked these tunnels with care once — support beams carved with spirals, ventilation shafts angled to catch mountain wind, niches where lamps could sit without smoking. Now the beams were rotten, the niches held only bat-grease and old wax, and the air tasted of rust and deep stone. Water dripped somewhere ahead with the patience of geology.

Fen signed: *The crown sealed the lower mines twenty years ago. They think nothing lives down here.*

"What does live down here?" I asked.

His answer was a long pause, and then a sign I didn't know. He repeated it slowly, shaping each motion so I could copy it into memory: hands cupped, then opening outward, empty.

*Nothing,* I guessed.

He nodded. Then he pointed at my chest, and at the dark below, and made the sign again.

Nothing lived down here. And yet there was something down here. I decided not to pursue this line of reasoning, on the grounds that pursuing it would require courage, and I'd left most of mine somewhere above the snowline.

* * *

We walked for hours. I kept count by lamp-oil and by the ache in my knees, which is how mercenaries measure everything. Somewhere in the third hour the tunnels changed character — the miner's cuts giving way to passages that no pick had made, walls fused smooth as cooled glass, curving in ways that made my eyes slide off them. The covenant-work was unmistakable. My mother's era. Maybe my mother's hands, though I tried not to think about whose hands had shaped the world's bones, because every time I did, the borrowed memories stirred behind my eyes like fish rising to feed.

That was the other thing about the descent. The memories were louder down here.

Since the snowline, since the word I'd spoken that brought a mountainside down, my own past had been leaking out of me and hers leaking in. I woke some mornings knowing how to weave a warding-lattice and not remembering my tenth winter. I knew the smell of a laboratory in the old capital, tallow and ozone, and I could not for the life of me remember the name of the woman who'd taught me to gut a fish. I'd started writing things down — *my name is Theo Brightwater, my captain betrayed me, I killed a man at the ford and his face was ordinary* — and reading them aloud each night like a litany against erosion.

Sera had noticed. Of course she'd noticed; she noticed everything, which was one of the approximately four hundred things I liked about her and the primary thing that frightened me.

"Say your anchors," she said quietly, when we paused at a junction where three glass-smooth corridors met. It wasn't a question anymore. Somewhere between the Sanctum and here it had become ritual, the way soldiers have rituals, the way the devout do.

"My name is Theo Brightwater," I recited, keeping my voice low because sound behaved strangely in these passages — it came back wrong, delayed, like an echo thinking it over. "My god is Vael, who is silent. My mother is Maerin Brightwater, whom I never met, and whose memories are eating mine alive, and I am going to take both keystones and make that stop. Also I owe Sera approximately nine hundred crowns in back wages, which she will never collect, because I intend to survive this and spend it all on wine."

"That last part isn't an anchor."

"It's the strongest one I've got."

Something happened to her mouth that on anyone else I'd have called a smile. On Sera it was more of a controlled release of smile-adjacent material, rationed like lamp oil. But it reached her eyes, and I banked it alongside my anchors, where it would keep.

Fen signed: *Here.* He pointed down the middle corridor, where the darkness ahead had a quality I can only describe as *attentive*.

* * *

An hour later, the sound began to die.

It wasn't gradual. That's what I want to be clear about, because everyone who writes about such places makes it gradual — a muffling, a hush, a softening. No. We walked around a bend, and the dripping stopped. Not faded. Stopped, mid-drip, as though the water had been caught holding its breath.

Then my own footsteps stopped reaching my ears.

I felt them — the impact traveling up through boot and bone — but the sound went nowhere. I snapped my fingers beside my ear and got nothing. I said Sera's name and watched my lips move and heard the vast interior silence of my own skull, blood and breath and nothing else.

Sera had gone white. She grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise and her mouth formed words — *what is it, what's happening* — and I couldn't hear her either. Fen turned, saw our faces, and made the sign again, slow and deliberate: hands cupped, opening, empty.

*The Hollow,* I realized. We'd reached it. Or its skin, anyway — the wound beneath Ironhollow where the border between realms had been torn open and badly stitched shut, twenty years ago, by a woman whose face I wore and whose memories I carried and whose reasons I still didn't know.

Sound died near the wound. Of course it did. Sound was a thing that crossed borders — pressure becoming meaning becoming understanding — and here the border was broken. Here, meaning fell into the gap.

We stood in that silence for what felt like a year and was probably ninety seconds, three people mouthing at each other like fish in a failing aquarium, and I confess that every rational thought I owned packed its bags and left. There is a reason humans fear silence, I thought, distantly, absurdly. It isn't death we're afraid of. Death has noise. It's the absence of anything answering.

And then — faint, at the very floor of hearing, or below it, arriving through my bones rather than my ears — I felt the keystone hum.

Not the case. Not the stone. *Me.* The shard of covenant-gift in my blood, dormant my whole life until Thornfell woke it, resonating with whatever lay below like a tuning fork laid against a cathedral bell. And threaded through the hum, almost lost in it, something else. A rhythm. Slow. Enormous. Patient in the way that geological things are patient.

Like breathing.

Like something down in the wound, sleeping, drawing breath across a span we measured in years per inhale.

I put my hand flat against the glass-smooth wall. The stone was warm. Stone that deep shouldn't be warm. I took my hand back and did not mention any of this, because Sera was already signing frantically at Fen — *how much farther, is it safe* — and because some knowledge changes shape when you speak it, and I wanted to hold this one private a little longer, the way you hold a splinter you haven't shown the physician yet.

* * *

Fen led us along the Hollow's rim for another hour, through galleries where the silence thinned and thickened like fog, until we came to a place where the miners' work simply ended — a wall of raw rock with a single doorway cut through it, framed in iron that had gone black with age. Old iron. Covenant-iron. Someone had guarded this threshold once, deliberately, and the wards woven through the metal were so dense they made my teeth ache.

Fen stopped. He wouldn't go further — I saw that in the set of his shoulders before he signed it. His hands moved carefully: *Beyond is the under-gallery. The altar. What your mother left. I cannot enter. No one of the Order can. We are bound to silence, and that place takes all voices.*

*Ironic,* I signed back, using the small vocabulary I had, and then, because irony doesn't translate well into finger-speech, I added the sign for *thank you*, which was the truest thing I'd said all day.

He gripped my forearm, warrior-fashion, monk-fashion, both at once. Then he pressed something into my palm: a smooth river stone, plain, gray, unremarkable except that it was warm the way the Hollow-warmth was warm. He tapped his own chest, then mine, then closed my fingers over it.

*Anchor,* I understood. Not the mnemonic kind. The kind you hold onto.

Sera and I went through the iron door alone.

* * *

The under-gallery was a dome, and the dome was a lie.

That sounds mad, but it's the truth of what I saw: a chamber perhaps sixty feet across, ceiling lost in darkness above, and every surface of it carved — no, *written*. Walls, floor, arching overhead, covered in covenant-script so dense it looked like bark, like feathering, like frost on the inside of a window. My mother's handwriting, I realized, with a lurch. All of it. Thousands upon thousands of lines of it, spiraling inward toward the center of the floor, where a block of black stone rose waist-high with a socket in its top.

Empty. The socket was empty. But the shape of the emptiness was familiar, because I'd spent weeks carrying its twin.

"The second keystone was here," Sera whispered — and her voice worked again, barely, thin as paper. "Someone took it."

"No." I was already walking the spiral inward, tracing the script with my eyes, feeling the shard in my blood lean toward the walls like a plant toward light. "Look at the dust. Look at the socket edges. Nothing's been removed. It was taken *out* of the stone — dissolved, or called — and the altar left as a..." I searched for the word. "*A socket waiting.* It's meant to come back."

"Meant to come back," Sera repeated. "By whom?"

I crouched at the black altar and read the final ring of script, the innermost circle, the last thing my mother had written before she sealed this place. My lips moved through the old covenant-tongue, translating without quite deciding to, the way you fall asleep without deciding to:

*Let it be found only by my blood. Let it be found only when the finding cannot be undone. And let what waits in the socket know her voice from mine, so that she may finish what I could not.*

Below the words, smaller, almost an afterthought — and this was the part that stopped my heart, because I recognized the cadence before I recognized the letters, the way you recognize a laugh through a wall:

*If you are reading this, little one, then I am sorry, and I am proud, and those are the same sentence.*

The lamplight guttered. The enormous slow breathing beneath the floor hitched — just once, just slightly, the way a sleeper stirs when a familiar footstep crosses the room above.

And out of the empty socket, in a voice I had never heard and knew instantly, warmly, terribly — a voice that fit the shape of every borrowed memory like a key finally meeting its lock — my mother said my name.

Not Theo. The other name. The one I'd been given at birth, by her, and never told.
