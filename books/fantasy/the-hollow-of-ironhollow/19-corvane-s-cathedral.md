## 19. Corvane's Cathedral

I have consecrated forty-one buildings in my service to the Crown. Chapels, garrisons, a granary in Duncarrow that the local priest insisted had been haunted — it was not; it had rats and a bad foundation, which is the more common condition of both buildings and souls. Consecration is a simple liturgy. You name what the place is for. You ask the Quiet to witness. You strike the iron bell once, so that the silence afterward has something to measure itself against.

The fortress at Ironhollow took four hours.

Not because the rite was longer. Because the place fought me.

I stood on the central platform of the great shaft — the miners call it the Hollow, and they say it the way sailors say *the deep*, with a superstition they'd deny if you asked them directly — and I spoke the words of dedication over a machine. That is what we had built, whatever the Chancellor's writ called it: an engine. A cathedral of iron and counter-song, built downward instead of upward, its spire a wound in the earth. The old faiths built toward heaven. We were building against it.

"Name what the place is for," I murmured, and found I did not know how to finish the sentence honestly.

*It is for killing a plague,* I told myself. *The gift is a plague. I have seen what it does.*

I have a ledger. Inquisitors keep ledgers the way merchants keep accounts, except our currency is people. Forty-one entries before Ironhollow. Names, villages, the nature of the manifestation, the disposition of the case. Entry nine is my sister's. I will not tell you her name. It is the one thing in thirty years of service I have never written down where another hand could read it, and I am not going to surrender the habit now, even in my own head, even here, standing above a hole in the world that eats sound.

She heard a god when she was eleven. A small one — a river-godling, the kind that survives in culverts and mill-races after the great names have gone quiet. She heard it, and she answered it, and by fourteen she was talking to things that were not gods at all, because the gift does not check credentials at the door. By sixteen she was gone in every way that matters, sitting in our mother's kitchen answering questions no one had asked, in a voice that was not hers, while her body forgot how to eat. The covenant-speakers call that *opening*. The Church calls it what it is: a door left swinging in a house full of wolves.

So no. I do not doubt the work. Doubt is a luxury of people who have never watched someone they love be eaten from the inside by a voice.

But I will confess — since this account will likely be burned with me, and honesty costs nothing among ashes — that the engine troubled me from the first hour. Not its purpose. Its method.

* * *

The Chancellor's engineers had designed the resonance lattice before I ever arrived. I understood perhaps a third of the diagrams, and I say that as a compliment to the engineers and an indictment of myself. What I understood was this: the gift is a voice. Voices are vibration. Vibration can be inverted, damped, cancelled. Build a lattice large enough, tune it cruelly enough, and you could silence not just one speaker but the *capacity* — dampen the old frequencies out of a whole region, the way a heavy curtain deadens a room.

A clean solution. An elegant one. I approved it in writing, with the small satisfaction of a man signing off on a well-digging during a drought.

Then I learned what the lattice needed to tune itself.

"You can't calibrate against nothing," Engineer Vasq explained to me on the second day, in the tone of a patient man explaining rain to a child. She was Ironhollow-born, broad-handed, unimpressed by my rank. "Silence isn't a frequency, Inquisitor. Silence is absence. To cancel the gift, the lattice has to learn what the gift sounds like. Live samples. Resonant ones."

"Samples," I said.

"Speakers." She met my eyes without flinch or apology, which I have learned to respect in engineers and distrust in everyone else. "The proclamations have been generous. Your bounty criers have been very thorough. We have nineteen in holding below."

Nineteen. I signed the writs that put those criers on the road. I wrote the phrase *covenant-speech is a contagion of the soul* myself, at a desk, with good ink, and felt righteous doing it.

There is a particular quality to the sound the lattice makes when it tunes. I went down to hear it on the third day, because I believe an officer should hear what he authorizes. It does not scream. Screaming would be honest. It hums — a chord slightly wrong, the way a room is wrong when two people are speaking different languages at each other with increasing desperation. And underneath the hum, threaded through it like wire through wax, were voices. Nineteen voices, pulled thin, singing a note none of them had chosen.

Entry nine's face kept arriving in my mind uninvited, the way faces do. I sent it away. It came back. I sent it away again. This is called discipline, and I have been commended for it.

* * *

Brannoc arrived on the fifth day, in a wagon, in irons, and asked immediately whether the food was included in his sentence or sold separately.

They had taken him at Greyfen crossing, selling information — our route, our quarry, the archivist and the Brightwater woman — and then, when the coin ran out faster than his debts, selling other things. Routes. Safehouses. The names of smugglers who trusted him. A mercenary captain's final inventory, itemized by an Inquisitor's clerk. When I read the manifest I felt something adjacent to pity, which I drowned quickly, because pity for men like Brannoc is how inquisitors go soft and soft inquisitors get people eaten by voices.

He was not a resonant sample — no gift in him, the testers confirmed within an hour — so he went to the general cells rather than the lattice. He seemed almost disappointed. I think a man who has spent his life being useful finds uselessness more frightening than chains.

He talked, of course. They always talk; the only variable is how long they pretend they won't. But what he gave me was not routes this time. It was Theo Brightwater.

"The girl doesn't know what she is," he said, across the interrogation table, working his bound wrists against the armrests with the absent rhythm of a man who had been tied up before and expected to be again. "That's the joke of it. Maerin Brightwater's daughter, and nobody told her. Twenty-odd years carrying that blood and she thinks her god just got bored of her."

"Maerin Brightwater broke the covenant," I said. "Every schoolchild knows the story."

"Schoolchildren know stories." He grinned at me with teeth gone grey at the edges. "You want to know about the daughter? She pays tithes to an altar that's never answered. Years. Every market day, salt on the stone, like clockwork. You know what that is, Inquisitor? That's not faith. That's grief wearing work clothes." He leaned forward as far as the irons allowed. "And your criers are hunting her for the crime of mourning."

I ended the session there. Not because he was wrong. Because he was right in a direction I did not want to look, and I have survived thirty years by looking only where looking serves.

Salt on the stone. Market days. I knew that gesture. I had seen it in a hundred border towns — the small stubborn offerings to gods who had gone silent after the Breaking, kept up by people who could not afford hope and bought it anyway on credit. My mother had done it. So had mine, I mean. After my sister died, she left bread at a wayshrine of some hedge-saint for eleven years, until her hands couldn't carry it up the hill. Was that contagion? Was that a plague vector?

It was grief. Grief wearing work clothes. The phrase lodged in me like a splinter, and I could not grip it to pull it out.

* * *

On the seventh day, the lattice achieved full resonance, and the Chancellor came to observe, and I stood at her shoulder on the platform above the Hollow and watched the anti-covenant engine sing with nineteen stolen voices, and I told myself the word *necessary* until it stopped sounding like a word.

Chancellor Ilvane is not what I expected, which I note because I pride myself on expecting correctly. I had imagined the architect of the Purification Edicts as either a zealot or a bureaucrat — fire or ash. She is neither. She is *attentive*. She listens the way predators listen, with her whole stillness, and when she speaks it is always quietly, as though loudness were a vulgar superstition. She watched the lattice hum with the expression of a woman listening to an orchestra tune, and there was something in her face I could not file. Anticipation, perhaps. Or appetite. I told myself it was devotion. Devotion looks strange on everyone from the outside.

"Inquisitor Corvane." She did not turn her head. "Your reports are precise. Precision is rare. Most men confuse conviction with accuracy."

"I try to keep them separate, Chancellor."

"Mm. Tell me — when the lattice completes its calibration, and the gift is silenced across the Marches, what do you imagine happens to the speakers?"

"They're freed of it," I said. "The voices stop. They grieve, perhaps. Then they live."

"And the ones in the lattice?"

"Released, once their resonance is no longer required. Pardoned, if their cooperation warrants—"

"Yes," she said softly, still watching the shaft. "That is what I imagined you imagined."

There was nothing in the words to object to. That is the thing about Ilvane I would take three more weeks to understand: she never says anything objectionable. She only says true things in an order that makes them into something else, the way letters make poison or medicine depending on arrangement.

Below us, the nineteen voices held their wrong chord. One of them — the testers had numbered them, Sample Fourteen, a cooper's son from Lowmarsh, seventeen years old — wavered, cracked, steadied. I thought: *he is holding the note because he hopes compliance will end it.* I thought: *entry nine held notes too, near the end, for voices that promised her the same.*

I stood at attention beside the Chancellor and did my duty, which that day consisted of witnessing, and I have rarely felt so much like a witness in the criminal sense.

* * *

It was Sample Nine who broke me. Not Fourteen. Nine.

She was a laundress from Thornfell's southern skirt, fifty-one years old, widowed, tested positive for residual covenant-speech at a checkpoint because she had talked to her late husband's memory aloud at his grave and the tester's rod had warmed. That is the threshold now. Talking to your dead warms the rod. Half the widows in the Marches would warm the rod.

I conducted her intake interview personally, because the regional clerks were overwhelmed and because I still believed, in the seventh day's last hour, that procedure was a form of mercy. Procedure means someone asks your name. Procedure means a chair, a table, water. Procedure is the difference between a prisoner and a sample.

She sat with her hands folded, and she looked at me with the flat patience of the very tired, and she said, "You have your sister's way of sitting."

The cell went very cold around me, or I did.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"When you wait for an answer," she said, "you fold your hands and count. I can see you counting. She counted too. Little Wren, counting under her breath in the chapel at Millbrook, waiting for the river-god to answer her prayers." She tilted her head, and her voice shifted — not theatrically, not the carnival medium's quaver, but subtly, the way a room changes key when someone else enters it — and said, in a girl's bright impatient voice I had not heard in thirty years and would have known in the dark, in drowning, at the end of the world:

*"Corrie. Corrie, it's not wicked, it's just listening—"*

I was on my feet. I do not remember standing. My hands were flat on the table and the table was the only solid thing in the province.

"That is not her," I heard myself say, and my voice was steady, which I count as the finest professional achievement of my life. "My sister is dead. Whatever you are, you are not her, and this performance is a documented manipulation tactic of—"

"She wants to know if Mother kept leaving the bread," the laundress said, in her own worn voice again, gently, horribly gently. "Even after. She watches, sometimes, through whatever's left of her. She saw the bread on the hill. Eleven years. She wants you to know it wasn't wasted."

I called the guards. I signed the transfer order to the lattice myself — Sample Nine, full resonance priority — and my signature did not shake, and I have hated my own handwriting since.

In the corridor outside, alone, I leaned against the stone and performed the breathing exercise they teach novices for confronting manifestations, and halfway through it I realized I was not calming myself. I was counting. Under my breath. The way little Wren used to count, waiting for the river to speak.

Here is the flaw in my cathedral, the crack I have papered over with thirty years of procedure: I built my certainty on the belief that the gift takes people away. That it is a wolf at a door, a thief, a devouring. And in one interview, a laundress handed me evidence — false, forged, impossible, I insist on *impossible* — that the door swings both ways. That somewhere on the far side of the voices, something of the taken might remain. Might be *waiting*. Might have seen the bread on the hill.

If the gift can carry love across death, then it is not only a plague. And if it is not only a plague, then the lattice below me is not a cure. It is an amputation performed on a body that might, in some region I cannot examine, still be alive.

I do not believe this. I want to be clear, in whatever record survives me: I do not believe it. Belief is for chapels, and I have consecrated enough of them to know what belief costs when it is wrong.

But I did not sleep on the seventh night. And on the eighth morning, when the watch reported movement in the mining warrens beneath the fortress — two intruders, descending, one of them matching the description of the Brightwater woman — I gave the order to seal the upper levels and take them alive.

Alive. I told myself it was for interrogation value. Interrogation value. As if I had not already learned everything I feared from a laundress with folded hands.

As the order went down the chain, I stood at the rail above the Hollow and listened to the nineteen voices hold their borrowed chord, and for the first time in thirty years of service, Inquisitor Corvane prayed — silently, without ritual, without the bell — to a god whose silence he had spent his career enforcing.

I do not know if anything heard me.

But far below, down in the dark of the warrens, something was already coming up toward the sound.
