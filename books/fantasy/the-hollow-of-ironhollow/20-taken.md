## 20. Taken

The monk's name was Brother Ellery, and I should have known he was trouble the moment he apologized for existing.

The Quiet Order didn't apologize. They didn't do much of anything with their mouths, which was rather the point of them. Fifteen years of silence had taught the monks of the Sanctum to say everything with their hands and their eyes, and there was a strange luxury in being understood without having to perform understanding — a thing I hadn't realized I was starved for until Sera and I spent four days among them eating barley porridge and learning how to hold the gift like water in cupped palms instead of a live coal.

Ellery was the one brother permitted to speak, because someone had to negotiate with the outside world, and he did it the way a man apologizes for his dog. *Forgive me, forgive us, forgive the interruption.* He'd been the one to meet us at the mountain door, bowing too many times, and he'd been the one who brought us tea we didn't ask for, and he'd been the one hovering at the edge of every lesson Abbot Maren gave me, listening with an expression I'd mistaken for hunger to learn.

It wasn't hunger. It was arithmetic. He was counting our supplies, our wounds, our route south through the mining warrens beneath Ironhollow — counting the cost of us against whatever price he imagined surrender would buy.

I know this now. I knew none of it then. Then, all I knew was that for the first time since Greyfen, I had slept four nights in a row without dreaming of iron, and I had let myself believe that meant we were safe.

Sera saw it before I did. Of course she did. She'd spent her whole life reading rooms full of dangerous people; I'd spent mine reading exits. When Ellery brought us the maps of the warrens on our last morning — hand-drawn, lovingly annotated, *here the old lift shafts still hold, here the air goes bad past the third junction* — Sera took them with both hands and thanked him, and then caught my eye across the cell-like guest room and did that thing with her mouth that wasn't quite a smile.

*Later,* that look said. And later, when we were packing in the grey pre-dawn, she said it out loud, quiet as breath:

"He asked me yesterday what day we planned to leave."

"You told him?"

"I told him we hadn't decided." She cinched the strap on the case holding the first keystone, the one that hummed against my hip like a second heartbeat. "Theo. Nobody asks a question they already know the answer to unless they're checking whether you'll lie."

"He's a mute order's only voice," I said. "He talks to everyone. It's his whole job."

"Mm," said Sera, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer she intended to open at the worst possible moment.

We left anyway. That's the part I keep chewing on, down here in the dark where chewing is most of what there is to do. We had the map, we had the route, we had two keystones between us and a fortress full of stolen voices, and the alternative to trusting Brother Ellery was going over the mountains in winter with no guide at all. So we trusted him, the way you trust a bridge that creaks — not because you believe in it, but because the drop is worse.

Abbot Maren saw us to the door of the sanctum herself. She was a small woman made almost entirely of knuckles and certainty, and when she pressed her palm flat against my sternum — the monks' way of speaking, though this felt less like speech and more like taking a measurement — she held it there a long moment.

"Your anchor is strong," Ellery translated, softly. "But anchors are not armor. Remember: name what is yours. Only what is yours."

"I will," I said, and meant it, and have thought about it every hour since, mostly because I failed at it so comprehensively within the day.

She touched Sera too, and something passed between them that I didn't understand then — some recognition, some old debt — and Sera came away from it looking like a woman who'd been handed a letter she wasn't ready to open. She tucked it away behind her eyes with everything else she carried, and we went down into the mountain.

* * *

The warrens under Ironhollow were older than the crown fortress above them, older than the town, maybe older than the names for things. The miners of three generations had honeycombed the roots of the Thornfells and then abandoned them when the veins ran thin, and what remained was a cathedral of bad decisions: timber props furred with white mold, rusted rails vanishing into black water, and everywhere that particular silence of deep places, which isn't quiet so much as *attentive*, like the mountain is holding its breath to hear what you'll do next.

The keystone case hummed louder down there. I tried not to think about why.

"Third junction ahead," Sera murmured, tracing the map by the light of a shuttered lamp. "Ellery says the air turns past it. We go slow, breathe shallow, and we're under the fortress foundations by nightfall."

"And then?"

"And then we find the altar." She glanced at me. "You feel it, don't you. Pulling."

I did. The second keystone — fused into its ancient altar somewhere beneath the crown's new walls, according to the monks — called to the shard in my blood the way a struck tuning fork calls to its silent twin. It wasn't sound exactly. It was more like an itch behind my ribs, a word sitting on the tip of the tongue, and every hundred paces deeper we went, the itch sharpened.

"Like a hook," I said. "In the soft part."

"That's how covenant-speech works on everything," Sera said. "Words are hooks. The question is always who's holding the line." She said it lightly, but I caught the flicker underneath it — the same flicker from the sanctum door, the letter-not-ready-to-be-opened. There were things Sera knew about hooks and lines that she wasn't telling me. I'd stopped keeping score. That should probably have worried me more than it did.

We made the third junction as the lamp oil burned low. The air did go bad — a wet mineral stink, the taste of old metal — and beyond it the tunnels narrowed and branched like the inside of a lung. Somewhere far above, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, we could hear the fortress: boots, bells, the clang of the forge where they were building whatever Corvane was building. The sounds of a machine that ate people. We moved quieter.

Which is why I heard it. Or almost heard it. A rhythm in the boot-noise above that didn't match forge-work or patrol-cadence. A rhythm like men assembling.

"Sera."

"I hear it." She'd gone very still, lamp shuttered to a coin of light. "How long has that been—"

The lamps in the tunnel behind us lit all at once.

Not fire-lamps. White, hard, chemical light, the kind the Inquisition favored because it didn't gutter and couldn't be smothered, and it poured up the passage we'd come through like water finding its level, and in it stood six men in grey-and-iron, crossbows levelled, and behind them, wringing his hands, face grey as the stone —

Brother Ellery.

"Oh," I said. Just that. Oh. The smallest word in the language, carrying the largest weight. Six days of porridge and breathing exercises and a small woman pressing her palm to my heart, and it had come apart because one frightened man had done sums with our lives and found the total acceptable.

"Don't," Sera breathed, as my hand went to my knife. "Don't. There are too many and he'll have told them about the gift. They'll have wards. Theo — *name what is yours.*"

What was mine. Two keystones. One archivist. One knife. One gift I barely controlled, in a tunnel where using it might bring half the fortress down on our heads or bring the mountain down on ours.

"Inquisitor's compliments," called the officer at the front of the white light, a broad man with a voice trained for cells. "And an offer. The archivist walks to us, unharmed, and the sword-hand walks free. Those are the terms. The Inquisitor is a reasonable man. I've seen him let people live."

"Has he?" Sera said pleasantly. "Recently?"

The officer's jaw tightened. Behind him, Ellery wouldn't look at us. He was studying the floor with the devotion of a man praying to be anywhere else, and I found — this surprised me — that I couldn't hate him. Not cleanly. He looked like what he was: a man who'd convinced himself that giving two women to the fire was mercy, because the alternative was watching the fire take everyone. Good intentions are just kindling with better manners.

"The case stays with me," I said. "That's my term."

"The case comes," the officer said, "or neither of you does."

And Sera laughed.

It was a terrible, wonderful sound, that laugh — bright and sharp as glass in a place where nothing had laughed in centuries, and I heard the officer's men flinch, because laughter in the deep tunnels sounded wrong, sounded like a word being spoken, and these were men who'd been briefed on words.

"You want the case," Sera said, "and you want me, and you want her, and your master wants the gift in her blood, and Chancellor Ilvane wants—" she stopped herself, neatly, a door closing, "—wants what chancellors want. Everyone wants. And none of you has asked the only question that matters."

"Which is?" the officer said, wary now.

"What the case *is*."

She reached down, unhurried, and lifted the keystone free of its wrappings, and held it up into the white light.

I've seen men look at gods. I've seen men look at money. I had never seen men look at a piece of carved stone the way those six looked — the way drowning men look at a light below the surface instead of above it. The keystone drank the Inquisition's white flame and gave back something older, a color that doesn't have a name, and along the tunnel walls the silence *shifted*, attentive, leaning in.

"Put it down," the officer said, and his voice cracked on the last word.

"It answers to her blood," Sera said, nodding at me, and I swear to every god silent and otherwise, she was smiling. "Maerin Brightwater's line. You can't take it from her. You can only take *her*. So here's your real choice, captain: go back to your Inquisitor empty-handed and explain, or come and try."

They came and tried. Men usually do. Hope makes them brave and fear makes them fast and the combination kills more soldiers than any enemy ever has.

The first bolt went wide because Sera threw the lamp, not at the men but at the wall beside them, and in the strobing dark the white light and the keystone-light fought each other and nobody could see anything, including me. I got my knife out. Someone grabbed my arm. I put the knife into wherever it met least resistance and the grip went away, and then hands — many hands, gloved, professional — and I heard Sera shout my name, not *run*, mind you, not *go*, just my name, like she wanted it to be the last thing I heard clearly—

—and then the gift woke.

I didn't call it. That's the truth and I'll keep telling it until it's true enough to sleep on. It rose out of the shard like floodwater, and a word was simply *there*, filling my mouth, a word for *stone* and *remember-your-place*, and I spoke it — or it spoke itself through me — and the tunnel answered.

The roof came down between me and the white light. Not all of it. Just enough. A curtain of rock and dust and screaming, and through it, muffled, the officer shouting orders, and under that, worse, Sera's voice — no longer shouting my name, now saying something else, calm, almost gentle, words I couldn't make out, words that were not for me.

They were taking her. And she was letting them. And I had one heartbeat, maybe two, to decide whether to dig through a fresh-collapse toward six crossbows or run deeper into the dark with both keystones and no map and no guide and no idea whether the calm in her voice was courage or a bargain being struck over my head.

Name what is yours.

The case was mine. The shard was mine. The coward's road was mine, apparently, because I took it.

I ran. Into the fourth junction, the fifth, off Ellery's memorized map into the unmapped dark, while behind me the Inquisition's lights swung and shouted and grew distant, and Sera's voice faded to nothing, and the last thing I heard from her — I will carry this to whatever grave I get — was not a scream.

It was three knocks. Deliberate. Spaced. The way the monks knocked: *I am here. I am alive. Wait.*

Then the mountain swallowed even that, and it was just me, and the dust, and the humming case against my ribs, and forty miles of dead miners' mistakes between me and nowhere at all.

* * *

I don't know how long I walked before I let myself stop. Long enough for the dust to settle on my shoulders like ash. Long enough to drink the last of the water and not remember doing it. Long enough to have a conversation with myself that went roughly:

*You left her.*

*I had both keystones. If they'd taken me too, Maerin's line ends in a cell and Ilvane gets everything she built her machine for.*

*You left her.*

*She told me to go. Three knocks. Wait. That's not leave, that's—*

*You left her, and you used the gift without meaning to, and the roof you dropped may have buried her, and you are now lost in the dark with a stone that sings and a head full of your mother's memories wearing your scars, and you are talking to yourself in a dead mine, Theo Brightwater, and the worst part —*

The worst part was that she'd known. Standing in the white light with crossbows leveled, she'd smiled, because she'd already done the arithmetic I was still refusing to do: that I was the keystone-bearer, that I was the mission, that if the choice came down to one of us, it was never going to come down to her. She'd walked into their hands carrying that knowledge like a packed bag, and she'd made sure the last thing she gave me was permission.

I sat down against a prop beam that groaned but held, and I opened the case, because I needed — stupidly, humanly — to see it. The first keystone lay in its cloth like a sleeping animal, drinking the darkness, and when I put my bare hand on it, the itch behind my ribs flared into something almost like warmth, and a memory surfaced that was not mine:

A kitchen. Rain on a window. A woman with my hands humming a song with no words, kneading bread, glancing over her shoulder at a child — at *me*, at a child who was me — and saying, clear across twenty years and a broken border:

*"Whatever they tell you I did, little fish, count the loaves. Count who ate."*

I took my hand off the stone. My face was wet. I had no memory of starting.

"Right," I said aloud, to the dark, to the humming, to the attentive silence of the deep places. "Count the loaves."

Somewhere above me, through a hundred feet of stone, the fortress bells began to ring — not alarm-rhythm, not work-rhythm. Something new. Triumphant, almost. They had her, and they were pleased, and every bell-strike came down through the rock and into the keystone and out again through my bones, and I understood, sitting alone in the black with two shards of a broken covenant and one friend's worth of guilt, that the next move was not running south.

It was going down. Deeper than the map, deeper than the monks' knowledge, deeper than anything with a name — following the pull in my blood toward the second keystone and whatever waited with it, alone, in the dark, with no one left whose knock I could answer.

I got up. I shouldered the case. And I walked toward the sound of my own blood calling home.
