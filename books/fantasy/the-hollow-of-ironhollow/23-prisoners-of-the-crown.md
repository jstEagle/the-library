## 23. Prisoners of the Crown

They took my boots first. Then my spectacles, then the little knife I kept in my sleeve for cutting thread and, on one memorable occasion, a man's purse strings. By the time they'd finished cataloguing my possessions into an iron tray, Inquisitor Corvane's people had reduced me to a woman in a grey cell with bare feet and no way to see three feet past her own nose.

I want it noted that I did not cry. I want it noted because I came very close, and vanity is the last luxury a prisoner is permitted.

The cell was small, dry, and lit by a slit window set too high to reach, which meant I could measure the days only by the angle of light falling across the far wall. A pale stripe at dawn. A gold wedge at dusk. Between them, nothing but me and the sound of Ironhollow fortress breathing above my head — boots on stone, chains somewhere below, water moving through pipes that had once served the mines and now served something else entirely.

I had been in worse places. This is not comfort; it is simply arithmetic. The archive under Callowmere flooded twice a decade and smelled of drowned vellum, and I had slept there willingly for six years. The difference was that in the archive, the dark was empty.

Here, the dark hummed.

* * *

They came for me on what I judged to be the second day. Two guards, methodical, and behind them a thin clerk with ink-stained fingers who carried my interrogation ledger as though it were scripture. They sat me in a chair bolted to the floor — a detail I appreciated for its honesty — and asked their questions.

Name. Occupation. Where I had acquired the case. Who I served.

I answered none of them, and this is where I must confess to a certain professional satisfaction. Six years cataloguing forbidden covenant-texts teaches you a great deal about questions: how they're built, where their load-bearing walls are, how to make them collapse politely. When the clerk asked who I served, I said, "The Crown, presumably. You're holding me in its dungeon." When he asked my occupation, I said, "Archivist," which was true, and watched him write it down as though truth were a concession he'd won from me.

Truth is a currency. Spend it in small denominations and they never notice you're rich.

The third session, Corvane himself came down. I had built him up in my mind during the intervening hours — tall, black-robed, eyes like banked coals — and so I was almost disappointed when he turned out to be a broad, tired man with grey coming in early at his temples and the posture of someone whose back hurt more than his soul. He dismissed the clerk. He sat across from me without ceremony and studied me for a long moment.

"You're not a speaker," he said. It wasn't a question. "We tested you while you slept. Nothing. So why does Maerin Brightwater's archivist matter enough to carry her keystones?"

I said nothing. He nodded as if I'd answered.

"You think silence protects you." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I've read the old interrogations, woman. Silence protected no one in the Breaking either."

"Is that what they teach you?" I said, before I could stop myself. "That the Breaking was a lesson? That it happened *to* us?"

His jaw tightened. For a heartbeat there was something underneath the discipline — grief, maybe, or its fossil record — and then it was gone, filed away, locked. "You'll talk eventually. They always do." He stood, paused at the door. "Eat your meals. I'm not interested in you dying stupidly."

Which told me two things. First, that he wanted something from me beyond information. Second, that whatever he wanted, it ran through the cells below mine — through the speakers he'd collected like a miser collects coins.

I spent the rest of that day mapping the sounds.

* * *

Here is what six years among forbidden texts teaches you about fortresses: they are never as silent as they believe themselves to be. Stone carries. Pipes sing. Guards gossip in the cadence of men who are bored, and boredom is the loosest lock in any prison.

From my cell I could hear, faintly, through the floor and along the old mining vents, the sound of the place beneath the fortress. Not machinery exactly — Ironhollow's new engine, if engine it was, didn't clank or hiss the way engines should. It *sang*. Low, arrhythmic, a drone that rose and fell like breathing, and threaded through it, voices. Dozens of them. Faint as memory, but unmistakable to anyone who had spent her life listening to covenant-speech.

They weren't singing hymns. They were being sung *through*.

I pressed my palm against the floor and felt it — a tremor too deep for ears, rising through the rock from somewhere below, from the Hollow itself. And threaded through the tremor, a hunger. I had read descriptions of the Devourer in texts so old the ink had gone brown. Every one of them described the same sensation: not fear, not dread, but *appetite*, vast and patient, the feeling of being read by something that intended to finish you.

It was here. Under us. Closer than it had been since Maerin Brightwater broke the world to cage it.

And the crown's engine — the harvest of captured speakers, their gift siphoned voice by voice — was feeding it.

* * *

The man in the next cell was named Odo. I know this because he told me, loudly, at intervals, for most of a day, until someone came and gagged him. Before the gag, he managed to convey the essentials: he was a hearth-speaker from the river country, could coax fire to behave, had been taken with his whole village when the proclamations went out. There were others near him. A girl who spoke to birds. An old man who'd heard his dead wife in the wind and reported himself voluntarily, believing surrender would spare his grandchildren.

"They told him mercy," Odo said through the wall. "They told all of us the tests were harmless. Then they put rings on our heads and started drinking us."

I lay on my cot in the dark and listened to the drone beneath the floor, and I understood what I was hearing: a choir being milked. Each speaker a spout. Each voice drawn off drop by drop and poured through the wound in the world, into the mouth of the thing Maerin had starved for twenty years.

The crown hadn't built an anti-covenant engine. The crown had built a straw.

* * *

You cannot organize a rebellion through three feet of granite with your bare hands. Fortunately, I have never needed my hands. Words are my tools, and words go where bodies can't.

Odo carried. He was loud, garrulous, half-deaf from years beside forges, which meant he shouted everything anyway — perfect cover. I taught him the rhythm: knock patterns, simple at first, then layered. One knock for yes, two for no, three for *listen carefully*. Through him I reached the bird-girl, whose name was Pell, and who discovered that sparrows came to the high slit windows if you sang to them softly, and that sparrows do not care about curfews.

Within four days — I counted by the light-stripe — I had a census. Eleven speakers in the lower cells, plus myself. Four guards on rotation, changing watch at the change of the light-stripe, which meant a quarter hour of confusion twice a day. One clerk who came alone to feed the prisoners on sixth-days and was afraid of the dark corridor between the stairs and our doors. Fear is a door. You only need to find its handle.

I will not pretend it was a plan. It was a seed. But I have spent my life around seeds — texts, fragments, first lines of books that became revolutions centuries later — and I know what a seed feels like when it's viable. This one was warm.

The problem, as always, was the keystone. My case had gone into the fortress inventory, tagged and shelved somewhere above, and Theo —

Theo was out there. Or dead. I refused, methodically and with great discipline, to consider the second option, and I succeeded except between the light-stripe and sleep, when the refusal wore thin and I let myself feel the full weight of what I'd done. I had found her. I had chosen her. I had known her name before she gave it, known her mother's face before she showed me hers, and I had led her into Thornfell with a story shaped like a job offer. She had every right to hate me. I rehearsed the speech I'd give her when we were both free — *you deserved the truth from the first day, here is the truth, every piece of it* — and I rehearsed it because rehearsing was easier than admitting I might never get to say it.

Then, on the eighth day, the light-stripe came late and wrong.

* * *

The drone stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-breath, the way a room goes quiet when someone dies in it. I was off my cot before I knew I'd moved, palm flat against the stone, listening with everything I had.

In the silence that followed, I heard something worse than the drone. I heard the floor *relax*. The constant tension in the rock — the pressure of the Hollow straining against its seams, which I had felt since my first hour in these cells without knowing what it was — eased. Slackened. Like a fist unclenching.

For one wild moment I thought: *it's over, it's caged again, someone closed the wound.* Relief hit me so hard my knees nearly buckled.

Then I understood, and the relief curdled.

A caged animal strains. That strain was the pressure I'd felt. If the pressure was gone — if the Devourer had stopped straining — then either the cage had been made vastly stronger, or something had opened a seam wide enough that straining was no longer necessary.

Below me, faint and terrible, came a sound I had only read about in texts from the Age of Seams: the Hollow, exhaling. A slow, wet, vast movement of air through stone, like the fortress itself drawing breath.

And threaded through it, distant but clear, a voice speaking with many mouths at once — the harvested speakers, still being drunk, still pouring out — and under their stolen voices, something older saying:

*Soon.*

I sat down on the cold floor in my stockinged feet and laughed, quietly, the laugh of a woman who has just learned the exact size of the problem and finds it almost funny in its enormity. Above me, the fortress of the crown went about its business. Below me, the oldest enemy in the world tasted the air through a hole my own order's history had helped open.

Somewhere out in the mountains, Theo Brightwater had both keystones and no guide.

"Well," I said aloud, to the cell, to Odo through the wall, to whoever was listening — the mute monks, the dead, the silent god, anyone. "We'd better hurry."

Because I finally understood the shape of it, the whole terrible architecture: Ilvane's hunt, Corvane's engine, the harvest of every speaker in the realm. It was never persecution. It was provisioning. They weren't silencing the covenant.

They were feeding it to the thing that eats covenants.

I pressed my hand flat against the stone floor one more time, and this time I sent something down through the granite, deliberately, the way Maerin had taught her order to send things before the Breaking — a wordless pulse, defiance braided with warning, aimed at whatever remained of the cage's ancient seams:

*Hold. We are coming.*

Deep below, something vast shifted its weight, and waited.
