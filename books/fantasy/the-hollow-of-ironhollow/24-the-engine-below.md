## 24. The Engine Below

I had been an Inquisitor for nineteen years, and in that time I had learned that faith is mostly paperwork. Prayers get filed. Heresies get stamped. The divine, whatever it is, keeps excellent records and so must we.

So when Chancellor Ilvane summoned me below the fortress of Ironhollow to "witness the culmination of the great work," I dressed in my formal grays, sharpened my quill out of habit, and prepared myself for a ceremony.

What I got instead was a wound.

* * *

The descent takes the better part of an hour. We go down through the old mining warrens, past levels the crown sealed decades ago, down stairs cut by hands that predate the kingdom itself. Ilvane walks ahead of me with two of her gray-cloaked attendants, and I notice — because noticing is my profession — that she does not carry a lantern. Neither do they. The dark ahead of them seems to make way, the way a crowd makes way for a magistrate. Or the way water makes way for something with teeth.

"You'll forgive me, Chancellor," I say, keeping my voice level, "but you've never shown much interest in the lower warrens before. The engineers called them unstable."

"They were unstable." She says it without turning. "They are becoming something else. You'll see."

The air changes as we descend. I have interrogated enough gift-bearers to know what covenant-speech feels like when it's working — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like coins, the sense of being listened to by something patient. The air down here has that quality the way a flooded cellar has damp. It is soaked in it.

And then there is the silence.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I will have left after tonight. It is not the absence of sound. Our boots still strike stone; our breath still fogs. But beneath those sounds there is a hush so total that my own heartbeat sounds like an intrusion — like knocking on a door where someone is trying to sleep. Or pretending to.

"Here," Ilvane says.

* * *

The Hollow opens before us like a throat.

I had read the old survey reports, of course. A collapsed chamber at the root of the mountain, where the miners broke into a void that swallowed their lamps and, eventually, their courage. I expected rubble. I expected darkness.

What I see is a border.

That is the only word for it. The far side of the chamber is not wall and not air. It is a seam — a place where the world ends and something else begins, visible as a faint wrongness, like heat-haze over cold ground. And stitched across that seam, running in vast intersecting lines like the cables of a suspension bridge, are threads of pale light.

Keystone-work. Covenant-lattice. The thing my Order spent nineteen years teaching novices was myth.

And feeding into it — driving into it like nails driven into a crucifix — are the conduits.

There are nine of them. Great braided channels of copper and bone and something darker, running from the walls of the chamber into the lattice itself. Each one thrums. Each one carries, unmistakably, voices.

I know voices. I have spent my career listening to them: the frightened ones, the defiant ones, the ones who bargain and the ones who pray. What comes down those conduits is the sound of many people speaking at once — or being spoken through — and every thread of it is being pulled out of them and hammered into that seam.

"The captured speakers," I say. My voice sounds very small. "Brannoc. The ones from the cells."

"And others gathered over years," Ilvane says pleasantly. "You've been more useful than you know, Corvane. Your hunts kept the supply moving."

I turn to look at her properly for the first time since we began descending. She is watching the lattice with an expression I have seen on the faces of new mothers and old martyrs. Tenderness.

"Supply," I repeat.

"Come closer. You should see the output."

She gestures, and one of her attendants brings a viewing-glass — a long tube of black glass mounted on a brass stand, aimed at the seam. I look through it because I am, God forgive me, still an Inquisitor. Still a man who looks.

Through the glass, the lattice resolves into detail, and the detail is horror.

The threads are fraying. Where each conduit strikes the lattice, the light is burned brown, like flesh cauterized. And beyond the seam — pressed against it from the other side, the way frost presses against a window — are shapes.

I will not describe them fully. Not because they are indescribable, but because describing them would be a kind of invitation, and I have finally learned enough to stop inviting things. I will say only this: they are patient. They are numerous. And they are pressing, with the slow constant pressure of roots under a road, toward the thin places in the weave.

One of them finds a frayed thread while I watch. It pushes. The thread whitens, strains —

— and above us, faintly, through half a mile of mountain, I hear a bell crack.

Not fall silent. Crack. Somewhere in the fortress chapel, a bronze bell that has rung for matins for sixty years splits down its middle, and the sound that comes out is less a toll than a scream.

I step back from the glass. My hands, I notice distantly, are steady. Nineteen years of discipline will do that even when everything else is coming apart.

"Chancellor," I say. "Explain to me what I am looking at."

* * *

We walk along the gallery above the chamber, and she talks. That is the terrible thing — how freely she talks. As if she has been waiting years for an audience worth telling.

"The old covenant was a cage built into a bridge," she says. "Your Order remembers it as harmony. Gods and mortals, speaking across the border, isn't that the pretty story? But a border is a wall with good manners, Corvane. And the thing on the other side — the one your theologians don't name because naming it feeds it — was never part of the harmony. It was what the harmony was built to keep out."

"The Devourer," I say. The word tastes like ash. I have burned books containing it.

"A hunger wearing a god's face. It eats boundaries. Selves. Distinctions. Where it passes, there is no inside and no outside, no you and no me — only it, digesting." She says this the way a physician describes anatomy. "Maerin Brightwater broke the covenant deliberately. Split the keystones. Killed the easy path between realms so that the Devourer could never be reached. Every prayer since has gone unanswered because the road was destroyed on purpose. Your whole crusade, Inquisitor — your sister's death, all of it — happened because a woman chose quarantine over communion."

My sister. She says the word like she knows what it costs me, and I understand suddenly that she does know, that she has always known, that grief is simply another resource to her.

"So the hunt," I say slowly. "The proclamations. The bounty on covenant-speakers."

"Was never about suppressing the gift." She smiles, and it is the smile of a woman showing a child a finished embroidery. "It was about harvesting it. The gift is the only force that can work the border. The keystones need living voices to bind them — the old covenant used willing gods, and look where that got everyone. We use the gifted. Their speech, drawn off through the conduits, thins the lattice exactly where we choose. Nine apertures. Nine doors. When the last of the speakers is rendered — when Brannoc and the rest are spent — the seam opens wide enough, and long enough, for what waits to come through."

I stop walking.

Below us, the conduits pulse. Somewhere in that rhythm I can almost pick out individual voices — a woman's, high and worn; a man's, cracked and stubborn. Brannoc, maybe. The captain who sold his own people and then sold us, and who is now having his voice pulled out of him thread by thread to open a door for the end of all distinction.

"Why," I say. It is not a question. It barely qualifies as language. "Why would anyone — "

"Because dissolution is mercy."

She turns to me, and her eyes in the lattice-light are calm as deep water.

"You of all people should understand, Corvane. You've watched the faithful suffer. You held your sister while the gift burned through her — I've read your reports, the ones you thought you'd sanitized. She heard things. Things from outside. And it killed her, slowly, while the gods she loved stayed silent, because the silence was the only kindness left in the system." She steps closer. "The Devourer doesn't torture. It doesn't punish. It *completes*. No more borders between self and world. No more grief, because nothing is ever lost — everything is absorbed. No more prayers going unanswered, because there will be no more 'other' to pray to. Every hunger satisfied by becoming the meal. Tell me that isn't what your broken-hearted faithful have begged for their entire miserable history."

"It isn't mercy," I say. "It's erasure wearing mercy's clothes."

"Yes," she agrees cheerfully. "Most mercies are."

* * *

I need to be honest in this account, because honesty is the only penance available to me now, and I intend to spend it all.

For the space of perhaps ten heartbeats, standing on that gallery with the lattice burning below and Ilvane's voice pouring reason into my ears like warm oil — for ten heartbeats, I wanted it.

I wanted no more borders. I wanted the ache gone. I have carried my sister's death for twenty-two years the way a man carries a stone he cannot put down without bending, and I have bent so long I'd forgotten I was ever straight. Ilvane offered me a world where the stone dissolves. Where the distinction between the grieving brother and the dead girl stops mattering because all distinctions stop mattering.

Ten heartbeats. Then I looked through the glass again, at the shapes pressing against the seam, and I saw one of them clearly — just for a moment — and it had my sister's posture. The particular set of her shoulders. And I understood that whatever came through that door would wear her, the way a soldier wears a uniform taken off a corpse, and it would call me brother with her mouth, and there would be nothing inside the words.

That is not reunion. That is predation with a familiar face.

"No," I say.

Ilvane sighs, genuinely regretful, which is somehow worse than triumph.

"I had hoped you'd come to it on your own," she says. "You're the best instrument the crown ever gave me, Corvane. Methodical. Faithful. Faith is just hunger with discipline, you know. I only ever needed to aim yours." She turns back to the lattice. "But instruments that refuse recalibration get retired."

"Chancellor Ilvane." I use her full title because it may be the last time it means anything. "By the authority of the Inquisition — which, whatever else it is, exists to protect the faithful from exactly what is waiting under this mountain — I am placing you under arrest."

She laughs.

It is not a cruel laugh. That is the worst of it. It is the laugh of someone hearing a child explain how they'll catch the moon.

"With what, Corvane? Look around you. My guards took vows to me, not to the crown. The writ you carry is paper; the power in this room is older than paper. And besides—" she glances back at me, and her expression softens into something horribly like pity— "you've already seen too much. You know what happens to men who see too much. You've made it happen yourself, often enough."

Below us, the conduits surge. One of them gutters — the voice inside it faltering, thinning, going quiet in the way a candle goes quiet. Somewhere in the cells above, another speaker has been spent. I find I am praying without meaning to. Not to Vael. Vael is silent; the road to Vael was burned on purpose, and the woman who burned it did it to save us. I am praying to whatever is left — to the lattice itself, to Maerin Brightwater's desperate handiwork, to the idea that a wall can be a kindness.

Hold, I think. Hold, you beautiful broken thing. Hold until someone comes who can mend you.

Because here is what I know, standing in the engine room of the apocalypse with my authority dissolved and my faith in ashes: somewhere in the deep warrens below this chamber, there is a fugitive archivist and a woman named Brightwater. Maerin's daughter. The heir to the breaking and possibly the only person alive who can undo it.

I have hunted her for weeks. I have read her file until I could recite it. I believed — God help me, I *believed* — that I was the righteous one.

Ilvane's attendants begin to move toward me, unhurried, and I understand that the conversation is over.

I have one advantage, and it is the same advantage I have carried through nineteen years of interrogations: I am very hard to kill quickly, and I never fight fair.

I put out the nearest lamp with my sleeve, drive my elbow into the throat of the first attendant as he reaches me, and run — not up, toward the fortress and her guards, but down. Down into the dark below the gallery, into the tunnels that even Ilvane's people avoid, toward the warrens where a hunted woman is hiding with the pieces of a broken god's bridge.

Behind me, unhurried, unafraid, the Chancellor calls down into the darkness:

"Run to her, Corvane. It saves me the trouble of fetching you both."

The dark takes me. Below, the lattice groans like a ship in a storm, and through the cracking bell's dying echo I hear — very faintly, threaded through the conduits — a voice that might be Brannoc's, screaming a name.

Mine.
