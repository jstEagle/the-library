## 25. A Bargain with the Hunter

I came up out of the warrens the way a drowned woman comes up out of a lake — not climbing so much as being spat out, blinking and furious about it.

Three days in the dark. Three days of eating my own memories like a miser eating her seed corn, choosing which ones to burn for warmth. I'd lost the smell of my first camp on the Salt Road. I'd lost the name of the boy who taught me to throw knives, though my hand still remembered the motion, which felt like theft in reverse. And somewhere down there in the lightless throat of Ironhollow, I'd made myself a promise: that if I ever saw sunlight again, I would not waste it on caution.

So when I crested the ridge behind the fortress's western midden-slope and saw a man in grey-and-silver sitting alone on a fallen watchstone with his head in his hands, I didn't reach for a knife.

I reached for the keystone in my pack, and I walked toward him.

* * *

Inquisitor Corvane without his escort is a different animal than Corvane at the head of riders. At the head of riders he'd been a blade — all edge and intention. Sitting here, helmet off, gauntlets set beside him in a neat row like a soldier preparing his own funeral, he looked less like a blade and more like a whetstone someone had worn hollow.

He heard me coming. Of course he did. A man doesn't rise to Inquisitor by napping on ridgelines. But he didn't move for his weapon either, and that told me more than anything else could have.

"You," he said, without turning around. His voice was rough, as if it had been used recently for things voices shouldn't be used for. "The Brightwater woman."

"Theo," I said. "If we're going to be allies, you'll want to use my name properly."

That got him to turn. His face was the face of a man who had been shown something in the dark and hadn't yet decided whether to believe it was real. Grey eyes, red-rimmed. A fresh cut along his jaw, already scabbed — not from us. He'd been in a fight with someone else.

"Allies." He said the word the way a man tastes spoiled milk. "You've been in the tunnels three days. You have no idea what's happening above ground."

"Enlighten me. I've been busy arguing with ghosts."

Something flickered across his face — not quite sympathy, but its cousin, the one who shows up at funerals and doesn't know what to do with his hands. "Then perhaps you already understand why I'm sitting on a rock instead of hunting you."

I came around the watchstone slowly and sat on its far end, close enough to talk, far enough to lunge. Old mercenary arithmetic. He watched me do it and almost smiled, which I took as a professional courtesy between two people who were very tired and very dangerous.

"Il vane," I said.

His jaw tightened. "You've met her."

"I've met her work. The engine under your cathedral. The speakers you've been harvesting." I kept my voice flat, because if I let any heat into it, I'd start shouting, and shouting wastes breath I'd need later. "Your machine isn't fighting the covenant, Inquisitor. It's *feeding* through the wound. Every voice you feed it thins the border a little more. Chancellor Ilvane built your holy engine to be a door key, and she's been turning it one prisoner at a time."

"I know."

Two words. They landed between us like a dropped coin, still spinning.

I stared at him. "Say that again."

"I said I know." He picked up one gauntlet, turned it over, set it down again — the movements of a man who needed his hands busy or they'd start shaking. "Last night. The output readings from the Hollow chamber. I went to her with them. I thought — I have served the crown for twenty-two years, and I thought she would be horrified. That it was corruption in the ranks, some clerk selling salvation for coin." He laughed, short and awful. "She thanked me for confirming the harvest rates."

"And then?"

"Then she ordered my execution, and I killed four of her honor guard in a stairwell, which is how I came to acquire this." He touched the cut on his jaw. "And how I came to be sitting on a rock reconsidering everything I have believed since I was nineteen years old."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hating a man: it takes maintenance. Like a fire, like a grudge-stone, like Vael's altar back in Greyfen that I'd fed out of habit long after the habit meant anything. For weeks I'd carried Corvane around inside my skull — the criers, the dragged neighbor, Sera's blood on the flagstones of his cells, Brannoc's name in his ledgers. He'd been useful. Hatred is fuel; it burns dirty but it burns.

Sitting six feet away from him now, watching him fail to keep his hands steady, I found my hatred sputtering like a wet wick, and I resented that almost as much as I resented him.

"You should know," he said quietly, "that I am not asking forgiveness. I burned the chapel at Marrow's Ford with eleven people inside. Two of them were children. I did it because the doctrine said covenant-speakers were plague-carriers, and I believed the doctrine because believing it gave shape to my sister's death." He looked at me straight-on, and there was no performance in it, which somehow made it worse. "I am telling you this so that whatever bargain we strike, you make it knowing exactly what kind of man you're bargaining with. Not so you'll trust me. So you won't."

"That's the most honest thing a crown officer has ever said to me."

"It's the most honest thing I've ever said to anyone. It cost me nothing, since everyone who could hold it over me is dead or about to be." He stood, joints cracking, a big man discovering his own exhaustion. "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"The Hollow. The truth of it." His voice dropped. "Ilvane showed me numbers. Charts of resonance. I want to see what my sister died defending the world against — or what she died because someone pretended she did. Either way. I need to see it with my own eyes before I open those cells and put every man who follows me into treason."

* * *

I shouldn't have done it. Every instinct that had kept me alive through Thornfell and the flooded dark said: never show your enemy the door to your own house. But there was a second voice underneath, quieter, and it sounded uncomfortably like the feeling I'd carried since the Drowned Chapel — the sense that inheritance isn't just blood, it's obligation. Someone had handed me this wound in the world, and handing truth to the man holding the keys to forty prisoners seemed like the least wasteful use of it.

"There's an observation gallery," I said. "Old miners' shelf, above the Hollow itself. Your engineers repurposed part of it. I can get us in through the ventilation adit — the guards are dead or fled, thanks to your former chancellor's staffing decisions."

"They're not my former chancellor's decisions anymore. As of last night, Ilvane commands the fortress. Those who didn't swear to her locked themselves in the barracks and are waiting to die with dignity." He pulled his helmet on, and just like that, some of the blade came back into him. "Which gives us perhaps until dusk."

We went down together, hunter and heretic, and neither of us mentioned how strange that was, because naming it might have broken it.

* * *

The adit breathed cold air that smelled of iron and old water and something else underneath — something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a sealed room. I felt it before I heard it: the pull of the Hollow, that slow tidal hunger I'd spent three days marinating in. My borrowed memories stirred. Maerin's echo turned over in the back of my skull like a dog dreaming.

Corvane stopped at the threshold of the gallery and crossed himself with the old sign, the one from before the Breaking, the one the church pretends it invented.

"Gods' mercy," he whispered.

Below us, the Hollow opened like a wound in a body that had forgotten how to heal.

You'd think a hole in the world would look like darkness. It doesn't. It looks like absence wearing darkness as a costume — a place where the rock simply declines to continue, where sound goes to die, where the eye slides off and reports nothing because there is nothing to report. And threaded through that nothing, faint as veins in marble, ran lines of pale light: the harvested voices, the stolen gift of forty-odd speakers, being bled upward through copper and prayer-steel into Ilvane's engine. The border around the wound pulsed. Thin as wet paper. Things pressed against the other side of it. I couldn't see them. I could feel them the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder.

And beneath it all — so deep I felt it in my teeth rather than my ears — a rhythm. Slow. Patient. A heartbeat that had been beating since before my mother broke the world to cage it, and would go on beating long after, unless someone did something final about it.

"That's it," I said. "The Devourer. Or the cage around it. From up here they look the same, and that's rather the point."

Corvane gripped the gallery rail. His knuckles went white. "My sister," he said, and his voice had gone somewhere young. "Lira. She was nine when she started hearing things. Voices in the well. Songs under the floorboards. Our father called a priest; the priest called the proto-inquisition; they took her away to be 'cleansed.'" He swallowed. "She didn't come back. And the doctrine said she had been carrying a plague, and I built my life on top of her grave like a house on a bad foundation, and I never once asked what the voices in the well had actually been saying."

"Do you want to know?"

"Yes." No hesitation. Whatever else he was, Corvane was not a coward. "Even if it damns me."

I took a risk. I reached into my coat and drew out the second keystone — fused black stone, warm as a living thing, humming against my palm. I held it between us.

"Touch it. Just touch it. It answers blood, and mine is... complicated, but it can share. If you let it."

He looked at the stone for a long moment. Then at me. Then, with the expression of a man stepping off a cliff to prove cliffs aren't real, he laid two fingers against it.

The memory rose between us like steam off a summer road:

*A girl kneeling at a well in the dark, small hands flat on the stones, listening. Under the water, under the earth, a vast slow pressure — and threaded through it, tiny and terrified, her own voice saying hello, hello, is someone there? And the answer, not words, just presence, ancient and sad and gentle: yes, little one. We hear you. We have always heard you. Tell no one.*

Corvane made a sound I will not repeat here and probably couldn't if I tried. He tore his fingers off the stone and staggered back against the rail, and for a moment the Inquisitor of the Crown Covenant hung there like a puppet with cut strings, breathing in ragged pieces.

"She wasn't sick," he managed finally. "It was — they answered her. Something answered her, and it was kind—"

"The old gods were kind," I said. "Mostly. The thing below us is not one of them, but it wears their face sometimes, which is how it eats. And Ilvane thinks surrendering to it is transcendence." I put the keystone away. My hand shook slightly and I let him see it, because fair was fair. "Forty of your prisoners are down there feeding that thing through their throats. One of them is Brannoc."

"Your captain."

"My former captain. He sold our route to you, and then he sold himself to Ilvane, and I have not yet decided how I feel about any of it, so don't ask." I looked down at the pulsing wound. "Dusk, you said. Here's my offer, Inquisitor. Hear all of it before you decide."

* * *

I laid it out plainly, the way you'd lay out cards in a game where the stakes were the world.

"You open the cells. All of them. The speakers, the suspects, the ones awaiting trial — every soul Ilvane has been milking. We get them out of the fortress before her loyalists seal it. In exchange, I give you my oath — sworn on the new covenant I intend to forge, witnessed by a god who has been holding this cage shut with his own silence for years — that the Devourer stays bound. Not escaped. Not freed. Not bargained loose, not accidentally loosed, not loosed at all. Sealed. Forever, if forever can be managed, and if it can't, then for as long as there is a Brightwater left alive to hold the seam."

"You'd bind yourself to it," he said. "Permanently."

"I'd bind myself to it *willingly*, which is more than the old gods ever got, and apparently matters." I shrugged. "I'm told I'm the co-author now. Authors don't abandon their books."

He studied me the way he'd probably studied a hundred heretics across an interrogation table — but the questions had changed. I could see him reordering his whole architecture behind his eyes, tearing out load-bearing doctrines and wondering if the roof would hold.

"If I do this," he said slowly, "every inquisitor who follows me becomes a traitor. Some will refuse. Some will have to be fought. Men I trained, ate with, buried brothers alongside." His hand closed around the rail hard enough to creak. "And if you break your oath — if that thing gets loose because of you —"

"Then hunt me until the end of time. I'll even leave a trail. I'm considerate that way."

"This is not a joke."

"No," I agreed, and let all the weariness I owned come up into my voice. "It's the least funny thing I've ever said. Which is why I'm swearing it in front of you instead of just promising, the way liars promise."

He stepped away from the rail. Drew himself up. And then he did something I didn't expect: he knelt. Not to me — to the Hollow, to the wound, to the girl at the well whose voice had been answered kindly once and punished for it ever after. He spoke the old sign again, and then he said, in a clear voice that echoed off the dead stone:

"Inquisitor Corvane, servant of the crown covenant, swears witness: the speaker Theo Brightwater has sworn the Devourer shall remain caged, and I will spend my life seeing both halves of that oath kept. Mine against hers. Held or broken together."

"Held or broken together," I repeated, and felt something settle into my bones with an almost audible click — the keystone warming in my pack, the echo of Maerin stirring, and somewhere very far away and very tired, a silent god paying attention.

He stood. We regarded each other across six feet of scorched history, and neither of us offered a hand, because some alliances are too honest for handshakes.

"Don't mistake this for forgiveness," he said.

"Wouldn't dream of it. Don't mistake it for trust."

"Also wouldn't dream of it." He settled his helmet, and the blade was fully back now, pointed in a new direction. "The cell block is beneath the east bastion. There are twelve guards on Ilvane's oath, plus her honor captain, Vess, who fights like a nightmare with a grudge. We go in through the tithe corridor during shift change — quarter hour after the bells, when the corridors run empty."

"Bells," I said. "Right. Loud things. Haven't missed them."

For the first time, Corvane smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful sight, like watching winter decide to be funny. "You will learn to hate them again shortly. Come. And Brightwater—" He paused at the gallery mouth, looking back down at the pulsing wound one last time. "When this is done, whichever of us survives owes the other a drink and the rest of the story."

"I'll hold you to half of that."

"Which half?"

"The drink," I said, and followed him up into the dark toward the bells.
