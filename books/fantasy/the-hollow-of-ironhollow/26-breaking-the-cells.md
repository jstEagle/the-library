## 26. Breaking the Cells

*Corvane*

I have spent eleven years teaching men to break doors with prayer.

Not metaphorically — that is the first lesson of the Inquisition's field manual, though no one says it aloud anymore. *The door is a lie. The lock is a lie. The wall between you and the heretic is a lie.* You put your shoulder to it and you speak the Litany of Unveiling and you do not stop speaking until wood gives or your voice does. Discipline is just faith wearing armor. I built my career on that sentence. I believed it the way other men believe in their mothers.

So when I turned to my six inquisitors in the torchlit corridor beneath Ironhollow's eastern stair and said, "Brothers. The door we are breaking tonight is the crown's," none of them flinched. That frightened me more than anything else that night. They had been waiting for this order without knowing it. Faith, when it discovers it has been fed to something hungry, does not shatter. It turns around and looks for whoever was holding the bowl.

"Chancellor Ilvane has made the fortress into an altar," I told them. "The prisoners below are not prisoners. They are kindling." I let that word sit. Kindling. I had used it myself, once, in a report I now wished I could burn out of every archive in the realm. "We are going down. We are going to open the cells. And then we are going to answer for what we did not see."

Brother Halloway — young, pious, the one who still polished his sigil-ring like a boy polishing a medal — asked the question I deserved. "Inquisitor. If we were wrong, why were we wrong?"

Because grief makes excellent mortar, I almost said. Because I showed them my sister's face when I taught them what covenant-speech looked like, and they learned to hate it by learning to love me. Instead I said: "Because we stopped listening. That is the whole of it. Now listen well, because tonight we may die doing the thing we should have done years ago."

They followed me anyway. That is either the best or worst thing about inquisitors. Possibly both.

* * *

*Theo*

Here is what nobody tells you about breaking into a fortress with a wounded inquisitor at your side: it is much easier than breaking in without one, and it feels considerably worse.

Corvane walked us through the outer gate like he owned it, which, in the way of bureaucracies everywhere, he nearly did. Two guards saluted him. One of them looked at me — hood up, blood on my sleeve from the warrens, Sera's borrowed knife at my hip — and Corvane said, "Prisoner transfer," in a voice so flat and bored that the guard actually apologized to me for looking. I have been threatened, hunted, betrayed, and sold, but being apologized to by a man who would have burned me last week was somehow the strangest thing yet.

"The cells are four levels down," Corvane murmured as we passed into the stairwell. "Thirty-one gift-bearers, by the last tally. They'll be weak. The engine draws on them nightly."

"How nightly?"

"Midnight bell. It's—" He stopped walking. His hand found the wall. "It's like hearing my sister call for help through a locked door. Every night. For months." He straightened before I could decide whether to feel sorry for him. "Don't look at me like that, Brightwater. Pity is a debt I haven't agreed to owe you."

"Then consider it interest," I said. "You're accruing plenty."

Below us, faint through stone, came a sound I will hear until I die: singing. Not pretty singing. Thirty-odd voices, cracked and thin, holding a note together the way starving people hold bread — because letting go meant admitting there might never be more. Gift-bearers, singing to keep each other's names alive. The crown had taken their voices for its engine and they were spending what was left on each other.

Sera squeezed my arm once. We went down.

* * *

*Corvane*

The midnight bell rang while we stood outside the cell block door, and I learned what Theo Brightwater had already learned in the dark below us: what the engine did.

It began as a pulling. Not sound exactly — a subtraction of sound, a draft in the ears, as if the corridor inhaled. Then the screaming started behind the door, and I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left: it was not the screaming of pain. It was the screaming of *removal*. Voices being pulled out of throats like thread off a spool. I have heard heretics scream under questioning. This was worse, because they were not being punished. They were being *harvested*, and harvest implies the crop exists for the reaper's use, and I had signed the requisitions.

Halloway vomited against the wall. I let him. A man should be sick when sickness is honest.

"Inquisitor," said Brother Dane, steady as ever, hand on his mace. "Guards' post beyond the door. Six men. Crown oath."

"Crown oath is to the crown," I said. "That thing behind the door is not the crown. It is what the crown is being fed to." I set my shoulder to the door and spoke the Litany of Unveiling, and brothers, if any record of mine survives, let it record this: I have never spoken it better. The door broke on the fourth line. The six guards beyond turned, saw my writ, saw my face, and made the only brave choice available to men whose faith had been stolen — three surrendered, and three did not, and Dane and Halloway handled it fast and clean, and I did not watch, because I was already moving toward the screaming.

The cell block opened before me like a wound. Rows of iron-barred pens, and in them, people. Old men. A girl no older than fourteen with frost crawling on her fingertips where she gripped the bars. A woman whose eyes had gone entirely white, whispering to herself in a language the Inquisition had outlawed and I had helped make her forget. And in the last cell, largest, alone, chained to the wall with engine-taps sunk into his forearms like a man nailed to his own veins —

Brannoc.

He looked up at me with one eye. The other had gone somewhere else, some time ago. He smiled with the half of his mouth that still worked.

"There he is," he rasped. "The god-botherer himself. Come to gloat?"

"I've come to open the door," I said.

"Shame. Gloat would've been more traditional."

* * *

*Theo*

I will say this for Brannoc: he was the same bastard in chains as he'd been at the head of the company. When I reached his cell — Corvane's people working the locks, keys taken off a dead sergeant — he looked past me first, counting heads, checking exits, assessing. Twenty years of habit. Then he found me and something moved across his ruined face that I refused, right then, to name.

"Brightwater," he said. "You look terrible."

"You sold me to an inquisitor."

"I sold your *route*. There's a difference. Ask any merchant." He coughed, wet and long. The engine-taps in his arms had left gray tracks under his skin, like frost killing a tree from inside. "Besides. Look how it worked out. You got a pet inquisitor. I always said you'd go far."

"Brannoc—"

"Don't." The word came out with real force, and behind it, real fear — not of me, of the moment. "Whatever speech you're loading up, save it. I know what I am. I knew when I took Corvane's coin, and I knew when I didn't spend it, and I knew when I sat in this cell and thought, *well, this is fair, actually.*" His good eye held mine. "You don't get to forgive me quick and clean, girl. That's not how debts work. You carry it. That's the whole job."

The lock gave. Corvane's monks poured into the block, cutting taps free, carrying the ones who couldn't walk. The girl with the frost-hands came out of her cell and stood blinking in the torchlight like something newly born, and then she saw me — saw whatever it is that Maerin's daughter carries in her face — and she didn't spit, or kneel, or run. She just nodded, once, the way soldiers nod. Like she'd decided I was worth following until further notice.

I could have used ten minutes to feel something about that. I got none.

Because the bells started ringing wrong.

* * *

*Corvane*

Wrong bells are a language every soldier speaks. Three quick, one long, repeated: not alarm, not fire, not attack. *Seal.* I had heard it twice in my career. Both times, afterward, there had been inquiries, and both times the inquiries had concluded that the sealing had been regrettable but necessary, and both times the sealed places had stayed sealed, and everyone inside them had become a footnote.

"Ilvane knows," I said. Theo appeared at my elbow, dragging Brannoc's arm over her shoulders — the man weighed nothing anymore, which told me everything about what the engine had been drinking. "She's closing the fortress. Gates, portcullises, the stairwells to the surface."

"Can you stop it?"

"No. But I know which way she'll leave open longest." I surprised myself by knowing it. Eleven years in this fortress; you learn a building's habits like a wife's. "The east sally gate. It's counterweighted — takes ninety seconds to drop. She'll seal the interior doors first, cut us into rooms, then kill the sally last because she'll want to come down herself and watch."

"Then we have ninety seconds and a head start," Theo said, "and about thirty people who can barely stand."

"Yes."

"And the warrens below — the tunnels I came up through —"

"Flood channels. She can drown them with a lever." I met her eyes. "Brightwater. I need to say something, and then we need to run, and neither of us needs to enjoy it."

"Say it fast."

"What's in those cells is my fault. My litany, my lessons, my grief dressed up as doctrine. Whatever happens in the next hour, I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to spend it." I turned and shouted the order — *east sally, double file, wounded carried, move* — and thirty broken voices answered with the discipline I had drilled into inquisitors for a decade, and gods forgive me, it was the proudest sound I ever made.

* * *

*Theo*

Ninety seconds is longer than you think and shorter than you need.

We ran. That's the whole of it, stripped of glory: thirty-three people, half of them limping, running through torchlit corridors while iron doors boomed shut behind us, each boom closer than the last, each one a room we'd never leave. Corvane led. I'll grant him this — the man knew his fortress the way I knew smuggler roads, calling turns before they came, *left, archway, down twelve steps,* without slowing. Behind us, his inquisitors fought a rearguard action against crown guards who kept hesitating, kept looking at Corvane's white writ and wondering which side of history the paperwork was on. Hesitation kills. Theirs did.

Brannoc lasted longer than he should have. I want that recorded too, wherever such things get recorded. He weighed less than his own armor and he ran anyway, and when the third boom came too close — when the corridor ahead split and the wounded needed carrying two directions and we were eight bodies short — he pulled his arm off my shoulder and shoved me forward so hard I staggered.

"Gate's that way," he said. He'd picked up a fallen guard's halberd somewhere. He held it like he'd been born holding it, which, near enough, he had. "Somebody's got to make the booms slow down."

"Brannoc—"

"Brightwater." He grinned with the whole ruined half of his face. "You carry it. That's the whole job. Go be somebody's debt."

Then he turned around and planted himself in the narrow of the corridor like a man planting a flag, and I ran, because staying would have insulted him, and I have never in my life forgiven myself for how easily I ran.

I heard it behind me. All of it. Halberd-work has a sound — wood cracking, steel finding gaps, a man's breath going ragged in rhythm — and Brannoc's rhythm held for a long time. Longer than it should have. Long enough for twenty-six people to reach the sally gate while it was still yawning. He bought us the whole price and paid it in full, and the last thing I heard from him wasn't words. It was laughter. Short, ugly, delighted — the laugh of a man who had finally, at the very end, been on the correct side of a fight, even if only for a minute, even if nobody wrote it down.

The portcullis dropped behind us like a full stop.

* * *

*Corvane*

Outside, snow, moonlight, the mountain breathing. Twenty-six souls in the courtyard of the fortress that had been our temple, gasping like fish. Halloway knelt in the snow and prayed aloud, and for the first time in my presence, his prayer had no litany in it. Just words. Just a boy talking to the dark. I found I preferred it.

Dane counted heads and came to me grim. "Inquisitor. The inner gates are sealed behind us. All of them."

"I know."

"And the sound from below—" He stopped. Even he couldn't name it. From deep under Ironhollow came a vibration that was not quite sound, a note below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, and with it, above the fortress, the sky had begun to do something the sky should not do. The stars over the Hollow were smearing. Running. Like paint in rain.

"She's begun," I said. "Ilvane. She's feeding it everything we gave her." Everything *I* gave her — every speaker harvested, every voice pulled thread by thread, every year of my righteous hunting delivered to her altar as fuel. I had not built the engine. I had built the *supply chain.*

Theo stood beside me in the snow, watching the sky smear, and her face did something complicated and then settled into the simplest expression I have ever seen on her: decision.

"The warrens flood when she pulls the lever," she said. "And the Hollow opens whether we're ready or not. So we're going back in."

"Back in." Dane's voice cracked. "Through the sealed gates? Through the *flooded*—"

"Not through." She rolled her shoulders, and I watched her close her eyes and breathe the way the mute monks must have taught her, and when she opened them again there was something in them that made even me, veteran of a hundred interrogations, take half a step back. "Under. Through the water itself. The spirits in those tunnels owe me — I've paid them enough memories to buy a kingdom." Her jaw tightened. "And there's someone still alive in those cells below. Sera. And I am done losing people to buildings."

She looked at me. Waiting. An inquisitor and a heretic, standing in the snow between a sealed fortress and a tearing sky, and the choice between us was not really a choice at all and both of us knew it.

"Well, Inquisitor," she said. "Still listening?"

I unslung my mace.

"Lead on, Brightwater. And pray your water-spirits take collateral."

Behind us, the sky tore a little wider, and far below, the first water began to rise.
