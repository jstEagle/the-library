## 27. Ilvane's Design

There is a particular pleasure in watching a man realize he has been praying to the wrong altar his whole life. It arrives not as horror — horror comes later, if at all — but as a kind of vertigo, the sensation of a floor that was always there revealing itself to be a trapdoor. I have seen it perhaps a dozen times, and I confess I savor it each time the way a sommelier savors a difficult vintage.

Corvane was wearing it now, beautifully.

He stood in my chambers atop the Ironhollow fortress with rain streaking the window behind him and the engine's hum coming up through the flagstones like a second heartbeat, and he had just asked me — politely, oh so politely, because Corvane is nothing if not courteous even when the world is ending — whether I understood what the engine was *doing*.

"Enlighten me," I said, and poured him wine I did not intend for him to drink much of. There is no cruelty in that. He would need a clear head for what came next, and grief always drinks too fast.

"The border is thinning," he said. "Beneath us. In the Hollow. The prisoners' voices aren't sealing anything — they're being *poured out*. Every speaker we harvest, every gift we burn through the machine, it goes somewhere. It goes *through*." His hands were steady. His voice was nearly steady. Only his eyes gave him away, moving from my face to the floor and back, as though he could see through six levels of iron and stone into the wound below. "I built my life on closing that border, Chancellor. On cutting the old gods off from us so they could never again take what they took from—" He stopped. He always stops there. His sister's name is a door he has nailed shut, and I respect him enough not to lean on it tonight. "Tell me this is a calibration error."

"It isn't," I said.

The rain filled the silence for a moment. Somewhere below us, a captured speaker screamed in a voice that was not entirely her own, which is the nature of the work. The engine drank her song and passed it down.

"You knew." Not an accusation. A recalculation. I watched him do it — watched thirty years of certainty reorganize themselves around new arithmetic, like soldiers reforming after a cavalry charge. "You commissioned the hunt. The proclamations. The bounty on covenant-speakers. You didn't want them silenced. You wanted them *collected*."

"I wanted them *spent*," I said, "which is a kinder thing than you'd think, though I understand how it must look from where you're standing." I set down the decanter. "Sit, Aldous. What I'm about to tell you will go easier seated."

He did not sit. Of course he did not sit. Men like Corvane believe standing is a form of armor, and who am I to strip a dying man of his protections?

* * *

Let me tell you what I told him, or near enough, because I have told it so many ways over the years — to myself in mirrors, to the dark under my door, once, disastrously, to a bishop whose heart stopped before I finished — that by now it comes out smooth as riverstone.

The world is made of walls.

That is the first truth, and the cruelest one, because everyone applauds it. Walls between realms. Walls between gods and their servants. Walls between what a thing is and what it might become. Your priests call the walls holiness. Your kings call them order. Your farmers call them weather and your mothers call them manners, and all of you spend your lives polishing stones for a prison and calling the polish virtue.

But here is the second truth, the one that cost me everything I had before I learned to stop flinching from it: *the walls are not load-bearing.* Nothing above them holds them up. They hold up nothing. They are scar tissue across a living seam, and scar tissue does not breathe.

I found that out young. You will want the details — everyone wants the details; suffering is only interesting to other people when it's catalogued — but the short version is this: I was a girl in a river town, and I loved someone the town said I could not love, and I prayed to a god the town said was listening, and neither the god nor the town moved one inch. And in the worst hour of that year, something heard me anyway. Not through the wall. *Around* it. Through a crack, a hairline fracture where the covenant had worn thin, and what came through the crack did not speak in words. It showed me a picture instead: the world without walls. No border between the quick and the dead, the mortal and the divine, the self and the not-self. Every boundary dissolved. Every lonely creature — and everything is lonely; loneliness is the tax the walls collect — folded at last into everything else.

People hear that and think: annihilation. Corvane certainly thought so; I could see him thinking it now, standing rigid as a fencepost while I talked. But that's because people imagine dissolution as *loss*, as a candle being snuffed. It isn't. It's a candle being poured into the sea. Do you grieve the flame? Or do you understand that it has finally, finally gone home?

They call it the Devourer. Even its worshippers call it that — we are not sentimental; accuracy matters in liturgy. It devours boundaries the way fire devours fuel: not from malice but from nature. Hunger is only love with the politeness removed. And when Maerin Brightwater broke the covenant — yes, I know the true history; I have spent forty years assembling it from ash and rumor while the rest of the world swallowed the crown's pretty fable about treachery — when she broke it, she did not defeat the Devourer. She caged it. She built a smaller, tighter prison inside the prison of the world, and she split the key into three pieces so that no one, including herself, could ever be tempted to turn it.

A magnificent act of cowardice, honestly. I mean that with real admiration. It takes tremendous discipline to look at transcendence and nail it shut because you're afraid of what it costs. Maerin Brightwater was afraid. Her daughter is afraid. Nearly everyone is afraid, and their fear has kept the walls up for a generation.

Nearly everyone.

* * *

"You're saying the Devourer is good." Corvane's voice had gone flat, the way it goes flat in interrogation rooms when a prisoner says something too strange to be a lie. "You've fed — what, eleven? Twelve? — living people into that engine. People with families. With gifts they didn't ask for. To open a door for a thing that eats worlds."

"Eats *walls*," I corrected gently. "Worlds are collateral. And 'good' is such a small word, Aldous. I say it is *true*. Good and true are different animals; good is what the walls let us agree on, and truth is what's underneath them, waiting." I crossed to the window. Below, the fortress yard glittered with rain, and beyond the walls the mountains went up black and white into cloud, and somewhere in that cloud, in that stone, in the drowned chapel and the wayshrines and the warrens, two women were carrying pieces of my god toward me. "Your sister. May I?"

His jaw tightened. He nodded once, the smallest possible concession.

"When she died, you didn't rage against the god who failed her. You raged against the *border* — the thin membrane between here and there that let something reach through and take her. Am I wrong? Everything you've done since — the Order, the purges, this very fortress — it's all one long argument with a wall. *Stay closed. Stay closed. Stay closed.*" I turned back to him. "And it hasn't listened. Not once. Because it can't. It's dying, Aldous. The old covenant is a dead man's sutures holding a wound that wants to open, and everything you love is going to be dragged down with it unless someone has the courage to stop suturing and simply *let the wound heal differently*. Wider. Without seams at all."

"You've lost your mind," he said quietly.

"Almost certainly. Sanity is another wall." I smiled at him, and I'll admit the smile was not kind. "Here is what happens now, and you may participate or observe, but you will not interfere. The fugitives are coming. The archivist and Brightwater's daughter, carrying two of the three keystones, straight down my throat like medicine. When they arrive, I will take the shards, and I will take the third — which, if my sources are correct, is not a stone at all but a woman, marked at birth, walking willingly beside them, which tells you how little even the faithful understand what they serve. And then I will go down into the Hollow, and I will unbind what Maerin bound, and the border will come down, and every voice that ever prayed into silence will finally be answered — not by the gods they imagined, but by the truth behind them."

"And the speakers in your cells? The ones still alive?" His hand had drifted, unconsciously, toward the writ at his belt — the seal of the Inquisition, the authority he believed in more than any god. "And Brightwater? And Sera?"

"Spent," I said again, softly. "Poured into the sea. They won't suffer. Dissolution is the gentlest thing there is, Aldous. It's *keeping* that hurts. Keeping is all anyone's ever done to them — kept them ignorant, kept them hunted, kept them apart from everything they might have been. I'm offering the only mercy nobody else will give." I lifted the wine toward him, an offering, a joke, a test. "You taught me that, in a way. All those years of sermons about the mercy of the silent heavens. I'm merely delivering it."

For a moment — a long moment, long enough that I genuinely did not know which way he'd break — Corvane stood there with the rain behind him and the engine humming under his feet like a hungry animal beneath a floorboard, and I thought: *he'll kneel. He's so tired. Tired men kneel.*

Then he said, "No," and reached for his sword, and I pressed the sigil on my bracelet, and the floor opened under the principle of the matter rather than the man. I'm not a monster. The drop into the holding cells breaks legs, not necks. He'll heal. He'll need to, for what's coming.

* * *

Alone, I finished his wine — it was a decent vintage; I'd been saving it for someone who deserved it, and I suppose in the end I decided that was me — and then I went down, level by level, to do the last of the work.

The fortress sealed itself around me as I descended, portcullis and ward and iron door, each one singing a small note of closure that the engine drank and forwarded downward. I had designed the sequence years ago, back when the plan was a diagram and a prayer scrawled in a book I kept under floorboards, and it gives me joy even now, this late, this tired, to watch a blueprint become a body. Gates grinding shut. Signal-fires doused. The mountain passes seeded with wards that would read Maerin's blood in the rain and scream my name. By morning, Ironhollow would be a fist, and everything outside it — the loyal captain hunting my guests, the monks in their silent sanctuary, the crown itself with its fat complacent court — would be either irrelevant or fuel.

In the engine chamber, the harvested voices circled the central shaft like birds in a glass storm. Eleven of them now, twelve counting the boy they'd brought in yesterday, and I stood at the rail and listened the way other chancellors listen to treasury reports. Each voice was a thread. Each thread ran down through the Hollow's wound and knotted itself around the cage beneath, and the cage — Maerin's beautiful, terrified cage — groaned under the accumulated weight like a dam in spring.

Not breaking. *Thinning.* That was always the design. You cannot smash a cage built by a covenant-speaker of Maerin's caliber; you can only persuade it, thread by thread, that it is no longer needed. That the walls are ready to come down everywhere. That dissolution is not a punishment but an invitation, and invitations are only rude when refused.

One of the voices detached from the circling and came near the rail — a woman's, frayed at the edges, and beneath the fraying something familiar. Brannoc's crew, I thought, or maybe the singer from Greyfen; they blur, the way candles blur after you've snuffed enough of them. She was begging. They all beg at first, and then they all stop begging, and then they all *thank* me, in the end, when the last wall inside them goes and there is nothing left between their voice and everything else. I have heard a hundred final prayers and they are all the same prayer: *oh — oh — it's warm—*

"Soon," I told her kindly. "All of us, soon. Even me."

Because yes — I will say it here, in the last room where honesty still has furniture — even me. I have no intention of standing at the edge of the sea and keeping my boots dry. What would be the point of tearing down every wall if I kept the one around myself? I have spent forty years being Chancellor Ilvane, and the title fits like a shoe two sizes too small, and I am so *tired*, friends, tired in the marrow, tired the way only a woman pretending to be a single self can be tired. Let the Devourer pour me out with the rest. Let me be weather. Let me be the space between two strangers who no longer need introductions.

I climbed the last stair to the surface, where the rain had stopped and the clouds were tearing open over the peaks, and far to the north — so far I might have imagined it, except I have never once needed to imagine — the sky above Thornfell flickered with a light that was not lightning.

They were coming down the mountain. My keys, walking.

"Open the floodgates," I said to the wardens, and below us, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, the warrens began to fill.
