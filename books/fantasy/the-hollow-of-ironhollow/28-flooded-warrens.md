## 28. Flooded Warrens

The first thing I learned about drowning tunnels is that they don't announce themselves. They just get wet.

We were maybe an hour out of the cells when the sound started — a soft, greedy gurgle somewhere below us, like the mountain clearing its throat. Behind us, Ilvane's engineers had cracked open the old sluice gates, the ones the miners had built to drown a seam of fire-gas two hundred years ago and then wisely forgotten about. Now she was using them to flush us out like rats. Or drown us like rats. With Ilvane, I suspected, the distinction was academic.

"Water's rising," said Dree, one of the freed speakers, a wiry woman who'd spent four years in Corvane's cages listening to her own gift being siphoned through copper wire. She had a way of stating horrors the way other people commented on weather. "Fast."

"How fast?" I asked, already knowing I wouldn't like the answer.

She held up her hand, palm flat, and let it fall a handspan. "That fast. Then faster."

Behind her came the rest of what we'd managed to pull from the cells: Dree; a boy named Pell who couldn't have been more than sixteen and whose covenant-gift was apparently limited to making candles gutter, which he apologized for twice; an old man called Hesk who hadn't spoken aloud since his capture and communicated entirely by tapping rhythms on things; and Sera, carrying both keystones in their sealed case, walking with the particular straightness of someone who refuses to admit how badly hurt she is. The wound in her side had been bound in the cells with strips of her own shirt and my last clean bandage. She'd smiled at me when I tied it off, which should have warned me something was coming. Sera only smiled like that before doing something stupid and brave.

And me. Theo Brightwater, mercenary, smuggler, former servant of a silent god, current holder of a gift that ate memories as payment. Standing in a tunnel that was filling with water, holding a torch that was going to die long before the air did.

I've had worse Tuesdays. I couldn't tell you when, but I'm fairly sure I have.

* * *

The water found us at the second junction. It came around the corner of the lower passage like something alive — because down here, under Ironhollow, near enough to the Hollow itself, most things *were* something alive — black and quick and smelling of iron and deep stone. Pell made his candle gutters. Hesk tapped three sharp beats on the wall: *run.*

So we ran.

The warrens of Ironhollow are a maze built by generations of men who were paid to dig and not to think, which means every passage looks like every other passage and half of them go nowhere on purpose. I knew them from the smuggling days, sort of. Knew them the way you know a lover you left angrily — well enough to find trouble, not well enough to avoid it. We took the high gallery toward the old drainage adit, the one that daylights on the western face, and the water took the low road and won.

By the time we reached the gallery's mid-point it was at our ankles. By the quarter-mile marker it was at our knees and cold enough to make my teeth ache. And ahead, where the gallery dipped through the old collapse zone — the one dip I had genuinely forgotten, because memory is a currency and I'd been spending mine like a drunken lord — the passage simply went *under*. A throat of black water, thirty feet of it, no air pocket visible beyond.

"We can't swim that," Dree said. "Not with the current pulling back toward the gates. Not wounded."

"Not without light," Pell added, and his candle did its sad little stutter, as if agreeing.

Sera set the case down on a ledge above the waterline and looked at me. She didn't say *you'll figure something out.* She didn't say *do something.* She just looked at me the way she'd been looking at me since Greyfen, since before I knew why, with a faith I hadn't earned and couldn't return and was starting to be terrified of.

Because there was a way. There is always a way, when you carry covenant-speech, and every way costs.

The water-spirits of the deep warrens aren't gods. Vael is a god, or was, or is again — a god is vast, a god has a name older than the mountains. What lived in this water was smaller. A river-thing that had gotten lost underground centuries ago and stayed, the way some people stay in towns that forgot them, growing strange and thin and stubborn. I could feel it in the flood-water now, all around my legs: a cold attention, curious, hungry in the small way that lesser things are hungry. Hungry for warmth. For stories. For *weight*.

For memory.

"You're joking," Dree said, when I told them what I meant to do. "You're going to *bargain* with it."

"I'm going to bargain with it," I agreed.

"With *what?*" Her eyes went wide, and I watched her do the arithmetic — everyone who knew anything about the gift eventually did the arithmetic. "Theo. Your memories. You've already—"

"Dree." I kept my voice level, which took more effort than killing Corvane's guards had. "If we stay here, we drown, and then Ilvane opens the Hollow, and everything anyone remembers stops mattering forever. One more memory against the end of the world is a price I can pay."

"That's what someone says right before there's nothing left of them," Sera said quietly.

I turned to her. Water to our knees, torchlight guttering, a dead boy's godless candle, and I looked at this impossible archivist who had walked into my life with a sealed case and triple pay and a face full of secrets, and I wanted to tell her — well. Later. There was going to be a later, because I was going to buy one.

"Get everyone ready to move," I said. "When the water parts, it parts for maybe fifty breaths. Nobody thanks it, nobody lingers, everybody swims."

"And you?" Sera asked.

"I'll be having a conversation."

* * *

Covenant-speech doesn't work like talking. Talking is air shaped into meaning. Covenant-speech is meaning shaped into the world, and the world, down here, was mostly water.

I waded out into the middle of the flooded gallery, past my waist, and let myself feel the thing in the water — really feel it, the way the monks had taught me, breathing slow, naming what was mine so I didn't lose track of it. *My name is Theo. My scars are mine. The ache in my left knee is mine.* Anchor-points. Mnemonic stakes driven into the soil of myself.

Then I opened the door in my chest and spoke downward, into the cold.

*Little river that lost its sky,* I said. *I see you.*

The whole tunnel shivered. The water rose in gooseflesh around me.

*Nobody sees,* it answered, in a voice like pebbles rolling in a current. *Long dark. Long cold. You see. What will you give to be carried?*

*I give weight,* I said. *A true thing, held long. Take it and bear us through your body to the western adit, and part your waters while we pass.*

*Show me the thing first,* said the river-thing, which was fair. Even minor gods read contracts.

So I reached into myself and chose, and here is the ugly truth of choosing a memory to lose: you don't get to choose perfectly. You reach for a shelf and your hand shakes and you take what you can hold. I meant to offer something small. The taste of a specific stew in a border town whose name was already gone. Something cheap.

What I got hold of instead was my mother's kitchen.

Not Maerin Brightwater the archivist, the breaker of covenants, the infamous dead woman whose face got spat at in Ashfall Village. Just the kitchen. Afternoon light through a window that faced east. Flour on a wooden table. A tune hummed off-key by someone whose voice I could no longer hear, only the humming itself, the shape of the melody without the notes. The feeling of standing on a stool too short for me, watching hands work dough, knowing — the way children know things absolutely and wrongly — that this moment would last forever.

I held it out. The water went very still.

*Old,* the river-thing said, almost gently. *Warm. Heavy with love gone sour with missing. This is a rich payment, land-speaker.*

"Take it," I said aloud, because my throat had closed around the covenant-words, and my voice came out rough. "Take it and carry us."

*It will hurt,* it observed.

"They usually do."

It took it.

There's no good way to describe losing a memory. It isn't like forgetting where you put your keys. It's like a room in your house burning down while you watch from outside — you see it whole, and then you see it less, and then you see the space where it was, and then, worst of all, you stop noticing the space. The kitchen went. The flour went. The stool went. Last of all went the humming, and I grabbed for it, and missed, and what I was left with was a shape of grief with nothing inside it — mourning for something I could no longer name, which is the cruelest arithmetic the gift ever does.

The water parted.

It split down the middle of the gallery like a wound opening in reverse, walls of black water standing taller than a man on either side of a bare, dripping channel of stone, and the river-thing's attention wrapped around us like a cold current bearing us forward. *Swim,* it said, though there was nothing to swim through. *Walk. Run. Fifty breaths.*

We ran. Pell slipped once and Hesk hauled him up by the collar without breaking stride. Dree cursed with the fluency of a career soldier, which under the circumstances felt almost holy. Sera carried the case, and I saw her stumble on the wet stone and catch herself and keep the case level above the water even as she went down to one knee, and I understood, in that moment, that if it came to a choice between the keystones and her own breath, I did not know which way she'd choose, and I hated her a little for it, and loved her — 

Well. Later. I keep saying later. The tunnels teach you patience the hard way.

Forty-eight breaths after the water parted, we came up the slope of the western adit with the black flood closing behind us like a mouth swallowing. The river-thing brushed past my mind one last time on its way back down into the dark.

*Sweet payment,* it murmured. *Come again when you have more warm things to burn.*

"Gods," Dree panted, bent double. "Gods. Did it work? Are we through?"

"We're through," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. Somewhere inside, a room was burned down to its foundations, and I was already forgetting to notice the empty space, and that was the part that frightened me — not the loss, but how quickly the loss became furniture.

Sera caught my arm. "What did it take?"

"A kitchen," I said. "It doesn't matter."

"Theo—"

"It doesn't *matter,* Sera." Sharper than I intended. The echo of it went down the adit and came back sounding like someone else. I softened. "We're alive. That matters. Walk."

* * *

The adit daylit onto a shelf of rock halfway down the western face of Ironhollow, and for one merciful hour we were outside, under actual sky, grey and snowing but *sky*, and I let myself believe — stupidly, comfortably — that the worst was behind us.

Then Pell, who had climbed the rubble at the adit's mouth to look back at the fortress, made a sound like a kicked dog.

"Smoke," he said. "From the mountain. From *inside* the mountain."

I climbed up beside him. Below and behind us, Ironhollow fortress wore a crown of steam, and the steam was rising from every vent and shaft and crack in the upper slopes, and beneath it, faint through the stone, came a vibration I felt in my teeth before I heard it with my ears. A hum. Deep and rhythmic and wrong, like a hymn sung by something with too many throats.

The engine. Corvane's engine, Ilvane's engine — fed with stolen voices, with harvested speakers, with Brannoc, gods help him, Brannoc — and it wasn't winding down after the escape. It was winding *up*.

"She's not trying to catch us anymore," Dree said, coming up beside me. Her face had gone the color of old ash. "Is she. She's trying to finish it."

Ilvane was flooding the warrens to clear her path to the Hollow. We weren't fugitives anymore. We were just early.

"Down," I said. "All of you. There's a service stair off the third gallery that runs under the old smelter works — it comes out above the antechamber, above the Hollow itself. If she's opening the wound, that's where she'll be, and that's where we have to be first."

"And if we're not first?" Pell asked. Sixteen years old, candle-guttering gift, asking the question the rest of us were too proud to ask.

"Then we're late," I said, "and we fight anyway. Move."

* * *

We went back under, and the mountain had changed its mind about us.

The water was everywhere now. Not the patient flood of before but a living thing, chasing us down the service stairs, filling the galleries behind us, and the river-thing was in it still — I felt its cold attention flicker past like a fish in murky water — but stretched thin, pulled apart across a hundred passages, no longer able to part itself for love or payment. The bargains were over. From here, the water took what it wanted.

Hesk went at the fourth landing.

I never saw exactly what happened. One moment he was tapping his steady rhythm on the rail — *onward, onward* — and the next a surge came sideways out of a flooded crosscut with a force no water should have, and took him off the stairs and into the dark, and the tapping stopped, and Dree screamed his name into the roar until her voice broke, and there was nothing any of us could do, nothing, and I have carried a lot of corpses in my life but Hesk's silence weighs more than all of them together, because he died wordless in a place where sound itself was dying, and I don't even know what rhythm he was tapping at the end.

Dree stopped speaking after that. Not mute like the monks — just done. She walked, and fought the water when it clawed at us, and kept Pell close, and said nothing at all.

The third gallery. The smelter works. The service stair, half-collapsed, ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then waist-deep, and the Hollow's deadness pressing in around us like wool packed in the ears — that absolute, devouring quiet where your own heartbeat sounds like an intruder. We waded. We climbed. We lost the torches and went on by touch and by Pell's guttering candle, which in that darkness was less a light than a rumor of one.

And then Sera fell.

No — she didn't fall. I want to write *fell* because it's cleaner, but the truth is she *let go*. The current hit us at the final bend, a wall of it bursting from the flooded shaft below, and the case strap — the strap she'd mended herself in Corvane's cells with thread pulled from her own cuff — tore, and the keystones went into the black water, and Sera went in after them.

"SERA!"

I heard her hit the water. I heard nothing else, because the Hollow eats sound, and I plunged in after her into a darkness so total that my own body was a theory. The water was freezing and pulling, and somewhere in it was a girl who had lied to me and used me and bled for me and carried a dead woman's burden across half a kingdom, and I reached into that blackness with my hands and my gift and everything I had left, and I *spoke* —

Not words. There was no breath left for words. I spoke the way the monks taught me at the deepest level, meaning without air, covenant without voice: *Give her back. Give her BACK. She is MINE — not yours, not the dark's, not the water's — MINE —*

And the water, which had been taking all night, hesitated.

Maybe it was the river-thing, remembering me. Maybe it was the shards of my mother's gift finally waking all the way in my blood. Maybe it was nothing but luck, which I've never trusted but have often used. Whatever it was, the current loosened its grip like a fist unclenching, and my hand closed on hair, on shoulder, on fabric, and I hauled, and Sera came up gasping against me with the case locked in both arms, both keystones clutched to her chest like a child holds a doll in a storm.

She was coughing water and blood. Her lips were blue. And she was laughing — ragged, half-drowned laughter shaking her whole body.

"Got them," she wheezed. "Got — both —"

"You *idiot.*" I was shaking. I was crying, I realized distantly, in water, where it made no difference. "You stupid, brave, impossible — I thought —"

"You thought what?" she asked, and her hand found my face in the dark, wet fingers on my jaw, and her voice had gone quiet underneath the coughing, quiet and clear as a bell rung once.

I thought I'd lost the last person who knows what my kitchen looked like, I didn't say. I thought the mountain would take everything and leave me nothing to rebuild from. I thought I loved you and I ran out of time to say it, the way I run out of time for everything, spending myself down to the last coin.

"I love you," I said. Because the dark was total and the water was rising and the world might end before dawn, and if I'd learned one thing from my silent god and my broken mother and a river-thing that hoards warm memories in the cold, it was that you don't keep the precious things. You spend them. That's what they're for. "In case the toll takes everything else. In case tomorrow I don't know your name. I love you, Sera. Put it somewhere safe."

Her forehead came to rest against mine, in the black, in the cold, in the last gallery before the Hollow.

"Safe," she repeated, and laughed again, softer. "Theo. I've been keeping you safe since Greyfen. You're the safest thing I own."

Above us, through a hundred feet of stone, the wrong-hymn hum swelled, and the water began to rise again.

We had one staircase left, and it led down.
