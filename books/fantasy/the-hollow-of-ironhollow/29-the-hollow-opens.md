## 29. The Hollow Opens

### Chancellor Ilvane

They tell you, when you are a girl in the provinces, that the world has edges. That the mountains end and the sky begins and between those two facts there is a border you can trust the way you trust your own skin.

I was eight when I learned the border is a wound that someone dressed as a wall.

I have spent forty years pulling at the bandage.

Now I stand at the lip of the Hollow of Ironhollow with two hundred harvested voices humming in the engine behind me, and I will confess something no chronicler will ever print, because no chronicler will survive to print it: my hands are shaking. Not from fear. From the particular trembling of a woman who has carried a heavy thing up a long stair and finally feels its weight about to leave her.

The Hollow does not look like much. That is the first deception. A bowl of black glass perhaps forty paces across, ringed by pillars of fused stone that predate the crown, predate the fortress above us, predate every name we give things. The air inside it does not move. Sound goes in and does not come out. When I spoke my own name over the rim this morning, as a test, I heard nothing — not even my own breath. It was like shouting into a mouth.

Below that black glass is the Devourer. Below that is everything it ate. And below *that*, stitched through all of it like thread through cloth, is the broken covenant — Maerin Brightwater's great work, the seam she died holding, the lie the whole realm is built on.

*You were right,* I say to her memory, because the dead deserve honesty from those who undo them. *It was a prison. You built it well.*

*But you built it alone,* I add, *and that was your error, and tonight I correct it.*

I signal to Warden Kesst, and the engine begins to sing.

It is not a machine in any sense the engineers would recognize. It is people. Forty-one gift-bearers taken from their homes and cells and roads, wired into resonance by methods I refined over nineteen years of patient, unforgivable work. Their voices — their covenant-voices, the ones they used to speak with gods and stones and rivers — are drawn out of them like silk from a cocoon, and woven together, and aimed downward through the Hollow's throat like a drill made of language.

The first voice is a boy's. Fifteen years old, taken off a toll road in Vessany. He speaks to water; did, before we took it from him. His voice goes down into the dark and comes back changed, and where it touches the black glass, the glass *opens* — a hairline seam, glowing the color of an old bruise.

The second voice is an old woman's. She spoke to hearth-fires. Her voice finds the seam and widens it.

One by one, the voices descend. One by one, the seam becomes a crack becomes a wound reopening after thirty years of false healing. And rising up out of that wound, thin as smoke but unmistakable, comes a sound.

Not a roar. Nothing so honest. It is a *pulling* — a sound like the world inhaling through its teeth, like a tide going out that intends never to come back. Every person on the rim takes a step backward without deciding to. Kesst vomits over the edge of his own dignity. I do not step back. This is what I came for. This is the only true thing left in a realm of borrowed time.

"Chancellor." Kesst, gray-faced, hand pressed to his ear where the resonance bleeds through even the wax plugs. "The readings — the output is exceeding projection. If it continues—"

"It will continue," I say.

"If it reaches the fortress levels—"

"Then the fortress levels will learn what they were built on top of."

He looks at me the way men have looked at me for twenty years: as though I am a door they expected to find a wall. Let him. They all think I am destroying something. None of them understand that I am the only one here performing surgery. Everything else — the hunt, the proclamations, the bounty on covenant-speakers — everything was triage. Find the speakers before the silence eats them raw. Harvest the gift before the gift rots. And then, when enough voices had been gathered, do the thing that must be done:

Open the cage.

Because here is the truth that Maerin Brightwater took to her grave and that Inquisitors burn villages rather than admit: the Devourer cannot be killed. It can only be caged, and every cage starves, and a starving god presses against its bars until the bars remember they were once just air. The old covenant bought us thirty years. The price of renewal was a god's death each time — Vael went silent not from abandonment but from hemorrhage, holding the first seam alone while everyone who might have helped called her traitor.

Thirty years. And then what? Another death? Another silence? Another generation taught to fear the very voices that hold up the sky?

No. There is a third option, and it is mine, and it is mercy, though no one alive is grateful enough yet to call it that.

Dissolution. Not destruction of the world — *dissolution of the borders*. Of the line between self and other, mortal and divine, hunger and hungered-for. The Devourer does not want to eat the world. It wants to *include* it. And what is transcendence, if not inclusion without remainder?

The seam widens. Through it I feel something vast turn its attention toward me, the way a sleeper turns toward a lamp, and I am not ashamed to say I wept. Just once. Just briefly. It has been so long since anything looked at me and saw me whole.

"More," I tell the engine. "Give it more."

And the forty-one voices rise together, and the sky above Ironhollow — far above, beyond the fortress, beyond the mountain's shoulders — begins, very softly, to tear.

### Inquisitor Corvane

I have knelt in consecrated ground on four continents. I have fasted in deserts and stood vigil in plague-pits and heard confessions that would curdle a lesser man's faith into cruelty. I know what holiness looks like. It looks like stillness.

So when the sky above Ironhollow split open like rotten cloth, my first thought — God forgive me, my first thought, in the moment the apocalypse arrived — was *at last, something still.*

The tear hung over the mountain like a wound in a painting. No thunder. No fire. Just a long ragged absence where the evening should have been, edges fluttering, and through it a light that was not light — the light you see when your eyes are closed too hard, pressed there from underneath. Underneath us. Beneath the fortress, beneath the warrens, beneath thirty years of my life's labor, something was looking out through the hole in the world the way a prisoner looks through a grate.

My inquisitors were screaming. Some were praying. Brother Aldous had thrown himself flat and was reciting the Rite of Sealing at the sky, which is like reciting a recipe at a flood.

I did none of these things. I stood very still and felt my certainties die quietly, one by one, like lamps going out in a large house.

Here is what I believed, and I ask you to weigh how much of it was true, because I have spent the walk down into the warrens weighing it myself and the scales keep moving:

I believed the gift was a plague. My sister caught it — that is how we said it, *caught*, the way you catch fever or bad air — when she was nine. She began answering questions no one had asked. She spoke to the well, and the well answered, and within a year she was gone somewhere none of us could follow, talking to things in the walls, and the things talked back, and whatever they told her hollowed her out until there was nothing left in her eyes but weather. The church said demons. The crown said madness. I said plague, and I built my life on the word, and I hunted carriers of the gift the way my father hunted rats, and I told myself every night that I was saving nine-year-olds.

But standing under that torn sky, I understood — with the sickening clarity that arrives always too late and always all at once — that my sister had not been hollowed out. She had been *called*. And the calling had been real, and the things in the walls had been real, and the tragedy was not that she heard them.

The tragedy was that nobody answered *for* her. Nobody held her hand in the dark and said, yes, the world is more than it seems, and you are not mad, and you are not alone, and here is how you bear it.

We burned the mapmakers and wondered why everyone got lost.

"Sir!" Aldous, scrambling up, face bloodless. "The chancellor — the engine — it's coming from below, from the Hollow itself, sir, she's *opened* it—"

"I know what she's opened."

"The prisoners, sir, the gift-bearers in the cells — some of them are — they're reacting. Singing. All of them at once, the same—" He stopped, swallowed. "The same song, sir. They've never met each other."

Of course they hadn't. But the covenant remembered them, and they it, and somewhere below us the last free piece of that covenant was moving — the fugitive, Brightwater, the archivist, the shards — and the pieces were singing to each other across the stone like tuning forks finding their pitch.

For nineteen years I had hunted that song as a disease. Standing under the tear in the sky, watching the Devourer press its face against the opening Ilvane had made, I finally understood the difference between the song and the silence that answered it.

The song was ours. The silence was the cage. And Ilvane had just thrown the cage open and called it mercy.

"Aldous." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Get me the cell keys."

He stared. "Sir?"

"You heard me. Every key. Every ward. Empty the cells." I was already moving, coat flaring, boots loud on stone that had never once been asked to carry good news. "And send runners to the eastern gate. If a woman named Brightwater reaches this fortress tonight, she is to be brought to me *alive* and *unharmed* — and Aldous—" I stopped at the head of the stairs, under the fluttering edge of the torn sky, and finished the sentence that ended my old life:

"—tell her the Inquisition asks for terms."

### Sera

The cell was three paces by four and smelled of iron and despair, and I had decided, somewhere around the second day, that I refused to die in a room with worse acoustics than my university office.

That was before the singing started.

It came up through the floor first — a vibration in the stone, then in the water bucket, then in my teeth. Then the woman in the next cell began to hum, low and wavering, and the man across from her picked it up, and within minutes every gift-bearer in Corvane's cells was singing a melody none of us knew and all of us recognized, the way you recognize your own name shouted across a market square.

I pressed my palm flat against the wall and let the song move through me, and I understood it the way I understand old manuscripts: structurally, instantly, completely.

It was a summons. Or rather — I corrected myself, scholar to the bone even now, especially now — it was a *roll call*. Somewhere below us, the keystones were assembling, and the covenant was counting its parts, and we — the captured, the drained, the forty-one voices already fed into Ilvane's engine — we were being counted too.

Counted, and found *needed*.

"Sera?" The girl across the corridor — Maret, sixteen, taken for speaking to moths, moths, the crown burns people for speaking to *moths* — her face pale at the bars. "What's happening? What's the song?"

"That," I said, "is the sound of the cavalry being assembled. We're the cavalry."

She didn't smile. Neither did I, quite. Because the roll call had a second verse, and the second verse was wrong — a dragging, hungry undertone beneath the melody, like a hand pulling the song downward. The engine. Ilvane's engine, drinking the same voices the covenant was trying to count, and feeding them to something below the Hollow that sang back only in appetite.

Two songs, one throat. The race was already running. The only question was whether the border would be rebuilt or swallowed before either side reached the finish.

I turned to the lock on my cell. Old crown work, but the mechanism was older than the door it sat in, and mechanisms are just stone that learned manners — and stone, given half a chance and a speaker with the bloodline, still remembers who it serves.

"Maret," I said, pressing my palm to the lock and letting the keystone's residue in my blood answer the singing, "how would you feel about a jailbreak with theological implications?"

Through the wall, faint but unmistakable, I felt the third pulse of the night: footsteps, many, descending — and among them, threaded through the clatter of armed men, one heartbeat I would have known in a crowd of thousands, steady as a metronome, coming down toward the cells at a pace that could only belong to a man who had just betrayed everything he'd built.

Corvane. With the keys.

And behind him, further up, further in, closer with every breath — her. Theo. Coming for us through the drowning dark with both shards in her keeping and her mother's memories bleeding through her veins, walking toward the worst room in the world because the worst room in the world was where we were.

The sky tore wider. The engine sang louder. And I put both hands against the lock and began, very quietly, to speak it open.
