## 32. March Into Silence

### Theo

The silence found us before we found it.

We'd been climbing out of the flooded warrens for half a day, wet to the bone and counting ourselves like a miser counts coins—Corvane limping with a spear-shaft for a crutch, Sera pale but upright with both keystones wrapped against her chest, eleven freed speakers whose names I was still learning. Then the sound of our own footsteps stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. One moment my boots were crunching slag-gravel, and the next the world had been struck dumb, like a god had put a hand over its mouth mid-sentence.

I nearly panicked. I'll admit that. My hand went to my knife on pure reflex, because when a mercenary can't hear her own breathing, every instinct she owns starts screaming *ambush*. But there was nothing to ambush us. There was just the Hollow ahead, drinking the world's noise like a drunk drinks his last coin.

Sera touched my arm and pointed. Above the fortress ruins, above Ironhollow itself, the sky had torn—a seam of wrong-colored light, thin as a scar, pulsing faintly. Like something on the other side pressing its face against the membrane of the world.

*Ilvane*, I thought. *She beat us to it.*

I looked at the people behind me. Eleven strangers who owed me nothing and had followed me anyway, because I'd pulled them out of cells and told them a mad story about reforging a covenant, and apparently freedom makes you stupid enough to volunteer for anything. Corvane, an Inquisitor of the Crown, leaning on a broken spear, whose entire life's work I was about to ask him to help undo. Sera, who turned out to be the third keystone herself, marked at birth by my mother's order, walking toward a wound in reality because I'd asked her to.

Wry observation for the road: nobody warns you that saving the world looks like this. Not glory. Just tired people walking uphill into silence, carrying what they can't put down.

I raised my hands so they could all see me. Speaking felt absurd—the words died somewhere between my mouth and the air—but I did it anyway, because some things you say for yourself.

"Threshold's ahead," I said, watching their lips more than hearing them. "The old covenant takes a toll. A memory each. You choose what you give. No one has to cross."

The mute monks had taught me that much: the border doesn't steal. It trades. It only ever asks for what you're willing to lose—which is worse, if you think about it too long, which I tried not to.

A woman among the speakers—one of the ones who'd hummed in her cell to keep from screaming, a cooper's daughter named Wren—stepped forward and pressed two fingers to her own throat, feeling for a voice that wasn't there anymore, and then nodded at me. *Lead.*

So I led.

### Sera

I have catalogued eleven thousand manuscripts. I have read accounts of every miracle, massacre, and marriage recorded since the Breaking. None of them prepared me for the silence, because none of them could be *read aloud*.

That is the cruelty of it. The archive survives only in voices—in scholars reciting to students, in scribes murmuring their own transcriptions to check them—and here, at the edge of everything Maerin Brightwater built her prison from, the archive goes deaf. I walked into the silence carrying the sum of everything we know about the old covenant, and I could not have told a single line of it to the woman beside me.

Theo walked like she'd been born to it. That frightened me more than the sky did.

I knew her mother. I want to write that plainly, because I've hidden it long enough that plainness feels like confession: I served Maerin Brightwater for six years before the Breaking. I watched her rehearse the ritual that would break the covenant. I saw her hands shake. And when Theo walks in silence, head slightly tilted, listening to a quiet no one else can hear—I see Maerin so clearly it hurts somewhere behind my sternum, in the place where grief keeps its ledgers.

But it isn't Maerin. That is the thing I keep having to relearn. The tilt is Theo's. The stubbornness is entirely hers. When Maerin was afraid, she went still and cold and planned. When Theo is afraid, she makes a joke under her breath that no one can hear, and then keeps walking anyway.

We reached the threshold at what would have been dusk, if dusk made any sound up here. I know it was dusk because the torn seam in the sky burned brighter against the darkening east. The threshold itself was unremarkable—a shelf of black stone where the mountain flattened, ringed by standing shards of keystone-glass, humming in a register I felt in my teeth rather than heard. The two fragments in their case against my chest answered that hum. They warmed. They wanted, very badly, to go home.

One by one, the survivors stepped onto the stone.

The toll took different shapes in different faces. Wren gave something and staggered, caught by the man beside her, weeping without sound—weeping for a memory she couldn't name, which is the worst way, I think. An old ferryman named Doss gave his and simply stood afterward for a long moment, mouthing the word *no* over and over, though whether he mourned what he'd lost or what he'd kept, I couldn't tell.

Then it was my turn.

I had decided weeks ago, in the cells, what I would pay. It seemed fitting. I crossed the threshold and let the border look through me, and I offered it the memory of the day Maerin Brightwater came to me with the three keystones and said, *"If I don't come back, find someone of my blood."*

The border took it.

I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave—present, then absent, then a strange smooth absence your tongue returns to obsessively. I remembered Maerin. I remembered her voice, her face, her terrible patience. I remembered *that she had asked*. I no longer remembered the asking. Somewhere inside me was a shaped hole, and I understood intellectually that it had held the beginning of everything, and I could not reach it.

I climbed down off the stone and Theo caught my elbow. She looked at me with a question in her face, and I shook my head—*it's done, don't ask*—because if she asked, I would try to answer, and the shape of the hole might collapse into grief I couldn't carry up the mountain.

She didn't ask. She just kept hold of my elbow a moment longer than balance required, and that was its own kind of sentence.

### Corvane

I was taught that the gift was a plague.

I want that written somewhere, if anything I write survives: not as excuse—as record. My sister Maren heard the old gods the way other children hear music. She spoke to hearths and rivers and once, memorably, to our father's plough, which she claimed was lonely. I loved her for it the way you love anything luminous and slightly embarrassing. And then the voices took her—not metaphorically; she went hollow over one winter, emptied out like a house after a fire, and the last thing she said that made sense was that something on the far side of the border was *drinking* the speakers.

So yes. I hunted the gift-bearers. I built engines to harvest them. I told myself it was quarantine.

And now I climbed a mountain in absolute silence beside eleven of them, on the word of a smuggler who had shown me the thing beneath the Hollow—shown me the wound in the border, the hunger pressing through it, the truth that the crown's great engine was not sealing the plague but *feeding* it—and I found that the arithmetic of my whole life had been done in the wrong column.

Maren was not killed by the gift. Maren was killed by whatever the gift was holding back, alone, unpaid, unheard.

I thought about that as we walked. I thought about how certain I had been. Certainty, I understand now, is what a man builds when he cannot afford to grieve. I had built cathedrals of it.

At the threshold, I hesitated longer than any of them. Not from fear of the toll—from fairness. What right did I have to cross? I had dragged speakers out of their homes. I had signed the orders. Brannoc had died in my cells, and however he'd earned his ending, I had provided the cell.

Theo looked back at me. In the silence her expression carried further than any voice. She did not offer absolution—she is not a foolish woman, and neither am I. She offered passage. *Come or don't. But come whole.*

I stepped onto the stone and searched myself for what to pay. The obvious candidates were useless: I could not surrender my guilt; guilt was load-bearing now. I could not surrender Maren; I had already given enough of her to bad purposes.

What I surrendered instead was my certainty itself—or rather, the memory of how certain I had felt. The sermons I preached to myself. I kept the facts. I kept the shame. I gave up the comfort of having ever been sure, and I will not pretend that was a small price. A man's certainty is his spine.

I crossed. My legs held. Behind us, the seam in the sky pulsed, and even without sound I felt it—like a heartbeat through a wall, patient and vast and getting closer.

### Wren

*(set down later, scratched into slate, because some of us have to write now that the mountain took our voices' reasons)*

Nobody tells you how a memory leaves. Mine went like water going out of a lock—slow, level, and then all at once.

I paid the border my mother's kitchen. That's what it felt like I paid. I know I had a mother's kitchen. I know there was bread in it, and a blue door, and that she sang while she worked because her own mother had sung. I know these things the way you know a country you've never visited—by report, by faith. The warmth of it is gone. The *smell* of it is gone, and I hadn't known until tonight that a smell could be a person.

Here's the thing, though, and I want it honest: I chose it. Nobody pried it out of me. After two years in the crown's cells being told my gift was disease and my memories were evidence, there was something almost holy about giving one away *on purpose*, to a border, for a reason.

Eleven of us crossed. Doss gave something he won't speak of, except he can't speak, so he mouths it sometimes at night: *the name of my first boat.* Tam gave a summer. The twins gave—well, they won't say, and they hold hands now when they didn't used to, so maybe they gave separate halves of one thing between them.

We are not soldiers. Let that be clear to whoever reads this slate. We are a cooper's daughter, a ferryman, a stable-boy, twins who talked to bees, and others, and we are following a mercenary woman and an archivist up a mountain because they are the first people in two years to treat what's in our heads as a trust instead of a harvest.

The sky is torn above us and getting worse. The silence gets thicker the higher we climb—it's not empty silence, it's *full* silence, like the inside of a held breath. Sometimes I feel my gift straining toward the tear, and I make myself stop feeling it.

Tomorrow we go into the Hollow. Theo says we leave our last memories at the final threshold, whatever we have left worth paying.

I've decided what mine will be. I'm not writing it down.

### Theo

We made camp in the throat of the pass, and I didn't sleep, which surprised no one.

You'd think silence would be restful. It isn't. Silence with weight behind it—the Hollow's silence, the kind that presses—is like being held by something very large that hasn't yet decided whether you're a guest or a meal. I lay against my pack and listened to nothing so hard my ears ached, and I inventoried myself the way the monks taught me.

*Naming what is yours.* Breath. Scar on left forearm—mine, earned falling out of Renna Kell's hayloft at fourteen. Wait. Was that mine? Or was that Maerin's echo, wearing my childhood like a borrowed coat? I pressed the heel of my hand against my eye until sparks came. This was the tax I'd already been paying since the snowline, unofficially, involuntarily: I was losing track of the borders inside my own head, and tomorrow I intended to walk into the place where all borders went to die.

There was a joke in there somewhere. I was too tired to find it.

Sera sat beside me in the dark. Neither of us could have spoken if we'd wanted to—the silence ate words at this altitude—but she found my hand in the dark, and her fingers were cold, and I held on.

I thought about what I'd pay at the last threshold. I had spent so many memories by now—my childhood home to the toll-keeper, whole years to the flood-waters, scattered pieces to the gift itself—that my interior landscape had become a country after war: familiar roads ending abruptly in fields where nothing grew. I had learned not to probe the gaps. Probing them hurt.

But I knew what remained that mattered. And I knew, with the cold clarity that comes at the bottom of sleepless nights, that I would probably have to pay it. The new covenant needed mortar, and the mortar was memory, and the deepest seams need the strongest stone.

*Let tomorrow-me negotiate with tomorrow's prices*, I told myself, and closed my eyes, and got perhaps an hour of dreamless nothing, which in the Hollow's shadow passes for mercy.

* * *

### Theo

Dawn—if it was dawn—came gray and sourceless, the sun somewhere behind the tear-light. We broke camp without a word, because words were pointless, and formed up into something that wasn't quite a procession and wasn't quite a battle line. Eleven speakers. One wounded Inquisitor. One archivist carrying two-thirds of a covenant against her heart. One mercenary carrying the rest of it in her blood.

We climbed.

The final threshold sat in a bowl of black glass below the Hollow's mouth—the fused remains, Sera had guessed, of the original covenant-stones, melted when Maerin broke the border. Walking across it was like walking across the surface of a frozen scream. And there, at the center, the border rose up to meet us: not a wall, not a gate. A *presence*. The sense of being regarded by something older than gods, patient as geology, waiting with its palm open.

*Toll*, it said, without saying. The pressure of it bent everyone forward at once, like wind.

Theo Brightwater's tactical assessment: we were eleven souls against an apocalyptic schedule, armed with whatever we could bear to lose. Morale: surprisingly good. Odds: don't ask.

Wren went first. She knelt, laid her forehead against the glass, and I watched her give—watched her shoulders drop and her face go briefly, terribly young. Whatever she'd saved for last, she gave it freely, and when she rose she was smiling through silent tears, and I understood that some payments are also offerings.

Doss went. Tam went. The twins went together, hand in hand, and came up lighter and clinging harder to each other. Corvane knelt like a man at final rites and stayed down a long time, and when he rose, something in his face had been set down forever—heavier and freer at once, the way men look after funerals they needed to attend.

Sera gave hers, and I watched the shape of the loss move through her, though I'd never learn its name.

Then it was me.

I knelt on the warm black glass and opened myself, and the border looked into me with Maerin's eyes—not cruelly, never cruelly, but thoroughly, the way an appraiser handles a dead woman's jewelry. It found what remained. It paused over the one thing I had guarded through every toll and flood and sleepless dark.

*This*, it said, without saying. *Or turn back.*

Below us, beyond the threshold, deep in the wound of the world, something answered. Not the border. Not Vael. Something else—something that tasted of Ilvane's ambition and a hunger older than hunger—and the tear in the sky above Ironhollow split wider with a sound I felt in my marrow though my ears heard nothing at all.

The Devourer, waking faster than we'd planned. The clock, audibly ticking.

I looked back once at my ragged congregation of the emptied—Sera with her steady eyes, Wren with her slate, Corvane with his bare soul and broken spear—and I made my choice the way I make all of them: quickly, before wisdom can catch up.

I opened my hands, and I began to pay.
