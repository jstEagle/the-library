## 33. Ilvane Ascendant

I have always hated waiting. It is the vice of small minds, my old tutor said, and I flattered myself for thirty years that I was not a small mind, that I merely *prepared* while lesser men fidgeted. But here, at the edge of the wound in the world, with my robes soaked through and my hands shaking in a way I refuse to call fear, I have learned the truth of it: waiting is what you do when you have run out of clever things to do.

The Hollow does not care for cleverness. That is its great virtue.

I descended the last stair alone. My acolytes — I had stopped thinking of them as prisoners weeks ago; they were instruments now, and instruments do not need names — stood in their niches along the walls, mouths moving in the harvested cadences I had taught them, feeding their voices into the engine's throat. Forty-one speakers. Forty-one little fires. The crown had spent two years hunting them down like rats, and Chancellor Ilvane had signed every warrant personally, and if any of them had known why, they would have spat at me.

They will understand soon. Understanding is the one gift dissolution gives freely.

The antechamber opened before me, and there it was: the wound.

You cannot describe the Hollow to someone who has not stood in it. The priests try. They speak of silence, of absence, of a god-shaped hole. All inadequate. The Hollow is not an absence. An absence is passive. This is *attentive*. It is a silence that is listening to you, that has been listening since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to make fire, and it is very, very patient, and it has opinions about you specifically.

I knelt at the threshold, as the rite required, and I gave it my first memory.

Not a toll, exactly. Not anymore. The old covenant demanded payment; the new arrangement — mine, my design, my masterpiece — asked only for *opening*. I gave it the memory of my mother's kitchen, bread and tallow smoke, her voice calling me in from the rain. I felt it leave me the way you feel a tooth come loose: a small shocking gap where something solid used to be, and then the tongue keeps going back to check, and it isn't there, it isn't there, it isn't there.

And in exchange, the Devourer let me feel it noticing me.

People imagine that communion with a hungry god feels like terror. It does not. It feels like being *seen* for the first time. Every prayer I had ever muttered into the dark, every night I had lain awake cataloguing my own inadequacies, every moment of the grinding loneliness of being the cleverest person in every room and therefore in no room at all — all of it, answered. Not with comfort. With *appetite*, which is a far more honest form of love.

"You are early," said the voice behind me.

Corvane. Of course. My hound, off his leash, standing at the top of the stairs with blood on his collar and doubt written across his face like a confession he hadn't managed to sign yet. I had known this day would come. Men like Corvane are load-bearing until suddenly they are not.

"Inquisitor," I said pleasantly. "You should be upstairs managing the fortress."

"The fortress is sealed." He came down three steps and stopped, which was wise of him. "The guards report to no one. The warrens are flooding. And I watched a prisoner beg me for mercy in my sister's voice today, Chancellor, and I would very much like to know what you did with her."

Ah. Lissa. He'd found her niche. I had wondered how long his certainty would hold once he saw the machinery's face rather than its purpose. Two years. Longer than I'd guessed, honestly. Faith is more durable than people think, right up until it isn't.

"What I did with her," I said, rising, "was save her. Sit, Corvane. Or stand. You won't believe me either way, so let us dispense with the theater." I gestured at the wound. "Do you know what killed your sister? Truly?"

"The gift," he said, flat. "The covenant-speech. It hollowed her out. I've seen it a hundred times since. Speakers burn through themselves and leave nothing but—"

"A vessel," I agreed. "Yes. That is what happens when you use the gift *through* the broken border. Maerin Brightwater shattered the old covenant twenty years ago, and every speaker since has been pouring water through a cracked cup. Your sister didn't die of the gift. She died of the *wound*. As would every speaker we've harvested, eventually, even without my engine." I smiled at him, and I confess I enjoyed it. "I am not killing them, Inquisitor. I am giving their deaths a purpose. There is a difference. Ask any soldier."

He looked at the Hollow for a long moment. I watched him want to disbelieve me. I watched him fail.

"And the thing in there?" he said finally. "What is it?"

"Hungry," I said. "That is all it has ever been. A hunger wearing a god's face, older than the border, older than the covenant, older than the petty little bargains our ancestors made to keep it outside. Maerin Brightwater caged it and called it salvation. The gods went silent holding the seams. And the world called it peace." I spread my arms. "I am simply proposing we stop pretending. Everything ends, Corvane. Every wall, every name, every grief, every debt. The Devourer is not evil. Evil requires preference. It is simply the truth about endings, given a mouth. I intend to open the door before it breaks the wall down anyway."

"You intend to *let it in*."

"I intend to *go out to meet it*." I turned back to the wound, because looking at him had begun to bore me, and because the Hollow was singing now, low and vast, and I had waited thirty years to hear it properly. "There is a difference, Inquisitor, between a city sacked and a city surrendered. One is a tragedy. The other is a treaty."

Behind me, silence. Then footsteps, retreating up the stairs. He did not arrest me. He did not draw steel. He simply left, and I noted it in the ledger of my mind under *loose threads*, and then I dismissed him entirely, because the seam above the wound had begun to glow, and somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred feet of iron-veined stone, the sky over Ironhollow was tearing open.

It was time.

* * *

Merging is not what the heresiarchs described. There is no ecstasy, no white light, no dissolving into warm nothing. What there is, is *scale*.

I stepped to the edge of the wound and spoke the unbinding — forty-one voices funneling through mine, the harvested names of the speakers arranged in the sequence I had spent two years deriving from fragments of the old liturgy — and the cage's first seam came apart like wet paper, and the Devourer looked at me.

I say *looked*. It has no eyes. But attention moved across me the way a searchlight moves across a field, and everything it touched of me it understood completely, and I understood, in return, the first true thing I had learned in years: I was not becoming its servant. Servants are retained. I was becoming its *herald*, which is a different office entirely. A herald belongs to the message. A herald is permitted to exist precisely as long as the message needs delivering.

I found I did not mind. Is that madness? Perhaps. But consider: I had spent my whole life building things — ledgers, engines, arguments, alliances — and every single one of them was designed to be used by someone else after me. Chancellors serve crowns. Crowns serve dynasties. Dynasties serve time, and time serves nothing and eats everything. I had simply found the thing that eats last, and offered it my services directly. Cut out the middlemen. Any competent administrator would applaud the efficiency.

Its power came into me like cold water into a dry cloth. I felt my body change — or rather, I felt my body become *optional*, a coat I was wearing rather than a self I was trapped in. My shadow moved wrong on the walls. When I spoke, my voice had harmonics in it that no human throat produces, and the stones around me drank the sound instead of echoing it.

And threaded through all of it, faint but unmistakable, I felt the rest of the cage. Three keystones. Two already joined, humming somewhere below me in the warrens like struck bells that refused to fade. And a third — ah, the third. Not stone at all. Flesh. A woman marked at birth by Maerin's order, walking beside Maerin's daughter even now, carrying the last piece of the lock in her blood.

They were coming. Of course they were coming. Brightwater's line had never once, in two generations, done the sensible thing.

I could have fled deeper. I could have set wards, traps, ambushes — I had prepared a dozen contingencies and abandoned all of them within an hour of merging, because contingencies are for people who can still be surprised, and I was rapidly becoming a creature for whom surprise was a historical curiosity. Instead I did something better. I waited for them in the antechamber, seated on the altar steps like a hostess awaiting late guests, and I composed my argument.

Because that is the thing no one understands about the Devourer, and the thing I understood immediately, the thing that made me worthy of this office: it does not conquer. It *negotiates*. It offers terms. It has been offering terms since the beginning, and Maerin Brightwater's great crime — greater than breaking the covenant, greater than silencing the gods — was that she never once told anyone what those terms were. She decided for all of us that dissolution was unacceptable, and she built a prison, and she made her daughter inherit the warden's keys, and she never asked a single soul whether they might prefer to simply *stop*.

I intended to correct that oversight.

* * *

They came up out of the warrens near dawn — or what passed for dawn, beneath a sky torn open and bleeding light of the wrong color. I heard them before I saw them: boots in water, the clink of the fused keystones, and underneath it all a silence moving with them, deliberate, shaped, a quiet that walked. The new covenant, already breathing. How interesting. How *presumptuous*.

Brightwater came first. Theo, they called her now — she had taken a new name somewhere in the dark, which showed either wisdom or desperation, and I suspected both. Behind her, the archivist, Sera, gray-faced and soaked, one hand pressed to her side where the anchor-mark sat under her skin like a coal. Behind them, a ragged procession: freed speakers, wounded soldiers, and — this genuinely delighted me — Corvane himself, limping, armed, fighting *beside* them. My hound had gone over the mountain. Loose thread, meet scissors.

"Chancellor Ilvane," Brightwater said. Her voice was wrecked — she had spent memories down in those flooded tunnels, I could smell the gaps in her, the places where herself had been paid away — but she stood like someone who had decided exactly how much of her remained and intended to spend every coin of it well. "Step away from the wound."

"There is no 'away' anymore," I said, and rose, and let her see what I had become. To her credit, she did not flinch. To her further credit, I watched her *catalogue* me — the wrongness of my shadow, the harmonics, the way the water stilled around my feet — filing each detail for later use. A bookkeeper's daughter after all, whatever else she was. "But please. Come closer. I have wanted to meet you for years, Theo Brightwater. You are the most interesting thing your mother ever made, and she made several remarkable things."

"Don't." Sera's voice, hoarse. "Theo, don't listen to—"

"Oh, *listen*," I said gently. "By all means. Listening is the one thing no one in this miserable story has done. Maerin didn't listen when the Devourer offered terms. Vael doesn't listen — it holds its seam in silence like a martyr, refusing even to explain itself. The crown didn't listen to its speakers; it harvested them. Everyone in this tale has decided, unilaterally, what everyone else must endure." I descended one step. "I am offering something novel. A choice."

"There's no choice with it," Brightwater said. "It's hunger. It doesn't negotiate, it consumes."

"It consumed *nothing*," I snapped, and felt my borrowed harmonics crack the air. "For an eternity beyond your counting, it sat outside the border and it *asked*, and your ancestors answered with walls. Do you know what it wants? Truly? I do. I hold its office. It wants what every starving thing wants: to be included. To end the exhausting business of being separate. And yes — that ending is death, as you understand death. But tell me, Brightwater, and answer honestly, you who have paid away your childhood home and half your own face to get here—" I watched her go still. Yes. I knew the ledger of her. I had helped write the tolls. "—what exactly has keeping yourself kept you? Grief? Debt? A dead mother's war, fought on your body, with your memories as ammunition? You have lost so much of yourself getting to this room that I could ask you your mother's face and you couldn't—"

"*Enough.*"

The word hit the chamber like a dropped bell. Covenant-speech, raw and unshaped, and the water around her feet froze into a ring of white lace. She was shaking. Good. Shaking meant I had reached her.

So I softened. This was the craft of it, the part I had rehearsed in the dark for months: not menace. *Mercy.*

"I am not your enemy," I said quietly. "I am the only person in this entire realm who is telling you the truth: that you are tired, and you have earned the right to be finished. Think what I offer. No more grief — not suppressed, not survived, *gone*, root and branch, because grief requires a self to grieve and the self is the wound. No more inherited weight. No more mother's echo in your skull deciding which scars are yours. No more silent god making you guess at your worth. No more watching everyone you love die in installments while you carry the arithmetic." I stepped closer, close enough to see my reflection in her eyes, and made sure the reflection was kind. "Your mother broke the world to spare you this choice. I am breaking it open to give it back to you. Dissolution is not punishment, Brightwater. It is *rest*. It is the end of being the one who pays. And you — of everyone alive — you have paid enough."

For a moment — I will be honest, since honesty is the one luxury I retain — for a moment I thought she would say yes.

She swayed. Her hand found Sera's without looking, the way you find a banister in the dark, and something in her face went young and unbearably tired, and I pressed the advantage with the gentleness I had practiced:

"No more weight," I whispered. "No more ledger. Just set it down. Set it all down. Let someone else carry it — or better, let there be no one left who needs to carry anything. Isn't that what you've wanted since Greyfen? Since the altar? Since you were a child praying to a god who never answered? *Rest.*"

And she looked at me — at the herald, at the hunger wearing my face and my convictions — and something shifted behind her eyes. Not surrender. Something worse. Recognition.

"You're not lying," she said slowly. "That's the horrible part. Every word is true." She drew a breath. "But it's half a truth, Ilvane. And I've spent this whole road learning what half-truths cost." Her grip tightened on Sera's hand. "Rest isn't the end of carrying. I learned that in the dark. Rest is *sharing* the load. You're offering to burn the cargo so no one has to hold it. That's not mercy. That's just arson with good manners."

Behind me, the wound shuddered. The Devourer, impatient, pressing at the second seam, and I felt its appetite lean through me like wind through an open door — and I raised my hand, and the torn sky above Ironhollow brightened, and the stones of the antechamber began, very softly, to sing.

"Then we are past negotiation," I said, and stood, and let the herald rise in me fully, robes lifting in a wind that came from nowhere, my voice splitting into the chord of forty-one stolen souls. "Pity. I had hoped to spare you the choice by removing it."

Brightwater lifted the fused keystones, and they blazed like captive dawn, and beside her the archivist bared the mark on her arm, and the walking silence at their backs gathered itself like a held breath.

"Last chance to step aside, Chancellor," Brightwater said.

"I never step aside," I told her. "I *ascend*."

And the third keystone — the girl, the marked one, Sera — looked past me into the wound itself, and went white as salt, and said the one word I had not planned for:

"*Theo — it's awake. It was never asleep. It's been awake this whole time — and it knows your name.*"
