## 34. The Reforging

The antechamber of the Hollow was a bowl of black glass, and we stood at its rim like fools at the edge of a well, deciding whether to drink.

I want to tell you I felt brave. I don't lie in my own head if I can help it — I've spent too many years lying everywhere else. What I felt was cold. Not mountain-cold, not fear-cold. The specific cold of a woman standing in a place where sound goes to die, holding two shards of a broken promise and about to make a third out of her own mother's face.

Sera knelt beside me on the glass, sleeves rolled back, the old scar on her forearm catching what little light the keystones gave off. That pale blue glow had been our lantern for three hundred miles of bad road, and now it was going to be something else entirely.

"Say it back to me," she said. "All of it."

"I know the words."

"Say them anyway."

I sighed. Even my sighs died quickly down here, swallowed by the Hollow's hunger before they could echo. "The border is not a wall. It is a promise. A promise is not stone. It is memory, spoken aloud, renewed by the living." I looked at her. "Happy?"

"It's not about happy." Sera's voice was steady, but her hands weren't. She'd been marked at birth by Maerin Brightwater's order — inked with an anchor-sigil she'd spent forty years thinking was a birthmark gone wrong. Her whole life had been rearranged in the last hour, and she was kneeling on cursed glass anyway. "It's about precise. If you garble one clause, the covenant takes the nearest available material to fill the gap."

"And the nearest available material would be—"

"You. Me. Everything with a pulse between here and Greyfen."

"Right." I flexed my fingers. They were shaking. I let them. Brannoc used to say a shaking hand that knows it's shaking shoots truer than a steady one that thinks it's steady. He also said never trust a woman who apologizes before she stabs you, so his wisdom came in mixed batches. "Then let's be precise."

I set the first keystone on the glass. Then the second. They didn't fit together — of course they didn't; Maerin had shattered them across half a kingdom precisely so they'd never fit together again. Two jagged halves of a word nobody had spoken whole in twenty years, sitting in front of me like a broken plate you're expected to serve dinner on.

And the third piece was Sera.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about speaking a god's grammar: your mouth isn't built for it. Covenant-speech doesn't come out of the throat. It comes up through the sternum, through the blood, through every scar you own, and it uses your memories as its ink. You don't recite the covenant. You pay for it, syllable by syllable, out of the only account you have.

I'd been paying all book, if I'm honest. The toll-bridge godling took my childhood home. The gift itself had been eating my edges since Thornfell, swapping my scars for my mother's, my dreams for hers. By the time we reached Ironhollow I woke some mornings not sure whether I liked honey in my tea or whether that was Maerin's preference wearing my tongue.

So when I opened my mouth to begin, I already knew what the mortar would be.

Her.

Not her bones or her deeds or her infamy among the faithful of Ashfall Village. The memories themselves. Every one I had left. The covenant needed binding material, and the only substance strong enough to knit a wound between realms was love that had survived being lied about.

Maerin broke the covenant to cage the Devourer. Everyone who learned the truth assumed she'd betrayed it. She let them assume. She walked into history as a traitor so the cage would stay shut, because a cage everyone believes is decorative is a cage no one tests. Twenty years of being spat at by elders, hunted by kings, erased from every archive Sera ever kept — that was the price of the lock. And her daughter grew up orphaned and godless, hating a ghost.

She hadn't even left me a letter. Just the echo under Ironhollow, and two stones, and a stranger with a birthmark.

Fine, Mother. I thought, and the thought went out into the silence like a struck bell. Fine. You paid in exile. I'll pay in memory. Let's see which of us runs dry first.

* * *

"Theo." Sera touched my wrist. "Whatever happens — whatever you see, whatever you lose — keep speaking. Don't stop to grieve mid-word. Grieve after. Promise me."

"That's a terrible way to live."

"It's the only way any of us are about to keep living. Promise."

"I promise." And then, because wry is armor and I was about to go into battle naked: "You know, most employers just dock your pay."

Sera laughed — one short, cracked note that the Hollow ate instantly — and pressed her palm flat against the glass. The anchor-sigil on her arm lit from beneath, blue-white, and I felt the keystones answer it like dogs hearing their names.

I put my hand over hers. I put my other hand on the shards.

And I spoke.

* * *

The first word cost me the smell of rain on the smuggler roads south of Greyfen. I felt it leave — a small clean absence, like a tooth coming out — and the keystones drank it and brightened.

The second phrase cost me the name of the innkeeper in Saltmarsh who'd watered our ale and lied about it with such conviction I'd respected him. Gone. The covenant took it and hammered it flat and laid it along the first seam, and I watched the two shards lean toward each other, hungry, not yet touching.

*The border is not a wall,* I said, and gave up my tenth winter — the fever, my crew's faces swimming above me, Vael's silence feeling for the first time like a held breath rather than an absence. The memory tore free whole. In its place the shards ground together with a sound I heard through my teeth rather than my ears, because the Hollow permits no sound it hasn't been paid for.

*It is a promise,* and there went the harbor at Greyfen at dusk, gulls and tar-smoke and Brannoc laughing at his own joke. I staggered. Sera's grip on my hand tightened hard enough to grind bone, and through her palm I felt the anchor working — her blood pulling the frame taut even as my memories fed it, her sigil burning like a keel-stone keeping the whole structure upright while I poured myself into the walls.

That was the design. I understood it then, in the wordless way you understand things mid-catastrophe. Maerin had tried to anchor the covenant alone — a god's death, a single point of failure, one woman holding a seam shut until she couldn't. My new covenant had two points and a triangle of trust between them. Sera's blood was the frame. My memory was the mortar. Neither of us could hold it alone, and that was the entire point, and I could have kissed her for it if either of us had a mouth to spare.

*Spoken aloud,* I said, and lost my mother's voice.

I hadn't known I still had it. Somewhere under everything, under the tolls and the erosion and the borrowed echoes, I'd kept the actual sound of Maerin Brightwater saying my name — *Theo, come inside, Theo, mind the hearth* — and the covenant found it the way water finds a crack, and took it, and I made a noise I will not describe to you because some things stay mine.

Don't stop. Sera's voice, inside my skull rather than my ears. Don't stop to grieve mid-word.

*Renewed by the living.*

The shards fused.

There was no light, exactly. There was the opposite of dark — a fullness, a pressure, the sense of a word becoming true. The fused keystone rose off the glass an inch, humming against my palms like a heart deciding to beat, and the seams around it began to knit. I could see them — or perceive them, seeing being one of many senses this work ignored — the long ragged tear in the border of the world, running away into distances that had no business existing inside a mine beneath a fortress. Twenty years of fraying. Things pressing at the threads from the far side. A hunger wearing a god's face, patient behind the weave.

And I stitched it. With rain-smell and fever and gulls and my mother's voice, with the taste of the bread Sera shared with me in the drowned chapel, with the exact weight of the memory I'd chosen to keep — the wordless one, the feeling of being loved once without condition or explanation. I hesitated over that one. The covenant paused with me. It knew a keystone when it tasted one.

No, I told it. Not that. Take anything else.

It took something else. I don't know what yet. I'll find the gaps later, cataloguing myself like a smuggler's ledger, discovering which rooms in the house of me stand empty. That's tomorrow's grief. Tonight the grief was simple: I gave, and gave, and gave, and the border drank it all and asked for more, and I kept speaking because I had promised a woman with a burning arm that I would.

* * *

Somewhere in the middle third of the work, I stopped being entirely in my body.

I don't mean I astral-traveled. Nothing so dignified. I mean the covenant was using all of me — blood, breath, bone, biography — and what was left over, the observing remainder, the part of me that narrates even when I'd rather it shut up, drifted up and back and looked down at two women kneeling on black glass in the throat of the world. One glowing like a struck flint. One pouring herself out like wine.

From up there it looked almost peaceful. From inside it was nothing of the kind. Inside it was labor — real labor, the body-wrecking kind, my muscles cramping around a posture I couldn't change, my lungs working air that had nothing in it, my heart hammering out the rhythm the words wanted. Sweat froze on my spine. My nose bled freely; I felt it drip off my chin onto the glass and each drop vanished where it landed, spent, accepted, entered into the record.

Sera was chanting now too — low, wordless, the anchor-hum her order had bred into her bloodline without telling her. Our voices braided. The frame held. The seam closed mile by mile, and I felt each closure as a small physical relief, like a splinter leaving flesh, and each one cost another room of my interior life.

The inn at Saltmarsh. The color of the sky the morning I signed on with Brannoc's company. The lullaby — gods, the lullaby, I didn't know I remembered a lullaby, and the covenant took it before I could decide whether I wanted to keep it, and maybe that was mercy, because I think it was hers, I think she sang it to me before she left, and it hurt more leaving than anything else had.

Keep speaking. Sera. Blood and iron. Keep speaking.

I kept speaking.

* * *

Near the end, the Devourer noticed.

Of course it did. You don't stitch a cage shut around a starving thing without the starving thing taking an interest. The pressure from the far side changed — stopped pushing and started pulling, started leaning against my words like a listener leaning toward a keyhole. Cold poured through the remaining gaps. Not temperature. Appetite. It moved across my memories ahead of the covenant's harvest, tasting them, and I felt it linger on the ones I treasured, savoring, learning the shape of what I loved so it could wear those shapes later.

Let it look, I thought, and kept stitching. Let it see exactly what it's caged against.

Because here was the thing the Devourer hadn't counted on, the thing Ilvane in her beautiful boundary-less transcendence had never understood: a memory given freely binds tighter than a memory taken. Every scrap the covenant harvested from me went into the wall willingly. Will was in the mortar. Love was in the mortar. The Devourer could press its face against that weave forever and find nothing to dissolve, because you cannot unmake a thing that was given.

The last seam lay directly beneath us. Of course it did. Maerin had always intended her daughter to finish what she started; she'd just had the decency to leave the choice buried deep enough that finding it meant wanting to.

I looked at Sera. She looked at me. Her arm was smoking faintly, the sigil burned nearly white, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain.

"Together?" she said.

"Together."

We spoke the final clause as one voice — hers the anchor, mine the mortar, the fused keystone between us blazing like a captive dawn — and the last gap in the border of the world drew shut around the Devourer with a sound like a book closing, like a door, like a mother finally allowed to rest.

The silence that followed wasn't the Hollow's silence. It was a whole different animal. It was the silence after.

I fell forward onto my hands. The glass was warm now. Everything ached, and worse than aching, everything was missing — I could feel the gaps in me the way you feel missing teeth, dozens of absences wearing the shape of a life. I tried to remember my mother's face and got a blank, a smooth white nothing where twenty years of longing used to live.

Gone. All of it. Paid out, thread by thread, into a border that would hold for a generation because a mercenary and an archivist had knelt in the dark and given away everything they were made of.

Worth it, I thought, and meant it, and hated how much I meant it.

Sera collapsed against my shoulder, breathing hard, alive, her sigil dimming to embers. "You did it," she whispered. "Theo. You did it."

"No," I said, and my voice came out scraped raw and strange. "We—"

The keystone flared once, blinding, and went dark.

And in the dark, from the far side of a border that should have been sealed tight, something spoke.

It used my mother's voice. It said:

*"Clever girl. Now let me tell you what she actually said, at the end."*
