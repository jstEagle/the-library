## 39. A New Covenant

The wayshrine at Greyfen's crossroads had been a pile of scorched stone when we arrived, and I use *we* loosely, since my contribution to its rebuilding was mostly hauling and swearing while Sera did the actual architecture of faith. Three weeks of mortar and argument. The locals helped once they decided I wasn't going to eat their children or their harvest, in that order of concern.

It was small, the finished shrine. Smaller than the old one, which had been tall enough to make you feel like an insect with opinions. This one came up to a grown man's chest, open on all four sides, roofed in slate the color of rain. No altar. No idol. Just a flat stone where people could set things down, if they felt moved to.

People were moved to.

I stood back that morning and watched them come. A drover left a twist of salt — honest payment, no ledger attached. A woman I didn't recognize set down a wooden comb, worn smooth, and stood there a long moment with her hand still on it before letting go. Two boys argued over who got to leave the apple, then solved it by leaving both, which struck me as either generosity or poor arithmetic.

Nobody burned anything. Nobody bled. Nobody knelt until their knees went numb hoping some distant power would notice the performance. They just left small true things and walked on lighter.

"That's the difference," Sera said beside me. She'd come up quiet, the way she always did, though after three weeks sharing a bedroll and most of my thoughts I should have heard her coming. "Before, worship was a transaction. You paid because you feared the invoice."

"And now?"

"Now they pay because they remember they owe something. To each other, to the border, to whatever's listening." She glanced at me. "To you."

"Don't say that where the boys can hear. They'll start leaving apples addressed to me."

"They might anyway. You rebuilt a shrine in three weeks and the sky hasn't torn open since. In this town, that's sainthood."

"In this town, not getting gored by your own ox is sainthood. Standards are low post-apocalypse."

She laughed — actually laughed, head tipped back, and I let myself look at her while she wasn't watching me watch her. Her hair was growing out from where the Inquisitors' healers had shaved it for the wound dressing, a soft dark fuzz that caught the morning light. There was a scar along her jaw from the snowline, thin as a pen stroke. She'd refused to have it magicked away. Said it was hers.

I understood that better than she knew.

* * *

Vael murmured around midday.

I want to be careful how I describe it, because for six years Vael's silence had been the loudest thing in my life — a held breath I'd learned to live inside. And then at Ironhollow he'd spoken again, faint and wrung-out, like a man surfacing after nearly drowning, and now his voice lived in me the way a hearth lives in a house: always there, mostly low, warming things without demanding attention.

*The salt-bearer,* he murmured, as the drover walked away. *She lost her husband on the toll roads. She leaves salt because he was a salter.*

I know, I said back. Not aloud. That part took getting used to — conversations nobody could see, conducted in the space behind my sternum.

*You do not know. You inferred. She told the woman with the comb.*

Sera talks too much, I said.

*She listens more than she talks. You simply never noticed the difference before, because you did not listen either.* A pause, and then, dry as old paper: *You are improved.*

"Was that a compliment?" I asked aloud.

Sera looked up from the shrine ledger — yes, we kept a ledger now, but it recorded offerings given freely and needs overheard, not debts owed. "What?"

"The god just complimented me."

"I'll alert the criers." She wrote something down. "What did he say?"

"That I'm improved."

"He's right. Three weeks ago you'd have called that boy stupid instead of bad at sums."

"Three weeks ago I was carrying two keystones and running for my life. Everyone gets a pass during an apocalypse."

"Mm," she said, which was Sera for *that's not the reason, and you know it, and I love you anyway*, all in one syllable. She had a gift for compression.

I sat down on the shrine's low eastern step and let the sun get into my bones. The border hummed — I mean that literally now, a note below hearing that I felt in my teeth and the soles of my feet, steady as a millwheel. Before the Breaking, the old covenant had been a wall: gods on one side, mortals on the other, traffic strictly regulated, everything rigid and silent. Maerin had built that wall with a corpse for a keystone and it had held for a generation, and then it hadn't.

The new one wasn't a wall. It was more like skin. It flexed. It remembered what touched it. Small gods — the kind who'd been starving in the margins since the Breaking, the toll-keeper's cousins, the spirit of a well, a river that wanted its name back — pressed gently against it from their side and found it gave, just enough, and settled against it like travelers leaning on a warm stone.

And on this side, people left apples and combs and twists of salt, and the covenant drank those small truths and grew stronger on them than it ever had on fear.

Restoration without erasure. That was the phrase Sera used, and I stole it shamelessly, because she was usually right and always better with words than me.

*The eastern watch fires are lit,* Vael murmured, and there was something in his tone I'd learned to read as amusement. *Your inquisitor rises early.*

"He's not my inquisitor."

*He carries your covenant's oath in his chest. He prays to a god he does not believe in, so that the believing ones have someone to guard them. If that is not yours, whose is it?*

I had no answer for that. Vael had gotten insufferable since coming back from the dead. Or the deep silence. Whatever you'd call six years of holding a cage-seam alone in the dark while everyone assumed you'd abandoned them.

Sorry about that, I thought. The apology was ritual between us now. He always waved it off. Today:

*Do not apologize. Stand up straighter. He will want to report, and he respects posture.*

* * *

Corvane arrived at dusk, as he always did, riding east-to-west along the ridge road with two of the freed speakers as outriders. He'd surrendered his writ the day after Ironhollow — handed it physically to Sera, of all people, because he said she'd earned the right to decide what to do with it, and she'd burned it in front of him, and he'd watched it burn with the expression of a man watching his own funeral go well.

Now he kept the eastern watch. Somebody had to. The Devourer was caged, but cages need guards, and the eastern marches — where the border ran thinnest, where Ilvane's engine had chewed the fabric worst — needed someone with Corvane's particular talents: methodical, tireless, and constitutionally incapable of leaving a job half-done.

He dismounted at the shrine, and I stood, and we regarded each other across ten feet of trampled grass with the warmth of two people who had fought side by side through hell and intended to keep the friendship strictly professional.

"Thackeray."

"Inquisitor."

"Not inquisitor anymore."

"No," I agreed. "Just a man with a very large sword and a schedule."

Something twitched at the corner of his mouth. From Corvane, that was a belly laugh. "Border holds. The Thornfell seam is knitting — I can feel it through my boots now, which I'm told means it's strong. Three more speakers came out of the high valleys last week. Farmers' kids. One of them hears stones." He said it evenly, but I saw his hands flex on the reins. His sister had been a speaker. His sister had died screaming things no child should know. Every new speaker was, to him, both proof the world was healing and a knife turning slowly in an old wound.

"How does he sleep?" I asked once, quietly, after he'd ridden on. Sera had shrugged.

"With discipline," she'd said. "Like everything else he does."

That evening he stayed long enough to eat — he always ate standing, plate in hand, like a man who distrusted chairs — and reported what he always reported: the seam, the weather of the border, the rumors from the capital. Ilvane's faction had scattered. Some had been taken; some had dissolved themselves rather than be unmade by capture, which was a sentence I'd have found poetic before Ironhollow and found only sad now. The new chancellor — some gray functionary elevated by default — had ordered the fortress decommissioned. Miners were already back in the upper warrens, under strict instruction to leave the Hollow itself sealed and marked.

"Marked how?" I asked.

"Your name," Corvane said. "On iron. The miners complained it was morbid. I told them morbid was appropriate."

"It's not my tomb."

"No. But something ended there." He looked at me directly, and for a moment I saw past the discipline to the thing underneath — the sister's voice in a prisoner's throat, the certainty cracked and regrown crooked but alive. "I spent my life hunting a plague," he said. "Turns out I was hunting the cure's symptoms. I don't expect forgiveness for that."

"I'm not in the forgiveness business," I said. "But the watch roster has your name on it, and the covenant doesn't lie about who serves it."

He nodded once, the way soldiers nod, and went to check his horses, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

Later, Sera and I walked the perimeter of the town in the dark, which was less security patrol and more habit — ours, specifically, the habit of two women who'd spent months measuring safety in sightlines and now couldn't quite stop.

Greyfen at night smelled like woodsmoke and the marsh, and somewhere a fiddler was playing badly in a tavern, and the sound carried clean across the water because the border hum steadied even sound these days, or maybe that was my imagination. The lamplighter worked his rounds whistling. A cat supervised us from a fence post with the contempt cats reserve for people who think they matter.

"Do you miss it?" Sera asked.

"Miss what? The cat's approval?"

"Being feared. Being hunted. Having a simple problem with a sharp solution."

I considered it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to Sera had a shelf life of roughly four seconds. "Some nights I miss knowing exactly what I was. Mercenary. Blade for hire. The moral questions fit in a coin purse." I kicked a pebble off the bridge we were crossing. "Now the moral questions have a postal route."

"You could go back to it. The company's rebuilding under Dalla. Brannoc's—"

"Don't." Not sharp. Just final. Brannoc had died in the cells buying us time, asking nothing, and grief for him was a room in my house I kept the door closed on. Maybe someday I'd open it. Not tonight. "No. I'm done selling the only sword I've got."

We reached the far end of the bridge and stopped, the way we did, to look back at the shrine. At night it was lit by a single lamp someone left burning — no one knew who started it, and no one would claim it, which Sera said was the most devout thing she'd ever witnessed.

"You're staying, then," she said. Not quite a question.

"Here? Greyfen? Sera, the mud here has opinions."

"You've grown fond of the mud."

"I've grown fond of the person who won't leave the mud," I said, "which is different, and considerably more inconvenient."

She smiled in the dark. "The order wants me to travel. Rebuild the archives. Find every fragment of the old lore before it rots or gets burned by somebody with good intentions." She paused. "They asked who I wanted as escort."

"Sensible. The roads are dangerous."

"The roads are fine. The roads are boring. I want dangerous and boring in the same package, and I'm told you come with both, plus a god who gives commentary."

*She is negotiating poorly,* Vael observed. *Ask for the archive stipend. She will fold immediately.*

Shut up, I told him affectionately, and out loud said: "Triple pay."

"You don't need pay. You live in a shrine."

"Sentimental value. Name your price and I'll counter outrageously."

She named it. I countered. We settled somewhere that made us both feel clever, shook hands formally like merchants, and then broke the handshake immediately because pretending we were professionals had never once survived contact with actual proximity.

Above us the stars were out, thick and ordinary, the kind of sky that meant nothing was tearing anywhere. The border hummed beneath our boots, patient and alive, stitched through with everything people had laid on that flat stone — salt and combs and apples, small true things, mortal memory doing the work a dead god's weight used to do.

Somewhere east, Corvane watched the dark so we wouldn't have to. Somewhere south, the archives waited. Somewhere beneath Ironhollow, my mother's echo kept its vigil in a place I'd visit again when the year turned — I could feel the appointment in my chest the way you feel a scar change with the weather, and I'd begun, cautiously, to think of it not as a debt but as a letter I hadn't finished writing.

But all of that was tomorrow's weather.

Tonight there was a lamp burning at a small shrine, and a fiddler playing badly, and a woman laughing quietly at my terrible negotiation, and a god murmuring at the edge of my hearing like a fire settling —

—and then Vael went still. All at once. The hearth-silence of him sharpening into attention, the border's hum shifting key beneath my feet, one note, low and wrong.

*Theo.* His voice was careful, the way a hand is careful near a burn. *Something has touched the western seam. Something small. Something that knows your mother's name.*

I looked west, toward the mountains, where the night lay black and unbroken.

"Well," I said. "There goes the boring."
