## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The valley had no name worth saying aloud. The maps called it the Hollow, and the people who lived there called it home, and Gideon Crane had spent nineteen years making sure nothing crossed its border that didn't belong.

He was on watch at the western rim when the first star went out.

He noticed it the way a man notices a missing tooth—by absence, by the wrongness of a sky he'd memorized. The star above the Split Horn peaks, the one old Marta used to navigate by, was simply gone. Not clouded. Not dimmed. Gone, as if someone had reached up with a wet thumb and smudged it from the dark.

Gideon stood very still and told himself it was cloud cover.

The wards hummed beneath his boots. He felt them the way he felt his own pulse, a low constant vibration he'd stopped hearing years ago because it never changed. Nineteen years of sameness. Nineteen years of the god's silence in his chest, that hollow place where Vessel-of-Rains had once spoken, sealed off since the day he'd sworn at the shrine and walked down into the valley to keep watch over people who would never know what they were being kept safe from.

The second star went out while he was still rationalizing the first.

Then a third, and a fourth, and Gideon's hand found the hilt of his knife without any decision being made about it. He watched the extinguishing move across the sky like a tide going out—stars winking away in a slow westward sweep, each one leaving behind not blackness but something worse: a pale, hungry gray, as if the night itself had been peeled back to show the bone underneath.

"No," he said, to no one. "No, no."

The wards screamed.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. A sound only he could hear, a frequency below hearing, a keening in the marrow of him—and then, one after another, like lanterns going out along a road, the wards failed. He felt each one die. Seven wards, seven deaths, each one a small implosion in the soles of his feet, and then silence where the humming had been. Silence so complete it rang.

For nineteen years he had believed the silence in his chest was grief. His god dead or gone, its voice spent, its mercenary left holding an oath with no one to enforce it. He had built a life on that belief. Watch rotations. Grain ledgers. A cottage with a leaky roof he kept meaning to fix. A sister who sang off-key in the kitchen and called him *brother-bear* when she wanted her way.

Wren.

The thought of her arrived with cold clarity, the way bad news does—as if it had been waiting politely outside the whole time.

*Magic cannot follow you here.* That was what the god had promised, all those years ago, when Gideon had asked why the oath required exile to this particular valley. *Nothing that hunts souls can cross these borders. You will be safe, and she will be safe, and that is the whole of the bargain.*

And now the stars were going out and the wards were dead and every instinct Gideon owned—the ones that had kept him alive through three wars before he ever swore anything to anyone—was telling him the bargain had just been called due.

* * *

He ran the rim trail east, toward the pass, because if anything came through it would come through there, and because running toward danger was the only discipline he'd ever fully mastered. The gray holes in the sky seemed to follow him, patient as eyes.

He smelled blood before he saw the courier.

The man lay crumpled against the ward-stone at the mouth of the pass—one of the old boundary markers, granite fangs worn smooth by centuries of weather that had never once turned violent until tonight. The courier was young, maybe twenty, dressed in travel grays with no livery, which meant either smuggler or messenger, and the arrow through his chest meant neither mattered much longer.

Gideon knelt. The boy's eyes found him, wide and glassy, and his lips moved.

"Don't talk." Gideon pressed a hand against the wound, knowing it was theater. The arrow had gone deep. "Who sent you?"

"Crane." The boy coughed pink froth. "You're Crane. Said... said I'd know you. Said you'd have the face of a man who doesn't know yet."

"Doesn't know what?" Gideon heard his own voice go flat and cold, the mercenary's voice, the voice that did its work before the heart could interfere. "Who sent you? From where?"

"Beneath." The boy's hand crawled across the frozen ground, fingers scrabbling at Gideon's wrist. "The court beneath the mountain. There's a contract. It has your name on it. It has—" Another cough, worse. "It has her name too. Wren. Wren Crane. There's a price listed. Gods, there's a *price*—"

The world went narrow and quiet, the way it did before violence. Gideon had felt this narrowing a hundred times, standing over men he was about to kill. He had never felt it standing over a dying boy who hadn't done anything but carry paper.

"Where is it," Gideon said. "The contract."

"Satchel. Under me. Take it. They said—" The boy grabbed his collar with sudden shocking strength, hauling himself half upright, blood on his teeth. "They said you'd want to read the terms. They said you've been paying for nineteen years without knowing what you bought."

*What you bought.*

The boy sagged. Gideon got the satchel out from under him—a plain leather case, wax-sealed, stamped with a sigil he didn't recognize: a circle broken by thirteen lines, like a moon cut into slices.

Inside was a single sheet of vellum, dense with script in two hands. One was beautiful, formal, inked in silver-gray. The other was cramped and hurried, added later, crowded into the margins like a beggar at a feast.

Gideon read the beautiful hand first.

*Contract of Purchase and Perpetuity, entered between the Undercourt of the Mountain Deep and the party designated Vessel-of-Rains, silent god, holder of one (1) mortal soul pledged as surety—*

His eyes slid to the margin. To the cramped hand. To the words written there in ink that looked brown rather than black, though he suspected it had never been brown at all.

*Soul designated: CRANE, WREN.*
*Valuation: paid in full, nineteen years past.*
*Collateral held: one (1) oath-bound servant, Gideon Crane, whose vigil constitutes interest accrued.*
*Terms of renewal: upon failure of the border wards, the debt reverts to principal. Collection to commence at the thirteenth turn of the moon.*

Nineteen years. Nineteen years of watch fires and grain ledgers and a leaky roof, and it had all been *interest*. Every dawn he'd stood guard, every night he'd walked the rim, he hadn't been protecting the valley. He'd been servicing a debt. The safe world wasn't safe. It was a cage, and he had been the lock, turning faithfully on himself.

And Wren—Wren, who had been nine years old when they came to the valley, who didn't remember their mother's face, who thought the wards were weather—

Her soul had a *price*. Someone had appraised it. Someone had held it in collateral for nineteen years while she grew up singing off-key in a kitchen, and now the note had come due.

The courier made a sound. Gideon looked down. The boy was watching him read, and there was something in his expression that wasn't fear anymore. Pity, maybe. Or recognition—the look of one doomed man seeing another.

"They said you'd understand," the boy whispered. "They said to tell you: the god didn't sell her. The god *bought* her. Once already. And everything bought can be..." His breath rattled. "...repossessed."

"The god bought her," Gideon repeated. The words made no sense. They went through him like cold water. "Vessel-of-Rains is dead. Its voice died the day I swore."

The boy smiled. It was the worst thing Gideon had seen all night, and he had watched the stars get eaten.

"That's what it wanted everyone to think," the boy said. "That's what it cost."

He died between one breath and the next, quietly, as if stepping out of a room.

Gideon stayed kneeling in the frost for a long time. Above him the sky was mostly gray holes now, a constellation of absences, and somewhere beyond the pass, beyond the mountains, whatever had extinguished them was still moving westward, unhurried, certain.

Thirteen turns of the moon. He counted without meaning to, the way he counted everything—arrows, paces, enemies. Thirteen turns was one season. One season to find a mountain he'd spent nineteen years pretending didn't exist, walk into a court that traded in souls, and buy back something that had never legally been theirs to hold.

Or watch them come collect.

He folded the contract into his coat, over his heart, where it sat like a second knife. Then he stood, took the boy's body gently by the shoulders, and laid him straight, arms at his sides, facing the ruined sky. It was all the burial he had time for.

Down in the valley, Wren's window was lit. He could see the small gold square of it from here, could imagine her inside—mending, probably, or reading, or humming that tune she always got wrong in the same place. Nineteen years of watching that light and believing it meant safety.

Gideon turned his back on it and faced the pass, and the dark, and the mountain he could just make out on the horizon, a jagged black tooth against the bone-gray sky.

The god had lied to him. Or the god had sacrificed for him. He wouldn't know which until he found out why a silent god had spent its voice buying back a child's soul—and what it had gotten in return that was worth so much silence.

One season. Thirteen moons.

He started walking.
