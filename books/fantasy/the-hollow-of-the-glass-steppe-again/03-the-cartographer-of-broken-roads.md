## 3. The Cartographer of Broken Roads

Sefa Venn had three rules, and the first was never to open the door after dark.

The knock came at midnight anyway. She knew who it would be before she touched the latch—had known since noon, when a boy with coins too new for her street told her a man from the valley was asking after maps of the steppe. Nobody asked after maps of the steppe. The steppe was where maps went to lie about themselves.

She opened the door on the third knock because the second rule of Sefa Venn's life was that she had never once followed her own rules when it mattered.

He stood in the rain with his hood back, letting it hit him like he hadn't noticed it was there. Big man. Valley-built, shoulders rounded from ducking under things that weren't low anymore. He held a rolled contract in one hand and a dead courier's seal in the other, and his eyes did something she recognized immediately: they kept flicking to her hands.

People who looked at your hands were people who'd learned that hands told you what mouths wouldn't.

"You're Sefa Venn," he said. Not a question. "You charted the steppe."

"Once." She didn't step aside. "The charts burned. All of them. Official record."

"I know. I read the official record." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's why I'm here."

Rain ran off the eaves between them in a thin silver rope. Behind him, the night was wrong—the sky over the valley rim still starless, a black so complete it made the rooftops look cut out of paper. Three days now. In the taverns they said it was an omen. In the temples they said nothing at all, which frightened Sefa more than the omen did.

"The stars went out over the valley," she said. "And you came to me for a map."

"I came to you because you're the only person alive who's crossed the glass steppe and come back to write about it."

"I came back," Sefa said, "because I sold something to come back. Everything else I left out there."

His eyes stopped moving. Settled on her face for the first time, and stayed.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in four days," he said.

"It'll cost you extra."

* * *

She let him in eventually, because the rain was turning to sleet and because—she admitted it later, in the dark, to no one—because she wanted to look at him longer than a doorstep allowed. There was a weight on this man the way there was weight on drowned things. She'd spent six years learning to recognize what people carried, since carrying things was all she did herself.

He sat at her worktable without being asked, which annoyed her, and set the contract down flat, which intrigued her. It was written on vellum gone yellow as old teeth. The Undercourt's seal sat in its corner like a spider in a web: the mountain, the ninefold circle, the little crown drawn upside down because down was up down there.

"Gideon Crane," she read aloud. "Bound servant of Vessel-of-Rains." She looked up. "You're oathbound to a god."

"I was bound to a silent god." His jaw worked. "Turns out silence was the cage."

Sefa had heard the stories by then; everyone along the valley's edge had, passed mouth to mouth faster than plague. The wards failing. A courier found dying on the salt road with his throat opened by something that left no wound. A contract bearing names.

"And Wren Crane." She traced the second name. "Your sister."

"My sister."

Something in how he said it. Sefa had charted coastlines for men who spoke of drowned wives in exactly that voice—flat, load-bearing, the voice of a man standing on a word because if he stepped off it everything fell.

"What do you want from me, Gideon Crane?"

"A route across the steppe. The real one. Not the pilgrim road, not the salt traders' line. The way through that doesn't exist on any map still in print."

"There's a reason for that." She pulled the lamp closer. "The steppe isn't land. It's memory that got tired of being carried and lay down. Glass over salt over older glass. Roads appear where enough people remember walking them, and close when they're forgotten. You can't map it. I tried. I mapped it anyway, because I was young and stupid and the Royal Cartographic Society pays by the league."

"And?"

"And the Undercourt bought my survey." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, smooth as water through a crack, and she hated herself for the smoothness. "Twelve years ago. A man with a face like a closed door paid me in memories instead of coin. Clean ones. My mother's voice, my first snowfall, the smell of my grandmother's house. I took them because I was starving and because memories seemed like nothing. You don't miss what you can't remember missing."

Gideon Crane said nothing. The lamp hissed. Outside, the sleet ticked against the shutters like small patient fingernails.

"So the court beneath the mountain has known every safe crossing for twelve years," he said finally.

"They've known every crossing, safe or not. They've moved their roads. Closed mine. I couldn't sell the same route twice even if I wanted to, which—" she laughed, and it came out ugly, "—which is a mercy, maybe. The one thing I have left to sell is the fact that I know how the steppe thinks. How it forgets. How to walk it while it's forgetting you."

"And you're telling me this because?"

Because he hadn't flinched. Because he'd listened to her confession the way a man listens to weather—not forgiving it, not damning it, just marking where it stood. Because in twelve years no one had ever heard *sold* and not reached for a word like *traitor* or *fool*, and this man had simply waited for the rest.

"Because you looked at my hands," she said. "When I opened the door. People who check hands have done things with theirs they're still paying for. I figured we could skip the part where we pretend otherwise."

* * *

They talked until the sleet stopped and the grey came up, and what they built between them wasn't trust—trust was a luxury good and Sefa had been broke for years—but something more honest. An audit. He showed her the contract, the soul-price, the thirteenth moon written into the margin like a deadline in a merchant's ledger. She showed him the fragments of her old survey that survived in her head, the ones the Undercourt couldn't repossess because they'd never been written down: the way the glass sang before a road closed, the direction water pooled uphill, the shrine-shaped hollow where the steppe forgot to be cruel for half a mile at a time.

"You'll guide me," he said, near dawn. Not asking.

"For a price." She'd thought about it all night, turning prices over like stones. "Not coin. When you get your sister's soul back—and I say *when*, Crane, because a man who crosses the steppe for family doesn't stop at *if*—when you get it back, I want you to take me to the court's memory-markets. I want to buy back what I sold."

"You know what they'll charge."

"I know exactly what they'll charge." She held up her hands, turned them over. Mapmaker's hands, ink-stained, steady. "I've had twelve years to price myself. Whatever it costs, it costs less than carrying this."

He studied her a long moment, and she watched him decide, and what decided it—she was almost sure—wasn't need but recognition. Two debtors, shaking hands across a table neither of them owned.

"All right," he said.

"One condition of mine," she added, shrugging into her oilskin, checking the buckles out of habit. "We leave within the hour. The steppe is forgetting faster than usual—I can feel it from here, the roads closing like eyes—and whatever put those stars out is going to make every crossing harder by the day. Also, the boy who brought your message? He was paid in coin minted this year, fresh-stamped, from the mountain mint."

Gideon went very still. "The Undercourt knows I'm looking for a guide."

"The Undercourt knew before you did." Sefa blew out the lamp. Grey light filled the room, the color of the steppe itself, and she felt the old pull in her chest, terrible and familiar, like a hook behind the sternum. Twelve years she'd sworn never to walk that glass again. Twelve years she'd dreamed of it anyway.

"Then let's not keep them waiting," she said, and opened the door onto a world with no stars in it, and led the way out.
