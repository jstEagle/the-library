## 4. Salt and Thirteenth Moons

The salt flats began where the steppe's glass gave up pretending to be anything but a wound. For two days we walked on ground that crunched like old bone, and the sky above us stayed starless — not clouded, just *empty*, the way a room is empty after the furniture has been carried out. Sefa navigated by cracks in the salt, reading them like script. She hadn't spoken much since the shrine. Neither had I.

The silence broke on the third morning, when we found the white tent.

It stood alone in the middle of the flats, absurd as a tooth in an open palm, its canvas so clean it hurt to look at. No tracks led to it. No tracks led away. Around it, in a perfect circle thirty paces wide, the salt had been swept smooth as a temple floor.

Sefa stopped walking so abruptly I nearly passed her. "That wasn't there when I charted this route."

"When did you chart it?"

"Eleven years ago." Her jaw worked. "Nothing that clean survives eleven years out here."

I put my hand on the axe at my hip out of habit more than sense. An axe against whatever bought a tent like that would be about as useful as a prayer, and I'd already learned what my prayers were worth. But you hold what you have.

The tent flap opened before we reached it.

The man who stepped out was dressed in white robes gone gray at the hems, the way all white things go eventually. He was older than me by a decade or more, lean as a salt-crusted post, with a face weathered into something almost gentle. Around his neck hung a medallion: a circle bisected by a vertical line, polished bright.

I knew the symbol. Everyone from the valley knew it, the way you know the mark of a rival house or a dangerous dog. The Circle-and-Blade. The sign of the Sundered Choir, god of judgment, of accounts settled, of debts called in at the worst possible hour.

"Pilgrims," the man said warmly, "or corpses-to-be. Either way, come in out of the glare."

"We're not pilgrims," Sefa said.

"No one crossing the salt is a pilgrim." He smiled. "Pilgrimage implies hope. Come in anyway."

* * *

Inside, the tent was larger than the outside allowed. I'd seen that trick once before, in the shrine of Vessel-of-Rains, and it made my skin crawl both times for the same reason: space that lies to you belongs to something that lies to you.

The priest — he introduced himself as Ovid, offered no surname, and his eyes said none would be given — poured three cups of something dark and steaming from a pot that never seemed to empty. The tent held a table, three chairs, a hanging lamp with no visible oil. Everything was clean. Everything was precise. It looked less like a camp than like a courtroom waiting for a case.

"You're far from any choir-house," I said.

"The Choir has no houses anymore. We have *positions*." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Mine happens to be here, on the one road every soul bound for the Undercourt must walk. Do you know why the gods fought over the valley, Gideon Crane?"

My hand tightened around the cup. "I didn't give you my name."

"You didn't have to. Your name arrived ahead of you." He sipped. "When the wards fell, everything sealed inside them became legible again. Every oath, every contract, every soul-price written in the dark of the last hundred years — the Choir read them all in a single night. Yours was among the more interesting entries. A mercenary bound to Vessel-of-Rains. A sister sold as surety. A debt coming due beneath the mountain on the thirteenth turn."

Sefa had gone very still beside me. I understood why. If the Choir could read our contract from a hundred leagues away, then so could the thing we were walking toward.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Want is such a small word." Ovid set down his cup and folded his hands, and for a moment the gentleness fell away like a mask slipping, and underneath was something older and harder and terribly patient. "What I want is justice. What I'm offering is passage."

"I have passage. My feet work."

"Your feet will work until the Salt Warden finds you. You've heard of the Warden? Of course not — the valley kept even its dangers illiterate. It is the Undercourt's collector, and it walks these flats, and it smells contracts the way sharks smell blood. Yours is bleeding all over the steppe, Crane. Alone, you'll be found by nightfall. With my escort—" he tapped the medallion at his throat, "—you are Choir business, and even the Warden hesitates to collect from us. We are, after all, in the same trade."

"And the price?"

He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which made it worse than if it hadn't. "A small thing. A word, really. When you stand before the regent Ilvane — and you will stand before her, that much is written — you kill her."

* * *

The lamp flame didn't flicker. That was the strangest part. Somewhere outside, wind was dragging across the salt, and inside the lying tent the flame stood straight as a nail.

"Say it plainly," I said.

"I am saying it plainly. Kill her. One life, freely taken, and the Choir calls your debt paid, your passage blessed, and your sister's freedom guaranteed by covenant." He spread his hands. "You wanted justice, don't you? Here it is, gift-wrapped. Ilvane built the thirteenth moon. She imprisoned your god. She holds your sister's soul in fragments and calls it administration. The world would be cleaner without her, and you would be free. All I ask is that you be the instrument. Consider it — what did your god call it? — *sanctified employment*."

Beside me, Sefa made a small sound. Not quite a laugh. "You rehearsed that."

"I rehearse everything. It's how I honor my god." Ovid turned to her, and his voice softened into something almost kind. "You know about instruments, cartographer. You were one, once. Maps are just weapons that point instead of cut."

Her face went white. I filed that away — another debt she owed, another door she kept locked — and turned back to the priest.

"Here's my problem," I said. "I was a weapon for twenty years. I killed for coin, then for a god, then for a valley that never learned my name. And every time, someone told me the killing meant something. Justice. Duty. Contract." I set the cup down carefully, because my hands wanted to throw it. "Every single time, the meaning belonged to someone else. The killing was always mine."

Ovid watched me with the patience of a man who has heard refusals before and collected most of them later.

"So no," I said. "I won't swear it. Not for passage, not for my sister, not for a guarantee stamped with your god's bright little circle. If Ilvane dies, it will be because I chose it in front of her, with my own reasons, at my own cost. Not because a priest in a clean tent told me the world would thank me."

"A noble speech." Ovid rose, unhurried, and began gathering cups that needed no gathering. "Nobility is expensive, Crane. You're paying for it in hours now. The Warden moves faster at night."

"Then we'd better start walking."

"One courtesy before you go." He produced a folded square of white cloth and laid it on the table between us. On it, drawn in salt, was a sigil I recognized from the shrine — the mark of Vessel-of-Rains, the falling drop inside the open vessel. Except this version was crossed through with a blade.

"My god's account of your god," Ovid said quietly, "ends with a question mark. Vessel-of-Rains did not simply lose its voice, mercenary. It spent it. Twice now, by my count. Gods don't impoverish themselves for strangers." His eyes lifted to mine, and for the first time all day they looked genuinely curious rather than merely patient. "Ask yourself what it saw in your family that was worth going mute for. Then ask whether the Undercourt ever really owned what it sold you back."

We took the cloth. I don't fully know why. Maybe because it was the first piece of my god I'd been handed by anyone who wasn't dead.

* * *

Outside, the light had begun to fail, and the emptiness where stars should have been felt suddenly very large. Sefa walked fast, arms wrapped around herself, and I let her get twenty paces ahead before I caught up.

"He knew about the maps," she said, without looking at me.

"He knows a lot."

"That's what terrifies me." She stopped, finally, and faced me, and the honesty in her face cost her visibly. "Gideon. If he read our contract, he read mine too. Everything I sold the Undercourt — the routes, the passes, the ways *in* — it's all in their ledger. Which means when we arrive, they won't see strangers. They'll see a delivery arriving exactly on schedule." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I need you to hear it from me before you hear it from them."

I looked at her a long moment. Behind her, far across the salt, something vast had begun to move — a shape low against the dying light, patient as arithmetic, kicking up no dust at all.

The Warden, right on schedule.

"Walk," I said. "Tell me while we run."
