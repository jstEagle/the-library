## 5. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The glass caves began where the salt ended, a seam in the steppe like a wound that had healed wrong. Sefa had charted them twenty years ago, when she was young and hungry and still believed maps were neutral things. She had drawn every passage, every chamber, every throat of stone that sang when the wind moved through it. She had sold the drawings for enough coin to eat through one winter, and she had told herself, then, that the Undercourt would never use them. That knowledge was only knowledge.

Now she walked her own map into the dark, and the dark knew her name.

"Left at the singing throat," she said, and Gideon obeyed without question, which made it worse. He trusted the guide she'd claimed to be. The lantern in his hand threw their shadows long across walls of smoked glass, amber and black, and somewhere below them water dripped in a rhythm too slow to be natural—counting something, Sefa thought. Counting down.

Ovid's priest had let them go at the flats' edge with a blessing neither of them wanted. *Kill the regent,* he'd said, *and your god's debt dies with her.* Gideon had refused, jaw tight, and Sefa had watched him refuse and thought: here is a man who has already been someone's weapon once and will not be again. She envied him that clarity. Her own hands were muddier.

They descended for hours. The glass thickened underfoot until it rang like a bell with each step, and the air grew warm and sweet, the smell of candlewax and old paper—the smell, Sefa realized with a lurch, of archives. Of ledgers. The Undercourt kept accounts the way other courts kept prisoners.

"You've been quiet," Gideon said finally. "Since the flats."

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

She almost told him then. Almost. But the passage opened ahead into the first antechamber, and there were doors, and doors meant clerks, and clerks meant questions asked in voices designed to make you answer honestly whether you wished to or not. She held her confession like a coin clenched in a fist.

* * *

The antechamber was carved from a single vein of blue glass, and it was full of people.

Not courtiers—Sefa had expected courtiers, silk and menace. These were petitioners. Farmers, soldiers, a woman holding a child who could not have been more than six, all of them waiting in lines before brass grilles set into the wall, speaking to clerks whose faces never changed. And everywhere, on every surface, small brass scales. Some held grains of sand. Some held nothing at all, and the emptiness of them seemed heavier than the weight.

A clerk looked up as they passed. "Business?"

"Gideon Crane," Gideon said. "I'm told I hold a contract."

The clerk consulted a ledger the size of a paving stone. "Crane. Yes. Outstanding balance: one soul, female, taken against a vow of silence." The clerk's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "You've come to pay."

"I've come to settle."

"Different words." The clerk stamped something Sefa couldn't see. "The Court will hear you at the turn of the watch. Until then, you are guests. Guests do not lie in the Undercourt. It is—" the clerk paused, savoring it, "—frowned upon."

Sefa felt the words land in her chest like a dropped stone. *Guests do not lie.* She had been composing her confession for two days, rationing it, choosing the moment. The moment was being taken from her.

They were given a cell—not a cell, a *room*, the clerk insisted, with a door that locked from outside—and left alone for the first time since the flats.

Gideon sat on the stone bench and rubbed his eyes. "That clerk knew my name before I gave it."

"They know everyone's name. Names are collateral here."

"How do you know that?"

And there it was. The question she'd been dreading, arriving not from a clerk but from the one person whose opinion of her she couldn't afford to lose. Sefa stood by the door with her back to him and heard herself say, lightly, lying badly: "Everyone knows that. It's common knowledge."

"It isn't," Gideon said. "I've lived my whole life one valley away from this place and I never heard it."

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't have."

He waited. He was good at waiting; she'd learned that on the steppe, watching him stand motionless through storms. He waited now, and the silence filled the room like water rising, and Sefa understood that he was not going to ask again. He was going to let her hang herself, gently, patiently, and she was going to do it, because the alternative was to keep carrying it down into the court itself, where it would be dragged out of her anyway, publicly, at the worst possible moment.

She turned around.

"I need to tell you something before we go any further," she said. "And you're going to want to leave me here, and I'm asking you not to decide yet."

* * *

She told him standing up, because sitting felt like permission to be forgiven.

"Twenty years ago I mapped these caves. All of them. Every passage, every chamber, the approaches, the weaknesses in the ventilation, the places where the glass is thin enough to break through. I was twenty-three and starving and I told myself it was just information." Her voice stayed level, which surprised her. "Then I sold it. To the Court. For food and a year's lodging and a new set of instruments. They used my charts to seal three escape routes that prisoners had dug over forty years. People died in the sealing. I don't know how many. The Court doesn't publish its arithmetic."

Gideon didn't move. That was the terrible part—she'd braced for anger, and anger would have been easier. He just looked at her with those gray eyes that had watched stars go out without flinching, and said nothing, and the nothing went on so long she started talking again just to fill it.

"The route we took today is mine. Every turn I've called out since the salt flats—I wasn't guiding you, Gideon. I was walking you down a corridor I built for them. If they wanted to trap us, they wouldn't need to try. They'd just consult me."

"And do they still consult you?" His voice was quiet. Not cold. Quiet, the way deep water is quiet.

"I don't know. That's what frightens me. My debts might be paid. Or they might be—" she gestured vaguely at the ledger-weighted air, "—held. Accruing. This place holds things. You've seen the scales."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because they're going to tell you first." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Don't you see? *Guests do not lie.* The moment we stand in that court, some clerk with a beautiful voice is going to open a book and read my name and my crime aloud, and they'll do it beautifully, and you'll hear it from them instead of from me, and I couldn't—" She stopped. Breathed. "I couldn't survive you hearing it secondhand. That's the truth. Not noble. Just true."

Gideon stood. He crossed the room slowly, the way he did everything, and stopped an arm's length away.

"On the steppe," he said, "you steered us around a sink of thin ice you never mentioned. I saw you do it. I said nothing."

"That's not the same."

"No. Yours is worse. Mine was instinct; yours was a choice you've carried for twenty years." He looked at her, and then he did the thing she hadn't braced for at all. He nodded. "But you told me before they could take it from you. In this place, that's worth more than a clean record. A clean record is just a debt nobody's found yet."

Sefa's legs nearly went. Twenty years of shame, and he'd weighed it in four sentences and found it survivable.

"So you trust me still?"

"I trust the version of you that confessed," he said. "Which is the only version that's ever told me the whole truth. Come on. We have a court to face."

* * *

The courtroom was worse than she'd imagined, and she had imagined it well—she'd drawn it once, from hearsay, in the margins of an old chart, and even her imagination had flinched.

It was a hollow sphere of green glass, big as a cathedral, and the benches rose in tiers like the inside of a throat. At the center, suspended in nothing, hung a single scale the height of three men, its pans wide as rowboats. One pan held a heap of grey sand. The other held nothing. The empty pan hovered slightly higher, straining upward, and the whole apparatus turned slowly, imperceptibly, like something dreaming.

The clerks sat in crescents. Above them, on a dais of black glass, sat the Bench: three figures in robes the color of dried blood, faces smooth as eggshells, no eyes that Sefa could find no matter how long she looked. They did not speak. When they wished to communicate, a clerk spoke for them, and the clerk's voice came out layered, three voices braided into one.

Gideon's case was called first. *Crane versus the Estate of Vessel-of-Rains*, the braid-voice announced, and Sefa's stomach dropped, because of course his own god was listed as the opposing party. Of course. Here, even gods were accounts.

But the proceedings blurred past—clerks reciting the oath, the soul-price, the thirteen turns of the moon—and Sefa barely heard them, because halfway through, the braid-voice paused, drew breath, and said:

"A matter ancillary to the case. The Court calls Sefa Vane, cartographer, to answer outstanding obligations."

The room turned. Not metaphorically—the benches physically rotated, tier by tier, until every seated petitioner faced her, and Sefa stood alone on the floor of green glass with two decades of buried sin rising up through the soles of her feet.

"Sefa Vane," the voice said, gentle as a lullaby, "in the year of the Salt Rebellion, you sold to this Court a complete survey of the glass caves, including three routes of prisoner excavation. The sealing of these routes resulted in forty-one deaths. Your fee was twelve silver pieces and lodging." A pause, delicately weighted. "The Court notes that the debt of twelve silver pieces was discharged. The Court notes further that the debt of forty-one lives remains open."

Somewhere behind her, Gideon shifted his weight. She didn't look back. She couldn't.

"What—" Her voice cracked. She started again. "What does the Court require?"

"Nothing tonight," the voice said, almost kindly. "The obligation is noted, entered, and preserved. Debts in this Court are not punishments, cartographer. They are *assets*. Yours may yet prove useful to us. Or to someone." The three-faced Bench tilted, all together, like birds regarding a worm. "We mention it only because honesty is the law of this room. And because the mercenary beside you deserves to know whom he walks with."

The room rotated back. The case resumed. Sefa stood through the rest of it feeling skinned, flayed open under green light while a hundred strangers rearranged their benches to get a better look—but underneath the humiliation, something else was happening. Gideon had known. He'd known since the cell, and he had stood beside her anyway, and when the braid-voice finished reading her sins he did not step away from her. He stepped *closer*. Half a pace. Enough that their shoulders touched.

Later, in the corridor outside, he said only: "Forty-one."

"I know."

"When this is done—if we walk out of here—those names go on a stone somewhere. We'll find them out. All of them."

"You can't resurrect anyone."

"No," Gideon said. "But you can stop pretending they were twelve silver pieces' worth of nothing."

They walked toward the guest quarters, and the green light faded behind them, and Sefa thought the worst was over. She had confessed; she had been exposed; she had survived both. Whatever came next, she'd face it with the truth already spent.

Then a clerk materialized at her elbow, soft-footed as dust, and pressed a folded paper into her hand.

"For the cartographer," it said. "From an admirer of your early work."

She opened it with shaking fingers. Inside was a single line, in a hand she recognized instantly, because she had invented the notation herself, twenty years ago:

*The sealed routes can be unsealed. For a price. Ask about the forty-one.*

Sefa stared at it until the letters swam. Someone down here was offering her absolution, priced and itemized. Someone down here knew exactly what she'd pay.

Behind her, Gideon was saying something about getting sleep before the hearing. Ahead, the corridor bent toward the cells, and the paper in her hand felt warm, alive, like a tongue.

She had one night to decide what she was willing to become to unseal a grave.
