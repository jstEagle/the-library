## 6. Wren, As She Is Now

The regent's household kept its servants in a gallery of white alcoves cut into the mountain's living bone, and Gideon found her on the third night, by accident, or by whatever the god's silence had become instead of accident.

He had been following Sefa through the memory-markets — she moved through them like a woman revisiting a crime scene, which she was — and when she stopped to argue with a broker over the price of a childhood, he drifted. That was all. He drifted down a side corridor where the glass in the walls ran thin as fingernails and the light came up from below, amber and slow, like honey held to a flame.

She was carrying a tray of candles. Unlit ones. She walked with the particular care of someone whose body belonged to an oath: each step placed, each turn of the shoulder measured, as if some invisible ledger tallied her movements and charged interest on waste.

He knew her before he saw her face. He knew her from behind, from the set of her shoulders, from the way she tucked a stray hair back with her little finger because her hands were full. Twenty years since he'd seen her do that. Twenty years since anyone did it.

"Wren," he said.

The tray did not waver. That was the first terrible thing — not that she turned, but how she turned: smoothly, completely, without surprise, the way a door turns on oiled hinges.

"You're standing in the service corridor," she said. "Guests aren't permitted here after the second bell."

Her voice was hers. Older, sanded smooth at the edges, but hers. Her face was hers too, and it wasn't — the girl who'd run laughing through the valley orchards had been replaced by a woman with still water where her eyes used to be, and Gideon understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that the stillness was not calm. It was depth. She was holding something enormous very carefully, the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room.

"It's me," he said. Stupidly. As if names worked down here, where everything was currency. "Wren. It's Gideon."

"I know who you are." Something flickered behind the stillness — a fish under ice. "You're the one they've been talking about. The mercenary with the drowned god. The thirteenth moon is for you." She said it the way she might have said *the soup is for the table*. "You shouldn't be here. If the steward finds you in the service corridor—"

"Wren." He stepped closer and she stepped back, precise, and the tray stayed level. "I came to take you home."

That was when she laughed.

It was a small laugh, almost polite, and it was the worst sound he had ever heard, because there was nothing broken in it. Nothing straining. She laughed the way a person laughs at a kind stranger's mistake.

"Home," she said. "The valley."

"The valley. The orchard. The house is still standing — I keep it standing. Your room's the same. I couldn't—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated the crack, hated that his first words to his sister in twenty years were breaking apart like wet paper. "I kept everything."

"I know you did." And there — there — something moved in her face, quick and painful, gone again. "I've always known. It was in the contract. The god made them show me. Every year on the anniversary, they showed me the house, the valley, you on watch at the border with your spear and your grief." She tilted her head. "Did you think I didn't watch you back?"

* * *

They sat in an alcove off the corridor because she could not leave it — literally could not; when he suggested walking, her whole body seized like a hand closing, and she went gray, and he never suggested it again. So they sat among the unlit candles while she told him, in the level voice of a servant reciting inventory, what she was now.

"They bought my soul," she said. "But souls don't come whole. Mine came apart into pieces, and each piece became an oath. I can't lie. I can't abandon a task unfinished. I can't harm a member of the court. I can't refuse the regent's household work." She touched the candle tray. "There are eleven. I could recite them all. Sometimes I do, at night, just to feel the shape of myself."

"And if you break one?"

"I don't break them." A pause. "Once, early, I tried to say something untrue. Just a small thing. I was so tired of being honest." Her fingers whitened on the tray's edge. "It was like swallowing glass. The oath isn't a rule, Gideon. It's a wall inside me. I go toward breaking it and the wall is just *there*, and I stop, and then I keep going about my day. You learn to live around walls."

He reached for her hand. She let him take it, and her skin was cold in the way the caves were cold, and he held on like a drowning man holds driftwood, and she watched him hold on with something close to pity.

"I spent twenty years thinking you were suffering," he said.

"I was. At first." She said it gently, the way you tell someone their dog died. "The first decade was terrible. But Gideon — listen to me, really listen — the valley was worse. Do you remember what I was there?"

He remembered. Of course he remembered. Wren at nineteen, brilliant and restless, mending fences badly because her mind was elsewhere, asking the valley questions the valley had no answers for. What's past the steppe. Why does magic die at the border. What am I *for*. The valley had answered with silence, and she had begun to answer herself, in ways that frightened both of them.

"You were going to leave," he said. "Before the oath. Before the debt. You were going to walk out onto the salt and keep going."

"I was going to leave," she agreed. "And it would have killed me, or worse, it wouldn't have. And instead the god sold me here, and here—" she looked down the corridor, at the amber light rising through the glass, at the vast slow pulse of the mountain, "—here I have work. Real work. I keep the regent's candles. Do you know what the candles are? They're memories, pressed into wax. Every soul that passes through this court leaves a residue, and someone has to carry it, sort it, shelve it. I'm good at it. I'm *needed* at it. There are four hundred people in this household and every one of them would notice if I stopped."

"The valley needed you too."

"No." No anger in it. Just fact. "The valley tolerated me. You loved me, but love isn't a task. Here, every morning I wake up and I know exactly what I am. Eleven oaths. Four hundred people. One regent." She finally looked at him fully, and he saw the fish under the ice again, closer to the surface. "You crossed the steppe to rescue a prisoner. I need you to understand that you found a woman with a job."

* * *

He argued. Of course he argued. He laid out the stars going out, the wards failing, the god's testament, the thirteenth moon — and at the moon her face changed for the first time, a real change, fear moving through the still water like a shadow of a bird.

"You should not have come," she said quietly. "Whatever you're planning. Whatever bargain. The court doesn't lose bargains, Gideon. It's older than losing."

"I'm not leaving without you."

"You are." She set down the tray — the first imprecise thing she'd done, and he understood it cost her something, some small fine levied against her soul for setting it down before the task was done — and took both his hands. Hers were steady. His were not. "Because I won't come. And you can't carry me, and you can't trick me, because I can't lie but you can't either, not to me, not really. Ask me. Ask me if I want to leave. Say it plainly, the way you'd say it to anyone, and I'll answer plainly, and then you'll know."

"Don't make me—"

"Ask me."

The corridor hummed. Somewhere far above, the false moon turned its thirteenth notch toward completion. He looked at his sister — at the woman carved out of his sister, polished and purposeful and walled — and asked.

"Do you want to leave? Do you want to come home?"

"No," said Wren.

No flinch. No flicker. The word came out clean as a struck bell, and the wall inside her stood exactly where it stood, and she was free to mean it, and she meant it.

"There," she said softly. "Now you know. Now you can go."

He didn't go. He sat there while she picked the tray back up and resumed her rounds, candle by candle, alcove by alcove, and he followed at a distance like a ghost attending its own funeral, until a bell rang somewhere and servants began to flow toward the great hall, and Wren paused beside him long enough to say, low:

"The regent will want to see you now. She always knows when someone's decided something. It's her gift." A beat. "Gideon — whatever she offers you, remember that I chose this. Remember that I'm happy. And remember that I have never once been able to say anything untrue since I got here."

Then she was gone into the flow of bodies, straight-backed, tray level, needed.

And from the far end of the hall, seated on a throne of layered glass with the thirteenth moon turning slowly behind her like a clock face made of night, Regent Ilvane beckoned him forward with two fingers, smiling like a woman who has already counted the price of everything in the room.

"Mercenary," she said. "Come and tell me what my servant refused to ask you for."
