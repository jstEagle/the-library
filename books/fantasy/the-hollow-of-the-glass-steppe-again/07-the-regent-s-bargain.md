## 7. The Regent's Bargain

Ilvane had worn many faces in her long tenure beneath the mountain, but the one she wore tonight was her favorite: the face of a tired woman doing arithmetic.

She sat behind a table of black glass with three objects on it — a ledger bound in rain-colored leather, a candle that burned without heat, and a small brass clock with thirteen hands. She did not look up when they brought Gideon Crane into the audience chamber. Let him stand. Let him wait. Waiting was the first lesson of the Undercourt: everything here was bought with time, and time was the only currency no one could counterfeit.

The chamber itself did the rest of the work. It always did. The walls were veined glass, and through them moved the slow colored light of memories being spent somewhere below — grief-blue, joy-gold, the sick green of guilt. Visitors saw their own worst hours flickering in the stone and assumed the court was showing them. It wasn't. The court never showed anyone anything. People simply brought their horrors with them, and the glass was honest.

"Sit," she said at last, and looked up.

He was exactly what the contract had promised. A big man gone lean from the crossing, salt still crusted in the seams of his coat, eyes like a man who had stopped sleeping on principle. Behind him, the mapmaker Sefa hovered at the edge of the light, and Ilvane noted with mild pleasure that the woman would not meet her gaze. Good. Old debts kept people honest.

"You crossed my steppe," Ilvane said. "Four hundred miles of glass and salt, past Ovid's little crusade, down through caves that have eaten better guides than yours. All for a soul." She turned the ledger toward him. "I confess I find it touching. I find most mortal devotion touching, the way I find weather touching. It happens to other people."

"You know why I'm here," Gideon said.

"I know what you *think* you're here for." She opened the ledger to a page marked with a ribbon the color of dried blood. "Wren Crane. Sold by oath-proxy, price paid in full by the god Vessel-of-Rains, term of indenture renewed upon default. Default date: the thirteenth turn of this moon." She let him read it upside down, because watching people read their own ruin was one of the few pleasures left to her. "You've come to buy her back. Or steal her. Or die loudly trying. They all arrive believing they're the first to try any of those things."

"And how do they end?"

"Quietly," Ilvane said. "Everyone ends quietly here. That's rather the point of the place."

* * *

She rose and walked the length of the table, unhurried, letting her shadow fall across him. In the Undercourt, shadows were not empty. Hers carried weight; she felt it drag along the floor behind her like a hem.

"Do you know what this is?" She picked up the brass clock and set it in front of him. Thirteen hands, each etched with a different name. Twelve of them had swept their full circles over the centuries. The thirteenth hand, the newest, trembled now against its final notch.

Gideon didn't touch it. Careful man. "A clock."

"A *clock*," she agreed, "is a machine for making gods answer questions. Your Vessel-of-Rains understood that before it lost its voice. Why do you think it traded everything it had? Not mercy. Not love. It saw this—" she tapped the thirteenth hand, "—and it knew what was coming, and it tried to buy its way out of the question." Her mouth curved. "It failed. Debts compound here, mercenary. Even divine ones. Especially divine ones."

"The moon," he said slowly. "The thirteenth turn. You built it."

"I built it four hundred years ago, when I was something else and had a name worth remembering." She let a fraction of the old self show through her face, just enough to make him flinch. "Every god in your sky made a bargain with this court once — silence for safety, worship for exemption, small print for eternity. And every one of them assumed the court would simply wait forever, patient as stone. But stone is patient. I am not. So I set a clock. When the thirteenth hand completes its circle, every contract ever written beneath this mountain comes due at once. Every god must appear. Every debt must be named. Every soul held in pledge must be either claimed or forfeit." She folded her hands. "Including your sister's. Including your god's."

"You're forcing an apocalypse over bookkeeping."

"I'm forcing an *answer*," Ilvane said, and heard the steel under the silk and did not soften it. "Your gods took everything this world had and gave back weather. I intend to ask them, in front of witnesses, whether the exchange was fair. Is that so monstrous?"

"It is if Wren pays for it."

"Wren pays either way," Ilvane said gently. "That's what I brought you here to understand."

* * *

She returned to her chair and sat, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, warmer, the register she used for the close work. This was the part she was actually good at. Anyone could threaten. Seduction was craft.

"Here is what happens in nine days, mercenary. The hand completes its circle. The court convulses — contracts snapping taut all at once, ten thousand souls pulled taffy-tight between claimants who cannot agree. Your sister's soul, fragmented as it is into oaths she cannot break, will be torn along every seam at once. She will not die. Death would be a mercy and we don't stock mercy. She will persist. In pieces. Serving everyone and no one, forever, the way the court persists." She watched his hands close slowly on the arms of the chair. "Unless someone holds her whole contract when the bell rings. A single claimant, unopposed, standing in this room at the final turn. Then the tearing passes her by. The law is very clear about that. I wrote the law."

"So I take her contract and run."

"Run where?" Ilvane spread her hands, and the glass walls obliged her, showing him — she'd seeded them with the images herself, hours ago — the steppe above, the salt flats, Ovid's white-robed column already marching toward the mountain with fire in their fists. "The valley wards are dead. The stars went out on my signal, did you know that? A courtesy. I wanted the sky dark so no one could pretend a god was watching. There is nowhere left in your world that isn't either mine or burning. Every road out of this room leads deeper into it." She leaned forward. "You've been walking downhill since the courier died, Gideon Crane. You simply haven't admitted it yet."

"What are you offering?"

"Employment." She slid the ledger aside and laid the third object flat between them: a contract, blank except for one line, waiting for a name. "Vessel-of-Rains held a station here. Beneath us. A pillar-position, older than the valley, older than the wards — the thing that keeps the rains falling and the borders drawn and the quiet quiet. When the thirteenth hand turns, that position falls vacant. Vacant positions get filled by whoever the court chooses." She smiled. "Or by whoever volunteers."

"You want me to become it."

"I want you to *choose* it. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire point of the exercise." She stood again, circling him now, speaking softly near his ear like a confessor. "Think of what it buys. Wren walks free — truly free, her oaths dissolved, her soul whole, her debts burned, because her brother's claim supersedes all others. Think of what it costs. Your memories go first — a vessel can't carry mortal attachments, they leak — then your name, then your face, then whatever's left after that. You will hold the rains. You will hold the border. You will sit in the dark under the mountain and keep the world's promises while the world forgets you were ever in it." She paused at his shoulder. "Which, I'm told, is more or less what you were already doing in that valley. Only now you'd be honest about it."

"That's not freedom. That's a cage with a title."

"All cages have titles," Ilvane said. "Yours was called an oath. Hers is called service. Mine is called regency. We are none of us above the merchandise." She came around to face him. "But hear me, mercenary, because I will say this once and I do not repeat myself: I am not lying to you. That is rarer than mercy down here. Take the position and Wren goes free — I swear it on the clock itself, and the clock does not permit me to break my word. Refuse, and in nine days she is torn apart by arithmetic. There is no third door. I've checked. I built the house."

For a long moment he said nothing. The candle burned cold between them. Somewhere below, faintly, she could hear the sound she had listened to for four centuries: the turning of the great mechanism, patient as tide, counting down.

"You said the god tried to buy its way out of the question," Gideon said finally. "And failed."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ilvane studied him, and for the first time in a long while she found the answer arriving slower than she liked. "Because a purchase only moves a debt," she said. "It doesn't erase it. Someone always holds the paper."

"Then whoever takes your offer doesn't save her." He pushed back the chair and stood, slow and deliberate, a man planting himself like a stake. "They just become the next person holding the paper. And the clock keeps turning. And in another four hundred years there's another mercenary sitting in that chair, and some other regent offering him the same beautiful deal."

Ilvane said nothing. The glass walls flickered gold with someone else's remembered joy, far below.

"You didn't build a clock to make the gods answer," he said. "You built it because you're the thirteenth hand. You're counting down to your own contract coming due."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the mechanism below seemed to hold its breath.

"There it is," Ilvane murmured, and something in her face — the tired arithmetic face, the favorite one — finally fell away. What remained was older, and hungrier, and afraid. "Ovid's army reaches the gates in nine days. My court is already fraying — the mapmaker's maps are circulating, the wardens are taking bribes, the prisoners can smell the bell. I have offered you the only clean exit in the building, and you're telling me you'd rather burn the building down."

"I'm telling you," Gideon said, "that I've heard your answer. Now hear mine."

Above them, muffled by a mile of glass and stone, the false moon began its twelfth turn — and somewhere in the dark of the steppe, Sefa's last remaining map caught fire in her pack, though neither of them knew it yet.
