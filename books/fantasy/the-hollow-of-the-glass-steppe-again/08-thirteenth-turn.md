## 8. Thirteenth Turn

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as arrive.

It came up through the floor of the Undercourt, a pale disc of light bleeding through the glass ceiling of the great hall, and every soul in the chamber felt it pass over them like a hand counting coins. Gideon stood at the center of the court with Wren's oath-threads still hanging from his fingers where he'd touched them, thin as spider silk, bright as wire. Above him the regent Ilvane waited on her throne of fused memory, patient as interest accruing.

"You have until the moon completes its turn," she said. "Take your god's place, and your sister walks free. Refuse, and the bargain seals forever. There is no third door, mercenary."

"There's always a third door," Gideon said. "You just have to be the one who builds it."

He had spent the climb down from the shrine doing what he'd done his whole life in the valley: watching. Watching how the court moved. Watching how Ilvane spoke. And watching, most carefully, the contract itself—the original one, the god's own signature pressed into the air above the throne like a scar that never healed. Vessel-of-Rains had bought Wren's soul once already. The testament said so plainly. Bought it back from this very court, paid full price, carried it up into the valley, and then—when the debt came due again—traded its voice to buy time.

Which meant one thing, if Gideon was right, and he had bet his life on worse odds in worse places.

*It was never the court's to sell.*

"You bought it back," Ilvane said, reading his silence the way Sefa read terrain. "Yes. Your god was thorough. It is why I wanted it beneath my mountain."

"Then you sold stolen goods." Gideon lifted his voice, and it carried strangely in the glass hall, bouncing off walls made of other people's remembered lives. "Every soul here. Every oath-thread, every fragment, every coin of memory in your vaults—if any piece of it was ever bought back and resold, your whole ledger is rotten. Your law runs on clean title, regent. You told me that yourself. *The court does not deal in what it does not own.*"

Ilvane's expression did not change. That was the terrifying thing about her. But the hall changed for her—a ripple went through the courtiers, a shifting like wind through wheat, because they all knew their own titles were not clean either. Nobody's were. The whole Undercourt was built on resale.

"The contract stands," she said.

"Then let it stand *openly*." Gideon turned to face the assembled court, to the ranks of the soul-banked and the oath-bound, to Wren standing pale among the regent's attendants with her fragments glinting under her skin like buried glass. "I invoke audit. Original terms. Produce the deed of sale for Wren Crane's soul—the *first* sale, the one your ledger says started all this—and show me the court owned it before my god bought it back."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the shoulders.

And then, far above, through the glass ceiling, the first star went out.

Not the moon. A star—one of the ones that had been returning since the wards fell, timid points of light relearning the sky. It guttered like a candle pinched between wet fingers. Then another. Then a whole constellation, gone, and the light coming down through the ceiling turned the color of old bruises.

"They're here," Sefa said at his shoulder. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman reading a map she'd drawn herself and hating the route. "Ovid's forces. They've been marching since the salt flats. He didn't need your oath, Gideon. He just needed to know when the moon would turn."

Gideon's stomach dropped. "You told him."

"I charted the steppe," Sefa said. "Every road on it leads somewhere. Including this one." She met his eyes, and there was no apology in her face, only the terrible honesty of someone who had run out of time to lie well. "I'm sorry. But look at it this way—I got us here first."

The ceiling cracked.

Not broke—cracked, a single fault line racing across the glass like lightning frozen mid-strike, and through it came the sound of Ovid's host: boots on stone, hymns sung in unison, the low grinding hum of a god's name being spoken by ten thousand throats at once. Chunks of the ceiling began to fall, and where they struck the floor they shattered into memories—little bursts of other people's lives, a wedding, a drowning, a childhood dog, scattering across the marble like spilled jewelry.

"Seal the hall!" Ilvane rose from her throne, and for the first time Gideon saw something move behind her face—not fear exactly, but the rapid recalculation of a merchant watching her shop burn. "The audit claim is void while the court is under assault! Defend the ledger!"

"An open claim doesn't void because you're having a bad night," Gideon said, and pulled the courier's contract from inside his coat—the dead man's paper, his own name on it, Wren's soul-price written in ink that had never dried. He held it up toward the scar of the god's signature hanging above the throne. "Vessel-of-Rains! You bought her once! If you can hear me down here in your prison—*say so!*"

Nothing. The god's silence was twenty years deep and had swallowed better prayers than his.

But the contract moved.

The ink stirred, crawling like insects, rearranging itself. The soul-price figures dissolved and reformed, dissolved and reformed, and Gideon understood with a lurch that the paper was trying to reconcile itself against the court's ledger and failing—that somewhere beneath the mountain, an accounting older than Ilvane had just been asked a question it could not answer without admitting the answer.

"Stop that," Ilvane said quietly. "Guards. Take the paper."

They came. Of course they came. Gideon had fought in a dozen wars and this was the part he knew best: the moment when everyone moves at once and the only thing that matters is what you do in the first three seconds. He threw the contract high, spun, caught the first guard's spear-haft and used it to put the second guard into the third, and Sefa was already moving—not fighting, never fighting, but running the room's edges with her satchel open, pulling out maps.

Her maps. Twenty years of the steppe, every route, every hidden door, every secret way into and out of the Undercourt, drawn in her own hand.

She fed them into the brazier beside the throne.

"No—" Ilvane's composure finally cracked, just a hairline, just enough. "Those are mine. I *bought* those."

"You bought copies," Sefa said, feeding page after page into the fire, and the flames burned strange colors, green and violet, the colors of routes dying. "You bought what I chose to sell. The rest stayed in here." She tapped her temple. "And now the rest goes wherever I go. Blind, regent. You're blind now. Every escape route out of your mountain just became a rumor."

The ceiling gave way.

Ovid's vanguard came down into the hall on ropes and falling glass, white-robed, singing, and behind them the pressure of their god filled the chamber like floodwater. Gideon felt it press against Vessel-of-Rains' silence and understood, suddenly, viscerally, why his god had traded its voice—because two gods in one room was not a conversation, it was a war, and the valley had been a dam holding back exactly this.

Wren was running toward him through the chaos.

He would know her walk anywhere, oath-fragments or no, and something in his chest tore loose and flew to meet her. She reached him, grabbed his arm, and shouted over the din: "Whatever you're doing—whatever you think you're proving—it won't work! The court always wins! There's always a price!"

"I know," Gideon shouted back. "I'm going to pay it!"

"That's what I'm afraid of!"

Above the throne, the god's scarred signature flared—white, then gold, then a color Gideon had no name for—and the contract in his hands went still at last, the ink settling into a single line that had not been there before:

*PAID IN FULL. ONCE.*

Three words. A dead god's last argument, delivered twenty years late.

Gideon held it up, and even over the crash of Ovid's host, even over the screaming of the courtiers whose fortunes were dissolving around them, his voice found the room.

"The court heard it! The ledger heard it! Paid in full, once—and what's bought outright can't be resold, can't be reclaimed, can't come due again unless someone *forged the debt*!" He turned, slowly, letting every soul in the hall see his face. "So who forged it? Who wrote Wren Crane's name into the books twice? Because I've read enough contracts to know a double entry isn't a mistake. It's a theft. Someone in this room has been collecting interest on a soul the court never owned—and I'd wager everything I have left that I'm looking at her."

Every eye in the Undercourt turned to the throne.

Ilvane stood very still amid the ruin of her hall, firelight and falling glass and the white robes of Ovid's faithful pressing in from every side, and Gideon watched her do the arithmetic she had spent centuries avoiding: that the ledger was public now, that the audit claim could not be unclaimed, that a god's three-word testimony hung in the air above her like a sword.

"You would destroy the court," she said, "to save one soul."

"No," Gideon said. "I'd destroy the court because it was already rotten. Saving her is just the reason I can live with."

The thirteenth moon completed its turn.

Light poured down through the broken ceiling, total and silver and merciless, and in that light the great ledger of the Undercourt opened itself—unbidden, unsealed, every page turning—and began, line by line, to call out its debts.

And the first name it called was Ilvane's.

* * *

The regent did not scream. Gideon would remember that about her afterward, in the years when remembering was all he had left of some things. She stood on the steps of her throne while her own ledger named her frauds—hundreds of them, thousands, souls bought back and resold, debts forged, interest collected on property the court had never held—and she listened the way a soldier listens to casualty lists, and when the light finished with her she simply said:

"So. The flaw was real."

Then the ledger took her. Not violently. It folded her up like a letter being returned to sender, and the throne of fused memory cracked down the middle, and the hall filled with a sound Gideon had no word for—the sound of a system devouring its own author.

The court did not fall gently after that. It fell the way a dam falls, all at once and everywhere. Oath-threads snapped and whipped free; vault doors burst and spilled decades of hoarded memory across the marble in glittering floods; the soul-banked woke up weeping or laughing or both, clutching pieces of themselves they'd forgotten they'd pawned. Ovid's faithful waded through it all singing louder, claiming the collapse as their god's victory, and maybe it was, but Gideon saw their faces when the memories touched them—saw priests stagger under the weight of strangers' griefs and joys—and understood that no one was walking out of this mountain unchanged.

Sefa found him by the shattered throne, soot on her hands, her satchel empty and light for the first time in twenty years.

"The deep ways are open," she said. "All of them. Every route I ever burned is just—gone from the maps, which means gone from the walls too. We can walk out any direction we want." She paused. "Including up."

Gideon looked at Wren.

She was standing in the flood of freed memory, and the oath-fragments under her skin were dimming one by one, going dark like windows in a house at dusk. She looked lighter. She looked terrified.

"It's working," she said. "I can feel them letting go. Gideon, it's actually—" She stopped. Frowned. Pressed a hand to her sternum. "Wait. Something's wrong. Something's still—there's still a lien on me. One thread left, and it's not snapping, it's *tightening*, and I don't—"

The last thread flared gold.

And Gideon felt it too, then—a pull behind his eyes, gentle and enormous, like a tide taking hold of something he hadn't known was moored. The ledger wasn't finished. The court was dead, but contracts outlive courts; that was the first law of the place, and the last law too. One debt remained unpaid, one balance unsettled, and the magic beneath the mountain had begun, patiently, inevitably, to collect.

"Wren," he said. "Listen to me. Whatever happens next—"

"Don't," she said. "Whatever it is, don't."

The gold light rose around them both, and somewhere in it, soft as rain beginning, he heard the ledger speak its final term.
