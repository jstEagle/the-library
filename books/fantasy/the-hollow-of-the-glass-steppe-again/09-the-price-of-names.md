## 9. The Price of Names

The court did not want my life.

That was the cruelest thing I learned in the hour after the convulsion, when the glass halls were still ringing like struck bells and Ovid's soldiers were dying somewhere far above us in the dark. The Undercourt had never wanted my life. Lives were cheap here—there were whole galleries hung with them, souls pressed flat and framed like specimens. What the court wanted, what it had always traded in, was rarer than blood.

It wanted the shape of a thing loved.

"Sit," Regent Ilvane said.

I sat. There was no dignity left to protect; the floor beneath me was warm glass and I had been kneeling on it for most of a night. Wren stood beside me with her hands bound in oath-light, screaming at a court that did not listen, and I kept my eyes on her because I was afraid that if I looked at anything else I would lose my nerve.

"You invoked the flaw," Ilvane said. She did not seem angry. That frightened me more than anger would have. "The god bought her soul once. You are correct. It was never ours to sell the second time." She folded her hands. "But contracts do not simply die, mercenary. They *settle*. And a settlement requires payment from someone."

"Then take me," I said. "You offered. I'm offering faster."

"I offered you your god's throne," she said. "That offer is withdrawn. You've made yourself useless as an instrument—you refused Ovid, you refused me, you'd refuse the mountain itself if it asked nicely. No one can aim you anymore." Something flickered across her face that might have been respect. "So we will take something else. Something of equal weight."

Wren stopped screaming. In the sudden quiet I could hear the thirteenth moon grinding through its last turn somewhere above the glass, a sound like a millstone full of stars.

"What," I said.

"The court keeps accounts," Ilvane said, "and accounts must balance. Your sister's soul was sold twice. Once by your god, once by your oath—the oath you swore at the valley's edge, the one you believed was empty. Both sales voided tonight, in a single stroke. Impressive. Expensive." She leaned forward. "Two voidings. Two payments owed. The first, the god already paid long ago—it gave its voice. The second falls to you."

"Name it."

"Every memory you hold of Wren Crane."

The words went into me slowly, like cold water finding its way through cloth. I understood them one at a time and then all together, and together they were unbearable.

"No," Wren said. Her voice came out small, stripped of all the oath-steel she'd wrapped herself in since I found her serving the regent's table. "No. Gideon. No."

"It balances," Ilvane said mildly. "A soul sold is grief without end. A memory given is grief without beginning. The court finds them equivalent. The court has always found them equivalent."

"There has to be another way," Wren said. She turned to me and her hands came up, oath-bound, useless, shaking. "Take mine. Take whatever's left of me, take the fragments, take—Gideon, look at me. *Look at me.*"

I looked at her.

Here is what I saw: my sister, four years old, standing in the rain outside the shrine because she liked the sound it made on the tin roof. My sister, twelve, teaching herself to whistle with a blade of grass until the whole valley whistled back. My sister, seventeen, standing at the border wards with her pack on, telling me the valley felt smaller every year and she couldn't breathe in it, and me telling her to stay anyway, because the world outside was dangerous and I was a coward who called his cowardice protection.

Here is what I knew: none of those things would be mine anymore. I would walk out of this mountain carrying a soul I had saved and not know why I had bled for it. I would see her face and feel nothing but the courtesy a stranger owes a stranger.

And here is what I decided, in the space of one heartbeat, with the moon still turning above us:

Duty was the only thing I had ever been any good at. Love, I had always done badly—too late, too tight-fisted, too much guarding and too little giving. So let me give it now. Let me give all of it, cleanly, the way you pay a debt you're proud of.

"All right," I said.

"*No.*"

"Wren—"

"Don't you dare say my name like that. Don't you dare make it sound like goodbye." She was crying now, and the oath-light around her wrists flared white with the force of feelings she could not act on. "You don't get to do this. You crossed the steppe. You crossed the *salt*. You dragged a mapmaker and a mad priest and half a burning sky down into this hole for me, and you think you get to walk out having forgotten why?"

"Yeah," I said. "I think that's exactly what I get."

"Gideon—"

"You told me the valley never gave you purpose," I said. "You were right. I was part of that. I kept you like a thing in a box and called it love." I stood up. My knees cracked; the glass hummed under my boots. "So this is the last thing I'll ever decide about you, and I'm deciding it right: you go free, and I pay, and neither of us owes the other ever again. That's not sacrifice. That's just finally doing it properly."

She screamed. Not words anymore—just sound, raw and furious and broken, the kind of scream I imagined she'd been swallowing for years inside this court, the kind the oaths wouldn't let out until now. Sefa caught her arms. Even bound, even held, Wren fought like the valley itself was coming apart, and I made myself watch every second of it, because I owed her that much: to remember her fighting for me, all the way to the last moment there was a *her* to fight for and a *me* to fight over.

Ilvane raised one hand.

"Begin," she said.

* *

They do it with water, in the Undercourt. Of course they do. Memory is currency, and currency is weighed, poured, counted. They brought a basin of black glass filled with something that wasn't quite water, and a cup, and a clerk with a ledger who told me, in the bored tone of a man reading tariff schedules, to speak each memory aloud before I drank.

"The rain on the shrine roof," I said, and drank.

Gone. I knew it was gone the way you know a tooth has come out—not pain, exactly, but absence, a hollow with edges.

"Her whistle. The grass blade. The valley answering."

Drink.

"Her seventeenth year. The wards. The pack. The argument."

Drink.

Somewhere past the tenth cup I lost track of which memories were leaving. The clerk's voice went on, patient as erosion. *The time she broke her arm falling from the pear tree and lied about how. The way she sang off-key on purpose to annoy the neighbors. The winter she was born, and how I carried her to the window to show her snow.* Each one named, each one drunk, each one leaving a clean round hole behind.

At some point Sefa was holding my shoulder. At some point Wren's screaming had gone hoarse and quiet, and then quiet altogether, and I did not look at her because I knew—if I looked at her now, mid-emptying, I would see a stranger weeping, and some animal part of me would panic and try to claw the memories back out of the basin, and I could not let that animal win. Not this close to done.

"The oath," the clerk said. "Your binding to Vessel-of-Rains. The day you swore it."

"That's not a memory of Wren."

"It contains her. The price was paid for her. It counts." The clerk turned the ledger toward me. "All of it or none of it, mercenary. Partial settlements bounce."

I thought about the silent god. About the years of praying into a silence I thought was indifference, and the testament in the ruined shrine, and the voice the god had traded away buying my sister back the first time. If the god could give up its voice for her, I could give up my remembering.

I drank the god too.

When it was finished, the basin was clear. Just water now. The clerk closed the ledger with a snap that echoed strangely loud in the hall.

"Accounts balanced," he said.

I stood. The room tilted and steadied. Everything in me was intact—my name, my trade, my hands, the scar on my palm from a knife I no longer remembered earning—and everything in me was missing, the way a house is missing after a fire leaves the walls standing.

Someone was standing in front of me. A woman. Dark-haired, red-eyed, watching me the way people watch a door they're afraid will close.

"Do I know you?" I asked.

Her face did something terrible and brief. Then she straightened, and wiped her cheeks with the back of her bound hands, and said, in a voice sanded smooth by effort:

"No. Not yet."

Behind her, another woman—gray-streaked hair, burn-scarred fingers, a soldier's tired stance—was feeding maps into a brazier one sheet at a time. She glanced at me with something like pity and something like relief.

"Steppe's open," she said. "Stars are coming back. We should move before the regent remembers she's furious."

I nodded. I picked up my sword because my hands knew to pick it up even if nothing else in me did. And I walked out of the Undercourt between two women whose names I had been told and immediately lost to shock, following a light I didn't understand toward a valley I no longer remembered leaving—

—except that at the mouth of the glass caves, where the night air hit me cold and wet and smelling of real rain for the first time in days, the woman with the red eyes took my hand.

Not to lead me. Just to hold it.

And though there was nothing in my head that knew her, something below memory—down where names are carved before language—turned toward her grip like a plant toward a window, and held on, and would not let go.

She noticed. I saw her notice.

"Well," she said softly, to herself or to the returning stars or to some third person I couldn't see. "That's a start."
