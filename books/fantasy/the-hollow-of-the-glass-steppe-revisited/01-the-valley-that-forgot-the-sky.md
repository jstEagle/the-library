## 1. The Valley That Forgot the Sky

The wards woke before I did.

They always did — a low thrum through the floorboards of the guildhouse, felt in the teeth more than the ears, like a bell rung somewhere beneath the world. Master Corvane used to say you could tell the health of Vael Hollow by that hum alone: steady as a sleeping heart, patient as stone. *Listen with your whole body, Emrys,* he'd say, one hand flat against the oldest of the border stones, his eyes closed, his breath slow. *The valley will tell you what it needs long before it tells anyone else.*

He had been dead eleven months, and I still listened with my whole body every morning, because no one else in the hollow knew how.

I dressed by candlelight and went down through the guildhall's cold corridors, past the nine empty chairs at the long table, past the wall where nine names were carved into slate — *Corvane Ashlock, Maren Duskwell, Tobiah Fen, Ilse Brightwater, Hallam Rook, Odile Marsh, Perrin Vale, Sef Andric, Liora Quill* — each name dusted with chalk-white lichen that no amount of scrubbing would remove. The guild had been nine strong when I was born. It was one strong now, and the one was me, twenty-three years old, last apprentice of the Wardwrights' Guild, keeper of a craft that had forgotten how to make new keepers.

Outside, autumn had come down from the ridgeline overnight. Frost silvered the rooftops of Lower Vael, and woodsmoke stood up straight from a hundred chimneys in the windless cold. The valley lay cupped between two arms of mountain like water held in two hands, green and sheltered and utterly enclosed. From the guildhouse steps I could see the whole of it: the terraced barley fields going gold, the millpond steaming faintly, the crooked lanes of the market town, the orchards heavy with late apples. Everything within sight of everything else. That was Vael Hollow. You could stand anywhere in it and see its edges.

Most people found that comforting.

I had spent my whole life trying to decide whether I did.

* * *

The border wards ran the valley's rim like a seam of stitching, forty-one wardstones set at intervals along the ridgelines, each one waist-high, river-smooth, older than any record we kept. They didn't look like much. Travelers who somehow found their way to the hollow — few did; the passes were treacherous and the maps, such as they were, showed nothing but blank glass-colored steppe beyond the mountains — sometimes mistook them for boundary markers or grave cairns. Children in Lower Vael used to dare each other to touch them on a dare, back when there were enough children to dare each other.

But touch one, and you'd feel it: a warmth like a hand pressed flat against your palm, and behind the warmth, a hum. The covenant's voice, the old texts called it. The sound of the hollow being kept.

My morning round took three hours if I walked it, less if I borrowed the mule, and I walked it because Master Corvane had walked it, because walking the wards meant learning them the way you learn a face rather than a description. I started at the Northgate stone — the largest, the one the founders themselves were said to have set — and worked counterclockwise around the rim, checking each stone for cracks, for moss, for drift.

Drift was the word Corvane used for the slow wandering of a wardstone's note out of true. Every stone held a tone, and all forty-one tones together made the chord that held the covenant taut. A stone drifting even a quarter-tone would, over years, fray the weave around it like a pulled thread in cloth. My job — the whole job, the entire surviving purpose of the Wardwrights' Guild — was to hear the drift and correct it, tuning the stones the way a musician tunes an instrument nobody else could hear.

I tuned six stones that morning. At the seventh, the one we called the Shepherd's Rest because it overlooked the sheep pastures, I knelt, laid both palms against the cool granite, closed my eyes, and listened.

And heard something I had never heard before.

Not drift. Drift is sourness, a wrongness you feel in your back teeth. This was none of those things. This was a note underneath the note — a second voice, very far down, so deep and quiet I might have imagined it, except that imagining things was the first sin Corvane had drilled out of me. *A wardwright trusts the stone over the self,* he'd said. *If the stone speaks, the stone means it.*

I stayed kneeling until my knees ached and the frost soaked through my trousers, listening to that buried voice. It wasn't sour. It wasn't wrong. That was the unsettling part. It sounded almost like — I searched for the word and didn't like the one I found — almost like longing. Like something under the valley humming toward something outside it.

Then it was gone, folded back under the chord, and the Shepherd's Rest hummed as it had hummed every morning of my life.

I sat back on my heels and looked out over the pastures, where the sheep moved like slow clouds across the gold grass, and told myself it was the frost. Cold made stone sing strangely; Corvane had said that too. I wrote the time, the date, and the stone's name in my round-book anyway, because a wardwright writes everything down, and because writing it made it smaller. Made it a fact instead of a feeling.

Facts I could carry. Feelings had a way of becoming journeys.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of beeswax and old paper, and in the afternoons it smelled of my failures.

I spent them in the archive room, the small windowless chamber off the main hall where the guild kept its books — ledgers of ward-tuning going back four hundred years, survey maps of the border, and, locked in the iron-banded chest at the room's heart, the ritual texts. There were nineteen of them. Corvane had taught me fourteen.

Fourteen, and then he died with the fifteenth half-learned, and I had spent eleven months trying to finish teaching myself what he never finished teaching me.

It was not going well.

The problem was not the words. The words I had; the fifteenth rite — the Rite of the Listening Year, performed once a generation to renew the covenant's deepest binding — was written out in full in Corvane's own careful hand, every syllable, every gesture, every pause marked with the little crescents he used for held breath. The problem was that the rite required two wardwrights. Its final movement was a call and answer: one voice speaking the covenant's terms, the other answering on behalf of the valley itself. Two voices, Corvane's notes explained, *because a promise made alone is only a wish.*

I had tried every workaround I could think of. I had recorded my own voice — the hollow had no such craft, but I'd rigged a system of resonant glass tubes that held a tone for a few seconds, long enough, I'd hoped, to answer me. The tubes answered in my own voice, which was the whole problem. The rite wanted the valley's answer, not mine. When I spoke the terms and the tubes gave back my own words in my own timbre, the wardstones along the border had gone silent for a full minute — all forty-one of them, the entire chord stopping like a held breath — and then resumed, and I had lain awake that night understanding that I had asked the covenant a question and answered it myself, impersonating the valley, and the valley had noticed.

So instead I memorized. That was the discipline Corvane had left me: know the rite so completely that when the answer came — if it ever came — I would be ready to receive it. I recited the fifteenth rite aloud in the empty hall every evening, both parts, switching voices, my shadow on the wall gesturing the responses a half-second behind me. Nine empty chairs watched. Sometimes I pretended the chairs were listening. Sometimes I pretended harder than other times.

That afternoon, deep in the fourth movement, someone knocked.

Nobody knocked at the guildhall. The hollow's people came to me for ward-mending and blessing-charms and the occasional dispute about property lines that the old stones technically adjudicated, but they came at civilized hours and they let themselves be announced by Wren, who lived in the cottage by the gate and served, without title or agreement, as the guild's doorkeeper, cook, conscience, and chaos.

I opened the door myself. Old Wren stood on the step with a basket of apples and her usual expression of a woman who has seen everything twice and been unimpressed both times. She was past seventy, small and wiry, with white hair she cut herself and hands that had mended broken bones and broken pottery and, once, according to her, a broken marriage, though she refused to say whose.

"You're talking to the chairs again," she said.

"I'm rehearsing."

"Mm." She handed me the basket. "Windfalls. The tree by the millpond. Take them or don't, but stop letting them rot, it's wasteful." She looked past me into the hall, at the nine chairs, and something crossed her face too quick to catch. "Which part?"

"Fourth movement. The call and answer."

"And how's the glass going to answer you today?"

"The glass isn't going to answer me."

"No," Wren agreed. "Funny, that." She stepped inside without invitation, as she always did, and settled onto the bench by the cold hearth as though the guildhall were her kitchen, which in every way that mattered it had been, once, before the deaths thinned us down to me. She had been the guild's mender for thirty years — not a wardwright, but bound to the guild all the same, the one who patched up whatever the wards couldn't prevent. Retired now, the others would have said. Survived them, was what I thought.

"Wren," I said, sitting across from her, "can I ask you something strange?"

"I'd be disappointed otherwise."

"When you were working with the guild — did Master Corvane ever mention a second voice in the stones? Something under the chord? Deep down?"

Wren went still in the particular way she went still, which was completely, like a bird deciding whether the cat had seen it. Then she reached into her pocket, produced an apple from God knew where, and began polishing it on her sleeve.

"Stones sing what they're given to sing," she said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that won't keep you up at night."

"It's already keeping me up at night."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she did perhaps twice a year, and I saw her weigh something. "Corvane talked to me once," she said slowly, "near the end. Last winter, when he knew — well. When he knew. He said the covenant had a second clause. Not written anywhere. Not in the nineteen rites, not in the ledgers, not in the founders' stones. He said the first guilds had kept it in memory only, passed mouth to mouth, and that the last man who'd carried it whole was Guildmaster Ellery Voss, dead sixty years, and that he himself had only gotten the edge of it." She bit the apple, chewed, swallowed. "And I said, well, what's the clause, you old fool, and he said—"

She stopped.

"He said what?" I leaned forward. "Wren."

"He said, *the debt isn't what we wrote down.*" Her eyes were on the middle distance, on some winter forty years gone. "And then he coughed, and I brought him tea, and he never picked it up again, and I decided an old man's riddles weren't worth chasing." She pointed the apple core at me. "You're chasing it, though. I can see it on you. You've got your mother's chase-face."

"Don't talk about my mother's face."

"Somebody has to remember it. You were seven."

The fire needed lighting. I lit it, mostly to have something to do with my hands, and when I turned around Wren was watching me with an expression I couldn't read, soft and sorry and sharp all at once.

"Tell me the rest of it," I said. "What Corvane said. All of it."

"There isn't any rest of it. That's what I'm telling you, girl. The man died holding most of his cards, and one of them was this, and all it says is that the thing we've spent four hundred years believing about ourselves—" she waved the apple core at the walls, at the nine chairs, at the slate with nine names, at all of it, "—might not be the whole truth. Might not even be the truth at all. Just the version that was easier to live with."

"Easier for whom?"

"Now that," said Wren, "is the right question." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and gathered her shawl. "Autumn remembrance is in five days. Nine candles, nine names, and you reading them out alone again. You'll want the hall swept."

"Wren."

"Mm?"

"What did the covenant actually say? The original. Before the guilds rewrote it. Does anything survive?"

She paused at the door, and for a moment the lamplight caught her face and made her look suddenly, terribly old, and not tired-old but frightened-old, a woman standing at the edge of something she'd spent a lifetime pretending was a shadow and not a cliff.

"Ask the stones," she said. "They were here first."

* * *

I walked the border again that night, which I was not supposed to do, alone in the dark, which I was definitely not supposed to do. But the moon was up, a thin paring of it, and the frost had come down hard enough to turn the whole ridge silver, and I could not sit in the guildhall with fourteen finished rites and one unfinished and a sentence rattling in my skull like a loose stone in a tin pail: *the debt isn't what we wrote down.*

I went to the Shepherd's Rest first. Kneeling, palms flat, breath slowed, I listened the way Corvane taught me — whole body, whole silence, whole surrender.

The buried note was still there. Stronger, now. Or maybe I was simply better at hearing it, the way you can find a cricket in a dark field once you've stopped looking with your eyes. It rose and fell beneath the great steady chord of the covenant like a swimmer beneath ice, and this time, when I stilled myself completely, I could almost make out its shape. Not random. Patterned. Repeating.

Almost like a name, repeated over and over by something that had forgotten everything else.

I sat back. My heart was going hard. Above me the sky wheeled, vast and indifferent and beautiful, crowded with stars — more stars than the hollow's people ever bothered counting, because the sky was the one thing in Vael Hollow that had no edge, the one direction you could look and not see the walls of your own life. I used to lie in the summer grass as a child and pick out the star-patterns from Corvane's star-charts, the Weaver, the Antlered One, the Hearth That Never Goes Out, and dream about the lands they hung over, the roads they guided travelers down, the whole unbordered world turning beneath them.

Contentment, everyone in the hollow called our life. Blessed, walled, warm contentment. I had learned to say the word with a straight face. I had nearly learned to mean it.

I pressed my palms to the stone one last time, and the buried note rose to meet me — and changed.

Every wardstone in the hollow hummed the same chord. I had known that since I was six years old. Forty-one stones, one voice, the covenant's single sustained note, steady as a sleeping heart.

The Shepherd's Rest sang a second note.

Just one, just once, thin and high and utterly alone, a harmonic that belonged to no rite I had memorized, no chord in any ledger, no entry in four hundred years of round-books. It slid up through my palms and into my sternum and rang there like a struck glass, and somewhere out along the dark ridgeline, faint and far away, another stone answered it.

Then another.

Then the night went quiet, and the chord resumed as if nothing had happened, and the frost ticked and settled, and the sheep shifted in their pastures, and above me the stars burned on, unaware, or pretending to be.

I stayed on the ridge a long time, until the cold drove sense back into me. Then I walked home through the silver fields with my round-book clutched to my chest like a shield, and in it, when I reached the guildhall, I wrote by candlelight:

*Ninth of Harvestmonth. Shepherd's Rest. A note I have never heard. Answered by at least two stones. Cause unknown.*

I stared at the entry for a while. Then, beneath it, in smaller letters, I added what I actually feared, because a wardwright writes everything down:

*I think the valley is trying to tell us something. I think it has been trying for a long time. I think we stopped listening before I was born.*

Outside, the frost thickened. Somewhere along the border, in the dark, the stones hummed on — and underneath, very softly, patiently, like a knock at a door no one had opened in four hundred years, the second voice went on calling.
