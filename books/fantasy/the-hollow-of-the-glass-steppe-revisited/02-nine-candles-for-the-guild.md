## 2. Nine Candles for the Guild

The guild hall smelled of beeswax and cold stone and the particular sweetness of dried rowan berries strung above the door — a charm against forgetting, Master Aldous used to say, though Emrys had never learned whether that was doctrine or an old man's joke. Nine candles stood on the long table where the workbenches had been pushed aside, each one set before a small brass plate bearing a name. The wax caught the draft from the shutters and leaned, all nine flames tilting in the same direction at once, like grass under wind.

Emrys had polished the plates themselves. That was the first duty of remembrance, Aldous had taught: *you do not mourn a name you cannot read.* So Emrys had sat by lamplight with a rag and a pot of whiting, working tarnish out of engraved letters until the brass gave back the firelight clean and bright.

*Master Aldous Vane. Master Corrin Hale. Mistress Ivet Sorrow.* No — not Sorrow; that was what grief did to memory, it salted things. *Ivet Sorrell.* And so on down the line: six masters of the Wardwrights' Guild, two journeymen who had died young and stupidly in a flood-tunnel beneath the western wards, and one apprentice whose name Emrys made a point of reading aloud to themself every year, because no one else was left who remembered how it was pronounced. *Bren Oduya.* Not *O-doo-ya*, as the council clerk always wrote it, but *Oh-DYE-ah*, with the weight on the second syllable, the way Bren himself had said it, laughing, the day he'd shown Emrys how to coax a wardstone into singing.

Nine candles. One answerer.

That was the whole of the guild now: Emrys Lockridge, twenty-three years old, last apprentice of the Wardwrights' Guild of Vael Hollow, standing alone in a hall built to hold forty, wearing their master's too-long coat because tailors were one more thing the hollow had stopped making.

The remembrance began at dusk, as it always had, when the light through the eastern windows turned the color of weak cider. There was no congregation to wait for. Wren would come or she wouldn't — she came most years, though lately her comings and goings had the quality of weather, arriving without warning and leaving without farewell. The council never attended. The council sent, instead, a deacon with a ledger, who recorded that the remembrance had been held, and whether the guild's tax-exemption as a house of mourning should be renewed for another year. Emrys had signed that ledger four times now. Each signature felt like a small treason against the people whose names were burning on the table.

They waited until the first star — the only star, the single fixed point called the Shepherd's Eye that hung low over the southern rim of the valley, the one light the founders' wards had been shaped to admit — cleared the ridge. Then Emrys lit the candles from a taper touched to the hearth, one by one, left to right, eldest name to youngest, and spoke the words Aldous had spoken, and his master before him, words worn smooth as river stones from centuries of mouths:

*"We were many, and we are fewer. What we built holds. What we owed, we remember. Answer, whoever remains."*

"I remain," Emrys said, to the empty hall, in a voice that cracked on the second word. "I answer."

Outside, the valley went about its evening. Emrys could hear it through the walls: cart wheels on the mill road, someone calling a child home, the bell of the tannery counting out the hour. Vael Hollow did not pause for its dead. It couldn't afford to. That was the unspoken covenant of the living — the wards needed tending twice a day, dawn and dusk, and grief was something you did in the margins, in halls like this one, with candles bought at your own expense.

Emrys read the names aloud. All nine. They took longer than they used to, because Emrys had begun, in recent years, to say something about each one — not eulogy exactly, but evidence. Proof of life. *Aldous Vane, who hummed while he worked and denied it. Corrin Hale, who could tell a cracked wardstone by smell alone. Bren Oduya, who laughed like the second syllable of his own name.* When Emrys finished, the hall was fully dark except for the candles, and the silence afterward had a texture, dense and close, like felt laid over everything.

That was when the door opened, and Old Wren came in trailing the smell of woodsmoke and camphor.

* * *

Wren had been the guild's mender for thirty years before her mind began to come apart at the seams — everyone said so, kindly, the way you say things about people who can still hear you through walls. She had mended more than cloth. In the old days, they said, she had mended *wards*, back when the guild still trusted hands other than wrightsmen's, and there were those who whispered she had known Aldous better than his own apprentices did. Now she lived in a lean-to behind the dyer's yard and collected buttons, keys, bits of colored glass, and sentences that didn't belong to this century.

She came to the table without greeting, as she always did, and stood looking down at the nine flames. Her eyes were pale as skimmed milk and did not seem to land anywhere in particular, but tonight they landed on the candle farthest right — Bren's candle — and stayed.

"You polished these," she said.

"This morning."

"Good. He hated smudges." She said it flatly, without sentiment, and Emrys felt it go through them anyway, straight through the breastbone, because *he* — she had said *he* — and Wren hadn't spoken of Bren Oduya in three years, not since the winter she'd mistaken Emrys for him and wept into their coat for an hour.

"Wren—"

"Hush. You're mid-rite." She lowered herself onto the bench with the care of someone negotiating with her own joints, and folded her hands, and looked at the candles the way a looker looks at a fire. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then, quietly, in the voice she used for recitations — the clear, schoolroom voice that surfaced through her confusion like a fish through pond ice — she said:

"The debt is not paid in blood. The debt is paid in bread."

Emrys went still. "What?"

"That's how it goes. That's how it *went*." Her brow furrowed, and for a moment she seemed to be listening to something very far away. "Hearth and hand. Hearth and hand, and the door stands open. But then they wrote it down wrong, or wrote it down small, and small writing is how covenants rot." She looked up at Emrys then, and her milky eyes were suddenly, terribly focused. "You've read the founding text. The true one. Tell me what the first clause says."

"'Hearth and hand,'" Emrys said slowly. "It's fragmentary. Half of it's burned. *Hearth and hand* is all that survives of the opening."

"And the rest? After the fragment ends? What does the guild teach comes next?"

Emrys recited it the way they'd been taught it, the way every wardwright for three hundred years had been taught it, kneeling on cold flags with their master's hand heavy on their shoulder: *"And when the hearth fails, the wards shall hold; and when the wards fail, the guild shall pay; and the payment shall be a life freely given, that the valley may live."*

Wren spat. Actually spat, off to the side, an old woman's gesture of contempt aimed at three centuries of doctrine. "Freely given," she muttered. "There's no such thing, boy. Girl. *Child.*" She waved a hand at Emrys's whole self, unable or unwilling to settle the question, which was fair; Emrys had never settled it either. "A payment isn't free. A payment is a payment. Your masters took a welcome and turned it into a bill, and now the bill's come due and none of you know what you actually owe."

The candles flickered. Emrys told themself it was the draft.

"Wren," they said carefully, the way you speak to someone standing near a ledge, "what are you telling me?"

She was already somewhere else. Her gaze had drifted back to the flames, and her hands had begun their small busy motion, sorting invisible buttons. "Nine candles," she said. "Used to be forty. Used to be the whole hall lit up like a sky full of—" She stopped. Her head tilted. Something moved across her face like a cloud shadow crossing a field.

"Sky full of what?" Emrys asked.

But Wren only shook her head slowly, and reached out one crooked finger, and — before Emrys could stop her — touched the flame of the middle candle, Aldous's candle, passing her fingertip through it without harm, without even a flinch, the way she'd done every year since Emrys had known her. Fire never burned Wren. Aldous had said it was an old mender's trick. Emrys had privately suspected it was something else entirely, something the guild had no word for anymore.

"They'll come asking," she said softly, to the flame, or to Aldous, or to no one. "When the lights go out, they'll come asking, and you'll want to give them what the book says. Don't. Give them what the *door* says." She withdrew her finger and looked at Emrys again, and this time her expression was lucid and urgent and frightened, and it was the fright that convinced Emrys, more than any words. Wren was not afraid of anything anyone could name. "Promise me you'll check the archive. The sealed shelf. Under the floor where Aldous hid the—"

The door banged open.

* * *

Deacon Harrow stood in the doorway with his lantern raised and his ledger under his arm, and behind him the night was very dark — darker than it should have been, Emrys thought distantly, though they couldn't have said why. Harrow was a narrow man with a narrow face, and he surveyed the hall — the candles, the old woman, the last apprentice — with the expression of a man auditing a room.

"The remembrance is concluded?" he asked.

"It's concluded when I say it's concluded," Emrys said, and heard Aldous in their own voice, and was glad of it.

"The council requires confirmation of attendance for the exemption record." His eyes found Wren. "Is *she* counted as attendance?"

"She's counted as family," Emrys said, and was surprised at how easily it came out, and how true it rang.

Harrow wrote something. The scratch of his nib was very loud. When he finished, he paused at the door, and — perhaps because the candlelight softened even men like him — he said, without turning around: "My grandmother was a Hale. Corrin's line. Whatever else the guild was, the valley owes it this much: the lights stay lit." Then he was gone, and his lantern with him, and the darkness outside the windows seemed to press closer, as if it had been waiting for the chance.

Wren was laughing. It was a dry, rusted sound, almost coughing.

"He doesn't know," she wheezed. "None of them know. The lights stay lit — oh, *child*. Do you know why there's only one star over this valley? Do you know what the wards cost to keep one little eye of heaven open? Ask your archive. Ask the sealed shelf. Ask—" She stopped again, mid-word, and this time the cloud shadow crossed her face and did not leave. Her hands resumed their button-sorting. "Cold tonight," she said pleasantly, to no one. "Cold for autumn. Colder than the year the river froze in *summer*, do you remember that? Nobody remembers that. Nobody remembers anything."

Emrys knelt beside her and took her hands and stilled them. "Wren. Look at me. The sealed shelf. Where?"

Her milk-pale eyes wandered and found Emrys's face, and for one moment — one long, hanging moment — she was entirely present, sharp as a needle, and she squeezed Emrys's fingers hard enough to hurt.

"Aldous knew the covenant was dying," she whispered. "He knew what was coming for it. He hid the truth where only a last apprentice would ever think to kneel." She released Emrys's hands and stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and shuffled toward the door. At the threshold she paused, silhouetted against a night that seemed, impossibly, to be missing something — some faint silver grain in the blackness overhead that Emrys's eyes kept reaching for and failing to find.

"Light your candles early tomorrow," Wren said, without turning around. "All of them. Every lamp in the hall. Because I think, my dear, my dear last child — I think this is the last autumn the sky will bother pretending."

Then she was gone into the dark, and Emrys stood alone with nine candles and the sound of the valley going about its evening, and did not sleep that night, and did not go to the archive either — not yet, not that night, because the wards needed tending at dusk and again at dawn, and duty is a patient creditor.

It was three nights later, kneeling at the western borderstone with their palms flat against the humming granite, that Emrys heard the note.

Every wardstone sang. That was the first thing a wardwright learned — the deep, sub-audible chord each stone held against the dark, steady as a heartbeat, the same since the founding. Emrys had known that chord their whole life the way you know your own pulse.

This was not that chord.

The stone beneath their hands had shifted, half a tone, into a key Emrys had never heard — a thin, high, grieving note, like glass about to break — and as Emrys lifted their head, heart slamming, and looked up past the wards into the steppe-dark sky where the Shepherd's Eye ought to have been shining its lone faithful point of light,

the sky was empty.

All the way up. All the way out.

The stars were gone.
