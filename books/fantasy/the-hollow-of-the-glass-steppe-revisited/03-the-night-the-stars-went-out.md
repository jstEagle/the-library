## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The note came back at dusk, and it was wrong.

Emrys had been walking the border circuit since midmorning, as they did every seventh day, mallet and tuning-fork slung from their belt, chalk dust ground into the creases of their knuckles. The autumn air smelled of woodsmoke and the last of the orchard windfalls rotting sweet in the ditches. Somewhere behind them, down in the bowl of the valley, Vael Hollow was lighting its lamps one by one, the way it had every evening for three hundred years, the way it would every evening forever, because the wards held and the wards had always held and Master Aldous had said, in the last lucid month of his life, *the wards will outlive us all, little wren, that is the whole point of them.*

The wardstone at the Mill Road gate hummed when Emrys touched the fork to it. It had hummed the same note for as long as anyone could remember—a low, patient tone the guild's records called *the Standing Note*, the sound of a covenant at rest. Every stone on the circuit sang it. Emrys had heard it ten thousand times. They could have hummed it in their sleep, and sometimes did, and woke with it still in their throat like a word they'd forgotten the meaning of.

Tonight it sang something else.

It was only a hair off. A third of a step, maybe less, up and sideways, into a register Emrys had no name for. Not a cracked note. Not a sick note. A *new* note—the way a string sounds when someone has touched it from the other side of the wall.

Emrys stood in the road with the tuning-fork still against the stone and felt the hair on their arms lift.

"That's not funny," they told the stone, because they talked to the stones, because Master Aldous had talked to the stones, because a wright who didn't talk to her stones was a mason and nothing more. "Who's been at you?"

The stone did not answer, of course. Wardstones never answered. They only sang, and held, and waited. But the note hung in the air a moment longer than it should have, the way a bell hangs after the clapper stills, and Emrys stood in the gathering dark listening to it fade and could not make themselves believe it had been nothing.

They checked the stone's seams. They chalked its sigils and found the lines true, the geometry sound, every stroke exactly where the old diagrams said it must be. They walked the rest of the circuit with the fork in their hand and touched it to each stone in turn, and at the Shepherd's Ford stone, and the Thorn Gate stone, and the high stone above the quarry where the border bent like a drawn bow, the same wrong note answered them, one after another after another, all the way around the valley's rim.

Every stone. Singing a note no guild record had ever written down.

Emrys stood on the quarry ridge as the last light drained off the steppe beyond the border and tried to think like a wright instead of like a frightened person. There were explanations. There were always explanations. Resonance drift, when the season turned. A tremor under the valley, deep and slow. The stones aging, the way everything aged, the way the guild had aged, down to one apprentice with a mallet and a book of half-finished lessons.

*The wards will outlive us all.*

Emrys looked out past the border, to the glass steppe, where the ground went mirror-flat and silver and no one from the hollow had walked in three hundred years. The sun was down. The stars were coming out over the steppe, as they always did, in their ancient scatter—the Shepherd, the Loom, the Nine Sisters strung like beads on a wire. Emrys had spent their whole life looking at those stars from inside a valley that could not leave them and had never once been able to decide whether the looking was a comfort or a wound.

They were still looking when the stars went out.

* * *

It did not fade. That was the thing Emrys could never make anyone understand afterward, the thing that made the old-timers cross themselves and the children cry. It did not fade, did not flicker, did not dim the way a lamp dims when the oil runs low. One breath the sky over the steppe was sewn with stars, and the next breath the sky was a black cloth pulled tight over a hole in the world, and every star was gone as if it had never been lit at all.

One breath. Emrys counted it. They were still counting things in the first hour, because counting was a wright's discipline, because numbers held the shape of the world when nothing else did.

The valley did not notice at first. Lamps were lit. Supper smoke rose. Somewhere down in Vael a fiddle started up at the Drowned Ox, playing the harvest tune with its usual three missed notes. The hollow had no windows facing the steppe; the hollow had never needed windows facing the steppe. Only the wardwrights watched the border, and there was only one wardwright left, and she—*they*, Emrys corrected themself, the way Master Aldous had taught them, *the wards don't care what you are, only that you're true*—was standing alone on a quarry ridge with a tuning-fork gone silent in their hand.

Then the stones began to sing.

All of them. At once. Not the Standing Note, not the wrong note from the morning, but every note at every pitch the guild had ever catalogued and a hundred it hadn't, a chord the size of the valley, rising out of the earth from the Mill Road gate and the Shepherd's Ford and the Thorn Gate and the high stone above the quarry, rising out of the ground under Emrys's boots, rising out of the bedrock of Vael Hollow itself, and the sound was so vast and so wrong that the fiddle at the Drowned Ox stopped mid-phrase, and the dogs began to howl, and Emrys fell to their knees on the ridge with their hands over their ears and their teeth aching in their skull.

The chord held for the length of three heartbeats. Emrys counted those too.

Then every wardstone in Vael Hollow cracked.

They heard it before they saw it—a sound like the sky clearing its throat, like a mountain shifting in its sleep—and then the light came, the stored light of three hundred years of held covenant pouring out of the stones all around the valley's rim at once, a ring of white fire burning through the dusk, and Emrys was on their feet and running without deciding to run, down off the ridge toward the high stone, because that was what a wardwright did, that was the whole of what a wardwright was, *you go to the broken thing*, and the white light was dying even as they ran, guttering from white to gold to a dull ember-red, and by the time Emrys reached the high stone it was over.

The stone stood in its socket of old mortar and older prayer, and it was split from crown to root, one clean crack, as neat as if it had been cloven with an axe. Through the crack Emrys could see the dark of the steppe, unbroken, unwarded, open as a door.

They put their hand against it. The stone was cold. Wardstones were never cold. Wardstones held the warmth of the covenant the way a hearthstone held the warmth of fire, hours, days, a patient banked heat that a wright could read like a letter. This stone was cold as the underside of a grave-marker, and the sigils Emrys had chalked that morning were gone—not smudged, not worn, *gone*, the stone smooth where they'd been drawn, as if the rock itself had forgotten them.

"No," Emrys said. Their voice sounded very small under all that empty sky. "No, no—you've held for three hundred years, you don't get to—"

The stone said nothing. The steppe said nothing. The sky over the steppe said nothing, and it was the nothing of the sky that was worst of all, because Emrys realized, standing there with their hand on a dead wardstone, that they had never in their life seen that sky without stars. The hollow's oldest maps drew the steppe with the stars over it, the same stars, always, the Shepherd and the Loom and the Nine Sisters, drawn by hands three centuries dead who had also never seen them absent. Even the founders' maps. Even the founders had only ever known a starred sky.

And the founders had gone out there. The founders had walked onto the steppe and sealed it behind them, and the records said they had gone to keep the covenant from the far side, and the records said a great many things, and Master Aldous had said, near the end, in one of his wandering hours: *we wrote down what we could bear to remember, little wren. That's not the same as what happened.*

The dark over the steppe was total. And in that total dark, at the far edge of the glass flats where the border had been, where the wards had been, where nothing living had crossed in three hundred years—

Emrys saw movement.

* * *

At first they thought it was the glass. The steppe's mirror flats caught and bent every light, and the valley's ring of dying wardfire had thrown reflections for a few mad minutes, and eyes made pictures when they were afraid. Emrys knew this. Emrys had been taught this. *A wright's first tool is doubt*, Master Aldous said. *Doubt the omen, doubt the vision, doubt the voice in the stone. Doubt everything twice, then believe it once.*

Emrys doubted. The movement came again.

It was walking. That was the horror of it, the plain, stupid, human horror of it: the figures were *walking*, upright, unhurried, in a line, out of the starless dark and across the glass flats toward the broken border, and there were nine of them, and Emrys knew it was nine the way you know the number of your own fingers, because the guild had nine names read aloud every autumn and Emrys had spent their whole life being told that the nine were out there, beyond the wards, gone where the guild could not follow, and that this was right, and good, and the shape of the world.

Nine figures, crossing the border where nothing living should pass.

They did not carry lights. They did not need them. Where each figure stepped, the glass of the steppe gave back no reflection—not a dark reflection, not a shadow, but *no* reflection, a figure-shaped absence in the mirror of the world, as if the steppe itself refused to remember them. Emrys could see the flats behind them through the place where they should have been doubled.

Emrys stood behind the cracked high stone with their heart slamming and did the arithmetic of terror. The border was a mile off, down the quarry road. The figures moved at a walk. A walk was slow. A walk was time. Emrys could run for the hollow, raise the alarm, get the council, get Wren—old Wren, half-mad, half-deaf, the only other soul alive who had ever stood a ward circuit—and be back with witnesses before the nine reached the gate.

They did not run. Later they would try to explain this, to Wren, to the council, to themself, and never quite manage it. But in the moment, with the sky above the steppe empty as an unwritten page, Emrys Lockridge stood at the broken border of the only world they had ever known and *watched*, because some deep-buried part of them understood that this was the last night of the world as it had been, and that a wardwright's final duty, above the mending and the tuning and the tending, was to witness. *Someone has to see it true*, Master Aldous had said, *or the mending is only guesswork.*

So Emrys saw them true.

They had been people once. That was the second horror, worse than the walking, worse than the missing reflections: the figures had the shapes of people, the proportions, the set of the shoulders, the tilt of a head listening to something. One was tall and carried their arms like a mason. One was small and quick, with a stride like Wren's. One wore what might have been the rotted ghost of a guildmaster's chain, links of dull metal hanging where a collar of office had been. They had been people, and they had gone out onto the steppe three hundred years ago, and something out there had spent three centuries unmaking them in slow measure, hollowing them like reeds, and now they were coming back.

Their eyes were the worst thing. The moon was up—Emrys clung to that, the moon was still up, the moon had not gone out, whatever had eaten the stars had left the moon alone, and Emrys would think about what that meant for the rest of their life—and by the moon's light Emrys saw the figures' faces as they came up the quarry road, and their faces were calm, and patient, and terribly sad, and their eyes were black. Not dark. Black. Starless, all the way through, holes in the faces where eyes should have been, and when the tall one's gaze found Emrys crouched behind the cracked stone, Emrys felt that gaze like a hand laid flat against their chest.

The nine stopped at the border line. At the exact line, Emrys realized, the line the wards had drawn, the line that was no longer warded but was still, in some older and deeper grammar, a line. They stood in the road in the moonlight, nine hollowed founders with starless eyes, and the tall one raised its head, and its voice, when it came, was the voice of a person speaking through the memory of a throat. It was quiet. It carried anyway, the way the ward-chord had carried, the way true things carry.

"Emrys Lockridge," it said.

Emrys had never told it their name. Emrys had never seen it before in their life. But the guild rolls were short, and the guild's living members numbered one, and perhaps the whole world knew it now, the way the whole world knows the last candle in a dark room.

"The covenant is called in," the founder said. "The debt is due at solstice. Bring the hollow to hear it, last wright, or we will bring it ourselves."

Down in the valley, the fiddle at the Drowned Ox had started up again, playing the harvest tune, three missed notes, the same three missed notes, and the lamps burned in their windows, and the whole of Vael Hollow went on being warm and small and certain that the wards would hold, because the wards had always held, because Master Aldous had promised, because no one had ever imagined a night with no stars in it.

Emrys got up off their knees. Their legs were shaking. Their hands, when they looked at them, were steady—a wright's hands, their master's hands, hands taught to go to the broken thing.

"Solstice," Emrys said, and their voice cracked on the word, and they hated it, and they said it again anyway, louder, so it would carry the mile down into the valley and the three hundred years back up to the founders both at once. "Then you'd better tell me what the debt *is*."

The tall founder smiled. It was the smile of someone remembering how.

"Come to the guild door at moonset," it said, "and we will read you the bill."

And the nine turned as one, and walked back down the quarry road into the starless dark, leaving no reflections behind them, and Emrys stood alone at the broken edge of the world, counting the time until moonset.
