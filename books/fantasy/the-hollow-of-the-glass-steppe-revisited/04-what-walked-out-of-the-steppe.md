## 4. What Walked Out of the Steppe

Emrys did not go down to the valley at once.

They stood at the quarry's broken rim for a long time after the founders' shapes had dissolved into the dark, because going anywhere felt like agreeing that the world still had edges, that the night was a night and not the inside of a closed eye. The wardstone at their feet lay in three pieces, its hum gone out of it like breath out of a body, and Emrys kept one hand flat on the largest shard as if they could hold the hollow together by touch alone.

The stars were still gone.

That was the thing their mind kept snagging on, the way a sleeve snags on a nail. Not the revenants — though the memory of them made something cold move behind Emrys's sternum — but the sky. Three hundred years of nights had been stitched with those lights. Every wardwright learned them before they learned their letters: the Shepherd, the Nine Sisters, the Long Wanderer that rose only in autumn. Emrys had spent their whole life under a ceiling they never thought to question, and now it was simply *not there*, and the dark above Vael Hollow was so total and so close that Emrys half expected to raise a hand and find it wet.

*Come to the guild door at moonset.*

There would be no moonset tonight. There was no moon. Emrys understood that the word meant only: when you can wait no longer. When the waiting itself becomes impossible. That was how the founders measured time, perhaps — not by light, but by the exhaustion of the people beneath it.

Emrys picked up the wardstone shards, wrapped them in their coat because carrying them loose felt like carrying bones, and started down the quarry road.

* * *

Wren had not slept either.

She sat in her crooked house on Tallow Lane with every candle she owned burning — eleven of them, jammed into saucers and bottles and one hollowed turnip — because old habits died hard and older fears died harder. She had been a mender for forty years before her hands shook too badly for fine work, and a mender's first law was this: *when something breaks, look for what changed.* Nothing had changed tonight except everything, and so Wren did what she always did when the world refused sense. She made tea she didn't drink and talked to her dead husband's chair.

"Stars don't go out," she told the chair. "That's the whole point of stars. They're the one thing that doesn't."

The chair said nothing, being a chair, which was more courtesy than the night outside deserved.

She heard them coming before she saw them — not footsteps, exactly, but a sound like frost forming, like the tick of ice taking hold of a pond — and she went to her window and pulled back the curtain, and there they were in the lane: nine tall figures walking down the middle of Tallow Lane where no one had walked since the flood year, moving with the unhurried gait of people who owned the ground and had merely been away.

Their eyes were wrong. That was what struck Wren first, even across the distance and the candle-glow through wavy glass. Not glowing, not black — *empty*, the way a well is empty, depth without anything at the bottom of it. Starless eyes. She'd heard the phrase her whole life from guild elders who spoke of the founders the way farmers speak of weather: distant, necessary, not to be argued with. She had assumed it was metaphor.

It was not metaphor. It was a description.

And behind them, in their wake, the frost followed — climbing the doorposts of houses, feathering across windows, silvering the cobbles of Tallow Lane — and where it touched, Wren noticed with a lurch, the candles in her own room guttered though no draft moved.

"Oh," she said softly, to the chair, to the dead, to herself. "Oh, you poor things."

Because here was the other thing, the thing she would tell Emrys later by firelight with her voice low and steady while everyone else wanted to talk about war: the figures did not walk like conquerors. They walked like beggars who had finally stopped being ashamed of begging. There was a hunch to the tallest one's shoulders, a carefulness in the way its hands hung, as if it had learned over a very long time that reaching for things only hurt.

The past wasn't arriving with an army, Wren thought. It was arriving with a bill. And bills, unlike armies, could be read.

She put on her boots, took down her mender's satchel from the hook where it had hung gathering dust for six years, and went out into the frost.

* * *

The guild hall stood at the center of the hollow the way a heart stands in a body, which is to say: taken for granted until it stops. Its doors were oak, older than the town around them, carved with the nine names of the founding covenant and, beneath them, the guild's motto worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands: *WHAT IS KEPT IS KEPT FOR ALL.*

By the time Emrys arrived, half the hollow had gathered in the square — three hundred souls in nightclothes and coats thrown over them, clustered in frightened knots, torches hissing in the damp air. The council had gotten there first, all seven of them in their formal gray, standing on the guild steps with the particular stiffness of people performing authority they no longer felt. Speaker Aldous Vane was mid-speech when Emrys pushed through the crowd, his voice pitched to carry, full of reassurances that convinced no one, least of all himself.

"—a temporary phenomenon, a fluctuation of—"

He stopped. Everyone stopped.

The nine had come up the square without anyone seeing them arrive. One moment the far end was empty; the next it was not. They came on in silence, and the crowd parted before them the way water parts, without decision, without courage, and the frost came with them and the torches dimmed as they passed, each flame shrinking like a man holding his breath.

Emrys found themselves at the front. They hadn't meant to be. But the crowd had opened around them too — the last apprentice, the last wardwright, the one whose face everyone knew from funerals and remembrances — and there was nowhere left to stand except beside the truth.

Up close, the founders were worse and better than they had seemed on the quarry road. Worse, because Emrys could see now that they were not wearing bodies so much as remembering them — the tall one's face was a face, but rendered in the way a dream renders a face, correct in outline and subtly, horribly unfinished at the edges, as if whoever had drawn it had run out of pigment. Their skin had the grey translucence of wax held near a flame. And better — this was the part that made Emrys's throat ache — because some of them were *familiar*. The woman second from the left had Master Corvane's jaw. The broad-shouldered one had hands like Old Tam's hands, scarred and clever. These were not monsters out of the steppe. These were guildmasters. These were the faces on the remembrance wall, come home.

"You are the last," the tall founder said. Its voice was dry wind through a doorway. Its starless eyes settled on Emrys with a weight like a hand on the shoulder. "The last who keeps the wards."

"I'm the last," Emrys said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Emrys Lockridge. Apprentice to Guildmaster Corvane, ninth of the name, who taught me the keeping rites." A pause. "All he had time to teach me."

Something moved across the founder's unfinished face. Recognition, maybe. Or grief wearing recognition's clothes.

"Corvane's line," it said. "Good. Then the words will not need explaining twice." It raised one translucent hand, and the crowd's murmurings died, and the frost on the cobblestones glittered like a field of fallen, murdered stars. "People of the hollow. We are the nine who built your walls. We are the nine your walls unmade. Hear the terms."

* * *

Wren arrived at the edge of the square just in time to hear the word *unmade*.

She had crossed half the town following the cold, and now she wedged herself between a cooper's cart and a shuttered fruit stall, close enough to see, and she watched the faces of her neighbors as the tall founder spoke, and what she saw frightened her more than the revenants did. Because the hollow's people were listening the way people listen to a doctor delivering a sentence — already surrendering, already rehearsing their endurance — and not one face in that crowd showed anger. Not one showed the simple human outrage of *who do you think you are?*

Three hundred years of living behind walls teaches a people to receive orders. Wren had forgotten that. Standing in the frost with her mender's satchel heavy in her hand, she remembered.

"The covenant," the founder was saying, "was written in two clauses. Your guilds kept the first and burned the second. You gave us the walls we asked for — and you forgot what we asked for them *from*. Every year, the keeping rite. Every year, the tithe of warmth. For ninety years you paid it, and then a guildmaster decided the tithe was superstition, and paid it with words instead of fire, and we starved so slowly we did not notice we were starving until we had forgotten our own—" It stopped. Its hand closed, slowly, on nothing.

"We are owed," said a second founder, the one with Tam's hands, and its voice cracked like river ice. "Ninety years of warmth withheld. Two hundred and ten years of forgetting. The debt is written in us. We carry it the way you carry blood."

"What is the payment?" Aldous Vane called from the steps, and his voice had the bright brittle ring of a man who has already decided what answer he wants. "Name it. Gold? Land? Whatever the hollow has, the hollow will consider—"

"We do not want gold." The tall founder turned its starless eyes on him, and Aldous Vane, to his credit or his terror, did not step back, though his hands gripped the rail of the steps white-knuckled. "Gold does not burn. We want what was taken from us at the founding and never returned. We want the hearth-fire. We want the *first* fire — the one your first speaker carried out of the First Hearth and hid behind your walls, the one your wards have fed on ever since while we froze on the steppe outside them."

Murmurs ran through the crowd. Emrys felt the murmurs rather than heard them, a shiver passing through three hundred bodies. Wren, in her corner by the fruit stall, went very still. Because she was old enough — old enough to have known the guild archivists of a previous generation, old enough to have heard drunk elders mutter about the *closed shelf*, the ledger no one opened — and the founder's words slid into a hollow place in her memory that she had never known was hollow until something fit it.

"There is a debt," the tall founder said, and its voice dropped until every soul in the square leaned forward to catch it, "and debts of this kind are called due at the turning of the year. You have until the winter solstice. Nineteen days. Bring the first fire to the border at moonrise on solstice night, and set it free, and the account is closed, and your walls may stand or fall as you choose. Fail—" and here the founder paused, and the pause was worse than any threat, because it was gentle, "—and we will take what we are owed ourselves. All of it. The warmth out of every hearth. The color out of every window. The names out of every mouth. We will unmake the hollow the way we were unmade, slowly, and completely, and you will not even know what you have lost, because losing everything feels, at the end, exactly like falling asleep."

Silence. Total silence, except for a child somewhere crying quietly and being hushed.

Then Aldous Vane stepped forward, and Wren saw it happen, saw the whole shape of the next nineteen days assemble itself behind his eyes — she saw him choose the old way, the easy way, the way of walls — because he bowed. He bowed to the revenants, deep and formal, and he said:

"The council accepts the debt. The council will determine the manner of repayment, according to the guild's own laws, and deliver it to the border at solstice. You have the hollow's word."

The tall founder looked at him for a long moment. Then at Emrys. Then, briefly — and Wren would swear this to her dying day, swear it over tea and candles to anyone who'd listen — its gaze swept the crowd and snagged, just for a heartbeat, on a bent old woman with a mender's satchel, and something almost like hope flickered through the emptiness of its eyes, and it said, very quietly, so that only those nearest heard:

"Read the second clause. Someone must remember how to read it."

And the nine turned, and the frost went with them, and the torches stood up straight again in the suddenly ordinary dark, and the crowd exhaled like one enormous animal.

* * *

Afterward, the square emptied in slow, shocked eddies, and the council withdrew into the guild hall behind closed doors, and Emrys stood alone in front of the carved doors with the motto under their hands — *what is kept is kept for all* — feeling the words shift meaning underneath their fingers like a trick of the light.

*Kept for all.* Kept for whom? Kept from whom?

"You heard it too."

Wren materialized at their elbow the way she always had, silent as a cat and twice as opinionated, her breath steaming in the cold. Her eyes were red-rimmed but level.

"Heard what?" Emrys said, though they had.

"'Read the second clause.'" Wren glanced around — the square was nearly empty now, just torches and frost and one drunk sleeping against the cooper's cart — and lowered her voice anyway. "I've heard that phrase before, Emrys Lockridge. Forty years I worked for the guild, and twice — twice — I saw archivists go pale over it. There's a shelf in the archive that's been locked since before Corvane's time. The closed shelf. Nobody says what's on it. And tonight a dead founder walks into my street and asks us to read it, and the council's answer is to bow and promise payment and lock the question away with everything else." She spat, delicately, into the frost. "Vane's going to pay this debt the way the guild pays everything. With somebody's life dressed up as duty."

"You don't know that."

"Don't I?" Wren looked at them, and her look was terrible and kind at once, the look of a mender assessing a wound she already knew the depth of. "Last apprentice. Last wardwright. Only one of you left to answer when the names are read. Tell me, child — when the council sits down to decide what the hollow can spare, what do you imagine is at the top of the list?"

Emrys didn't answer. The frost glittered. Somewhere across the square, the drunk began snoring.

"Nineteen days," Wren said. She hefted her satchel onto her shoulder, an old soldier's gesture, absurd and magnificent on a woman of seventy. "I'm going home to dig through forty years of notes. You're going to find a way into that archive. And tomorrow, whatever the council decides, you and I are going to sit down with the guild's oldest ledgers and find out what a *second clause* looks like — before Vane finds someone to pay the first one."

She was three steps away when she stopped, and turned back, and for a moment the lamplight caught her face and Emrys saw that her hands were shaking.

"They remembered my face," she said quietly. "Did you see? Out of three hundred people, that creature looked at *me*. Things that forget their own names don't look at strangers like that." She shook her head slowly. "Whatever they were, Emrys — whatever they are — they weren't asking for a sacrifice tonight. They were asking for a reader."

Then she went off down Tallow Lane, small and straight-backed against the dark, and Emrys stood alone before the guild doors with the motto smooth under their fingers and nineteen days ticking down like a pulse, and did not sleep, and did not go in, because the doors were locked, and the keys, they would learn by morning, were already in Speaker Vane's pocket — along with, though Emrys did not yet know it, the fate of everyone in Vael Hollow, sealed up tight behind seven votes and a locked shelf, waiting to be paid.
