## 5. A Ledger of Debts

The guild archive had not been opened in forty years.

I knew this because I was the one who locked it, three days after the revenants came to our door with their starless eyes and their terrible arithmetic. I had pressed my palm to the ward-seal myself, meaning only to keep out the cold, and felt the old magic settle over the door like a shroud. No one had asked me to open it since. No one had wanted to know what was inside.

Now, on the morning after the council's first failed attempt to answer the founders' ultimatum, I stood before that door with a lamp in one hand and Wren's key in the other—the key she had pressed into my palm at the remembrance fire, muttering that I'd need it sooner than I thought. Old women and their keys. Old guilds and their doors.

The lock turned with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

Inside, the air tasted of dust and tallow and time. The archive was smaller than the legends claimed—a single stone room beneath the guild hall, shelved with ledgers whose spines had faded to the color of bone. Nine generations of Wardwrights had worked and argued and died above this room, and all their knowing was here, stacked and waiting, patient as sediment.

I lit every lamp I could find. Then I began.

* * *

I will not pretend I knew what I was looking for. The revenants at the guild door had spoken only one sentence whole: *The covenant's debt is due by the winter solstice, or the hollow is unmade.* Everything else they said came out in fragments, in that dry rustle of voices like wind through a dead field—phrases half-finished, words that crumbled as they left their mouths. But one fragment had lodged in me and would not work loose. The tall one, the one who might once have been called Aldous Vael himself, had leaned close to the door and rasped:

*"Read what we wrote. Not what they wrote after."*

What we wrote. What they wrote after.

So I read. Ledger by ledger, year by year, I walked backward through the guild's memory, hunting for the moment the covenant was born. The later volumes were thick with procedure—ward rotations, tithe schedules, the meticulous bookkeeping of a valley that had decided safety was the same thing as virtue. I learned how much wool the eastern farms owed the guild each autumn. I learned which families had paid the *watching-fee* and which had been granted exemption for service. I learned, page after page, how thoroughly the hollow had taught itself to think of magic as debt.

It was near dawn on the second day, my eyes raw and the lamp oil low, when I found it.

Not in a ledger at all. In a box.

It had been shoved behind the oldest shelf, a flat case of blackwood gone grey with age, sealed with wax stamped by a sigil no living guildsman would recognize: a hearth flame cupped in two open hands. My hands shook so badly I could barely break the seal. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that crumbled at the touch, lay a single sheet of vellum—and beneath it, a second document, newer, written in the sharp administrative hand of some guildmaster four centuries dead.

The vellum was the older thing. I could feel it, the way you can feel the heat still living in a stone pulled from a fire. The script across it was not the guild's formal hand but something looser, warmer, letters that leaned toward each other like people sharing a table. And it was signed—not by one name, but by nine.

Nine names. Nine candles. Nine founders.

My breath fogged the lamplight. I read it once in silence. Then I read it aloud, because some things need a voice to be believed:

*"Let this stand between the hollow and the steppe, between hearthfire and hunger-wind, so long as both peoples keep faith:*

*To any soul who comes to our border, hollow-born or steppe-born, storm-tossed or star-led, we pledge HEARTH AND HAND. There shall be warmth offered without price. There shall be work shared without rank. What one people has, the other may ask for, and what is asked in need shall be given in welcome.*

*This is the whole of the covenant. It asks nothing. It offers everything. It holds so long as we hold it together."*

That was all. That was everything.

No blood-price. No sacrifice clause. No *one life for the many*. The covenant my guild had tended for nine generations, the covenant the revenants had come to collect on, the covenant I had spent my whole life believing was a wall built on bones—it had begun as an invitation.

I sat down hard on the cold floor and read the second document, the forgery, with hands that would not stop trembling.

It was shorter. Colder. The same administrative hand that had filled the ledgers above me. Someone—some guildmaster, some council, some frightened committee of the comfortable—had taken the founders' promise and rewritten it in the language of accounting. Where the original pledged *hearth and hand*, the rewrite demanded *a keeping*: "the covenant requires maintenance; the maintenance requires offering; the offering, in years of great need, may be measured in lives." They had even invented a term for it, a soft ugly word designed to sound like weather rather than murder: *the winter toll.*

They hadn't just altered the covenant. They had inverted it. They had taken a door thrown open and turned it into a gate that only opened outward, over the bodies of whoever the valley could bear to lose.

And then they had buried the truth in a blackwood box behind the oldest shelf, and taught nine generations of children—including one small apprentice named Emrys Lockridge—that the walls around Vael Hollow were holy.

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the lamps to burn down twice. When I finally climbed the stairs into the grey morning, I carried the vellum against my chest like something newborn, and my face must have shown it, because the first person I met in the guild hall—a mender's boy hauling water—took one look at me and stepped back as if from a stranger.

Perhaps he was right to. I no longer recognized the guild I belonged to.

* * *

The council convened at noon in the long chamber above the hall, and by then everyone in Vael Hollow already knew what they intended to decide.

News travels fast in a valley. Faster than shame.

I arrived early and took a seat along the wall, where apprentices sit, and laid the vellum on my knees beneath my cloak. Speaker Maren Corvane presided from the high chair, her silver hair bound tight, her face arranged into the particular gravity that hollow leaders wear when they are about to do something unforgivable and want it to look like wisdom. Around the crescent table sat the twelve: farmers and smiths and two guild elders, respectable people, tired people, people who had spent five sleepless nights listening to the wind carry fragments of revenant voices up from the border.

"The matter before us," Corvane began, without preamble, "is the solstice."

No one spoke. Outside the shuttered windows, I could hear the hollow going about its business too loudly, too deliberately—the ring of hammers, the barking of dogs, all the ordinary sounds of a town pretending very hard to be ordinary.

"The revenants have given us until midwinter," she continued. "Our scholars tell us the wards cannot be rebuilt—they cracked through their foundations, and the craft to mend them died with the guild." Her eyes flicked to me, briefly, and away. An apology, maybe. Or an inventory. "We have one option the founders themselves sanctioned. The covenant, as every child in this valley learns it, permits a winter toll in extremity. One life, freely given, to satisfy the debt. The wards take the payment and hold."

"Freely given," someone repeated, as if tasting the phrase.

"That is the requirement. It cannot be coerced. Which is why—" and here Corvane's voice did something careful, something rehearsed, "—we have prepared a list of those whose devotion to the hollow is beyond question, and whose circumstances... permit such a gift."

A list. They had made a list. While I was reading the truth by lamplight, these twelve had been sitting in this room making a *list*.

I stood up. I didn't decide to; my body simply refused to stay seated, the way a hand refuses to touch a hot iron. Every face turned toward me, and I heard the murmur go around—*the last wardwright, the last wardwright*—and understood, in one sick drop of the stomach, whose name sat first on that list.

"Speaker Corvane." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Before this council votes, there is something you must see."

"You were not called upon, apprentice."

"I was not called upon to be sacrificed either, yet here we are discussing it." The murmur sharpened. I unwrapped the vellum and held it high, the founders' warm leaning letters catching the window-light. "I found the original covenant. The true one. In our own archive, sealed behind the oldest shelf, where someone put it to be forgotten. Will you hear it? Will you hear what your founders actually wrote?"

For a heartbeat—one long, teetering heartbeat—I thought the room might listen. Elder Haskam, oldest of the twelve, leaned forward with his clouded eyes fixed on the vellum. A woman near the door whispered, "*Hearth and hand?* I've never heard that phrase."

Corvane rose. She did not look at the parchment. She looked at me, and her expression was the expression of someone closing a window against rain.

"Apprentice Lockridge has spent two nights alone among old papers," she said, loud and level. "Grief does strange things to the mind. Whatever fragment they've found, it changes nothing. The revenants' demand stands. The debt is real. The question before us is only *who*."

"The question," I said, "is whether the debt was ever meant to be paid this way at all—"

"*Who,*" she said again, and struck the table once with the flat of her hand, and the sound went through the chamber like a crack in glass. "The council will vote."

Twelve hands. I watched them rise one by one—farmers I had known all my life, a smith whose daughter I had taught to whistle through cupped hands, faces I had loved in the unthinking way you love the scenery of your childhood. Eleven hands went up without hesitation. The twelfth, Elder Haskam's, hovered, trembled, and rose last, his eyes never meeting mine.

Twelve votes. One life. Me.

"There being no dissent," Corvane said, "the council names Emrys Lockridge, last of the Wardwrights, as the acceptable payment for the winter toll. The giving shall occur at the border stones on solstice eve."

The word *acceptable* rang in my skull. Acceptable. As though I were a coin checked for clipping. As though my whole life had been weighed in some back room and found sufficient.

And the worst of it—the thing that still wakes me some nights—is that no one in that room looked cruel. They looked sorrowful. They looked grave. They had dressed the murder of their last guildsman in mourning clothes and called it duty, and every one of them believed, sincerely believed, that they were saving the hollow. That was the horror of it. Not malice. Just arithmetic. Nine generations of forged ledgers had taught them that love could be entered as a debit.

I looked around the chamber one last time—at the carved beams my master had restored, at the guild sigil above the high chair, a flame in cupped hands, copied wrong, the hands closed instead of open—and I understood that I was already a ghost to these people. They had voted my death with the same efficiency they brought to tithe schedules.

Then the doors banged open, and Old Wren walked in.

She moved slowly, leaning on her stick, her white hair unbound, wearing the full regalia of a retired guild mender that she had presumably not worn since before I was born—and she was laughing. A dry, crackling, terrible laugh that silenced the chamber more completely than any shout.

"A freely given life," she said. "That's the rule, isn't it? Freely given. Well then." She planted herself before the high chair and jabbed her stick at Corvane like a sword. "Here's mine, freely given: I defy this vote. I defy this council. And I'll tell you something else for free—" her voice dropped into the register she used for telling children true things about the dark, "—you've got the covenant backwards, every one of you, and I've known it sixty years, and now the boy's found the proof, and you're going to kill him anyway because the truth costs less than he does. Is that the arithmetic? Is that the hollow's famous honor?"

"Wrenna Grieve," Corvane said coldly, "you hold no seat on this council."

"No," Wren agreed. "I gave it up when you lot started writing down lies and calling them law." She turned, slow as a door on a cold hinge, and faced the room. "Anyone else? Anyone at all? One voice?"

Silence. Twelve grave, sorrowful, murderous faces.

Wren nodded, as though she'd expected exactly this, and maybe she had. "Then cast me out with him," she said. "Strip my name from the guild rolls. I'd sooner be exiled with the truth than seated among liars."

"So be it," said Corvane. "Wrenna Grieve is cast out of the Wardwrights' Guild. Let it be recorded."

Let it be recorded. In another ledger, of course. Another line in the account.

I gathered the vellum against my chest. Wren took my arm with her bird-boned hand, firm as a root. And as we walked the length of that chamber—past the council, past the sigil, past everything I had ever belonged to—I heard behind us the quiet scraping of chairs as the twelve resumed their agenda, as though the sentencing of the last wardwright had been no more than a difficult item, now handled, crossed off, done.

At the doors I stopped. I couldn't help it. I turned back.

"For what it's worth," I said, and my voice broke and I let it, "the founders are coming to collect a debt of *welcome*. You're about to pay them in the exact opposite. May the stars forgive us all—when there are stars again to do the forgiving."

The doors closed behind us on silence.

Outside, the afternoon light lay cold and gold on the cobbles, and somewhere beyond the border stones the revenants waited in their starless patience, and somewhere south of them the glass steppe stretched toward a First Hearth I had only heard of in forbidden songs. Wren squeezed my arm.

"Well, lad," she said. "You've been cast out, condemned, and disowned before supper. How does it feel?"

I looked back once at the guild hall—my home, my inheritance, my betrayer—and found, to my surprise, that the grief had a strange bright edge to it, like frost in sunlight.

"It feels," I said, "like the wards just came down."

"Good," said Wren. "Because we've a gate to reach before nightfall, and I know three people waiting there who'll want to meet you—and one of them, if my old bones haven't forgotten everything, used to be a founder."
