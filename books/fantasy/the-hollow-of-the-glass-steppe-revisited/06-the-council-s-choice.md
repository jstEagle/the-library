## 6. The Council's Choice

The council chamber had been a chapel once, back when the hollow still believed in something older than itself. Emrys could see it in the bones of the room: the arched windows now shuttered with wardglass, the altar-stone repurposed as a speaker's dais, the faint ghost of soot above the hearth where a fire had burned before the founders' fire became the only fire anyone spoke of. Nine chairs sat in a crescent. Nine speakers, nine faces turned toward the single stool set in the center of the floor, and Emrys sitting on that stool like a specimen pinned under glass.

They had been talking for an hour.

Emrys let the words wash past without catching them. Speaker Aldous Vane droned about precedent, about the ledger of debts, about the *proportions of payment* as though a life were grain to be measured at market. Speaker Maren Cole kept her eyes on her folded hands and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of speech. Old Speaker Harrow—Harrow, who had taught Emrys their first ward-chalk alphabet, whose knuckles had guided theirs around the first crooked circle they ever drew—Harrow would not look at them either.

That was the thing Emrys would remember afterward, in the years when memory came uninvited: not the vote, not the verdict, but the way no one looked at them. As if looking might make the debt personal. As if Emrys were already becoming an abstraction, a line item, a *payment*, and to meet their eyes would be to admit that payments breathed.

"—and the guild archive is clear on this point," Vane was saying. He stood at the dais with the covenant fragment laid out before him on a cloth, handled like evidence at a trial rather than what it was—a promise someone had made, once, in good faith. "The covenant requires blood of the hollow's own. The founders' revenants named the solstice. They named the terms. And the covenant—" his voice took on the cadence of a man reading scripture he did not believe but found useful, "—the covenant names a warden."

The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well.

"The last wardwright," said Speaker Ossia, from the end of the crescent. She was the youngest of them, forty perhaps, and she at least had the decency to look ill. "The last apprentice. The one who tends our walls."

"The one whose master died teaching her duties she never finished learning," Emrys said.

It was the first thing they'd spoken aloud since being called in. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Nine heads turned—not quite meeting their eyes, but turning—and Emrys understood suddenly that they had been expected to sit quietly through their own condemnation, the way the guild's dead had sat quietly through worse. That was what apprentices were for. That was what the last apprentice was *for*.

"Master Corvane spent eleven years trying to finish my training," Emrys went on, rising from the stool because sitting felt too much like kneeling. "He died with half the high rituals untaught. Half the deep wards unmaintained. You are proposing to pay your debt with a wardwright who is barely a wardwright, and then—who will tend your border? Who will hold the stones when they crack again?"

"The revenants will not return once the debt is paid," Vane said.

"You don't know that. No one knows that. You have a fragment, Speaker. A fragment you've read backward for two hundred years." Emrys pointed at the cloth, at the old vellum with its burnt edges and its careful, dead hand. "Read me the clause. Read it aloud, word for word, the way it's written. Not the way the Third Guild interpreted it. Not the way your grandmother taught you. Word. For. Word."

Vane's jaw tightened. But he was a man of procedure, and procedure was a cage he had built around himself so long ago he'd forgotten it was a cage, and so he bent to the fragment and read:

"*And the hollow shall keep hearth and hand toward those beyond the wall, and give of its blood—*"

"Stop." Wren's voice came from the doorway.

Emrys hadn't heard her come in. Old Wren, retired mender of the guild, half-mad by every polite account and entirely mad by every honest one, stood in the council door with her grey hair loose down her back and her mender's satchel slung across her chest like a bandolier, and her eyes—those watery, wandering eyes that could never seem to fix on anything—were fixed on Vane with terrible precision.

"That's not how it goes," she said. "I was there."

A murmur ran the crescent. Speakers exchanged glances. Vane sighed the sigh of a man humoring a child. "Mender Wren. This session is closed to—"

"I was there," Wren repeated, louder, stepping into the chamber. Her boots left small wet prints on the stone; she had walked through the rain from the mending-house without stopping, Emrys realized, without even her coat. "Not at the writing—I'm not that old, whatever Aldous tells his grandchildren—but I mended the hands that copied it. I sat with Guildmother Ellery for three winters while her mind went soft, and she taught me the covenant the way her mother taught her, which is to say *whole*. Not in fragments. Whole." She stopped in the center of the floor, between the crescent of chairs and Emrys, and turned to face all nine speakers at once, and Emrys saw her hands shaking and saw her plant her feet anyway. "You've cut the sentence in half and built a knife out of it."

"Then recite it whole," Emrys said quietly. "If you know it."

Wren looked back at them, and for a moment her eyes lost their focus entirely—went somewhere far away, some winter room where an old woman's voice had counted words into her like coins into a jar—and then she closed her eyes and began:

"*And the hollow shall keep hearth and hand toward those beyond the wall, and give of its blood freely, as the hearth gives warmth to any hand that asks; and this shall not be taken, but given; and what is given in welcome shall sustain the wall, and what is taken in fear shall starve it.*"

Silence.

"*Taken*," Wren said, opening her eyes. "*In fear.* Do you understand what you're about to do? You're not paying a debt. You're confirming it. You're telling the founders, after two hundred years, *yes—you were right about us. We are people who take.*"

Speaker Maren Cole finally lifted her head. "The alternative is that the hollow is unmade. Every soul in it. Is that your proposal, mender? That we do nothing?"

"My proposal," Wren said, "is that we find out why the payment stopped working before we spend a life on a broken mechanism."

"We don't have time to *find out*," said Harrow, and his voice cracked on it, and Emrys watched him—the man who had taught them chalk-circles, the man whose hands had been gentle over theirs—and watched him choose. "The solstice is weeks away. The revenants stand at our border every night. We have a fragment, we have a tradition, and we have—" and here his gaze slid, at long last, unwillingly, to Emrys, and Emrys watched him do it, watched him make himself say it—"we have the covenant's warden. It has always been the warden. The guild knew that. Corvane knew that. He trained the girl for exactly this."

"He trained me to *mend walls*," Emrys said. Something hot had risen in their chest and they let it rise, let it carry their voice. "He never once told me I was a payment waiting on a shelf. If he believed what you believe, he was a coward who raised a lamb and called it an heir. And if he didn't—" they swept the crescent with their eyes, daring any of them to hold the look, none of them did—"then you are doing something he would have shattered his own stones to stop."

"The vote stands regardless," Vane said, with the flat finality of a man laying the last stone of a wall. "This council does not require the warden's consent. The covenant's terms supersede individual—"

"Say it," Emrys said. "Say the whole sentence. Say *we vote to take a life in fear*. Out loud. In front of everyone. Since you're so fond of reading the words as written."

Vane did not say it. He looked at his papers instead, and called the vote.

Nine hands. Seven rose. Maren Cole kept hers folded. Speaker Ossia abstained, her face grey.

"It is decided," Vane said. "By covenant law, the warden presents herself at the border at solstice dusk. Until such time, for the security of the payment—" and here the abstraction completed itself, Emrys felt it happen, felt themselves become an object in the room's grammar, "—the warden will be kept under watch."

Two council guards stepped forward from the walls. Emrys hadn't noticed them arrive. Of course they'd been posted there all along; the vote was a formality, the verdict written before Emrys ever climbed the chapel steps. They had been summoned here not to decide but to witness.

Something in Emrys went very quiet and very cold, the way the hollow went quiet just before a wardstone cracked.

"Wren," they said. "Go home."

"I'll do no such—"

"*Go home.*" Emrys put everything into it—all the love, all the apology, all the eleven years of soup and mended bones and midnight lessons in the guild kitchen—and Wren heard it, and her mouth twisted, and for one moment she looked less than half-mad and more than half heartbroken. Then the guards reached Emrys, took an arm each, firm and not yet cruel, and Wren surged forward shouting something about covenants and cowards and the names of three separate guildmothers, and Vane snapped *"Remove her, she's a disruption,"* and the guards hesitated—she was an old woman, after all, a guild woman, *their* mender—and in that hesitation Emrys did the only mathematics that mattered.

One guard's grip was on their right arm. Their right hand hung free.

Eleven years of chalk and ritual lived in that hand. Eleven years of circles drawn in dust and salt-lines renewed in wind and the small, secret craft of *unbinding*—the least-taught art of the Wardwrights' Guild, the shame of the profession, the skill reserved for broken things: how to undo a ward gently enough that nothing shatters. How to open, without breaking.

Emrys had practiced it on knots. On locked boxes. On the little privacy-wards apprentices wove around their bunks. Never on a person, never on a binding laid by another wright, never on anything that mattered.

Their fingers moved.

It was four gestures folded inside one—a sleight of craft disguised as a stumble. Emrys let their knees buckle, gasped, sagged, and the guards adjusted their grip, and in the half-second of that adjustment Emrys's free hand traced the release-sigil against the air between the left guard's fingers and their wrist: a small, quick, spiraling motion, invisible unless you knew to look, humming with the same note the wardstone had sung three nights before the stars went out.

The guard's grip opened like a flower.

Emrys spun out of the remaining hold, shoved the first guard into the second, and ran.

Behind them the chamber erupted—shouting, the scrape of chairs, Vane's voice cracking upward into register—and ahead of them the chapel doors stood open to the rain, and Wren was somehow already there, holding one door wide with her body, her face lit with an expression Emrys had never seen on her before: fierce, lucid, *delighted*.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"I'm not running *from* the debt," Emrys gasped, taking her arm, hauling her through, feeling rather than seeing the pursuit spill out behind them into the rain. "I'm running *at* it. There's a difference. There has to be a difference—"

"There is," Wren said, pulling them left, away from the main street, into the tangle of lanes behind the mending-house where every gutter flooded and every gate hung broken and no council guard had chased a cat in thirty years. "Difference is the whole point, child. Now run properly and tell me—which way did Sable say the smugglers' gate lies?"

Emrys skidded on the wet cobbles, caught herself against a post, stared at the old woman sprinting beside her with her satchel bouncing and her grey hair streaming like smoke. "You *knew*? About Sable?"

"I mend everything in this hollow, dear. Including other people's secrets." Wren laughed—a wild, breathless, joyful sound, utterly unfit for a funeral or a flight, exactly fit for this. "Now *run*."

So Emrys ran.

Rain in their eyes, the council's voices fading behind them, the broken gates of the low quarter opening one after another like a road assembling itself out of ruin—and beneath the fear, beneath the guilt of Harrow's averted eyes and the cold arithmetic of seven raised hands, beneath all of it, something new and unfamiliar kindled in Emrys's chest, small and stubborn as a coal shielded in cupped palms.

They had spent their whole life keeping the walls intact. Tonight, for the first time, they had unmade a binding with their own two hands—and the world had not ended. Nothing had shattered. The sigil had sung its small note, and a grip had opened, and they were free in the rain.

*What is given in welcome shall sustain the wall,* Wren had said. *What is taken in fear shall starve it.*

Seven hands raised in fear. And somewhere out on the glass steppe, in the dark where the stars used to be, the revenants waited with their starless eyes—and Emrys was beginning, with the first vertiginous thrill of rebellion, to suspect that they were not waiting for a sacrifice at all.

They were waiting for someone to ask the right question.

The smugglers' gate lay somewhere past the flooded mills, at the border ruins where nothing living should pass—and tonight, Emrys meant to walk straight through it.

* * *

They found Sable at the Broken Gate an hour past midnight, exactly where the smuggler had said she'd be, leaning against a cracked wardstone with her arms crossed and an expression of profound unsurprise.

"Took you long enough," Sable said.

Wren snorted. "I told you they'd say that."

Emrys stood dripping in the border ruins, heart hammering, and looked past Sable's shoulder—at the steppe beyond, vast and black and mirror-flat, reflecting nothing now that there were no stars to reflect. Somewhere out there, the First Hearth. Somewhere out there, an answer that wasn't a life given in fear.

"Open the way," Emrys said. "Before the council catches up."

Sable smiled, slow and sharp, and pushed off from the wardstone.

"Oh," she said, "I thought you'd never ask."
