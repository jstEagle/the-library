## 7. Outcasts at the Broken Gate

The border ruins smelled of rain and old iron.

Emrys had walked the wardline since they were six years old, first trailing Master Corvane's hem through the frost-grass, later alone with a satchel of chalk and tuning rods and the particular loneliness of a person talking to stones. They knew every gate in the hollow's ring: the Salt Gate where traders once passed, the Ash Gate sealed since their grandmother's grandmother, and here, the Broken Gate itself—a horseshoe arch of fused glass and granite where the steppe began and the hollow's story ended.

Tonight it was a wound. The wardstone at its crown had cracked down the middle like a split tooth, and from the fracture came a sound Emrys had never heard a stone make: not the deep hum of working magic, but a thin, continuous whine, the note of something dying slowly rather than all at once. Beyond the arch, the glass steppe lay flat and silver under a sky that still had no stars. It went on forever. It went on without them.

*You could turn back,* Emrys thought. *You could go to the council, kneel, say yes, I'll be the payment. One life against ten thousand. That's arithmetic even Wren couldn't argue with.*

But the council hadn't asked for a life. Not really. They'd asked for a *willing* one, which was worse—it meant they wanted Emrys to agree, wanted the ledger balanced clean, wanted no guilt clinging to their hands when winter came. And Emrys had looked around the guild hall while they voted, at faces they'd known since childhood, and understood that what they were being asked to give was not blood but permission. Permission for the hollow to keep being small. To keep forgetting the sky. To call it safety.

Emrys had run instead. Out the postern door, past the tanneries, up the switchback path with the council's lanterns swinging behind them like angry fireflies, and out onto the ridge road until Vael Hollow was just a bowl of light below and the whole dark world above.

Now they stood at the Broken Gate with a stolen archive page folded against their heart—hearth and hand, hearth and hand—and waited to see who would come.

* * *

Wren came first, because Wren always did.

Emrys heard her before they saw her: the click of her walking stick on stone, arrhythmic, like a heartbeat that had forgotten its own time signature. She emerged from the shadow of the arch wrapped in three shawls and a coat that predated the guild's last century, her white hair escaping its braid in every direction, as if she'd been arguing with the wind and lost.

"You're late," she said.

"I left as fast as I could."

"You left *slow*, child. You stopped at the gate and had a whole conversation with yourself. I heard you from the road." She planted her stick and squinted up at the cracked wardstone. "Hm. It's singing flat."

"It's dying."

"Everything worth keeping sings flat before it dies." She lowered herself onto a fallen lintel with the care of someone assembling herself piece by piece. "I brought bread. Hard bread. The kind that keeps. And I brought this." She dug into some interior pocket and produced a small tin box, rattling. Inside: nine candle stubs, each burned down to a thumbnail's height. "One for each name we read last autumn. If we're going out there—" she nodded at the steppe, at all that silver nothing, "—we're taking the guild with us. All of it. Even the dead parts. Especially the dead parts."

Emrys sat beside her. For a moment neither spoke, and the broken stone whined above them like an insect trapped in amber.

"They cast you out," Emrys said. "For defying them. For me."

"Cast out." Wren snorted. "I've been cast out of better places than that hall. I was cast out of the menders' wing twice, once for biting. This is nothing new. Besides—" she bumped her shoulder against theirs, feather-light, "—you didn't run *from* anything, Emrys Lockridge. You ran *toward*. There's a difference. Old people can smell it."

"What if there's nothing to run toward? What if the First Hearth is a story?"

"Then we'll be the first people to know it's a story," Wren said comfortably, "and we can tell everyone we died finding out. Very dignified. Now." She held out the tin. "Take your candle. We light them when we cross. Guild rules."

"There is no guild anymore."

"There's you," said Wren. "That's the guild now. Congratulations. Terrible pay."

* * *

Sable came at moonrise—or where the moon should have been; the sky was still a blind gray ceiling, and the light came from nowhere and everywhere, the way it does on overcast nights when the world forgets which direction things fall.

She didn't come up the road. She came *out of the steppe*, which was how Emrys knew what she was before she said a word. No one from the hollow walked out of the steppe. People who crossed the border going outward were exiles, smugglers, or corpses, and only one of those categories walked back.

She was small and quick and dressed in layered leather the color of wet slate, a hood thrown back from a sharp brown face, eyes that moved constantly—road, sky, Emrys, Wren, road again—like a woman counting exits in a room. A long knife rode her hip, but casually, the way a carpenter carries a hammer.

"You're the wardwright," she said. It wasn't a question. "The one the council wants dead."

"Wants *given*," Emrys corrected. "There's paperwork."

"Hm." She crouched, touched two fingers to the base of the cracked wardstone, and pulled them back as if scalded. "Cold. Your whole border's gone cold. Do you know what that means?"

"That the wards are failing."

"That the wards are *hungry*." She straightened, wiping her fingers on her thigh. "I've run this line eleven years. Wardstones are warm. Always warm, even the dead ones—residual heat, like a banked stove. Yours aren't banking anymore. They're starving. Whatever's eating them from outside..." She glanced at the steppe, and for the first time her eyes stopped moving. "It's almost done."

"How do you know about the council?" Emrys asked. "About me?"

"Because Idris told me. Because Idris watches everything on this side of the flats, and he told me a wardwright ran east three days ago with a page of old paper and a face like a person deciding whether to jump." Her mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, filed down by use. "I'm Sable. I move things across the steppe that people want moved. Silk, salt, sometimes people. Mostly I move *myself*, quickly, away from trouble. So understand: I'm not here to be brave. I'm here because Idris asked, and I owe Idris, and the debt comes due whether I like it or not."

"And Idris is?"

"Coming." Sable turned to face the open steppe, and her voice dropped into something careful. "He's coming slow. He doesn't like to be seen arriving. He doesn't like to be seen at all, mostly. But he'll come, because he's the only one of his kind who still can."

"His kind," Emrys repeated. The word landed cold. "You mean the revenants. The founders. The ones who walked through my wards and told my people the debt was due."

"The same." Sable looked at them sidelong. "Problem?"

"You want me to walk into the steppe with one of the things that gave the hollow until solstice to bleed."

"I want you to walk into the steppe with the only one of them who remembers his own name." Sable's voice hardened, flint on flint. "The rest of them are echoes wearing shapes. He's a man. He refused the hollowing—paid a price for it, a price I won't speak for him—and he has spent longer than you've been alive trying to find a way to unmake what his kin became. He knows where the First Hearth sleeps. He knows what the covenant actually says, in the old tongue, before your guild got hold of it and started translating 'welcome' into 'sacrifice.'" She let that sit. Emrys felt the folded page against their chest like a second heartbeat. "So. Problem?"

Emrys thought of the archive fragment. *Hearth and hand.* Thought of the council's vote, the raised hands, the terrible tidiness of it. Thought of Master Corvane, who had died mid-lesson, mid-sentence, leaving Emrys half-taught in a world that needed whole answers.

"No problem," they said. "Just fear. Fear can walk."

"Fear always walks," said Sable. "Bravery stays home."

* * *

Idris came with the deepest hour, when even the wind had given up.

He did not approach along the ground. He simply was there—one moment the space beyond the arch was empty silver distance, and the next he stood within it, tall and still as a fencepost, as though the steppe had grown him overnight. He wore traveling clothes gone gray with dust, and his face—

His face was the hard part. It was a founder's face, high-boned and ancient, the face carved into the guild hall's lintel above words none of the apprentices could read. But where the revenants' eyes were starless pits, his were merely tired. Deeply, terribly tired, the tiredness of someone who has been awake for a very long vigil—but they were eyes. They saw. They reflected the gray light back.

And they were sad in a way that made Emrys's chest ache.

"Emrys Lockridge," he said. His voice was low and rough, like a bell that had been silent so long it had to remember how to ring. "Last apprentice of the Wardwrights' Guild. I watched you tend the Salt Gate stone every seventh day for six years. You talked to it. Did you know that?"

"I—yes. It helps the tuning. Master Corvane said—"

"He said it was nonsense and you kept doing it anyway." Something moved at the corner of his mouth, the ancestor of a smile. "That's why I'm here. The others crossed your border full of hunger and old arithmetic. I crossed it full of watching. Six years of watching a child talk to stones like they were friends." He inclined his head, formal as a courtier. "I am Idris Vael. Founder. Traitor to my own kin. Keeper of what remains."

Wren had gone very still on her lintel. Now she rose, slowly, stick and all, and looked up at him with an expression Emrys couldn't read—grief and recognition tangled together, like a knot pulled tighter.

"I knew your name," she said. "From the remembrance rolls. Before the guild cut them down to nine. There used to be more names. Founders' names. They read them the first year I was apprenticed and never again." She tapped her stick against the ground, once, decisive. "Idris Vael. Say it again. I want to hear it in the air."

"Idris Vael," he said, gentler now.

"There." Wren sat back down as if satisfied with a day's work. "A name spoken is a candle lit. That's guild rules too. I invented them just now, but they're real."

Emrys stood. The four of them occupied the space beneath the broken arch—the last apprentice, the mad mender, the smuggler, the revenant—and the steppe stretched beyond, vast and mirror-flat and utterly indifferent to whether any of them survived crossing it. Somewhere out there, the First Hearth slept or didn't. Somewhere behind them, the hollow counted down toward solstice and a vote already cast. Above everything, the sky stayed empty, starless, waiting.

"We don't have a plan," Emrys said aloud, because it seemed important to say it honestly, at the start, while honesty was still cheap. "We have a page, a rumor, a debt map, and nine candle stubs. The council thinks I'm running from the payment. I'm not. I'm running toward a different answer, and I don't know what it is yet."

"Good," said Idris.

"*Good?*"

"If you knew the answer, you'd be certain, and certainty is what hollowed my kin." He looked at each of them in turn—Wren with her candles, Sable with her counting eyes, Emrys with their stolen page. "We were certain, once. Certain of the covenant, certain of the walls, certain of what we were owed. Certainty is how a people becomes a hunger." He stepped back through the arch, out onto the glass, and gestured east with one long hand. "Come. Be uncertain together. It's sturdier than it sounds."

They crossed the border one at a time. Wren first, stick clicking on glass, muttering names like a rosary. Then Sable, quick and low, already reading the flats for cracks and false shine. Then Emrys, who paused beneath the broken stone and put one palm against its cold flank.

"Keep singing," they whispered to it. "Flat or not. Keep singing till I come back with the tune."

Then they stepped through, and the hollow was behind them, and the steppe took their weight like a held breath.

Behind them, faint and fading, the cracked wardstone's whine changed pitch—just slightly, just for a moment—into something that was almost, almost, a chord.

Ahead, under the blind sky, four outcasts began to walk.
