## 8. Crossing the Glass

The first thing the steppe did was take Sable's shadow.

She noticed it an hour out from the broken gate, when the sun—thin, colorless, like a coin rubbed nearly blank—climbed high enough that shadows should have pooled under their feet. Hers was gone. She looked down at the pale glass beneath her boots and saw only the faint ghost of her outline, smudged, as if someone had tried to erase her and given up halfway.

"Your shadow's still there," she said to Emrys, who walked beside her with the cracked wardstone wrapped in cloth against their chest, humming its flat note. "So's yours," she added to Wren, shuffling behind with her bundle of mending and her mouth full of mutter. "And yours."

Idris said nothing. He hadn't said much since the gate. His shadow lay under him, black and whole, and Sable thought there was something obscene about it—a revenant casting a better shadow than she did.

"The glass remembers weight," Idris said at last. His voice had the dry rustle of wind through dead reeds. "It just doesn't remember everyone equally."

"And what's that supposed to mean?"

"It means the steppe knows what you left here." He didn't look at her when he said it. He kept his starless eyes on the horizon, where the flats ran silver to the edge of the world. "Everyone who crosses leaves something. The glass keeps it. Shows it back."

Sable snorted and lengthened her stride. "Then it's got nothing of mine. First time I've ever been on this cursed plain."

That was true, and it wasn't, and she knew the difference even if she pretended not to. She'd crossed the border a hundred times—but always in the dark, always by the smugglers' paths, always through the folds in the wardline that she'd spent six years learning to find and three more learning to sell. She had never once walked out the front way, in daylight, in company, with her name attached to her feet.

The glass flats stretched ahead, mirror-smooth, veined with faint lines of deeper blue like cracks in old porcelain. No grass. No stone. Just the steppe's endless polished skin, reflecting a sky with no stars in it even now, at midday, when there shouldn't have been stars to miss. But Sable found herself looking up anyway, checking, the way you probe a tooth that's already gone.

They walked until the light began to fail, which on the steppe came strangely—not a sunset but a dimming, as if the whole world were being covered with gauze. Wren called a halt at a place where the glass rose into low ridges, curled like the rims of enormous frozen waves.

"We camp in the troughs," Sable said, before anyone could ask. "Wind runs over the top of them. Down here it barely touches you." She was already unrolling her bedroll, already building the little ring of stones she'd built ten thousand times on ten thousand cold nights alone. It was only when she reached for a fourth stone for the fourth traveler that her hand stopped.

Four. She'd never provisioned for four. Her hands knew exactly how much food one person needed to walk a day, how much water, how much fuel. They knew nothing at all about sharing.

Emrys crouched across the fire from her, unwrapping the wardstone to check it. In the firelight their face looked young and exhausted and stubborn, all at once—the face of someone who had run toward a thing instead of away from it and hadn't yet figured out the difference.

"You've crossed before," Emrys said. Not accusing. Just setting the words down between them like a card turned face-up.

"I told you. Smuggling routes."

"How many times?"

Sable fed a twig to the fire. "Does it matter?"

"You knew the flats were mirror-glass before Idris said it. You knew about the troughs and the wind. You knew to check your shadow." Emrys's voice was gentle, which was worse. "You've been out here alone a long time, Sable."

"Alone is a skill," she said. "It's the only one I've mastered."

Wren, half-asleep against her pack, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough. "Mastered," the old woman murmured. "Like mastering drowning."

* * *

The reflections started on the second day.

At first they were small things. A flicker at the edge of the glass, gone when you turned your head. Then, as the flats deepened toward the heart of the steppe, they grew bolder. The glass stopped showing the sky and started showing people.

Not the people walking on it. Other people.

Sable saw Wren stop dead mid-stride, staring down at a reflection of a young woman in guild blues with steady hands and no tremor in them, kneeling at a loom of ward-threads, weaving light. The woman looked up out of the glass and smiled, and Wren made a sound like a hinge rusting open, and then she stamped her boot hard on the image and walked on without a word, jaw set, eyes wet.

Sable didn't ask. You didn't ask, out here. The glass showed who you *were*, Idris had said. Not who you are. And everyone walking this plain was someone's abandoned draft.

Her own turn came near noon.

She saw it from thirty paces: a figure standing on the glass ahead, and her body knew it before her mind did, the way a dog knows its own name in a stranger's mouth. Dark hair braided back tight. A coat with the collar turned up. A satchel slung across the chest, stuffed with shapes that bulged wrong, that clinked.

The reflection stood at the base of a wardline fold—the hidden seam in the border she'd found at nineteen, the crack in the world that had made her fortune and her exile both. In the reflection, the girl was prying a shard of wardstone loose with a chisel, and she was laughing.

Sable stopped walking.

The others moved past her. Emrys glanced back once but kept going; they'd all learned by now that the glass demanded you face your own exhibit alone. Only Idris hung back, standing a few paces off, saying nothing, being nothing, his stillness almost kind.

"That's not me," Sable said to the reflection.

The girl in the glass laughed again, and the sound came up out of the ground itself, thin and clear as a struck bell. Her hands were sure. Her eyes were bright with the particular greed of someone who has just discovered the world can be picked open like a lock. Behind her, in the reflected dark of the hollow, lights burned in windows. Whole streets of them. People who trusted the wards because the wards had always held.

"They held," Sable whispered. "Right up until I started selling pieces of them."

Because that was the truth the glass had dug up and laid out for her like a corpse on a slab. She hadn't stolen from strangers. She'd chipped fragments off the covenant itself—one shard here, two shards there, sold to collectors beyond the border who paid in coin and asked no questions—and every piece she'd carried out had been a thread pulled from a tapestry, and she'd told herself threads were small, threads wouldn't matter, and the night the stars went out she'd been sitting in a tavern on the far side of the world spending the money.

The girl in the glass finished prying the shard free and held it up to the blind sky, admiring it.

"Stop it," Sable said.

The girl turned and looked at her. That was the worst part. The reflections weren't supposed to look at you—they were memories, echoes, dead things—but this one turned its head with deliberate slowness and regarded her with her own nineteen-year-old eyes, and smiled, and mouthed something.

Sable read the shape of it. *You'll come back.*

"No," she said aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle. "I came back different."

But the glass didn't care about different. The glass held what it held. She stood there a long time, long enough for the others to become specks on the silver horizon, and the girl in the glass stood with her, patient as stone, holding her stolen shard up to a sky that had no stars to admire it in.

Finally Sable knelt. She put her palm flat on the cold glass, over the girl's laughing face.

"I'm sorry," she said. It came out rusty. She couldn't remember the last time she'd said it to anyone, including herself. "I didn't know what I was taking. That's not an excuse. It's just the truth, and it's all I've got."

The reflection didn't forgive her. Reflections don't. But as she stood and walked on, she found she was navigating differently—choosing her line across the flats not by the sun or the wind but by the places the glass showed her nothing at all, the clean stretches, the ones with no ghosts in them. She steered the party around other people's haunts too, without explaining why. Around the place where Emrys went white and silent for an hour. Around the ridge where Idris stood motionless for so long she thought he'd hollowed on his feet.

And when, near dusk, they came to a stretch where the glass showed a hearth fire burning in a round room, and Emrys froze, and the reflection of a younger Emrys sat feeding that fire with pages torn from a book—Sable was the one who took their arm.

"Don't watch," she said quietly. "It lies by telling the truth. Come on. Eyes forward. The glass only owns you if you keep looking."

Emrys let themselves be led. But at the edge of the hearth-reflection, Sable heard them whisper, half to her and half to the fire in the glass:

"It smiled at me. My own face. It smiled, and I wasn't smiling."

"I know," Sable said. "Mine laughed."

"What did yours show you?"

Sable was quiet for eleven steps. Twelve. "A debt," she said finally. "It showed me a debt I've been pretending isn't mine."

* * *

On the fourth day the flats changed.

The mirror-sheen roughened, clouded like breath on a window, and the reflections faded to smears. Sable felt the shift in her knees before she saw it—the vibration underfoot, a deep slow pulse, regular as a heartbeat.

Idris felt it too. She watched him stop, tilt his head, and something moved through his ruined face that she'd never seen there before. Hope, maybe. Or fear wearing hope's clothes.

"The Waystation," he said. "Small Mercies. It's close."

"You know it?" Emrys asked.

"I was born closer to it than to your hollow." His starless eyes fixed on the horizon. "My people traded with the waystation folk, before. Before the hollowing. Before everything." He started walking faster, and the pulse underfoot quickened to meet him, or seemed to.

Sable hung back a moment and checked the sky out of habit. Still starless. Still blind. But far off to the north, where the glass met a line of low black hills, she saw something that made her stomach drop: a smudge of movement, slow and vast, like dust rising off a road where no road should be.

Revenants. More of them than the four who had come to the guild door. Dozens. Converging—from the hollow, from the deep steppe, from everywhere the old founders had scattered their hollowed selves—all of them drifting, with terrible patience, toward the same point on the map.

Toward the First Hearth. Or toward whatever was walking toward it.

"Sable," Emrys called back. "Keep up!"

She spat, shouldered her pack, and ran to catch them, leaving her nineteen-year-old self standing alone on the glass behind her, laughing at a sky with no stars, waiting for a woman who was never coming back.

Ahead, the ground pulsed like a heart, and somewhere past the clouded flats, a light was burning that the steppe had forgotten how to put out.
