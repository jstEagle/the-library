## 9. The Waystation of Small Mercies

The wind changed an hour before we saw the lights.

I felt it first in my teeth—that old steppe habit, reading the air like a page. The glass flats had been singing their empty note for two days, a high thin whine that got under the skin and stayed there, but now the wind carried something else: woodsmoke, and beneath it, faint as a memory, tallow and boiled grain and wet wool.

"People," Sable said. She had stopped walking without seeming to decide to stop, one hand raised, her whole body turned into an ear.

"Or bait," Wren said cheerfully. She was walking with her eyes shut again, feeling for the starless wind the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "Out here, those are often the same thing."

"Not this," I said.

They both looked at me. Emrys looked at me longest. I had gotten used to that over the days since the broken gate—the way they watched me when I spoke of the steppe, cataloguing everything, filing it away against some need they hadn't met yet. It should have made me feel like a specimen. Instead it made me feel *useful*, which was worse, or better; I hadn't decided.

"You know it," Emrys said. Not a question.

"I know the smoke." My voice came out rougher than I meant it to. "Tallow rendered from steppe-goat, not hollow cattle. Bog-myrtle burned to keep the midges down. There's only one kind of place that smells like that."

"A waystation," Sable said. Her face did something complicated—hope and dread fighting for the same ground. "A living one."

"There were nine on the old caravan roads," I said. "When my people still traveled. When there was still travel." I looked north, toward the smudge of light the smoke promised, small and gold against the vast gray dark of the steppe. "This will be the third. Maybe the last."

"The last," Wren repeated, tasting it.

"Because the others are dead," I said. "Or because the people in them are."

No one answered that. We walked toward the light, because there was nowhere else to walk, and because—though none of us said it—we had all been walking toward lights like that our whole lives.

* * *

The waystation resolved out of the dark slowly, the way things do on the steppe, where distance lies. First the glow above the horizon. Then the low black line of walls, sunk into the earth so that from any distance it looked like nothing at all—a fold in the glass, a shadow pretending to be terrain. Then the smell, close and real: smoke and goat and rendered fat and, underneath it all, something that stopped me mid-stride with my heart slamming like a fist on a door.

Bread. Someone was baking bread.

"What is it?" Emrys asked quietly. They had learned to read me faster than I liked.

"It's nothing."

"It's not nothing. You've gone white."

"I'm always white. I'm half revenant, remember. White is the family color." But my joke came out flat, and Emrys didn't laugh, and I stood there in the cold breathing bread-smell like a man drowning in shallow water, because I knew that smell. I knew it from before. From the years when I was still whole, still named, still someone's grandson—I knew the long ovens sunk into the earth, the dough set to rise under cloth by the banked hearth, the way a waystation's first act toward any traveler was to feed them before asking a single question.

*Hearth and hand.* The words rose in me unbidden, the old covenant-words, the ones Emrys had found rotting in the guild archive while the council voted to spend a life. I had thought them hollow rhetoric then. Standing in the dark smelling bread baked by exiles' grandchildren, I understood for the first time that they had never been rhetoric at all. They had been instructions.

A challenge rang out from the wall—not a voice but a struck note, iron on iron, three beats and a pause. Sable answered it with four beats and no pause, and after a silence long enough to hold a whole argument, a gate that I had genuinely not been able to see opened in the earth itself, spilling warmth and light across the glass.

Figures stood in it. Five of them, maybe six, wrapped in layered wool and hide, faces brown and wind-scoured, eyes narrowed against the light behind them. They held spears the way farmers hold tools—competently, without love.

Sable stepped forward, hands open. "Travelers. Four. Bound for the old road south. We ask shelter, fire, and water, and offer work, news, and truth."

The litany was older than either of us; I could hear the archaisms rubbing in her mouth like stones in a shoe. The tallest of the figures—a woman with gray shot through her black braids and a burn-scar running from her jaw down into her collar—did not answer at once. She was looking past Sable.

She was looking at me.

At my eyes, specifically. At the places where the stars used to be and weren't anymore, the pale hungry emptiness that the hollowing leaves behind, that I carry in me like a debt of my own. I watched her see it. I watched her decide what it meant.

"Hollowed," she said. Her voice was neither cruel nor kind. It was the voice of someone stating weather.

"Refused," I said. "Mostly. I'm Idris. I was born Vael Idris-an-Sarai, of the caravan folk, before your grandparents' grandparents were ever driven out of the valley. If the blood counts for anything here."

Another silence. Behind the woman, the younger figures shifted, spears turning by degrees, and I understood with terrible clarity exactly what I looked like to them: a revenant walking up to their door in the company of strangers, wearing a dead man's patience, asking to be let inside.

It was Wren who broke it. She pushed forward, planted herself beside me with all five feet of her rumpled, half-mad self, and announced, "He snores. If he were going to eat us, he'd have done it on the flats where there's more room. Also he cries at songs, which is undignified in a monster."

"Wren," Emrys said, pained.

"Well, it's true."

The scarred woman looked at Wren. Then at Emrys—at the wardwright's guild-mark still inked on their wrist, faded but unmistakable—and something moved through her face like weather moving across the flats, too fast and too large to name. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into something quieter and far more dangerous.

"A wardwright." She said the word the way you'd say a diagnosis. "Walked out of the valley that walled us in, and walked up to our door, and asks for bread."

"Yes," Emrys said. Simply. No apology, no justification, no flinch. I had watched them learn that steadiness over the weeks since the council named them a payment, and I still marveled at it. "We ask. And we'll work for it. And if the answer is no, we'll walk on, and we won't hold it against you, because you owe us nothing. That's rather the point of everything we're doing."

The woman studied them for a long moment. Then she stepped aside from the gate.

"I am Mereth. This is the Waystation of Small Mercies, third of the nine, though we're the only ones left who say its name. Come in. Wash the steppe off. Then you'll tell me why a wardwright, a smuggler, a madwoman, and a hollowed man are crossing my flats toward the First Hearth in the dying of the year—and you'll tell me all of it, because I will know if you don't."

* * *

Inside, the waystation was a single great chamber dug into the earth and roofed with glass salvaged—or grown; I'd never learned which—from the flats themselves, so that the ceiling held the night sky like a bowl of dark water. Except there was no sky in it tonight. No stars anywhere, not over the steppe, not in the glass. Just black, and the fire below throwing its light up against that blackness like a plea.

Around the fire sat perhaps thirty people. Old and young, a handful of children chasing each other between the sleeping platforms, a baby crying somewhere and being hushed with a patience I had forgotten existed. Goats in a pen along the eastern wall, complaining. Racks of herbs. Long tables scarred by generations of knives. Everything worn, everything mended, everything *used*—and everywhere, on every surface, in every corner, small mercies: a chipped cup set out for whoever needed it. A basket of mended socks, free for the taking. A shelf of books with no lock on it.

I stood in the doorway with the heat rolling over me and could not make my feet move.

I had grown up on stories of these places. Caravan children all did. The waystations were the spine of the old roads, the chain of welcome that ran from the valley gates to the far salt courts and back—every one of them built on the same covenant-law, *any traveler fed before questioned, any stranger warmed before judged*. My grandmother had worked the third waystation in her wandering years. She used to sing its door-litany to me as a lullaby, and I had sung it to myself in the hollow's alleys when the guild apprentices threw stones, and I had believed, in the dumb faithful way of children, that the stations would always be there, burning at the edge of the dark, because that was what they were *for*.

And here was the third one, alive. Thirty souls keeping a promise my people had made and the hollow had helped break.

"You're crying," Wren observed, appearing at my elbow with two bowls of stew.

"I have ash in my eye."

"Both eyes?"

"The wind is very thorough."

She shoved a bowl into my hands and steered me to a bench without further comment, which was, I was learning, her particular genius: she gave you exactly the mercy you could accept and none of the mercy you couldn't.

Emrys sat down across from me. They ate for a while in silence, watching the room the way they watched everything—with that hunger they tried to disguise as scholarship. Then, quietly: "You knew this place. Before."

"My grandmother kept it, for some years. Before I was born." I turned my spoon in the stew. Goat, root vegetables, bog-myrtle, salt. It tasted like being eight years old. "She used to say the waystations were the covenant's true body. Not the wards, not the guild, not the sacrifices. This. A chain of doors that stay open. She said if the hollow ever forgot that, the whole thing would rot from the inside, and everyone would keep calling it holy right up until it fell on them."

Emrys was quiet a moment. "She sounds like she'd have had opinions about the council."

"She'd have burned the guildhall down." I looked up. "With the ledger in it."

That startled a laugh out of them—short, surprised, real. Around us the waystation went on being alive: a girl showing a younger boy how to mend a net, an old man telling a story with his hands because he'd lost most of his voice to the starless wind years ago, Mereth moving among her people with a pitcher, refilling cups, the host's ancient office performed like breathing.

Then Mereth sat down at our table, and the room's noise seemed to lean away from us, giving her space, and I understood that the softness was only one of her faces.

"Now," she said. "Tell me why."

So we told her. All of it, because she'd know if we didn't—the vanishing stars, the cracked wardstones, the revenants at the guild door, the council's vote, the archive fragment, the flight through the broken gate, the mirrors on the flats that showed Sable her smuggling and showed Emrys a smile they hadn't chosen. Mereth listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of terrifying, and when we reached the part about the solstice deadline and the stolen flame and the First Hearth lying cold, her hands went still around her cup.

"The First Hearth," she said. "You mean to relight it."

"We mean to try," Emrys said.

"With what rite? The guild's?" Her mouth twisted. "The guild wrote its rites for people behind walls. For a valley that had already decided the rest of the world was weather."

"The original covenant wasn't the guild's," Emrys said. "It was older. *Hearth and hand.*"

Something flickered across Mereth's face then—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. I caught it because I was watching for it. So, I think, did Emrys.

"You know the phrase," I said.

"Everyone of the steppe knows the phrase." She set her cup down with great care, the way you put down something you might otherwise throw. "It's carved over the door of every waystation ever built. It's the law we kept when your valley sealed itself and called us monsters. *Hearth and hand.* We kept it for six generations in the dark, feeding travelers, honoring strangers, holding the roads—while the hollow's wards drank the land dry on their side of the border and starved whatever leaned against them from ours." Her voice never rose. That was the worst of it. "Do you know what the wards did, wardwright? Do you know what it was like, living against that border? The land went gray for a mile in. The wells sweetened to poison. Children born on the wrong side of the line came out *wrong*—light-hungry, name-forgotten. Your founders called it the wilds' curse. It wasn't a curse. It was hunger. The wards didn't keep magic *in*, whatever your guild taught you. They kept the hearth-magic *from flowing*. And everything downstream of that wall starved."

The fire popped. Somewhere a child laughed, oblivious, and the sound went through me like a blade, because I knew what she was saying, and I knew what it cost her to say it in front of me.

I was the proof. I was the child born on the wrong side of the line—no. Worse. I was the child whose *people* had been on the wrong side of the line for six generations, drinking the runoff of the hollow's fear, thinning year by year, forgetting names, forgetting songs, until the hollowing took the last of us and wore our shapes into the valley to collect a debt no one remembered how to read.

"I know," I said. My voice came out low and steady, which surprised me. "I've seen it. I *am* it. The revenants at your border, Mereth—they're not invaders. They're the end of a starvation that started the day the first wardstone was set. They crossed that line because there was nothing left of them on this side to be." I made myself hold her eyes. "I refused the hollowing. Barely. I remember my name, most days. And I am telling you that everything your people lost, my people's fear took. Not mine—I was born after, I was a child, I threw stones at nothing—but my people's. The hollow's. I won't insult you by claiming otherwise, and I won't ask you to forgive it. I'm asking you to believe we're trying to unmake it."

Mereth looked at me for a long time. The firelight moved in her eyes and found nothing to warm there yet.

"And if you fail," she said. "If the solstice comes and the Hearth stays cold. What then?"

"Then the hollow is unmade," Emrys said. "Every soul in it."

"And the steppe?"

Emrys hesitated. I watched them refuse the easy lie in real time—it moved across their face like a shadow refusing to settle. "The steppe survives. The hollow dies. That's the shape of the debt as the revenants count it."

"And you'd carry that," Mereth said. "Walk back into the valley that voted to spend you, and die with it, rather than let it fall on us alone."

"I'd rather *fix* it," Emrys said. "But yes. If fixing fails."

Mereth sat back. For a moment she looked very tired, in the way of people who have been carrying a weight so long they've forgotten it isn't part of their body. Her gaze went around her waystation—the children, the goats, the open cup, the unlocked books, thirty lives balanced on a chain of promises in the middle of a starving dark—and when she looked back at us, she had decided something, and it frightened her, and she was doing it anyway.

"You can't relight the First Hearth with four people and good intentions," she said. "The Hearth was never a place. It was a *practice*. A thousand small fires, kept by a thousand open doors, all leaning toward one flame. If it's truly gone out, you won't relight it alone—you'll relight it the way it was first lit. With everyone. Every hand. Every hearth that will answer." She stood, and the room quieted around her the way water stills around a dropped stone. "Which means you'll need the steppe. All of it. The stations that are dust, the clans that scattered, the salt courts that forgot us. You'll need every door on these flats to open at once."

"That's impossible," Sable said.

"No," Mereth said. "It's a network. And you—" her eyes found Sable's, and something passed between them, smuggler to keeper of ways, recognition without warmth, "—you smugglers have spent thirty years mapping every hidden road, every buried station, every clan that still keeps an ember under its ash. Haven't you."

Sable's jaw tightened. I saw her weigh it—her guilt, her maps, her whole secret architecture of shame—against thirty lives and a dying valley and a debt six generations deep.

Before she could answer, the watch-iron at the gate rang out. Three beats. A pause.

Then three beats again—and a fourth, ragged, wrong, dragged out of the dark like a sound from a dream.

Everyone in the waystation went still. Mereth's face drained of everything but attention.

"That's not our signal," she breathed. "Nobody living knows the fourth beat."

I was on my feet before I knew I'd moved, cold flooding through me from the soles up, because I knew it too. I knew it from the other side of the border, from the years when I still answered to Vael, from the nights the elders drummed it on the wagon-tongues to call the scattered home.

The fourth beat was the founders' summons. And something out on the flats—something that knew the old roads, the old signals, the old names—was calling us out into the dark.
