## 10. What the Revenants Remember

Idris had not spoken his name aloud in ninety years, and the saying of it—*Idris*, *Idris*, like a bell struck in an empty room—had left a ringing in him that had not stopped since the broken gate. He carried it now the way he once carried firewood: carefully, close against the chest, mindful of the wind.

They were camped in the lee of a shattered waymarker, one of the old covenant stones that had once lined the pilgrim road from the hollow to the First Hearth. Half of it stood; half of it lay in the glass like a fallen tooth, its carved words worn to shallow ghosts. Sable was on watch, though there was nothing to watch for but the horizon, which here did nothing but shine. Wren slept by the banked fire with her boots still on, muttering fragments of guild litanies into her collar, as if even dreaming she were afraid of forgetting them.

Emrys sat across the embers from Idris, knees drawn up, watching him with that particular patience the young sometimes had—the patience of someone who has decided to wait out a storm rather than argue with it.

"You knew them," Emrys said. It wasn't a question. "The ones at the gate. The hollowed founders."

"I knew all of them." Idris turned his hands over in the firelight. They were good hands still, a mason's hands, though the knuckles had gone gray as unglazed clay where the starlessness had touched him first. "Before they were founders. Before they were revenants. When they were only nine people who were very tired and very frightened and very certain that walls would save them."

"Tell me."

And because his name was ringing in him, and because a named thing could no longer be silent, Idris told it.

* * *

"The hollow was not always a hollow," he said. "It was a valley like any other valley, open on every side. The steppe wasn't glass then—it was grass, high and silver, and it moved in the wind like water. You've never seen grass move. I'm sorry for that. It was the most beautiful thing in the world.

"We came out of the north in the year the long winter broke. Nine families, or nine hearths—I say nine because the guild remembers nine names, but really it was more, it was hundreds, and the nine are just who signed their names to the founding. People forget that. The guild reads nine candles every autumn and thinks the story began and ended with nine people standing in a field. It didn't. It began with hunger, and it ended with hunger too, just a different kind.

"There was a sickness in those years. Not a sickness of flesh—a sickness of the sky. The stars went strange. They flickered. They dimmed, some nights, to almost nothing, and everything that drew its strength from them grew weak. And hearth-magic—you'd call it that, your guild calls it that—is star-magic. It is the warmth that comes down from far away and pools in a kept flame. Every hearth in the world is a little mirror of the sky. That's what we believed, anyway. We built our lives around it.

"But when the stars sickened, our flames guttered. Children born in those years came in pale and quiet, like letters never opened. Our elders died early. The land thinned. And so the nine went looking for a place where the last of the starlight pooled deep enough to drink, and they found this valley, and in the valley they found the First Hearth—an old thing, older than us, a flame that someone had tended before memory, burning in a ring of stones at the valley's heart.

"It fed us. Do you understand? Not bread. Not warmth, exactly. It fed the part of us that the sickened sky had starved. We drank from it the way you'd drink from a spring, and we grew strong again, and our children stopped coming in pale and quiet, and we thought: *we have been saved.*

"And then the nine looked around and saw how many people wanted what we had."

Idris paused. The fire ticked. Somewhere out on the flats, the glass sang its low night-note, the one that sounded like a finger run along a wet bowl's rim.

"Fear," he said, "is very creative. Fear can build anything. So the nine built wards. Not to keep something out—at first, truly, at first, it was only meant to be a hedge, a boundary, a way of saying *this much and no further*. A garden wall. But a garden wall around a well becomes a dam, and a dam around a miracle becomes a theft, and none of them saw it happening because each stone was laid for a reasonable reason by a reasonable person on a reasonable day.

"The wards were made of covenant-stone, and the covenant was written into them: *hearth and hand.* That's the oldest version. Hearth and hand—meaning, the flame is shared and the labor is shared, meaning, anyone who comes hungry may sit down and anyone who sits down will be put to work and fed. That's all it ever was. Hospitality with a spine. The First Hearth was the heart of it, and the border stones were its ribs, and as long as the flame burned openly and the gates stood open, the whole thing breathed.

"But the nine were afraid. And fear writes small. Within a generation the covenant had been rewritten—not crossed out, nothing so honest, just... annotated. Amended. Each council added a clause, tightened a hinge. *Hearth and hand* became *hearth for the faithful.* Then *hearth for the born.* Then, eventually, *hearth for those already inside,* and the gates closed, and the wards went up full height, and the valley sealed itself like a fist around the flame.

"I was a young man when the gates shut. I helped lay the last course of wardstone on the eastern rise. I remember thinking it was the finest work I'd ever do." His mouth bent, not quite a smile. "I was right. Nothing I built after mattered half as much, or cost half as many people their souls.

"Because here is what no one inside understood, Emrys, and what your guild's books will not tell you because your guild's books were written by the people who benefited from not understanding it:

"The hearth-magic did not respect the wards. It couldn't be walled. It was starlight; it moved like starlight, through everything, toward everyone. But the wards *filtered* it. Thinned it. The flame inside the hollow still burned, but the people drinking from it were drinking through felt—getting the shape of the nourishment and almost none of it. And outside, on the steppe, those of us who had been turned away, and worse—those who had been cast *out*, the dissenters, the mixed-blooded, the ones who married travelers, the ones who asked questions—we were cut off entirely.

"No. Worse than cut off. The wards didn't just exclude. They *drew*. A ward needs feeding, and the guild fed theirs with the only coin the covenant recognized: belonging. Every soul sealed safely inside, every traveler turned away, every exile walking the steppe—the wards drank the connection between them and the flame. The covenant-stone remembered every name that had ever sat at the hearth, and it pulled those threads taut and used them as guy-wires for the walls.

"So the exiles starved. Not of food. We had food. We starved of the thing the flame gave, the thing the sky had stopped giving, and after enough years of it, the starving does something to you. It doesn't kill you. Killing requires taking everything. It takes everything *except* you—the memories go soft, the name goes soft, the wanting goes soft, until what's left is a shape that walks and hungers and cannot say why. We called it hollowing. The steppe-dwellers called it becoming weather. The founders' own journals called it *the quiet*, and the journals stop being written in a hand you can read about halfway through, because the hand forgets how letters work."

Emrys's face had gone white in the ember-light. "The founders. The nine—"

"Were the worst off," Idris said. "Because they had built the thing. Their names were woven deepest into the covenant-stone—they were the anchors, the first threads, the strongest guy-wires. When the wards finished sealing, the walls drank *them* first. They felt themselves going for years. Vael herself wrote about it—she was the last, she held out nearly forty years, keeping her journal going, teaching the guild, pretending. Her last legible entry says: *We meant to build a shelter and have built a mouth, and it is eating its own mother, and I am too hollow to tear it down.*"

He let that sit. Out in the dark, Sable had gone very still on her watch-rock, and Idris knew she was listening, and he was glad, because this was not a story for one listener.

"They didn't come back as conquerors," he said. "Whatever they said at your gate—and yes, I know what they said, I heard it, the debt and the blood and the solstice—that is not what they are. That is the hunger talking. Hollowed things can still want, Emrys. Wanting is the last thing to go. They want the flame back the way a drowned man wants air, and they have been walking the steppe for centuries, circling the valley they built, unable to come closer than the wards allowed, unable to die because hollowing doesn't grant you even that courtesy. And now the wards are cracked, and the stars are dark, and the hunger has finally got words again, and the words it found were *debt* and *blood* because hunger is not eloquent.

"But the debt is real. I won't lie to you about that. The covenant was made, and the covenant was broken—broken by the sealing, broken by the rewriting, broken by every gate shut in a traveler's face for three hundred years. The founders paid for that break with their selves. The exiles paid with their names. Someone owes, and the ledger is true.

"It's the payment that was misread. Your council read the old fragment and saw sacrifice, because councils see sacrifice everywhere; it is the cheapest currency they can spend, since it is always someone else's. But *hearth and hand* was never a price. It was an invitation. The debt isn't payable in blood because blood was never the coin. The coin was always welcome. The flame goes out when no one shares it. It comes back the same way."

Wren stirred in her sleep and said, clearly, though her eyes stayed closed: "*Vael. Vael Ashfoot, who laid the western stone.*"

Idris went cold from crown to heel.

She rolled over, muttered something else—half a litany, half a lullaby—and slept on.

"She knows the names," Idris whispered. He hadn't meant to speak above the fire. "Emrys, how does she know the founders' true names?"

* * *

Later, when the moonless dark was at its deepest, Idris walked the camp's edge alone, as he always did, because sleep and he had reached an arrangement that involved very little sleeping. The glass reflected nothing now—no stars above to give it stars below—and walking on it felt like walking on a frozen lake over abyssal water, except the abyss was himself.

He heard footsteps behind him and knew them before he turned. Emrys walked quietly for a wardwright; it was one of the hundred small contradictions about them that Idris was still collecting.

"You stopped the story," Emrys said.

"I told the part that matters."

"You stopped before the part where you fit in it." Emrys came to stand beside him, shoulder almost touching. "You said the founders were the anchors. You said the wards drank them first. But you're not hollowed—not fully. You refused it somehow, and you still remember your name, and you knew Vael Ashfoot's journal by heart. You weren't just an exile, Idris. You were one of *them*. One of the nine."

The glass sang under their feet. Far off—or perhaps not far off; distance lied on the steppe—something answered the note, a voice like wind through a ribcage.

"I was the tenth," Idris said. "The one who argued. There were nine signatures on the covenant and a tenth who refused to sign, and history keeps better records than people think, but it keeps them in places no one looks. I argued against the sealing until the day the last stone was laid, and then I walked out through the gate while it was still closing, because I would not live inside a mouth. And because my name was known to the covenant—because I had *sat at the hearth*, had earned the sitting—the wards tried to drink me too, on my way out. Tried to take my thread along as building material.

"I fought it. That's all refusal is, in the end. Fighting. I fought it for three days at the border, alone, and I won badly—I kept my name and lost everything easy. Every year since, it comes back for another installment. Some years I win. This—" he lifted his gray-knuckled hand, turned it in the dark, "—this is ninety years of winning badly. And some years, lately, I feel the wins getting smaller. The hollowing doesn't stop, Emrys. It waits. It is the most patient creditor in the world, and I have been paying interest my whole life.

"I came back now because I felt the wards crack. I felt it like a bone breaking in my own chest. And I thought: either I die out here with the rest of them, hollow, nameless, or I walk into the valley one last time and try to fix what I helped build. I chose wrong at thirty years old. I am trying to choose right at a hundred and twenty."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was careful, the way voices get when they're carrying something heavy across ice.

"The revenants at the gate. If the hunger is what speaks for them—if underneath it they're still *them*—then they can be reached. They can be answered, not just fought."

"Maybe." Idris watched the dark horizon, where the faintest gray suggestion of dawn was assembling itself out of nothing, patient as everything on the steppe. "But understand what you'd be reaching toward. I have kept my name for ninety years by fighting every single day, and I am losing, slowly, and I am the *luckiest* one. The founders have been hollow for centuries. What's left of Vael Ashfoot is hunger wearing her memory like a coat. To reach them, you'd have to give them back enough of themselves to hear you—and I don't know how you do that. I don't know if anyone knows. Maybe the flame. Maybe the flame remembers its people the way they remember it. Maybe if it burned again, truly burned, open and unwalled—"

He stopped.

Because the horizon had changed.

Not the dawn—dawn came up gray and gradual, but this was a line of deeper darkness moving along the edge of the world, slow and deliberate, the way a tide moves. And within it, points. Figures. Many figures, more than the nine, more than Idris had ever seen gathered in one place on the steppe in all his long life—hollowed ones, dozens of them, generations of the exiled and the forgotten, all walking with the same terrible patience toward the same point.

Not toward the camp.

Toward the road ahead. Toward the way the party must travel, between them and the First Hearth.

"Emrys," Idris said quietly. "Wake the others. Whatever we do next, we do it now—and we should pray your master taught you more than I think he did."

Behind them, Wren sat bolt upright in her bedroll, wide awake, and said in a voice that was not entirely her own:

"*They are asking for their names back.*"
