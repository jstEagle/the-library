## 11. The Hollowing of Wren

The wind came out of the north an hour past dusk, and it did not behave like wind.

Wren felt it first in her teeth—a high thin ringing, like a tuning fork struck against bone. She had been sitting by the waystation's low fire, mending the strap of her medicine satchel with waxed thread and the particular patience of a woman who had spent sixty years putting broken things back together. The steppe-dwellers had gone quiet inside their felt-walled shelters. Even the fire seemed to lean away from the dark.

"Wind's turning," she said to no one in particular.

Emrys looked up from the covenant fragment they'd copied onto their forearm in ink that wouldn't wash—*hearth and hand*, over and over, like a rosary written on skin. "Should we bank the fire?"

"It's not the fire I'm worried about."

She knew hollowed things. She had been the guild's mender for forty years before her mind began slipping its moorings, and in those years she had stitched wounds made by everything the valley could produce: wardstone splinters, revenant frostbite, one memorable incident involving a goat and a cracked boundary-stone. Hollowing had a texture. It felt like cold water filling a boot—slow, patient, indifferent to how much you kicked.

This wind had that texture. This wind was *made* of it.

"Get inside," Wren said, and her voice came out with the old authority, the guild-mender's voice, the one that made apprentices drop what they were holding. "All of you. Now."

Sable moved fast; smugglers always did when a professional told them to. Idris was already moving, his starless eyes wide—not afraid for himself, she noticed, but for them, which was somehow worse. Emrys hesitated, because Emrys always hesitated, always wanted to stand in a doorway and be the last one through, bless them.

"Wren—"

"*Inside.*"

They went. And Wren stayed a moment longer, because she was old and stubborn and because some part of her—the part that still answered to nine names read aloud every autumn—wanted to see the thing that was coming.

She saw it as a shimmer, the way heat shimmers off summer stone, except this was the opposite of heat. A ripple in the air where the darkness was somehow darker than the darkness around it, rolling down off the glass flats toward the waystation like a tide deciding whether to bother with the shore.

It caught her at the threshold.

Not violently. That was the horror of it. It touched her the way a grandmother touches a child's cheek—gently, familiarly, with terrible entitlement—and something in Wren simply... opened. Something she hadn't known was latched.

The cold went in.

* *

Inside, the fire was too small and everyone was talking at once. Sable wanted to move camp in the dark; Idris said the hollowed didn't hunt at night, they *waited*, which was worse; Emrys stood with their hand pressed flat against the wall as if they could feel the covenant through the stones.

Wren sat down heavily by the fire and tried to remember why her hands were shaking.

That was the first thing: not forgetting something, but forgetting that there was something to forget. A gap with smooth edges, like a river stone worn blank. She looked at her hands—her good hands, her famous hands, hands that had set bones in three generations of Vael Hollow—and thought: *whose are these?*

No. No, they were hers. They were hers. She flexed them. Calluses in the right places. A scar across the left thumb from a wardstone shard, the year the east border sang wrong.

*Tobbin,* she thought, with relief so sharp it was almost pain. *Tobbin Merriweather, apprentice, third-year, cut himself proving he could catch falling stone faster than the ward could shed it. I stitched him while he cried and told him crying was just the body being honest.*

Good. Good, she remembered Tobbin. Her mind held. Her mind—

"Old Wren?" Emrys crouched beside her. "You've gone grey. Are you hurt?"

"I'm fine." She heard her own voice and didn't quite recognize the tremor under it. "Just tired. Just—" She reached for the next name in the litany, the one she always reached for, the anchor she'd used for years as her memory eroded. *Nine names, nine candles, say them every night like counting sheep.* "Alder Voss," she said. "First speaker. Died the winter the south ward hummed flat."

Emrys blinked. "That's right. That's one."

"One of nine." She closed her eyes and went down the list. Alder Voss. Maren Quill. The twins, Oskar and Ilse Brandt, who died within a day of each other and were buried in one grave because neither would have tolerated being apart even in death. Fen Halloway. Petra Sorrel. Old Master Corvin himself, who had taken her on when she was a gutter-child with quick hands and no last name worth speaking.

Seven. Eight.

The ninth name wasn't there.

There was a shape where it should have been—a candle-shaped absence, a place her mind had kept warm for sixty years—and she reached into it and found nothing, and worse than nothing: she found that she couldn't remember ever having put anything there. As if the name had never existed. As if the guild had only ever been eight souls, eight candles, eight lines read into autumn air.

"Wren?" Emrys's voice, very careful now. "What's wrong?"

"The ninth name." She hated how her voice cracked, hated it, hated being old and breakable in front of this child she'd half-raised. "The guild's remembrance. Nine names. There's—I can't find the ninth."

Silence around the fire. Then Idris, softly, in his voice like ash settling: "The wind touched her."

"What wind? What do you mean, touched—"

"It doesn't take things all at once." Idris crouched across the fire from her, and up close his starless eyes weren't empty at all—they were full of something that looked terribly like recognition. "I told you. Hollowing is hunger without appetite. It eats outward from the edges. Names first, because names are how we're tied to things. Then faces. Then the reasons you had for anything." He paused. "Then yourself."

"That's not going to happen," Emrys said, with the flat certainty of someone daring the universe to disagree. "It's not. Wren, look at me. We'll fix this."

"You can't un-eat a memory, child," Wren said gently. "Believe me. I've been losing pieces of myself for ten years. You don't get them back. You just get good at building around the holes."

"This is different." Emrys took her hands—their young warm hands around her old cold ones—and their grip was fierce. "This isn't your age. This is the same thing that happened to the founders, to Idris's people, and Idris is still here. Still himself. So there's a way to hold on. Tell me there's a way to hold on."

Idris was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, he spoke slowly, choosing words like a man crossing ice.

"When I refused the hollowing—when I ran—the others called me traitor, and the hunger followed me, and for the first hundred years I forgot almost everything. My mother's face. My craft. My own name, for a while; I answered to whatever people called me." He looked at the fire. "What saved me wasn't strength. Strength runs out. What saved me was other people remembering *at* me. Talking to me. Saying my name until it stuck again, saying stories about me until I recognized myself in them." He lifted his gaze to Emrys. "Memory is a hearth-fire, little wright. It goes out alone. It lasts when it's shared."

Wren laughed—a wet, cracked sound. "Sixty years a mender and I never heard it put better. Write that down, Emrys. Put it in the new covenant, if there is one."

"There will be one," Emrys said. "And you'll be there to argue about the wording."

* *

They built the night against her forgetting the way you build a dike against a flood: handful by handful, hour by hour, knowing the water rises anyway.

Sable went first, because Sable understood debt better than anyone Wren had ever known. She sat cross-legged across the fire and told the story of the first time they'd met—or rather, the story Wren had told *her* about it, since Sable had been unconscious at the time, bleeding out in a ditch behind the Salt Row warehouses after a deal gone wrong. "You sewed me up with sail-thread," Sable said, "because it was all you had, and then you yelled at me for a full quarter-hour about how if I was going to get knifed I should at least have the decency to do it closer to your house. You charged me two coppers and a jar of pickled onions. Best trade I ever made."

"I remember the onions," Wren said, startled. "I do. Green ones. They were excellent." And the gap where the evening should have been filled itself in, edges knitting, and she gripped that memory like a rope in current.

Then Idris, who told her things she'd said to him on the road—she'd lectured him about eating properly, about how a body was a house and you couldn't let the roof go just because the tenants were depressed. He repeated her own proverbs back to her, one after another, and each one landed like a key turning in a lock she'd forgotten she owned.

And Emrys. Emrys most of all. Emrys sat with her hands in theirs the whole night through and recited her life to her like scripture.

"You were born on a barge on the Vael river, and you'll tell anyone who stands still long enough. Your mother was a net-mender; your father was nobody, or so you say, though you once let slip you have his elbows. You came to the guild at eleven with stolen scissors in your sleeve and Corvin caught you stealing *more* scissors and instead of thrashing you he handed you his own and said, 'If you're going to take up the trade, take it up properly.'"

"I'd forgotten Corvin's scissors," Wren whispered. "Steel. Never rusted. Where did—" And then it came back, whole: the weight of them, the worn horn handles, the way Corvin had watched her with that look, that *there you are* look, like he'd been waiting for her without knowing it. She started to cry and didn't fight it.

"Nine candles," Emrys said. "Say them with me. We'll say them until the ninth comes home."

"Alder Voss."

"Alder Voss. Maren Quill."

"Maren Quill."

They went around the circle of names, and around again, and somewhere past midnight the ninth name surfaced—Petra Sorrel's apprentice, the quiet boy who'd died of revenant-frost carrying a message no one ever read, whose name Wren had spoken every autumn for fifty years because somebody had to and she'd decided it was her.

"Dannel," she said, and sobbed it. "*Dannel Ostrey.* Oh—oh, Dannel. I nearly lost you. I nearly lost you twice."

"Not tonight," Emrys said.

* *

But the night was long, and the hunger was patient, and near the smallest hours it stopped taking the edges and went for the center.

Wren knew it was happening because the fire suddenly looked strange to her—wrong colors, wrong height, a fire belonging to some other story—and then the faces around it went strange too. Not unfamiliar, exactly. Un-*anchored*. She looked at Emrys and saw a kind young person holding her hands, and could not for three full heartbeats find the word for what Emrys was to her. Apprentice? No. Child? Closer, but not it. There was a word, a specific word, a word with weight and history and grief braided through it, and it was gone.

"What's my name?" she asked, and heard the terror in her own voice, plain as a bell.

"Wren," Emrys said instantly, steady, no hesitation, as if the answer had been waiting on their tongue since sundown. "Wren. Old Wren. Wren of the Guild of Wardwrights, mender, master in your own right though you never took the title because titles were 'hat-feathers,' your words."

"Wren," she repeated. It fit her mouth. It fit her mouth like a shoe fits a foot that has walked a thousand miles in it. "Wren. Yes. And you—you're—"

"Emrys Lockridge. Last apprentice of the guild. Your apprentice. Mine and Corvin's both, in a manner of speaking." Their voice caught, just slightly, and steadied. "You taught me to set a broken wing on a marsh-hawk when I was nine. You told me the bone would forgive me if I was honest with it. You tell everyone that. Bones, bread dough, people."

"Honest with the bone," Wren breathed. The phrase settled into her like warmth into cold hands. "Yes. Yes, I did."

The hunger pulled again—she felt it, a smooth cold tug behind her eyes, patient as tide—and this time it took the marsh-hawk, took the river, took the barge, and she grabbed for them and found Emrys already giving them back, already speaking, faster now, urgent: the barge, the nets, the stolen scissors, Corvin's *there you are* look, the nine names forward and backward, Dannel Ostrey, Dannel Ostrey, the pickled onions, the sail-thread, the two coppers, *hearth and hand*, *hearth and hand*, the covenant fragment copied on their arm, shown to her now, pressed close so she could trace the letters with her fingertip like a woman reading braille in the dark—

"Why does it matter," she heard herself ask, in a small voice from very far away, "why does any of it matter, if it can just be eaten—"

And Emrys leaned in close, so close she could see the fire reflected in their eyes, one small flame each, burning stubborn.

"Because I remember you," they said. "Even when you can't. Everything the wind takes from you, I keep. You're not losing yourself, Wren. You're leaving copies with the people who love you. That's what a guild *is*. That's what a family is. Nobody gets erased if somebody else is holding the page."

Wren looked at this child—this impossible, stubborn, tender child, who had spent the whole night giving her own memories back to her one coin at a time, spending themselves without counting—and something in her chest that had been coming loose for ten years, long before any starless wind, quietly stopped coming loose.

"Well," she managed. "Someone ought to write *that* in the covenant."

"They will." Emrys smiled, exhausted and ferocious. "Chapter one."

* *

Dawn came grey-gold over the glass flats, and the wind had passed, leaving the waystation standing and Wren diminished but present. She catalogued herself like inventory, the way she'd done every morning for a decade: names, mostly intact. Faces, intact. The ninth candle, home. Somewhere in her there were new gaps she couldn't see—holes are invisible from the inside—but she was still Wren, and she knew it the way you know a repaired joint holds: by leaning on it.

Idris stood at the eastern door, looking out at the flats, and his posture made her stomach drop before her mind caught up.

"What is it?"

He didn't turn. "The hollowing in you—it wasn't random. The wind wasn't hunting. It was *counting*." His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone reporting damage. "It touched you, learned the shape of this company, and moved on. They know where we are now. They know what we're carrying." He turned, and his starless eyes were bright with something awful and certain. "And Wren—they know I'm with you. The hunger in me has been quiet since we crossed the glass. Last night it woke up."

Wren followed his gaze to the horizon, where the dawn light lay along the mirror-flat like a seam of silver, and saw—far off, at the edge of seeing—shapes standing in the glare. Not moving. Waiting.

Counting.

Behind her, Emrys rolled down their sleeve over the written covenant and reached for their pack.
