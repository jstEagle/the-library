## 12. The First Hearth, Cold

They smelled it before they saw it.

Not smoke—Sable had been sniffing the wind for two days and swearing there was no smoke—but something older than smoke. Something like a hearth that had gone out in a house where no one lived anymore: cold ash, cold iron, cold stone. A smell Emrys had known their whole life without knowing its name, because every wardstone in Vael Hollow carried a grain of it at its heart.

"Emrys." Idris stopped on the ridge ahead, his hand rising as if to ward off a blow. His voice came out strange, flattened, the way it did when memory pressed too hard against him. "I remember this place."

"You've never been here," Wren said from behind, breathless, her bundle of charms rattling. "None of us have. That's rather the point of the whole wretched journey."

"I haven't." Idris's starless eyes—they were not so much black as *empty*, Emrys had learned; a window with nothing behind it, though lately, day by day, something flickered behind the glass again—"I haven't been here. But the part of me that was hollowed has. It remembers being made near this place. It remembers the warmth going out of the world." He swallowed. "It remembers the door being closed."

No one answered that. There was nothing to answer with.

The ridge they stood on was the last of the glass steppe's gentle swells before the land dropped away into a bowl miles wide, and in the bowl's center rose the First Hearth—or what the old guild maps called it, drawn in a master's careful hand four hundred years gone: a ring of standing stones around a low dome of black rock, and above the dome, in the map's faded ink, a single word repeated in nine languages of the valley and the steppe both.

*Here.* Here. Here. Here.

The maps had not shown them the color. The stones were white—not painted, not bleached, but white the way bone is white, the way frost is white, the way a page is white when everything written on it has burned away. And the dome beneath them, which should have glowed with the covenant's first fire, was the same white. The whole hollow of the bowl was white as an unwritten letter.

"It's snowing ash," Sable said quietly. She crouched and dragged two fingers through the drift at her feet. It was fine as flour and utterly cold, and when she lifted her fingers the wind took it away in threads. "Not snow. Ash. This whole valley burned once, or went out once. One of the two."

They went down into it together, because there was nowhere else left to go.

* * *

The path wound between fallen stones, each one taller than a cottage and carved with script Emrys half-recognized—the root-tongue of the wards, the language Master Corvane had drilled into them until their dreams ran in it. But the carvings were wrong. Not damaged. *Wrong.* Every character had been cut through with a second stroke, deep and deliberate, transforming each glyph into its negation. Emrys knew those negation-strokes intimately; apprentices learned them first, learned how to unmake a ward cleanly rather than let it shatter wild.

Someone had come here, long ago, and unmaded every word.

"This wasn't looting," Emrys said, tracing one with numb fingers. Their voice sounded small in all that whiteness. "This was *editing*. Whoever did this wanted the text unreadable, but the stone intact. You don't do that if you're afraid of the power. You do it if you want the power forgotten but the structure kept."

"You sound like the archive room," Wren said. "You get very precise when you're frightened, dear. Have you noticed? Your sentences go stiff as starched linen."

"I'm not frightened."

"You've been frightened since the stars went out. We all have. The trick is saying it aloud before it says itself in worse ways." Wren patted the nearest stone almost affectionately, the way she patted feverish patients. "Go on then. Be precise. It helps."

Emrys took a breath. "Nine days to solstice. The revenants said the debt comes due at solstice, and the council voted to pay it with my life, and we ran across the steppe to reach the covenant's origin because Idris said—if the flame still burns at the First Hearth, the covenant can be renegotiated at its source. That was the plan. That was the whole plan. Reach the flame, speak the old words, buy the hollow another four hundred years while we find a better answer." They looked up at the white dome ahead, at the ring of blank, negated stones around it. "And now we're here, and I can't feel any fire at all. Not banked. Not sleeping. Nothing. This isn't a hearth, Wren. It's a grave."

"There," Idris said suddenly.

He was pointing past the ring, to where the dome met the ground on its far side—and where, half-swallowed by ash-drift, a doorway stood open. Not broken. Open. As if someone had walked out and simply never thought to close it, or had wanted whoever came next to walk in.

They crossed the ring together. Emrys counted the stones as they passed—nine, of course, nine, everything in the covenant came in nines—and at the ninth, set into the stone at knee height, was a hollow shaped exactly like a wardstone. Exactly. To the finger-width. The same socket, the same keyway, the same faint spiral channel worn smooth by centuries of handling.

Master Corvane's voice rose in their memory, clear as the day he'd said it: *Every ward in the hollow is an echo, child. The border stones sing the First Stone's note, quieter, always quieter. We are not the music. We are the room the music plays in.*

Emrys had spent their whole life tuning rooms.

They knelt and put their palm flat in the socket, the way they'd done ten thousand times at the border, expecting—hoping—for even a whisper of resonance, the way a struck bell keeps humming in the metal long after the ear gives up.

Nothing.

Not silence, even. Silence would have been something. What they felt was the absence of ever having been sound at all—a socket shaped for a stone that no longer existed anywhere in the world.

"Emrys?" Sable's voice, carefully casual, the smuggler's tone for bad news delivered gently.

"It's gone," Emrys said. They heard their own voice go precisely stiff. "The First Stone. The original wardstone—the source-stone every other stone echoes. It's been removed from its socket. Carefully. Recently enough that the wear-marks haven't reformed." They stood, knees cracking in the cold, and turned to look back the way they'd come, east, toward the hollow, toward home, toward the border they'd fled across with the revenants' ultimatum burning behind them. "It didn't crumble. It didn't die. Someone *took* it."

Idris had gone very still. "When the founders hollowed themselves," he said slowly, "they told us—I was told, in the last hour before I forgot—that they were cutting themselves off from the hearth. From the source. But you can't cut a people off from a source that sits in their own capital, under their own guard. Unless..."

"Unless the source wasn't there anymore when they did it," Emrys finished. The precision helped, just as Wren said it would. It gave the terror somewhere to stand. "Unless the theft happened first. Unless the hollowing was a symptom, not a cause."

Wren sat down abruptly on the ninth stone, right on the empty socket's edge, as if her legs had filed their own resignation. "Four hundred years," she said. "We've been paying tithes and reading remembrances and teaching children to fear the steppe for four hundred years, over an empty socket." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, bright and breaking. "Oh, the Guild. Oh, you beautiful, stupid, reverent fools. We memorized rituals to feed a fire that was already stolen."

* * *

Inside the dome, the dark was total until Sable lit her storm-lantern, and then the light showed them the keepers' hall, and the light was almost worse than the dark.

It had been a great room once. Benches in concentric rings, facing inward, facing a central dais where the flame must have stood—there was a scorch-fan on the floor, ancient and beautiful, radiating outward like the petals of a stone flower, marking where heat had poured for centuries. Hooks along the walls for cloaks. Niches for lanterns. A long table scarred by generations of cups set down carelessly, meals eaten hurriedly, arguments conducted with hands.

Everything was dust. Everything was cold. And everywhere, on every surface, the same negation-strokes had been chiseled through the inscriptions, the blessings, the names.

But it was the table that stopped Emrys's heart.

Set out along it, arranged with terrible care, were the keepers' things. Cups, upside down. Cloaks, folded. Boots, paired side by side, toes pointing toward the door. Nine sets. Nine.

"They left," Idris whispered. He had drifted to the table and stood over the folded cloaks like a man at a bedside. "They weren't driven out. They weren't killed. They *left*. They put their houses in order and walked out of their own hearth and shut nothing behind them because—" His voice cracked. "Because there was nothing left to guard. My people found the hearth already dying. Or already dead. Or already *emptied*." He touched a cup, and dust slid from it like grey water. "And then we starved, because whatever fed the founders lived in that flame, and it was gone, and none of us knew why, and so we did the only thing left. We cut ourselves off cleanly, before the hunger drove us mad. We chose to be hollow rather than be hungry." He looked up at Emrys, and in the lantern-light his eyes were wet, which Emrys had not known starless eyes could be. "I have hated your walls my whole existence. I built some of the hatred myself, in the years I still had a name. But your walls weren't the crime, Wardwright. They were the bandage. Somebody stole the wound."

Emrys could not answer. Their throat had closed around something enormous.

Because it fit. It fit with the archive fragment, the original covenant they'd found and fled with: *hearth and hand, freely given, renewed at the turning of the year.* It fit with the revenants at the guild door, demanding a debt no living soul understood. It fit with Wren's half-mad mutterings about a second clause no one wrote down. Four hundred years of ritual, of remembrance, of nine candles lit every autumn for nine names—and underneath all of it, an empty socket and a folded cloak and a scorch-mark on a floor, the fossil record of a theft so old it had become theology.

The plan collapsed inside Emrys not all at once but the way a snow-laden roof goes—invisible cracks, then everything at once.

They had crossed the glass flats where reflections lied. They had buried Sable's shame at the waystation and Idris's grief among the exiles, and they had held Wren through the night when the starless wind began eating her memories, reciting the guild's names into her mouth until dawn like a man bailing a leaking boat. They had spent everything. And the treasure at the end of the road was a cold room and a hole where the miracle should have been.

"We have nine days," Emrys said at last. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "Solstice. The debt comes due. And the thing we crossed the world to find doesn't exist anymore."

"Then we find who took it," Sable said.

"With what? We don't know who. We don't know when. We don't know *where*—" Emrys gestured at the negated stones, the folded cloaks, the whole patient whiteness of the dead valley. "There are no tracks four hundred years old, Sable. There's no trail. There's just an absence wearing the shape of a plan, and I am the last apprentice of a guild that guarded an empty box, and in nine days the hollow gets unmade unless—" Their voice finally failed them, precisely, like everything else.

Wren got up off the stone. She moved slowly—she'd been moving slowly since the wind got into her, since pieces of herself started going missing like buttons off a coat—but she crossed to Emrys and took their face in her two mender's hands, thumbs on their cheekbones, the grip she used to hold a patient still for stitches.

"Now you listen to me, apprentice," she said. "I have mended broken bodies for fifty years, and I will tell you the one true thing I know. A wound is not an ending. A wound is information. The body does not lie down and die because it is hurt—it *reroutes*. Blood finds new roads. Bone knits crooked and holds anyway. So." She tapped Emrys's sternum, once, hard. "The flame is gone. Fine. Flames can be carried. Fires can be stolen, which means fires can be *re*-stolen. Which means somebody, somewhere, knows where it is—and nobody hides a fire forever, dear, because a hidden fire warms no one, and thieves are not keepers, and keepers leave doors open when they expect someone to come home." Her eyes, clouded and half-eaten as they were, glittered. "We are someone coming home. Whoever emptied this place left the door open *on purpose*. Think about what kind of person does that."

Emrys thought. The precision came back, stitch by stitch.

A thief who wanted the power forgotten but the structure kept. Negations cut clean, not smashed. A door left open. An empty socket maintained, not filled. As if the whole hearth had been preserved like a specimen—as if someone had wanted it possible, someday, for the fire to be *returned to its place*—but only on terms the thief controlled. Only if someone came asking. Only if someone proved worthy of the answer.

Or desperate enough to bargain.

"Oh," Emrys said softly. "It's a trap. It's a four-hundred-year-old trap with a warm chair in it. Someone wants us to find them."

"Does it matter," Sable said, "if it's also the only road?"

Behind them, out in the white valley, the wind changed.

Emrys felt it before they heard it—the fine hairs on their arms lifting, the wardwright's sense flaring with a wrongness like a chord played a half-step flat. They turned toward the doorway. Beyond it, beyond the nine blank stones, the ash-drifts had begun to move against the wind, streaming uphill in slow grey ribbons, converging, and far off across the bowl, on the rim where the sky should have shown stars and showed nothing, shapes were standing up out of the whiteness. Many shapes. Patient as winter.

Idris made a sound low in his chest, half recognition, half horror. "They've found the hearth," he breathed. "The revenants. They followed us—or they *felt* it, the emptiness, the way you feel a missing tooth. Emrys—they're coming to collect. And we're standing in the vault."

Wren's hand found Emrys's sleeve. Sable snuffed the lantern without being asked, plunging them into the keepers' dark, and in that darkness Emrys heard their own heart counting down the nine days like a wardstone ticking toward failure.

One thought rang through the cold, clear and terrible:

*Somewhere in the world, someone is keeping our fire—and we have eight days left to learn their price.*
