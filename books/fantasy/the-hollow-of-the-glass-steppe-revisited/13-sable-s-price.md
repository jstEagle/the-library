## 13. Sable's Price

The waystation's fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Sable finally spoke.

She'd been waiting for the others to sleep. Wren was already gone under her blankets, mumbling half-remembered names into the wool like a woman counting coins she no longer trusted. Idris sat propped against the doorframe with his eyes open—revenants, even un-hollowed ones, slept little, and when they did it looked more like dying slowly than resting. Emrys sat across from her, turning a shard of cracked wardstone over in their fingers, and Sable thought: *of course they're still awake. Of course it has to be now.*

"You keep looking at me," Emrys said, not looking up. "You've been looking at me since the flats."

"I look at everyone."

"Not like this."

Sable turned her knife over on her knee. The blade was old, older than her trade, pitted along one edge where someone had once tried to grind a guild mark out of it. She'd never told anyone whose knife it had been first.

"Your reflection," Emrys said. "On the steppe. It smiled at you before you smiled at it. I saw."

"I know what you saw." She said it too fast, too flat, and hated herself for both. She took a breath and made herself slow down. "Everyone saw something down there. You saw whatever you saw. Wren saw—" She stopped. Wren had seen nothing at all, just an empty glass where a face should have been, and none of them had been able to speak of it after.

"And you?" Emrys asked.

Sable looked into the fire instead of at them. It was easier to tell stories to fire. Fire didn't flinch, and it didn't remember longer than the burning lasted.

* * *

"I was fourteen when I first went through the border," she said. "Not through a gate. Through a gap."

Emrys's head came up sharply. "There are no gaps."

"There were then. There are always gaps, if you know where the wardwrights got lazy, or tired, or bought." She let that sit between them, because it needed to sit there. "My mother ran cargo. Not the romantic kind—not spices, not silk. People, mostly. Steppe-born who'd married into the hollow generations back and whose kin still lived outside, and who wanted letters carried, and medicine, and sometimes a last look at a face they'd never see again. The wards kept everything out and everyone in, and my family made its living in the difference."

"That's illegal."

"That's Tuesday," Sable said. "The hollow calls it smuggling. The steppe calls it the only road that ever existed. Same road. Different names, depending on which side of the wall you learned to say them."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, wind moved across the glass steppe with that sound it made—the sound of a bottle being rubbed at its rim, a long thin note that never quite resolved. Sable had grown up hearing that note from the wrong side of the wall and had spent thirty years telling herself she'd left it behind.

"My mother taught me the routes. Nine ways through, in her day. She said the wardwrights knew about four of them and pretended to know nothing about the other five, because pretending kept everyone comfortable. And she taught me the rule above all rules: *you carry things out, but you never carry stone.*"

Emrys's hand closed around the wardstone shard they'd been holding. They hadn't seemed to notice they were doing it.

"Wardstone is the hollow's blood," Sable said. "Cut a piece out of a living wardline and you don't just weaken the border there. The whole weave feels it. It pulls tight around the wound, like skin around a splinter—and every pull tightens the ward everywhere else. Every smuggled stone made the wall higher. Every piece of the border that left the hollow made the border mean more."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I didn't follow the rule." Her mouth was dry. She reached for the water skin, found it empty, and spoke anyway. "When I was nineteen, a buyer came up from the salt courts offering more money than my family had seen in three generations. Not for medicine. Not for people. For stone. A fist-sized cutting from the western line, near the Broken Gate, and he wanted it taken clean, with the matrix intact, so it could be read."

"Read," Emrys repeated. "Wardstone can't be read from outside. The script only resolves inside the field. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone in the hollow knows that," Sable said. "It's what the hollow teaches so no one wonders why anyone would want a piece. But the stone remembers what it held, Emrys. Even cut free. Especially cut free. There are people on the steppe who've spent two hundred years trying to understand what your walls were built *from*—what the covenant actually said before it got written into stone by men who liked walls better than words."

The word landed between them like a dropped lantern. *Covenant.*

"The buyer told me it was a historical curiosity," Sable went on. "He told me a lot of things. I was nineteen and my mother was dying of lung-rot from years of cold runs, and we needed a healer, and healers cost what they cost. So I went out at new moon, found the seam my mother had shown me when I was small—she'd have flayed me if she'd known what I used it for—and I cut."

She held up her hands. They were steady. She'd made them steady through practice; they had not been steady that night.

"Do you know what it sounds like?" she asked. "Cutting a wardstone? Everyone thinks it must sound like breaking glass. It doesn't. It sounds like a person. Like something breathing fast through cloth, trying not to cry out. And then when the piece comes away—" Her voice caught, and she let it catch, because hiding it would have been worse than the catching itself. "When it comes away, the whole line screams. Not loud. Just once. All along the border, all at once, quiet as a held breath. And then it pulls tight. Everywhere. Every wardstone in the hollow leaned toward that wound, and the wall got a little harder, and a little colder, and a little more itself."

Emrys had gone very still. "The western line," they said slowly. "Near the Broken Gate. That's the section that's always run hot. Every wright in my master's generation talked about it—the resonance there was wrong, over-tightened, like a scar that never softened. We thought it was age."

"It was me," Sable said. "I'm the scar."

* * *

She stood, because sitting still while saying this felt like a lie, and walked to the doorway where Idris sat. The revenant regarded her without expression, but his eyes—his eyes that still remembered his name—followed her face with something that wasn't quite pity. Pity would have been easier to bear.

"The mirrors showed you," Idris said. It wasn't a question.

"They showed me a girl with a chisel standing in a field at night, and a wall growing taller as she watched, stone sliding up out of the ground like teeth. Over and over. The reflection wouldn't stop smiling." Sable wrapped her arms around herself against a cold that wasn't in the air. "That's the trick of those flats, isn't it? They show you who you were. Not who you are. Who you were doesn't get to change."

"You changed," Emrys said from behind her. "You've spent fifteen years running people *through* the gaps instead of widening them. Your mother's routes—you've used them to move exiles home, haven't you? That family at the waystation, the ones who fed us—they came back through one of your roads."

"Aye. And every soul I've brought through, I've brought through wounds I made." She laughed, short and joyless. "Do you understand the arithmetic of it? One stone, one night, nineteen years old and stupid with love for a dying woman. And the wall grew, and the wall meant more, and the wall stayed up another fifteen years it might not otherwise have. Everything the wards did after that night—every exile refused at the gate, every letter burned at the border, every steppe-born child who grew up thinking the hollow was a story about a country that hated them—all of it flowed through the cut I made. The debt collectors on the steppe call it interest. I call it what it is."

Idris spoke quietly. "The founders' hunger began long before you were born, smuggler."

"I know what began it." She rounded on him, then mastered herself. "I know. The wards starved your people. The covenant curdled. None of that was me. But the night the stars went out, Idris, I felt the wards break, and do you know what I felt under the terror?" She pressed her fist against her own sternum. "*Relief.* Fifteen seconds of pure relief before I remembered I was supposed to be afraid. Because some part of me has wanted those walls down since I was nineteen and heard a border scream. And then I walked out here with all of you, and I told myself I was coming to save the hollow, and maybe I was, but the mirrors showed me the truth underneath: part of me came out here hoping the hollow would fall. Hoping the debt would be paid in full, in fire, in ruin, and I'd finally be free of what I owed."

The silence afterward was very long. Wind sang against the glass beyond the door. Somewhere in the dark, a steppe-fox cried twice and went quiet.

"So there it is," Sable said at last. "That's my price. Not coin. Confession. You want to know what I'm carrying into the solstice besides rations and knives—there it is. Guilt with fifteen years of interest. Now you know why I navigate by shame. It's the truest map I've ever owned."

* * *

Emrys rose and crossed the room. Sable expected anger—wanted it, in the ugly honest place inside her, because anger would have been simpler to carry than what she saw on their face instead. Emrys looked at her the way a wright looks at a damaged stone: not with condemnation. With assessment. With the terrible professional attention of someone deciding whether a thing can be mended or must be remade.

"When you cut the stone," Emrys said, "the matrix stayed intact. You said the buyer wanted it readable."

"Yes."

"Then the stone still exists. Somewhere on the steppe. Whoever holds it can read the covenant as it was written into the western line—including any amendments, including the original clauses, including—" Emrys stopped, and Sable watched the thought land on their face like a bird landing on a wire. "Including what the flame was *for*. The First Hearth is dead. Its keepers are gone. But the stone remembers what it guarded."

"The buyer's name was Corvane," Sable said. "A salt-court factor with soft hands and a ledger mind. He paid in hollow mint, which should have been my first warning—he had access to currency that only moves inside the walls, which meant he had friends inside the walls. He took the stone south. Toward the Court of Ash and Salt."

"You're certain?"

"I tracked it for six years afterward," Sable said. "Off and on. When I could afford to. When I could stand to." She met Emrys's eyes. "Every route my mother taught me, every gap, every safehouse, every smuggler from here to the salt flats who owes me or fears me—that network knows where Corvane's caravans went, because I asked, year after year, telling myself I was asking for closure. I wasn't. I was building a map to my own sin and calling it penance." She pulled her coat off the peg by the door and dug into the inner lining, where a flat oilcloth packet was sewn against her ribs, worn soft from years of carrying. She'd sewn it there the night the stars vanished, though until this moment she hadn't admitted to herself why.

"Now it's a map to the flame," she said, and put it in Emrys's hands.

Emrys unwrapped it carefully. Inside lay a sheet of good rag paper covered in Sable's cramped, slanted hand—a web of routes and marks, dates and names, the whole secret anatomy of the steppe's underworld rendered in ink. At the center, ringed twice, a single notation: *Corvane — Ash & Salt — carries his god in a box.*

"What does that mean?" Emrys asked. "'His god.'"

"It means a man who steals fire starts worshipping it," Sable said. "I've met his porters. They talk about the thing he keeps like it talks back. Like it's warm even through iron." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than the confession had. "Or it means nothing. Or it means a madman with a lantern. Either way, it's nine days to solstice, and the Court of Ash and Salt is six days hard travel south, and the man who holds the flame also holds the only complete reading of the covenant that exists outside your archive."

Emrys looked down at the map, then up at her. "Why give it to me now? Why tonight?"

"Because tomorrow we walk, and I don't confess well on the move." Sable allowed herself the ghost of a smile—the first real one since the flats. "And because the mirrors were right about who I was. So I've decided to make them wrong about who I am. That's how you beat a mirror, wright. You change what stands in front of it."

Behind them, from her nest of blankets, Wren's voice drifted up, thin and wandering, half-asleep: "...nine candles... no. Ten. There were ten. Who's forgotten..." and then she was quiet again, and none of them could bear to finish the thought.

Sable looked at Idris, at Emrys, at the map lying open between them like a wound laid bare for stitching.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When we find Corvane—if the stone's there, if the flame's there—I'm the one who faces him. Nineteen years I've owed that night a reckoning. I'm done sending it ahead of me like a bill."

Emrys held out their hand, and Sable took it, and the grip between them was the grip of two people agreeing to walk into the same fire from different directions.

Outside, far to the south, lightning flickered once against the horizon—except there were no clouds, and the light was amber, and it pulsed again, slow as a heartbeat, from the direction of the Court of Ash and Salt.

Idris rose to his feet, staring south, and his voice when it came was hollowed to a whisper.

"It knows we're coming," he said. "The flame is answering."
