## 14. The Court of Ash and Salt

Sable's map was not drawn on paper. It was drawn on skin.

She bared her forearm by the light of the stolen flame's echo—the ember we carried in its iron lantern, small and furious and warm as a held bird—and traced the inked lines with a cracked fingernail. Rivers that no longer ran. Roads that had forgotten their names. And there, in the crook of her elbow where the skin was softest, a spiral of ash-grey marks coiling inward like a snail's shell.

"The Court of Ash and Salt," she said. "Three days' walk, if the flats don't lie about distance. They usually do."

"You've been there," Idris said. It wasn't a question. He had not slept since the waystation; hollowed people, Emrys was learning, slept the way the dead did—seldom, and shallowly, and always with one ear turned toward a wind no one else could hear.

"I've been everywhere on this arm." Sable rolled her sleeve down with something that was almost shame and almost defiance, and Emrys had learned by now that those two things, in Sable, wore the same face. "Smugglers don't pick their customers. We pick our routes. The Court pays in salt, and salt buys everything on the steppe."

"And the flame?" Emrys asked.

Sable looked at the lantern. The ember inside it pulsed once, like a heart remembering its work. "Everything on the steppe comes from somewhere, wardwright. Even fire."

* * *

They walked the last day through country that had stopped pretending to be land. The glass gave way to a plain of grey cinder, fine as flour, that rose around their ankles with every step and refused to settle. The sky above it was the color of an old bruise. No stars. There had been no stars since the night the wards broke, and Emrys had stopped looking up except to check, again and again, that the absence was still there. It was. The absence was the most faithful thing left in the world.

The ash deepened. Then, without warning, it began to arrange itself.

Emrys saw it first as a trick of the light: drifts heaped into walls, walls into colonnades, colonnades into a sprawling court of grey pavilions roofed with slabs of white salt, translucent as ice, glowing faintly with the last of the drowned daylight. Ash fell perpetually from a sky that held no clouds, sifting down onto the roofs and sliding off in slow white sheets, so that the whole city seemed to be breathing. Figures moved among the colonnades—robed, salt-pale, their faces wrapped against the cinder wind. None of them looked up. None of them looked at anything, Emrys realized. They moved the way the ash moved: because something larger than themselves was falling.

"They don't speak," Sable murmured. "Or they do, but only to him. You'll see."

"Who?"

"The one who pays in salt." She stopped at the edge of the court, at a gate that was not a gate—two pillars of compacted ash with nothing between them but a curtain of falling cinder. She would not go through it. Emrys watched her decide that, watched the decision settle into her shoulders like an old weight finding its old groove. "I'll wait here. Idris—"

"I'm coming," Idris said. "He'll want to see what I've become. People like him always want to see."

Emrys looked at Wren. Wren had been quiet for three days—not the quiet of her old half-madness, which had always had music in it, but the quiet of someone counting something. She met Emrys's eyes and said only, "Listen to what he offers. All of it. Then listen to what he doesn't say. That's where the cage door is."

* * *

The court's interior was a single vast hall, or a thousand small ones; the ash walls shifted when Emrys wasn't looking, colonnade becoming corridor becoming chamber, so that every step forward seemed also a step deeper into the same room. The salt roofs filtered the bruise-light into something like candle-glow. It would have been beautiful if it hadn't been so deliberately, painstakingly still.

At the center, where all the shifting walls finally agreed to meet, sat an old man at a table of black glass.

He was not what Emrys expected, which was itself a kind of shock. Emrys had imagined the thief of the First Hearth as something enormous, something revenant, something with starless eyes and a voice like a landslide. Instead: a thin old man in a plain grey robe, white-haired, clean-shaven, with a scholar's stooped shoulders and a mender's scarred hands. His eyes were brown. Warm brown. The eyes of someone who had once been kind and had never entirely stopped, and that was somehow the worst thing about him.

He was drinking tea. There was a second cup on the table, untouched, steaming.

"Emrys Lockridge," he said. His voice was soft, unhurried, the voice of a man who had spent decades being the only one in the room worth listening to. "Last apprentice of the Wardwrights' Guild. Sit. The tea is from the hollow—your master's blend, or near enough. I've had a long time to perfect the imitation."

"You know my name."

"I know all the names the guild forgot to keep." He gestured to the chair across from him. Idris moved to stand at Emrys's shoulder, and the old man's gaze flicked up—brief, clinical, grieving. "Hello, Idris. You held on longer than most. Sit if you like. The chairs are honest, whatever else here isn't."

Emrys sat. The tea smelled like the guild hall in autumn: smoked bark, honey, the faint mineral bite of wardstone dust. Their hands shook around the cup, and they hated that he noticed.

"You have something of mine," the old man said.

"The flame was never yours."

"No." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and Emrys felt the floor of their certainty tilt. "It was never mine. That was rather the whole problem."

* * *

He told it the way a mender tells the story of a wound—precisely, without flinching, in the order the damage was done.

His name, he said, had been Aldous Vael. The hollow was named for his family, though the hollow had forgotten that, and he had made sure of it. He had been the first speaker of the first council, in the years after the Sundering, when the steppe was dying and the hollow's founders were starving on hearth-magic that no longer had a hearth. He had sat in the room—Emrys could see him seeing it, could see the room through him, the smoke and the shouting and the founders' hollowing faces—when the covenant was first drafted. *Hearth and hand.* He had written those words himself, or helped to, before the guilds rewrote them into blood and stone.

"And then I watched what the covenant became," Aldous said. "A wall with a liturgy. A cage with a calendar. Your guilds spent four hundred years teaching children that safety was the highest virtue and that anything beyond the border was not other people but *weather*. I sat in that council chamber for thirty years and I watched the hollow get smaller. Not its borders—those stayed. Its *ideas*. Its questions. Its courage. Every generation the wards grew thicker and the people grew thinner, until they were warding against their own reflections." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "So I did the only thing a warden can do when the cage is the patient. I took out its heart."

"You killed the First Hearth," Emrys said. "You started the hollowing. You—people are *dying*, Idris is dying—"

"I started nothing." For the first time his voice had an edge, and it was an old edge, worn smooth by centuries of use. "The founders hollowed themselves the moment your ancestors' fear cut them off from the hearth. I only stopped pretending there was a difference between the hollow and its walls. I took the flame so that it could not be put back into the machine. So that the covenant could not be reforged as it was—as a lock. And I have kept it here, in the one place the hollow's wards could never reach, for four hundred years, and in all that time the hollow has not grown one inch braver." He looked up, and his brown eyes were terribly gentle. "Until you. The apprentice who ran. Who crossed. Who carries my fire back toward the cage and calls it salvation."

* * *

Emrys set down the cup because their hands could not be trusted with it.

"You've brought me here to gloat."

"I've brought you here to bargain, which is what gloaters never have the courage to do." Aldous rose, and the ash walls leaned in around him like courtiers. "Walk with me. It's the last thing I'll ask."

He led them through the shifting colonnades, and Emrys saw, finally, what the Court of Ash and Salt *was*. Not a city. An archive. Every chamber held them: salt-glass cases in their thousands, and inside the cases, preserved in cinder and crystal, fragments. Shards of wardstone. Pages of covenant drafts in nine different hands. A founder's staff, snapped. A child's drawing of the hollow's border wall, with the sun drawn on the wrong side. Four hundred years of everything the hollow had ever thrown away, and Aldous had caught every piece.

"I am the only person alive who remembers the hollow whole," he said quietly. "Do you understand what that means? Everyone else who remembers is hollowed. Everyone else who isn't hollowed never knew. I am the last unbroken vessel of the truth, and the truth is this: *the hollow was always a mistake.* A beautiful one. A loving one. Our ancestors were afraid, and fear is the most forgivable sin, and they built a wall around their fear and called it a covenant. And every generation since has inherited the wall and forgotten the fear, so they think the wall *is* them." He stopped. The falling ash hissed softly on the salt roofs, the only sound in the world. "You could end it, Emrys Lockridge. Not the way the revenants want—not with blood, not with the debt. End it *gently*. Take my bargain."

"Which is?"

"Let the flame die here." He said it simply, the way one says *let the fever run*. "Let the First Hearth stay cold. Let the revenants finish their hollowing and go to ash—they are four hundred years dead already; you would be granting them rest, not death. Let the wards stand. The hollow goes on. Safe. Small. Warm. Your guild reopens, your wards hold, your people never learn what the covenant really was, and no one ever has to find out what they are without walls." He turned, and the bruise-light through the salt made his face look young for a moment, or old in a different way. "I know what you'll say. I've read your file, apprentice. I know what you crossed the steppe for. So let me say the part no one else will say to you, because I am old and I have earned the right to say one true thing:

"*You are afraid too.*

"You've been afraid since the night the stars went out. You're afraid of the hollow's council, who named you a payment. You're afraid of the revenants, who want your blood. You're afraid of the steppe, which has already taken pieces of your friends. And I am offering you the only thing anyone has offered you since this began: a world where the fear stops. Where the wards hold and the questions end and you can go home and be the last wardwright of a small, safe, sleeping valley, and tend your stones, and grow old, and never again have to decide anything larger than which wardstone hums off-key." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "I have lived four hundred years in that peace, Emrys. It is *real*. It is the only real peace there is. And it costs exactly what you'd expect: everything you might have been."

The ash fell. Somewhere far off, Idris's breathing had gone thin and rattling—the hollowing, advancing, keeping its own ledger.

Emrys thought of the hollow. Not the council, not the wards, not the guild's nine candles. The hollow: the smell of bread on remembrance-day. Wren's laugh through the guild hall floor. The children who ran the border road at dusk daring each other to touch the wardstones. The valley in the first snow, small and warm and lit from every window, and how Emrys had loved it, and how the love had always had a seam of grief in it, like a coin with a flaw, like a note in the wardstone they'd never heard before and never stopped hearing since.

Safe. Small. Forever.

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it. Not that the bargain was a trap, but that it wasn't. It was exactly what it claimed to be. A whole life, wrapped and warm, with the terrible questions buried under four hundred years of ash.

"No," Emrys said.

Aldous closed his eyes, and for a moment he looked like a man receiving a sentence he had been appealing for four centuries. "You didn't even take a day to consider it."

"I've been considering it my whole life. I just didn't know that's what I was doing." Emrys reached into their coat and drew out the folded scrap of the original covenant—the fragment from the guild archive, *hearth and hand*, in the founders' own hands—and held it out. "You wrote this. Or helped to. Which means you know it was never a cage. You've *always* known. You didn't steal the flame to free the hollow from its covenant. You stole it so you'd never have to learn whether the covenant could have been kept as it was written. Four hundred years, Aldous, and you've been warding yourself against your own reflection."

The ash stopped falling.

It did not drift to a halt; it *stopped*, every mote hanging in the bruise-light like a held breath, and the salt roofs dimmed, and the shifting walls went rigid, and Emrys understood that the Court of Ash and Salt was not a place at all. It was him. It had always been him—his long refusal given architecture, his fear made into weather—and he had just been told no for the first time in four hundred years, in his own heart's chambers, by a child with a scrap of paper.

Aldous opened his eyes. The warmth in them was gone—not replaced by cruelty, which Emrys could have fought, but by something far worse: *certainty*, vast and tired and immovable.

"Then you'll have to be put out," he said gently, "the way one puts out any fire that has spread beyond its hearth. I am sorry, apprentice. I did so want you to choose the cage. It's so much kinder than the door."

The hanging ash came down all at once like a grey snowfall, and every salt-glass case in the court opened at the same instant, and what stepped out of them was everything the hollow had ever buried.

Behind Emrys, at the edge of the court, Sable's voice rose over the hiss of falling cinder, raw and carrying:

"EMRYS—*RUN!*"
