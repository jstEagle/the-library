## 15. The Bargain Refused

Emrys ran.

Not because Sable shouted it—though that helped—but because some older instinct, guild-trained and ward-deep, had already read the room the way one reads a failing wardstone: by the sound it makes just before it breaks. The salt-glass cases had opened. The things inside them were unfolding, joint by joint, like instruments being tuned for a single long note of grief.

The First Speaker stood at the center of his court with ash sifting down through his white hair, and he did not run, and he did not shout. He only watched Emrys go with an expression of profound, patient disappointment—the look of a man who has waited three hundred years and can wait three hundred more.

"Apprentice," he called after them, mild as a tutor correcting sums. "You are carrying fire you do not understand into a valley that will not thank you."

Emrys did not answer. Answering was a door, and doors could be held open against you.

The court was a cathedral of salt and cinder, its pillars fused glass, its ceiling lost in hanging veils of grey. The flame rode in the lantern at Emrys's hip—a smuggler's lantern, iron-caged, warded inside and out—and even through the cage it *moved*, leaning toward Emrys's body heat like a cat toward a warm hearthstone. It had been cold for so long, Sable had said. It wanted to be near anything alive.

Behind them, the buried things finished unfolding.

They did not chase. That was the horror of it. They simply began to walk, in no hurry whatsoever, in step with one another, dozens of them, their footsteps ringing on the glass floor in a rhythm too regular to be natural. A procession. An escort. A sentence being carried out on schedule.

"They're herding us," Idris gasped, falling in beside Emrys. He had hold of Wren's arm; the old mender ran lopsided but game, muttering names under her breath—not the founders' names now, but her own list, the nine, the guild dead, holding onto them the way a drowning woman holds driftwood. "The court doesn't need to catch us. It needs to *point* us."

"Point us where?" Sable demanded. She ran ahead, checking corners with a smuggler's economy of motion, knives out, though Emrys suspected she knew as well as they did that knives would matter very little here.

"The revenants," Idris said. "The hollowed ones on the steppe. They'll feel the flame now. Every one of them. We aren't fleeing the court—we're being delivered to the hunger."

Emrys's stomach dropped, but their feet kept moving, because the alternative to moving was standing still in a room full of everything the hollow had ever buried, and that was not an alternative at all.

* * *

They found the way out where Sable said it would be: not a door but a flaw, a seam in the salt-glass wall where two centuries of heat had warped the pane until it sang when struck. Sable struck it with the pommel of her knife, once, twice, and on the third blow the whole pane shivered and came away in a sheet that shattered on the floor into a thousand small mirrors.

For half a heartbeat, every shard showed Emrys something different. A child at a wardstone, chalk on their fingers. Their master's hands over theirs, guiding a ritual they'd never finish learning. Themselves, older, standing at a border that wasn't there anymore, watching people cross freely both ways—

Then Sable hauled them through by the collar, and the shards lay behind, and the steppe took them back.

Night out here was not night as the hollow knew it. With the stars gone, the dark came down total and close, a darkness you could lean against. The party moved by Idris's sight—he saw warmth the way others saw color—and by the lantern, though Emrys kept its shutter drawn to a thread. Light, out here, was a shout.

They made perhaps two miles before the first of them arrived.

It came across the flats without disturbing the glass, which should have been impossible—nothing crossed the mirror flats without leaving a reflection, that was the whole nature of the place—but this thing left none. It was tall and wrong-jointed and its eyes were holes where stars should have been, and Emrys knew it. Not its name. Its *shape*. It matched the figures carved on the oldest lintel in the guild hall, the founder-statues the hollow had stopped looking at so long ago they'd become furniture.

It stopped thirty paces off. It looked at the lantern. And it *wept*—a dry, whistling sound, air through a hollow reed, grief with nothing left inside it to grieve with.

"Oh," Wren said softly. She had gone still, and her voice had changed, dropped its mad flutter, become the voice she must have had fifty years ago bending over broken bones. "Oh, you poor creature. You're not angry. You're *starving*."

"Wren." Idris's hand closed on her shoulder. "Don't."

But she stepped forward anyway, out beyond the reach of anyone's arm, into the space between the lantern-light and the dark, and she looked up at the hollowed founder the way she'd once looked at patients: seeing the wound, not the monster.

"You were the keepers," she said. "Weren't you? Before the covenant got rewritten. You kept the hearth, and the hearth kept you, and then your own children built walls between you and your supper and called it safety." She shook her head slowly. "And then we wrote down that *you* were the debt. That we owed you blood. As if hunger were a bill and not a wound."

The revenant tilted its head. The whistling changed pitch. Somewhere in it, Emrys thought—far down, behind the hollowing—something was trying to remember how words worked.

"We have what you want," Emrys called, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they let it crack, because hiding fear had never once made fear smaller. "The flame. But we're taking it home. To the hollow. To be relit where it belongs—and if it belongs anywhere, it belongs to *all* of you."

The revenant did not move. Behind it, out of the starless dark, came others. Five. Ten. More than Emrys could count without turning, and they did not dare turn.

Sable's knife-hand was shaking. "Emrys," she breathed. "Whatever speech you're about to give, give it faster."

Emrys reached for the words they'd carried out of the archive, the ones they'd thrown in the First Speaker's face an hour ago, the ones that had gotten them sentenced. The original fragment, ink faded to rust, in a hand older than the guild:

*A covenant of hearth and hand: that none who come hungry shall be turned from the fire, and none who tend the fire shall go unfed.*

Hospitality. Not sacrifice. Not a cage. A table, set wide enough for everyone—including, apparently, the dead.

"You don't owe us your patience," Emrys said to the revenants. "We owe you three hundred years of supper. And I can't pay that tonight. But I'm going to light the hearth again, and when I do, the door is going to be open, and there will be a place for you at it. Not as debts. As *guests*. As family, if you'll have us, because gods know we've been poorer company than you."

Silence. The wind moved across the flats with a sound like sand through an hourglass.

Then the nearest revenant—the first one, the one Wren had spoken to—did something none of them expected.

It knelt.

Not in worship. Emrys understood that immediately, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely. It knelt the way a starving traveler kneels at a stranger's door: not to grovel, but because the legs simply stop believing in standing when the smell of bread reaches them.

And it spoke. One word, ground out through whatever remained of its throat, worn smooth as a river stone:

"*Hearth.*"

The others knelt too, all down the line, a field of bowed shapes in the dark, and the sound they made together was not a threat and not a moan but something worse and better: it was *hope*, and hope out of that many ruined mouths was almost unbearable.

"It's not enough," Idris said quietly. He had gone grey around the mouth. Emrys realized he was feeling it too—the pull, the hollowing, the hunger answering hunger. "It's not enough to promise. They need the fire lit, or the promise is just another cruelty. Nine days." His voice frayed. "Eight, now. And every hour I stand out here, I lose a little more of—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My mother's face went last night. I checked this morning. It's gone."

Emrys took his hand. It was cold. It had been getting colder for days, and they had all been pretending not to notice, the way families do.

"Then we walk," Emrys said. "All night if we have to."

* * *

They walked.

The revenants walked with them—not herding now, not escorting, but *following*, at a distance, the way the poor follow a rumor of bread. By the grey false-dawn of the steppe, Emrys counted forty-one. By midmorning, sixty. Word—or whatever passed for word among the hollowed—was spreading across the flats, and they came out of the dark in twos and threes, kneeling, rising, falling in behind.

"It's a pilgrimage," Wren said, delighted and horrified in equal measure. She was fading; everyone could see it. The starless wind had gotten into her back on the steppe days ago, and since the court she'd forgotten the name of the third guildmaster twice, and once had called Emrys by her dead daughter's name and wept without knowing why. "Sixty years I patched the guild's broken bodies and nobody told me the dead could be mended too."

"Hold on, old woman," Sable muttered, steadying her elbow. "Hold on till the hearth. Then you can fall apart properly."

"I intend to fall apart *spectacularly*," Wren said, "with witnesses."

They crossed the salt shallows at noon, where the glass gave way to crusts of white crystal that cut like razors, and it was there, in the blinding glare, that the court caught up with them.

No warning. No procession this time. The First Speaker simply appeared atop a ridge of fused glass with six of his salt-cased dead arrayed around him, and he looked down at the ragged column of pilgrims and pilgrim-warders with an expression of genuine sorrow.

"You see what you've done," he said, and his voice carried impossibly across the flats, the way voices carry over ice. "You've gathered them. All the hunger in the world, walking toward my valley in a herd. I offered you the cage, apprentice. I offered you kindness. And you've chosen instead to lead a famine to our door."

"It isn't your valley," Emrys called back. Their legs were shaking. Their hip was bleeding where a salt-crystal had opened it during the crossing—they'd been hiding the limp for an hour, badly. "It was never yours. You stole its fire because you couldn't bear that safety might require *sharing*—"

"Safety requires walls," the First Speaker said, "and walls require that someone be outside them. That is not cruelty, child. That is arithmetic." He raised one hand, and the six dead around him stepped forward off the ridge, down the glass slope, moving with that terrible unhurried precision. "I am sorry. Truly. Take the flame back if you can carry it. But I will not have you reach the hollow. The solstice will pass, the debt will default, and the valley will seal itself forever—blind, small, and safe. In a hundred years no one will remember there was ever a sky."

"Then they'll remember me," Emrys said, "as the last fool who tried to open a window."

And they did the thing that every sensible part of them screamed against: they stopped running, unshuttered the lantern, and let the flame see the sun.

It had been dark for three hundred years. Even shuttered, even caged, it had spent the whole journey straining toward warmth like a plant toward light—and now Emrys gave it the sky, and the flame *rose*, a pillar of gold out of the iron cage, and every revenant on the steppe turned toward it at once with a sound like a forest bending in wind.

The six dead of the court stopped.

They stared at the fire with their starless eyes, and Emrys watched—watched in real time—as something moved behind those eyes. Not memory exactly. Deeper than memory. The body remembering warmth the way the body remembers its mother's heartbeat, from before the mind existed to store such things.

One of them sat down on the glass. Just sat, cross-legged, like a tired child, and turned its face to the light.

"No," the First Speaker said, and for the first time his mildness cracked, and underneath it was something old and frightened. "No—you were *buried*—"

"They were *starved*," Emrys said. "There's a difference. You of all people should know it. You built your whole life on pretending there isn't one."

The flame sank back into the lantern on its own, sated, dimming to a contented ember-glow, and in the ringing silence that followed, Sable grabbed Emrys's sleeve and pulled, and the party ran—ran hard, ran stumbling, ran with sixty revenants rising behind them to follow the fire and six of the court's dead standing confused on the glass, having forgotten, perhaps forever, which side of the arithmetic they belonged to.

* * *

They ran until full dark, and then they ran until Emrys's leg gave out entirely, and then they didn't run anymore.

Idris bound the wound by feel, in the black, with strips of Sable's spare shirt and a paste of steppe-moss that burned like a lie and worked like a prayer. The cut was deep but clean. Emrys bit down on a strap of leather and made no sound, mostly.

"You should have left me," they managed afterward, lying against Wren's shoulder while the wind combed the flats. "Slower now. All of you, slower because of me."

"Yes," Sable agreed cheerfully, keeping watch. "Terrible plan, saving you. Unfortunately we're stuck with it."

"You're stuck with *me*," Emrys corrected. "There's a difference."

"There is," Wren murmured, half-asleep, her fingers combing absently through Emrys's hair the way she must once have done for a daughter whose name had gone into the wind. "That's rather the whole point, dear. A covenant of hearth and hand. Nobody ever asks what the *hand* is for." Her breathing slowed. "It's for holding on."

Emrys lay awake long after the others slept, one hand resting on the warm iron of the lantern, watching the horizon where the hollow would be, somewhere past all that dark.

Sixty revenants slept standing in a ring around them, patient as fence posts. Somewhere behind, a man who had chosen a cage was regrouping. Somewhere ahead, a council was nailing shut a valley's doors. And somewhere inside Emrys, quieter than any of it, the archive's rust-brown ink kept repeating itself like a heartbeat:

*None who come hungry shall be turned from the fire.*

Eight days.

Behind them, far across the steppe, the dark began—at last, faintly, unmistakably—to move. Not the revenants. Something else. Something the flame had woken that even Idris's warmth-sight flinched from, a pressure on the edge of perception like a storm deciding whether to be born.

Idris sat bolt upright in the dead of night, eyes wide, staring south.

"That," he whispered, "is the rest of them. Every hollowed thing the steppe ever made. And Emrys—they're not following the fire."

He turned to look at them, and his starless eyes were wet.

"They're following *us*."
