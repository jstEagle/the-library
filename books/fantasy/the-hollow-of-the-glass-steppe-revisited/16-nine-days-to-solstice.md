## 16. Nine Days to Solstice

**Emrys**

The flame rode in a lantern of black iron that Sable swore had carried stranger cargo, and it did not behave like fire. It leaned. All night, across the salt-crusted flats of the steppe, the little light inside the glass bent toward Vael Hollow like a compass needle made of hunger, and Emrys found they could not stop watching it.

Nine days. The solstice was nine days off, and the First Hearth lay a hundred miles behind them, cold as a dead man's hearthstone. Everything now depended on getting the stolen flame home before the longest night—before the revenants came to collect a debt the hollow had spent three hundred years misreading.

"Stop looking at it like that," Sable said without turning her head. She walked at the front of their strung-out line, reading the ground the way Emrys read wardscript—by feel, by habit, by things too small for anyone else to see.

"Like what?"

"Like it's going to tell you something. It's not. Fire doesn't talk." A pause. "Well. That fire might."

Emrys looked anyway. Inside the glass, the flame was the color of a hearth at evening, orange shot through with gold, and when the wind gusted off the flats it bent against the wind, always toward home. Toward the hollow. Toward the people who had voted to give Emrys's life to pay for their safety.

*They don't know yet,* Emrys reminded themselves. *By now Wren's words have reached them—or the council has kept them quiet.* Either way, the people of Vael Hollow were waiting behind their cracked wards for salvation or annihilation, and both were walking toward them in a lantern.

Behind them, on the horizon, the sky had been wrong all morning.

It was wrong the way it had been wrong the night the stars went out—a pressure more than a color, a bruise spreading along the southern edge of the world. Idris had named it an hour ago with a voice gone flat and careful: *They've smelled it. The flame moving is like blood in water for them.*

"How far?" Emrys asked now, because someone had to say it aloud.

Idris didn't answer at first. He stood at the rear of the line, where he'd insisted on walking since they left the Court of Ash and Salt, facing backward more than forward, watching the horizon grow its bruise. His hands were steady. His eyes were not.

"A day," he said. "Maybe less. They don't tire, Emrys. We do."

Emrys looked back at Wren, who was riding strapped into the smugglers' cart Sable's network had stashed at the waystation, because her legs had begun to forget how legs worked. Half-hollowed. The starless wind had gotten into her on the third day out from the Hearth, and what it took, it took in order: first small things, then large. She still knew Sable's name. She sometimes called Idris *the boy*, which he tolerated with a patience that broke Emrys's heart, because Emrys understood what the tolerance cost him.

"Wren," Emrys said gently. "How are you doing?"

Wren turned her head. Her eyes were still hers—that was the mercy, the terrible temporary mercy—but there was a faintness around her edges now, like a sketch left out in rain.

"I was just thinking," she said, "that I know I ought to know your name, dear, and I'm furious about it."

"You knew it this morning."

"This morning I knew nine hundred things. This afternoon I know eight hundred and ninety." She smiled, and it was the old Wren smile, crooked and defiant, and Emrys had to look away before their face betrayed them. "Don't you dare mourn me while I'm still here to kick you for it. Walk. All of you. We're burning daylight we can't afford."

They walked. The steppe rang under their boots like struck glass. And behind them, patient as arithmetic, the dark came on.

* * *

**Sable**

Sable had crossed the glass steppe eleven times in her smuggling years, and she had a private taxonomy for its weather. There was mirror-wind, which showed you your reflection in the air itself. There was salt-haze, which softened everything until the flats seemed to go on into a white forever. And there was shame-weather, the rarest kind, when the steppe decided to stop being scenery and start being honest.

It was shame-weather now.

She felt it coming an hour before it arrived—a stillness, a hush, the sense of a room full of people turning to look at you. By then it was too late to route around it; the flats ahead were the only pass wide enough for the cart, and the dark was closing behind them like a hand.

"Brace yourselves," she said. "The mirrors are waking up."

The glass underfoot clouded, then cleared, and their reflections rose to meet them—not the reflections of now. The reflections of *was*.

Sable saw herself at nineteen, standing in a cellar in Lower Vael with a chisel in one hand and a wardstone the size of a loaf in the other, prying loose the corner-stone of the border while her contact held the lantern. She saw her own face in that moment, and it hadn't been guilty. That was the worst of it. She'd been *excited*. She'd thought of it as freeing something, cracking open the cage, and she'd pocketed the stone and walked away whistling, and she had never once imagined the slow starvation that followed—the wards thinning year by year, the founders beyond the border cut off from the hearth-magic that fed them, hollowing by degrees into the things that had come knocking on the guild door.

One stone. One night's work. Three centuries of hunger, paid forward.

"Keep walking," she said aloud, to herself as much as the others. "They're not real. They're just—"

"They're real enough," Idris said quietly.

His reflection had stopped walking. He stood in the middle of the pass staring down at his own image, and the image stared back, and between them passed something Sable couldn't see and didn't want to.

"What do yours show?" Emrys asked him. Carefully. Gently, the way you approached any wound on this steppe.

Idris was silent long enough that the wind filled the space with a sound like far-off singing, which meant the revenants were close, though Sable didn't say so.

"Mine shows the last morning I was whole," he said at last. "Before I let them begin the hollowing. Before I ran from it halfway through." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It shows me choosing. That's what I can't forgive. Everyone else in my line was taken. I *chose*, and then I chose again to stop, and I have spent two hundred years telling myself the second choice undid the first."

He started walking again, fast, and the reflection stayed behind, and Sable understood—Irrefutably, in her bones—that his had shown him the same thing hers had shown her. Not the crime. The appetite for it.

She put the cart's handles over her shoulders and pulled harder, and the shame-weather broke apart around them like a fever sweating out, and she told no one what she'd seen, because some debts you carry rather than spend.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren watched her memories the way other folk watched birds: knowing most would fly off and never return, loving each one fiercely while it perched.

She had made a game of it, in the cart, to keep from screaming. *Name it before it goes,* she told herself. *Every memory that surfaces, speak it aloud, pin it down like a moth.* So she narrated, in a voice grown thin, the inventory of a life:

"The winter the guildhall chimney smoked backward for a month and old Master Corvin blamed everyone but himself. The smell of tallow and pine resin in the mending-room. The sound Emrys made the first time they grounded a live ward—like a cat stepping on a hot stove, only pleased about it. Nine names read every autumn. Nine candles. My sister's face, which I have not thought of in forty years and which I will not lose tonight, I will not—"

Her sister's face went.

She felt it leave, the way you feel a tooth come loose—not pain exactly, but the sudden strange lightness where weight used to be. She sat in the jolting cart and reached for it and found smooth blankness where a beloved face had lived, and she heard her own voice continue the inventory, steadier than she felt, because if she stopped speaking she would start weeping and if she started weeping she might not be able to find the words to stop.

"—and the boy who delivered our flour had a laugh like a door hinge, and the river froze solid the year of the double comet, and I mended the east wall stones twice in one summer because Corvin over-torqued them, bless his stubborn ghost—"

Emrys appeared beside the cart, running to keep pace with it, their face pale and set. "Wren. You don't have to do this."

"Yes, I do." She fixed them with what she hoped was still her teacher's stare. "And so do you. When I can't remember anymore, *you'll* remember for me. That was always the arrangement, wasn't it? I hold the guild's past; you hold its future. Well. The arrangement stands. Take the list."

"I'm not ready—"

"No one is ever ready. That's what makes it a gift instead of a chore." She grabbed their wrist, and her grip was still strong; whatever the hollowing took, it had not yet come for her hands. "Listen, child, because I'll only say this once, and it may be the last clear thing I ever give you. The hollowing isn't death. Do you understand? It's not darkness, it's *drainage*. What leaves us goes somewhere. The founders weren't destroyed—they were poured out, spilled into the wards, spread so thin they forgot themselves. Which means—" her breath caught, and she pushed through it, "—which means everything they take from me is still *here*." She struck the ground with her fist, the glass steppe ringing faintly. "Here. In the land between. Nothing is lost that love won't go looking for. Now say it back."

"Nothing is lost that love won't go looking for," Emrys repeated, and their voice cracked on the second half.

"Good. Write that down somewhere. Put it in the covenant." She released their wrist and settled back against the crates, exhausted in a way sleep wouldn't touch. "Now get back in formation. And Emrys—when we reach the gate, if the council has sealed it—and they will, cowards always bar the door when the bill arrives—you tell them Wren sent you. Tell them Old Wren remembers their names even when they've forgotten their own."

She smiled at them. Then she closed her eyes to run the inventory again, from the top, and prayed to no one in particular that the list would still be long tomorrow.

* * *

**Idris**

On the sixth day, Idris stopped at the crest of a rise and looked back, and saw them clearly for the first time.

They came across the flats like a tide of unlit lamps—hundreds of the hollowed, moving with the terrible economy of things that waste nothing, not even motion. At this distance they might have been a migration of gray birds, or ash given purpose. But Idris had been half of one once. He knew what moved beneath that grayness: hunger, ancient and specific, the hunger of creatures built to be sustained by a hearth that had gone cold, wandering the dark between worlds for three hundred years with nothing to feed on but memory.

They had smelled the flame. It drew them the way spring draws thaw-water, and they could not stop coming, because stopping was not in them anymore.

"They'll reach us by tomorrow night," he told the others at the evening halt. He knelt by the cold campfire—they dared no flame but the lantern's caged gold—and drew lines in the salt crust with a stick. "We're faster downhill, slower up. The cart slows us further. Even at our best pace, we cross the border on the eighth day. Solstice eve. They arrive—" he tapped a line well ahead of theirs, "—on the seventh."

"Then we fight," Sable said flatly. She had her knives out, cleaning them, a ritual that seemed to organize her thoughts.

"With what? They aren't flesh. Blades pass through like smoke." He hesitated. The thing he had to say had been climbing his throat for two days, and it tasted of ash, and he said it anyway, because Wren had taught him—without meaning to, simply by being—that courage was mostly saying the true thing out loud. "There's another way. But it costs."

Everyone looked at him. The lantern-light made their faces small and warm and mortal, and he loved them, these three, with a sudden ferocity that nearly undid the speech entirely.

"The hollowing runs on attention," he said. "That's what it is, underneath—it's a creature made of need, and need seeks its object. When they come near me, they... hesitate. Because I'm partly theirs. Partly hollowed already, from the old days, the part I never finished shedding. They recognize me. And recognition slows them." He met Emrys's eyes. "If I walk toward them instead of away—if I stand in their path and let them *look* at me, really look, the way they can't help doing—I can hold them. An hour. Two, maybe. Long enough for you to gain a full day."

"And what happens to you," Emrys said. Not a question. Their voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when they were holding something down.

"I begin to hollow again. For real, this time. All the way." He said it evenly, because the alternative was not saying it. "I'd forget my name. I'd forget all of you. I'd become one of them, Emrys. Permanently. There's no coming back from a finished hollowing—I'd be at the gate on solstice night, reaching for the flame with empty hands, and none of you would be able to reach me."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off, the wind sang its revenant-song, closer than yesterday.

"No," Emrys said.

"It's not your choice."

"It's absolutely my choice. You're mine. You're *ours*." Emrys's composure finally split, and grief and anger came through together. "We didn't cross the steppe to trade one sacrifice for another. Don't you see? That's the whole point—that's what the covenant got wrong for three hundred years! Somebody always paying, somebody always spent! I am *not* building the future out of the same broken material!"

"Then build it quickly," Idris said softly, "because they're coming regardless, and I would rather buy your hours with my name than watch you all spend your lives instead. Let me choose this. It may be the only fully mine thing I've ever chosen."

He saw Emrys waver—saw the exact moment duty and love collided and neither won. He saw Sable look away and jaw tight. He saw Wren, wrapped in her blanket by the cart, watching him with eyes still bright and knowing, nodding once, slowly, the nod of one mender approving another's diagnosis.

"We don't decide tonight," Emrys said at last, hoarse. "We decide at the pass. If there's another way—even a bad one—we take it."

"There isn't," Idris said gently. "But all right. At the pass."

* * *

**Emrys**

The pass came on the seventh day, a slot of shadow between walls of glass taller than the guildhall, the last choke-point before the long descent to the border ruins. And the revenants arrived at its mouth an hour after sunset, exactly as Idris had promised, pouring into the narrow ground like spilled ink finding its level.

There were hundreds. In the lantern-light their eyes were holes in the world—not dark, but *absent*, places where seeing should have been and wasn't. The nearest one raised its head, scenting, and the sound it made was almost a word, worn down by centuries until only the shape of longing remained.

Emrys stood at the front of the party with the lantern held high, and understood, with total clarity, that there was no clever way through. No trick. No ward left in them strong enough to turn this tide. Every step of the journey had cost something—the Hearth, Wren's memories, Sable's secret laid bare, Idris's wholeness already half-spent—and now the final toll booth stood before them, and the currency it wanted was the person Emrys loved best among the living.

"Get the cart ready," Idris said behind them. His voice was calm. He had already taken off his pack. "When I raise my hand, run. Don't look back. Looking back slows you, and every hour is—"

"I know what every hour is."

Emrys turned. Idris stood in the lantern-gold, and he was smiling, and it was the smile of someone setting down a weight carried two hundred years. Behind him the revenant tide pressed closer, filling the pass wall to wall, and above it all the sky was a bruise with no stars in it anywhere.

"Idris," Emrys said. "Your name. Say it once more. For me. So I can hold it."

Something flickered across his face—the first crack in his calm.

"Tomas," he whispered. "My name was Tomas, before I was anything else. Idris is the name I made after. Keep them both."

The revenants were thirty paces off. Twenty. The nearest one opened its absent eyes wider, and Idris stepped forward, raising his arms, becoming a banner, a beacon, a door—

And that was when Wren's voice cut across the pass like a blade of dawn.

"*Hollowed ones!*"

She stood atop the cart, blanket thrown back, gaunt and blazing, half-erased and utterly unafraid, and in her hands she held the covenant fragment torn from the archive, and she began—clear and carrying, in the old tongue of the founders—to speak the true names of the dead.

The entire tide stopped.

And in the ringing silence that followed, every starless eye in the pass turned toward her, and Emrys felt the flame in the lantern lean—not toward home anymore, but toward *her*.
