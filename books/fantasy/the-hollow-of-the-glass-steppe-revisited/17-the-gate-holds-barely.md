## 17. The Gate Holds, Barely

Wren had spent seventy years learning the shape of forgetting.

She knew how it came for a mind—not like a storm but like a slow tide, taking first the far things and then the near ones, until a woman stood in her own kitchen unable to say why she'd come there. She had watched it take Old Maren of the weavers' row, who died calling her husband by the name of a chair. She had watched it take half the guild, name by name, candle by candle.

So when the starless wind found her on the third day out from the Waystation of Small Mercies, she knew exactly what it was doing to her, and that knowledge was its own particular cruelty.

It started with the guild's names. She was walking beside Emrys—dear stubborn Emrys, who carried the covenant fragment folded against their heart like a second heartbeat—and she reached for the roll of the dead to steady herself, the way she always did, nine names like nine stones across a stream. Halvard. That one came easy. Then the second name, and it was *there*, she could feel the groove of it where it had been worn into her by forty years of remembrance nights, but the sound wouldn't come. It was like reaching into a pocket and finding the coin gone smooth.

"Emrys," she said, and her voice came out thinner than she meant it to. "Say me the names."

Emrys looked at her sideways. The steppe's grey light made them look younger and older at once, which Wren supposed was what grief did to a face. "The guild names?"

"The nine. Say them to me. Don't ask why."

Emrys didn't ask. They said them, all nine, slow as a mender counting stitches, and Wren held each one as it passed, pressing it down into herself like a seed into winter soil. Halvard. Brenna. The twins, Osk and Osla. And so on, down to the last, the youngest, the one whose death had broken the guild's back and left only an apprentice standing in an empty hall.

"There," she said. "That's better." It wasn't better. She could feel the tide pulling at the hem of her, patient as water. But the names were planted now, and some part of her would keep them even if the rest of her forgot where they'd come from.

Sable walked ahead with her smuggler's stride, saying nothing, but Wren saw her glance back twice. Idris walked apart, as he always did now, his edges going soft and grey where the hollowing had taken hold of him again. He had given pieces of himself away on the road to buy them time—Wren had watched him do it, watched him stand in the revenants' path and let the starless wind pour through him, spending his memories like a man spending his last coins. He still remembered his name. He'd told her that was the line he would not cross. Everything else was negotiable.

"You're doing it too," Idris said quietly, falling into step beside her. He nodded at her hands. They were trembling, and worse than trembling—the fingertips had gone faintly translucent, like glass held up to a window. "The wind got you. At the flats."

"I know what it did."

"It can be stopped. If we get inside the hollow, if the wards—" He stopped himself. They both knew what the wards were now, or were about to be. "If we get home."

*Home.* Wren turned the word over. She had been born outside the hollow—that was the thing almost no one living knew, the thing that had gotten her quietly excluded from every guild supper and council seating for sixty years, the reason she'd been permitted to grow strange and half-mad at the edge of things. Her mother had been a steppe-woman who married a hollow mender and was never quite forgiven for it. Wren had been the child of both worlds and welcome in neither, and she had made a life out of the space between, the way a lichen makes a life out of stone.

Now the wind had found that space and poured itself in.

"I'm not afraid of it," she said, and found, to her surprise, that it was mostly true. "I've been forgetting my whole life, boy. Everyone has. This is just faster about it." She squeezed his arm, careful not to let her translucent fingers pass through him. "What I'm afraid of is arriving too late."

They crested the long rise above the border ruins two hours past midday, and Wren stopped so suddenly that Sable nearly walked into her back.

Below them, the valley of Vael Hollow lay green and small as a garden seen through a keyhole. And along its rim, where the old wardstones stood in their broken ring, the council was building a wall of light.

* * *

There must have been two hundred people working the border. Wren could see the speaker's white sash moving among them, and the glint of hammers on new-cut stone, and the shimmer of fresh-laid wardwork rising in a seamless curtain where the shattered stones had left gaps wide enough to drive a cart through. The light of it was wrong—she knew wardlight the way she knew birdsong, and this was wardlight twisted tight, wound to exclude rather than to shelter. It hummed a note she had heard once before, on the night the stars went out. A note of closing.

"They're sealing it," Emrys said. Their voice was flat with disbelief, as though saying it plainly might make it less true. "We have the flame. We're three days from solstice. We're carrying the answer, and they're *sealing the door*."

"People don't want answers," Sable said. Her voice had the hard brightness she used when something hurt. "People want walls. I've sold enough contraband over enough borders to tell you: the wall is never about what's outside. It's about what they can't bear to look at inside."

Idris had gone very still. Wren followed his gaze and understood: down among the workers, here and there, moved figures that did not sweat, did not bend, did not speak. Hollowed ones. The council had bound whatever revenants it could catch—or perhaps the revenants had simply come, drawn to the work of exclusion like moths to a cold flame—and set them to laboring on the very wards that had starved them. There was a horror in it that Wren felt in her teeth.

"They're using them," Idris said. His voice was quiet and terrible. "My people are carrying the stones for their own prison."

"We're going down there," Emrys said.

"No," said Wren.

Everyone turned. Wren heard her own voice as if from a distance, thin and cracked and absolutely certain. "Look at it, child. Two hundred people and a curtain of closed light, and four of us on a hill with a stolen flame and a dying mender. We walk down there, they'll bar the gate before we reach it, and they'll be right to. From where they stand, we're the ones bringing the plague home." She pointed with a hand that was more glass than flesh now, nearly halfway up. "You don't argue with a wall, Emrys Lockridge. You go around it, or under it, or—you make it ashamed of itself."

Sable snorted. "And how does one shame a wall?"

Wren looked at the curtain of light for a long time. The tide was pulling at her; she could feel it worrying loose the name of the village where she'd learned to knit, the color of her mother's good shawl, the taste of the first tea she'd ever drunk alone. Let it take those. She had spent her life at the threshold, unwelcome on either side, and she had never once thought of that as anything but a wound.

But thresholds, she thought now, are also doors. And doors open from both sides.

"The founders built the first wards," she said slowly, thinking aloud, feeling for the shape of the idea the way an old mender feels for a fracture beneath skin. "Every ward since is a copy of a copy of their work. The words have been rewritten—the covenant was rewritten, we know that—but the *names*, Emrys. The founders' true names. Those were never written down because written names can be forged. They were passed mouth to mouth, wright to apprentice, and the last mouth that held all of them—" she tapped her own sunken chest, "—was mine. Old Wren, who sat with the dying guildmaster through his last winter while he babbled everything he was afraid anyone would write down."

Emrys stared at her. "You never told me that."

"You never needed it before." She smiled, and felt the smile crack her face like ice on a pond. "A ward knows its maker's name the way a dog knows its master's whistle. You can't force it open. But you can call it, and it will remember what it was for. And what it was for, child, was *welcome*. Whatever else those fools down there have wound into it, underneath, in the bones of it, is the founders' intent. And the founders meant it to open."

"To whom?" Sable asked.

"To whoever speaks the names truly." Wren began to walk down the hill, and after a moment they all followed. "To the excluded one. That's the joke of it, see. Sixty years they kept me outside every door in that valley because I was half steppe-blood, and it turns out the door only opens for someone who belongs to neither side entirely. I am the key they locked out."

* * *

They came down to the border as the sun failed, and the council's sentries saw them coming from a long way off, because Wren made no effort to hide.

She walked in front. Behind her came Emrys with the flame wrapped in salt-cloth and iron—a dull red glow at their chest, like a coal refusing to die. Behind them came Sable with her knives visible, because Sable believed honesty was best delivered sharp. And behind them, hands bound with his own belt, walked Idris, offering himself as proof of good faith, though Wren suspected the gesture would be wasted on people who had already decided what he was.

The curtain of light rose before them, twenty feet high, humming its note of closing. The speaker stood at its base with the council's gray-robed elders fanned behind her, and her face, when Wren got close enough to read it, was the face of a woman who had chosen cowardice and was determined to call it wisdom.

"Wren Galloway." The speaker's voice carried flat across the trampled grass. "Cast out by vote of this council. Returning armed, in company of a smuggler and a revenant, carrying—I'm told—stolen fire. You will turn back."

"The solstice is in three days," Emrys called out. "We have the flame. We can end this. We can pay the debt the way it was actually written—hearth and hand, welcome, not blood—"

"The debt," the speaker said, "is being paid. Have you not seen?" She gestured, and Wren saw then what she had missed in the failing light: beyond the wardline, in the hollow's central square, scaffolds of pale wood. Not gallows. Something older. Offering-beds, the kind the rewritten rites described, the kind that took a life and fed it to the wards. "One life, freely given by council decree, and the hollow endures. That is the covenant as it has stood for three hundred years. We do not need your stolen fire. We need you to leave."

"Whose life?" Wren asked.

The speaker's jaw tightened. One of the elders looked away.

"Whose life?" Wren said again, louder, and her voice came out with a resonance that surprised her, a mender's voice, the voice that had talked fevered children through the worst nights of their lives. "Say the name. Go on. Say it aloud and see if your new wall likes the sound of it."

Silence. Somewhere among the workers, a hammer stopped.

"You haven't told them," Wren said, turning, making herself heard to the crowd now, to the two hundred frightened people with hammers in their hands. "You've voted to kill someone and you haven't even told the valley whose name is on the paper. Because you know what happens when you say it. You know they'll hear it, and they'll start remembering that this covenant was supposed to be about *all of us*, and walls are so much easier to build when nobody's naming what's buried under them."

"Enough." The speaker lifted her hand, and the ward-curtain brightened. "Last chance. Turn back, all of you. Even the revenant may turn back—it changes nothing, it saves nothing, but you may all live out your small lives on the steppe."

"No," Wren said.

And she stepped forward, alone, to the foot of the wall.

* * *

The light pushed at her like deep water. She felt it trying to read her, sorting her the way a customs officer sorts cargo: hollow blood, steppe blood, guild-trained, council-cast-out, half-hollowed, half-everything, belonging nowhere. She felt it searching for the category that would let it refuse her, and finding none, and hesitating.

Good, she thought. Stay confused. Confusion is a crack.

She raised her voice, and she spoke the names.

Not loudly, at first. The way you speak to a sickroom. The way the guildmaster had spoken them to her, that last winter, between coughs, making her repeat each one back until it lived in her mouth like her own.

"Halvard Under-Root, who laid the first stone and wept doing it, because he knew what the valley would forget. Brenna Ash-Hollow, who fed the first hearth from her own two hands and asked nothing back but that the door stay unlatched. Osk and Osla Twin-Born, who wrote the words *hearth and hand* and were not listened to."

The curtain flickered. Along its whole shining height, a tremor ran like wind through wheat. Down the line, workers had stopped. The elders were shouting. The speaker's voice cut through everything, ordering the wards reinforced, and the wards did not reinforce, because they were listening.

Wren kept speaking. The tide in her head pulled hard now, taking the color of her mother's shawl, taking the village where she'd learned to knit, taking—she felt it go and grieved without stopping—taking the name of her father's favorite horse. She let them go. Names cost. These were worth it.

"Corvess Night-Mender, who argued at the First Hearth that a ward should be a doorway with a latch, never a wall. Ilset Farwalker, who crossed the steppe barefoot to learn the steppe-folk's tongue so the covenant could be spoken in both voices. Maren Glasswright, who blew the first mirror-flat pane and saw her true self in it and was not ashamed."

Her knees were going. She felt Emrys move to catch her and waved them off without breaking cadence. The light above her was strobing now, bright and dim, bright and dim, like a lamp with a moth in it, like a heart remembering how to beat.

"And last," Wren said, and here her voice broke, and she let it break, because the last name deserved breaking, "last, Aldous Vael himself, founder of Vael Hollow, who built the border not to keep the world out but to give the wounded a place to heal—and who said, on the last night anyone bothered to record, *when the healing is done, take the walls down*. Three hundred years, and the healing is done, and you have built the walls higher."

She looked up at the curtain of light, at the storm it had become, and she said the thing she had come down the hill to say, the thing sixty years of exclusion had taught her:

"You are ashamed of yourselves. I can feel it. Every stone you were copied from remembers what you were for, and you have forgotten, and it *hurts* you, doesn't it? Humming that ugly little note, day after day, sealing out the very people who built you. You were never meant to be a cage. Open."

The silence lasted three heartbeats.

Then the curtain of light tore—cleanly, silently, from crown to ground, the way cloth tears when the weave is finally shown its flaw—and the border of Vael Hollow stood open for the first time since the stars went out.

* * *

Afterward, Wren would remember very little of the next hour. She remembered the crowd's faces, blank as eggs, then not blank. She remembered the speaker screaming for the wards to be relaid and the workers not moving, because they had heard the names too, and names are a kind of infection. She remembered Sable's hand under her elbow, solid and unglamorous and exactly where she needed it. She remembered Idris stepping through the torn wardline first, into the hollow that had starved him, and pausing there as if waiting to be struck, and not being struck, and weeping without any change in his expression, tears simply falling off a still face like rain off a statue.

Mostly she remembered the light going out of her fingertips. Color flooding back into her hands like dawn into a window. The tide receding—not all the way, never all the way, she could feel what it had taken and knew some of it was gone for good—but receding, because the hollowing fed on severance, and she had just spent the last of herself stitching two worlds together.

"Old woman," Sable said beside her, "you are the single most terrifying person I have ever met, and I once smuggled fire-salts past a dragon's nest."

"Flattery," Wren said. "Keep it coming."

Emrys crouched in front of her, and their eyes were wet and furious and full of something Wren recognized from the other side of a sickbed: the look of someone who has just realized how close they came to losing what they love. "Your hands," they said. "Before. They were—"

"I know what they were." She flexed her fingers, watching the lamplight of the flame-wrap move through them, opaque and ordinary and hers. "Some of it's still gone. Some names. Doesn't matter. I'll plant new ones." She gripped Emrys's shoulder and hauled herself upright, and together they looked through the open border into the valley, where the offering-beds stood pale in the central square, and the council's authority lay in pieces around the speaker's feet, and somewhere in the dark streets the people of Vael Hollow were waking to the fact that their wall had opened like a flower and no one had ordered it to.

"Three days," Emrys said. "Solstice eve is tomorrow. We have to carry the flame to the center, and we have to bring the valley with us, and half of them think we're the plague."

"Then you'd best start talking fast, child," Wren said. "And I'd best rest my voice." She paused. The tide had taken things tonight, but it had left her this, and she turned it over once more like a coin: the memory of the guildmaster's last winter, his last instruction, whispered when he thought she was asleep.

*When the time comes, girl, don't fight the wall. Remind it.*

"He said you'd do it," she told Emrys. "The guildmaster. His last coherent hour, he said: *the apprentice won't be the one to open the gate. It'll be the one we never let inside it.*" She laughed, a dry rustle like wind through winter reeds. "He meant me. Mad old fool was right."

Down in the valley, a bell began to ring—the council's alarm bell, ragged and urgent—and lights were moving in the streets, converging on the central square, converging on the offering-beds.

Three days to solstice. One night to change a valley's mind.
