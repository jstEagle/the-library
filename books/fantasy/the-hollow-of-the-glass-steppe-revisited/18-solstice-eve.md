## 18. Solstice Eve

The guild hall had never held so many people.

Emrys stood at the great hearth—cold for nine days now, swept clean and ringed with white chalk in the old wardwright's pattern—and watched the hollow file in out of the dark. Farmers first, in their winter coats, faces stiff with fear they hadn't learned to hide. Then the weavers and the smiths and the mill families, then the council's retainers, then people Emrys had never seen before: steppe-folk who had come down through the broken gate behind Wren's spoken names, wrapped in felt and ash-grey wool, moving through the hall the way strangers move through a church built by someone else's god.

And at the far end of the room, where the doors still hung open to the solstice-cold night, the revenants stood.

They did not come inside. Not yet. They filled the doorway like a held breath—nine tall figures, starless-eyed, their skin the grey of things kept too long from the sun. The hollowed founders of Vael Hollow, come home to collect a debt three centuries old. Idris stood apart from them, half a pace forward, as if he could not decide which side of the threshold he belonged on. His hands shook. Emrys could see it even from across the hall.

*One night*, Wren had said on the road back, her voice fraying at the edges like cloth worn through. *You get one night to say it right, boy. After that, the wind takes what's left of me and the rest of them take what's left of you.*

The wind had taken more than Emrys wanted to count. Wren sat now in the front row, wrapped in three blankets, her hands folded in her lap like a woman attending her own funeral. She had forgotten Sable's name twice that morning. She had forgotten her own once. But when Emrys had recited the guild's nine candles—the dead masters, one by one, the ritual she'd taught without ever meaning to—her mouth had moved along with every name, silent and exact. What the hollowing took, it took from the outside in. The oldest things held longest.

Emrys looked down at the table before them.

On it lay the covenant fragment, pressed flat between two panes of river glass. It was small—a hand's width of vellum gone brown as an autumn leaf, its edges chewed by three hundred years of archive moths. The ink had faded to the color of dried blood, but the words were still legible, because Emrys had spent four nights copying them onto every surface they owned until they were carved into memory:

*Hearth and hand. To all who come cold, warmth; to all who come hungry, bread; to all who come nameless, a name freely given. This is the whole of the covenant. Let no one add to it, for additions are the shape of fear.*

Below that, in a later hand, in ink still sharp and black after centuries, someone had written: *Amended by order of the Council of Speakers. See the Rite of Payment.*

The amendment was the lie. Three hundred years of it. Generations of guild masters teaching apprentices that the covenant demanded blood, that the wards were walls against hunger rather than arms around guests, that the steppe beyond the border was exile instead of home. Emrys's own master, Aldous Vane, had died still believing it—had died *teaching* it, his last lesson unfinished, his last apprentice left holding a ritual whose first page had been torn out before either of them was born.

The hall went quiet the way snow goes quiet: gradually, then completely.

First Speaker Halvard Merren rose from the council bench. He was an old man, straight-backed, dressed in the deep blue of his office, and his face was composed into something that wanted very badly to be dignity. Emrys had known him their whole life. He had presided over the remembrance feast every autumn. He had read nine names every year and never once stumbled.

He had also, nine days ago, voted to give Emrys to the revenants.

"Last Wardwright," Halvard said, and his voice carried the practiced weight of thirty years of council proclamations. "The hour grows late. The debt is due at dawn. Whatever you mean to do, do it quickly."

"I mean to read," Emrys said.

Murmurs moved through the crowd like water finding its level. Emrys lifted the glass panes away and picked up the fragment, and their hands were steady, which surprised them. They had expected to shake. Perhaps the shaking was finished. Perhaps there was nothing left in them to shake with.

"This is the covenant," Emrys said. "Not the version the council keeps under seal. Not the version taught to every apprentice for three hundred years. *This.* The original. Found in the guild archive, in Master Aldous's own strongbox, beneath a ledger of payments the council ordered made and never explained." They turned the vellum so the candlelight caught the faded ink. "I will read it aloud. All of it. Every word, including the ones someone hoped would rot."

They read. Their voice was not large—it had never been large—but the hall was so quiet that it reached the back wall anyway, and the open doors beyond it, and the nine figures standing in the cold.

*"Hearth and hand. To all who come cold, warmth—"*

Someone in the crowd made a sound, low and wounded, like a person remembering a dream.

*"—to all who come nameless, a name freely given. This is the whole of the covenant. Let no one add to it—"*

Emrys lowered the fragment. "Let no one add to it," they repeated. "Because additions are the shape of fear. Someone added to it. Someone wrote the Rite of Payment. Someone decided that *hearth and hand* meant *a life*, and taught your grandparents, and their grandparents, that the walls around this valley were bought with blood and had to be repaid the same way. And when the founders came back across the border last week—hollowed, starving, asking for what was promised—they came to collect on a debt that was never theirs to demand and never ours to pay."

"That is not—" Halvard began.

"It *is*," said a voice from the doors.

It was one of the revenants. A woman, tall as a doorframe herself, her starless eyes fixed on the speaker's bench with an intensity that made several councilors lean away. When she spoke again, her voice was dry as wind over salt flats, but underneath it Emrys heard something human and ruined: grief, very old, very patient.

"I am Ysolde Marrowgate," she said. "I helped raise the first stone of this valley. I remember the covenant because I spoke it—we spoke it, all nine of us, standing in the snow, giving it to every traveler who knocked. No one asked us for blood. We asked the world for *guests*." Her gaze moved slowly across the crowd, across the farmers and the weavers and the felt-wrapped steppe-folk, and settled at last on the council bench. "Then you built the wards too high. You walled off the hearth-magic that fed us. You did not mean to, perhaps. Fear does not mean to. But we starved behind your beautiful walls, and when we could no longer bear it, we walked into the steppe and let the wind have our names. And your councils, generation after generation, wrote down that *we* had demanded sacrifice. That *we* had asked for blood."

She raised one grey hand—not in accusation, Emrys thought, but the way a person reaches toward a fire.

"We only ever asked who was cold."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for Emrys to count their own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then Halvard Merren stepped down from the speaker's bench.

Emrys had imagined this moment on the long road home—in the waystation among the exiled steppe-dwellers, in the ruins of the Court of Ash and Salt with the stolen flame burning against their chest like a second heartbeat, on the frozen miles where Idris walked slower every day as the hollowing crept back into him. In those imaginings, the confession always came hard, dragged out, a battle. Emrys had braced for denial, for fury, for the old man's blue robes sweeping toward the door.

What came instead was worse and better: Halvard simply looked tired.

"You found the strongbox," he said.

"The strongbox was Aldous's," Emrys said. "He hid the original where no councilor would look—in a dead man's keeping. He knew, didn't he. Near the end. He knew what the guild had become."

"He suspected." Halvard's hands, clasped behind his back, were trembling slightly. Up close, in the honest light of the hall's few candles, Emrys could see how old he truly was—how many remembrance feasts, how many names. "When I was young, I served under Speaker Corwin Hale. On the night before his death he called me to him and told me to burn a certain document. He said the covenant as written would unmake the hollow—that if the people ever learned the walls were built from fear rather than necessity, they would tear them down, and the steppe would come in, and everything would end." He closed his eyes briefly. "I burned a copy. There is always another copy. Documents are like debts that way—I should have understood sooner."

"And the flame?" Sable's voice, from the second row, flat and hard. She stood with her arms crossed, her smuggler's scars catching the light. "The First Hearth didn't die. It was *robbed*. Somebody carried the fire out of the valley three hundred years ago and hid it in a court of ash and salt. Who gave that order?"

Halvard opened his eyes. He looked at Sable, and then—at the last—he looked at the revenants in the doorway, at Ysolde Marrowgate and her eight silent companions, and something in his spine finally gave.

"I did," he said. "No. Forgive me. My predecessor did, and I kept it done. That is the same thing, in the end. That is what office means." His voice dropped, and Emrys had to strain to hear it, but the hall was still so quiet that every soul heard anyway. "Corwin believed the covenant was a cage. He believed that if the flame stayed lit—if the welcome stayed *open*—the hollow would be eaten by everyone it fed. So he stole the fire and carried it beyond the border, and sealed the true text in the strongbox, and wrote the Rite of Payment so that the covenant would demand from the hollow instead of asking of it. A covenant that demands can be paid. A covenant that welcomes—" His breath caught. "A covenant that welcomes never stops being owed."

Ysolde made a sound. It might have been laughter. It might have been weeping. From a throat that had forgotten both, it was impossible to tell.

"And so we paid," Halvard continued, and now the words came faster, as if a dam had cracked and he could not stop what poured through. "Once a century, or when the wards weakened, or when the revenants stirred—the Rite of Payment, a life given to the border stones. My predecessors chose the condemned, the friendless, the ones no one would miss. And when the stars went out and the debt came due again, the council looked around this valley for the one life no one would fight to save." His eyes found Emrys. "We chose you. Last of your line. Keeper of a guild we let die. We chose you because we had already chosen, years ago, to forget you."

Wren stirred in her blankets. When she spoke, her voice was thin as paper, but the hall bent toward it.

"Aldous Vane knew," she rasped. "Knew what you'd done to his guild. Knew what you'd ask of his student. That's why he hid the text. That's why he died unfinished—because a finished teaching would've been a teaching *you* approved, and he wouldn't give you the satisfaction." She looked up at Emrys, and her eyes—cloudy now, half-hollowed, but still hers—were fierce. "Tell them, boy. Tell them what he'd have said."

Emrys's throat tightened. They thought of their master's hands, ink-stained, folding the strongbox shut. They thought of the last lesson, cut off mid-sentence, and how Emrys had spent twenty years trying to finish it alone.

"He'd have said that a ward is not a wall," Emrys said quietly. "He'd have said that a ward is a promise you build, and that a promise you keep with other people's blood isn't a promise at all. It's just a debt wearing its clothes."

They set the covenant fragment down on the table between the chalked circle and the cold hearth, where everyone could see it. Then they turned to face the whole hall—councilors and farmers, weavers and strangers, the felt-wrapped steppe-folk and the grey revenants in the door, Wren and Sable and Idris, who stood now exactly on the threshold, one foot in each world, shaking like a man standing in a wind only he could feel.

"Dawn is coming," Emrys said. "At dawn the debt comes due, and the council's answer—the old answer—is that I walk to the border stones and die, and the wards drink, and the hollow sleeps another hundred years behind its walls, blind and safe and small. That is one path. I am not going to take it. And I am asking you—" their voice caught, and they let it catch; there was no more time for a steady voice— "I am asking you not to make me carry it alone."

Sable shifted. Wren's chin lifted. But Emrys wasn't finished.

"The flame is here." They drew it out—from the iron lantern at their belt, the stolen fire of the First Hearth, banked and breathing, casting shadows that leaned the wrong way. "The First Hearth can be relit. But a hearth is not a fire. Anyone can strike a spark. A hearth is what a fire *means*—it's the word *stay*. It's the word *you're welcome*. And I cannot speak that word alone, because a welcome spoken by one person to no one is just noise in an empty room." They looked out at the faces. "The covenant says *hearth and hand*. It needs hands. All of them. Yours."

Silence. Long enough to hurt.

Then Halvard Merren did the thing Emrys would remember for the rest of their life, longer than the vanished stars, longer than the shattered wards. He walked to the cold hearth, knelt down inside the chalked circle—an old man's knees cracking on stone—and placed his bare hand flat upon the floor.

"If the hollow will have my hand," he said, "it may have it. It is the least of what I owe."

And the hall broke open—not into chaos, but into movement. A miller's wife knelt next, then her sons. A weaver. A smith. The steppe-folk looked to one another, and their elder, a woman with a face like weathered wood, nodded once, and they came forward too, kneeling at the edges of the circle, hands reaching toward a fire that was not yet lit. Sable swore softly and knelt. Idris crossed the threshold at last, fully, both feet, and knelt among people who had been taught to fear him, and no one drew away.

Outside the doors, the nine revenants had not moved. Ysolde watched with her starless eyes, and Emrys could not read her face—could not tell whether they were witnessing a homecoming or a verdict.

Dawn was two hours away. The chalk circle waited. The flame breathed in its lantern, small and stubborn, waiting to be asked.

Emrys knelt at the center of it all, beside the covenant's brown and fading words, and reached for the lantern's latch—and heard, from the doorway, Ysolde Marrowgate begin to speak in a language older than the hollow, older than the wards, older than any word for *welcome* that the valley had ever learned.
