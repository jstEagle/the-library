## 20. The Stars Return

The flame went up like a held breath finally released.

Wren felt it before she saw it — a warmth that started in her chest and spread outward through the places where the hollowing had eaten her hollow, filling them the way water fills a dry cup. She was standing at the edge of the crowd in the guild square, half-hollowed still, her memories returned to her only hours ago and sitting strangely in her mind like borrowed clothes. Around her, the people of Vael Hollow pressed shoulder to shoulder, and beyond them, at the square's open edges, stood the revenants — the founders, starless-eyed no longer, their faces wet with wept-back names.

And above it all, Emrys Lockridge stood at the relit hearth with their hands outstretched, speaking welcome in two tongues.

*Come in,* they had said, in the old steppe-speech and in the valley tongue both. *You are expected. You are wanted. Sit by our fire.*

The flame answered.

It rose from the hearth-stone not orange but white-gold, the color Wren remembered from her girlhood, from the one time she had seen a true star fall over the border wards and burn itself out against them like a moth against glass. The light climbed. It climbed past the guild hall's eaves, past the watchtower, past the height of anything built by hands, and it kept climbing, and the whole hollow tilted its face upward to follow it.

Then the sky opened.

Not all at once. The first star came back over the eastern rim of the valley — a single point of silver, so small and so impossibly far that for a moment no one dared believe it. Old Hollis the cooper made a sound like a man being struck. A child began to laugh and could not stop. And then a second star kindled beside the first, and a third, and then the darkness of the sky began to tear along seams no one had known were there, and the stars came home one by one, each ignition a small silent detonation of light, until the whole bowl of heaven over Vael Hollow blazed with them, more than anyone living had ever seen, more than the oldest songs had promised.

Wren looked up and felt her knees go soft beneath her.

She had spent seventy years under a blank ceiling. She had forgotten — truly forgotten, the way you forget the weight of your own name — that the night was supposed to be *inhabited*. That the dark was not an ending but a vast room full of distant fires. She gripped the arm of the woman beside her, a stranger, a steppe-dweller who had walked through the dissolved border that very morning, and the stranger gripped her back, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing in either language equal to it.

The wards did not shatter. That was the thing Wren would remember longest, when she told this story in later years, in classrooms full of children who had never known walls: the wards did not break. They *unmade themselves*, gently, the way frost leaves a window when the sun finds it. Along the old border stones, the last threads of wardlight thinned to gossamer, brightened once as if taking a final breath, and let go. Where the Broken Gate had stood — where Emrys and their outcasts had slipped into exile weeks ago — there was now simply an opening in the world, a road-shaped absence of anything keeping anyone in or out.

The wind that came through it smelled of salt and cold stone and something green growing far away.

"Look," whispered the stranger beside her, though everyone was already looking. "Look what it does."

The wardlight, released from its long duty, did not die. It drifted. It rose off the border stones in slow luminous motes, thousands upon thousands of them, and caught the new-starlit wind, and streamed inward across the valley like a river running backward through the air — a migration of light coming home. Some of it settled on the rooftops of Vael Hollow and lingered there like early snow. Some of it wound around the guild hall's chimney. And some of it, Wren saw, drifted to the revenants standing quiet at the square's edge, and sank into them, and where it sank, their eyes kindled — not starless anymore, not hollow, but lit from within with small steady points of white-gold fire.

Idris was the first. He had been standing apart from the other revenants, as he always did, the deserter, the one who'd refused the hollowing — and now the light found him too, and he gasped, and pressed both hands over his heart as if catching something falling.

"It doesn't hurt," he said, to no one, to everyone. "All these years I thought — going back would hurt. It doesn't hurt at all."

* * *

Sable had never been afraid of much. Fear was bad for business; she'd trained it out of herself the way you train a dog not to beg. But standing in the guild square with the whole sky on fire overhead, Sable discovered she was afraid after all — afraid in the specific, vertiginous way of a woman whose map has just been declared obsolete.

She had crossed the glass steppe eleven times. She knew its flats and its mirages, its waystations and its salt courts, every smuggler's cut and honest road alike. Her whole life had been built on knowing where the borders were: between the hollow and the steppe, between lawful and stolen, between the person she pretended to be and the one the mirrors showed. Borders were how she navigated. Take away the border and—

"Hey." Emrys appeared at her elbow, soot-smudged, exhausted, wearing the expression of someone who had just done the largest thing of their life and hadn't yet come down from it. "You're doing the thing where you look like you're calculating escape routes."

"I'm always calculating escape routes."

"I know. I'm asking if you need one."

Sable looked at them. This kid — this last apprentice, this former sacrifice, this person the council had tried to spend like coin — stood in the middle of everything they'd unmade, and asked her, genuinely, if she needed a way out.

"No," she said, and was surprised to find it true. "No. It's just—" She gestured up at the impossible sky. "I spent my whole life trading in things people weren't supposed to have. Wardstone. Passage. Truth, sometimes. And now everything's just— *open*. Anyone can walk anywhere. What am I supposed to do with that?"

Emrys considered this with the seriousness they gave everything, which Sable had decided, somewhere on the steppe, was her favorite thing about them.

"You could chart it," they said. "The steppe without the border. Nobody's ever mapped what's on the other side of everywhere. You know the flats better than anyone alive. And now there's no *anywhere else* — it's all one country, and nobody's drawn it yet."

Sable stared at them. Then she laughed — a short, disbelieving bark that turned into something warmer halfway through.

"A cartographer," she said. "You're proposing I go legitimate. Wren will never let me live it down."

"Wren is currently crying on a stranger," Emrys said. "Let her have the night."

Above them, another star ignited — a bright one, low over the western rim, and its light ran down the length of the old border road and turned it briefly to a ribbon of silver. Sable watched it and felt the vertigo shift, slowly, into something else. Not freedom as absence. Freedom as *blank paper*.

"All right," she said. "One map. But I'm naming landmarks after myself."

"There's already a Sable's Cut on the steppe."

"Then I'll get a second one. I'm worth it."

* * *

Idris walked the border that night, or where the border had been.

He could not sleep. None of the revenants could, he suspected — the newly-named, the freshly-lit, wandering the hollow's lanes like people returned from a long illness, touching doorposts and well-covers and the bark of orchard trees as if confirming the world still took their hands. But Idris had somewhere particular to go. He walked up through the terraced fields, past the last farmsteads, to the line of ancient border stones that crowned the valley's northern rim, and there he sat down on the cold grass beside the nearest stone and put his palm flat against it.

No hum. No resistance. The stone was just a stone now, lichened and patient, older than his people's hunger.

Beyond it, the glass steppe lay silvered under the returned stars, and it looked — this was the thing that undid him — it looked *small*. All those centuries, the steppe had been the great dark outside, the wilderness, the place of exile and thirst. Now, under a hundred burning stars, it was just land. Beautiful land. Land with roads on it, and waystations, and communities of exiles who lit lamps against the night because they believed, still, that no one was coming for them.

Someone should tell them, Idris thought. Someone should walk out there tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, and say: *the sky is fixed. The wall is down. You were never the reason for any of it.*

Footsteps behind him. He didn't turn; he knew the tread. Emrys came and sat down beside him in the wet grass, pulling their coat close, and for a while they both just looked at the steppe.

"My people will be afraid of this," Idris said at last. "The ones still out there. A wall coming down isn't freedom to everyone. To some it's the roof falling in."

"I know."

"And yours—" He glanced sideways at Emrys. "Your council sealed the gate against us while you were gone. They chose blindness. Walls don't come out of the ground, little wright. They come out of people. Those people are still here."

"I know that too." Emrys picked up a blade of grass, tore it, let the halves go. "But so is this." They nodded back toward the hollow, where the guild square still glowed with hearth-light and drifting motes of released wardlight, where music had started somewhere — actual music, fiddle and drum, the first festival Vael Hollow had held in living memory with the sky watching. "We rewrote the covenant tonight. Not on paper. In front of witnesses. *Hearth and hand.* If the council wants to rebuild the wall, they'll have to do it in front of everyone who saw the stars come back. And I don't think they can. I don't think fear survives being watched by that many stars."

Idris huffed — the closest his people came to laughter, once. Then he grew quiet again, and when he spoke, his voice had dropped into something older.

"When we hollowed ourselves," he said, "it was because the wards cut us off from the hearth-magic. We thought we were choosing death with dignity. We didn't know we were choosing *hunger with eternity*. Every year since, part of me has hated your founders for building the wall. And part of me has hated myself for what we became outside it." He turned his hand over, looked at the faint gold light still gathered in his palm like pollen. "Tonight I can't find either hate. Do you know what that's like? To set down a weight you've carried so long you'd built your posture around it?"

"Yes," said Emrys simply. "That's what the archive felt like. The day I read the original covenant and understood my whole guild had inherited a lie. Everything I was stood on a floor that wasn't there." They smiled, tired and crooked. "You get used to the vertigo. Or you don't, and you learn to love the falling."

They sat until the moon — the *moon*, returned and fat and silver, another thing the hollow had lived without — cleared the eastern ridge. Below them the festival burned on. Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and generous, and the steppe lay open in every direction, and no line anywhere said *this far and no farther.*

"We'll need teachers," Idris said eventually. "Both tongues. Both histories. Children born next spring won't know what a border was unless someone tells them, and someone should tell them true."

"The guild reopens," Emrys said. "With open doors. It was always supposed to be a school. My master said so, near the end — said the wards were just the guild's least interesting work." A pause. "Will you come? As a student, I mean. First one from beyond the border."

Idris looked at them for a long moment. Three hundred years old, give or take, and hollowed, and healed, and sitting in wet grass under a sky he'd mourned since before the hollow had a name.

"Yes," he said. "But I warn you — I ask questions."

"Good," said Emrys. "So do I. We'll get along."

* * *

Far out on the glass steppe, at the Waystation of Small Mercies, the exiled community stood in the street with their lamps unlit and their faces turned up.

They had seen it happen — the horizon whitening to the north, then east, then west, the darkness peeling back like a bandage. The eldest among them, a woman named Corvane who had been born in the hollow three generations after the exile and had never once stopped calling it home, dropped the bread she was carrying and did not pick it up.

"The stars," she said. "Children, look. *Look.*"

And in the doorway of the waystation, a boy of perhaps nine years — born on the steppe, knowing the sky only as story — tugged his grandmother's sleeve and asked the question that would travel, in one form or another, through every settlement on the steppe that night, and back along the open road toward the valley, arriving ahead of any messenger:

"If the stars came back," he said, "does that mean somebody finally opened the door?"

Corvane knelt down and took his face in her hands, and looked north, toward Vael Hollow, toward the thin gold thread of light visible now along the rim of the world where a wall used to be.

"Yes," she said. "And tomorrow, we knock."

* * *

In the guild hall, near dawn, Wren found Emrys asleep at the master's desk, cheek on the open archive, the original covenant fragment spread beneath their ink-stained fingers. She stood in the doorway a long moment, memorizing it: the last apprentice of the Wardwrights' Guild, keeper of a finished covenant at last, dead asleep with the stars pouring through the window onto the page.

On the fragment, below the old words — *hearth and hand, hearth and hand* — there was space left blank. Nine generations of guildmasters had left it blank, whether from humility or fear, Wren couldn't say.

She took up a pen, woke Emrys with a hand on their shoulder, and set the pen in their fingers.

"Well?" she said. "It's your covenant now, wright. Write the next line."
