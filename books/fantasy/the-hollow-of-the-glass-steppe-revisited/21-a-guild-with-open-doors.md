## 21. A Guild With Open Doors

The snowmelt came down from the rim of the hollow in a hundred bright threads, and by the second week of thaw the guildhall's roof was dripping like a patient thing learning to speak again.

Emrys heard it before they saw it — that steady percussion on the slate outside the dormer window, water keeping time where bells used to. They had slept in the master's loft for one hundred and forty days now, and still they woke some mornings expecting the silence of an empty building, the particular hush of a house that had run out of people. Instead: dripping. Footsteps below. Wren's voice rising through the floorboards in the cadence she used when someone had moved her tools without asking.

Emrys lay a moment longer, watching light move across the covenant fragment where it hung above the desk.

They had written the line in winter. It had taken three nights and most of a bottle of iron-gall ink, and even now, in the grey-gold of early spring, Emrys could not look at it without their chest doing something complicated.

*Hearth and hand, hearth and hand* — the old words, nine generations deep, written in a hand so fine it looked like frost. And beneath them, in ink still darker than the rest:

*A door left open is also a wall, if you build it wide enough to hold everyone.*

It wasn't elegant. Wren had told them so twice, with great relish. But it was true, and it was theirs, and every morning the light touched it and Emrys thought: *we are still here, and we said yes.*

They dressed, took the stairs two at a time because the banister was finally mended enough to bear it, and came out into the hall that had been, four months ago, a place of nine candles and one voice answering roll.

Now there were thirty-one voices, and not one of them answered to *here* the way guild apprentices used to. They answered to their own names, in their own accents — hollow-bred and steppe-bred and three children from the river families who'd come over the old border the first week the road opened, drawn by rumor of a place where you could learn to mend things instead of being taught to fear them.

Someone had propped both front doors wide. Both. The east door that had faced the valley, and the west door that had faced the steppe, which no one had unbarred in living memory until the solstice. Morning air moved through the hall like a slow animal, smelling of wet stone and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the flowering scrub that grew on the glass flats and nowhere else.

Emrys stood in it a moment and let the hall be what it was becoming.

* * *

Wren taught in the old remembrance room, because she said the dead would want to see this and she refused to have them looking over her shoulder while she worked in the warding yard.

She had hung no portraits. She had done something better: she had chalked the nine names across the blackboard's top edge in her looping, half-mad hand, and beneath them, in smaller letters, the eleven names of the revenants who had wept their names back at the reforging and chosen, afterward, to stay — to sit at the back of classes with their starless-turned-starbright eyes and learn, at their age, at their history, how to begin again.

"Again," Wren was saying as Emrys slipped into the doorway. She held up a cracked wardstone the size of a loaf, turning it in her knotted hands. "Say it with me. A ward is not a wall. What is a ward?"

"A promise," said the class, raggedly.

"A promise," Wren agreed. "Made of stone and patience. Now — who can tell me why this one failed?" She tossed it lightly to a girl in the third row, a steppe-born child named Ferrow who had crossed the glass with her grandmother the first spring month. "And don't say *because it broke*, Ferrow, or I'll set you copying the founders' names until your hand falls off."

"Because whoever made it was afraid," Ferrow said. "You can feel it. It's all clenched."

Emrys watched Wren's face do the thing it did — the flicker, the almost-loss, the grip on the present. Four months since the starless wind had caught her on the steppe. Four months since Emrys had sat up through the night reciting her memories back to her: *your name is Wren, you mended the Kettering child's leg with a splint and a prayer, you hate the smell of tallow but love the smell of beeswax, you defied the council in front of everyone and were glad.* Some mornings the wind still found her. Some mornings she forgot the word for *trowel* and stood holding it like a stranger's object until someone said it aloud and gave it home.

But she had not forgotten how to teach. That had gone down to the bone, past the reach of any wind.

"Clenched," Wren said now, delighted. "Exactly. Fear makes a tight weave, and tight weaves snap. Write that down." Her eyes found Emrys in the doorway and narrowed with mock suspicion. "And you. You're supposed to be at the yard."

"I'm supervising."

"You're hiding from the ledgers."

"I am *strategically delaying* the ledgers."

Wren snorted and turned back to her class, and Emrys lingered one more breath in the doorway, watching the nine names on the board watch over the room — dead masters, dead friends, and beneath them the eleven returned, and beneath them, in fresh chalk from yesterday's lesson, a list titled *Things a Ward Must Never Do*, the first entry of which, in Ferrow's careful hand, read: *Keep anyone out who has knocked politely.*

* * *

Sable kept her charts in the map room, which had been the archive, which had been, before that, a chapel to a hearth that had gone cold for nine generations without anyone admitting it.

The long table was buried under her work: the steppe rendered in ink and colored sand, the mirror flats marked in silver leaf that caught the window-light and threw it around the room like scattered coins. She had charted every waystation, every salt court, every crack in the world where the wind got through — and she had done it, she said, so that no one would ever again cross those flats the way the five of them had, hungry and hunted and running on stubbornness.

"The smugglers' routes," she'd told Emrys the first week, tapping the silver lines. "That's all I've got that's worth anything. Twenty years of knowing how to move through a country nobody wanted to see. Might as well spend it on something that isn't shame."

"It was never only shame," Emrys had said.

"It was mostly shame," Sable said, with the flat honesty she wore like armor. "But the charts are good. Let me keep the good part."

Now she stood over the table with Idris bent beside her, and they were arguing about the Waystation of Small Mercies — whether its springs ran year-round or only after melt, whether the road should bend east to spare travelers the flats entirely.

"You're drawing it wrong," Idris said. "The eastern pass floods. My mother's caravan lost two wagons there the year I was born."

"And your mother's caravan was carrying *salt*," Sable said, "which is the one cargo that doesn't care if it swims. For anyone else, the flats are safer. Flat. Level. No bandits because there's nothing to steal."

"There's always something to steal."

"Yes," Sable agreed, "and I know where all of it is, which is why the route goes through the flats." She looked up, caught Emrys watching, and grinned — quick, crooked, real. "Lockridge. Tell him."

"I'm not getting between a smuggler and a revenant about roads."

"Coward."

"Wardwright," Emrys corrected. "We're trained for it."

Idris laughed — he laughed easily these days, though sometimes in the middle of laughing his eyes went distant, checking, the way Wren's did, making sure the name was still his. He was twenty-two years hollowed and six weeks un-hollowed, and everything about him was new growth: the appetite, the questions, the fierce attention he paid to every lesson as though knowledge were bread and he'd been starving. He slept in the apprentice dormitory. He had asked, formally, in writing, to be enrolled — *the first student from beyond the border*, the council's proclamation had said, though Emrys privately thought the honor belonged to Ferrow, who had simply walked in one morning and sat down.

"Actually," Idris said, straightening, "since you're here. The warding yard. This afternoon's lesson. May I—" He stopped. Started again. Emrys had learned that Idris restarted sentences when the feeling behind them was too large for the first attempt. "May I help teach it? The steppe-side wards. The ones my people used. I remember them now, all of them, and they're different from yours, and maybe—" His hands moved, shaping the thought. "Maybe the students should learn both. Maybe that's the point."

Emrys looked at him a long moment.

When the revenants had come out of the dark last winter, demanding blood, Idris had stood apart from them — the one who refused the hollowing, the one who kept his name like a coal cupped in bare hands. Emrys had trusted him on the steppe because there had been no one else. Somewhere between the mirror flats and the First Hearth, trust had become something with roots.

"That," Emrys said, "is exactly the point. Afternoon yard. Bring your mother's caravan stories too. Ferrow will want them."

Idris smiled like sunrise over glass.

* * *

The warding yard had changed least and most.

Least, because the stones still stood in their teaching rings, the same concentric circles guild apprentices had paced for nine generations, learning to hum a note into granite until the granite hummed back. Most, because the outermost ring — the one that had always faced the border, the one where Emrys had spent their whole childhood learning to keep the world *out* — had been broken open. Deliberately. On the solstice morning after the reforging, Emrys had taken a mason's hammer to the ring's western arc, and the whole guild had watched, and no one had stopped them, and the gap had never been repaired.

Through the gap you could see the road. The old border, now a road: the wardstones sunk into the earth at intervals like sleeping animals, no longer singing separation, just singing — low and steady, a note that said *this place exists* rather than *you cannot enter*. Travelers passed along it daily now. Steppe-folk with salt-carts. Hollow farmers with apples, trading for glass-flowers. Children racing each other across a line that had once been death to touch.

This afternoon the yard held twelve students and two teachers, and the lesson was harmonics.

"Your stone wants to sing with mine," Emrys said, kneeling at the ring's center with a palm flat on warm granite. Around them, students knelt at their own stones, steppe-folk and hollow-folk mixed together because Wren had insisted on mixing them — "separate them and you're building the wall again, just with desks" — and because, mostly, they had begun choosing each other anyway. Ferrow sat beside a hollow boy named Aldous who had spent the first week terrified of her and the second week following her around asking questions about glass-flowers.

"But it doesn't know your song yet," Emrys went on. "So you don't force it. You don't clamp it into your key. You listen until you find the note you share. That's all warding ever was. That's all it should have been." They paused. "What we built instead — the walls, the wards that cut, the debt we let grow for nine generations — that wasn't warding. That was forgetting how to listen."

Idris raised his stone, and sang.

It was a sound Emrys had heard only once before — at the reforging, when the revenants had wept their names back — a note with winter in it, vast and old, the resonance of people who had carried their hunger across centuries. Beside it, Emrys set their own note: the guild-note, small and human, the one their dead master had taught them on a night much like this one.

For a heartbeat the two notes fought.

Then they found each other.

The sound that rose from the yard was not either song alone. It was a chord neither tradition contained, and every stone in the ring lit faintly with it, and Ferrow said, very quietly, "*Oh,*" in the voice of someone seeing a horizon.

Emrys stayed kneeling after the chord faded, hand on the stone, heart hammering.

*There,* they thought. *That. That's the covenant. We had the words wrong for nine generations and the sound was waiting the whole time.*

* * *

Evening came down soft. The students went home to the boarding houses the council had finally funded — grudgingly, then enthusiastically, once they realized visiting families spent money — and the guildhall settled into its evening rhythms: Wren clattering in the kitchen, Sable inking charts by lamplight, Idris in the dormitory reading aloud to himself, savoring words like a man eating after famine.

Emrys climbed to the loft and stood before the fragment.

The lamp caught the old frost-fine letters. *Hearths and hand, hearth and hand.* Nine blank generations. Then their own line, written in winter with shaking hands: *A door left open is also a wall, if you build it wide enough to hold everyone.*

Below it, space remained. There would always be space remaining; that was the nature of covenants, Emrys understood now — they were not documents but conversations, and conversations ended only when the people did.

They took up the pen.

Not to add another line. Just to stand there, pen in hand, reading their own words back the way Wren read her memories on bad nights — *this is yours, you wrote this, you meant it, it is still true.* Outside, the meltwater ticked on the slate. Downstairs, Wren dropped a pan and cursed with tremendous fluency, and Idris's reading stumbled with laughter, and Sable called out something dry that Emrys couldn't catch but could hear the smile in.

Belonging chosen, not inherited. That was the whole of it. They had been born inside walls, handed a debt like an heirloom, and told that love meant payment. They had crossed the glass and learned that love meant setting another place at the table.

Emrys set down the pen uncapped — tomorrow there would be ledgers, and lessons, and the council's envoy arriving next week to argue about funding, and all the ordinary friction of a world learning its new shape —

— and stopped.

Because through the floorboards, under Wren's clattering and Idris's reading and the meltwater's ticking, they heard the front doors.

Both of them, still propped open, as they always were now.

And in the doorway below — audible even from the loft, in the pause between sounds — footsteps had stopped. Someone was standing there. Someone hesitating on the threshold, in the lamplight spilling out onto the spring-dark road, working up the courage to knock.

Or perhaps — Emrys leaned toward the stairwell, listening — perhaps not knocking at all.

Perhaps simply waiting, the way travelers waited at open doors everywhere now, to see if the light inside was for them.

"Well?" Wren's voice floated up the stairs, warm and rough and utterly unsurprised. "Don't just stand up there, wright. Go answer it. That's rather the whole point, isn't it?"

Emrys was already moving.
