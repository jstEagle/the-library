## 22. The Hollow of the Glass Steppe

The road where the border used to be was paved with the old wardstones, and Emrys had insisted on it.

Not out of sentiment — or not only. The stones were good stone, quarried from the hollow's own hills, and they remembered. Travelers who walked the road at dusk sometimes swore they felt a warmth rise up through their boot soles, like standing on a hearth that had gone cold centuries ago and only just remembered fire. The steppe-folk called it *the kind road*. The hollow children called it nothing at all, because to them it had never been anything but a road, and Emrys loved them most for that.

They stood there now on a morning in late spring, twenty-two years — no. Twenty-three. Time had begun to move like water instead of honey, and Emrys had stopped keeping exact count somewhere around the year the first steppe-born child enrolled in the guild school and could not understand why the older hollow children flinched when she laughed too loudly near a wardstone. There were no wards left to flinch at. Only habit, which took longer to dismantle than any working of stone and starlight ever had.

The morning mist was burning off the glass flats beyond the road, and the light came up through it the way it always did now — refracted, doubled, the whole horizon glittering like a struck bell. Once, Emrys had crossed those flats believing they would kill them. They nearly had. Now a mail cart rattled along the road's far shoulder, its driver whistling something tuneless, a crate of steppe-honey and hollow wool stacked behind her seat, and she did not even glance at the place where the world had once ended.

Emrys watched her pass and felt the thing in their chest that had lived there since the night the stars went out finally settle into stillness. Not peace exactly. Peace was too smooth a word for what remained. It was more like a scar that had learned to be useful — the way an old break in a bone comes back stronger at the knot.

"You're doing the staring thing," said a voice at their elbow. "The one Wren says makes you look like you're auditing the sunrise."

Emrys turned. Pell was nine, gap-toothed, barefoot in defiance of three separate adults, and carrying a basket of bread loaves nearly as large as she was. She was the daughter of a dyer from the hollow and a chart-maker from the Waystation of Small Mercies, born two years after the reforging, and she had never known a world with walls in it. She looked up at Emrys with the particular impatience of a child who has been promised something and intends to collect.

"Wren says a lot of things," Emrys said.

"She says you were here when the stars went out." Pell shifted the basket to her hip. "She says you saw them go."

"I did."

"And then come back."

"Eventually."

Pell squinted eastward, where the last of the mist was unraveling off the flats, toward the faint smudge that was the First Hearth's rebuilt ring — less a building now than a gathering place, open on all sides, because that had seemed the only honest way to build it. "What did it look like? When they went?"

Emrys considered lying. It would have been easy, and kind in the shallow way kindness is easy: *it was quick, little one, one breath and they were gone, no one was hurt.* But Wren had spent twenty years teaching at the guild school with half her memory held together by other people's recitations, and she had taught Emrys one lesson above all the rest, the one she'd screamed at the council through the gate with her mind coming apart: *the truth is the only door that opens from both sides.*

So Emrys crouched down, setting their knees in the dust of the kind road, until they were level with her.

"It looked like the end of everything," they said. "It was the middle of the night, and I was alone in the guild hall, and every candle I had lit guttered at once — not blown out, just *stopped*, like the dark had decided it was done asking permission. And then I looked up through the skylight, and the sky was—" They paused, reaching for it, the way you reach for a word in a language you dreamed in once. "You know how a page looks before anyone has written on it? Blank, but *expecting*? That's what the sky was. All that black, and nothing written on it. And I remember thinking: this is my fault. I'm the last apprentice. I let the ink run dry."

Pell frowned. "Was it your fault?"

"No." Emrys said it plainly, without the old flinch. It had taken years to say it that way. "That's the first thing you should know about that night, if you're going to ask about it. Everyone thought someone was to blame — the council blamed the revenants, the revenants blamed the council, the council blamed me, and I blamed myself hardest of all. And we were all wrong. The covenant wasn't broken by a crime. It was broken by a misunderstanding so old that everyone who could have corrected it was either dead or hollowed. That's usually how the worst damage gets done. Not by villains. By people reading the wrong book very sincerely."

"What was the right book?"

Emrys smiled. Somewhere behind them, down the road toward the hollow, a bell rang — the guild's morning bell, the one Sable had salvaged from a smuggler's wreck and rehung herself, refusing to let anyone else touch the ropes. "Come walk with me and I'll tell you. Bread waits for no one, and neither does Wren."

* * *

They walked the road together, Pell matching Emrys's long stride with the fierce economy of short legs, the bread warming the basket between them. Around them the morning assembled itself: a family of steppe-dwellers heading into the hollow market with baskets of salt-flowers; two young wardwrights — actual wardwrights, plural, the word no longer a wound — arguing cheerfully about whether the new waystation at the flats' edge should have a roof at all, or whether hospitality ought to be able to see the sky. A caravan of guild students on their spring crossing, led by Idris, who raised a hand to Emrys as he passed. He did not stop. He never stopped anymore; there was too much ground to cover and too many years to make up, and he wore his restored name like a man wears a coat he is determined never to take off again.

"He looks better," Pell said, watching him go.

"He remembers himself," Emrys said. "It suits him."

"Did it hurt? When he forgot?"

"Yes." Emrys did not soften it. "And it hurt worse for the ones who loved him while he couldn't love them back. Your teacher Wren held his hand through the worst of it, and she was going hollow herself at the time, and she did it anyway. That's the second thing you should know about that night. Love isn't a feeling you have. It's a thing you do with your hands when your mind is failing. Wren taught me that by almost dying of it."

Pell was quiet for a few steps, processing this with the gravity children reserve for information about death and dessert. Then: "Wren says the hollow used to be scared of the steppe."

"The hollow was terrified of the steppe."

"Why?"

"Because we'd made it a monster." Emrys gestured at the glittering flats, at the waystations dotting them now like lamps along a hallway, at the thin thread of the road stitching two worlds that had spent four hundred years pretending to be one. "See, the founders came here fleeing something terrible — that part of the story was true. And they built the wards to keep the terrible thing out. But fear doesn't know when to stop working. Fear keeps building after the danger's gone. Generation after generation, the wards got taller in every way except stone. We stopped trading. We stopped speaking to the steppe-dwellers — our own kin, Pell, exiled by our great-grandparents for the crime of being outside when the wall went up. We told ourselves stories about what lived out there, and the stories got hungrier every telling, because a story nobody checks will eat anything you feed it. And the whole time, the people we'd locked out were starving on the other side of our fear. And the people we'd locked in — us — were starving too. Just slower. Starving for sky."

Pell looked up. The morning sky was a pale, washed blue, and the stars were long gone with the dawn, but she looked anyway, the way hollow children still did — a habit that had survived the walls, a reflex of gratitude. "And the stars going out was because of that?"

"Because the covenant was a promise between the hollow and the steppe, and you can't keep a promise to someone you won't speak to. The magic thinned. The founders — the real ones, underneath the hollowing — they'd given themselves to the covenant willingly, to keep both worlds fed. But we rewrote their gift into a ransom. We turned 'hearth and hand' into 'a life for the wall.' And a lie that old gets heavy. Eventually something has to give." Emrys shrugged. "Something gave."

"That's when the revenants came."

"That's when the revenants came. Our own founders, hollowed hollow, walking out of the steppe to collect a debt we'd miswritten. And I stood in that doorway—" Emrys's voice caught, briefly, on the old terror, and they let it, because Pell deserved to hear that the past had teeth even now, "—and I was twenty years old and the last of my guild, and I thought: this is what I was trained for, and my training is useless, because my master died before teaching me the ending of the ritual. And do you know what saved us? Not the ritual. Not the wards. A mad old mender, a smuggler with a guilty conscience, a revenant who refused to forget his name, and a sentence in a dead archivist's handwriting that said the covenant was never about sacrifice at all."

"What was it about?"

Emrys stopped walking. They had reached the midpoint of the old border — there was a marker there now, a low stone bench facing both directions, hollow hills on one side and glass flats on the other — and they sat, and Pell climbed up beside them, and set the basket between them like a treaty.

"It was about welcome," Emrys said. "That's all. That's everything. *Hearth and hand.* You light a fire, and you leave room beside it, and you keep the room even when you're afraid of who might sit down in it. Especially then. The founders understood that, at the very beginning, before the fear got organized into institutions. Every generation after them understood it a little less, until we understood it not at all, and the sky noticed." They looked at Pell. "When I relit the First Hearth, I didn't offer it a life. I offered it a seat. I spoke welcome in the hollow tongue and the steppe tongue, back to back, and the flame took it, because that was the payment all along. Not blood. Room."

Pell turned this over. She broke a heel off one of the loaves — Wren's dark rye, dense enough to stop a door — and chewed thoughtfully.

"So the hollow learned," she said at last. "To be more."

Emrys laughed — a real laugh, startled out of them. "Where did you hear that?"

"Wren. She says it at the end of every history lesson. She says, *the hollow was small, and it was home, and it learned to be more,* and then she hits the table and says *and if any of you build walls, I'll haunt you personally, I've already practiced being dead.*"

"That sounds like Wren."

"She's frightening."

"She's the bravest person I've ever known, and yes, she's frightening. Those are the same fact." Emrys tore their own piece of bread and looked out over the steppe, where the sun was fully up now and the flats blazed like a second dawn lying down. "But she's right. That's the honest answer to your question, Pell. What was the hollow like when the stars were gone? It was small. Small isn't the same as evil — remember that too. We were warm inside our smallness. We had good bread and good bells and people who loved each other fiercely within arm's reach and barely knew the names of anyone beyond it. Smallness can be home. It was mine for twenty years, and I loved it, and loving it is why the loss of it nearly killed me. But smallness that builds walls stops being home and starts being a cell with a hearth in it. And the day we opened the doors — not because the revenants forced us, though they did, and not because the archive shamed us, though it did, but because one by one, people *chose* it — that was the day the hollow became more. More than a valley. More than a wall. More than a story we told ourselves about safety."

Pell swung her feet, heels drumming softly against the bench. "And now?"

"Now?" Emrys spread their hands at the road, the travelers, the morning. "Now the road goes both ways. Now the guild teaches steppe children wardwrighting and hollow children star-reading, and half of them are hopeless at both and magnificent at arguing, which is how you know it's working. Now Sable's charts hang in every waystation from here to the Court of Ash and Salt — which is a library now, did you know? The thief's descendants turned it over. He lived long enough to see the flame returned and to hear Emrys Lockridge say *no* to him, and I think it was the hearing that undid him more than the saying. His granddaughter runs the place. She sends us books. She's coming to the solstice feast this year."

"The whole town will be there."

"The whole *steppe* will be there. We'll need three fires and Wren has already claimed the biggest one, and Idris is bringing students from the far stations, and Sable swears she can fit forty people in the guild hall if we remove the furniture, which Wren refuses to do because the furniture is load-bearing, emotionally." Emrys smiled and looked down at the girl beside them, at the crumbs on her chin and the unafraid set of her shoulders. "And you'll be there. And you'll grow up and teach someone else, and none of it will feel like a miracle to you, because you'll never have known the other thing. And that — that is the whole point. That's what winning looks like, Pell. It looks ordinary. It looks like a road with bread on it."

Pell considered this, then leaned her head briefly against Emrys's arm, the way children do when they've received more wisdom than they can carry and need to put some of it down.

Then she straightened, suddenly urgent. "Wren also said — she said if I found you on the road, I should tell you the guild bell rang twice this morning. Twice means someone's come from far away and wants the last apprentice specifically." She grinned around another mouthful of bread. "Except you're not the last anymore. So who does the bell mean?"

Emrys stood. Far down the road, the hollow lay open in its valley — no walls, no wards, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, the guild hall's doors propped wide with wedges of old wardstone, exactly as they had been every single day for twenty-three years. Someone was coming. Someone always was; that was the covenant now, kept not in stone but in habit, in open doors and extra chairs and the stubborn daily practice of leaving room.

"Well," Emrys said, brushing the dust from their knees and taking the basket from the girl's hip, "there's only one way to find out. Come on. Walk with me. And tell me everything Wren's been teaching you — I want to hear if she's gotten the table-slamming right."

Side by side, the last apprentice and the first of the unwalled generation, they walked down the kind road toward home — which was no longer a place the world ended, but a place it began.
