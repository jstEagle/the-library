## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The luck-knot took Jonah eleven minutes to tie, and she spent nine of them pretending it was nothing.

That was the trick of it, the whole architecture of her careful life: the knot had to look like a knot. Cord dyed river-grey, bought in honest lengths from the ropewalk on Tanner's Lane, worked into a pattern any fisherman's daughter might learn from her mother. If a neighbor leaned over the sill of her shop window — and they leaned, they always leaned — they would see a woman doing fiddly, pious handwork, the kind superstitious sailors paid a copper for and priests pretended not to mind.

What they would not see was the small breath she fed into the center of it, the warmth she coaxed down through her fingers like water into dry soil, the way the cord *listened*. Magic was outlawed in Vell. Had been for twelve years, since the coronation, since the king's voice rang from every bell tower in the city announcing that the gifted were an infection and the crown would cut them out. Jonah had been fourteen. She had learned to fold her gift small and keep it folded, the way one folds a letter one cannot bear to read.

She blew on the knot once, softly, as if cooling soup, and set it on the counter beside the others.

The shop was called Blackwood's Sundries, and it was exactly what it appeared to be: candles, needles, soap, bootlaces, dried lavender in twist of paper, a shelf of cheap charms that did nothing at all except comfort the people who bought them. The real charms — the ones that worked — she sold only to a handful of customers she had known for years, people who came at dusk, who never spoke of it aloud, who paid in silence and left by the back stair. Twelve years, and not one of them had been taken. Not one.

She meant to keep it that way.

The bell above the door rattled at half past nine, and Brannick ducked under the lintel with the easy stoop of a man who had been doing it for thirty years. He was a dockworker, broad as a barge, with forearms like mooring rope and a laugh that started in his chest and rolled out of him like cargo going over the side. Salt crusted the cuffs of his coat. He smelled of herring and tar and the particular cold of the harbor at dawn.

"Morning, mistress Blackwood." He set a copper on the counter, ceremonially, the way he did every second Thursday. "The grey one. If it's ready."

"It's ready." She slid the knot across the wood. "Third one this season. You'll wear the luck right off your wrists, Brannick."

"Luck's like rope." He wound the cord around his left wrist, tucking the knot against the pulse point, where the swell came up strongest. "Wears through, you splice in new. My mother's trick, and her mother before her." He grinned, gap-toothed, and lowered his voice to the register he used for the real business, the business that did not exist. "Storm coming in off the deep water, they say. Three days out, maybe four. Old Toma read the gulls."

"Then I'm glad you have it." Jonah kept her voice light, her hands busy sorting needles she did not need to sort. "Though you know I'll deny it in court. You bought a decorative knot. Very handsome. No practical application whatsoever."

"Wouldn't dream of implicating you." He touched two fingers to his brow, mock-solemn, and his grin faded into something gentler. "You're a good woman, Jonah Blackwood. Twelve years you've been splicing my luck, and never once asked a thing."

She had asked one thing. *Never tell anyone. Never, not your wife, not your drinking mates, not under torture, not in your sleep.* He had promised, and he had kept it, and that promise was the only reason a dockworker's luck-knot was the most dangerous transaction in the city of Vell.

"Go home to your wife," she said. "And tell her the lavender's half price this week."

He laughed and went, the bell rattling behind him, and Jonah stood a moment in the quiet with her hands flat on the counter, feeling the faint residual warmth of the working fade from her fingertips. It always felt like that afterward — like blowing out a candle in a dark room and having to trust the dark.

* * *

The trouble with Vell's lower quarters was that the buildings leaned close enough to whisper to one another, and so did the people.

Mistress Halloway kept the corner room across the lane, and she kept her window open the way other people kept knives — always within reach, always pointed at someone. She was seventy if she was a day, thin as a candle-end, with eyes that had gone pale and sharp as a cat's, and she had watched Jonah Blackwood for twelve years the way a heron watches shallow water.

Jonah felt the look before she saw it, that prickling between the shoulder blades. She was sweeping her step that afternoon — sweeping being the great camouflage of the watchful, a broom making any loitering look like industry — when she caught the pale gaze across the lane and lifted her chin in polite greeting.

"Fine weather for it," Mistress Halloway said, which was what she always said, which meant nothing, which meant everything.

"Always is, this time of year."

"Busy day? Heard your bell go early. Dock fellow, was it?" The old woman's voice was pleasant, conversational, a fisherman's line drifting on still water. "Brannick. Nice man. Superstitious as a cat, though. All them knots and tokens. You'd think a grown man would have more sense."

"He buys candles too," Jonah said. "And soap. He's a good customer."

"Mm." The pale eyes did not blink often enough. "My sister's girl married into a family up on Chandler's Hill. They say the Inquisitors have new orders. New *methods*." She said the word delicately, tasting it. "They can tell, you know. The gifted. There's a test now. Something they carry. Touches you and it knows."

Jonah's broom did not stop. Twelve years of practice lived in that broom. "Then it's a good thing there's no one in this lane to test," she said, and smiled, and the smile cost her more than the knot had.

Mistress Halloway smiled back, and drew in her head like a tortoise, and Jonah finished sweeping a step that had not needed sweeping, her heart going hard and quick against her ribs. *They can tell. There's a test now.* Rumor, probably. Rumor was the weather of Vell — it changed by the hour and meant nothing until it meant everything.

But she went inside and did not work any charms that night, and lay awake listening to the bells, counting them, as she had done every night for twelve years. Nine bells. Then ten. Then the watch.

Then, at an hour when no honest bell rang, a bell she did not know.

* * *

The criers came on a grey morning with the smell of rain that never arrived.

Jonah heard them from her doorway, as the whole street did — the rattle of a drum, and the crier's voice, trained and flat, the voice of a man reading words he had read so many times they had worn smooth as river stones.

"By order of His Majesty the King: let it be known that the crime of sorcery is high treason against crown and country. Let it be known that any person found practicing, harboring, or concealing the practice of magic shall be taken, tried, and burned. Let it be known that a bounty of fifty crowns is offered for information leading to the taking of any practitioner of the forbidden arts. Fifty crowns. By order of His Majesty the King."

Fifty crowns. Jonah did the arithmetic without wanting to. Fifty crowns was two years of a dockworker's wages. Fifty crowns was a new roof, a daughter's dowry, a debt forgiven. Fifty crowns was the price of every neighbor she had ever smiled at.

The street was quiet in the way streets go quiet — not silent, but listening. Doors stood ajar. Faces at windows. She watched Mistress Halloway's pale eyes move along the lane, door to door, face to face, and understood with a cold, settling clarity that the old woman was counting. Not doors. *Possibilities.*

Jonah went inside and closed her shutters.

* * *

Old Toma came at dusk.

He was her oldest customer — older than the ban itself, a river-pilot gone grey and stooped, who had been buying small workings from her since before she understood what her hands could do. He had been the first to say *you have the gift, girl, and it isn't a sin* — Maren's words, really, Maren's teaching passed along secondhand, back when Maren was alive and the world was a different shape. Toma bought charms for his boat, for his bad knee, for his dreams. He paid in small fish and gossip and, once a year, a bottle of plum brandy that could have stripped paint.

He came in through the front door, which he never did, and that was the first wrong thing. The second was his face — grey as ash, and looking at her the way men look at a fire they cannot put out.

"Jonah." His voice was low and cracked. "They came to the harbor at noon. Not the watch. The grey coats. Inquisitors. They had a thing on a chain — an iron compass, needle spinning — and they walked it up and down the quay like they were dowsing for water." He swallowed. "They took Pell. Took him right off his boat in front of forty men, and not one of them said a word, and not one of them stopped it."

Pell. A crab-fisher, soft-spoken, who bought candle-blessings from her every spring for the safety of his boat. Jonah's hands went still on the counter.

"Fifty crowns," Toma said bitterly. "That's what they're paying. Fifty crowns, and half the harbor would sell its own mother." He leaned close, and she could smell the brandy on him, fear dressed up as courage. "You've got to go, girl. You hear me? Tonight. There's boats — I can get you out on the tide —"

"Toma —"

"Don't *Toma* me. I've watched you fold yourself up small for twelve years and I've said nothing because you asked, but they're not playing at this anymore. The compass-thing, Jonah. It *found* Pell, and Pell was careful. Careful as you."

She wanted to tell him he was frightening himself. She wanted to say the city was big, that careful people survived, that twelve years of silence was a fortress. But she looked at his face — at the terror in it, terror for her, an old man shaking — and the fortress felt very thin.

"I'll think on it," she said, which was a lie, because she had already begun thinking of the back stair and the sewer grate and the road east, and hated herself for thinking it.

"Think fast." He pressed something into her hand — a coin, wrapped in paper, more than he owed her. "For the road. Don't argue. An old man's allowed one good deed."

He went out into the dusk, and Jonah stood holding the coin, and the last thing she ever saw of old Toma was his back, stooped under the lintel, disappearing into the lane where the shadows were already gathering wrong.

* * *

They took him two streets from her door.

She heard it before she saw it — the drum had stopped, but the shouting had not, and then a sound she had never heard before and would never forget afterward: the wet, heavy crack of a man being struck down in the street. Jonah was out her door and around the corner before any sense in her could stop her legs, and the lane was full of torchlight and grey coats, and old Toma was on the ground.

He was not fighting. He was old, and there were six of them, and he had put his hands up, palms out, the universal language of *I am no threat*, and one of the grey coats had struck him anyway, and now he was kneeling in the gutter with blood in his mouth, and a witch-hunter was holding the iron compass on its chain above him, and the needle was spinning, spinning, spinning, and pointing — Jonah felt it like a hook behind her sternum — *at her*.

No. Not quite at her. At the lane. At the whole street, the whole quarter, the whole city of Vell, the needle drunk on the fear of ten thousand people, and somewhere in all that noise, her small folded gift, hidden twelve years, was one more candle-flame in a burning house.

"Old man," the hunter said. He had a bored voice. That was the worst of it, the boredom. "You're Toma Vess, river-pilot. We have testimony you purchased unlawful workings. Name your supplier."

Toma spat blood into the gutter, and looked up, and his eyes — clouded, frightened, brave — swept across the faces in the lane and passed over Jonah without a flicker.

"I buy my charms at the shrine of the Tide-Mother," he said. "Like every honest man on the river."

The hunter struck him again.

Jonah stood in the dark of a doorway with her hand pressed over her mouth, and she could have warned him. That was the thought that would follow her out of the city, across the walls, into whatever waited beyond — she could have warned him. She could have sent a message by the back stair, could have told him *don't come, don't come near me, they have a compass and it listens.* She had had a whole afternoon. She had had a coin in her hand and a road east already forming in her mind, and she had chosen the road, and old Toma had walked two streets to warn *her* and walked into the grey coats instead.

They dragged him up. His boots left two dark lines in the gutter muck. As they hauled him past her doorway, past the shadow where she stood, his head turned — slowly, an old man's head on an old man's neck — and for one moment his clouded eyes found hers in the dark.

He did not look at her with accusation. That was the unbearable part. He looked at her the way he had looked at her when she was fourteen and frightened and holding her first finished charm: *it isn't a sin, girl. Go.*

Then the torchlight swallowed him, and the lane was empty, and the drum started up again somewhere across the city, and Jonah Blackwood stood alone in the dark with a coin for the road in her fist and the taste of her own cowardice like ash on her tongue.

She went inside. She did not light a lamp. In the dark, she began taking down the shelf of harmless charms — the ones that did nothing, the ones that had kept her safe — and she set them one by one into a satchel, and her hands did not shake until the last one, and then they did not stop.

Because that was when she saw it, pinned to the inside of her shutters where no one could have reached without passing through a locked door: a slip of paper, folded once, written in a hand she knew — a hand she had last seen cold, twelve years ago, on a dead woman's face.

*Maren's* hand.

*They are coming for you, little spark. Run east, and do not look back.*
