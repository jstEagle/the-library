## 2. The Face That Isn't Hers

The note did not burn. Jonah tried three times before she understood that it wasn't going to.

She stood over her little iron brazier with the paper held in a pair of tongs, watching flame lick across ink that refused to fade, and the fire passed over Maren's handwriting the way water passes over glass — touching everything, changing nothing. *They are coming for you, little spark. Run east, and do not look back.* The words sat in the heat, patient as a promise.

Twelve years she had kept Maren's letters hidden inside the false bottom of her flour tin, wrapped in oilcloth, and every one of them burned like ordinary paper when the fear grew too loud and she needed to watch something of hers turn to ash and remain ash. But this — this had been written by whatever wore Maren's hand now, or it had been written by Maren before she died and carried since then through places Jonah did not want to imagine. Either way, it would not burn, and that told her more than the words themselves.

It told her the thing that wrote it was not done with her teacher. It told her the thing was not done with *her*.

Jonah dropped the note into the cold grate instead and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the dark behind them went red. East. The Steppe lay east — the Glass Steppe, past the tanneries and the wall-gate that hadn't opened for pilgrims in twelve years, the plain where the ground mirrored the sky so perfectly that travelers wrote home claiming they'd walked on clouds until their letters simply stopped coming. Nobody ran east. People who ran east were never heard from again, which was precisely why it would be the one direction the hunters would not think to watch.

Maren had taught her that too: *when they expect you to hide, hide in the open; when they expect you to run, run toward the thing they fear.* Her teacher's voice came back so easily sometimes that Jonah could pretend, for whole minutes at a time, that the woman was still alive somewhere, still smelling of chalk and rosemary, still rapping her knuckles against a slate to make a point. Twelve years of pretending. And now someone had gone and put Maren's actual face back into the world like a coin into a beggar's cup, and every pretense Jonah had built came down around her ears.

She packed the way she'd practiced in her head a hundred times without ever believing she'd need it. The satchel of harmless charms stayed. The real work — three luck-knots with genuine thread-of-intent woven in, a fever-bane salve, a small jar of borrowed courage that hummed faintly when she held it near her ear like a seashell — those went into a second bag, wrapped in a butcher's apron so they'd read as meat to any searcher who didn't look closely. Her mother's ring. The flour tin with its false bottom, emptied of everything except the letters, because she could not leave Maren's words behind even knowing what hand had written this newest one. A flint. A knife. Bread, cheese, the good apples from Mrs. Harrow's stall that she'd meant to eat over three days and ate in ten minutes standing at the window instead, because grief and terror both lived in the stomach first.

Then she sat down at her worktable and looked at her shop — the shelves, the drying racks, the bundles of lavender and rowan hanging from the beams, the little brass bell above the door that had announced eleven years of customers who believed they were buying nothing — and she made herself say goodbye to each corner of it. Because she already knew what she was going to do. She had known since she saw the note, and she had spent the whole night refusing to know it.

A shop full of charms could be searched. Could be catalogued. Could be *evidence*. And if the Inquisition found one true charm in this room, they would not need proof beyond it; they would pull the street apart looking for everyone she'd ever sold so much as a knot to. Old Tam-with-two-hands at the docks. The widow Pell and her asthmatic son. Every fearful soul who'd ever slid a coin across this counter hoping for a little luck they couldn't name.

Jonah struck the flint anyway, and then she made herself wait, hands flat on the table, until she heard the knock.

Three knocks. Polite. Almost gentle. The kind of knock that has never once in its life needed to force a door.

Her blood went to ice water. No hunter knocked like that. Hunters kicked. But she knew — she knew the way she knew weather coming in her bad tooth — that behind her door stood the reason the note said *run*, and it had come anyway, early, hungry, and polite.

She lit the oil.

It caught along the shelf-ends where she'd soaked the wood herself, hours ago, in another life, while telling herself she wouldn't need it. Flame went up the drying racks like ivy growing fast, took the lavender first so that the room filled suddenly with a sweetness so wrong and so familiar that her knees nearly folded — her shop, burning, smelled exactly like her shop, and there was no preparing for that. The rowan bundles crackled. The brass bell above the door swung once in the heat-draft and rang, one bright absurd note, like the shop itself calling out.

Jonah went out the back, down the stairs she'd oiled that afternoon, and into the alley reeking of fish heads and old rain, and she did not look back at the light spilling orange across the cobbles behind her. *Do not look back.* Fine. That much, at least, she could obey.

* * *

The sewers under Vell were older than the city above them, older than the king, older maybe than the crown, and the people who lived down there — the pipe-wrights, the mudlarks, the ones the city called the Undermarket — charged tolls in news rather than coin, which was why Jonah had spent eleven years being generous to them. Kindness, she'd learned from Maren, was the only magic nobody outlawed.

She gave the toll-taker at the Grate — a woman with river-cold eyes named Sefa who owed her three favors and knew it — the whole truth in nine sentences, and Sefa listened with her head cocked like a bird's.

"Hunters came through an hour ago," Sefa said. "Four of them, gray cloaks, asking after a charm-seller with black hair. Asking *politely*." Her mouth twisted around the word. "Polite is worse. You know that."

"I know that."

"They're walking the tunnels in pairs now. Looking for anyone moving east-ways." Sefa studied her. "You're moving east-ways."

"I'm moving everywhere-ways," Jonah said. "That's the trick of it."

Sefa almost smiled. Then she reached up and pulled a rope, and somewhere in the dark a sluice groaned open, and dirty water began rushing through the main channel with a sound like a crowd clearing its throat. "When the flood passes, walk in the channel. The water covers scent, covers tracks, covers the listening-charms they drop at junctions — the little glass beads? Water kills them. Walk the channel to the Third Fork, take the left arch, and may whatever's left of your god go with you, Jonah Blackwood."

"And yours."

"Mine left years ago." But Sefa touched two fingers to Jonah's wrist, quick and hard, the Undermarket's version of a blessing, and pushed her toward the channel's edge.

The flood came and went. Jonah waded into the aftermath — knee-deep, cold enough to ache in the bone, moving slow so as not to splash — and the darkness down there was total except for the pale glow of the glow-moss the pipe-wrights cultivated, green-gray light that made everything look drowned and dreaming. She counted junctions. At the Fourth Fork she heard them.

Voices. Gray-cloak voices, echoing off wet stone, distorted but unmistakable: *"—the shop's alight, sir, but no body—"*

And then another voice, and the world stopped.

*"No body,"* the voice agreed, warm and dry and amused, a voice that had read aloud to a frightened girl of thirteen on the nights the witch-fear got bad, a voice that had said *you have talent, little spark, real talent, don't you dare waste it hiding it* — Maren's voice, Maren's exact voice, coming out of the dark of Vell's sewers attached to someone hunting her.

Jonah pressed herself flat against the channel wall, heart slamming, and listened to her dead mentor's murderer discuss her corpse-that-didn't-exist in her mentor's own cadence. There was a pause, long enough that she thought they'd moved on. Then:

"Blackwood." The voice curled around her name like smoke finding a draft. Not shouted. Just set down in the tunnel, gently, the way you'd set down bait. "I can hear your heart, little spark. It always beat too fast for a liar. Come out, and we'll talk about your teacher. I remember things about her you've never heard. I remember *everything* about her now."

Every muscle in Jonah's body screamed to answer. To call out *what did you do to her, who are you, give her back* — to spend her voice on rage the way she'd wanted to for twelve years, ever since the night Maren was taken and the kingdom was told the gifted had done it. Her throat actually opened. Sound gathered behind her teeth.

And then she thought: *it wants me to speak. Everything down here listens, and it knows I know that.*

Maren had taught her that too. Silence is also a spell.

Jonah bit the sound back until it turned to copper in her mouth, and she moved — slow, slow, foot by foot through the cold water, away from the voice, while it talked on behind her in her teacher's warm tones, telling stories about Maren to the dark, stories that were true, that only Maren could have known, laid out like a trail of bread crumbs leading nowhere she could afford to follow.

At the Third Fork she took the left arch. Behind her, faintly, the voice stopped mid-sentence, as if it had finally noticed the room was empty.

She climbed. Rust rungs, a grate heavy with twelve years of paint, and then night air — real air, rain-washed, tasting of coal smoke and freedom — as she pulled herself up into the ruin of an old watchtower on the city wall's landward side. Below her, Vell glowed with its ten thousand lawful lamps. Somewhere in it, her shop was ash, and something wearing Maren's face was reading the ashes like a letter.

East, the Steppe waited under the moon, pale and mirror-flat, holding the sky the way a bowl holds water.

Jonah tightened the straps of her satchel, wiped her face with a sleeve that came away wet whether from the sewers or from her or from both, and looked back once — one last time, breaking the rule, letting herself break just this one rule — at the city that had been her whole careful life.

"You took her face," she said quietly, to the towers, to the palace, to the thing behind all of it. "You took her face and her voice and twelve years of my life. So keep them. Keep them close."

She turned east.

"Because I'm coming to take everything else back."
