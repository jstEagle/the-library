## 3. Out Onto the Glass

The gates of Vell closed behind Jonah with a sound like a held breath finally released — and then, immediately, taken back in.

She did not look at the walls. Looking would mean seeing the watchtowers, the cressets already lit against dusk, the shapes of hunters moving along the parapet like insects on a corpse. Looking would mean thinking of Maren's face on another woman's skull, and she could not afford that yet. Grief was a luxury for people who had stopped running. So she walked, head down, hood up, one hand curled around the strap of her satchel where three luck-knots, two fever-charms, and a single dried sprig of rowan were all that remained of twelve years of careful, quiet work.

The road east out of Vell was rutted clay and old cart-tracks, lined with pollarded willows that looked, in the failing light, like women bent under washing. She followed it until it thinned, then frayed, then gave itself up entirely to grassland — because no one went east. Everyone knew that. Farmers pointed their children away from the eastern horizon the way they pointed them away from deep water. *That's the Steppe,* they said, in the tone reserved for plagues and tax collectors. *Ground remembers too much out there.*

Jonah had spent her whole life being told what not to do, and she had obeyed so long and so well that disobedience now felt less like rebellion than like taking off a boot after a long day.

Still, when the last hedge fell behind her and the plain opened out like a held palm, she stopped.

* * *

It was the ground that undid her.

Not the vastness — though the vastness was real, a flatness running to every horizon without a single tree or stone to break it — but the surface of it. The earth out here was not earth as she understood it. It was smooth, faintly translucent, like river ice over dark water, and it reflected. Not dully, the way packed mud reflects a gray sky, but perfectly, mercilessly, the way a mirror does. The clouds above her moved below her feet. A late hawk wheeled overhead and again beneath her heels, twin birds hunting a doubled sky. When she lifted her hand, there were four hands: hers, pale in the gloaming, and theirs, ghost-bright, rising from the glass to meet her.

"Oh," she said aloud, and her voice came back strange, flattened, as if the plain were tasting the shape of her words before returning them.

She crouched and pressed her palm flat to the surface. It was cool but not cold, and it gave — barely, barely — like the skin of milk just set. Beneath the reflection, deep down where the sky's image faded into darkness, something *moved*. Slow shapes. Slow lights. Like fish seen from a bridge at night, except these lights pulsed in colors she had no names for: the green of a smell remembered, the gold of a voice half-heard through a wall.

Magic. Old, ambient, unowned magic, pooled in the land like rainwater in a cellar. Her fingers prickled inside her gloves, and for the first time in twelve years Jonah Blackwood felt her gift rise toward something instead of shrinking from it. In Vell, her talent had lived folded small as a moth in winter. Here it unfurled — tentative, disbelieving, wings testing the air.

She snatched her hand back. Careful. Careful was how you stayed alive. Careful was how Maren had taught her — *small charms, small life, never let them see the shape of what you are* — and Maren was dead, or worse, and careful hadn't saved either of them.

But the plain hummed under her boots like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing, and Jonah found, to her considerable unease, that she wasn't afraid of it. That frightened her more than fear would have.

* * *

She walked through the night because stopping felt like sinking. The doubled world made distance impossible to judge; an hour's walking brought her no nearer to anything, since there was nothing to be near. Twice she thought she saw figures far off — upright smudges standing motionless on the glass — and twice, when she blinked, they were gone, leaving only ripples in the reflection, rings spreading outward like the memory of someone having stood there.

Her grandmother's voice, dead thirty years and buried under other memories, surfaced unbidden: *Don't drink the standing water, girl. You don't know who left it there.*

There was no water. But there were pools.

She found the first of them an hour past midnight, or what she judged to be midnight by the ache behind her eyes. It lay in a shallow depression of the glass, a gathering of liquid light no wider than a cartwheel, silver-edged and utterly still. It reflected nothing — that was the first wrongness. The sky passed over it and the pool ignored the sky. Instead its depths churned gently with images: a kitchen, a loaf, a woman's flour-dusted forearms, a child laughing somewhere off-frame. A memory, rendered visible, lying out in the open like a dropped coin.

Jonah circled it at a respectful distance, the way you'd circle a sleeping dog. *Lost memories*, Ovid's kind would have called it — she'd read the old accounts in Maren's hidden books, smuggled pages describing the Steppe in the dry voice scholars used for things they'd only ever seen in nightmares. *The Steppe collects what the living mislay.* Griefs. Names. Whole childhoods, abandoned by people who could not afford to carry them.

She should have walked around it. She meant to walk around it.

But the edge of the depression crumbled — the glass there was thin as sugar-work — and her boot went through, and she pitched forward, and her mouth, opening on a curse, met the pool.

* * *

The taste was cold and clean, like snowmelt, like the air after lightning. Then the world turned inside out.

— candlelight everywhere, guttering, gilded, caught in a hundred crystals strung from a beam. Music — fiddles, badly tuned but played with abandon. Her hands — no, *someone's* hands, broad and scarred and holding a cup of something hot and honeyed. A woman turning toward her through the crowd, laughing, wearing flowers in her hair and a borrowed dress too fine for her, saying words Jonah's ears received but which belonged to no language she knew, and understanding them anyway: *I never thought I'd get this. I never thought anyone like me got this.*

And the body she wore — the groom, some dockhand or drover by the calluses — was so full of joy it hurt, joy like a fever, joy with terror under it, *don't wake, don't let this be the dream, remember this, whatever happens remember this* —

And then the memory *tore*, the way cloth tears along a weakened seam, and Jonah was falling out of it, out of the wedding, out of the stranger, onto the cold glass of the Steppe with her cheek against the frozen-smooth ground and her heart slamming like a fist on a door.

She lay there gasping. The pool beside her was smaller now, its edges drawn back, dimmer — she had drunk part of it, she realized. Part of him. Somewhere in the world, if he still lived, some man was missing an hour of his wedding night, and she was carrying it in her mouth like stolen honey.

"I'm sorry," she whispered, absurdly, to no one. "I'm sorry."

But God help her — and she was not at all sure God helped anyone anymore — it had been *beautiful*. Twelve years she had peddled luck to the frightened, knotted thread and whispered blessings into buttons, and never once, not once, had she felt what that stranger had felt. Magic in Vell was a crime, and so her gift had become a crime's tool: small, sly, ashamed. But this — this raw, pooled, unhidden wonder — this was what her gift had been *for*, once, before a crown outlawed the shape of a human soul.

Jonah sat up slowly. Above her the stars came down to the horizon without interruption, and below her the same stars rose up to meet them, and she sat at the seam of two skies like a needle through cloth.

This place was terrible. This place ate names; she'd find proof of that soon enough. The hunters would come — Serane would come, wearing Maren's smile like a mask nailed over a lamp — and out here there was nowhere to hide, no alley, no cellar, no crowd. Every instinct she had honed in Vell screamed at her to turn around, creep home, fold herself small again, survive.

But survival, she was beginning to understand, was not the same as living. She had survived twelve years. She had done it by amputating everything that mattered, and she had watched Maren do the same, and in the end the crown had come for them anyway, patient as rot. Smallness had not saved them. It had only made them easier to swallow.

Out here, her gift breathed. Out here it stood up straight and filled its lungs, and Jonah could feel it moving in her chest like a second heartbeat, unfamiliar and enormous and *hers*.

"Terrible," she said to the doubled stars, and her reflection said it back from below, and for the first time the echo sounded less like a warning than like an agreement between friends. "Absolutely terrible. All right, then."

She got up, shouldered her satchel, and walked east, into the country where forgotten things went to wait.

She had been walking perhaps half an hour when she saw the boy.

He burst out of the dark ahead of her — a small ragged shape, sprinting flat-out across the glass with his arms pumping, bare feet slapping the mirror-surface without breaking it. He ran as though something behind him wore his name. And when he saw her, when his wild white-rimmed eyes fixed on the first human figure he'd likely seen in longer than he could remember — which might have been days, which might have been years — he skidded, stumbled, and screamed one word at her in a voice scraped raw:

"*Run!*"

Then his eyes rolled white, and he dropped like a puppet with cut strings, and the glass beneath him began, very softly, to glow.
