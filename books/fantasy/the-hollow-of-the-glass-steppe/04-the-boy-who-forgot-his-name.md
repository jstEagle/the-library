## 4. The Boy Who Forgot His Name

The boy came out of the mirage-light at dawn, running.

Jonah saw him first as a smudge against the glass horizon, a dark stitch in a seam of silver. The Steppe did strange things to distance — she had learned that much in three days of walking — and for a long while she could not tell whether the figure was near or impossibly far, whether it was a child or a crow, whether it was even moving at all. Then it stumbled, righted itself, and kept coming, and she understood with a lurch that it had been running toward her the whole time.

She stood very still. On the Steppe, stillness was survival; she'd learned that too, when she'd stepped around a pool of someone's drowned grief and felt it tug at her ankles like a current. The ground here was smooth as poured glass, faintly warm underfoot, and it gave back her reflection so faithfully that she sometimes caught herself flinching at her own approach.

The boy was perhaps eight, maybe ten — it was hard to say, because he was thin the way wire is thin, all tension and no softness. His clothes had once been good: a sailor's coat, brass buttons gone green, hemmed twice to fit a smaller body. His feet were bare and unmarked, which was wrong, which was impossible, unless the Steppe had decided not to cut him. He ran the way animals run when they have forgotten everything except running — head down, arms pumping, breath sawing — and he did not stop until he was three paces from her, where he skidded, dropped into a crouch, and stared at her with eyes like chips of sky fallen into mud.

"Hello," Jonah said carefully.

His mouth opened. Nothing came out. He tried again, and what emerged was not a word but a sound, a dry clicking, like a lock testing its own mechanism. He pressed his lips together, furious at them, and Jonah watched him reach for something behind his eyes and find the shelf empty.

"You don't remember how," she said softly. "To talk."

He flinched at her voice but didn't flee. That surprised her. Everything on the Steppe fled from her — the pale hares that weren't quite hares, the birds that flew in patterns too regular to be natural. But this boy looked at her hands, at the satchel slung across her body, at her face, with the desperate arithmetic of someone counting doors in a burning house.

Then his gaze snagged on the charm at her wrist — a small woven thing, thread and river-glass, the first knot she'd ever learned, the one Maren had put in her fingers when she was six years old and told her, *this is how you hold onto something without squeezing*.

The boy made a sound then. Not a word. A keening, low in his throat, and he reached out one grubby hand toward the charm and stopped himself an inch away, trembling, as though touching it might cost him whatever was left inside.

Jonah knelt. Slowly, the way you'd kneel before a wounded bird, she unwound the luck-knot from her wrist and held it out flat on her palm.

"It's yours," she said. "It doesn't do much. It just — remembers how to be lucky. Sometimes that's enough."

He took it. His fingers closed over it and he made the keening sound again, softer now, and pressed the knot against his sternum as if he could push it through his ribs into the hollow place. And Jonah, watching him, felt something crack open in her chest — an old door she'd bricked over years ago, back when hiding had become the whole shape of her life.

*When did I last matter to anyone?* she thought. *When did anyone last look at me like I was water?*

* * *

She named him Tam because he wouldn't tell her his name — because he couldn't, because there was nothing there to tell with. She tried asking him directly, walking beside him along the mirror-flat miles, pointing at herself and saying "Jonah," then pointing at him and waiting. He would frown, strain, go red with effort, and produce nothing but that terrible clicking, and finally he would turn his face away with a shame so complete it made her throat ache.

So she said, "Tam, then. Just for now. We'll find your real one later." And he looked at her with such sudden, blazing hope that she had to walk a few steps ahead so he wouldn't see her cry.

He remembered nothing. Not his name, not his mother's face, not the city or the sea or bread or rain. But his body remembered things his mind had lost: he could start a fire from sparks and dry grass, he could read weather in the way light bent off the glass, and he could run — gods, he could run, for hours, in great looping arcs across the plain, as if somewhere in his erased past there had been a reason to run and his legs had refused to forget it.

And he was drawn to her magic the way thirst is drawn to water.

She noticed it the second evening. She'd built their little camp in the lee of a waystone — one of the ancient markers that studded the Steppe every few miles, carved with script nobody living could read, warm to the touch like sleeping cattle. She'd warded the site with a simple circle, chalkless, drawn in the air with two fingers: a boundary-ward, the kind any hedge-witch could cast asleep. Nothing grand. A thread of intention, a whispered word, a shimmer only visible if you already knew to look.

Tam watched her do it with his whole body. His eyes went wide and wet. When she finished, he reached out and touched the place where the ward hung in the air, and it rippled around his finger like the skin of a soap bubble, and he laughed.

It was the first sound he'd made that wasn't grief. It was rusty and cracked and half-swallowed, but it was a laugh, and it undid her completely.

"Oh," Jonah said, and her voice came out strange. "Oh, you like that."

* * *

She began teaching him that night, and discovered that teaching was a hunger she hadn't known she had.

Not the old charms at first — those needed words, and words were still locked rooms in him. She started with the wordless ones, the craft below language: how to feel the grain of a thing, how to hold intention steady instead of grasping, how to let power move through you the way heat moves through iron rather than snatching at it like a child grabbing a kettle. She cupped his small hands around a pebble and showed him how to warm it. She taught him to breathe slow, four counts in, four counts out, until the warmth came.

It took him most of the night. Most children would have quit. Tam did not quit. He sat cross-legged by the fire with his jaw set and his eyes fierce, failing and failing and failing, and then — well past midnight, when Jonah had almost suggested sleep — the pebble glowed a dull cherry-red in his palms, and he looked up at her with an expression she recognized because she had worn it once, thirty years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary, in front of a woman who had told her she was talented.

Joy. Pure, ferocious, disbelieving joy.

"There," Jonah whispered. "There it is. You see? It's still in you. Whatever they took, whatever this place ate — it couldn't take that."

He slept that night holding the warm pebble against his cheek like a talisman, and Jonah sat up watching him breathe and understood that she was in trouble. Because caring was a door, and she had spent twelve years nailing hers shut, and now here was this hollowed-out child breathing softly by her fire, and the nails were pulling loose one by one, and she could feel the draft coming through the cracks — the cold, sweet, terrifying draft of being needed.

She hadn't been needed in twelve years. She'd been useful — dockworkers wanted their luck-knots, frightened neighbors wanted their fever-charms — but useful was a transaction, and transactions kept you safe. Needed was different. Needed meant that if she walked away, something would break. Needed meant her absence would have weight.

She looked at Tam's sleeping face, slack and young and unhaunted for the moment, and admitted it to the dark: she did not want to leave. Whatever waited at the heart of this terrible shining plain, whatever truth Ovid-less and alone she was walking toward, she was glad — guiltily, selfishly glad — that she wasn't walking toward it by herself anymore.

* * *

On the fourth morning, he spoke.

They were crossing a stretch where the pools ran close together, memory-water gleaming in shallow basins like coins scattered on a table, and Jonah was steering them carefully between them, warning Tam away from each one. He'd drunk from one on the second day — she'd turned her back for a minute and he'd crouched at a pool's edge and scooped a handful into his mouth before she could shout — and he'd spent an hour afterward weeping silently at a stranger's funeral, sobbing for a woman he'd never met, and she'd held him while he grieved someone else's dead, and neither of them had ever mentioned it since.

But this morning, as they picked their way through the glittering field, he stopped. He pointed at a pool no bigger than a dinner plate, set apart from the others, and made his clicking sound — urgent now, insistent — and tugged her sleeve.

"No," Jonah said gently. "We don't drink those. You know we don't."

He shook his head hard. He pointed at the pool again, then at himself, then — with agonizing slowness — he pressed both fists against his temples and pulled them outward, the universal gesture, the one every child knows: *something came out of me. Something was taken.*

Jonah went cold.

"That's yours?" she breathed. "That pool — it's your memory?"

He nodded, bouncing on his toes, grabbing her wrist, hauling her toward it with a strength that belied his starved frame. And she let herself be hauled, heart hammering, because if the Steppe pooled memories, and if some of those pools belonged to the people it had emptied — then maybe. Maybe the boy could be filled again. Maybe what was taken could be given back.

They knelt at the edge together. The water was clear as breath. And lying at the bottom of it, small and bright as a sunken coin, was a single word — visible somehow, legible somehow, glowing faintly up through the liquid glass:

*Tamuel.*

His name. His true name, sunk in the shallows like a stone in a stream.

Tam leaned forward, lips shaping the syllables, tasting them before he drank — and Jonah, watching his face, saw recognition flood it like dawn flooding a valley. He sat back. He looked at her. And in a voice rusty as an unused hinge, cracked and small and utterly his own, he said:

"I had a sister. She's still in Vell."

The wind moved across the Steppe, carrying the smell of rain that never fell, and far behind them — so far it was only a tremor in the glass underfoot — Jonah felt footsteps. Someone else was out on the plain. Someone who walked without drinking, without stumbling, without reflection.

Someone who was following them, and gaining.
