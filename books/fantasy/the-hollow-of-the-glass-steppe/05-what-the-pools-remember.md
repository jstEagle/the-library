## 5. What the Pools Remember

The Steppe did not want her here.

Inquisitor Serane had understood this within the first hour of passing the walls of Vell, when the ground beneath her boots began to mirror the sky so perfectly that walking felt like wading through the underside of heaven. Clouds drifted below her heels. A hawk's shadow crossed her path twice — once above, once beneath — and she could not say which bird was real. The plain rejected her the way a body rejects a splinter, with a low, grinding pressure behind her eyes, a whisper at the edge of hearing that might have been wind and might have been warning.

She walked anyway. She had orders.

*The witch Blackwood. Alive if possible. The child, if there is one. Bring them to the palace.*

The words sat in her mind like stones in a streambed, smooth and unquestionable. She could not remember receiving them. She could not remember the face of the officer who had spoken them, or the room, or the hour. She knew only that they were hers, the way one knows one's own hands — and when she reached for the memory of *when*, it slid away like water off glass.

That should have frightened her. She noted the fact the way a physician notes a symptom in another man: interesting, regrettable, not fatal. Then she walked on.

Behind her, Vell's smoke still smudged the horizon. Ahead, the Steppe unrolled in sheets of polished silence, mile upon mile where nothing grew and nothing decayed, where lost things came to rest like sediment in still water. The crown called this place cursed. The crown had posted no maps of it. The crown, Serane reflected, had never once in her twelve years of service explained anything at all — and she had never once thought to ask why.

Until now. Until this ground, which showed a woman her own reflection everywhere she looked and yet somehow never quite her face.

* * *

She found the first pool at dusk.

It lay in a shallow depression of the glassy earth, no wider than a washbasin, and it did not reflect the sky. It held something else: lamplight, warm and gold, the smell of bread, a woman's laughter caught mid-note. Serane crouched at its edge out of professional habit — witches used pools like these, the texts said, drew power from them, hid truths inside them — and looked down into a stranger's remembered evening.

A kitchen. Rough hands kneading dough. A child on a stool too tall for him, flour to his elbows, singing a song about a fish who wanted to be a bird. The mother — the pool belonged to the mother; Serane understood this without knowing how — loved the boy so fiercely that the feeling came up out of the water like heat from a hearthstone, and Serane flinched back from it as though scalded.

She told herself she had lingered only to catalog the phenomenon. She told herself a great many things, standing there in the failing light with her heart beating strangely against her ribs.

There were more pools as the land rose westward. Dozens. Hundreds. Some held whole afternoons; some held only fragments — a name, a scent, the last line of a lullaby. And some, she noticed with a cold that had nothing to do with the night air, held *nothing*. Perfectly round depressions in the glass, dry as bone, rimmed with a faint gray residue like ash. Emptied. Drunk dry by something, or someone.

She slept sitting up, back against a waystone whose carvings had been chiseled flat — deliberately, recently, by tools she recognized as crown issue. Even here, even in this place the kingdom pretended did not exist, the crown had been erasing. She dreamed of a door she could not open, and behind it, her own voice saying *yes, I volunteer, I volunteer,* over and over, and she woke with wet cheeks and no memory of weeping.

*Volunteered.* The word followed her all the next day like a stray dog. She had volunteered. She was certain of it, the way she was certain of her own name — and her certainty stopped there, at the edge of a cliff with nothing beyond it. Why does an Inquisitor volunteer? For honor. For faith. For grief. For love. Four doors, and every one locked, and behind each one the same voice saying *I volunteer* in a tone that made her chest ache as though she'd been running.

At noon she found tracks — small ones, bare feet, and larger ones beside them, a woman's boots, and the ghost of a third set, older, heavier, heading toward the waystones. She allowed herself the first flicker of something like satisfaction. Prey left trails. Prey could be run down. Certainty of purpose was a comfort, and she took it greedily, and did not examine why she needed it so badly.

* * *

On the third night, the Steppe stopped pretending to ignore her.

She had made camp in the lee of a shattered monolith, and the pools around it had begun to glow faintly in the dark, each one lit from within by its own hoarded past. And the nearest pool — the one three paces from her bedroll, which she had already passed twice without pausing — was showing her *her own face.*

Not a reflection. A memory.

She crawled to it before she could decide whether to forbid herself. In the water: a corridor in the palace, white stone, banners of the old king's father still hanging. A young woman in novice grays stood before a desk, and the young woman was Serane — younger by fifteen years, unlined, her hair braided crown-fashion the way novices wore it, her eyes bright with something that might have been zeal or might have been terror.

Across the desk sat a man whose face Serane did not know, and that was wrong, that was deeply wrong, because he wore the chancellor's chain and she should have known him, she must have known him, she had surely served under—

"Say it again," the young Serane said in the pool. Her voice shook. "Say what happens to them. To my—"

"The arrangement protects them," the chancellor said gently, in the tone of a man soothing a horse toward the slaughter pen. "You have my word. All you must do is serve. Willingly. That is the whole of the bargain — willingness. It is the coin the crown values most."

"I volunteer," young Serane whispered. "I volunteer."

And then the pool went dark. Not faded — *darkened*, as though a hand had passed over a candle, and the last thing Serane saw was her own young face crumpling with grief for someone whose name would not come.

She knelt at the pool's edge until the stars wheeled. She said her own name aloud, twice, to prove she still owned it. She tried to reconstruct the missing shape — *to my what? To my sister? My daughter? My—* — and felt the thought slide sideways each time, deflected by something smooth and total, a wall built from the inside of her skull.

Willingly, the chancellor had said. That is the coin.

She served willingly. She had always served willingly. And now, alone on a haunted plain under a sky that mirrored itself, Serane finally heard the sentence entire, heard the trap built into its grammar: a thing that takes your memories can make you willing for anything. Willingness proved nothing except that there was nothing left in you to refuse.

She looked east, toward Vell, toward the palace and the crown she had given her life to — or been made to give it, there was no longer any telling — and for the first time in twelve years she felt the correct emotion, the one that had been carved out of her and replaced with duty.

Fear.

* * *

She found the witch's trail again at dawn, and it was wrong.

The tracks led north toward the Hollow Road, but they wandered, doubled back, circled. Here the woman had stopped — the boot-prints deep, facing a pool. Here the child's prints scattered in panic and returned. Here both had stood a long time at the edge of something, and the glass around that particular pool was scorched in a perfect ring, as though a charm had been loosed in anger or in grief.

Serane crouched and studied the ring. Witchfire, the manuals called it — undisciplined, emotional, dangerous. But the scorch was precise. Controlled. A controlled burn meant control, and control meant training, and training meant someone had taught the Blackwood woman once, carefully, the way you teach a child to hold a blade you hope she'll never need.

Maren, she thought, and did not know why she thought it. The name arrived unbidden and settled into her like a hook finding flesh. Maren. She turned the name over. An old woman of the slums, the files said, executed years ago. But the files were written by the same crown that had written *willingly* into her bones, and Serane found she no longer trusted a single word they contained.

She rose. Far to the north, half-lost in haze, a waystone shrine stood against the sky — and beside it, movement. Figures. Two small ones and a third, bent and robed, gesturing.

An old man, teaching. A witch, a hollowed child, and an old man who moved his hands like a court magician, like someone who had once stood in the white corridors and spoken bargains aloud.

Serane felt her orders pull at her like current — *bring them to the palace* — and for the first time she asked the question she had never permitted herself:

*Bring them to whom?*

The question rang in her like a cracked bell. She put her hand on her sword hilt, and it steadied her the way it always did, and she began to walk north through the pools of other people's pasts, carrying almost none of her own — while behind her, unseen, unnoticed, the emptied pools lay in their hundreds across the glass, patient as graves, waiting for the crown to come drink again.

She was three hundred paces from the shrine when the hour-bell in her mind — the one she hadn't known was there — quietly finished counting down, and everything Ovid had bought her began to pour back in.
