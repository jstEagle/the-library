## 6. The Pilgrim at the Waystone

The waystone rose out of the glass like a broken tooth.

Jonah had seen it for half a day before they reached it — a pale finger of stone standing alone on the plain, taller than a man, taller than three men, its surface worn smooth and veined with lines that might have been script and might have been only the scars of weather. Around its base, the ground did something the rest of the Steppe refused to do. It grew things. Pale grasses, silver as spun sugar, bent in a wind Jonah couldn't feel. A ring of white flowers she didn't know the name of opened their faces toward a sun that had no business nourishing anything here.

Tam stopped at the edge of the grass and would not go further. He crouched in his permanent half-run posture, fingers curled against the mirrored ground, watching the stone with the flat wariness he gave to everything new.

"It's all right," Jonah said, though she wasn't sure it was.

She had learned, in the days since the city fell away behind them, that the Steppe rewarded honesty. Lies made the pools churn. Fear made the reflections ripple wrong. When she spoke plainly to Tam — *we're going to walk until evening, we'll drink from my flask, I won't leave you* — the plain around them went quiet as a held breath, and she could almost believe it was listening.

She stepped into the silver grass. It whispered against her ankles like paper. Up close, the waystone was worse than strange: it was familiar. The veined markings climbed its height in columns, and something in her chest pulled tight with recognition, the way a song half-remembered pulls at a dancer's feet. She raised her hand and laid her palm flat against the stone.

It was warm. Warm as skin.

And beneath her palm, faint as a pulse in a sleeping wrist, something answered.

Tam made a sound — not quite a word, not quite a cry — and Jonah turned to tell him it was nothing, it was only stone —

"You shouldn't touch it bare-handed."

The voice came from behind the stone. An old man unfolded himself from its shadow where he'd been sitting so still he might have been part of it — long grey robes gone the color of dust, a beard braided with small tarnished rings, eyes the deep brown of river water over stones. He held a tin cup in one hand and a book with no cover in the other, and he looked at Jonah the way a man looks at weather he has been expecting.

"Oil the skin first," he said. "Or ask permission. They were carved from living wood once, you know. Before your king's grandfather's grandfather. Stone is just what grief does to wood, given enough centuries."

Jonah snatched her hand back. "Who are you?"

"A pilgrim." He smiled, and there was rust in the smile, disuse. "Ovid. Formerly of the court of Vell. Currently of nowhere, which suits me fine." His gaze slid past her to Tam, and softened. "Hello, little ghost. Are you hungry? I have bread. Real bread — I bake it on the stone itself, and don't ask me how, because the answer will upset you."

Tam crept forward one careful step. Then another. Hunger, Jonah had learned, was the one thing strong enough to drag him across his fear.

"How long have you been out here?" she asked.

Ovid poured tea from a dented kettle into three cups, counting them out with the ease of a man who had done this ritual many times and been interrupted by strangers rarely enough to treasure it. "Longer than the exile. Shorter than the forgetting." He handed her a cup. Steam rose off it smelling of honey and something sharper underneath — juniper, maybe, or juniper's memory. "You're the charm-seller."

Her spine went cold. "I don't—"

"You do. You did. Luck-knots, fever-charms, little knots of quiet for frightened people in Salt Row." He said it without accusation, almost fondly. "I've watched you come for two days across the glass. The Steppe shows me things sometimes. It likes to show me the ones who are coming here, because almost no one comes here, and when someone finally does, it means the story has started moving again." He sat down heavily on a flat stone, cup balanced on his knee. "So. Tell me what the witch-hunters burned in Vell, and then I'll tell you why."

* * *

She told him. It came out in pieces at first — the criers, the bounty, Maren's face wearing Inquisitor Serane's uniform, the shop going up in flames under her own hands — but Ovid listened without moving, without blinking, and something about his stillness wrung the rest out of her. The hunters in the sewers. The pools. Tam, found running from nothing beside a pool shaped like a nursery. The fragment of wedding she'd drunk by accident, and how it had felt more real than anything since the fire.

When she finished, Ovid was looking at the waystone.

"They took Maren's face," he said quietly.

"Yes."

"And wore it while they burned children out of the slums."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed a sum he'd already carried in his head for years. "Then it's feeding well this season. It always feeds best after a purge." He set down his cup. "Sit, girl. What I have to say takes longer than standing legs want to hold."

"I'm not sitting until you tell me how you knew my name."

"I told you. I was court magician." He said it the way other men said *convict*. "Thirty years in the palace of Vell. I taught the young king his letters. I stood at his left shoulder at his coronation." His hands, Jonah noticed, had begun to shake, very slightly, around the cup. "I was there for the bargain."

"Bargain."

"The night Aldric was crowned. You know the official history — every child in Vell knows it. The young king, beloved, wise, and in his wisdom he looked upon magic and saw its dangers, and he outlawed it, and the kingdom prospered under law instead of wonder." Ovid spat into the grass, a precise, contemptuous motion. "That is the story. And it is a lie so complete, so seamless, that even the man at the coronation believed it within the hour. Even *me*, girl. I stood ten feet from the throne and I believed it, because the thing that made the bargain also made everyone forget the making."

The wind moved through the silver grass without touching Jonah's skin. Tam pressed close against her side, drinking his tea in small scalding sips, watching Ovid with his hollow dark eyes.

"What bargain?" Jonah asked. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

"The king was afraid." Ovid's voice dropped low, intimate, the voice of a teacher. "He was twenty-two years old, crowned too early, his father dead in a bad winter, the court full of knives. And on coronation eve, something came to him. Out of the old places, out of the deep glass, out of whatever was here before the kingdom was a kingdom. It offered him what such things offer: certainty. Safety. A reign without end and an enemy without a face. All it asked in return was to be let in. To be bound to him. To share the crown."

"And he said yes."

"He said yes," Ovid said. "Because he was afraid, and because it lied beautifully. But Aldric was not a fool, whatever else he became. Before dawn, before the binding closed all the way, he ran to me — to me, his old tutor — and told me everything, and begged me to help him unmake it if it ever turned on him. So I began to study. Quietly. For years." He turned his cup slowly in his hands. "And then one morning I woke up and could not remember why I was studying. Could not remember what I'd found. There was a hole in my mind shaped like the truth, smooth-edged, painless, and around it a conviction that magic was dangerous and the ban was good and I should be grateful to live in such a wise kingdom."

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. "It rewrote you."

"It rewrote *everyone*. That is what it does. That is what it eats." Ovid leaned forward, and the light through the waystone's shadow fell across his face in bands, like bars. "Fear, girl. Think about what the ban actually is. Not a wall. Not a sword. A *feeling*. Twelve years of it. Every gifted child taught to hate the thing inside them. Every mother teaching her daughter to hide. Every neighbor watching every neighbor. Every arrest, every burning, every whisper of *witch* — all of it fear, all of it flowing up through the glass like groundwater, straight into the Hollow beneath the palace, where the thing wearing the king drinks it." He tapped the ground with one knuckle. "This whole Steppe is its throat. The pools, the memories it swallows, the names children like your little ghost lose. It doesn't outlaw magic because magic threatens it. It outlaws magic because *fear is food*, and nothing seasons fear better than making people afraid of themselves."

The words landed in Jonah like stones dropped into still water, ripples going out and out. Twelve years of hiding. Twelve years of selling luck-knots with her sleeves pulled down, flinching from prying eyes, hating her own hands. She had thought she was surviving the king's law. She had been *dinner*.

"Maren," she said. Her voice cracked on the name. "My mentor. She taught me before the ban got teeth — back when—" She stopped. Started again. "They took her face. Why take a face? If it can rewrite minds—"

"Because rewriting costs it," Ovid said softly. "Every erasure costs. The pools are full of what it spends. But a face — a face it takes *whole*. Faces keep. It wears its meals." He looked at her with terrible gentleness. "Your Maren is dead, girl. But she isn't gone. Nothing here is ever gone. Which means somewhere in that Hollow, in whatever passes for its larder, your Maren still exists. And so does everything it stole from everyone it ever ate."

Tam tugged Jonah's sleeve and pointed at the waystone, at the veins of marking climbing its height, and made his sound again — that almost-word. Jonah looked, and this time, with Ovid's truth sitting cold in her stomach, the markings resolved. Columns. Script. Names.

Thousands of names.

"What is this place?" she whispered.

"A ledger," Ovid said. "One of seven, scattered across the Steppe, older than the parasite, older than the crown. The old people who lived on this glass before Vell was Vell knew what comes out of deep places hungry. They wrote the bindings down. How such things are anchored. How they're fed. And how—" he paused, and drank the last of his tea, and Jonah saw that his hands were shaking properly now, "—how they are unbound."

"You know how." It wasn't a question.

"I know how," Ovid said. "Which is exactly why, nineteen years ago, the crown remembered me all at once — why the Inquisitors came to my tower in the night with a writ of exile signed in the king's own hand by a king who has never once left his throne room since his coronation. It forgot me for fifteen years. Then I got too close to remembering, and it remembered me *fast*." He laughed, short and joyless. "I ran here because it cannot easily wear its faces this far out. The glass confuses it. But you—" he pointed the empty cup at her, and his river-brown eyes were suddenly fierce, suddenly young, "—you crossed the whole Steppe carrying a living charm-worker's gift and a hollow child and you weren't swallowed. Do you understand what that means? The pools showed you a stranger's wedding instead of eating yours. Something in you it can't digest."

Jonah looked down at Tam, asleep now against her hip, breathing slow, his fists loose for once. She thought of the waystone warm as skin under her palm. Of the Hollow waiting somewhere ahead, beneath the palace at the heart of everything.

"The king," she said. "Aldric. Is he still in there? Under it?"

Ovid was quiet a long moment.

"When I left the court," he said at last, "I passed the throne room door. And through the crack I heard — not the king. Not the thing either. I heard both. Arguing. The king's voice, thin as paper, saying the same thing over and over, like a man who has been saying it for years to no one." He met her eyes. "He was saying *let them go*. Let my people go. So yes, girl. He's in there. And he is losing."

Jonah stood. The silver grass whispered around her knees. Somewhere far to the east, back along the way they'd come, the light was turning the color of a bruise, and she thought of a stolen face walking patiently across the glass, following her trail through the pools, hour by hour getting closer.

"How far to the Hollow?"

Ovid rose too, groaning, and gathered his kettle and his coverless book. "Three days' walk, if the glass lets you pass. It won't. But I'm coming with you, and I am *extremely* annoying to eat."

Behind them, in the deepening dusk, the waystone's carved names caught the last light — and one column, low near the base, flickered. Just for a heartbeat. Just long enough for Jonah to read the newest line, still sharp-edged, still wet-looking in the stone:

*Maren Coal-hands, of Salt Row. Taken whole.*

Jonah put her hand over the name, and the stone pulsed once beneath her palm, like a heart remembering how to beat.

"We're going to get her back," she said, to the stone, to Ovid, to the dark coming on. "All of them."

From the east, faint and far, came a sound she had hoped never to hear again on open ground: dogs. And under the dogs, thinner than wind, someone singing a Salt Row lullaby in a voice that belonged to a dead woman.
