## 7. Serane's Hunt

The Glass Steppe did not know how to be night. Dusk came to it like a rumor — the sky's colors pooling in the ground until there were two horizons, one above and one below, and Serane walked between them as though through the inside of a jewel. Amber bled into violet in the glass beneath her boots. Somewhere far off, a pool of someone's forgotten grief shimmered rose-gold, and she made herself look away from it. She had learned that much in three days of hunting: do not drink the Steppe, do not stare too long at what it offers, do not wonder why you cannot remember your mother's voice.

She could remember her orders perfectly, though. Orders were the architecture of her mind, load-bearing walls she had built herself against.

*Find the charm-witch from Vell. The old man Ovid, if he travels with her — kill him last, so he can watch. The child is incidental; bring it if convenient.*

Bring it if convenient. She turned the phrase over as she walked, testing its edges the way she tested all her orders, looking for the hairline crack that would tell her where they'd been forged. They always rang true. That was the trouble with being what she was: obedience fit her like a key fits its own lock, and she had never once thought to ask who cut the lock.

The witch's trail was easy, because the Steppe itself was betraying her. Where Jonah Blackwood walked, the pools stirred. Memories rose toward her like fish rising to bread — hungry for a person who still *had* memories, still hoarded them, still spent them carefully the way a poor woman spends coins. Serane followed the glimmering wakes across the glass: a first kiss here, a funeral there, little silver scars in the plain where someone had knelt and wept over something they'd found and lost again.

And threaded through them all, fainter than scent, the taste of magic. Charms. Small ones, domestic ones, the kind Serane had spent eleven years burning out of gutters and garrets. A warming cantrip. A knot of luck. Magic like a candle in a window of a house you'd been ordered to burn down.

She hated that she could tell the difference now. There had been a time — she was almost sure there had been a time — when all magic had tasted the same to her: like copper, like sin.

*Almost sure.* The word *almost* had been following her across the Steppe for three days, patient as a dog.

* * *

She found them at dusk, in the lee of a fallen waystone, and understood immediately that they had seen her coming an hour ago and chosen to stop anyway. That told her things. It told her the old man was nearly spent. It told her the witch had decided running was no longer worth what it cost the boy.

They made a small tableau against the violet glass: Blackwood standing in front of Tam with her arms half-spread, not quite shielding, not quite surrendering. The boy behind her, thin as a whip handle, watching Serane with the flat black attention of something wild. And Ovid seated on the fallen stone as if he'd been waiting for her his whole exile, hands folded on his knee, court robes gone gray and ragged but worn with a kind of defiant correctness, like a flag kept folded in a drawer for twenty years.

"Inquisitor," he said pleasantly. "You've been walking in my footprints for two days. I left them wide on purpose. My knees are seventy-three years old."

"You knew." Serane stopped ten paces out, sword loose, the way she'd been taught — close enough to strike, far enough to read. "You led me."

"I *herded* you. There's a difference, and it's the whole of strategy." He smiled, and it was a good smile, ruined slightly by how tired it was. "I needed you here, at this stone, at this hour. The Steppe is generous at dusk. Everything is closer to the surface. Even things buried deep in a person."

Blackwood's breath went sharp. "Ovid—"

"Hush, girl. Let me have my last lecture." He rose, slowly, joints audibly objecting, and faced Serane fully. Up close his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of things left too long in water. "Inquisitor Serane. Court-trained, crown-sworn. Tell me — what did you swear? Exactly. Word for word. You remember oaths, don't you? People like us keep oaths the way other people keep faces."

"I swore to—" And there it stopped. Not resistance; absence. She reached for the oath the way her hand reached for her sword hilt, by pure habit, and found smooth nothing where the words should have been carved. Her mouth had said them once — she could feel the shape of the saying in her jaw, muscle-deep — but the words themselves were gone, scooped out, and something else poured in their place like lead poured into a mold.

*Orders,* said the thing in her head that was hers and also not, calm as liturgy. *You have your orders. The witch. The old man last. The child if convenient.*

"The child if convenient," Serane heard herself say aloud, and watched Blackwood flinch.

"Yes," Ovid said softly. "That's the voice, isn't it. I remember that voice from court. It used to come out of prettier mouths." His hands came apart from each other, and light gathered between them — not the flash-and-thunder of battle magic but something older, slower, the color of lamplight through honey. "I was the king's memory-keeper once, before any of it. Before the coronation. Do you know what my art actually was, Inquisitor? Everyone thinks magicians throw fire. We don't. We hold things. We hold them so they can't be stolen, or rewritten, or fed upon. And I have been holding one small thing for eleven years, saving it, hoping I'd never need to spend it."

"Stand down," Serane said, and her sword cleared its sheath, and her body moved with the beautiful terrible fluency of total obedience, and some small room deep inside her — a room with a locked door — began to scream.

Tam broke first. The boy had spent his whole remembered life running, and he ran now, straight past her, straight into the open, and Serane pivoted with the economy of a machine and caught him by the collar. He fought like a snared hawk, silent, vicious, all elbows and teeth, and she pinned him against the waystone with one forearm and felt — distantly, from behind glass — how small his wrists were.

"No!" Blackwood lunged and Ovid caught her arm. "No, let him go, he's just a *child*, he doesn't even have a name, take me—"

"They will take everything eventually," Ovid said. "That is the nature of what wears the crown. So we choose what it takes and when." He looked at Serane over the boy's head, and his voice gentled into something almost like pity. "This will hurt you, I think. I'm sorry. I never learned a kinder version."

"What will hur—" she began.

He spoke the spell.

It was not loud. It sounded like a page turning, like a name being crossed out, like snow sliding off a roof. The lamplight-colored light went through Serane without touching her body at all — she felt it pass, cold and clean as well-water — and struck the place where her orders lived.

And the load-bearing walls came down.

For one staggering instant she saw the architecture entire: the oath hollowed out and filled with lead; the lead stamped with a seal she now recognized, a seal like a closed eye; eleven years of perfect service built on a foundation of theft, every order ringing true because it had been *made* true, forged and fitted to her the way the face she wore had been fitted — taken from someone, someone whose name—

*Maren,* said the room behind the locked door, opening at last. *Your face belonged to Maren. You gave it willingly. Why? Why did you give—*

Then the hour began.

Ovid had not destroyed her orders. No one could destroy what the crown had written; it grew back, like ivy, like rot. He had only cut the vine at the root — erased the commands from her mind entirely, and with them the compaction that enforced them — and bought her one hour of being nobody's instrument before the writing rewove itself somewhere deeper and reclaimed her. One hour of free fall. One hour in which Serane stood in the violet dusk holding a stranger's child by the collar, with no orders at all, and had to decide — *decide*, the word itself felt like a wound — what she was.

Her sword arm dropped.

Tam twisted free and scrambled back to Blackwood, who caught him and held him and stared at Serane with an expression Serane had no category for, because it wasn't fear. It was recognition.

"You're crying," the witch said.

Serane touched her cheek. Wet. When had she last — she couldn't remember. That was the whole shape of her life now, a country of couldn't-remember. "Old man," she said, and her voice came out wrong, cracked, human. "How long?"

"As long as I can give." Ovid sat back down on the waystone, and she saw then that the spell had taken its price out of him in advance; his hands wouldn't close properly anymore, the light guttering out of his fingers like a candle pinched between wet ones. "One hour. Maybe less, if the crown is paying attention this closely — and it is, girl, it felt that, it *felt* its leash go slack. When it rewrites you, you won't know. That's the mercy and the horror of it. You'll simply find yourself walking toward us again with a clean heart and a full set of instructions."

"Then run," Serane said. "All of you. Now. I'll hold this ground."

"With what?" Blackwood demanded. "You're one woman with a sword—"

"With exactly that." Something was waking up in Serane, something older than the orders, something that had volunteered for the crown once — *why? why had she volunteered?* — and knew how a hunt worked from the inside. "I know how they'll come. I know what they'll smell for. And I know the Hollow road, better than Ovid does — he fled it; I patrolled it. Take the boy. Take the east channel under the third waystone. Don't drink anything, don't grieve anywhere, and whatever pools offer you your childhood — *don't look.*"

"And you'll follow?"

"I'll follow." She looked at Ovid, and the words came hard, like pulling nails. "Why did you spend it on me? Your one spell. Eleven years of holding it. You could have blinded me. Killed me. Bought yourselves a week instead of an hour."

Ovid smiled again, and this time the tiredness in it was bottomless, and peaceful, and terrible. "Because someone once spent a great deal on me, when I didn't deserve it. Debts want settling, Inquisitor. Besides—" his gaze slid to the darkening horizon, where far away, faint as frost, a light was rising that had no business existing out here, a white attention, a closed eye opening, "—it isn't buying you an hour. It's buying *her* an hour. The hour she'll need at the Hollow. I'm just the purse it's carried in."

"What's coming?" Blackwood whispered. "What's that light?"

"That," Ovid said, "is the crown noticing. And I am afraid, dear girl, that my hour and yours begin now — and mine ends considerably sooner."

He stood, planted his ruined hands on the waystone, and began, softly, to sing — a court hymn, very old, the kind sung at coronations — and the glass of the Steppe lit beneath him in slow rings, and the white light on the horizon leaned toward him like a listener.

"Go," Serane said, and shoved Tam toward Blackwood, and drew her sword at last — not against them, but facing the light. "Run. Both of you. Every minute you argue, the old man pays for it."

* * *

They ran. Serane heard them — the slap of the boy's bare feet, Blackwood's ragged breathing — fading east along the glass, and she stood her ground in the dying light and waited for the crown's attention to arrive, counting minutes in her head. Fifty-eight. Fifty-seven. An hour, Ovid had promised, and already it was bleeding away, and somewhere beneath her fear lived a question she had one hour left to answer:

If her orders returned and she found herself hunting these people again —

— would she obey?

The light crested the horizon like a second dawn, and Serane lifted her stolen face to meet it.
