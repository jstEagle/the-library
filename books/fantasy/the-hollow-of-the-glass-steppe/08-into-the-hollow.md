## 8. Into the Hollow

The guardian waited at the edge of the world, where the Glass Steppe thinned into a horizon like the rim of a bowl.

Jonah had felt it for two days before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, a hum in her teeth, the way the air itself seemed to lean away from something vast. Tam had stopped asking questions somewhere on the first day, but he pressed closer to her now, his small hand fisted in the hem of her coat, and she was grateful for it. The weight of him anchored her. Twelve years of hiding, and here at last was someone who needed what she could do.

The pools grew scarcer as they walked, as though even memory feared to gather this near. What remained lay flat and mirror-bright, and in them Jonah caught glimpses of herself — not reflections, but recollections. Her hands younger. Her hair longer. A shop with warm lamplight and jars of dried rowan on the sill.

*Don't look,* she told herself. *You'll need everything you have.*

The guardian did not look like anything at first. It was a stillness in the glass, a place where the Steppe's endless reflection simply *stopped*, and then it was a figure — tall and wrong-angled, made of the plain itself, its body a lattice of frozen images. Faces moved beneath its surface like fish under ice. A woman laughing. A soldier's oath. A child's name being called across a field. It wore centuries of other people's remembering the way a tree wears rings.

It turned its attention on them, and Jonah's knees nearly buckled. Not from fear. From *recognition* — the awful sense of standing before something that knew every moment she'd ever tried to forget.

"You carry much," it said. Its voice came from everywhere, from the ground, from the shallow pools, from inside her own skull. "The gate beneath the palace opens only to payment."

"I know." Ovid had told her, in his last calm hour before Serane's hunters found them: *the toll is memory, girl, and it doesn't take coin or blood. It takes what you can least spare.* "I've come to pay."

Tam tugged her sleeve. "Jonah. What's it going to take?"

She knelt so they were eye to eye. His face — hollowed once by the Steppe, slowly filling back in since she'd found him, a dimple surfacing here, a remembered lullaby there — watched hers with terrible attentiveness. He had lost everything once. He would know what it cost to lose again.

"Whatever I give," she said, "it will still be me. Do you understand? Whatever it takes, I'm still Jonah, and I'm still coming back for you."

He nodded, unconvinced, and held her sleeve tighter.

She rose. The guardian extended one long arm of glassed memory, palm open, waiting.

"Show me my options," Jonah said. "What do you want?"

The guardian tilted its head, and the faces beneath its skin stilled, listening. Then it spoke, and it spoke *with her own voice*, which was somehow worse:

"Your mother's kitchen. The smell of bread and burnt honey. You were four."

"No."

"The night your gift first woke. The candle that lit itself. Your father's shout, your mother's silence after."

"No." Her heart slammed. Those were roots. Take those and she was a tree cut to a stump, still standing but no longer growing anywhere true.

"The twelve years," the guardian murmured. "Every charm sold small and harmless. Every lie about what you are. We could take all of it, and you would walk light as a bird into the Hollow."

For one dizzying moment she considered it. Twelve years of fear, of flinching, of watching Maren's empty chair across the market. To set it all down —

But no. That fear had made her careful, and that care had kept Tam alive through three nights of the Steppe. Fear was a debt she'd already paid; surrendering it now would be spending nothing, and the guardian would know a counterfeit when it tasted one.

"What else?" she whispered.

And the guardian smiled, if a thing of frozen memories could be said to smile, and showed her.

* * *

Maren's workshop smelled of cedar shavings and hot iron, the way it always did before a lesson. Jonah was sixteen, sleeves rolled, wrists ink-stained from copying sigils she wasn't supposed to understand yet. Outside the window, Vell rained its ordinary rain.

"Do it again," Maren said.

"It won't work. It never works when you watch."

"That's because you believe it works better when I don't." Maren settled onto her stool, heavy and unhurried, gray braid over one shoulder. "Magic isn't shy, girl. It's honest. It does exactly what you believe of yourself. Now stop believing you're a fraud for five seconds and *show me*."

Jonah closed her eyes. Breathed. Reached into the knot of warmth behind her sternum that she'd spent four years pretending wasn't there.

The little copper wire on the workbench trembled. Curled. Bloomed open like a fern frond, delicate and impossible, and hung spinning in the air between them.

Silence. Rain. Jonah opened one eye, terrified.

Maren was not smiling. She was leaning forward, both hands braced on her knees, staring at the floating wire with an expression Jonah had never seen on her — raw, unguarded, almost grief-stricken with wonder.

"Do you know how rare that is?" Maren said quietly. "Shaping raw intention without a focus, without words, at sixteen? I trained at the court for eleven years and I couldn't do that until my twenties." She looked up, and her eyes were bright. "You're talented, Jonah. Truly talented. The real thing. Don't you ever let this city make you small."

*You're talented. The real thing.*

Fourteen years later, kneeling on mirrored glass before a creature made of everyone's forgotten moments, Jonah felt the memory leave her the way a tooth leaves the jaw — a wrench, a strange lightness, an ache where something living used to sit.

She hadn't said goodbye to it. She understood, distantly, that the guardian had taken the whole of it: Maren's voice, the rain, the blooming wire, the word *talented*. There was a hole in her afternoon shaped like a workshop. She knew a woman named Maren had mattered enormously, and she could not remember why.

"That will do," the guardian said, in her own voice, and sounded satisfied. Beneath its glassy surface, a new image took its place among the others: a gray-braided woman leaning forward in wonder. Filed away. Kept.

"The way is open," it said. "Go down."

* * *

The ground split along a seam Jonah hadn't been able to see, and stairs descended into the dark — not carved, but *grown*, each step a sheet of the same mirror-glass, holding faint images that faded as her feet touched them. Tam tried to follow.

"No." She crouched, gripped his shoulders. "Listen to me. If what's down there goes wrong, someone has to go back. Someone has to tell Vell what happened here. Ovid's dead for this. Maren's —" she faltered; the name was a door with nothing behind it — "someone died for this too. You're the one who carries it now."

His chin trembled. He was maybe nine years old and he had already buried more than she had.

"Count to ten thousand," she said. "Then start walking back toward the waystone. Can you count that high?"

"I can count forever," Tam whispered.

"Good. That's the spirit." She kissed the top of his head, memorizing the smell of dust and steppe-grass in his hair, and turned to the stairs.

The dark swallowed her gently, like water.

* * *

She descended for hours, or minutes; the glass steps gave no purchase to time. The images in them grew older as she went — she glimpsed coronations, plagues, festivals, a city rising stone by stone — until at last the stairs ended and she stepped out into a vast cavern, and stopped breathing.

The Hollow beneath the palace was not a room. It was a wound.

The ceiling arched up into darkness hung with the roots of the palace above, and the walls were veined with pale threads like frost or fungus, pulsing faintly, rhythmically — and Jonah realized with a lurch that the rhythm matched her own heartbeat, that the whole chamber breathed with stolen life. And at its center, tangled in those threads like a fly in amber gone soft —

A man.

Or what remained of one. He sat slumped against a throne of black glass that might once have been beautiful, and the parasite's filaments ran through him everywhere — through his arms, his chest, the sagging skin of his face. Where they touched, he was translucent, half-dissolved, flickering like a bad memory. His crown sat askew, fused to his skull by threads of pale light. He was old. He was very old, older than any king should live, and most of him had already been eaten.

But his eyes, when they lifted to find her, were still fighting.

"So," the king rasped. His voice was dry paper, ruined, but underneath it Jonah heard iron — a man who had once commanded armies and, apparently, still commanded himself. "Another one comes down the stairs. How many years has it been? Are you real, or is it dreaming me again?"

"I'm real." She crossed the chamber slowly. Up close, she could see the battle still raging in him: wherever his will gathered, the threads recoiled; where he tired, they crept back in. He had been fighting for decades with no weapon but stubbornness. "My name is Jonah Blackwood. I came to unbind you."

Something moved in his ruined face. Hope, perhaps, or the ghost of it.

"Unbind me." A sound that might have been laughter, cracked and terrible. "Child, I made the bargain willingly. I was young and the kingdom was hungry and something in the dark offered me a crown that would never tarnish. I didn't read the price written on the inside of it." His hand twitched toward her — flesh, mostly, still — "and by the time I understood, it had eaten my coronation out of the world's memory. No one remembers I ever bargained. No one remembers magic was ever free. It feeds on the forgetting, and the forgetting grows, and I grow *smaller*."

"Then we take the forgetting back." Jonah knelt beside him, close enough to see the threads around his throat pulse with each labored breath. "There's a way. Ovid found it before they killed him. An unbinding — it needs a key. Something precious enough that the parasite will loosen its grip to take it."

The king studied her with his fading eyes, and she saw the moment he understood what she was offering — saw horror move through what remained of his face.

"No," he whispered. "No, girl, whatever it costs —"

"It's already paid," Jonah said quietly.

And it was true. Somewhere above them, filed away in a creature of frozen glass, a woman with a gray braid leaned forward in wonder and called a sixteen-year-old girl talented. Jonah could feel the absence of it, a smooth blank space where the best moment of her life used to live, and she found she could not weep for it — the grief needed the memory to grieve *with*.

That was the cruelest part. She had paid and could not even mourn.

"Can you stand?" she asked instead.

The king looked at her a long moment. Then, slowly, with the grinding effort of a man hauling himself up from the bottom of a lake, he reached up and gripped the armrest of his throne of black glass.

Above them, far above, something in the palace shifted — and Jonah felt, through the soles of her feet, the first faint tremor of the hour ending. Somewhere on the Steppe, Serane's orders were knitting themselves back together.
