## 9. The Unbinding

The Hollow did not echo. That was the first thing Jonah understood about it — that a place so vast could swallow sound so completely, as though the dark beneath the palace were not empty but *listening*. Her boots found no purchase on stone; the floor was something older, a surface like cooled glass shot through with veins of dim gold, and every step she took sent a slow pulse traveling outward from her heel, as if the ground itself had a heartbeat and hers was disturbing it.

Above her, impossibly far above, hung the underside of the Glass Steppe — a ceiling of mirrored earth, and beyond it, faint as a drowned sun, the palace's foundations descending like the roots of a black tree. She had traded Maren's voice saying *you're talented, girl* to stand here. The memory was gone from her now, scooped out clean, and in its absence there was only a smooth-walled hollow of her own, an ache shaped exactly like that day. She kept touching the ache the way one touches a missing tooth. It was how she knew the trade had taken.

Tam waited at the mouth of the passage behind her, too small to come further, clutching the luck-knot she'd tied for him with hands that had finally stopped shaking. He had a name now. He had said it aloud three times before they descended, practicing, the way children practice things that frighten them. *Tam. Tam Blackwood.* She had given him her name without thinking, and he had received it like bread.

Now she walked alone into the beating heart of the Hollow, toward the thing that had eaten a kingdom's memory one fear at a time.

* * *

Serane woke on the Steppe with an hour in her mind that had not existed before.

She stood at the edge of a memory-pool at dusk, the sky going the color of old iron, and looked down at her reflection — and for the first time in longer than she could measure, the face looking back at her was *hers*. Not Maren's. Not the borrowed face the Inquisition had grafted onto her when she swore her oaths, the face she had worn so long she'd stopped feeling it as theft. Ovid's dying spell had burned the orders from her mind like frost off a window, and beneath the frost: sky. Beneath the orders: *her*.

She remembered nothing of why she had volunteered. That was the horror of it — the erasure went all the way down. But she remembered the shape of the wanting, the way an amputee remembers the limb. There had been a reason. Something had been done to her, or promised to her, or taken from her, and the crown had smoothed the record flat and handed her a dead woman's face and called it service.

In the pool below her, shallow water held fragments that were not hers but might have been once — a kitchen with copper pots, a woman singing off-key, a scarred hand kneading dough. Serane knelt at the edge. The water did not ripple. It simply opened, the way the Steppe's pools did for those who came asking honestly, and she saw herself — or someone who had been herself — younger, laughing, unafraid.

*I was somebody,* she thought, and the thought landed in her chest like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

An hour. Ovid had bought her one hour before the crown's orders rewove themselves across her mind like ivy over a ruin. One hour in which she was not the Inquisitor's instrument. One hour in which the word *duty* meant nothing and the word *mine* meant everything.

She spent the first minutes of it walking. Not toward Vell. Toward the sound — the faint, rhythmic pulse rising from the ground ahead, the heartbeat of whatever sat beneath the palace, feeding. She had hunted Jonah across forty miles of mirrored plain, had captured the child, had watched an old man die rather than surrender his spellcraft, and she had told herself each act was righteous because she could not remember a single reason to doubt it. Now the reasons were still absent, but the righteousness was gone too, and what remained was arithmetic. Dead man. Stolen child. Burned shops. A kingdom that could not remember its own coronation.

The arithmetic did not balance. It had never balanced. She had simply been forbidden to check the sum.

* * *

Jonah found the king in a chamber like the inside of a bell.

It was round, and ribbed, and every surface thrummed faintly with the parasite's pulse, and at its center stood a figure that made her stop mid-stride and simply look, the way you look at a thing you have imagined your whole life and never believed you'd see.

He was old. Older than any king should be — the coronation had been twelve years ago, and the man before her looked eighty, ninety, his spine curved like a drawn bow, his skin the gray-white of bone left too long in rain. Half of him was *missing*. Not wounded — dissolved. His left arm faded into translucence at the shoulder, his left leg into nothing at all, the edges of him blurring into the chamber walls as though the parasite had been drinking him drop by drop, year by year, converting a living man into fuel. What remained of his eyes burned. Two coals in a face otherwise going out.

And around his throat, seated there like a tick grown fat past all decency, was the crown.

Not a crown of gold. A crown of *living* material — black filaments, rootlike, hair-fine, thousands of them, threading down into his scalp, his jaw, the soft tissue of his throat, branching, dividing, drinking. Where the metal should have gleamed it instead *moved*, slowly, the way a tide moves, and light that fell on it did not reflect but vanished.

The king was fighting. That was what undid her. She had expected a husk, a puppet, a hollowed thing wearing a man's grief. Instead she found a man braced against his own dissolution with both remaining hands, as it were — a consciousness still clawing, still refusing, twelve years of siege written in the tremor of him. When he saw her, the burning eyes fixed on her, and his mouth moved, and what came out was barely a voice, more a wind through a keyhole:

"You came. Someone *came*."

"I came," Jonah said. Her voice cracked on the second word. She knelt — not from protocol; there was no protocol left in her — but because he was tall even ruined, and she wanted to look up at him the way you look up at a person, not down at a specimen. "My name is Jonah Blackwood. I'm nobody. I sold charms in Vell for twelve years and broke the law every day of it, and I'm sorry it took me this long."

"Sorry—" His breath caught. The wind-through-the-keyhole sound became something almost like a laugh, terrible and thin. "Child. I outlawed magic to save it. Do you understand? It was the only bargain it would take. Feed me the kingdom's remembering, and leave the gifted alive — hunted, yes, feared, driven underground, but *alive*. Every law, every pyre, every witch-hunter — it wrote them, and I — I let it, because the alternative—"

He couldn't finish. His translucent shoulder flickered, dimmed, steadied. The crown drank deeper. Jonah watched the filaments tighten and felt something in her go very cold and very clear.

"The alternative was it taking magic whole," she said. "Swallowing the gift itself. So you fed it fear instead. Memory instead. You starved it slow."

"For twelve years I have held its teeth," the king whispered, "one inch from the throat of everything you are. And I am *losing*, Jonah Blackwood. I am nearly gone. When I go, it stops pretending. It takes the gift entire — every charm, every blessing, every child born knowing how to knot luck into thread. It will drink the world's remembering until nothing is left anywhere but itself."

"I know," Jonah said. "Ovid told me. Ovid Court-magician, exiled, disgraced, who died yesterday buying me the hour I needed." She drew a breath. "I've come to finish his work. I've come to unbind you."

* * *

Serane reached the descent at the edge of the Hollow with twenty minutes left on her borrowed hour.

She had passed Tam's prison — a hunter's warded circle on the open Steppe, the boy sitting inside it cross-legged and furious, tying knots in a length of twine with fingers that would not stop moving. She had broken the ward with her own warrant-seal, the seal she had used a hundred times to *make* such circles, and the boy had looked up at her with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life: not gratitude. Recognition. As if he had known all along that the face she wore was stolen, the way animals know a snare.

"Go to the descent," she told him. "Wait in the dark where it pulses. Don't touch anything that shines."

He had gone. He had not asked why she was helping him. Children hollowed by the Steppe did not waste questions on mercy; they simply ran toward it while it lasted.

Now she descended, and the mirrored ceiling closed over her head, and the pulse grew in her chest until it was less a sound than a possession. Fifteen minutes. She counted them the way the condemned count steps.

She found the bell-chamber by its light — a sick, sourceless glow, the color of the moment before a fever breaks. She came through the passage with her blade drawn and her warrant-hand raised, and then stopped, because the scene before her refused every order in her mind.

The Inquisitor's script said: *the gifted woman is performing sorcery upon the king's sacred crown. Kill her.*

Her own eyes said: a half-dissolved old man kneeling in his own dissolution, begging a charm-seller in a whisper to hurry. A crown that moved like a tapeworm. And the woman — Blackwood, the fugitive, the heretic — standing over the king with her hands raised not in violence but in *care*, the way Maren used to raise her hands over a sickbed, and the sight of that gesture on Jonah's body, echoing the face on Serane's own skull, broke something loose in her that no erasure had ever fully packed down.

Ten minutes.

"Blackwood," Serane said, and her voice came out wrong, doubled, the Inquisitor's cadence fraying over something rawer underneath. "How long does your working need?"

Jonah turned. She saw the stolen face of her dead mentor wearing an expression Maren had never made in life — terror, and shame, and a soldier's desperate clarity — and she understood in one lurching instant what had happened, what Ovid had bought with his death, what was standing in the doorway holding a sword pointed at the floor.

"Minutes," Jonah said. "If nothing interrupts me. Less if the crown fights back. It will fight back."

"Then I'll hold the door," Serane said. "Whatever comes through it."

* * *

The unbinding began with a word, and the word was *remember*.

Jonah spoke it into the pulse of the chamber, and the pulse stuttered. The crown flinched — visibly, the black filaments recoiling from her voice like worms from salt — and then it counterattacked, and the counterattack was the worst thing she had ever felt. It did not strike her body. It struck the hollow where Maren's memory had been. It poured itself into the absence, offering—

Offering.

*Give up the king,* said a voice that was not a voice, a pressure that spoke directly into the wound-shaped space inside her, *and I will give you back the day. Every word of it. Maren's voice, warm in your ear. Talented, girl. Talented. You can have it whole, and the twelve years besides — every charm you sold in secret, every night you lay awake afraid, returned, restored, yours again. Only walk away. Let me finish my meal. The old man is nearly empty anyway. Why spend yourself on a husk?*

And Jonah stood in the bell-chamber with tears running freely down her face, because the offer was *real*. She could feel it waiting, the memory hovering at the rim of her hollow like water at the lip of a cup, and God, she wanted it. She wanted Maren's voice more than she had ever wanted anything — more than safety, more than Vell, more than her own life. For twelve years she had built herself around that absence. Here it was, held out to her, and all she had to do was let an old man dissolve and a kingdom forget forever.

Behind her, Serane said quietly, "I know what it's offering. It offered me the same, once. I just can't remember when."

That decided it. Not courage — kinship. Everyone in this room had been robbed by the same thief.

"No," Jonah said aloud, to the crown, to the dark, to the thing wearing a kingdom like a coat. "You don't get to buy me with stolen goods. That memory is *mine*, and I will take it back myself or live without it, but I will not take it as a *bribe*."

She set her hands above the king's head, palms down, fingers spread, the way Maren had once — some instinct told her — and began to speak the unbinding.

It was not a grand spell. Ovid had warned her of that: bindings of appetite answered only to small, stubborn truths, spoken plainly, repeated until they became load-bearing walls. So she spoke the truth. *You are not a crown. You are a hunger wearing one.* Again. *This man is not your throne. He is your hostage.* Again. *We remember what you ate. We remember the coronation. We remember the bargain, and the bargain is void.* Again, and again, tears streaming, voice breaking and restarting, while the filaments writhed and the chamber's pulse became a shriek and the king beneath her hands burned brighter, brighter, his remaining eye-coals flaring like a forge remembering fire—

—and Serane turned at the passage-mouth and faced the dark as it came for them, the parasite's servants boiling up out of the deep places, shapes without faces, and she held the door with steel and with the last four minutes of her free mind, screaming the old Inquisitor's war-cries in reverse, un-saying her own oaths word by word—

—and the crown shattered.

Not like metal. Like ice on a spring pond — a crack first, fine and bright, racing around the band of it, then a sound like a thousand bells dropped down a well, and the filaments went black, then gray, then drifted apart into ash that fell upward, drawn back into the mirrored ceiling, returning at last to the Steppe that had fed it. The pulse stopped. The Hollow, for the first time in twelve years, was silent.

The king collapsed into Jonah's arms, weightless as a winter branch, and laughed — actually laughed, a cracked and human sound — and said, "I remember my wedding. I remember my mother's name. Oh — oh, child, I remember *everything*—"

And in the doorway, Serane swayed, dropped her sword, and put both hands to her face — and screamed, because the hour was over, and the orders came flooding back into a mind that no longer had any crown to obey.

Jonah caught her before she hit the glass floor, and held on, and did not let go.
