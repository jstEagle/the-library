## 10. A Kingdom That Remembers

The bells of Vell rang for three days.

Jonah heard them from the palace infirmary, where she had refused to leave the king's side until his color returned and his hands stopped trembling like a drunkard's. He was a small man, it turned out — smaller than the coins stamped with his profile, smaller than the shadow he'd cast over twelve years of fear. Age had been waiting for him all this time, patient as debt, and now that the parasite no longer fed on him it collected all at once. His hair went white in a week. His voice, when he spoke, was thin and papery and *his*, and every word came out like something rescued from a fire.

"You could stay," he said on the last morning, when the light through the tall windows had gone gold and gentle. He was propped against pillows that seemed to swallow him. "I am told I owe you a kingdom. A position at court would be a start. Master of Arcana, perhaps. We would have to invent the office again, but inventing things is what crowns are for."

Outside, the city was loud in a way Jonah had never heard it loud. Not the frightened hush of criers announcing bounties, not the crackle of witch-fires in the slums — but noise. Singing. Arguments in the street about whether the old charms worked better than the new ones being taught. Someone had strung luck-knots across an entire bridge on the river, hundreds of them, red thread dancing in the wind like a shoal of fish.

"I'm honored," Jonah said. And she was; the honor sat in her chest like a warm stone. "But I spent twelve years learning how to be invisible in your city, Your Majesty. I don't think I know how to be visible in it yet."

"Jonah." He said her name carefully, the way one handles something cracked. "You unbound me. You gave me back my mother's name."

She looked at her hands. They were just hands — small, charm-scarred, the knuckles of a woman who had tied ten thousand knots. Somewhere inside her there was a hole shaped like a day: Maren's workshop, the smell of tallow and cedar, the moment an old woman had looked up from a half-finished sigil and said *you're talented, girl* — said it in the particular voice that meant *I choose you*. Jonah had paid it into the guardian at the Hollow's mouth, and the Steppe had drunk it down, and now when she tried to remember being chosen she found only smooth glass where the memory should have been.

"I gave something away too," she said. "We're even."

"It is not a ledger," the king said.

"No," Jonah agreed, and smiled at him — really smiled, for the first time since the crown had shattered. "That's rather the point."

She left the palace that afternoon, walking out through gates that no one guarded anymore because there was nothing left to guard them from, and the crowds parted for her without knowing why, and a child pressed a wildflower into her hand because children in Vell had apparently decided that was a thing one did now.

Tam was waiting at the foot of the steps.

He was cleaner than he had been on the Steppe, though he still flinched from running water and still ate like the food might be recalled. He wore new clothes that he clearly distrusted. But he had learned his name — *Tam, Tam, Tam*, he said it to himself sometimes like a charm against forgetting — and he had learned other words too, and when he saw her he did not run to her the way a hollowed child runs to anyone. He ran to her the way a boy runs to someone who is *his*.

"Did you say no?" he asked.

"I said no."

"Good." He took her hand with the fierce matter-of-factness of the very young. "The palace smells like lying."

Jonah laughed until her ribs hurt.

* * *

They rebuilt the shop first.

It had stood empty for a season, a black tooth in the row of tenements, its beams charred to lace. The neighbors came out when they saw her measuring the foundations — the same neighbors who had once watched through shutters while the hunters dragged Maren's students away, the same faces that had known and said nothing — and for a moment the silence between Jonah and the street was so heavy she could hear the river.

Then Mrs. Aldery, who had bought luck-knots for her husband's boats for eleven years without ever meeting Jonah's eyes, stepped over the rubble and picked up a hammer.

"Well," she said, not looking at her. "Are we going to stand about or are we going to build?"

They built. Carpenters who had never touched magic learned to plane wood alongside hedge-witches who had never held a nail, and by midsummer the shop stood whole again — wider than before, with big south-facing windows and a door that stayed open all day. Jonah planted a garden over the spot where the old floor had burned: marigold and rue and feverfew, and a rowan tree at the center, because Maren had always said a rowan kept honest company. She could not remember the exact day Maren had said it. She remembered only that she trusted the saying, the way you trust a path your feet remember though your mind does not.

Some nights the loss woke her — the hole where being chosen used to live, aching like a phantom limb. Some mornings she woke reaching for a memory of tallow-smell and an old woman's voice and found nothing but the shape it left behind.

But then Tam would appear at her elbow demanding to learn the knot for mending nets, brow furrowed with terrible concentration, and she would put her hands over his small ones and guide them, and the ache would quiet. Because this — this was hers. No one had chosen her for this. She had walked into the Hollow and back, and she had walked into this life the same way: on her own feet, by her own choice.

She was not chosen. She chose.

It turned out that was enough.

* * *

Serane came on a gray morning in autumn.

Jonah knew before she turned around — knew by the quality of the silence in the doorway, the particular discipline of a body trained to stand at attention. When she did turn, she found the Inquisitor out of uniform for the first time: plain wool, plain boots, her face — *her own face*, finally, narrow and tired and scarred at the eyebrow — wearing an expression Jonah had never seen on it. It looked, Jonah thought, like a woman standing at the edge of deep water.

"They pardoned me," Serane said. "Officially. Unofficially, half the city wants me hanged and the other half wants me to lead them." Her mouth twisted. "I told them I'd rather dig ditches."

"What did you tell the king?"

"That I owed a debt to a charm-seller from the slums." She paused. "That I couldn't remember most of what I did under the crown, and that maybe that was a mercy, and maybe it wasn't, and I intended to find out which."

Jonah set down the knot she was tying. In the garden, Tam was teaching himself to whistle to bees, badly; the sound drifted in through the open door along with the smell of feverfew.

"The school," Jonah said. It was not quite a question.

"The school," Serane agreed, and her jaw tightened as if the words cost something. "There will be gifted children, won't there. Children who hide. Children like—" She stopped. Started again. "Someone has to teach them that hiding isn't the only thing they're good at. And I know every way this kingdom ever found to hurt people like us. Every trick, every law, every lie. Seems wasteful, throwing that away."

"It seems," Jonah said slowly, "like the making of a very dangerous teacher."

"I was a very dangerous student."

They regarded each other across the workbench, two women who had hunted each other across a haunted plain, and something passed between them that was not forgiveness — forgiveness was a longer road, and both of them knew it — but was at least the decision to walk it in the same direction.

"There's a room upstairs," Jonah said. "It needs a roof patched. The rent is that you let Tam beat you at cards and pretend not to notice the cheating."

Serane almost smiled. It transformed her face entirely, the way dawn transforms a battlefield.

"I accept," she said.

* * *

The school opened on the first day of spring, with no ceremony except that Mrs. Aldery brought honey cakes and the rowan tree in the garden had come into blossom all at once, overnight, as if impatient.

Nine children came the first week. Then fourteen. Word traveled the way it always had in Vell — whispered between mothers, passed hand to hand like contraband, except now it was legal, now it was *wanted*: there is a place above the old charm-shop where they teach the gift instead of burning it. There is a woman there who knows what the pools of the Glass Steppe taste like. There is a soldier who teaches you how to keep your magic quiet when quiet is wise, and a boy who was hollow once and filled himself back up, and he'll show you how.

Jonah stood in the doorway some evenings, after the last child had gone home, and watched Tam erase the practice sigils from the slate with enormous satisfaction, and listened to Serane arguing philosophy with herself in the upstairs room, and felt the hole inside her where Maren's memory used to live — and found that it had changed. It still ached, sometimes. But around its edges, quietly, without her noticing exactly when, it had begun to fill with newer things: a boy learning his letters in charm-script. A pardoned enemy laughing over cheated cards. Marigolds coming up through scorched earth.

Not a memory of being chosen. Better. An evidence of choosing, renewed daily.

One evening in late spring — the anniversary, though she marked it only privately — Jonah locked the school, watered the rowan, and walked down to the river. The luck-knots were still strung across the bridge, faded now, replaced year by year by children who thought it was simply tradition. She stopped at the middle and looked at the water, and the water looked back with the sky in it, briefly a mirror, briefly the Steppe, briefly everything.

Somewhere behind her, footsteps: Tam and Serane, come to walk her home, because that was what they did now, because home was a thing with three people in it and a garden and a bell over the door.

"Coming?" Tam called.

Jonah took one last breath of river air — air that smelled of nothing haunted, nothing stolen, only bread from the bakeries and green things growing — and turned toward them.

"Yes," she said. "I'm coming."

And she walked off the bridge and into the city that remembered, and the bells began to ring.
