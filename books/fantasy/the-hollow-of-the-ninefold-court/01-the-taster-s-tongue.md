## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The pear arrived on a silver dish at half past nine, as pears always did, halved and glistening under a lacquer of honey that caught the morning light like something freshly wept.

Wren Sterling stood where he always stood: two paces behind the Lord Chancellor's right shoulder, close enough to be summoned, far enough to be furniture. He had learned, over nine years of standing in that exact spot, that the secret to survival was not vigilance. Vigilance was expected of tasters; vigilance made people look at you. The secret was *smallness*—a quality he had cultivated with the devotion other men gave to swordsmanship or prayer. He kept his shoulders slightly rounded, his hands folded in an attitude of mild patience, his face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a man thinking about nothing at all. When courtiers glanced at him—and they rarely did—they saw a thin, unremarkable functionary with ink-stained fingers and the kind of face you forgot while looking directly at it.

He had worked very hard for that face. It had cost him more than anyone knew.

"Chancellor Vessick." The courier who brought the pear bowed low enough for his collarbones to show. "From the ambassador of Callowmere, with the ambassador's compliments."

The Chancellor did not look up from his letters. "Set it down. Sterling will see to it."

*See to it.* Three words that meant: put your life in your mouth, little man, and tell me if I may live another hour.

Wren stepped forward. The dish was cool under his fingers, the silver chased with vines, the pear itself a thing of obscene perfection—skin like green-gold silk, flesh firm at the edges and melting toward the center, the honey pooled in the hollow where the core had been. He took the small silver spoon from its rest and carved away a portion from the cut face, choosing carefully. Not the center, where poison would pool thickest if one wished subtlety. Not the skin alone, where a coarse hand might leave residue. He chose the place where honey met fruit, because that was where clever men hid things, and Wren had spent nine years learning how clever men thought.

The first taste told him nothing. That was normal. The tongue was a blunt instrument; even the finest palate could be fooled by a skilled poisoner working through food rather than wine. So he did what he always did next—the small ritual that had kept him alive longer than any taster in the Chancellery's history. He let the morsel sit on his tongue, closed his eyes as though savoring, and *listened*.

That was the word he used privately, though no taster's manual contained it. Others called it instinct, or a lucky nose, or the peculiar sensitivity of a man who had survived three accidental poisonings without so much as a stomachache. Only Wren knew the truth, and he kept it folded away beneath nine years of practiced ordinariness: the truth that when the poison touched his tongue, it spoke to him. Bitter notes sang where they should not. A sweetness went wrong in some way he could never describe to another living soul but recognized the way one recognizes a wrong note in a familiar hymn.

The pear tasted of autumn and orchard smoke and the particular honey of the southern hives. And beneath it, threaded through the honey like a hair frozen in ice—

Nightshade.

His heart did not stutter. Nine years had taught it better. He swallowed the mouthful, opened his eyes, and said, in the flat, faintly bored voice of a craftsman reporting on his craft:

"Honeyed pear, southern hive stock, the ambassador's own recipe by the clove in it. Clean."

The Chancellor grunted and reached for the dish.

And Wren—because there was no other move available, because there had been no other move for nine years—took a second spoonful, larger than the first, deliberately deep into the poisoned hollow, and ate it while the Chancellor watched.

It was the oldest trick in his repertoire and the one that frightened him most. A taster who only nibbled was a taster who suspected everything; suspicion invited scrutiny, and scrutiny was death. But a taster who ate generously, who took the Chancellor's portion without hesitation, was a taster whose confidence was itself a kind of proof. *Clean,* his appetite said. *Eat.*

The nightshade went down cool and slow, spreading through him like dusk through a valley. He felt it find him—the cold bloom behind the breastbone, the faint metallic whisper along the back of the throat, the way the candle flames at the edge of his vision seemed suddenly very bright and very far away. His body, which should have begun its dying, instead did what it always did. It absorbed the poison the way sand absorbs rain, breaking it apart, drinking it down, leaving nothing but a headache and the old familiar exhaustion settling into his bones like sediment.

"Good," said Chancellor Vessick, around a mouthful of murdered fruit. "Send my compliments to the ambassador. And someone open a window; this room smells of ink."

* * *

By noon the headache had arrived, punctual as rent.

Wren sat in the tasters' antechamber off the great hall, a narrow room furnished with three benches, a basin, and a window that looked out onto the Chancellor's private garden, and he waited for the worst of it to pass. Around him the other tasters dozed or gossiped in low voices. Old Maren was complaining about her knees. Young Petch was boasting about a dinner he'd tested for the Admiral, where he'd detected rancid butter through three courses of heavily spiced eel.

"And how'd you catch it, then?" Maren asked, in the tone of a woman humoring a child.

Petch shrugged, pleased with himself. "Nose for it, I suppose. My mother always said I could smell a lie cooking at forty paces."

They all had their stories. A grandmother who brewed simples. A father who died of a bad mussel and left them paranoid ever after. Everyone in the Chancellery believed that tasters were made by grief and sharpened by fear, and Wren had long ago supplied himself with exactly such a history—a dead mussel-eating father, a superstitious aunt, a childhood spent smelling everything twice. It was a good story. It had holes in it only if you knew what to look for, and no one looked. No one ever looked.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of his life, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall: he was merely skilled, merely careful, merely lucky. The immune were hunted in most of the nine provinces—or had been, once upon a time, before the crown decided immunity was useful and licensed it instead. There were papers somewhere, he supposed, that proved he had passed whatever examination the licensing required. He had never seen them. Corvess handled such things. Corvess handled everything.

Corvess. Even thinking the name made Wren's shoulders tighten, and he made himself stop, made himself think instead about the garden outside the window, about the ordinary green of it, about anything except the ledger waiting for him below the city.

But thoughts are disobedient things. They went where they liked, and they went, as they always went on the bad days, to the counting.

Three accidental poisonings in nine years. Once was fortune. Twice was remarkable. Three times was a pattern, and patterns were the one thing Wren truly feared—not the poison, not the Chancellor, not even the pyre, but the moment when some clerk with too much time and too little imagination laid the incidents side by side and noticed what Wren noticed every time he counted them: that the man who survived was always the same man.

*You are a ledger entry waiting to happen,* Corvess had told him once, in the cellar office that smelled of tallow and dust and older, stranger things. *Your job is to make sure no one writes it down.*

"You're gray," said Maren, appearing beside him with a cup of watered wine. "Pear disagree with you?"

"Just a headache."

"Eat something greasy. My mother swore by it." She pressed the cup into his hands and shuffled back to her bench, and Wren drank the wine because refusing kindness drew eyes, and drank it knowing that if Maren had wanted to poison him she'd have had better sense than to use wine.

That was the joke he never told anyone: he checked everything now. Every cup, every crust, every breath of air in every room he entered, the listening happening beneath the surface of his attention like a second heartbeat. He could not remember what it was like to eat a meal unexamined. He suspected he had never known. Somewhere beneath the mountain—in a place he did not yet know existed, in a ledger he did not yet know bore his name—the boy who might have grown up eating carelessly had been traded away before he could walk, and the man who remained was built entirely out of caution, dish by dish, year by year, a cathedral constructed from the simple refusal to die.

He finished the wine. The headache receded to a dull percussion. And when the Chancellor's steward appeared in the doorway and said, "Sterling. His lordship wants you this evening—the Ambassador's banquet," Wren rose, bowed, and said, "Of course," in the voice of a man whose evening held nothing but work.

* * *

The banquet ran long, as banquets did. Seven courses, four wines, two sorbets, and a candied violet garnish that Wren tasted four separate times because the Ambassador of Callowmere kept sending dishes back to the kitchens for "adjustment," and each adjustment was a new opportunity. By the time the guests staggered out into the lamplit fog, Wren had ingested enough trace poisons to kill a plow horse, and he walked home through the sleeping streets feeling each of them humming in his blood like bees in a closed jar.

Home was a room above a chandler's shop in Saltmarket Row, chosen for its anonymity: shared stair, thin walls, a landlord who asked no questions because questions cost money he didn't have. Wren climbed the stairs, lit no lamp—for light drawn across a window was a habit killers noticed—and stood for a moment in the dark, letting the day drain out of him.

Then he did what he did every night. He pressed his palm flat against the floorboard beneath the bed until he found the seam, lifted the board, and drew out the plain wooden box that held his other life: a plain coat, a hood, and a key of black iron worn smooth by thirty years of other thumbs before it ever touched his.

The Ninefold Court did not keep offices above ground. Above ground, impossible things attracted taxes, inquisitors, and sermons. Below ground—in the cellars and drowned streets beneath Saltmarket Row, in rooms the city had forgotten it owned—impossible things simply changed hands, quietly, between people who understood that the world's true economy ran on a currency no mint ever struck.

Wren dressed in the dark, pulled the hood up, and descended.

* * *

The cellar office was lit, as always, by a single lamp turned low, and Corvess was waiting, as always, behind the great desk with its green leather top gone bald at the corners from decades of elbows. He was a small man, dry as August grass, with spectacles he did not need and wore anyway, and he greeted Wren the way he greeted everyone: with a nod toward the chair and a word that was almost warm.

"The week's entries," he said. "Late again."

"There was a banquet."

"There is always a banquet. That is why we employ you above and employ you here." He slid the ledger across the desk. "Read them back to me. I want the numbers clean before the Court sits."

So Wren read. This was the work no one above ground would ever believe, and no one below ground would ever speak aloud: that the Ninefold Court kept books. Meticulous, honest, ruthlessly accurate books. A widow in Tanner's Lane had sold her grief for her drowned son—three ounces, weighed fair, paid out in coin enough to bury the body she couldn't stop washing. A soldier home from the border wars had bought back three years of his life, the years the war had eaten, and would spend the next twenty paying for them in installments of luck. A brewer had pawned the memory of his first love against a bad harvest, redeemable at interest.

Wren recorded each entry in his small, precise hand, and Corvess watched him with those dry, unblinking eyes, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere above them the city slept on top of its debts without dreaming of them.

It was near the third bell, near the end, when Wren turned the page and stopped.

The entry was old. Older than any on the page—older, by the paper, than most in the book. The ink had gone brown at the edges, and across the top corner, stamped in wax the color of dried blood, was the seal of the crown itself.

*One soul, royal-pledged,* he read. *Held beneath against the crown's surety. Payment due at the completion of the thirteenth moon.*

No name. No date beyond that. Just the seal, and the due date, and beneath it in a different hand, faded almost to nothing, a single line that might have been an annotation or might have been a prayer:

*Bought to end the hunger. God forgive the price.*

"Wren." Corvess's voice came quiet, which was worse than loud. "That page is not part of the week's entries."

"I—" Wren looked down. His finger was still resting on the line, on the words *thirteenth moon*, and he realized he had been reading them aloud in his head, over and over, like a man touching a bruise. "What happens at the thirteenth moon?"

Corvess removed his unnecessary spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with great care, and replaced them. When he spoke again, his voice had the flat, courteous finality of a man closing a door.

"When the debt comes due," he said, "the Court collects in person. Put the book away, Wren. Some entries are not yours to read."

But Wren had already seen the wax seal. And he had already begun, in the silent arithmetic that never stopped, to count.
