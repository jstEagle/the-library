## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The cellar beneath the Chandlery on Vesper Lane smelled of tallow smoke and old paper and, faintly, of iron—the way all the guild's rooms did, as though the walls themselves had been bled at some point in their long history. Wren Sterling came down the worn stairs just after the ninth bell, carrying a candle stub in one hand and a loaf of black bread under his arm, and paused at the bottom to let his eyes adjust the way he always did: first to the dark, then to what lived inside it.

Nine shelves ringed the room in a perfect circle, each one bowed under the weight of ledgers bound in leather gone soft as skin. The oldest sat at eye level on the northern shelf, its spine cracked into pale seams like frost on a windowpane. No one touched that one but him. That was part of his value, Corvess liked to say—Wren had the only hands in the city that knew where everything was and how little any of it mattered.

He hung his cloak on the hook by the desk, set the candle in its dish of melted wax, and opened the week's ledger to a fresh page.

*Complicity dressed up as bookkeeping.* He'd heard someone say that once—a drunk philosopher outside a tavern on Salt Row, shouting at no one—and the phrase had lodged in him like a fishbone. He thought of it every Thursday night when he dipped his pen.

The ink was special, of course. Everything here was special. It was cut with a drop of grave-water from the flooded chapel under the salt market, so that whatever was written in it could not be erased, amended, or denied. A trade recorded in grave-ink was *true*, in the way that few things in the capital were true. Wren had come to find this both comforting and obscene.

He began, as he always began, with the smallest entries, working his way up to the ones that made his hand shake.

*Item the first:* Marta Ellerby, widow of the ropemaker, aged fifty-one years. Sold: her grief entire, being the mourning for one husband dead these four years. Received: eleven crowns, paid in silver, plus remission of rent owed to the Chandlery through winter.

Wren remembered Mrs. Ellerby from Tuesday. She'd sat in the little antechamber above with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a dentist—or an execution—and when Corvess had asked her, gently, whether she understood what she surrendered, she had said, "I understand I cannot feel my own kitchen anymore." Grief, it turned out, soaked into everything. The bread on the counter. The chair he'd died in, or near. The shape of the doorway he'd walked through ten thousand times. Eleven crowns seemed very little. But Wren had written the entry anyway, in his small, even hand, because the alternative to writing it was asking questions, and asking questions in the Ninefold Court was a habit with a short life expectancy.

What had unsettled him most was how light she'd looked walking out. How *relieved*. As if the grief were a stone she'd been carrying and someone had finally offered her a cart.

The guild would bottle it, of course. Grief kept well. There were buyers who preferred it fresh from the source—a widower who wanted to know how another man had loved; a playwright, once, who had purchased six months of a stranger's mourning and turned it into the most devastating tragedy the capital had seen in a generation. Nothing was wasted here. That was the guild's whole theology: nothing wasted, everything weighed, every debt balanced against some other debt until the whole edifice stood in perfect, terrible equilibrium.

*Item the second:* Sergeant Aldric Voss of the Third Foot, aged thirty-four. Bought back: three years of his life, being the years 811 through 813, previously sold to fund his mother's physician. Received by the guild: fourteen crowns per annum of interest accrued, plus the sergeant's oath of service should the Court ever require a body.

That one had been Wednesday, and it had been almost pleasant, which somehow made it worse. Voss had wept when they gave the years back—not dramatically, just quietly, tears sliding into his beard while he stared at his own hands as if checking them for newness. Three years was a long time to be missing yourself. He'd sold them at twenty-eight, when his mother's lungs were drowning, and had spent the interval feeling like a house with a bricked-up room: functional, furnished, and wrong. Now he had them again—the taste of army bread, the letter he'd written and never sent, the whole lost country of himself.

Wren had entered the repayment in green ink, the color reserved for redemptions. There weren't many green entries. Most pages ran black to the margin, black as a burned field.

He worked for an hour. A cooper selling the memory of his father's face to pay a gambling debt—bought back within the year, mercifully, though the memory came home faded, like cloth washed too many times. A midwife trading five years off her own death for a charm that would let her save one more infant a season; the guild took the years gladly, since time was the one commodity it never lacked for sellers. A young woman who wanted to forget a man entirely, name and voice and the particular way he'd held a cup, and who left the office smiling and hollow as a struck bell.

And then, near midnight, with the candle burned down to a thumbnail and the wax pooling over his knuckles, Wren turned to the last document of the week.

It lay apart from the others, in a folio of grey vellum that Corvess had handed him without comment, and the moment Wren broke the seal he felt the temperature of the room change—or believed he did, which amounted to the same thing at one in the morning in a cellar full of impossible debts.

The seal was royal. Not a minister's cipher, not a chancellor's stamp, but the full arms of the crown: the stag and the mountain, pressed deep enough into the wax that the wax had cracked around it. Documents bearing that seal were supposed to pass through chancery, through registry, through a dozen offices designed to make sure no single pair of eyes ever saw the whole of anything. This one had come down through none of them. It had simply arrived, the way heavy things arrive.

Wren read it twice before he trusted himself to transcribe it.

The language was archaic—older than the current reign, older perhaps than the regency—and it took him a while to parse the clauses, which were knotted together like roots. But the substance, once he dug it free, was simple in the way avalanches are simple.

A pledge. A surety held in escrow. One soul, unnamed in the body of the instrument, referred to only as *the collateral*, held *beneath*—and the word was capitalized, *Beneath*, the way you'd capitalize a place that had stopped being a metaphor—against a debt incurred by the Crown of Ashfall. Payment due: the completion of the thirteenth moon of the present reckoning. Upon default, collection to be made in person.

In person. Wren set down the pen and rubbed his eyes until colors bloomed behind them.

He had kept the guild's books for nine years. He had seen souls traded like horses and griefs decanted like wine. He had learned to write *one soul* in the same unbroken line as *eleven crowns* without letting his pen stutter. But there were rules even here, customs so old they'd hardened into law, and one of them was that souls held against royal debt did not sit in the surface ledgers. They went Below, past the salt market's drowned twin, past the gates Wren had never seen and did not want to see, into the court beneath the mountain where the Ninefold had been founded—or so legend said—before the capital had a name.

This document said the court had come up. Or rather, that its claim had, climbing through the dark like water through stone, until it reached a desk in a cellar on Vesper Lane and waited there for a food-taster with steady hands to copy it into the record.

*Why me? Why now?*

He told himself the obvious answers. Because he was discreet. Because grave-ink required grave-ink's keeper. Because Corvess was old and tired and delegated everything that could be delegated. All true. None of them sufficient. There was something in the phrasing—*collection to be made in person*—that felt less like a term of contract than a threat with good manners.

He began the transcription.

*Entry the last of the week, being entered under protest of silence:* One soul, held Beneath as surety against the debt of the Crown, said debt arising from [here the vellum was deliberately scorched, the words burned away by design, a hole in the record exactly the size of a famine or a war or whatever the crown had once been desperate enough to buy] ... payment falling due upon the thirteenth moon. Name of collateral: [blank]. Weight of collateral: [blank]. Standing of collateral: [blank].

Every identifying field empty. Every empty field deliberate.

Wren had handled hundreds of contracts, and he knew the difference between a blank that meant *unknown* and a blank that meant *none of your business*. This was the second kind. Someone, somewhere in the chain of custody between the crown and the mountain, had decided that the identity of this particular soul should be legible to exactly one reader—and that reader was not Wren Sterling, no matter whose hands held the pen.

Except.

He turned back to the first leaf, the one with the seal, and looked at it properly this time, the way he looked at food he suspected: not reading it but *weighing* it, letting the thing tell him what it was before he decided what it meant. The wax was old. Not ancient—decades, not centuries—but old enough that the crimson had gone the color of dried blood, and the impression of the stag and mountain had softened at the edges.

Decades. Which meant the debt predated the regency. Which meant whoever had pledged this soul had done so under the old king, or in the chaos after him—and had pledged it so absolutely, so far below the sight of chancery, that even now, thirty years on, no clerk alive could look up its terms without coming down a hidden stair to a cellar office staffed by a poison-proof nobody.

Thirty years. A soul held for thirty years, maturing like a cask, like a grudge. And now, this year, this autumn, the thirteenth moon was coming around to complete it.

Wren blew out the candle stub and sat in the dark for a while, which was a luxury he rarely permitted himself. In the dark, the cellar sounded the way it always did at this hour: the tick of cooling tallow, the whisper of the river far overhead finding its way through the city's bones, and—if you believed the apprentices, and Wren tried not to—the slow turning of pages on the north shelf, as though the oldest ledger read itself when no one watched.

He didn't believe it. Mostly.

But he did think, as he gathered his coat and climbed the stairs into the cold blue hour before dawn, about the arithmetic of the thing. A soul owed by the crown. Held thirty years. Collected in person at the thirteenth moon. Somewhere above him, right now, the capital slept—chancellors in their beds, bakers firing their ovens, a whole city of people who had sold nothing and bought nothing and owed nothing, or believed they did—and one of them, one single sleeping somebody, was collateral.

Wren walked home through streets still wet with fog, past the watch-fires and the shuttered stalls, and found that his hands, which never shook, were not perfectly steady.

Three days later, the heralds rode out at noon with trumpets and a proclamation nailed to every door in the city, and Wren understood, standing in the square with the crowd pressing at his back, that whatever the thirteenth moon was going to collect, it intended to start gathering early.

* * *

The parchment on the temple doors bore the royal arms, and beneath them, in letters tall enough to read from the far side of the square:

*BY ORDER OF THE LORD CHANCELLOR: Let all persons gifted with unlawful arts present themselves for registration within forty days. Those who register shall be examined and, if judged harmless, licensed under seal. Those who do not register, being discovered, shall be put to the pyre. Reward is offered for information leading to the discovery of the concealed gifted. Let every loyal subject look well at his neighbor.*

Forty days. Wren did the arithmetic without wanting to, the numbers assembling themselves in his head with the terrible neatness of all his bookkeeping: forty days brought the deadline to the same week the royal-sealed entry fell due. The purge and the collection, arriving together, like two creditors who had agreed to meet at the same door.

Around him the square did what squares do. People read. People murmured. Then—faster than Wren would have believed if he hadn't spent nine years learning exactly how fast—people began to *look* at each other.

He saw the baker's wife turn toward the fortune-teller's stall with an expression that was half apology and half appetite. He saw two men from the tanners' row drift together, heads bent, one of them gesturing at a third man across the lane. He saw a boy, no more than twelve, staring up at his own grandmother with his mouth open, sounding out the word *reward* the way children sound out hard words, tasting it.

By evening there would be a line at the inquisitors' door. By tomorrow, names. Wren knew how it went; he kept the guild's books, and the guild's books had a section for indemnities paid to informants, and that section had grown fat every time a hunt had ever been declared. Fear was the most liquid currency in the capital. It needed no mint.

And underneath the fear, threading through it like wire through wax, Wren felt the other thing—the private, shameful arithmetic of his own position.

Because here was the truth he could tell no one: the proclamation was aimed, in part, at people like him. Not the fire-callers and storm-speakers, not the gifted in their dramatic guises—but the quiet ones, the subtle ones, the ones whose gift was so folded into their ordinary lives that registration would mean ruin and discovery would mean worse. A woman who never sickened. A man whose dice always favored him. A food-taster who had swallowed, over nine years of service, enough arsenic to kill a garrison, and who walked out of the Chancellor's hall every morning pink-cheeked and hungry.

*Only the immune survive accidental poisonings so calmly.*

He had built his safety dish by dish, dose by dose, a fortress constructed out of the very evidence that would condemn him. If anyone ever asked why the taster never blanched, never sweated, never so much as hiccupped at a table where three lords had taken ill—he had no answer that wasn't a confession. His immunity regimen, his masterpiece of invisibility, had made him the most conspicuous gifted man in Ashfall. He was hiding in plain sight, and the plain sight was beginning to itch.

He kept his face arranged in its usual expression—mild, attentive, slightly bored, the face of a functionary thinking about his lunch—and moved with the crowd toward the temple steps, because a man who read the proclamation quickly and moved on was invisible, while a man who lingered was memorable.

That was when he saw her.

She stood at the edge of the square, beneath the eaves of the moneylenders' row, where the shadow was deepest, and she was not reading the proclamation. She was reading the crowd. Her coat was grey, her hair bound back with severe practicality, and she held a slate against her hip the way a soldier holds a shield—not for comfort but for use. She wasn't watching the fortune-teller or the grandmother or the boy. She was watching the people who were trying not to be watched.

Her gaze crossed the square, swept past Wren—

—and stopped.

It stopped the way a hawk stops, not with drama but with total attention, the whole world contracting to a single point of interest. Wren felt it land on him like a hand on the back of his neck. He did not flinch. Flinching was memorability. He gave her the functionary's face, the lunch-thinking face, and let his eyes slide off hers with the mild indifference of a man who had already forgotten her.

She didn't move. Didn't blink. Just watched him walk the length of the square, unhurried, ordinary, one more clerk-shaped citizen in a city full of them, and Wren counted his heartbeats the way he counted coins—all the way to the corner, all the way around it, until the square and the grey-coated woman and the proclamation with its forty days were safely behind him.

Then he let himself breathe.

He didn't know her name yet. He wouldn't learn it until it was spoken across an interview table with his file open between them. But he knew what she was—inquisitors wore authority the way priests wore vestments, fitted to the body by years of wearing—and he knew, with the certainty of a man who has spent his life weighing invisible things, that she had marked him.

Forty days, the proclamation said. Forty days until registration closed. Forty days, give or take, until the thirteenth moon completed its turn and something beneath the mountain sent someone up to collect a soul whose name was written in blanks.

Wren walked home through the gathering dusk, past doors where neighbors were already talking in lowered voices, past windows where lamplight showed families sitting closer together than usual, and made himself a promise in the privacy of his own skull, where no ledger could reach it:

*Before those forty days run out, I am going to find out whose soul the crown pawned. Even if the answer is one I can't afford.*

He was right about the answer. He was wrong about affording it.
