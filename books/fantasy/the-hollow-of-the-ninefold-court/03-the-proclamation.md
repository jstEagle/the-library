## 3. The Proclamation

The bells began at dawn, and Wren Sterling knew before he opened his eyes that something had gone wrong in the world above his attic room.

Not the chapel bells—those rang at six and seven with the dull regularity of a heartbeat. These were the palace bells, the deep bronze ones that lived in the tower over the Chancellery, and they were ringing out of sequence: three strikes, pause, three strikes, the pattern reserved for proclamations of state. He had heard it twice in nine years. Once for the old king's death. Once for the famine's official end, which had been announced with feasting while the Ninefold Court quietly collected its fee beneath the mountain.

He dressed in the dark, by feel, because he always did. Grey coat, grey waistcoat, the collar high enough to hide the faint silver scarring at his throat where the immunity regimen sat like a second spine. In the small cracked mirror he practiced the face he would wear today: mild, unremarkable, a man whose most interesting quality was his punctuality.

The mirror gave him back nothing worth remembering. Good.

* * *

By the time he reached Chancellor's Square, the crowd had already thickened to a press of shoulders and breath. Frost still clung to the cobbles in the shadowed places, and the morning light came down flat and white as unwashed linen. Wren did what he had trained himself to do in crowds: he became furniture. He drifted along the edge, found the post of a shuttered fruit stall, and leaned against it with the loose patience of a man waiting for nothing in particular.

On the steps of the Hall of Records, a herald in scarlet unrolled a scroll long enough to hang past his knees.

"By order of His Majesty's Regency Council," the herald read, his voice pitched to carry, "and pursuant to the Act for the Preservation of the Realm: all persons possessing gifts unnatural, heretical, or contrary to the common order shall present themselves for registration within thirty days."

The words fell into the square like stones into a well. Wren heard them land in a hundred different ways—the held breath, the small involuntary sounds, a woman near him whispering *no, no, not now, the boy is only eight*—and he kept his own face smooth as poured wax while everything beneath it went very cold and very quiet.

"Those who register shall be assessed, catalogued, and, where their gifts prove harmless, licensed." A pause, theatrical, rehearsed. "Those who do not register, being discovered thereafter, shall be judged in league with the hollow powers—and shall burn."

The crowd made a sound then that was not quite a gasp and not quite a cheer. It was the sound of a city recalculating itself, every neighbor suddenly a variable.

Wren had known this was coming. Corvess had told him three weeks ago, in the cellar office that smelled of tallow and old paper, tapping one long finger on a ledger column that tracked grain futures and grief-pawnings with equal indifference. *"The crown needs an enemy it can see, Wren. Famine is embarrassing; sorcery is convenient."* Knowing had felt like safety. Knowledge always did, right up until it didn't.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape, standing in the frost with his collar buttoned to the chin: he was immune. Not registered, not licensed, not gifted in any way the Act would recognize—but immune, which meant that when poison crossed his path, he alone walked away smiling. And the nightshade in the Lord Chancellor's pear, four days ago, had been no accident. Someone had wanted a death at that table. Someone had watched a food-taster swallow the fatal bite and live, and had said nothing, and had begun, perhaps, to wonder.

Only the immune survive accidental poisonings so calmly.

He turned the thought over the way he turned ledger pages, looking for the flaw, the entry that didn't balance. There wasn't one. His whole life—the regimen built dish by dish since childhood, doses measured in grains of dust, in sips of decoction, in the slow education of his blood—he had constructed the perfect evidence against himself. A man who never sickened. A man who tasted death and swallowed it like honey. In a city about to start asking who among us is not what we seem, Wren Sterling was the answer written in his own veins.

* * *

The line formed faster than he expected.

It began outside the Hall of Records almost before the herald had finished—a queue of citizens clutching papers, pointing fingers, eager to be first through the doors where clerks waited with fresh ink and fresh ledgers. Registration, they called it. But Wren watched the faces in that line and saw what it actually was: denunciation, organized, sanctioned, made respectable by a queue and a clerk's table.

He recognized people. That was the worst of it. The baker's widow from Salt Lane, who had once sold her grief to the Court for enough coin to keep her ovens lit through winter—she stood third in line, jaw set, holding a folded paper with names on it. The cooper from two streets over, who had bought back three lost years after a fever took them, years he now visibly regretted owning. And behind them, dozens more, ordinary faces wearing the particular expression Wren knew intimately from mirrors: the look of people performing ordinariness so hard it left dents.

They weren't registering themselves. They were registering each other.

A boy of perhaps ten ran up the steps with a scrap of cloth clutched in his fist—a charm, some grandmother's knot-work, harmless as string—and handed it to the clerk with the terrible solemnity of a child doing what he'd been told. The clerk wrote something down. Somewhere, a door would be knocked on tonight.

Wren's hands, tucked in his pockets, curled into fists around nothing. He thought of the ledgers below the city, the neat columns of impossible trades, and understood with a lurch that this was simply another ledger—one the crown kept in public, in daylight, with better handwriting. *One soul, entered freely. One soul, entered under duress. Balance maintained.* He had spent nine years telling himself that keeping accounts was not the same as making them. Standing in the square, watching the baker's widow give up a name, he could no longer find the seam between those two things.

And then, across the square, someone looked directly at him.

She stood apart from the crowd the way a blade stands apart from the scabbard—not tall, not imposing, but absolutely still in a way that drew the eye against its will. Dark hair cut short and practical. A long grey coat that was not quite uniform but cost more than uniforms usually did. She wasn't watching the herald or the queue or the crying woman being escorted up the steps. She was watching the edges of the crowd. Watching, with the patient thoroughness of a fisherman reading water, the people who were trying not to be watched.

Her gaze passed over Wren and stopped.

He did not flinch. Flinching was a luxury he had trained out of himself alongside the gag reflex. He let his eyes slide past hers with the mild blankness of a man who has noticed nothing, who is thinking about bread, about bootlaces, about anything. He counted to ten. When he glanced back—casually, the way anyone might—she was still looking at him.

Not suspiciously. That was what unsettled him. She looked at him the way he looked at ledger entries that balanced too perfectly. With interest. With the beginning of a question.

Then a clerk called her name—*Vane*—and she turned away without hurry, ascending the steps into the Hall of Records, and the square closed behind her like water.

Wren exhaled slowly through his nose. *Inquisitor,* he thought. The new office, created by the same Act, announced in the same breath: *Inquisitors of the Regency Council, empowered to examine, detain, and refer.* He had read the draft legislation weeks ago in Corvess's office, had even inked the guild's marginal notes predicting exactly this. Reading about an inquisitor and being read by one were different experiences entirely.

He pushed off from the fruit stall and walked home the long way, unhurried, a grey man in a grey morning, while behind him the line of neighbors lengthened and the clerk's pen scratched like a small animal gnawing through the city's walls.

* * *

That evening he did not go to the cellar office. He was not due there, and going would mean passing through streets where the new watch had already begun stopping people, asking questions with the cheerful menace of men enjoying new authority. Instead he climbed to his attic and lit one candle and sat with his back against the chimney breast, where the bricks still held a ghost of warmth, and made himself think.

Fear, for Wren, had always been a kind of bookkeeping. He managed it in columns. *Assets:* immunity, forged papers, knowledge of the Court's inner workings, a face no one remembered. *Liabilities:* the nightshade incident, the woman Vane's eyes, the simple fact that in thirty days' time every unregistered gift in the city would become contraband, and his was written into his body where no clerk could license it.

He had survived nine years at court by being forgettable. But forgettable was a strategy for avoiding attention, and attention had just been made mandatory. The proclamation hadn't asked *who are you hiding from?* It had asked *what are you?*—and demanded an answer in writing, witnessed, sealed.

There was a knock at his door.

Wren went still. No one knocked at his door. He had lived in the attic off Cooper's Yard for six years and could count his visitors on one hand with fingers to spare. The knock came again—two raps, polite, unhurried.

He rose, took the tasting knife from his belt—habit, useless habit, a knife against whatever stood beyond the door—and opened it a hand's width.

An envelope lay on the landing. Cream paper, heavy stock, sealed with grey wax bearing the sigil of the Regency Council: a tower over crossed keys. No messenger in sight; footsteps already fading down the stair. Inside, when he broke the seal with his thumb, a single card in a fine secretary's hand:

*Master Sterling — Your presence is requested tomorrow at the tenth hour, offices of the Inquisition, Hall of Records. A matter concerning the late Lord Chancellor's table. Come prepared to discuss your health.*

*Your health.* Two words doing the work of twenty. They knew about the pear. Of course they knew—the Chancellor's household physician would have examined the remains of the fruit, found the nightshade, and asked the only sensible question: *how did the taster live?*

Wren sat back down against the warm bricks and read the card again, and again, until the letters stopped meaning anything. Then he did what he always did with fear: he filed it. He set the card on the floor, straightened it so its edges ran parallel with the floorboards, and began, methodically, to plan.

Tomorrow he would need a story. Not a good story—a forgettable one. He would be modest about his constitution, vague about his methods, grateful for the opportunity to assist. He would be the grayest man the inquisitor had ever interviewed, so bland that she would file him away within the hour and never think of him again.

Except.

Except she had looked at him across the square not with suspicion but with curiosity, and curiosity was the one thing his entire life had never taught him to survive. He could outlast hatred; hatred grew bored. He could outlast fear; fear looked elsewhere. But a person who *wondered* about you would come back, and come back, and come back, turning you over like a page, until either they found what was written there or you burned the book yourself.

Wren pinched out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the city below him rearrange itself into something new and hungry. Thirty days. A summons at the tenth hour. An inquisitor with patient eyes.

Somewhere beneath the mountain, he thought absurdly, there was probably a ledger with his name in it already, an entry dated today, flagged and waiting.

He had no idea how right he was.

*Very well,* he told the darkness, and the darkness, being honest, did not promise him anything back. *Then we balance the books ourselves.*
