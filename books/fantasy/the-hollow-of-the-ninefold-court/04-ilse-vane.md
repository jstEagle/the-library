## 4. Ilse Vane

The file on Wren Sterling was thin enough to slide beneath a door.

Ilse Vane had read it four times before the taster was shown into her office, and four times she had felt the same small wrongness, like a note held a half-tone too long in an otherwise perfect hymn. Nine years of service. Nine years of tasting for the Lord Chancellor's table and the tables of three successive Chancellors before him. In nine years, the man had detected poison eleven times—eleven—and had never once been taken ill himself. Not a stomach complaint. Not a fever. Not the mild chronic ailments that afflict every man who eats for a living: no gout, no flux, no wasting. The court physicians had examined him twice at the Chancellor's request and pronounced him *unremarkably healthy*, which was itself remarkable, because no physician alive used the word *unremarkably* without meaning its opposite.

She had the examination transcripts in the file. She had read them five times.

"Master Sterling." She did not rise. Rising was a courtesy, and courtesies were levers; she preferred to keep all her levers in her own hand. "Thank you for coming. Sit."

He sat. He sat the way water finds its level—without decision, without resistance, as though sitting were simply what happened to bodies in rooms where chairs existed. He was smaller than she'd expected, or perhaps not smaller but *less*, the way certain men contrive to be less than the space they occupy. Brown hair going grey at the temples too early. Hands folded in his lap with the patience of a man waiting for bread to rise. His eyes were the color of weak tea, and they rested on her face with an attention so mild it could not possibly be called attention at all.

"You know why you're here," she said.

"The proclamation, Inquisitor." His voice was quiet, unhurried, faintly apologetic—the voice of a man sorry to be taking up her time. "Registration of the gifted. I received my summons this morning."

"You received it yesterday. The courier's log says yesterday evening."

"Then I misremember," he said pleasantly. "Days run together, in my trade."

*In my trade.* As if tasting for the most paranoid table in the kingdom were a trade like cooperage. She let the misremembering lie on the desk between them, unremarked. It was nothing. A day's drift in a courier's recollection was weather, not evidence. But she wrote it down anyway, because she had learned long ago that evidence was never born; it was accumulated, grain by grain, the way a riverbed is made of things that seemed, each one, too small to matter.

"Tell me about your gift," she said.

"I don't have one, Inquisitor."

"Eleven poisonings detected in nine years. The last Chancellor called you *the finest tongue in the Nine Provinces*. The current one doubled your stipend after the Aldermarch affair. That is not a trade, Master Sterling. That is a talent. And talent, under the new law, must be registered."

"Talent." He considered the word as though it were an unfamiliar coin, turning it over to see whose face was stamped on it. "A cook has talent. A fletcher. My mother could tell a bad egg by smell through the shell. Should she have registered?"

"Your mother is dead?"

"Years ago." No grief crossed his face—or rather, grief crossed it and was gone so quickly and so completely that Ilse could not afterward say whether she had seen it at all. "I taste what's there, Inquisitor. Nightshade tastes bitter. Arsenic brightens a wine in a way I can only describe as *greedy*. I've trained myself for nine years to notice these things, the way a sailor trains himself to notice weather. If noticing is a gift, then every midwife in the capital should burn."

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, a *perfect* answer—each clause shaped to the question, each example humble and domestic and verifiable, each deflection landing exactly where a deflection should land. And that perfection was the wrongness again, the held note. In six years of inquisitorial work, Ilse had interviewed two hundred and forty-one suspected gifted. The guilty lied badly and the innocent lied worse, tangling themselves in outrage or terror, over-explaining, under-explaining, weeping, blustering. Not one of them—not one—had ever answered like a man reading from a well-rehearsed catechism while appearing to improvise it.

Except, she reminded herself, the ones who were genuinely innocent and genuinely calm. There were such men. Dull, honest men whose ordinariness was real all the way down. She had released four of them herself, and signed the warrants for thirty-one others, and the difference between the two groups had never once been anything she could point to on paper. It lived somewhere behind the sternum, in the place where certainty went when it wasn't certain anymore.

"Describe the nightshade," she said. "The pear. Two weeks ago. You flagged it."

"Honeyed pear, third course, the Chancellor's own plate." He didn't hesitate; he didn't rush. "Bitterness underneath the honey—faint, at the back of the tongue, like the ghost of a flavor rather than a flavor. I spat, called for the assay, and the assay confirmed. Atropine in the syrup."

"And you tasted it before the Chancellor raised the fork to his mouth. Before anyone else at the table noticed anything."

"Lucky timing."

"Nine years of lucky timing."

"Nine years of paying attention," he said, and smiled—a small, tired, entirely reasonable smile—and something in Ilse's chest tightened around nothing at all.

She asked him about his childhood. He described a village she would later find did not appear on any map drawn in the last twenty years, renamed or drowned under the reservoir at Callowmere, impossible to check. She asked about teachers, and he gave her names that checked out perfectly: a hedge-tutor, a chandler's widow who taught letters, a temple scrivener. Every stone she turned over had another stone beneath it, fitted so precisely that no light came between them. An hour into the interview she realized she had stopped looking for cracks and started admiring the masonry, and the realization frightened her more than any lie would have.

Because here was the thing she could not put in the file: Wren Sterling was either the most innocent man in the capital or the most dangerous, and he performed innocence the way great actors perform kingship—from somewhere deeper than the words.

"One more question," she said, and closed the file. "If the assay tomorrow found nightshade in your blood—you understand the assays are random now, under the proclamation—what would it find?"

He looked at her then. Really looked, for the first time in the hour, and his tea-colored eyes were suddenly, briefly, bottomless—not hostile, not afraid, just *deep*, the way a well is deep, and she had the vertiginous sense of standing at the lip of something that went down much further than a food-taster's life had any reason to go.

"It would find whatever it finds," he said gently. "I'm a taster, Inquisitor. Everything I am, I am on the record."

*Everything I am, I am on the record.* Later, walking the colonnade alone in the blue hour, she would turn the sentence over and hear what she'd missed: that it was not a denial. It was an invitation to check. And no honest man invites the knife to test the wound.

"Thank you, Master Sterling. You're free to go. You may be summoned again."

"Of course." He rose, bowed the precise degree owed to her rank—no more, no less—and paused at the door. For a moment he stood with his hand on the frame, and she thought he might say something else. He didn't. He simply left, quietly, the way he seemed to do everything, closing the door so softly that the latch made no sound at all.

Ilse Vane sat alone in her office with the lamp burning and the file open in front of her, and for a long time she did not move.

* * *

The filing room was in the basement of the Bastion, three floors down, behind a door that required two keys and a spoken word. It held eleven thousand files. She knew the number because she had counted them once, in her first year, when counting had still seemed like a form of control.

Every suspected gifted in the Nine Provinces had a folder there. Inside each folder was a life reduced to testimony, assay results, informant reports, and a final page bearing one of two stamps: **CLEARED** in blue, or **FOR THE PYRE** in red. The red-stamped folders were stored separately, in the iron cabinets along the east wall, because paper burned more easily than people and the Bastion liked its ironies tidy.

Ilse stood in the corridor outside with Wren Sterling's file under her arm, and her feet would not carry her through the door.

It was absurd. She had filed hundreds. She had walked this corridor with folders under her arm more times than she could reckon, and her feet had always known the way, because duty was a road and roads did not require the heart's permission. But tonight the corridor stretched in front of her like a held breath, and the file under her arm weighed what a life weighs, which is nothing, which is everything.

*Thoroughness,* she told herself. *Keep it on your desk until the second interview. Until the assay comes back. Until you've checked the village records from Callowmere. Filing it now would mean deciding, and you haven't decided.*

But she had decided, hadn't she? That was the filthy truth of it. Files went into the room either blue or red, and a file left on a desk was neither—it was a door left open, a verdict deferred, and she was keeping it on her desk for the same reason a gambler keeps a losing card in his hand: because while it was unplayed, it was still possible that it was a winning one.

She turned around and climbed the three floors back to her office, and set the file in the center of her blotter, squared its edges, and weighted it with the brass inkstand, and told herself—firmly, in the voice she used on junior inquisitors—that this was procedure.

Her office looked wrong with the file on the desk. Her desk was famous among her subordinates for its bareness: lamp, blotter, inkstand, the day's papers, nothing else. Order was how she kept the work at arm's length. Now there was a folder sitting in the middle of all that order like a stone dropped in still water, and every time her eye touched it during the evening's correspondence, she felt the ripple.

Near midnight she opened it one more time. Not to read—to look. Eleven detections. Nine years. No illness. No vices on record, no debts, no lovers, no quarrels, no tavern debts, no gambling, nothing that could be leveraged, nothing that could be bought, nothing that could be *threatened*. A man without handles. She had broken harder subjects than Wren Sterling with less than a debt to their name, but breaking required a handle, and this man had sanded himself smooth as riverstone.

Or—he was exactly what he appeared. A careful, lonely, disciplined man who had spent nine years making himself indispensable and invisible, which was, when she thought about it honestly, the most natural thing in the world for a food-taster to want. Poisoners died young in this city. So did tasters. Caution was not conspiracy.

She closed the file. She did not sleep. Toward dawn she lit a fresh candle and, in the margin of the interview transcript, beside his answer about the nightshade, she wrote a single word in her small, severe hand:

*Why?*

Not *is he lying.* She knew the shape of lies; lies had weight and warmth, they breathed. This was a different question, and she was afraid of it, and she wrote it anyway: why would a man with no recorded gift survive eleven years at the most poisoned table in the kingdom, and why did the survival feel less like luck than like *architecture*?

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, in the iron cabinets, eleven thousand lives slept in their folders, and hers was not among them, and that fact—which had once been her whole comfort—sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

Her sister's name had been Maren.

Ilse did not think of her often anymore, or she told herself so, the way one tells oneself one doesn't dream. Maren had been nineteen the year the last great purge swept the river provinces, twenty years ago now, and she had been gifted in the small, warm way that country girls are gifted: she could coax milk that had soured back to sweetness, mend a cracked bowl by holding it, make a garden come a fortnight early. Nothing that would frighten a magistrate. Everything that frightened a magistrate, if the magistrate was having a bad season and needed a fire.

Ilse had been fifteen. She had stood in the square at Dolefield with the whole town, close enough to see the sparks lift off the kindling, and Maren had found her in the crowd—of course she had, sisters always find each other—and Maren had not screamed, had not cursed, had only looked at her with an expression Ilse would spend the rest of her life failing to name. Not reproach. Worse than reproach. *Recognition.* As if Maren had already forgiven her, in advance, for the thing Ilse was doing by standing still.

And Ilse had stood still. Fifteen years old, silent, hands at her sides, while the smoke went up straight in the windless cold. Their mother had wept. Their father had stared at his boots. The priest had said the words about cleansing. And Ilse had done the unforgivable ordinary thing: nothing. She had gone home. She had eaten supper. She had survived, the way everyone survives, by being nobody, by being furniture, by letting the fire be someone else's.

It took her four more years to understand that the silence had gotten inside her and set up housekeeping. She joined the Bastion at nineteen—the same age Maren had burned—telling herself it was penance, telling herself she would work within the machine, that from inside she could save one in ten, one in fifty, one, someday, the way you bail a sinking boat with a thimble and call it swimming.

Six years later she had signed thirty-one warrants. She kept the count the way other women kept prayers. Thirty-one red stamps in her own hand, each one a small clean act of state, each one a version of standing still in a square while smoke went up. She had saved perhaps a dozen—the bailing, the thimble. She had learned to stop doing the arithmetic that compared the columns, because the arithmetic always ended the same way, with Maren's face in the crowd, forgiving her in advance, forever.

That was why the Sterling file would not go into the drawer. She understood this now, in the grey hour before the lamps were lit, sitting at her immaculate desk with the folder under the inkstand like a patient under a blanket. It was not thoroughness. Thoroughness was a virtue and this was not virtuous. It was that some deep part of her—the fifteen-year-old still standing in the square, the part she had never managed to burn out or bury—had looked at Wren Sterling across an hour of perfect answers and recognized the furniture in him. The stillness. The survival-by-invisibility. Herself, wearing a man's face, holding very carefully still in a world that had started collecting firewood.

And beneath that recognition, quieter, stranger: the suspicion that he was not hiding from the fire at all. That the stillness was not fear. That a man who answered an inquisitor with the gentleness of a nurse talking to the wounded was a man who had already seen the shape of the game and found it small.

The bell rang for first watch. Below her window, the city woke into its new season of fear: registration lines already forming at the Bastion doors, neighbors queuing to denounce neighbors, the proclamation nailed to every post with its black-letter promise of mercy for the registered and fire for the hidden. She watched a baker's boy copy the proclamation's list of forbidden signs into a slate, practicing, learning what to look for in his own family.

Ilse pulled the file from under the inkstand and read the cover sheet once more. *Sterling, Wren. Occupation: taster. Status: pending.*

Pending. The word had never looked so much like a door left open.

She took up her pen to write the summons for his second interview—and stopped, because the morning report had just come up from the clerks' room, and the top line of it concerned the assay schedule, and the assay schedule had changed. Random blood assays for all household staff of the Chancellery, beginning this week. Beginning, in fact, tomorrow.

Beginning with the tasters.

Ilse set down the pen. On the transcript, in the margin, her own handwriting waited for her—*why?*—and tomorrow, one way or another, the assay would answer it for her. She found that she was not sure which answer she was hoping for. That was the chafe of it, the splinter working deeper under the skin of her duty: thirty-one warrants had never cost her a night's sleep, and one thin file on one forgettable man was costing her everything.

She squared the folder's edges one last time and left it on the desk, out in the open, where any visiting superior could see it and ask her why.

Let them ask. She was beginning to want, dangerously, to hear herself try to answer.
