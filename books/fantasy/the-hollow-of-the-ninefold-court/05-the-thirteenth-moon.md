## 5. The Thirteenth Moon

The almanac arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of tallow and old paper, the way all things from the guild's suppliers smelled—of cellars, of things kept from the light. Wren signed for it at the kitchen door of the Chancellor's residence with the same blank nod he gave every delivery, as though it were nothing more than the season's accounts. The cook's boy who carried it in never looked twice at him. No one ever looked twice at him. That was the whole architecture of his life: a man built to be overlooked, load-bearing walls of dullness, a face like furniture.

He waited until the household slept before he unwrapped it.

The almanac of the Ninefold Court was not sold in any shop. It was printed once a year in an edition of nine copies, each hand-numbered, each bound in grey leather that drank candlelight rather than reflecting it. Ordinary almanacs tracked tides and harvests and the feast days of saints nobody believed in anymore. This one tracked other things. The ebbing of borrowed years. The ripening of debts. The phases of moons that did not appear in any sky above the city but which the court beneath the mountain counted anyway, patiently, the way a moneylender counts the days between loan and due date.

Wren turned the pages by feel more than sight, his fingers knowing the weight of the paper, until he reached the table of lunations for the coming year.

There it was. Printed in the small, unadorned type the guild used for facts too heavy for ornament:

*The Thirteenth Moon completes its turn in forty days.*

Forty days.

He sat very still in the cellar office with the candle burning low and let the number settle into him the way a stone settles into still water. Forty days was nothing. Forty days was the space between one breath and the next, stretched out and dressed in weeks. He had served the Lord Chancellor for nine years and had learned to measure time in dishes—in the pears and the broths and the wedding feasts he had tasted without dying—and forty days was fewer meals than he could count on both hands.

And somewhere in the ledgers around him, in this room where he had spent hundreds of nights keeping accounts of impossible things, there was an entry flagged with the royal seal that came due when that moon completed its turn.

He had been avoiding it. He knew that now, could name it honestly at last, sitting alone with the truth the way he sat alone with everything. For three weeks he had shelved the flagged ledger high, out of easy reach, and told himself he would get to it after the quarter's trades were posted, after the widow's grief was properly bottled and catalogued, after the soldier's three years were entered back into circulation. There was always an after. Bookkeeping was a religion of afters.

But the almanac had ended his patience for himself.

Wren rose, took down the ladder, climbed to the highest shelf, and brought down the oldest ledger in the room—not the oldest in the guild; he knew there were older ones below, in the mountain, in places he had never been asked to go—but the oldest here, in this office that smelled of ink and beeswax and his own long complicity. Its cover was cracked black leather gone soft as cloth. The royal seal was stamped into the first page in wax the color of dried blood, and beneath the seal, the flag: a slip of grey ribbon, the guild's color for *pending collection*, tied through a punched hole like a thread through flesh.

He untied the ribbon. His hands were steady—he was always steady; steadiness was the first thing they taught a food-taster and the last thing they ever let you lose—and their steadiness frightened him more than shaking would have. Even now, even alone, even on the edge of whatever this was, he performed calm for an audience of no one.

He opened to the entry.

The script was old-fashioned, the looping hand of a scribe trained before the current reign, before perhaps the last two reigns. The ink had faded to the brown of dried roses. He read the header first, because headers were safe, headers were just categories:

*Nature of pledge: One soul, whole and unencumbered.*
*Pledged against: Termination of famine, realm-wide, in perpetuity.*
*Surety accepted by: The Ninefold Court, seated beneath the mountain.*
*Collection due: Upon completion of the thirteenth moon.*

Then the line that mattered. The line he had read a dozen times in fragments over three weeks, letting his eyes slide off the name field the way his tongue slid past poison without swallowing—or rather, precisely the way it didn't; he swallowed everything, that was his curse and his craft—and now, with the almanac open beside him and forty days printed in grey ink, he made himself look.

*Name of pledged soul:* **Wren Sterling.**

*Date of pledge: The day of his birth.*

The candle guttered. Or perhaps it didn't. Perhaps the room simply tilted, the way a floor tilts when you learn the building has been standing on something hollow all along.

He read it again. And again. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder. His own name, in ink older than his memory, entered into a ledger of souls on the day he was born—which meant someone had brought him here, or sent word of him here, or stood over a scribe while the scribe wrote, before he could walk, before he could speak, before he was anything at all except a new-made person with a name that someone else had already spent.

Sold. Not stolen. Sold. The distinction mattered in the guild's books; he had recorded the difference himself a hundred times. Stolen souls were crimes, and crimes created enemies. Sold souls were contracts, and contracts created obligations. Whoever had given his soul to the court beneath the mountain had done it lawfully, in exchange for something, and the court had held it ever since the way a bank holds collateral: patiently, impersonally, waiting for the note to come due.

Against a famine. *Termination of famine, realm-wide.* He knew that famine. Everyone knew it, the way you know a war your grandparents survived—the Winter of Ash, the three years the fields gave nothing and the granaries gave less, the years the crown's histories called *the Trial* and the old women in the salt market still called *the Hunger*. It had ended suddenly, miraculously, in the spring of some distant year, and the chronicles credited a change in weather and the wisdom of the regency council.

The chronicles, Wren thought, were bookkeeping too. Just bookkeeping performed for people who couldn't read the real ledgers.

His parents. He had almost no one to ask about them. He had been raised in the foundling wing of a charity hospital, one more anonymous child among the plague-orphaned and the abandoned, and the matrons had long ago stopped pretending to keep individual records. He had no mother's face to search for clues in. He had a birthdate—that was all, a birthdate and a name, and now he understood that even those had been transactions. Someone had named him Wren. Someone had carried him into the world on a specific day, and on that same day, someone had walked him—carried him? written of him?—into a contract that traded his soul for bread on every table in the realm.

He tried to feel grateful. That was the horrible part, the part he would lie awake turning over for many nights after: the arithmetic almost worked. One soul against a kingdom's starving. Any chancellor would make that trade. Any merciful god would. He had spent nine years tasting poison for exactly that kind of arithmetic—one body risked so that a greater body might be fed—and he could not claim the trade offended him on principle.

But no one had asked him. That was the wound under the wound. He had built his entire life on consent freely given—he chose the taster's post, he chose the guild's ledgers, he chose invisibility the way other men chose careers—and underneath all of it, beneath the foundation stone itself, was a choice made about him before he existed, signed in his name, spending the only thing he owned outright.

*The past was always hunting him.* He felt it now with total clarity: not pursuing, not chasing, but hunting the patient way debt hunts, walking behind him at his own pace for thirty years, close enough to touch the back of his neck, never needing to hurry because the thirteenth moon was never in any danger of arriving early. It had watched him learn to survive. It had watched him build his immunity dish by dish, dose by careful dose, watched him become the man who could swallow nightshade and walk away. All that cunning, all that discipline, and it had never once put him outside the ledger. He had been fortifying a house whose deed belonged to someone else.

Footsteps on the stairs. Slow, deliberate, unhurried—the tread of a man who owned the stairs and every secret they kept.

Wren closed the ledger, but he did not hide it. There was no point. Corvess always knew.

* * *

Corvess descended into the office the way frost descends on a window: gradually, silently, and with the implication that morning was over. He was a tall man gone stooped in a way that seemed chosen rather than aged, as though he had bent toward his work for so long that the work had set in his spine. His robes were grey, of course. Everything in the guild was grey. Grey was the color of things that refused to be either light or dark, and Corvess wore it like armor forged from neutrality itself.

"You've read it," he said. It was not a question. He crossed to the desk, looked down at the ledger, at the untied ribbon, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been pity in a harder organization.

"The almanac came today," Wren said. "Forty days."

"Thirty-nine, now. You spent one reading." Corvess drew out the chair across the desk and sat, folding his long hands. In the candlelight his face was all planes and shadows, a face designed for negotiating in dim rooms. "I wondered how long you'd leave the ribbon untied. Three weeks, Wren. I've watched you shelve it higher and higher. You're the best bookkeeper I've ever had, and you thought I wouldn't notice a man avoiding his own shelf."

"Whose entry is it?" Wren asked, though the ledger lay open between them, though his own name sat in it in brown ink like a beetle pinned to a card. He needed to hear it said aloud by someone else, needed the words to exist in another voice so they might stop echoing only inside his skull. "Whose soul is pledged?"

"You know whose."

"I want to hear it."

Corvess regarded him for a long moment. Then, quietly, with the gentleness of a man setting down something fragile: "Yours. It has always been yours. I told the court, thirty years ago, that the child should be told at fifteen. I was overruled. The reasoning was—" his mouth twisted, "—that a man who knows he is collateral behaves differently than a man who doesn't. Less usefully."

"Less usefully," Wren repeated. The phrase lodged in him like a fishhook. Nine years of service. Hundreds of nights in this office. Every trade faithfully recorded, every widow's grief bottled and shelved, every soldier's purchased years entered into circulation—and the whole time, he had been inventory. Stock. An asset with a collection date, employed by the institution that held the lien on him, doing its books with hands that belonged, in the eyes of the law beneath the mountain, to the bank.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because the almanac came today." Corvess leaned forward. "Because in thirty-nine days, the thirteenth moon completes its turn, and when it does, the court collects in person. Not agents, Wren. Not a summons delivered by courier. The magistrates themselves come up out of the mountain, masked, nine of them, and they go to the address written in the ledger, and they take what is owed." He paused. "Your address is written in the ledger. It has been updated annually. By me."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"There must be a procedure," Wren said. His voice came out level, professional, the voice he used for difficult clients. "There's always a procedure. Redemption, substitution, refinancing—I've recorded all of them. Souls get bought back. You've told me so yourself, a dozen times, when widows wept on those stairs."

"And I told them the truth, as far as it went." Corvess spread his hands. "Yes. Debts can be settled. But hear me, and hear me carefully, because I am about to give you the only kindness available in this room: the pledge against your soul was not made by you. It was made *about* you, by parties I am not free to name, for consideration I am not free to describe, under terms sealed below. A man can redeem his own contract. A man cannot easily redeem a contract written on his body by someone else. The court will hear arguments. The court hears everything—it is what the court is *for*. But the collection comes regardless, on the night the moon completes, and what happens after the collection begins depends on papers neither of us holds."

"Then who holds them?"

Corvess stood. He straightened his grey robes with two precise tugs, and for a moment he looked less like a guild master and more like what Wren had slowly begun to suspect he was: a clerk of enormous power who had mistaken administration for wisdom for so long that the difference no longer interested him.

"That," he said, "is the question you have thirty-nine days to answer. My advice—" and here his voice dropped into something almost human, almost sorry, "—is that you stop being invisible. Whatever shield your forgettable-ness has been, it ends the moment the magistrates knock on your door. A man about to be collected has nothing left to protect by hiding. Spend these weeks learning who sold you, and why, and what they received. Because I promise you, Wren Sterling: whoever they were, they are still alive, or the contract would have collapsed decades ago. The court does not hold liens on dead men's children. Only living ones."

He climbed the stairs. The door closed above with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Wren sat alone in the cellar with the ledger open and the candle burning down toward its final inch, and he did not move for a long time. Above him, the city slept—the Chancellor in his bed, the guards at their posts, the neighbors who would soon line up in the square to denounce one another, all of them dreaming in a kingdom that had bought its survival with a foundling's soul and filed the miracle under *weather*.

Thirty-nine days.

He dipped his pen. It was habit, pure habit, the reflex of nine years: when something true and terrible happened, record it. He pulled a fresh page toward him, wrote the date, wrote *Thirteenth moon: 39 days remaining*, and then, beneath it, in his small precise hand, the only resolution available to a man whose past had finally caught up with his present:

*Find the seller.*

The candle went out. In the dark, the ledger lay open on the desk, his name gleaming faintly where the wax seal had pressed the page, and somewhere far beneath the city—beneath the cellar, beneath the foundations, beneath the drowned twin of the salt market and the long cold throat of the mountain—a candle was lit, or so the guild's lore claimed, for every soul in holding. Thirty years, that flame had burned for him. Patient as interest accruing. Waiting for the moon to finish its count.

He began, in the dark, to plan.
