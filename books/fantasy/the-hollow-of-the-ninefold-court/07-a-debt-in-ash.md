## 7. A Debt in Ash

The poisoner had used too much.

That was the first thing Ilse Vane noticed, standing over the body of Master of Coin Aldery Fenwick in his own study, and it was the thing that would keep her awake for the next six nights. Nightshade was a patient poison. A careful poison. Two drops in a cup of mulled wine, and a man simply grew tired, lay down, and did not wake—indistinguishable from a heart giving out after a rich supper. That was how it was done properly. That was how it was done by anyone who had read even one page of the inquisitorial primer on toxins, which was required reading for every clerk above the third rank.

But Fenwick's lips were blackened. His fingertips were stained violet to the second knuckle. He had swallowed enough belladonna to drop a horse, and he had done it at a banquet with forty witnesses, which meant either that the poisoner was an idiot or that the poisoner wanted him seen dead. Wanted it loud. Wanted it *attributed*.

Ilse crouched beside the body and did not touch it. The physician's assistants hovered at the door with their canvas stretcher and their tallow-smell, waiting for her leave, and she gave it eventually, but not before she had looked long enough at the dead man's mouth to form the question that would follow her through the week like a debt collector:

Who benefits from a poisoning that everyone can see?

The court's answer arrived within a day, as the court's answers always did, swift and self-serving: the food-taster. Wren Sterling, who had tasted the Chancellor's table for nine years without incident, whose very competence made him the only man in the palace with regular access to the kitchens *and* a professional obligation to notice what passed through them. The rumor moved through the marble corridors the way water moves through limestone—slowly, invisibly, carving channels—and by the second morning three separate informants had come to her office to suggest, with the elaborate casualness of people reading from a script, that perhaps the Inquisitor should look closely at the taster.

She looked. That was her work; looking was the whole of it.

What she found was a file so clean it sang.

Nine years of service records, each signed and countersigned. A lodging above a chandler's shop in Saltmarket Ward, rent paid punctually, neighbors interviewed twice yearly by the ward watch who remembered nothing about him except that he was quiet and polite and unremarkable. No debts. No vices anyone could name—not drink, not dice, not women or men or the church's particular vices of pride. No letters. No visitors. His mother deceased, his father unknown, no siblings recorded. He had entered the Chancellery's service at nineteen with papers in perfect order and had been, in every way the crown measured a man, a blank page.

Ilse sat in her office with the file open before her and the rain writing its grey grammar down the window, and she thought: *no one is this empty.*

Emptiness was a construction. She knew that from her years working the provinces before the purge, before the Proclamation turned her into a huntress of her own kind's shadows. The smugglers of Vell's Landing kept emptiness like this—a surface with nothing written on it because everything was written underneath. The spies the old king had employed kept files like this. And the gifted, when they wished to survive among the ungifted, learned to keep themselves like this: sanded smooth, unnamed, forgettable. She had known such people once. She had grown up beside one.

She closed the file. Then, instead of carrying it to the archive room where closed files went to be forgotten in the proper order, she set it on the corner of her desk, beneath the lamp, where she would see it every morning.

*Telling herself she was being thorough.* She heard her own mind make the excuse and let it stand, the way one lets a child's lie stand because correcting it would cost more than the lie was worth.

* * *

The nightshade itself told a stranger story than the rumors did.

Ilse spent two days in the apothecaries' quarter doing what she privately called the low work—the work beneath an inquisitor's rank, the work of asking shopkeepers questions and writing down their answers and comparing the answers against each other until the seams showed. Belladonna was not difficult to obtain; it grew wild along the city walls and in every widow's medicinal garden. But refined nightshade, the concentrated tincture that blackened a man's lips in a single swallow, was another matter. It required a still, a knowledge of dosage, and a supplier willing to sell to someone who asked no questions.

Three suppliers in the city sold refined tincture. Two of them kept honest ledgers, and their honest ledgers accounted for every dram. The third was a crooked little establishment behind the salt market called the Sign of the Scales, and its ledger was a masterpiece of the genre: columns that balanced perfectly, entries in a hand so uniform it might have been printed, dates that never contradicted one another.

It was, in other words, exactly like Wren Sterling's file.

Ilse stood in the shop while the proprietor—a soft man with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who had been threatened competently at some point in his past—explained that yes, he sold tincture, no, he could not recall to whom, yes, his ledger was complete, no, there was nothing unusual about any recent sale. He said all this in the tone of a man reciting lines he had been given, and Ilse listened with the particular patience she reserved for liars who were lying under duress rather than for profit, because those liars were usually more frightened of the person who had scripted them than of any inquisitor in the realm.

"Your ledger balances," she said pleasantly. "To the grain."

"I take pride in my accounts, Inquisitor."

"So do I." She let the silence stretch until it creaked. "Mine have a way of finding the entries other people miss."

Something flickered behind the soft man's eyes—not guilt, she noted. Fear, and beneath the fear, something worse: pity. As though he were sorry for her, sorry for what she was about to walk into, sorry the way one is sorry for a character in a tragedy who has not yet read the last act.

He sold her nothing. He told her nothing. But when she left, walking back through the salt market with its white drifts of commerce and its fish-stink and its shouting porters, she carried with her the shape of an absence: someone had bought nightshade here, recently, in quantity, and the purchase had been erased so cleanly that only its erasure remained.

And the erasure had been done by a professional. By someone who understood ledgers the way a surgeon understands flesh.

She stopped in the middle of the market square, letting the crowd part around her the way water parts around a piling, and admitted the thought she had been refusing since the first night over Fenwick's body.

The hand that cleaned that ledger was the same kind of hand that had cleaned Wren Sterling's life. Meticulous. Invisible. *Balanced.*

Either Wren Sterling had poisoned the Master of Coin and then laundered the evidence through his own immaculate record—or someone else had done both, using the same craft, and left Wren Sterling standing in the open like a door left unlocked on purpose.

Protected, or framed. The two possibilities wore identical clothes, and only one of them could be undressed by asking the man himself.

* * *

She chose the chandler's shop on a Tuesday evening, in the blue hour when the ward lamps were lit and the streets emptied of respectable people. She came alone, out of uniform, in a plain wool cloak, which was itself a transgression—an inquisitor did not conduct interviews off the record, did not climb strangers' stairs unannounced, did not knock on doors with her hood up like a common thief of secrets. Every step of the staircase she told herself this was procedure. An informal preliminary inquiry. A fishing expedition dressed as a courtesy call. The words sounded thin even inside her own skull.

Wren Sterling opened the door before she finished knocking.

He was smaller than his file photographs suggested, or perhaps it was that the photographs had captured his face and missed his quality, which was a kind of deliberate dimness, a candle turned low. He looked at her and she watched him recognize her—not with surprise, which would have been natural, but with the weary resignation of a man who has been expecting a visit for years and is only relieved it has finally come, the way a debtor is relieved when the collector's knock finally sounds.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said. "You'd better come in. The stairs are narrow but they hold."

"You know who I am."

"Everyone knows who you are." He stepped aside. "You've been watching me across the square for eleven days. I count things. It's an occupational failing."

The room above the chandler's shop was clean and nearly bare: a bed made with military corners, a table, two chairs, a single shelf of books that proved, when she glanced at them, to be almanacs and accounting manuals and a battered volume of agricultural law. No letters. No keepsakes. The emptiness of the file, made physical. Except—

Except for the smell. Underneath the tallow-draft from below and the cold ash of the hearth, there was something else, faint as a memory: candle smoke of a different character, sweeter, laced with something resinous she almost recognized. And on the table, half-hidden beneath a folded cloth, the corner of a book far too large and too old to be an almanac.

He saw her see it. To his credit—he would remember later, if he remembered anything, that she had given him the chance—he did not move to cover it.

"Master of Coin Fenwick," she said, taking the chair he offered, "was poisoned with refined belladonna. Enough to kill him four times over, administered publicly, at a banquet where forty people watched him die badly. You taste for the Chancellor's table. You had access to the kitchens. The court has already decided it was you."

"The court decides quickly," Wren said. "It saves time."

"Do you want to know what I decided?"

He folded his hands on the table. They were steady hands, she noted. Taster's hands, trained to steadiness. "I imagine you haven't decided anything yet, Inquisitor. I imagine you're a person who decides late and correctly. That's why you're here at night, alone, out of uniform, instead of at dawn with soldiers."

The observation landed with a precision that raised the hair on her arms. This was not the flat, forgettable man from the file. This was someone reading *her* the way she read ledgers—finding the seams, the erasures, the entry beneath the entry.

"Someone bought refined nightshade from a shop behind the salt market," she said. "The purchase was removed from the seller's ledger afterward. Removed expertly. By someone who understands double-entry bookkeeping better than the man who keeps the books." She leaned forward. "I've spent two days trying to decide whether that describes you protecting yourself, Wren Sterling, or someone else destroying you. And I've come to ask you which it is, off the record, because on the record I am required to arrest you for the murder of Aldery Fenwick, and once I do that, whatever the truth is will die in a cell before it ever reaches daylight."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp between them hissed softly. She watched him weigh her, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever he decided in the next ten seconds would decide something in her too—that she had already walked further along this road than duty permitted, and that some part of her had known it since she set his file on the corner of her desk instead of filing it away.

"It wasn't me," he said.

"Prove it."

"I can't." And there it was—the terrible honesty of a man with no proof, the nakedness of it. "I can't prove it, because proving it means showing you things that would get us both burned, and you don't want to see them. Believe me. You think you do, but you don't."

"Try me."

"No." He shook his head, and for the first time his composure cracked, just at the edge, just enough to show the exhaustion underneath—nine years of it, ten, a lifetime of tasting other people's food and keeping other people's secrets. "Inquisitor. Ilse." Her name in his mouth startled them both; he had never been given it, and she had never offered it, and neither of them remarked on it. "I didn't kill Fenwick. I don't know who did. But I know that whoever did wanted it blamed on me, or wanted me destroyed in the blaming, and I know that if you write down tonight's conversation, you'll be signing my death warrant and your own complicity in it, and the worst part—" his voice dropped, and she leaned in despite herself— "the worst part is that you already know I'm telling the truth. Don't you? You knew it before you climbed the stairs. That's why you came alone."

She should have denied it. She was trained to deny it; there were exercises for exactly this, the moment when a suspect turned the lens around, and she had performed them flawlessly in examinations twenty years ago. Instead she sat in the narrow chair in the narrow room above the chandler's shop and said nothing, and her silence was the most damning testimony she had ever given.

Because he was right. She had known. From the first night, from the blackened lips and the theatrical dose, some part of her had known that Wren Sterling was not a murderer but a mark—a man being framed with the same meticulous, invisible, balanced craftsmanship that had built his empty life. And she had come here tonight not to catch him but to warn him, or to be warned, or simply to sit across from the one person in the city whose emptiness matched hers, whose whole existence was a ledger with the true entries torn out.

"I have to write something," she said at last. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, as though someone else were speaking through her mouth. "If I return with nothing, my superiors will send someone who returns with something. There are inquisitors who would burn this building on suspicion alone and call the ash evidence."

"Then write what you have to write."

So she took out her notebook—her official notebook, the one with the crown's seal pressed into the leather—and she wrote. She wrote the interview as it should have happened: the suspect questioned, the suspect evasive, the suspect dismissed for lack of evidence, the inquiry continuing elsewhere. She wrote a fiction in the exact style of fact, and her hand did not shake until the end, until the signature line, where it shook so badly she had to press the pen down hard to make the letters legible.

Then she tore out the real page—the notes she had actually taken, the observations, the ledger-seams, the truth—and held it over the lamp.

She hesitated. One breath. Two.

Burning testimony was treason. Not the grand treason of armies and assassins, but the quiet kind, the rot-in-the-beam kind, the kind that brought down inquisitors years later when some clerk found a discrepancy, some archivist found a draft, some enemy found a witness. She had seen it prosecuted. She had *conducted* prosecutions of it. She knew the procedure: the tribunal, the stripping of rank, the pyre if the destruction of evidence touched a capital case, and this was a capital case, this was murder at court, this was everything the Proclamation had made her to prevent—

Her sister's face rose in her memory, young and terrified and certain that the law would distinguish, that someone would ask the right question, that the machinery would stop before the fire started. Ilse had stood silent then because the law was the law and the fire was the fire and she had believed, with all the strength of a fifteen-year-old's cowardice, that she was powerless. She had joined the inquisition afterward precisely so that she would never again be powerless, never again be the silent sister, never again watch the machinery run over a person she knew to be innocent.

And now the machinery was in her hands, and it was running toward Wren Sterling, and the only lever that could stop it was a match.

The page caught. Light bloomed orange between them, and in its glow she saw Wren Sterling watching her with an expression she would spend many nights afterward trying to name—something between grief and gratitude and a horror that mirrored her own, because he understood what she had just done, understood it better than she did, understood that she had stepped off the solid ground of her whole life and was now falling in the company of a stranger.

The ash drifted down onto the table like black snow. She brushed it into her palm, methodically, and carried it to his cold hearth, and let it go among the older ashes where no clerk would ever find it.

When she turned, he was standing, and for a moment she thought—feared—he meant to thank her, and she could not have borne thanks; thanks would have made it real in a way that ash could not undo.

"Don't," he said instead, quietly, reading her again, damn him, always reading her. "Don't thank me, don't apologize, don't explain. Just go home, Inquisitor. And be careful." He paused at the window, pulling the curtain aside a finger's width, checking the street below with the reflexes of a man who had checked streets his entire life. "Because whoever killed Fenwick isn't finished. And whoever they are—they're better at this than both of us."

She went home through the wet streets with the crown's seal pressing against her ribs like a brand, and she did not sleep, and in the morning she set his file back on the corner of her desk, beneath the lamp, and told herself—thinner than ever, thinner than paper—that she was still being thorough.

But thoroughness, she was beginning to understand, had never been the reason. And somewhere across the city, in a cellar office she could not yet imagine, the oldest ledger in the Ninefold Court waited with an entry flagged in royal seal, and the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its due date, and the hand that had erased the nightshade purchase was already moving toward its next erasure—one that would not leave Wren Sterling's name intact, or Ilse Vane's either.
