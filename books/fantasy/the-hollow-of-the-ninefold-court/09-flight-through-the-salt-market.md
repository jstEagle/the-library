## 9. Flight Through the Salt Market

The ledger was warm.

Wren noticed it as he reached for it—warm, when the cellar office held its perpetual chill like a held breath. The oldest book of the Ninefold Court had been cold for nine years, sitting in its iron cradle beneath the counting desk, and tonight it lay against his palm like a sleeping animal. He should have read that as an omen. Instead he thought only: *the ink is fresh.*

He had written in it that morning. That alone should have frightened him. In nine years he had entered trades into the working ledgers—the widow's grief, the soldier's years, the hundred small impossibilities that passed through the guild's hands—but never once into the oldest book. The oldest book wrote itself. It recorded what could not be unsaid, and it did so in Corvess's presence or not at all.

Tonight it had opened itself to no page at all. It simply lay there, breathing warmth, and Wren understood with the particular clarity of a man standing on ice that has already cracked beneath him: *it knows something I don't.*

He heard the boots on the stairs before he heard anything else. Six pairs. The guild's stairwell was built to swallow sound—it swallowed grief, it swallowed years, it would certainly swallow footsteps—and yet he heard them, because they wanted him to hear. They came down slowly, deliberately, each step placed like a word in a sentence meant to be understood.

Wren's hands moved before his mind finished the thought. Nine years of being forgettable had trained his body into a creature of exits. He took the oldest ledger from its cradle and slid it inside his coat, against his chest, where it pressed warm as a second heartbeat. Then he snuffed the lamp and stepped through the door behind the shelving—the one that smelled of brine and old money—and into the dark passage that ran beneath the salt market.

Behind him, the office door burst inward. Light bloomed. A voice, official and flat, announced itself to an empty room:

"Inquisition. By decree of the Crown. Remain where you are."

*Remain where you are.* As if the room were full. As if the empty chair at the counting desk were a man who might obey.

Wren did not remain anywhere. He ran.

* * *

The salt market at midnight was a cathedral of white.

By day it roared—hundreds of stalls under sagging canvas, merchants crying the purity of their wares, children darting between cartwheels while their mothers haggled over grains measured by the pinch. By night it became something else: a plain of mounded salt stretching away under the moonlight, drifts of it piled against the shuttered stalls, the whole space silent and pale and glittering like the inside of a frozen lung.

Wren knew every inch of it. He had walked it for nine years, head down, unremarkable, learning the routes a forgettable man takes. There were four ways out of the market and three ways to make yourself invisible within it, and he had used them all. But never running. Running was memorable. Running drew eyes, and eyes drew inquisitors, and inquisitors drew fire.

He ran anyway.

The first shout came from the market's eastern gate—a whistle, sharp, answered by another from the west. They had ringed it. Of course they had. Corvess had sold him, and Corvess did nothing halfway; the guildmaster would have described not just Wren's face but his habits, his gait, his preference for the northern exit near the tallow-chandler's stall. The guild that had sheltered him for nine years had handed him to the pyre with the same tidy efficiency it applied to all its transactions. *The books must balance.* Wren had refused to balance them, and now he was the debt.

He cut south instead, toward the fishmongers' row where the salt lay thickest and deepest, drifted against the stalls in walls waist-high. Behind him, boots crunched. Lantern light swept the white expanse in long yellow blades. And above it all, rising over the market like smoke from a distant pyre, came the sound he had dreaded since the proclamation was nailed to the cathedral doors:

"By order of the Crown! The gifted are commanded to surrender themselves! Shelter them and burn with them!"

They weren't shouting for the crowd. There was no crowd at midnight. They were shouting for *him*—for the pleasure of it, perhaps, or because fear made the quarry faster.

A lantern flared to his left. Two figures in grey-and-silver livery, hexed nets coiled at their belts like sleeping serpents. Wren dropped behind a salt drift and pressed himself into the cold white mass, feeling it bite into his palms, his cheek, the hollows of his coat. Salt got into everything. Salt preserved. Somewhere in the back of his mind, the bookkeeper observed that he was, at this moment, being pickled for posterity.

The lantern passed. He counted to ten, then rose and ran again—south, south, toward the drainage arches beneath the chandlers' row, where the market floor gave way to the old channels that carried melt-water down toward the river. If he could reach the arches—

The net caught him across the shoulders.

It came from behind and above—he never saw who threw it—and it settled over him with the soft, almost tender weight of a thing designed not to strike but to embrace. For half a heartbeat it was merely rope, wet and heavy. Then the hex woke.

Fire. Not heat exactly—heat he could have borne—but *burning*, a white agony that sank through cloth and skin straight to whatever it was the inquisitors believed lived in him. The gift he kept hidden, the tongue that tasted truth in every dish, the quiet second sense that had let him survive nine years at court—it lit up along the net's every knot like a fuse, and Wren screamed, or thought he screamed, though the sound seemed to happen somewhere far above him.

He tore at the net. His gloves smoked. The salt around him hissed where drops of blood fell, tiny red stars blooming in the white. He had a single coherent thought, absurd and precise, the thought of a man who has spent his life among ledgers: *this will be entered. This pain will be entered somewhere, in some book, and someone will draw a line beneath it.*

Then he pulled, and the net tore—not broke, but tore, as if the hex found less to burn in him than it expected, as if the part of him it sought had been sold long ago and the net could only grip the remainder. He stumbled free, leaving strips of rope and strips of skin behind him, and ran.

The arches swallowed him. Dark water, knee-deep, cold enough to make him gasp after the burning. Behind him the shouts multiplied; ahead, the channel bent down toward the river and freedom, or something like it. He ran with the water dragging at his legs and the ledger pressed against his chest, still warm, warmer now, as if it approved.

At the last bend he stopped—one breath, one look back. Lanterns swarmed the market above, tiny and furious as wasps. Blood trailed behind him in the water, thinning, diluting, becoming no one's blood in particular.

*Good*, thought the man who had spent nine years being no one in particular. *Let it be no one's.*

Then he turned and waded into the dark, and the mountain, far off to the north, stood black against the stars like a door left open.

* * *

Ilse Vane arrived forty minutes late, and she would think about those forty minutes for the rest of her life.

She had spent them doing nothing actionable—checking the registry, confirming the warrant's seal, telling herself that procedure mattered, that a raid done properly was a raid done right. She told herself this while her horse ate the miles two abreast with Sergeant Ommel, whose enthusiasm for the night's work she found herself disliking more with every league. Ommel talked about the net-hexes the whole way. New pattern, he said, woven by the guild-craftsmen at Vhal, tuned to find the gift wherever it hid. *Burns them clean,* he said, and smiled.

She arrived at the cellar office to find it torn open like a dissected animal. Shelving splintered. Ledgers stacked for confiscation, their spines cracked, their pages fanned by careless gloved hands. The lamp lay broken in its own oil. And on the counting desk, in the iron cradle built to hold the oldest book of the Ninefold Court, there was nothing.

"The master's records say a keeper worked here," Ommel said, consulting his papers with the satisfaction of a man reading a menu. "Name of Sterling. Food-taster at court, if you can believe it. Corvess himself gave us the description." He grinned. "Quiet little fellow. You'd never think."

"No," Ilse said quietly. "You wouldn't."

She walked the room the way she walked every room—slowly, reading it like testimony. The chair pushed back in haste. The snuffed lamp: he'd heard them coming, which meant he'd had seconds, which meant he'd chosen the ledger over escape time and paid for it in seconds. A man who chose a book over his own neck was a man who knew what was in the book.

In the passage behind the shelving, she found the blood.

It began as smears on the doorframe—handprints, dragged, desperate—and continued out onto the salt market floor, where it told its story in red script across the white. Drops at first, then a spray, then a wide scorched arc where something had burned and bled at once. Kneeling, she touched the edge of the scorch mark. Still warm. Still faintly humming with residual hex-work, the pattern frayed, torn through rather than dispelled.

Torn through. She had read the reports from Vhal herself. No ungifted man tore through a Vhal-pattern net; the hex ignored them entirely, passed through them like smoke. No gifted man tore through it either—they burned until they stopped moving. What she was looking at was impossible: a man caught in the hex and surviving it, bleeding from it, *incomplete* in some way the net could not fully grasp.

She looked at the trail of blood leading down toward the drainage arches, and she knew.

Not suspected. Not deduced. *Knew*, the way you know your own name in the dark. The food-taster who survived poisonings too calmly. The file too neatly empty, still sitting on her desk because she hadn't been able to bring herself to file it. The man she had interviewed and found nothing in—nothing except the terrible, meticulous absence of anything at all. Wren Sterling had run through a hexed net that should have killed him, and he had left his blood behind like a signature, and every instinct she owned told her that the signature was his.

"Sir?" Ommel called from the doorway. "Trail goes into the channels. We can have dogs on it by dawn—"

"Post riders at the river crossings," Ilse said. Her voice came out level. It cost her something to make it level. "Seal the mountain road. No one enters or leaves the high passes without warrant."

"And the trail?"

She looked at the blood a moment longer. Red on white. A wound that should have been fatal, walking away under its own power, carrying a book that nine masked magistrates would want back.

"Leave the trail," she said.

Ommel blinked. "Ma'am?"

"I'll follow it myself." She stood, brushing the salt from her knee, and did not look at him. "You'll hold the market. Log everything. Nothing leaves this site—including the confiscated ledgers—until I've catalogued them personally. Is that understood?"

He understood badly, but he understood. When his boots had faded up the stairwell, Ilse knelt again beside the scorch mark and let herself feel, for exactly five seconds, what she actually felt: not triumph, not duty, but a fierce and terrible relief that the blood had gone north. Toward the mountain. Away from the pyres.

Then she stood, and put her duty back on like a coat, and went to find her horse.

Because here was the thing she would admit to no one, least of all herself: she had known for weeks. Since the interview. Since the file that stayed on her desk. Since the night she burned a page of testimony rather than enter it into the record, an act that still sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. She had known, and she had waited, and some cowardly part of her had hoped the crown's hunters would never force the question.

Now the question had blood on it, and it was running toward the mountain, and she was going to follow it—not to arrest it, though she wore the warrant that said she must. She was going to follow it because there was one question she had never asked him, the question no inquisitor asks, the question she had needed answered since her sister's ash went up a chimney twenty years ago:

*What was taken from you?*

Ilse Vane rode north through the freezing dark, following a trail of blood that was already fading, toward a shrine she didn't yet know existed, carrying a warrant she was beginning to suspect she would never use.

Behind her, the salt market glittered under the dying moon—white, immaculate, preserved.

Like something pickled for posterity.

Like a page waiting to be written.
