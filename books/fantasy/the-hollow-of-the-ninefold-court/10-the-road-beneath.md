## 10. The Road Beneath

### Wren

The mountain did not appear all at once. It assembled itself out of the road the way a debt assembles itself out of small concessions—one day you look up and it is there, gray and enormous, wearing snow like a collar, and you cannot remember a time when it was not waiting for you.

Wren had been walking for six days.

The oldest ledger rode under his coat against his ribs, bound in calfskin gone soft as cloth, heavier than any book had a right to be. He had learned on the first night not to sleep with it outside his shirt. On the second night he had learned to sleep in ditches rather than inns, because inns kept registers, and registers were read by people whose business was reading them. On the third night the hex-burn along his left side had finally stopped weeping. The net's mark was still there—a lattice of pale scars across his hip and flank, cold to the touch even in summer heat, as though the magic had left a window open in him and something on the other side breathed through it.

He had stopped pretending, somewhere around the fourth day, that he wasn't gifted. The net had settled that question. Whatever immunity he had built dish by dish over nine years—whatever careful, unglamorous craft let him swallow poison and smile—it was not what the net had found in him. It had found the other thing. The thing his mother had signed away before he could walk, or perhaps the thing she'd left behind in him when she did, a residue, a remainder, a soul-shaped absence that magic could grip where flesh could not.

He didn't know which. He only knew it burned.

Now the road climbed through pine country, and the air smelled of resin and old stone, and in the blue distance the shrine appeared—first as a suggestion, then as a fact: a low stone shelter crouched at a bend in the pilgrims' path, roofed with slates furred green, its doorway dark as a held breath. Travelers' shrines dotted every high road in the Nine Provinces. This one had no name carved above the door. That was unusual. Named shrines belonged to named gods, and named gods kept accounts.

Wren stood at the edge of the tree line and looked at it for a long time.

He was not a fool. He knew how pursuit worked; he had spent nine years watching inquisitors work from the comfortable remove of the Chancellor's breakfast table. They did not chase. They waited. A man running from the capital would need food, water, bandages for a burn that festered in the heat, and there was exactly one place on this stretch of road where all three could be had.

She would be there already. Or she would come. Either way, the shrine was a trap, and he walked into it anyway, because the alternative was the mountain's colder traps, and because—though he would have denied this if anyone had asked, and no one would ask, since no one knew where he was—he was tired of running alone.

Inside, the shrine was one room, dim, smelling of tallow and rain. A stone altar bore the usual offerings: a stub of candle, a clay cup of milk going sour, three copper coins arranged with a precision that suggested someone had wanted very badly to be forgiven for something. A water jug sat in the corner, sweating. Bread wrapped in linen. Bandages, clean, folded—the kind of folded that meant military hands.

And sitting on the bench against the far wall, her sword across her knees, her gray cloak still dripping from the ford two miles back, Inquisitor Ilse Vane looked up at him with an expression he could not read, which frightened him more than any expression he could have read.

"Wren Sterling," she said.

"Inquisitor Vane." His voice came out steadier than he felt. He made himself step fully into the doorway, so that the light fell on him and she could see his empty hands. "You're a long way from your desk."

"You're a long way from your cellar." She didn't move. Neither did the sword, which somehow was worse than if she'd drawn it. "Sit down before you fall down. You're grey around the mouth."

"I've been told I have a forgettable face."

"Your face isn't the problem." Something moved behind her eyes—something tired. "Sit."

He sat, because his legs had quietly decided the matter without consulting him. The bench was cold stone. He unwrapped the bread because she nodded at it, and ate because his body insisted, and drank because the water tasted of iron and mountains, and the whole time she watched him with the particular patience of someone who has already won and is deciding what winning means.

"You know," Wren said finally, "if you'd wanted me alive, you've got me. If you'd wanted me dead, you had six days of empty road and no witnesses."

"Yes," Ilse said. "I've been thinking about that."

* * *

### Ilse

Ilse had rehearsed this arrest eleven times.

Not aloud—she was not a woman who talked to herself, whatever the junior inquisitors believed about the hours she spent alone with her files. But she had laid it out in her mind the way she laid out everything: the approach, the words, the binding, the walk back down the mountain with the taster's wrists in hex-cuffs and the ledger confiscated under warrant, and then the tribunal, and then the pyre, and then the paperwork, which was always the worst part, because the paperwork required her to write *guilty* in her own hand, and her own hand had begun, lately, to hesitate.

Eleven rehearsals, and in none of them had he looked like this.

Wren Sterling sat on the pilgrims' bench eating bread like a man who had forgotten food existed, and the hex-scars showed when his coat shifted—a white lattice over his hip, and she had seen that pattern before, on the bodies of the gifted after the nets came down. He should not have survived it. Most didn't. The nets gripped whatever the soul left exposed, and they gripped until there was nothing left to grip, and here was this small, quiet, deliberately dull man with the mark spread across him like frost, drinking sour-smelling water and making dry remarks, alive.

Her sister had not been alive, at the end. Her sister had screamed. Ilse had been fourteen and standing in the square with her mother's hand crushing hers, and she had learned that day that silence was survivable, and she had built a whole life on the lesson, and the life was called the hunt, and the hunt was called justice, and none of it had ever once felt like justice, but it had felt like order, and order was the next best thing.

She watched him eat. She thought: *I could take him now.* The cuffs were in her pack. He was wounded, exhausted, half-starved; it would take less effort than the interview in the Chancellor's antechamber had taken.

Instead she heard herself say: "Why did you swallow it?"

He looked up. Pear juice, nightshade, the Chancellor's table, the whole morning three weeks ago now—she saw him understand, and she saw him decide whether to lie, and she saw the decision fail.

"Because spitting it out would have told everyone what I am," Wren said.

"And what are you?"

"A dead man walking toward a mountain." He set down the bread. Under the grime and the exhaustion his eyes were startlingly level. "You already know I'm gifted, Inquisitor. The net told you. So ask me the question you actually came up here to ask."

Ilse's hand had drifted, without her permission, to rest on the pack where the cuffs were. She made herself move it to the bench instead, flat on the cold stone, visible.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like pages turning.

"What was taken from you?" she asked.

It was not the question in any of her eleven rehearsals. It was not even, strictly speaking, a question an inquisitor was permitted to ask; the catechism ran the other way. *What do you confess? What did you steal? Whose years, whose grief, whose name?* The hunt asked what the gifted had *taken*. It had never once, in her memory, asked what had been taken *from* them.

But she had read his file forty times. Too-neat nothing, page after page—a man sanded down to smoothness—and then the cellar office, and the ledgers, and the blood in the salt market that she had knelt beside and known instantly, the way you know handwriting, was his. And underneath the warrant in her breast pocket, folded so many times its creases had gone soft, a single page of testimony she should have delivered to her superiors and instead had burned in her own hearth while her hands shook.

She had burned a confession. She was an inquisitor who had burned a confession. Whatever she was now, she was standing in a shrine asking questions the catechism forbade, and she found, to her horror, that she needed the answer more than she needed her warrant.

Wren stared at her.

"No one's asked me that," he said.

"I know," Ilse said. "That's why I'm asking."

* * *

### Wren

He told her everything.

He had planned never to tell anyone anything, ever again; telling was how the ledger-keepers described the unforgivable trades—*she told her grief, he told his years, the family told their daughter's name*—and Wren had lived his whole adult life on the strict arithmetic of silence. But the shrine was dim and cold and smelled of tallow, and the woman across from him had asked the forbidden question with the voice of someone paying for it out of her own pocket, and the words came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked jar: all at once, and unstoppable, and leaving him lighter and emptier than he expected.

He told her about the honeyed pear, and the nightshade folding itself sweetly into the syrup, and the choice he'd made in the space of one heartbeat—to swallow, to burn, to stay ordinary.

He told her about the cellar office beneath the chandler's shop, the ledgers of impossible things, the widow who sold her grief and walked out smiling and hollow, the soldier who bought back three lost years and wept because they fit him wrong. He told her about the entry flagged with the royal seal—*one soul, held beneath, due at the thirteenth moon*—and how he had carried that line in his head for weeks like a splinter before he dug deep enough to find it had his name on the other end. Entered the day of his birth. By his mother.

"My mother," he said, and the word came out strange, a coin from a country he'd never visited. "I don't—I have a first memory of her hands, and after that, nothing. I was raised in a foundling house that received an anonymous stipend every quarter for eighteen years. I thought I was lucky. I thought—" He laughed, and it hurt. "I thought I was forgettable by nature. Turns out it was purchased. Someone paid good money to make sure no one ever looked twice at me. And it worked. For nine years it worked beautifully."

"The crown bought a famine's end," Ilse said slowly. She had gone very still. "Thirty years ago. Before the regency. My grandmother used to sing about it—the Winter Without Bread, ended in a single season. Everyone said it was a miracle."

"It was a contract." Wren touched the ledger through his coat. "A royal-blooded soul pledged as surety. The pledge was made by a regent without royal assent—that matters, I think, I'm still reading—but the Court doesn't care about *should*, only about *due*. And the due date is nineteen days from now."

"And your mother traded your soul to save the realm." Ilse's voice was careful, the way voices are careful around wounds. "And then disappeared."

"She didn't disappear." He hadn't meant to say it yet. He said it anyway. "I've been reading the old entries. The founder of the Ninefold Court—the one Corvess claims died before the guild had walls—the contracts all run through her seal, and the seal never lapsed, and the Court below sustains her because if she dies the crown's collateral collapses and every trade built on it unravels. She's alive, Inquisitor. She's been alive this whole time, under the mountain, holding my soul the way you hold something fragile in a house full of people who want to drop it." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it and kept going. "She didn't sell me. She *guarded* me. Badly, maybe. At a price I never agreed to. But she guarded me."

Silence. The candle on the altar guttered and righted.

When Ilse finally spoke, her voice had changed—stripped of the interview-room polish, down to something rawer. "Corvess sold your location to us. To me. You know that."

"I assumed."

"He didn't sell it because you're gifted. Gifted is routine; we have carts for gifted." She looked at the fire's reflection in her own eyes, or perhaps just at the fire. "He sold it because you've read too much. Because you can trace the contracts back past the point where his story begins. There is no founder's death certificate, Wren, because there was no death. If you reach the hollow and stand in front of that court and start talking about voidable pledges—" She stopped.

"Then Corvess loses everything," Wren finished softly. "And the purge above ground loses its engine. Is that it? Is the hunt—is the registration, the pyres, the neighbors denouncing each other—is that the Court's enforcement arm? Is that what keeps the debtors in line?"

Ilse was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, it cost her visibly, word by word, like paying out rope over a cliff edge.

"When I joined the hunt, I thought I was building a wall between the fire and the next girl like my sister. Three years in, I noticed the warrants we served most often weren't for the dangerous ones. They were for debtors. People who'd defaulted on guild contracts—sold a year, sold a grief, missed a payment. The Court couldn't seize them openly, so it flagged them, and we collected." Her jaw tightened. "I told myself it was coincidence. Order requires a certain amount of not-looking. I've been not-looking professionally for eleven years."

"That's why you kept my file on your desk."

"That's why I kept your file on my desk." She almost smiled; it didn't survive. "Thoroughness. I'm very thorough."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, blue shadows pooling under the pines, and somewhere downslope a bird was making a sound like a hinge. Wren became aware, all at once, of how much the day had cost, of the hex-scars throbbing cold at his hip, of the nineteen days shrinking like a candle.

"So," he said. "Arrest me. Or don't."

* * *

### Ilse

She took out the cuffs.

She did it deliberately, wanting him to see, wanting herself to see—wanting to feel the weight of them and check whether duty still lived in her hands. Cold iron, guild-stamped, humming faintly with the same craft as the net that had marked him. She turned them over once.

Then she put them back in the pack.

"Not tonight," she said.

"Not ever?"

"Don't push it." But there was no edge in it, and both of them heard the absence. She stood and walked to the shrine door and looked out at the mountain filling half the sky, snow catching the last red light, and she thought about her sister's name, which she had not spoken aloud in twenty years, and about the page of testimony burned in her hearth, and about how a person becomes complicit one silence at a time and calls the accumulation a career.

"If I walk away," she said, not turning around, "you die on that mountain or under it, alone, and the ledger closes, and Corvess wins, and the hunt keeps grinding. If I arrest you, I deliver a man to a pyre for the crime of being stolen from, and I go back to my desk and my thoroughness, and the hunt keeps grinding." She turned. "There's a third option. I hate it. It's insubordination, probably treason, definitely career-ending, and I've slept badly enough as it is."

"Which is?"

"I walk with you. As far as the mountain's mouth. Provisionally." She held up a finger before he could speak. "Provisionally means I haven't decided anything except that I haven't decided. It means if you lie to me again—and you lie badly, Wren, it's honestly your least convincing skill—I reconsider. It means I am not your friend and I am not your rescuer and if the Court below turns out to be everything the stories say, I will make my own choices when we get there."

"Understood," Wren said. Then, because he apparently couldn't help himself: "You do realize that when your superiors learn an inquisitor escorted a fugitive soul-debtor to the Ninefold Court—"

"They'll learn nothing, because you're going to fix this before anyone has occasion to write it down." She shouldered her pack. "Nineteen days. We lose no more nights to weather. And Wren—"

"Yes?"

"You said the pledge was made by a regent without royal assent." Her eyes had sharpened into the thing that had made junior inquisitors afraid of her files. "Voidable. You said *voidable*, which means you've already found the flaw, and it needs testimony from both parties. Which means somewhere under that mountain, a woman who has spent thirty years being nobody is going to have to become somebody very loudly, very publicly, in front of nine masked magistrates." She picked up her sword. "So we'd better hope your mother wants to be found."

"She guarded me for thirty years," Wren said, standing, wincing as the scars pulled. "I'm choosing to believe she'll want to finish the job."

They banked the shrine's candle together—her hands, then his, an oddly domestic choreography for two enemies—and stepped out into the blue dusk. The mountain stood over them, patient as a creditor.

Behind them, in the dark shrine, the unnamed altar held its offerings to no god at all: sour milk, three coins, and the stub of a candle still warm, as if something down in the stone had briefly leaned close to listen.
