## 13. Terms of Surrender

The candle that measured Wren's soul burned low in its niche, and Ilse Vane could not stop watching it.

It was a stub of grey tallow, no taller than her thumb, set apart from the hundreds of others that lined the walls of the counting-chamber in the court beneath the mountain. Each flame, the clerks had explained with the patience of men describing weather to children, was an account. A life held in surety. A debt accruing in silence. The wax wept slowly down onto iron saucers, and somewhere in the dark above them—far above, where the sun still rose and set over a city of frightened people—each flame's burning meant something.

Wren's flame guttered when he breathed near it. She had noticed this on their second day below, and had said nothing, and had hated herself for noticing.

"You're staring again," Wren said. He sat cross-legged on the cold stone floor with the oldest ledger open across his knees, the one he'd carried out of the guild cellar pressed against his ribs through the whole flight from the salt market. He hadn't let it out of his sight since. "You do it when you think I'm reading. I can feel it."

"Inquisitors are trained to observe," Ilse said.

"And what have you observed?"

She considered lying to him. It would have been easy. Lying to Wren Sterling was like lying to a ledger—he caught the imbalance, but only if you gave him something to check.

"That your candle is shorter than yesterday," she said.

He went very still. Then he smiled, and it was the smile she had come to dread, the one he wore like a coat over the fear: mild, forgettable, the smile of a man who had spent nine years making himself into furniture. "Candles do that."

"Not these." She crossed the chamber and crouched beside him, close enough to see the salt-pale scar where the hexed net had kissed his shoulder. It had stopped weeping, but the flesh around it stayed bloodless, as if the burn had taken more than skin. "The clerk said last night that the account is active again. That the debt is being *weighed*. What does that mean, Wren?"

"It means," he said quietly, "that they've offered terms."

* * *

They had come to him at dusk—or what passed for dusk below the mountain, when the candle-clerks trimmed the wicks and the great doors of the counting-chamber groaned open for the day's reckonings. Nine magistrates, nine masks of pale wood, featureless as eggs, seated behind a table of black salt. They did not speak with voices so much as with the room; when they wished a thing known, every candle on every wall leaned toward it, and the words arrived already inside the listener's skull, wearing the listener's own tone.

*The debtor may reclaim his soul,* they had told Wren, while Ilse stood witness at the chamber's edge, uninvited and unchallenged, which itself should have frightened her more than it did. *The court does not hoard. The court balances.*

"And the balance?" Wren had asked.

*A soul of equal weight takes its place. That is the oldest term. There is no other.*

"Equal weight," Wren repeated. "Meaning what? Another gifted soul? An innocent?"

*Weight is not virtue,* said the room, and all the candles bowed. *A murderer may weigh less than a child. We take what weighs the same. The court has never once been cheated of its measure, and will not begin with you.*

Then the magistrates had risen, all nine together, and poured out of the chamber like smoke finding a draft, and the candles righted themselves, and Wren had stood in the middle of the floor looking like a man who had just been handed a knife and told the blade was his to hold or give away.

Ilse had watched him understand it. Watched him arrive at the arithmetic she herself had done before the masks finished speaking. Someone had to fill the ledger. Someone had to burn in his place, slow as tallow, year by year, until the crown's old debt was paid down or forgotten—and the court beneath the mountain had existed long enough to know that forgotten was the likelier ending.

"No," she had said, before he could say anything.

"I haven't asked you anything."

"You were going to ask me to help you find someone else. Some criminal. Some volunteer. You were going to make it tidy." She had heard her own voice crack on the last word and despised it. "There's no tidy version of this, Wren. Whoever goes into that book goes in forever."

He had looked at her then—not with the furniture-smile, not with the taster's blankness, but with something rawer, younger, the face of the man who had confessed everything to her at the mountain shrine while she sat with her hand on her sword hilt and her heart in her throat. *What was taken from you?* she had asked him. No one had ever asked him that. He had answered for two hours without stopping.

"I know," he'd said. "I know there's no tidy version. I'm trying to find the least cruel one."

And Ilse had nodded, and agreed to help him look, and begun, that same hour, negotiating his replacement in secret.

* * *

She told herself it was thoroughness. She was good at telling herself things; nine years in the inquisition taught a woman to keep two ledgers of her own, the one written in ink and the one written in whatever lived under the ribs.

The negotiations happened in the third hour after trimming, when the clerks withdrew to their alcoves and the chamber belonged to the candles. Ilse would stand before the black salt table, alone, hands clasped behind her back in the posture of inspection she had worn for a decade, and she would speak to the empty masks' chairs in a voice pitched low enough that no one sleeping in the guest cells could hear.

"I'm offering," she said, the first night. "Inquisitor Ilse Vane. I don't know my weight. Weigh me."

The candles had flickered—amusement, or contempt, she couldn't tell. *The inquisitor is not party to this debt.*

"The inquisitor is standing in your counting-house aiding your debtor. That makes me party to something."

*Sentiment is not weight.*

"Then weigh the sentiment too. Weigh all of it. My sister burned when I was fifteen because I stood in a square and held the torch-basket and said nothing. Weigh thirty years of saying nothing. Weigh what it costs a woman to hand over her sword." Her voice had gone ragged, then, and she had hated that too. "I am not offering you kindness. I am paying an arrears."

Silence. Long enough that she thought the negotiation was over before it began.

Then, softly, every flame in the chamber bending toward her like grass toward wind: *Return tomorrow. Bring proof of what you are willing to surrender.*

Proof. She had laughed, alone in that forest of small fires, and the laugh came out wrong. Proof of willingness. As if the inquisition hadn't spent her whole career teaching her that confession proves nothing—that any fool can kneel and recite contrition. They wanted the thing itself, the coin, not the promise of it.

She had walked back through the drowned-salt corridors to the guest cells, past the niche where Wren's candle wept its slow grey tears, and she had stood before it longer than she should have, doing sums she couldn't finish.

What did she have to surrender? Her commission—worth little now; Corvess's testimony had surely seen to that. Her name—the Vane name was already ash in the capital; her sister had seen to that, or the pyre had. Her freedom, her future, her—

Her hand had drifted, without permission, to the hilt of her sword.

Not the sword. Not yet. But she knew, the way she had known since the shrine, since *what was taken from you*, that the answer was going to be everything, and that the only question left was whether she told him first.

She decided—there, in the candlelight, with the smell of hot tallow in her nose—that she would not tell him first. Telling him first meant letting him argue. Letting him argue meant letting him win. And Wren Sterling had survived nine years at court by losing gracefully; he could lose this argument in his sleep.

Some sacrifices, she reasoned, were only possible if performed in secret. Anything done in the light invited gratitude, and gratitude was a debt, and debts were how all of this had started.

* * *

On the fourth day below, word came down from the surface, and it came the way all news came down: carried in the memory of a courier-candle, lit topside and snuffed, its final moments of burning pressed into a twin flame here in the deep. The clerks called it *hearsay-fire*. Ilse called it sorcery and made herself stop calling it that, because the word had started to sound like an accusation aimed inward.

The clerk who brought it was young, round-faced, with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic air of a man delivering a bill. He lit the twin flame on a stand before them both, Wren and Ilse side by side, and the fire spoke in a voice like paper tearing:

*"—purge escalates. Chancellor doubles bounties. Registration lines burn through the night. The guild-master Corvess has testified before the High Court of Inquiry—"*

Ilse felt it coming the way you feel a stair missing in the dark.

*"—testified that the Inquisitor Ilse Vane is herself gifted. Concealed gift. Undeclared. Witnesses produced. Warrant issued. Reward offered, dead preferred."*

The flame finished, and bent, and went out, and the stand held nothing but a thread of smoke that smelled absurdly, horribly, of the courthouse above—of ink and cold stone and the particular lye soap they used on the interrogation floors.

Wren turned to her slowly. His face had gone the color of the candle-wax.

"They'll believe it," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Corvess doesn't testify to things people might believe. He testifies to things they want to." She heard how steady her voice was and marveled at it, the way you marvel at a stranger's house. "I built that machine, Wren. I wrote the manuals for it. Of course it believes me guilty. I trained it to."

"What will you do?" And then, before she could answer, softer, with the horror arriving properly: "Ilse. If they take you up there—if they put you in a cell—you can't be here when the moon turns. You can't—"

"I know what I can't be."

Because the thirteenth moon was four days away now. Four days, and the terms required the substitute to stand in the chamber when the court assembled, to be weighed, to be entered. Four days, and above them a warrant with her name on it—her true name, the one she'd kept clean for thirty years—was being nailed to every post from the salt market to the sea.

She looked at his candle. Shorter again. The wax pooling fat and grey on the iron saucer.

"If I go up," she said carefully, "and they catch me—"

"Don't go up."

"If they catch me, they'll interrogate me. And I'll confess to being gifted, whether it's true or not, because I know exactly how to make a confession stick—I wrote that chapter too." She met his eyes. "Which means that either way, Wren, I end up in a ledger. The only choice left to me is whose."

He stared at her. She watched the taster's blankness come down over his face, the furniture-mask, the survival habit—and then, credit to him, watched him fight it off. Watched him choose, visibly, painfully, to stay present.

"Whose," he repeated.

"Mine," she said, "or yours. Those are the ledgers in play. I've simply decided which name goes in which book."

"You're not—Ilse, you can't negotiate with them behind my back, this isn't yours to—"

"It became mine," she said, "the moment I asked you at the shrine what was taken from you. Nobody had ever asked. Do you understand what that means? It means everyone who ever dealt with you dealt with the *debt* and never with the *man*. The court, the crown, your mother, Corvess—all of them moved pieces named Wren around boards named survival, and not one of them, in thirty years, asked the piece what it wanted." Her voice cracked clean through this time, and she let it. "I will not be another hand moving you. If I go into that book, it will be because I chose it, fully, in secret, the way you swallowed that pear."

He flinched at that. The pear. The nightshade. The story he'd told her at the shrine with a laugh that had frightened her more than any confession of treason: how he had tasted death in the honey and swallowed anyway, because spitting it out meant admitting he could taste it, and admitting that meant the purge, the pyre, the end of the invisibility that kept him alive. He had poisoned himself to remain ordinary. He had spent nine years dying by inches in order to live.

"You see it," he whispered. "You see why I did it."

"I see it better than you do. Because I did the same thing for thirty years, just with a torch-basket instead of a spoon."

* * *

That night she returned to the black salt table with her proof.

She had thought long about what to bring. Not the sword—she wasn't ready, and the court would know a half-offering, and half-offerings insulted them. Instead she brought the file. Her own file—the one she'd kept on Wren Sterling, the one she'd never filed away, the one that had sat on her desk in the inquisitor's wing for weeks while she told herself she was being thorough. She had carried it the whole way down the mountain. She didn't know why, until now.

She laid it on the black salt and opened it to the last page: her own marginalia, her own handwriting, the note she'd written the night she burned his testimony rather than arrest him. *Nothing found. Nothing lost. Subject cleared.* A lie in ink. The first lie she had ever entered into an official record.

"This," she said to the empty chairs. "This is what I'm willing to surrender. Not the sword. Not yet. This—the inquisitor. The woman who kept files and believed in the hunt and stood silent in squares. Burn her. Enter me without her. Whatever's left after that is what weighs."

The candles leaned. All of them, wall to wall, a thousand small flames bowing toward her like a congregation, and the voice arrived inside her skull wearing her sister's tone—which was impossible, which was cruelty, which was also perhaps simply the truth, since the voice always wore the listener's own tone, and her sister's voice had been living inside her own head for thirty years.

*Weigh the inquisitor,* said the court, musing. *Weigh the silence. Weigh the basket and the torch and the square. Yes. It may suffice. It may even exceed.* A pause, and the flames shivered. *But the substitution requires consent of the debtor's holder, not the debtor alone. The soul is collateral. Collateral speaks with its owner's voice.*

"Ilse?" Wren's voice, from the chamber door.

She turned. He stood in the doorway with the oldest ledger hugged to his chest, and his face was white, and he had heard—there was no way he hadn't heard, the candles carried sound like water carries blood—everything.

"How much," he said, very quietly, "did you offer them?"

"Wren—"

"*How much?*"

"All of it," she said. "Everything except the sword. And tonight I gave them the rest of me anyway—the part that hunted you. I gave them the inquisitor." She lifted her chin, because if she was going to be furious at, she refused to be ashamed before. "I did it in secret because secrets are the only place I've ever been free."

He crossed the chamber in six strides and took the file off the table—her file, her lie, her thirty years—and held it against his chest the way he held the ledger, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the candles burned straight up, indifferent, balanced.

"You absolute hypocrite," he said finally, and his voice broke on it, and it was almost a laugh. "You lectured me about moving pieces."

"Yes."

"And you did it anyway."

"Yes."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and angry, and underneath the anger—she had spent a decade learning to read the layers under anger—something that had no name yet, something new, being built in real time out of grief and gratitude and fury at being loved.

"We have four days," he said. "Four days, and Corvess has sold my mother's seat, your warrant is on every post between here and the sea, and you want to spend them teaching me how to lose gracefully." He shook his head slowly. "No. If someone is going into that ledger, we decide together. Out loud. In front of each other. That's the price of you staying—no more secrets, Ilse. Not even kind ones. Especially not kind ones."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of mountain, the world went on burning. Below them, a candle the size of a thumb wept another grey tear onto its saucer.

And in the deepest alcove of the counting-chamber, where the founder's own candle had burned untouched for thirty years—a flame tall and white and patient, tended by no one, owed by everyone—a clerk on his evening rounds paused, frowned at the wax pooled strangely thick around its base, and reached for his trimming knife.

The flame, without wind, bent away from the blade.
