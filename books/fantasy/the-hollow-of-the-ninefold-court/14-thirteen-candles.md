## 14. Thirteen Candles

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Wren found the flaw.

He would remember that, later—the smallness of it. Not a thunderclap. Not a revelation written in fire across the walls of the hollow. Just a wick guttering in its own wax, and a line of cramped clerk's hand on the fourth page of a contract older than his life, and the sudden vertigo of understanding that everything he feared might be undone by punctuation.

*The Crown of Ashenmark, being sorely afflicted by famine, does pledge unto the Ninefold Court one soul of royal blood, as surety against default.*

One soul of royal blood.

Not one person. Not one child. Not Wren Sterling, born on a salt-market morning with his mother's grey eyes and his father's crooked little finger. A *soul*. An abstraction. A thing that could be held, weighed, traded—and therefore, by the court's own obsessive logic, a thing that could be defined, delimited, and contested.

Wren set down the page with hands that were not quite steady and read it again from the beginning, because he had learned long ago—first as a taster, then as a ledger-keeper—that miracles rarely announce themselves. They hide in the margins. They wait for someone patient enough, or desperate enough, to read the same sentence eleven times.

The cellar office above him was silent now. The guild had fled or scattered after the raid; Corvess's people had stripped the shelves of anything portable, and what remained was dust, broken shelving, and the oldest ledger pressed flat beneath his coat where it had ridden for three days like a second heartbeat. He had not dared return to the surface. Ilse had gone up two days ago to buy them time, and every hour since had been an hour of waiting, of reading, of feeding the stove with broken chair legs because there was nothing else to burn.

Forty days had become nine.

He counted them the way he counted everything now: against the thirteenth moon. Nine days until the court convened in its full assembly, nine masked magistrates and their candles and their echoing ledgers, nine days until the debt came due and the crown's old promise came to collect whatever remained of him.

Nine days. And on the fourth page, in ink faded to the color of dried blood, the flaw.

*Pledged by the Lord Regent Aldous Crane, exercising authority vested in the Privy Council during the minority of Her Royal Highness, the Princess Amaranth Sterling.*

Wren read the name four times before it stopped looking like a stranger's.

Sterling.

His own name—not the name he had built like a wall around himself, forgettable Wren Sterling, taster, clerk, nobody—but the name as it had been *given*, carried down through a bloodline the regent had spent thirty years pretending did not exist. His mother had not been merely hidden. She had been *erased*. A princess struck from the rolls so thoroughly that even her son had grown up believing himself the child of merchants, of travelers, of anyone convenient.

And the regent who had signed away his soul—

Wren turned back to the first page. There it was, in the flourish of a man who loved the sound of his own authority: *Aldous Crane, Lord Regent of Ashenmark, for and on behalf of the Crown.*

For and on behalf of.

But the crown had been a child. The crown had been *his mother*, a girl of perhaps ten years, hidden away in the country while a regent governed in her name—and a regent, Wren knew, could govern but could not *pledge*. Not souls. Not sureties of royal blood. The Ninefold Court itself had codified that law centuries ago; he had copied the statute into the guild's reference ledgers a dozen times without ever thinking it would matter to him. *No pledge of a person or of the essence of a person may be made save by the person themselves, or by the sovereign in full possession of their authority.* It was the guild's oldest protection, the clause that kept kings from selling their subjects wholesale, that kept fathers from pawning daughters, that kept the whole trade of impossible things from collapsing into simple slavery.

The regent had no such authority. The princess was a minor. Therefore the pledge was voidable.

Therefore—

Wren stood up too fast and knocked the candle, caught it before it fell, and stood there in the dark holding a flame in cupped hands like something stolen.

Voidable. Not void. Voidable. The distinction mattered, and the distinction was the trap. A void contract died quietly; no one need ever know it had lived. But a *voidable* contract required an act to kill it—an invocation, a contestation, made before the court that held it. And the court's rules on contestation were the rules he had transcribed a hundred times without hearing them:

*Contestation shall be heard only upon testimony of both parties to the original pledge, given openly before the assembled Court.*

Both parties. The crown—and the court. Or rather, the crown's true voice, and the court's claim. The regent was dead these twenty years; his signature could be challenged by proving the defect of his authority, but the challenge required the other signatory. The party who had received the pledge. The party who had *held* the soul all these years, guarded it, kept it safe in a hollow beneath a mountain while her son grew up believing himself ordinary.

His mother would have to stand before the Ninefold Court and say her name.

Not to him. Not in the dark of the founder's chamber, where she had already told him everything—where she had held his face in hands that trembled and said *I never sold you, I saved you, and saving you cost me the right to be known*. That confession had been a gift between two people. What the court demanded was different. What the court demanded was a public act, witnessed, entered into the ledger forever, unerasable.

To say *I am Amaranth Sterling, rightful Queen of Ashenmark, and this pledge was made without my assent* was to end her erasure—and with it, every power that had fed on it. The regent's line, which still ruled through a chancellor grown fat on borrowed legitimacy. The guild, whose entire trade rested on contracts the crown had blessed by example. Corvess, who needed her dead and the contract intact, who had already sold Wren's location once and would sell anything else the moment it profited him. Every creditor, every conspirator, every quiet hand that had kept a princess invisible for thirty years would have reason to want her silenced—and Wren too, the living proof, the second signature on the family's ruin.

She knew this. She had known it when she told him about the flaw's neighbor-clause, the requirement of open testimony, and she had said it plainly, the way she said everything: *If I speak my name before the court, I become mortal again. Not in the way I am mortal now—kept alive by candlelight and contract—but mortal the way you are. Poisonable. Burnable. Huntable. Thirty years of enemies will wake up and remember me.*

And then she had smiled, that tired, luminous smile, and said, *Ask me anyway, when you're ready. It should be your choice to make, not mine. You've had so few.*

Wren sat back down at the ruined desk. The candle had recovered; it threw his shadow huge against the far wall, a giant hunched over a scrap of paper, guarding a flame.

He thought about immunity. He had built it dish by dish, year by year—microdoses of nightshade, of arsenic, of things with no common names, until his body had become a fortress and his tongue a locked room. It had kept him alive through nine years of poisoned pears and careful chancellors. It was also, he understood now, the last piece of himself the court would demand if the contract fell: balance demanded payment, and payment would come due in some coin. He had assumed the price would be his soul. Now he began to suspect the court would settle for something crueler—something he'd have to give up willingly, knowingly, dish by dish in reverse.

That was a problem for another night. Tonight there was only the flaw, and the choice, and the woman below the mountain who had left the choosing to him.

He thought of her hands. Of the thirty years of candles burning in the dark on her behalf, each one a year of her life held in trust by a court that could not let her die without collapsing its own greatest contract. She had guarded his soul the way he had guarded his ordinariness: perfectly, silently, at the cost of everything a life was supposed to contain. And now the law she had served—the law she had *founded*, before Corvess usurped her seat and rewrote her into rumor—demanded that she unguard herself. That she walk into an assembly of masked magistrates and hostile powers and speak one sentence that would make her the most dangerous woman in Ashenmark.

*It should be your choice to make, not mine.*

Wren laughed—a short, cracked sound in the empty cellar, startling him. Because it wasn't a choice at all, and she had known that too. There was no version of him that walked away from this. There was no version of him that let her keep paying for his survival. He had spent nine years swallowing poison to protect a lie about himself; he would not spend one more day letting his mother swallow silence to protect a lie about the realm.

But knowing a thing and doing it were separated by nine days, one mountain, and a court that convened on the eve of the thirteenth moon.

He smoothed the fourth page flat one more time. Then he took out the knife he'd taken from the raid-abandoned kitchen, cut a strip from his sleeve, and wrapped the contract in it—carefully, the way he handled everything irreplaceable. Into the coat. Against the heart. Where the oldest ledger already rode.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, came footsteps. Measured. Deliberate. Boots that did not belong to rats or wind.

Wren pinched out the candle and stood motionless in the dark, listening, one hand on the contract over his heart.

The trapdoor at the top of the stairs creaked.

Then a voice, low and roughened by cold and a hard ride down from the capital—a voice he knew better than any living person's except perhaps his own mother's.

"Wren." A pause. "It's Ilse. Don't reach for anything. We have a problem, and we have six days, and—" another pause, longer, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to something he had never heard her use, not in interrogation, not in the square, not anywhere: fear. "Corvess has testified. They're saying the inquisitor is gifted. They're coming for both of us, Wren. And they're coming *down*."

* * *

He lit the candle again because hiding was over; hiding had been over since the salt market, since the hexed net, since the blood on the stones that told Ilse exactly who had escaped. He watched her come down the stairs into the light—mud to the knee, cloak torn at the shoulder, a bruise flowering along her jaw—and felt something in his chest turn over that he refused, for the moment, to name.

"They burned my office," she said, before he could ask. "My files. Everything. Officially I'm a fugitive. Unofficially—" she gave a short laugh with no humor in it, "—unofficially I'm evidence. A gifted inquisitor is worth more to them than a hundred guilty tasters. Scandal keeps the machine oiled."

"Are you?" he asked quietly. "Gifted?"

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. The candlelight moved in her eyes.

"My sister could hear the dead," she said. "I can't hear anything but the living. That used to feel like a deficiency." She pulled out the chair opposite him—it was the only one left unbroken—and sat, wincing. "Show me what you found. You have the look of a man who found something."

So he showed her. He unwrapped the contract from his sleeve and laid the fourth page under the candle, and he watched her read it the way he had read it—once, twice, eleven times—and he watched the exact moment it landed, the moment her breath changed, because Ilse Vane had spent her career finding flaws in testimony and she knew a fatal one when she saw it.

"A regent," she said slowly. "Signing for a minor. Without royal assent."

"Voidable," Wren said. "By the court's own statutes. I've checked them against the reference ledgers—I copied those statutes myself, years ago, never dreaming—"

"I know the statutes." She looked up. "I helped enforce them. Do you understand what you're holding? This doesn't just free you. If the pledge falls, every contract the crown has blessed by example becomes contestable. Half the guild's trade rests on the precedent this contract set. Corvess isn't protecting a debt, Wren. He's protecting the foundation of everything he stole."

"And to void it," Wren said, and here his voice did what voices do when they carry weight they were never built for, "both parties must testify. Openly. Before the assembled court."

Ilse was silent. She understood faster than he had—she always had—and he watched her arrive at the thing he had been circling all night.

"Your mother," she said. "She has to say her name."

"She has to say her name in front of nine magistrates, Corvess, whatever escort he brings down from the inquisition, and every power in Ashenmark that profited from making her disappear." Wren folded the page closed, gently, as if tucking in a child. "Thirty years of enemies, Ilse. All of them waking at once."

"And you're going to ask her."

It wasn't a question. He met her eyes.

"I'm going to ask her," he said. "Because she told me it was my choice to make. And because—" he stopped, and started again, and the words came out plainer than he meant them to, "—because I swallowed a poisoned pear to protect a lie about myself, and I swore I'd never do it again. Not for her. Not for anyone. Not even to keep her safe. Safety bought with her silence is just the same old ledger with new handwriting."

Ilse studied him across the candle. Then she did something he had never seen her do: she reached across the ruined desk and put her hand over his, briefly, hard, like a seal pressed into wax.

"Six days," she said. "Corvess comes down with an armed escort and forged papers claiming the founder is dead. We come down with a contract and the truth. It'll have to do." She stood, grimacing, and looked toward the dark mouth of the passage that led deeper, toward the candlelit halls where debts echoed forever and a woman had waited thirty years to be asked one question.

"Come on, then," Ilse Vane said. "Let's go wake up a queen."

They went down together, carrying the flame between them, and somewhere far below—in a chamber where a single candle burned for every year of a stolen life—a thirtieth candle flickered, as if it had heard them coming.
