## 15. What Corvess Sold

The mountain did not want her to leave.

Ilse felt it in the way the passage leaned against her shoulders on the climb back up, in the way the candle she carried guttered at every bend as though the dark were drinking at its flame. Wren had argued with her for an hour before she won—or before he surrendered, which amounted to the same thing and cost him more.

"The escort will be riding for the shrine," she had told him, folding the map of the salt roads into her coat. "Corvess sold your location, which means he sold mine too, whether he knows it or not. If they reach the hollow gate unopposed, your mother is a dead woman and the contract stands and everything we've done down there was for nothing." She had met his eyes and held them, the way she held suspects' eyes, except that this time it was not a technique. "I can't stop six armed men. But I can make them late."

"And if they take you?"

"Then you'll know," she said, "because the warning will stop coming."

He had hated it. She had watched him hate it—the arithmetic of it moving behind his face, a man who kept ledgers even in grief—and she had understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than any pyre, that he was weighing her life against his mother's and finding no column in which either of them belonged. That was what the Ninefold Court did to people. It taught them to balance instead of to choose.

Now she climbed alone, and the mountain let her go the way a creditor lets a debtor walk out of sight: patiently, certain of collection.

* * *

She came out of the shrine at dusk, into air that tasted of iron and coming snow, and found the world below already changed.

The village that huddled at the mountain's foot—Kessel's Rest, eleven houses, a chapel, a well that froze by midwinter—was lit wrong. Not lamplight. Torchlight, carried in formation, moving up the pilgrim road in a line as neat as stitches. Six riders. Two outriders ahead. And behind them, at a distance that pretended to be coincidence, a seventh figure on foot, robed in the grey of the Office of Inquiry, walking with the unhurried gait of a man who has never once needed to hurry because the world arranges itself around him.

Corvess.

Ilse doused her candle and stood in the shrine doorway and did the thing she had spent nine years training out of herself: she thought like a hunted woman instead of an officer of the hunt.

The road passed within thirty paces of the shrine. The riders would reach Kessel's Rest by full dark, water their horses, question the miller—who would tell them nothing, because the miller owed the Ninefold Court two years of rain and knew it—and press on toward the high pass by morning. Unless someone slowed them. Unless someone gave them a reason to stop, to search, to spend hours on procedure while a message traveled down through passages no horse could follow.

There was one way to slow them. It required her to stand in the open and be exactly who she had been three weeks ago, before a boy's blood on a cellar floor had taught her what her own hands were for.

She stepped onto the road and lit her lantern and waited.

* * *

They came around the bend and the lead rider saw her and reined in so hard the horse screamed. She recognized him—Dain Merek, broad, careful, the kind of man who checked a door twice. He had trained her in restraint craft, years ago, back when she still believed restraint was a virtue rather than a wound you learned to keep clean.

"Inquisitor Vane." His hand stayed on his sword, which was answer enough. "You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"I'm a long way from having one," Ilse said. "You're a long way from yours too, Captain. This is shrine ground. Old law. No warrant of the crown runs here without the warden's mark, and the warden's been dead forty years." She said it flatly, the way one recites weather. "So before you draw, ask yourself whose law you're standing in."

Behind Merek, the grey-robed figure came on, unhurried, until Corvess stood at the edge of the lantern light like a man at the edge of a pond deciding whether the ice would hold.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said, and his voice was warm, and that warmth was the most frightening thing on the mountain. "We were told you'd gone missing. Taken, possibly. Coerced." He spread his hands, apologetic. "There's a warrant. There have been warrants since the night the Salt Market burned. You were seen leaving the scene with the fugitive Sterling. You were seen again at the shrine at Vesper's Ford. And now—" he gestured gently at the mountain, at the dark, at everything she was standing between him and "—here."

"You have me at a disadvantage," Ilse said. "You seem to know where I've been better than I do."

"I keep good books," Corvess said.

And there it was—the phrase dropped between them like a coin on a counter, and only she heard what it purchased. *I keep good books.* The guild's phrase. The guildmaster's pride. He was telling her, in front of six men who could not possibly understand, that he knew she had been below, that he knew what she carried, and that none of it mattered, because the books always balanced in the end.

She made herself smile. "Then you'll know I filed my itinerary with the Office before I left. Escort duty. The fugitive Sterling, surrendered voluntarily, pending testimony." She turned to Merek. "Captain, check the seal on that warrant. If it bears the Chancellor's privy mark, it's genuine and I'll come quietly. If it bears a countersignature from the Guild of the Ninefold Court—which it will, if you look—you are not executing a warrant of inquiry. You are muscle for a debt collector, and every one of you will hang for it inside a year, when this purge collapses under its own weight, as purges do."

Merek hesitated. She watched the hesitation move through him like weather through wheat—down the line, rider to rider, doubt passing hand to hand. They were not zealots. Zealots were expensive. These were men who had signed on for pyres and paperwork and had gotten, somewhere along the salt roads, a job that smelled increasingly like something else.

Corvess sighed, a small, regretful sound, and produced a folded paper from his sleeve. "Perhaps this will settle the captain's mind."

It was her own confession.

Not the one she had written—not the true one, the one she had sent down the mountain with Wren's warning, the one that named her sister and named her silence and named the night the smoke had smelled like tallow. This one was neater. This one confessed to conspiracy, abduction of a crown witness, and trafficking with the gifted, in language so clean it could only have been written by a man who thought confession was a genre. Her signature sat at the bottom, perfect. She had never written it. But she had signed enough documents in her life that some clerk with a steady hand and an hour's practice could wear her name like a stolen coat.

"This is forged," she said.

"Of course it is," Corvess agreed pleasantly. "But Captain Merek cannot know that, and neither can the tribunal, and by the time anyone could prove it, the matter below the mountain will be concluded and the document will have served its purpose. That is how paper works, Inquisitor. It does not need to be true. It needs only to arrive first." He folded it away. "You taught them well, by the way. Your methods. I studied your case files for years. You are the finest instrument the Office ever produced."

"And now?"

"Now you are an obstacle," Corvess said, "and obstacles are simply entries awaiting their date." He turned to Merek. "Bind her. Gag her if she speaks—she speaks very well; it's her whole trade. We ride for the pass within the hour."

Merek dismounted. Ilse stood still and let it happen, because struggling would cost minutes she could not afford to spend on dignity, and because—this was the part she would remember later, in the cell or on the scaffold or wherever the books finally balanced for her—because some traitor part of her wanted to see how far Corvess would go in front of witnesses. He went all the way. Rope, hood, the whole liturgy of arrest performed by men who kept glancing at each other as if to say *is this right*, and Corvess watching over it all with the serene face of a man auditing a transaction.

They bound her to her own horse, which was either arrogance or economy, and rode for Kessel's Rest.

* * *

In the dark behind the hood, Ilse worked.

Her hands were tied in front—Corvess's one error, or perhaps not an error; perhaps he wanted her hands free to write a second confession, a real one, later, in ink she chose herself. It did not matter. The knot was serviceable, not cruel, and Merek had tied it, and Merek had once spent an entire winter teaching her that a prisoner who panics dies and a prisoner who plans survives. She loosened it by degrees, a fraction an hour, the way water opens stone.

The hood smelled of lamp oil and old wool. Through it she counted: the creak of saddles, the crunch of frost, the bell of the chapel at Kessel's Rest striking nine, then ten. They had stopped. Horses being watered. Voices, low, arguing—Merek's among them, and she closed her eyes behind the wool and gave him silently to whatever gods governed men who checked doors twice.

At the eleventh hour, a rider came alongside her, close, and a gloved hand adjusted her blanket with unnecessary gentleness, and under the cover of the adjustment something small pressed into her bound fingers.

A knife. Merek's belt knife, the one with the chipped bone handle, the one he'd sworn he'd lost at Vesper's Ford.

She did not move. She did not breathe differently. Somewhere under the hood she was aware that she was weeping, briefly, without sound, and that it changed nothing and meant everything, and then she stopped, because there was work to do.

* * *

They camped at the tree line below the pass, and the cold came down off the mountain like a verdict.

Ilse waited for the watch to turn—waited through the long middle of the night when even guilty men sleep deepest—and slipped the rope, and slid from the horse, and was gone into the pines before the fire had finished a single pop of settling wood. She did not run far. Running leaves sign. She walked, bent double, following the memory of a route she had mapped by candlelight three nights ago: the drainage channel behind the shrine, the crack in the rock that the pilgrims called the Widow's Stitch, the vertical throat of stone that dropped, drop by drop by drop, into the warm dark where candles burned for debts.

At the shrine's back entrance she stopped, shaking with cold, and did the thing she had promised Wren she would do.

The warning was not words. Words could be intercepted; Corvess kept good books, and books were made of words. The warning was older than the guild and simpler. In the shrine's inner wall, behind the loose stone that generations of smugglers had worn smooth, there was a mechanism of the Ninefold Court's own design—a relay of sympathetic flames, candle linked to candle linked to candle, down through the mountain's bones, so that messages could travel faster than any rider and leave no trail but light. The guild used it for market signals. Prices. Calls in debts.

Ilse struck flint, lit the relay candle, and tipped it.

Three flashes. Pause. Three flashes. The signal every courier in the court's employ knew, and that only two people below would understand in time: *the escort comes early. Burn what must burn. Wake the founder.*

The flame leapt down the channel and was gone, a spark swallowed by the mountain, and Ilse knelt in the dark with her bleeding palms pressed to the cold stone and hoped—hoped, with the crude desperate tool she had left where faith used to be—that it would arrive before morning.

Then she heard boots on the pilgrim road, and put out her light, and became stone.

Corvess walked past the shrine without stopping. Of course he did. He had never intended to search for her in the dark; searching was for men who doubted their books. He stood a moment in the moonlight at the mountain's mouth, and Ilse, watching from ten feet away through a gap in the stones, saw him do something she would carry to her grave.

He bowed.

A small bow, precise, almost tender, aimed at the black opening in the rock—as a man bows to a church, or to a grave, or to a creditor whose terms he has finally accepted. And then he straightened, and the tenderness was gone as if it had never been, and he turned back toward the fires of his escort, and Ilse understood that she had just seen the only honest moment of the man's entire life, and that it had lasted four seconds.

* * *

What follows was pieced together later—from tribunal records, from Merek's testimony given under seal, and from a ledger page that survived the burning of the guild's upper office, water-stained but legible, written in Corvess's own immaculate hand. It is set down here because the truth of a man is rarely in what he does. It is in what he tells himself while doing it.

* * *

Corvess had never founded anything.

He had been, thirty-one years ago, a junior factor of the Ninefold Court—a bookkeeper of the second rank, competent, invisible, the sort of man whose name was misspelled on his own employment contract. He had served under the founder in the years of the famine, when the court's trade had saved the realm and damned itself in the same motion, and he had watched her do the unforgivable arithmetic: a princess trading her son's soul to end a famine, and then erasing herself to guard the collateral. He had watched her descend beneath the mountain and become a clause. A condition of contract. Alive, forever, because dead she would void everything.

And Corvess, alone in the cellar office with the ledgers, had understood something the founder never had: that the court did not need her. It needed only the *contract*. The living woman was not the guarantee. She was the flaw—the single thread by which the whole tapestry could be unpicked, if anyone ever learned her name and dragged it into the light.

For thirty years he had tended the machine she built and told himself he was honoring it. He had risen. He had taken the founder's chair when the succession was a formality and the founder was a rumor. He had signed himself *Master of the Ninefold Court* in a hand that grew steadily more beautiful, and the beauty of the hand was the measure of the lie: the more grandly he wrote his title, the less of it was true.

He had not usurped a throne. Usurpers want thrones. He had done something worse and smaller—he had moved into a house whose owner was not dead, hung his coat in her hall, and spent three decades praying she would never come home, while building the locks higher every year.

And now the boy had come. The boy with the ledger-keeper's memory and the taster's tongue, the collateral itself, walking down the mountain asking questions. Asking about the contract. Asking about the founder.

Corvess had weighed it all with the cold pleasure of a man balancing a column that finally comes out even. The boy must not reclaim his soul, because a reclaimed soul voids the pledge, and a voided pledge exposes the famine bargain, and the famine bargain exposed means the crown's shame, and the crown's shame means tribunals, and tribunals mean the court's books opened to daylight—and Corvess's own name, on thirty years of entries, in thirty years of beautiful handwriting, attached to trades no daylight could survive. The widow's griefs resold twice. The soldier's years lent at interest to men who never knew they borrowed. The souls pawned against bad harvests, foreclosed in secret.

The founder must die. The contract must stand. The boy must be collected, quietly, on schedule, at the thirteenth moon, and the inquisitor who had gone soft must be buried under forged paper, and the books would balance, and the house would remain his.

That was all. It had never been more than that. Empires are rarely stolen by giants. They are stolen by clerks, one honest-looking entry at a time.

On the last night before the descent, Corvess sat in the guild's upper office and wrote the order that would go down the mountain with his escort: *Upon presentation of proof of the founder's death, execute the collection. Leave no witnesses below.* He signed it with the title he had not earned, in the hand he had perfected, and blotted it carefully, and sat for a long moment looking at the signature as a man looks at his own face in a mirror he suspects is lying.

Then he took the oldest ledger from its shelf—the one with the founding hand on its first page, the true hand, the mother's hand—and fed it to the stove, page by page, because a lie survives best when the truth has ashes for a grave.

He did not know that the boy had taken the first volume down the mountain weeks ago, sewn into a coat lining, sleeping against a fugitive's ribs.

He did not know that the relay flame had already fallen, three flashes and three, through the dark veins of the mountain, carrying one word to the candlelit halls below:

*Hurry.*

* * *

Far above, Ilse Vane lay flat in the frozen bracken and watched the torches of the escort begin to climb toward the pass, six riders and a grey-robed clerk of theft, moving with terrible patience toward the gate of the hollow.

She counted them twice. Six riders. One clerk.

And then she counted again, because the frost was in her eyes or because the truth refused to be believed: seven riders now, not six. The seventh had joined at Kessel's Rest sometime in the night—a big man, silent, riding a horse she knew, wearing a cloak she knew, moving with the careful competence of a man who checks every door twice.

Merek had not stayed behind to search for her.

Merek was riding down into the mountain, and she could not tell—from this distance, in this dark, with her heart hammering like a fist on a locked door—whether he was going to stop what was coming below, or to make sure of it.
