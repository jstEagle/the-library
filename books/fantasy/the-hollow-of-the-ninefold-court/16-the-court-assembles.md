## 16. The Court Assembles

The candles were already lit when they came for him.

Not soldiers—candles. That was how Wren knew the hour had arrived. In the hollow beneath the mountain, light was currency, and the Ninefold Court spent it lavishly only twice a year: at the settling of accounts, and at the settling of debts that could not be settled any other way. The great chamber beneath the mountain had been dark for the six days since his mother had led him down through the drowned twin of the salt market, and now every sconce along the nine walls burned with a flame that did not flicker, because down here even fire kept its promises.

He stood in the antechamber in clothes Ilse had procured from a shrine-keeper two levels up—plain wool, undyed, the garments of a man with nothing to declare and everything to lose. His hands would not stop moving. He folded them. Unfolded them. Pressed them flat against his thighs the way he did before tasting, before swallowing something that might kill him, and realized with a strange, cold clarity that this was the same act. He was about to put something in his mouth—the truth—and hold it there while nine judges watched to see if he lived.

*You have survived worse,* he told himself. *You have swallowed nightshade and smiled.*

But nightshade had been simple. Nightshade had an antidote, a regimen, a discipline of years. Truth had no antidote. Truth, once spoken in this room, could not be spat into a napkin.

His mother's hand found his shoulder from behind. She had not touched him often in six days—as though she feared her fingers might leave marks on him the way the ledger left marks on everything—but now her grip was firm, and warm, and trembling very slightly, which frightened him more than anything else in the mountain.

"When you speak," she said quietly, "speak as a taster."

Wren turned. Up close, in candlelight, she was both older and younger than the woman he had met at the founder's table. The court's long tenancy had preserved her the way amber preserves a fly—flawless and terrible—but tonight there were lines around her eyes that had not been there yesterday, lines that deepened by the hour as the contract's flaw worked its way toward the surface of things. Voiding it required her death-in-law, her return to mortality, her name spoken aloud. She was dying back into the world one day at a time, and she was doing it willingly, and Wren still did not know how to carry that.

"Speak as a taster," he repeated.

"You know what I mean." Her voice was low, pitched beneath the hearing of the acolytes who moved through the antechamber carrying ledgers like pallbearers carrying coffins. "A taster does not argue. A taster does not plead. A taster presents the dish and lets the flavor do the work. You have spent nine years making yourself believed without ever once asking to be believed. Do that. Let them convince themselves."

"And if they don't?"

She smiled, and it was his own smile, the small closed one he used when the Chancellor asked whether the soup was safe and the soup was not safe. "Then we will be ash together, and at least the books will finally balance."

An acolyte struck a bell that made no sound but was felt in the teeth. The doors opened.

* * *

The chamber of the Ninefold Court was a bowl carved from the mountain's living bone, and it had been built to make a single man feel like a crumb on a scale. Nine tiers rose in a ring, and upon each tier sat a magistrate behind a mask of pale salt-glass, featureless except for a single slit where eyes might have been—or might have been nothing at all. Wren had seen masks like these in the guild's oldest iconography, worn by figures weighing hearts against feathers, souls against harvests, grief against gold. Below the tiers, the floor of the chamber was a disc of black stone polished to the depth of a well, and when Wren stepped onto it his reflection looked up at him from underneath, faint and inverted, as though the floor were water and he were the drowning thing.

The echo was the worst part. Every transaction in this room echoed forever—that was the law of the place, older than the guild, older than the crown—and so when the ninth magistrate spoke, its voice came back to Wren nine times, from nine directions, in nine slightly different tones, until it seemed the whole mountain was saying it:

*"The Court convenes upon the eve of the thirteenth moon. State your name and your business."*

Wren had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it in the shrine-cell, walking the words like a man pacing a cell, and he had rehearsed it in dreams he did not remember, and still his mouth went dry as salt.

"Wren Sterling," he said, and the echo took his name apart and gave it back to him in pieces, *Sterling, Sterling, Sterling*, until it sounded like a coin being dropped again and again. "Keeper of ledgers, formerly of the Ninefold Court above. I come to contest a debt."

*"There is no debt of yours to contest,"* said the third magistrate, and the others murmured agreement in voices like wind through a crypt. *"The entry is flagged under seal. The soul held in surety belongs to the Crown of the realm above. The Crown's agent has been summoned. The Crown's agent will pay."*

"The Crown pledged a soul," Wren said. "It never specified whose."

Silence. Not absence of sound—presence of it, a listening silence, the silence of nine masked faces turning by some fraction of an inch. His mother had told him this was possible. She had told him the old contracts were drafted in haste during the famine years, by regents and chancellors who never dreamed anyone would read them closely enough to find the seams. But telling was different from standing on black stone with your own inverted face staring up at you and saying it aloud.

"I have read the original instrument," Wren continued, and here he let himself do the thing he did best, the thing that had kept him alive through nine years of poisoned pears and smiling chancellors: he became unremarkable. He became a clerk. He became the most boring man in any room, the man whose voice made listeners' eyes glaze, and he buried the knife inside the boredom. "Dated the last day of the Hunger Winter, sealed with the privy signet of the Lord Regent Aldous, witnessed by three parties whose names are recorded in the founding ledger of this Court. The pledge reads, and I quote the old tongue precisely: *'the Crown doth bind itself to render unto the Court one soul of royal blood, in surety, against the ending of the famine, said soul to be named or nominated at the pleasure of the bearer of the contract.'*"

He paused. Let the echo do its work. *Nominated. At the pleasure of the bearer.*

"A soul of royal blood," he said. "Not a person. Not a name. A nomination, held open for thirty years because no regent dared spend it and no chancellor dared void it. And I have read the succession rolls, honored magistrates, as I read everything—it is my trade. The Regent Aldous signed without royal assent. There was no king. There was no queen. There was a boy of four years old hidden in a convent in the north, and a regency council ruling in his name, and a privy signet used by a man who had no right to pledge what was not his to pledge. A pledge made by a regent without assent is voidable. It has always been voidable. This Court, which honors the letter of every bond above all things—" he spread his hands, the gesture of a clerk presenting a column of figures that refused to sum— "cannot enforce a bond that was never validly made."

The echoes died away one by one, like candles being pinched.

And then, from the highest tier, the first magistrate leaned forward, and its mask caught the candlelight and threw it back in a single white line, and it said:

*"Proof."*

One word. Nine echoes. Wren felt the word land in his chest like a swallowed stone.

*"You claim the instrument is flawed. You claim the pledging authority was defective. Words, taster. This Court deals in instruments, in seals, in signatures. Bring us the proof of life and the proof of law, or bring us the soul, for the moon does not wait and neither do we."*

And that was when the doors at the far end of the chamber opened, and Corvess walked in wearing his best gray coat, flanked by six inquisitors in the black-and-silver of the hunt, and Wren understood that the answer to the Court's demand had just arrived before he could give it.

* * *

Corvess had aged twenty years in six days, or perhaps he had simply stopped pretending otherwise. The master of the guild above walked down the tiers' stair with the unhurried gait of a man entering his own parlor, and the inquisitors behind him carried a strongbox banded in iron, and Wren's stomach turned over slowly, the way it did when he tasted almond where almond should not be.

"Magistrates." Corvess bowed—not deeply, not shallowly, the bow of an equal. "Forgive the interruption of your proceedings. I bring documents relevant to the matter before you, and a witness of the Crown, given the escalation of affairs above." He glanced at Wren then, briefly, with an expression Wren had catalogued a thousand times on the faces of men watching poison take hold: regret, professionally rendered, worth nothing. "And I see the fugitive has found his way home."

"He is not a fugitive here," said the fifth magistrate. "All debts come home. Speak your documents."

Corvess gestured, and an inquisitor opened the strongbox and lifted out a scroll case of black lacquer, and Wren's heart began to beat somewhere high in his throat, because he recognized the case. It belonged to the sealed archive. It belonged to the shelf where the founding papers slept.

"The founder of the Ninefold Court," Corvess announced, in the round, carrying voice of a man who had practiced, "died thirty-one years ago. These are the certificates of her death, sworn by physicians of the guild, countersigned by the chapter masters of that era, entered into the public record of the realm above." He unrolled the first document across a reading stand that an acolyte hastened to provide. "I have served in her seat for three decades. I hold her authority by lawful succession. And I am here to tell the Court that the woman this taster claims can void the Regent's pledge—" his voice dropped, softened, became almost gentle, which was the most obscene thing Wren had ever heard him do— "is ash. Has been ash since before the man accusing me was weaned. Whatever he has found beneath the mountain, whatever ghost has whispered to him, it is not the founder. It is a fraud, or a revenant, or a desperate mother's trick, and none of those things have standing in this Court."

The echoes rolled out and came back changed. Wren heard them change. Heard the listening silence shift its weight, the way a jury shifts, the way doubt moves through a room like cold air through a cracked door.

Because it was clever. That was the horror of it. It was exactly clever enough. The Court dealt in instruments, and here were instruments—seals, signatures, sworn attestations, thirty years of paper stacked against one man's testimony and one woman's face. Wren thought of Ilse, somewhere in the upper galleries where his mother had arranged for her to sit, forbidden by the Court's protocol from speaking unless called. He thought of the forged papers burning in Chapter Seventeen of a story he had not yet written, and hated himself for the thought, because stories were not evidence, and hope was not a seal.

"The documents appear regular," said the second magistrate slowly.

"They are regular," said Corvess. "They are true. The founder is dead. I mourned her. I built her monument. Ask the guild above; ask the city; ask the very stones of the market hall she raised. Dead women cannot void contracts, magistrates. Dead women cannot testify. If the Court requires proof of life—" and here he turned, at last, fully, to face Wren across the black stone floor, and smiled the smile of a bookkeeper whose columns finally sum— "then let the taster produce it. Produce the woman. Produce her name, her body, her breath. Or concede, and let the thirteenth moon close the account as written."

Nine masks regarded Wren. The candles did not flicker. Somewhere in the walls of the mountain, water moved through channels cut before the guild existed, patient as arithmetic.

And Wren Sterling, food-taster, keeper of ledgers, son of a hidden princess, stood on the floor of the Ninefold Court with a forged death certificate hanging in the air between him and everything he loved, and did the only thing a taster knows how to do.

He swallowed.

"Then I will produce her," he said, and his voice did not shake, because he had trained it not to shake across nine years of lies, and this was the first time in his life the training served the truth. "Before this Court, before the thirteenth moon completes, I will produce the founder of the Ninefold Court alive, breathing, and named. And when I do, magistrates, I want it entered into the record—" he pointed at Corvess, and the echo carried his finger's accusation nine times around the bowl of the mountain— "that the master of the guild above brought forged papers into this chamber, and that forgery, in this Court, carries a price. You deal in balance, honored ones. So do I. Weigh his papers against my proof. Weigh them."

The first magistrate rose. Its mask threw back the candlelight like the edge of a blade.

*"The Court grants the taster until moonrise tomorrow. Proof of life, or the account closes as written."*

The bell that made no sound rang anyway, in the bones of everyone present.

And across the chamber, Corvess looked at Wren with something new in his face—not regret now, not professional sorrow, but the flat, bright attention of a man recalculating a ledger in which he has just discovered an entry he did not write, an asset he did not count, a living woman he had spent thirty years declaring dead—who was, at this moment, sitting three tiers above him in plain wool, with her heart beating audibly, to Wren's ears, like a drum.
