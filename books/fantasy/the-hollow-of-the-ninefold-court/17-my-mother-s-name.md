## 17. My Mother's Name

The candles had been burning since before Wren was born.

He understood that now, standing in the hollow of the Ninefold Court with the thirteenth moon one night from full. Nine magistrates sat behind a crescent of black stone, their faces hidden by masks of salt and silver, and between each pair of them a candle burned in a niche cut into the living rock. Some of the flames were tall and vigorous. Others had burned down to blue nubs barely taller than a thumbnail, guttering in air that never moved. He had learned, in his days below, what the candles were: every contract the Court had ever sealed left a flame, and the flame lived as long as the debt did. Somewhere behind him, he knew without turning, there was a candle thirty years old and nearly spent—the candle of his own soul.

Corvess stood to the left of the magistrates' crescent, flanked by four inquisitors in ash-grey cloaks. He wore his master's chain of office like it had grown into his neck. In his hands, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, he carried documents Wren could not read from this distance but knew by heart anyway: forged letters of succession, a false death-registry entry, a declaration that the founder of the Ninefold Court had died nameless forty years ago and passed her seat—rightfully, properly, in the eyes of every law both above and below—to her dutiful apprentice.

Wren stood alone at the center of the hollow, on a disc of polished obsidian that showed his reflection upside down beneath his feet, as though the floor remembered being sky. His coat still bore the scorch-marks of the hexed net. His ribs ached where Ilse had bound them with shrine-linen. He had no documents. He had no advocate. The oldest ledger lay on a reading stand beside him, open to the page that had started everything, and the royal seal at its head gleamed dully in the candlelight like an old wound gone to wax.

*Proof of life,* the middle magistrate had said, when Corvess produced his papers. *The contract stands or falls on whether the pledgor lives.*

And now the nine masks turned toward him, waiting.

"Speaker," said the leftmost magistrate, in a voice like sand poured slowly through an hourglass, "you have invoked the flaw. You claim the pledge was made without royal assent, by a regent exceeding her charge. You claim the pledged soul was never the crown's to give." A pause, in which Wren heard the drip of water somewhere far off in the dark, patient as bookkeeping. "But a claim is wind. The Court trades in weight. Show us what balances."

Wren's mouth was dry. He thought, absurdly, of the morning he had swallowed the nightshade pear, of how he had learned to let poison pass his lips with a steady face. This was the same discipline, only inverted. Then he had survived by swallowing harm in silence. Now he would survive—or not—by speaking the one truth he had spent a lifetime not saying.

He looked at the reading stand. He looked past it, into the dark gallery where the Court's clerks sat with their quills poised, recording everything, forgetting nothing, echoing forever. And somewhere in that dark, he knew, she was listening. She had promised him nothing. That was the cruelest and kindest thing about her. *If you name me,* she had said, in the little room lit by her own guttering candle, *I cannot unsay it after. What is spoken below is spoken forever.* She had not told him not to do it. She had only made sure he knew the price was real, because she was the last honest merchant in a market of liars, and honesty, for her, was a kind of mothering.

"I have no document," Wren said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted, so he planted his feet and made it larger. "Documents can be forged. I have seen forgery tonight—I have seen it done well, by a man who has kept these books for thirty years and knows exactly how a true page smells." He nodded toward Corvess, who did not blink. "So I will not offer you paper. I will offer you the thing your own law says outweighs it."

"And what is that?" asked the middle magistrate.

"A confession," Wren said, "before witnesses who cannot forget."

The quills in the gallery went still. Even the candles seemed to lean.

"The contract of the Famine Years," Wren said, "was signed by a regent. It bears the seal of the crown, but not the assent of the crown, because there was no crowned head old enough to give it—the king was six years old and eating porridge while his council sold a soul to end the hunger. That much, the ledgers show, and I do not dispute the ledgers. I have kept them. I know they are honest even when the people using them are not." He drew breath. "But a voidable contract needs more than a flaw. It needs testimony from both parties. The regent is dust. So the Court must hear from the other party—the woman who carried the pledged soul beneath her heart, and brought it here, and paid it over. The woman whose death was written into these papers tonight." He looked directly at Corvess. "The founder of the Ninefold Court."

"You assert," said the leftmost magistrate, "that the founder lives."

"I assert more than that." Wren's hands were shaking now; he let them. There was no more use for steadiness. Steadiness had been his whole life, dish after dish, year after year, the performance of a man with nothing inside him worth looking at. He set it down like a tool he would not need again. "I assert that I know her name."

Corvess moved. It was small—a half-step, a tightening of his grip on the oilcloth—but Wren saw it the way he had once seen nightshade bloom in honeyed wine, a wrongness in the pattern, and he understood that the man was calculating whether he could stop this with force and deciding, with the cold arithmetic that had always been his religion, that nine masked magistrates were too many witnesses even for him.

"Then speak it," said the middle magistrate, "or sit down."

Wren closed his eyes. Behind his lids he saw the cellar office, the smell of tallow and ink, nine years of entries in his own neat hand—griefs sold, years borrowed, souls pawned against bad harvests—and beneath all of it, flagged in the oldest ledger, the entry dated the day of his birth. *One soul, royal-blooded, held beneath. Payment due at the thirteenth moon.* He had read that line a hundred times since learning it was his. He had imagined a hundred mothers: a traitor, a coward, a stranger who had sold him cheaply and run. He had built her out of grievance, because grievance was easier than hope.

Then he had come down the mountain and found an old woman keeping a candle alive with the stubbornness of someone holding a door shut against a flood, and every version of her he had invented had died at once, and what remained was worse and better: a mother who had saved him by vanishing, who had guarded his soul for thirty years at the cost of ever holding him, who had let him hate her rather than make herself known and mortal and hunted.

She had given him the choice. That was the whole of her, he thought. Everyone else in his life had decided things for him—what he would taste, what he would record, what he would be. She alone had handed him the knife and stepped back.

He opened his eyes.

"Her name," Wren said, and the hollow took his voice and gave it back multiplied, ninefold, echoing off stone that remembered every transaction ever spoken in this place, "is Maren Sterling."

The silence afterward had texture. It pressed against his skin like deep water.

"No," said Corvess, too fast. "The founder's name is lost. The registry—"

"—is a forgery you wrote yourself," Wren said, "and we will come to it." He did not look at Corvess. He could not afford to; his courage was a candle too, and it needed all its air. "Maren Sterling. Born Maren of House Aldery, third daughter of the old king, sister to the boy who became the crown. Hidden during the succession wars, married to a chandler's clerk to bury her blood, widowed within the year. When the famine came and the council needed a soul of royal blood to seal the bargain, they came to her—not because she volunteered, but because she was the only royal-blooded subject the realm had forgotten hard enough to be expendable." His voice cracked on the next words, and he let it crack. "They were wrong that she was expendable. They were right that she would say yes. Because the famine was killing ten thousand people a season, and she was the kind of person who counted other people's children in her arithmetic before her own."

He turned, at last, to face the dark gallery.

"She said yes. She carried the price herself instead of letting them take it. She signed the pledge with her own hand and her own name, which means—" and here he raised his voice, pitched it up into the masks and the candles and the listening dark, "—she is the second party to the contract, and she is alive, and I am asking her, mother, if she will speak."

For a long moment, nothing. The water dripped. A clerk's quill hovered.

Then, from the shadowed archway at the far side of the hollow, a candlelight moved that was not attached to any sconce.

She came forward slowly, one hand trailing the wall the way she must have trailed it for thirty years, learning every inch of stone that had become her whole world. She was small. Her hair had gone white as the salt in the walls, and she wore the plain grey robe of the Court's keepers, and she walked with the particular care of someone whose body had been borrowed against for three decades and was finally, at long last, being returned. But when the light reached her face, Wren forgot every prepared word he had. She was luminous—not young, not restored, but lit from inside, the way a lamp is lit, the way a face is lit when it has spent thirty years doing one difficult thing and has come at last to the end of doing it.

The magistrates rose. All nine of them. Masks tilted toward her, and in the gallery the clerks stood too, and somewhere in the ranks of the Court's servants a sound went around like wind through wheat—the sound of people recognizing, after a generation, the woman whose seat they served.

"State yourself," said the middle magistrate, but gently, the way one speaks to something rare.

"Maren of House Aldery," she said. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to talking to candles. "Called Maren Sterling. Founder of the Ninefold Court. Pledgor under the contract of the Famine Years." She stopped at the edge of the obsidian disc, close enough that Wren could see the fine lines at her eyes, could see that her eyes were his eyes, grey going silver, the exact grey he shaved around every morning in a cracked mirror without once knowing where he'd gotten them. She looked at him then—one heartbeat, two—and whatever passed between them neither of them would ever manage to put into words, and both of them would spend the rest of their lives not needing to.

Then she turned to the Court.

"I confirm the speaker's words. I am the second party. I am alive." She lifted her chin, and Wren saw her hands trembling and her voice not trembling at all, and he thought: *there it is, that's where I learned it, the tongue that doesn't shake.* "I pledged the soul of my son to end the famine, and I have kept faith with that pledge for thirty years, and I have kept my son's soul safe for thirty years, and I have never once regretted the first and only once, on his birthday every year, regretted the second." A pause. "I am prepared to answer any question the Court puts to me. I am prepared to swear it in whatever form the law requires. And I am prepared," she said, and here her voice did finally shake, just once, "to be mortal again, if that is the price of clearing my son's debt. I have been dead on paper for thirty years. It is time the paper and I agreed."

"Forgery," said Corvess.

It came out flat, automatic, the word of a man whose whole architecture depended on it. He stepped forward, unwrapping the oilcloth with hands that were almost steady. "This court has been deceived. Here—" he thrust the papers toward the magistrates' crescent, "—the registry of the founder's death, witnessed and sealed. Here, letters of succession naming me heir to her seat. Here, her own mark renouncing—" 

"Bring them to me," said the leftmost magistrate.

A clerk carried the documents across the hollow. The magistrate took them in grey-gloved hands and held the topmost page toward the nearest candle-niche—not toward the tall flames, Wren noticed, but toward the old ones, the blue nubs, the candles that had burned longest and therefore, presumably, knew the most.

What happened next took perhaps four seconds, and Wren would remember all four for the rest of his life.

The nearest old candle bent. Not flickered—bent, its tiny flame leaning out of its niche like a person leaning out of a window to look at something, and the corner of the forged death-registry curled toward it as if drawn, and touched the flame, and caught. Fire ran up the page with a soft rushing sound, and the fire was not orange but white, white as salt, white as truth, and where it passed the ink did not char but simply *left*, lifting off the vellum in threads of smoke that spelled, briefly and legibly, the same sentence over and over—*false, false, false*—before dissolving. The magistrate let the pages burn down to his gloves. Ash sifted onto the obsidian floor. The candle straightened in its niche, satisfied.

"The Court's oldest flames," said the middle magistrate, into the ringing quiet, "are sworn to the founding covenant. They know the founder's hand. They know her mark. They know her *name*, which was spoken into the covenant at its making and has been waiting, these thirty years, to be spoken aloud again by anyone with the courage to say it." The mask turned toward Corvess. "Your papers were written well. They were not written true. The Court weighs truth, Master Corvess. It has weighed yours."

Two of the salt-masked figures detached from the crescent and moved toward Corvess with the unhurried certainty of tide. The inquisitors behind him looked at one another, and Wren watched—watched with the strange clarity of a man watching his own life tip over—watched the ash-grey cloaks decide, almost visibly, that whatever Corvess was, he was no longer their warrant. One of them actually stepped aside to let the magistrates pass.

Corvess did not struggle. That was the terrible thing. He stood very straight while they took his arms, and his eyes found Wren's across the hollow, and there was no hatred in them—only accounting, the look of a man revising a ledger he had gotten badly wrong, entering the loss, closing the column. "The books must balance," he said, to no one, to everyone, in the tone of a prayer or an epitaph. "Someone always pays."

"They do," said Maren Sterling quietly. "That was never the lie. The lie was that it should always be the same someones."

They took him away into the dark, and his footsteps echoed for a long time, diminishing, until the hollow swallowed even the memory of them.

Then the middle magistrate spoke again, and the warmth went out of the air.

"The contract of the Famine Years is found flawed. The pledge was made without royal assent; the second party lives and confirms; the forgery is burned and its author taken. The contract is voidable." A pause, delicate as a scale settling. "*Voidable*. Not yet void. Balance, Speaker, is the law beneath all our laws, and balance demands payment. Your soul returns to you—but something of equal weight must go back into the ledger in its place." The nine masks regarded him together, and Wren felt the gaze like nine cold coins laid on his skin. "You have one night. The moon completes tomorrow. Come to us with your payment—or come with nothing, and the original terms stand."

The candles bowed as one, and the magistrates rose, and the hearing was over, and Wren stood alone on the black glass with his mother's hand finding his in the dark, her fingers thin and cold and shaking now that it was finished, shaking the way his shook, and her voice at his ear, dry and wry and thirty years overdue:

"Well," she said. "Now we find out what you're worth."

Above them, through a mile of mountain, the thirteenth moon began to rise.
