## 19. Ascension

The mountain let them go the way a debt is forgiven: slowly, with paperwork.

Ilse felt it in her knees first, on the third day of climbing—each stair worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of feet, and hers aching in a rhythm that said *you are older than you were*. Behind her, Wren breathed in the measured cadence of a man who had learned to make even exhaustion quiet. His bandaged arm swung at his side where the hexed net had burned him, and every hundred steps she caught herself counting his breaths, the way she had once counted the seconds between a suspect's question and answer.

Lying was a skill. Silence under questioning was a skill. But Wren Sterling did neither. He simply made himself small enough that the truth passed over him like weather.

Not anymore, though. Not since the hollow. Since he had stood before nine masked magistrates and spoken his mother's name aloud like a man setting fire to his own coat, and the whole architecture of the Ninefold Court had shuddered around them, candles guttering, ledgers snapping open to their own pages as if the mountain itself were reading over their shoulders.

"Tell me again what happens when we surface," Wren said, because he had asked twice already and she had given him two different answers, both honest.

"The hunt doesn't die," Ilse said. "It starves." She kept her eyes on the stairs. "Every pyre I ever lit needed fuel. Names. Confessions. A registry that told us who to fear. And underneath all of it—" she tapped the wall, where the stone ran damp and faintly warm, "—this. The Court's enforcement. Corvess's people, feeding the inquisition its targets for thirty years. Selling us our witches so the guild's debts stayed hidden and its founder stayed dead on paper. We weren't hunting the gifted, Wren. We were harvesting the guild's accounts receivable."

"And without them?"

"Without them, the machinery grinds against nothing. Orders come down naming people who no longer exist in any ledger anyone can find. Informants stop informing—they can feel the floor shifting. Inquisitors start asking why the list never changes and who writes it." She allowed herself the smallest breath of a laugh, which in her family was practically a hymn. "Bureaucracy is a monster, but it is a monster that must be fed on schedule. Starve it, and it eats itself."

Wren was quiet for several steps. Then: "And what does it eat first?"

Ilse did not answer, because they both knew. She could feel it in the satchel against her ribs, where two documents rode against her spine like stones sewn into a pilgrim's cloak: her resignation, written by candlelight in the hollow while Wren slept, and beneath it, folded smaller, the confession—the full account of what the hunt had been, what she had known too late, and what she had done before knowing. Her sister's name was in it. Her sister's name had been in every version she'd ever drafted, in eleven years of drafts, and she had burned every one until now.

The stairs ended at a door of grey oak banded in salt-white iron. Beyond it, thin and cold and smelling of pine and distance, was morning.

* * *

They came out onto the mountain's southern shoulder just past dawn, and Ilse understood that she had forgotten what sky cost.

In the hollow there was no above and below, only candlelight and echo, transactions layered on transactions going back further than any reign. You forgot horizons existed. Now the world opened out of the rock in every direction at once—the valley below stitched with fields gone gold and ragged, the river a bright wire, the capital a smudge of smoke on the far plain like a bruise someone had tried to powder over. Wind came up the slope and went through her coat as though she owed it money.

Wren stood beside her and did not look at any of it. He looked down at his own hands, turning them over, palms then backs, as though checking whether they still belonged to him.

"It's strange," he said. "I keep waiting to taste something."

"You gave it back. The immunity."

"I know what I gave back." He flexed his fingers. "But nine years teaches the body things the ledger can't unwrite. Every hour since the gate, I've been braced for poison. I reach for bread and my tongue braces. Like a soldier flinching at doorways." He closed his hands. "She says it fades. My mother. That living unhidden is a muscle too."

"She would know."

"She would." Something moved across Wren's face—grief and its opposite sharing a coat—and Ilse looked away to give him privacy, which was the only gift she had ever been fluent in giving.

They walked down through the shrine pines, past the little stone shelter where, a lifetime ago it seemed now, she had caught him and asked him the one question no one else had asked. Someone had left dried flowers on the altar. The wind moved them like small brown hands applauding nothing.

At the treeline, where the pilgrim path met the cart road, they stopped, because the road forced the question.

West lay the capital: her superiors, her desk, her file cabinet, the chair where Wren Sterling's too-neat dossier had sat for weeks while she told herself she was being thorough. East lay everything else.

"I should go with you," Wren said.

"No."

"Wren Sterling testifying before the Chancellor carries more weight than Wren Sterling missing—"

"Ilse Vane walking into the Chancellery with a confessed traitor at her side?" She shook her head once. "They'll be looking for me anyway, once word climbs down from Corvess's escort about where I went and what I carried. If I arrive alone, unarmed, with paper instead of a prisoner, I'm an anomaly. Anomalies get questioned. If I arrive with you, I'm a conspiracy, and conspiracies get crushed. You testify better alive and separate from me." She paused. "Besides. It has to be your voice alone. That was always the price. Truth delivered secondhand is just another transaction. The Court taught us that much."

Wren studied her with those forgettable grey eyes that had stopped being forgettable to her somewhere on the road, possibly at the shrine, possibly earlier, possibly the moment she'd looked at blood on salt-market cobbles and known exactly whose it was.

"And you?" he asked. "What do you think they'll do to you?"

Ilse considered lying, found she had lost the habit somewhere in the last forty days, and said: "Anything. Demotion. Disgrace. The same pyre I built my career stacking wood for." She adjusted the satchel strap on her shoulder. "I've read the statutes more carefully than anyone in the building. There's no clean exit from the inquisition. There's only the one you walk through with your name attached."

"That's not a plan."

"It's a confession. Plans are what people make when they intend to keep something."

He almost smiled. It transformed his face the way dawn transforms a field—you suddenly see how much was always there.

"When this is done," he said, "when the testimony's given and whatever falls has fallen—I'll find you. Or you'll find me. However it works when neither person knows where the other lives."

"No promises," Ilse said.

"No promises," Wren agreed. "Which is its own kind of promise."

She turned west. He turned east. Neither of them hurried; hurrying would have meant believing the other might change their mind, and neither did.

She did not look back. She was proud of not looking back for nearly four hundred paces, which was, she reflected, about three hundred and ninety more than her sister would have managed.

* * *

The capital received her the way a body receives a splinter: with heat, and swelling, and the long slow work of pushing something foreign toward the surface.

The Chancellery had changed in six weeks, or perhaps she had. The registries still lined the great hall in their locked cabinets, but half the drawers stood open and empty. Notices on the board announced postponements—three hearings deferred, two burnings rescheduled indefinitely, a registration drive in the river districts cancelled without explanation. Clerks moved through the corridors with the particular gait of people carrying buckets during a fire they have been officially informed does not exist.

Corvess was already in custody, she learned. Delivered to the Chancellery by his own inquisitorial escort, who had discovered en route that the man they'd been sent to protect had sold them, the crown, and eleven years of hunts to a guild of soul-brokers, and who had decided—with the fluid pragmatism of soldiers everywhere—that arresting him beat answering questions about him. His forged papers were being examined. His real papers were being sought. Somewhere in a sealed archive, clerks were beginning, with dawning horror, to compare the inquisition's target lists against the Ninefold Court's old ledgers and finding the same handwriting throughout.

The purge wasn't collapsing. It was being *unwritten*, entry by entry, and everyone involved was pretending they'd always known it was fiction.

Chancellor Aldery's office smelled of beeswax and old decisions. He received her standing, which was itself an omen; Aldery sat when he intended to lecture, and remained standing when he intended to survive.

"Inquisitor Vane." His eyes went to her empty hands, then to the satchel. "You were seen leaving the city six weeks ago in pursuit of the taster. You return alone. Report."

Instead of reporting, Ilse set the satchel on his desk and removed the two documents, laying them side by side with the care of a surgeon arranging instruments.

"My resignation," she said. "And my confession. Read whichever order you prefer. The contents don't change."

Aldery did not touch them. "You were sent to apprehend a fugitive gifted with evasion and suspected fraud. Instead you—what? Walked into a mountain?"

"I walked into the truth of what we've been doing for thirty years, Chancellor. It's in the confession. So are the names of every target we were fed by the guild. So is my sister's name, and the date of her burning, and the fact that the order for it originated—" she watched his face as she said it, "—from a ledger entry, not a threat assessment. We didn't burn the dangerous. We burned the indebted."

The silence lasted long enough that she heard the clock in the corner tick off some small interval of history.

"You understand," Aldery said at last, quietly, "that I cannot simply accept this."

"I don't require acceptance. Only receipt."

"If I take your confession into the record, it becomes evidence. Evidence demands action. Action implicates this office, this administration, my predecessor, and quite possibly the throne's regency of forty years ago." He pressed his fingertips together. "You are handing me a fire and asking me to hold it."

"I'm handing you the truth, Chancellor. What you hold it with is your affair. But it will come out regardless—there is a man in the east of this country right now preparing to stand in public and say all of it aloud, with documents, under his own name, asking no protection and offering none. When he speaks, everything I've put in that envelope arrives whether you've read it or not." She straightened. "Your only choice is whether it comes from an inquisitor's hand or a fugitive's. One of those versions lets you steer."

Something flickered behind Aldery's eyes—the look of a chess player realizing the board was never the game. He picked up the resignation first, read it in its entirety, and set it down.

"And the confession?"

"Read it when you're ready to act on it. Not before. Reading it sooner would only tempt you to burn it, and we both know you're tempted."

For a moment—one unguarded, almost human moment—Aldery looked at her with something adjacent to respect. "Where will you be?"

"Nowhere," Ilse said. "That's rather the point. I've spent eleven years being precisely somewhere, doing precisely this. I'd like to find out what I am when I'm not a function." She walked to the door, paused with her hand on it. "Chancellor. When the testimony comes—it will come within the fortnight—remember that you chose to receive it. Write that down somewhere safe. Men in your position have survived worse than scandal, but none have survived believing themselves innocent."

She left the confession on his desk and the office with her shoulders square, and if her hands shook in the corridor afterward, there was no one she trusted enough to see it.

* * *

She lodged that night at a coaching inn near the west gate, under a false name she chose without effort—*Maren*, her sister's middle name, which was either sentiment or penance and probably both—and sat by the window with a cup of wine she had no intention of drinking.

Below, the city went about its evening. Lamplighters worked their rounds. A fishmonger argued cheerfully about credit. Two children played at catching moths against the inn's lit sign, shrieking with a joy that no registry had ever managed to catalog or extinguish. Ordinary life, persisting like grass through flagstones, indifferent to every ledger ever kept about it.

Somewhere in the east, a forgettable man with a burned arm was finding a pulpit, or a magistrate's steps, or a market cross, and deciding how loudly to speak.

Somewhere south of here, a woman who had been a princess and then a ghost and then a founder and then, for one night, merely a mother, was learning—or relearning—how to sleep under an uncandlelit sky.

And Ilse Vane, formerly First Inquisitor of the Gifted Hunt, holder of eleven years' worth of signatures on warrants she would spend the rest of her life not forgiving herself for, sat in a window with her sister's name for a pseudonym and discovered that the feeling in her chest was not dread.

It was lightness. Terrifying, undeserved, entirely provisional lightness—the sensation of a debt discharged not because it was forgiven but because it was finally paid in full, in the only currency that clears such accounts.

She took out a fresh sheet of paper. Not for the Chancellery. For no address at all, because she had none to send it to, and because some letters are written the way prayers are: for the writing, not the delivery.

*I burned the page of your testimony,* she wrote. *I want you to know I'd do it again. I want you to know I'm done needing to.*

She signed it with no name, sealed it unsent, and tucked it into the pocket over her heart, where it lay against her ribs like a second confession—this one addressed to a future instead of a judge.

Down in the street, one of the moth-catching children laughed, high and clear, and Ilse raised her untouched cup to the window glass, to the city, to the east, to whoever was listening.

Two days later, a courier found her at the inn, mud-spattered, half-dead his horse, carrying a single folded sheet bearing no seal but pressed with the unmistakable thumbprint of a man who had once been the most forgettable person in the capital.

She broke it open with steady fingers.

*It's done,* the note read. *Come and hear.*
