## 20. Testimony

The capital had not changed. That was the first cruelty of it.

Wren had expected the city to feel different after the mountain—expected the stones to know what he knew, expected the air itself to carry some residue of candle smoke and old contracts. But Ashfall-on-Vane was exactly as he'd left it: the same gray light pooling in the gutters of Saltmarket Row, the same bell tolling the hour from the Chancery spire, the same smell of coal smoke and wet wool and bread that no one could quite afford. The same city that had lined up in the square to denounce its neighbors. The same city where he had swallowed nightshade on a Tuesday morning and smiled about it.

He walked through the gates without hiding.

That alone felt like heresy. For nine years he had moved through this capital the way water moves through cloth—invisible, unremarked, a man whose face clerks forgot before he reached the door. He had cultivated it the way other men cultivated gardens: patiently, daily, with an eye for what needed pruning. He had worn the color of walls. He had spoken at the volume of furniture. He had made himself so forgettable that even now, passing the guard at the Vane Gate—a man who had seen him four hundred times—the guard looked through him and saw nothing worth remembering.

Wren stopped. Turned back. Tapped the man on the shoulder.

"Good morning," he said, clearly, at a volume meant to be heard. "Lovely day for testimony."

The guard blinked at him, confused, and Wren walked on before the confusion could become recognition. It didn't matter anymore whether they remembered him. That was the point. He had spent nine years learning how to disappear, and he was about to spend one morning making himself unforgettable, and the strangest thing—the thing that kept catching in his chest like a fishhook—was that both acts required exactly the same discipline: you had to know precisely who you were.

His mother had told him that, on their single night together in the hollow beneath the mountain, over a meal she cooked with her own hands because the court's kitchens were for debtors and she was, for one evening, neither creditor nor collateral. *You learned to hide so well you forgot there was anything underneath,* she'd said, and then, gentler: *There always was. There always will be. Hiding is a room you live in, not a person you are.* She had taught him to eat without flinching. To look a stranger in the eye. To say his own name out loud as though it belonged to him, which, as of thirteen days ago, it finally did.

*Wren Sterling.* Written freely. Owned by no one.

Except, of course, by the crown—which still believed he owed it his tongue, his stomach, and his silence. Which was why, on the morning of the fourth day after his return, he presented himself at the Chancery not through the servants' entrance he had used for nearly a decade but through the great doors on Chancellor's Square, ledger under his arm, and asked the doorwarden—loudly, in front of six witnesses—to announce him.

"Announce me," he said again, when the man hesitated. "Wren Sterling, food-taster to the Lord Chancellor, returned from unauthorized absence. And tell him I've brought evidence."

---

They gave him an hour in a cold anteroom, which he understood completely. It was not contempt; it was arithmetic. Somewhere above him, clerks were counting the costs of a missing taster against the costs of a public scene, and somewhere above them, the Chancellor himself was being informed that the quiet little man who tasted his pears had crawled out of whatever hole he'd fallen into and was asking, of all things, to be *heard*. Wren used the hour the way he'd once used cellar hours: reading. Not the guild's ledgers this time, but the room—the guards' rotation, the clerk who kept glancing at him with something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite pity, the draft that came from the left-hand corridor, meaning the Chancellor's private stair was in use, meaning the old man was taking an interest.

When they finally called him up, the Chancellor's audience chamber had been arranged for intimidation: the long table angled so visitors stood in the light of the high windows while the Chancellor sat half-shadowed, flanked by two secretaries and, at the far end, a man in inquisitorial gray whom Wren recognized with a small cold shock—Vane's former colleague, the one called Drossart, whose face he'd last seen across a salt market full of screaming.

The Chancellor looked older than Wren remembered. Grief or guilt had been working on him; it was hard to say which. "Sterling." His voice was dry as old vellum. "Nine days. Nine days absent from your post without leave. Do you understand what that is, in wartime?"

"It's desertion, my lord. From a post I intend to resign."

A secretary made a small sound. Drossart did not move, but his attention sharpened the way a hawk's does—not toward movement, but toward stillness.

"You intend—" The Chancellor stopped. Recalibrated. "You are a taster. You are *property*, in law if not in name. Your resignation requires my consent, and my consent requires a reason."

"I have several." Wren set the oldest ledger on the table. The sound it made in that hushed room was absurdly loud—a book the size of a paving stone, bound in hide gone dark with age, its corners worn round by a century of hands. "But reasons require context, and context requires that I speak for some time. I ask only that you let me finish before you decide what I am."

"And what are you, Sterling?"

Wren looked at the high windows, at the light falling across the table like a blade laid flat. He thought of Ilse, somewhere north of here, writing her own confession in a hand that shook. He thought of his mother, choosing to remain below until the courts untangled thirty years of her erasure—one more month, she'd said, one more season, because freedom taken too fast shatters. He thought of the pear, and the nightshade, and the taste of his own courage going down his throat.

"A witness," he said. "Under the old statutes, any sworn servant of the crown may give testimony touching the security of the realm. I am still, this morning, a servant of the crown. And I am about to touch the security of the realm very deeply indeed."

---

He began, as his mother had taught him, with the truth stated plainly.

"The Ninefold Court exists. It trades in borrowed years, bottled grief, souls pawned against bad harvests. It has done so for three hundred years, from beneath Mount Cael, and every famine this kingdom survived in living memory was survived because someone paid the Court in currency the ledgers record and the histories omit." He opened the ledger to the ribbon-marked page. "This is the original contract of the Famine Year. The crown—specifically, the office of the Regent Aldous Vane, acting without royal assent, which makes it voidable under the very statutes your inquisitors enforce—pledged a royal-blooded soul as surety for the ending of the famine. The soul was delivered. The soul was mine. The regent was your grandfather's grandfather's fixer, my lord, and the debt has sat on your books ever since, accruing."

He watched them take it. The Chancellor's face went carefully blank—the blankness of a man doing sums he doesn't want anyone to see. One secretary wrote nothing; he simply stared. Drossart had gone very still in the way of men deciding whether to reach for irons.

"That's madness," the Chancellor said at last, but softly, the way men say *that's madness* when they are already revising their understanding of the world. "Even if I believed it—and I believe nothing before breakfast—you stand alone with a book. Books can be forged."

"They can. Test this one." Wren turned pages with a scholar's economy. "Here: the entry flagged with the royal seal, payment due at the thirteenth moon. Here: the disbursement records matching the grain imports of the Famine Year to the week. Here—" he turned to the page he had saved, the one he had read forty times in the hollow by candlelight until he could recite it backward— "the pledge itself, in the Regent's own hand, witnessed by nine magistrates of the Court. And here is the flaw, my lord, in the Regent's own words: *a soul of royal blood, to be rendered upon demand.* Rendered. Not named. Because the Regent could not name me without naming my mother, and he could not name my mother without confessing that the crown had hidden a princess for thirty years while her child's soul sat in a cellar paying interest."

The silence had a texture now. Wren pressed on.

"But the purge—that is why I'm really here. You were told the hunt for the gifted protects the realm. My lord, the hunt was built by the Court. Corvess—the man who usurped the founder's seat—sold the inquisition its lists, its methods, its certainty. Every pyre above ground balanced a book below. The purge was never about protecting you from the gifted. It was about ensuring that no one gifted enough to see the truth of these ledgers lived long enough to read them. Ask your Inquisitor Vane. She walked into that mountain. She saw the candles. She'll confirm every word, if your own machinery hasn't already destroyed her for it."

Drossart spoke for the first time. "Inquisitor Vane is under investigation."

"I know," Wren said. "I'm rather hoping to make her investigation easier."

---

It took three hours. He had expected the pyre; he had rehearsed for the pyre—had composed, in the anteroom, the sentence he would say while they bound his hands, something plain and unhidden, his mother's name spoken clearly so that even the fire would hear it. Instead the Chancellor dismissed his secretaries, dismissed Drossart with a look that promised a conversation later, and then sat for a long time with his fingertips steepled, looking at Wren the way a man looks at a bill he cannot dispute and cannot pay.

"You understand what you're asking," the Chancellor said finally. "If I act on this—if I bury the hunt, quietly, the way you suggest—it is because burying it is cheaper than the scandal of it. Not because it's right."

"Yes, my lord."

"You don't object to being used?"

"My whole life has been an exercise in being used, my lord. I'd simply like, this once, to choose the purpose."

Something moved behind the old man's eyes—weariness, maybe, or the ghost of a conscience that had been dormant so long everyone assumed it dead. "The hunt will be wound down. Quietly. Registration suspended, pending review. The lists will be lost in an archive fire—I'm told archives burn beautifully." He said it without shame, and Wren found he felt none either; he had learned beneath the mountain that the difference between corruption and statecraft was often just which ledger you kept. "And you, Sterling. What do you want? Land? A pension? Silence?"

"Dismissal," Wren said. "Public, formal, on the record. I want my dismissal read aloud."

The Chancellor stared. "Most men beg to keep their posts."

"I spent nine years keeping mine. It nearly cost me everything I actually owned." He closed the ledger. "I want it written down that Wren Sterling served the crown faithfully, detected treason in a honeyed pear, and was released from service with honor. I want witnesses. I want my name in the record where anyone can find it."

"Why?"

"Because I was sold before I could walk," Wren said, "and no one wrote it down. Everything else follows from that."

---

They read the dismissal that afternoon in the Chancery courtyard, before a scattering of clerks and two bored guardsmen and, unexpectedly, a crowd that gathered the way crowds do—drawn first by curiosity, then held by the sheer strangeness of hearing a servant's name pronounced with honor in a place where names were usually pronounced with sentences attached. The herald's voice rang off the stone. *Faithful service. Vigilance beyond the requirements of his office. Released with the gratitude of the Chancery.*

Wren stood in the light and let them all look at him.

It should have been terrifying. For nine years, being seen had been the one unforgivable sin, the crack in the wall through which the whole architecture of his survival might collapse. He waited for the old vertigo, the animal urge to shrink, to blur, to become furniture. It came—but smaller than he remembered, and it passed, the way a wave passes, leaving the sand rearranged.

A woman near the front—a baker's widow he half-recognized from the square, from the terrible days of denunciations—looked at him afterward and said, not unkindly, "You're the taster. The one who never got sick."

"I got sick," Wren said. "I just never showed it."

She laughed, and something in the laugh was an absolution, and he walked out of the courtyard lighter than he had walked into it.

---

That evening he took a room at an inn on Copperlane—the kind of modest establishment he had passed ten thousand times without entering, because entering meant registering, registering meant a name in a book, a name in a book meant visibility, and visibility meant death. The innkeeper asked his name and he gave it. Full. Unhurried. *Wren Sterling.* She wrote it in her register in a round, careless hand, next to a farmer and a cloth merchant, and handed him a key, and that was all. No bells. No pyre. A name in a book, and the world went on turning.

He ate downstairs. He ordered the stew without asking who had cooked it or what was in it, and when it came he tasted it the way ordinary people taste food—slowly, with pleasure, without analysis—and found it good, oversalted and slightly burned at the bottom, the best meal of his life.

Afterward, in his narrow room, he took out the few things he owned: the ledger (which the Chancellor, in a fit of either wisdom or exhaustion, had let him keep, sealed under writ), a change of clothes, and a letter that had been waiting at the inn's desk, forwarded through three hands and carrying no return address. The handwriting was precise, disciplined, and—underneath the discipline—shaken.

*I have submitted my confession,* Ilse had written. *All of it. The sister, the silence, the mountain, the rest. They will strip my rank. They may do worse. I find I don't mind, which frightens me more than the tribunal does. I wanted you to know that the page I burned in your defense was the first page of this. Whatever comes of it—*

The letter stopped there, unfinished, as though the writer had looked at what she was about to write and stepped back from the edge of it. Wren read it twice, then folded it along its existing creases and put it inside his coat, over his heart, where important documents go.

Below the window, Copperlane went about its business: lamplighters, late carts, a drunk singing something mournful and off-key. Ordinary life, in all its unsupervised glory. Somewhere in it was a future he hadn't planned yet—he had plans for survival, nine years of them, intricate as clockwork, and not one plan for what came after. He would need work. He was, he realized with a smile, spectacularly qualified for exactly one profession, and the kingdom was suddenly full of people trapped in impossible contracts who needed a keeper of ledgers who had finally learned the difference between balancing books and balancing lives.

He blew out the lamp and lay down in the dark, visible, unemployed, free, and slept without dreaming of cellars for the first time in nine years.

* * *

Morning came gray and clean. Wren rose, dressed, and went down to find the innkeeper setting out bread and a pot of honey, and on the table beside the register a folded note that had arrived with the dawn runner.

He knew the hand before he broke the seal. Not Ilse's—sharper, older, written by someone accustomed to signing death warrants and recently reduced to writing letters. The Chancellor's seal was broken carelessly, as if the man had sealed it in haste or in disgust.

*Sterling,* it began. *You should know that before the archive fire was lit last night, someone removed three pages from the hunt's master list. The pages are missing. The archivist swears they were there at dusk. I am telling you this because the missing pages concern names beginning with S and V, and because I am too old to pretend I don't know what that means. Whoever took them knows the game is not over. Neither should you.*

Wren stood in the doorway with the note in his hand, watching the street wake up, feeling the old alertness rise in him—not fear, not anymore, but attention. Someone was still playing. Someone had kept the names.

He put the note in his pocket, picked up his ledger, and stepped out into the open street, where anyone at all could see him.
