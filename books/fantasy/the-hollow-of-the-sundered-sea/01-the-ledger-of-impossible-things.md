## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Bram Brightwater finished the third column and discovered the fog was short.

Not missing—nothing in the Gildenscale was ever simply *missing*, not while Bram kept the books—but short. The inventory slip from Tuesday's intake said eleven bottles, shelf-marked F-7 through F-17, each stoppered with wax and labeled in Mistress Vael's looping hand: *Fog, harbor-born, one night's worth.* But the ledger, Bram's ledger, the great salt-stained tome that had lived on the counting desk longer than Bram had lived anywhere, said twelve.

Bram sat back in the chair that creaked in the same place it always creaked, behind the left shoulder blade, and looked at the discrepancy the way a physician looks at a pulse that has skipped. Somewhere in the building there was either an extra bottle of fog or an honest mistake, and both possibilities made the skin between their shoulders prickle.

Numbers did not do this. Numbers were the one thing in Bram's life that had never done this. A number entered true stayed true; a sum carried faithfully arrived at the same answer whether you checked it tonight or in ten years, whether you checked it weeping or laughing or half-asleep at a desk by candlelight with your fingers blue from cold. Bram had learned arithmetic before they had learned to read properly—not letters, which lied and shifted and could be made to mean whatever a clever person wanted, but figures, which stood still. Figures were stones you could stand on. Everything else in the world was water.

So: the fog.

Bram rose, took the candle, and went down into the vaults.

* * *

The Gildenscale's undercroft smelled of tallow and brine and old paper, a smell Bram had long since stopped noticing except on nights like this, when they came down alone and let it notice them instead. Shelves ran back into darkness farther than the candlelight cared to follow, and on those shelves sat the guild's stock-in-trade, arranged with a precision that would have made any respectable merchant weep and any respectable magistrate call for a trial.

Row one: bottled weather. Fog in fluted green glass, storm-light in squat brown jugs that hummed faintly if you put your ear against them, one jar of last autumn's particular golden afternoon sun that Vael refused to sell because, she said, some things appreciate better than coin. Row two: borrowed time, decanted into vials the color of weak tea—a year from a farmer who hadn't needed his, a month from a bride saving up for something, three days from a dying man who'd wanted to see the spring one more time and had, thanks to the Gildenscale, gotten four. Row three was locked, and Bram did not hold the key, and did not want to. Row three held names.

That was the rule Bram had understood fastest, in those first bewildered weeks three years ago when Pell had found them shivering under the customs-house stairs and Vael had looked at their ink-stained fingers and said, *Can you keep accounts?* That everything here belonged to someone. Nothing in the Gildenscale was stolen, whatever the harbormaster's men said. Every bottle, every vial, every name in row three had been sold willingly, or traded, or left in trust, and every transaction was written down twice—once in the seller's memory, once in Bram's ledger—and the ledger did not forget. People forgot. Ledgers didn't. That was the whole trade, when you stripped away the wonder of it: the Gildenscale dealt in the things people couldn't afford to carry, and Bram kept the proof that those things had ever existed at all.

*Bottled fog,* the ledger said. *Twelve.*

The shelf said eleven.

Bram counted again, touching each bottle lightly with one finger, the way Maren used to make them touch the words in her grimoire—*gently, child, gently, the text remembers rough hands*—and stopped themselves mid-thought, the way they always did, whenever the hedge-witch surfaced uninvited.

Eleven. And then, at the end of the row, wedged behind the eleventh bottle where the shadow pooled thickest, a twelfth after all—unlabeled, its wax seal broken and re-melted clumsily, as if someone had opened it and thought better of it and tried to pretend otherwise.

Bram lifted it out. Inside, the fog moved against the glass like something breathing.

They stood very still for a moment, listening. Above them, through the floorboards, came the ordinary sounds of the Gildenscale at evening: Osk's boots on the stairs, slow and tide-heavy; Juniper laughing at something in the kitchen, that quick bright laugh she used when she was working and wanted everyone to know she wasn't; Pell coughing in his room by the door, the deep wet cough of a man who had spent forty years breathing other people's cargo smoke. The sounds of a family, if you squinted. If you didn't look too closely at what held it together.

Bram looked at the bottle. Someone had taken a taste of the fog—opened it, breathed it in or poured a measure out, sealed it back badly. And no one had written it down. No one had come to Bram's desk and said *enter it against my account*, the way Osk did when he borrowed lamp oil, the way even Vael did, scrupulous about it in a way that surprised people who only knew her from the auction block.

Which meant whoever it was hadn't wanted it known. Which meant, in the small quiet country of Bram's chest where such thoughts lived, that someone in this house was keeping secrets. And Bram, who kept more than anyone, knew exactly how heavy a secret could get, and how it always, always found its way into the numbers eventually.

They carried the bottle upstairs, set it on the counting desk, and opened the ledger to a fresh line. Their pen hovered.

Then they wrote, in their neat, upright, unremarkable hand—the hand that said nothing about where they'd come from or who had taught them to form letters so precisely: *Twelfth bottle found unlabeled, seal broken. Entered to the house account, pending claim.* And beneath it, smaller, in the margin where only they would ever read it: *Ask gently.*

Because that was the other rule, the one Vael had laid down on their second day, when Bram had caught an error in the accounts of a senior member and panicked about whether to say so. *We are not a court, little clerk,* she'd said, turning her rings with one finger. *People bring their broken things here to be mended, not measured. You find a discrepancy, you ask gently. Half the wonders we sell started out as somebody's shame.*

* * *

"Ask gently," Bram said to Juniper an hour later, setting a cup of chicory beside her elbow without looking up from the drying ink.

Juniper glanced at the bottle on the desk, then at Bram, and her eyebrows performed a brief silent conversation above her eyes. "Whose?"

"That's rather the question."

"Mm." She turned back to the document pinned under her hands—a bill of lading, ostensibly, though Bram knew better than to ask whose name would appear on it by morning. Juniper's forgeries paid for the good coal and the winter boots, and everyone agreed not to examine that too closely, the way everyone agreed not to ask where Osk went on the dark mornings, or what Pell had smuggled before his knees gave out. The Gildenscale ran on a gentle mutual blindness, and Bram had come to understand that this was what families actually were: groups of people who agreed, collectively, not to count certain things.

"You could just leave it," Juniper said. "Unclaimed fog. House drink."

"It's not the fog." Bram turned the ledger page. "It's that nobody told me. Three years I've kept these books and nobody has ever—" They stopped. Chicory steam curled between them.

Juniper set down her pen. When she spoke again, the brightness was gone from her voice, replaced by something older and careful. "You think one unlabeled bottle means someone doesn't trust you."

"I think it means someone trusts me less than they trust the fog."

"Bram." She reached over and tapped the ledger, right on the fresh line, her finger leaving a faint smudge of iron-gall ink next to their neat entry. "You write down everything. You know what everyone in this house owes and is owed, to the copper. Do you honestly believe none of us have noticed that you're the only one whose debts don't appear anywhere? Whose account is blank?"

Bram's pen stopped moving. Outside, the wind off the harbor had begun to moan in the chimney, a low note like a struck bell underwater.

"I pay my way," they said.

"With work. With hours." Juniper picked up her pen again, dismissing the matter, or appearing to. "Nobody here bought their place, little clerk. Not even you. Especially not you—we found you under a stairwell with frostbitten ears and a head full of somebody else's handwriting." She smiled, quick and crooked. "The fog will keep. Ask gently tomorrow."

But Bram lay awake that night in the narrow room above the counting desk, listening to the wind and thinking about blank accounts. About the fact that they had never once, in three years, entered anything into the ledger under their own name. That the column where their own history should have been—where Maren's cottage was, where the lessons were, where the years of learning to touch text gently and read it truly had been paid for and by whom—was empty. Not because there was nothing to enter.

Because they had run from the person who held that debt, and running, they had learned, meant tearing the page out.

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of snow that wouldn't commit. Bram was at the desk early, as always, entering the previous day's transactions while the house slept around them—Vael home late from a viewing in the Salt Quarter, Osk not yet back from wherever the dark mornings took him—when Pell came shuffling through the front hall with the post.

Pell delivered the mail the way he did everything, slowly and with commentary. "Bill from the chandler. Bill from the chandler. Something from the tax assessor that I've put near the fire in case it catches. And—" he paused, and Bram heard the pause the way you hear a wrong note, "—one for you."

"For me?" Bram did not receive letters. In three years, Bram had received perhaps four letters, all of them guild business, all of them addressed to *the Keeper of Accounts, Gildenscale House*, because almost no one alive knew Bram's other name, the one from before, the one that lived in the torn-out pages.

Pell set it on the desk and lingered, which he never did; Pell's whole philosophy was that other people's business was like other people's soup, best admired from a distance. The letter was small, square, sealed with plain brown wax. The hand on the front was neat and upright and precise, each letter formed exactly the same way, every time, without variation.

Bram knew that hand. Had watched it move across a grimoire's margins. Had been taught to copy it, letter by letter, until their own writing became a mirror of it—which was why, afterward, they had taught themselves to write differently, slantwise, carelessly, anything but *that*.

"Bram?" Pell said quietly. "You've gone white as the tide-line."

"It's nothing." Bram's voice sounded far away to their own ears. "Old acquaintance."

They waited until Pell's footsteps had faded toward his room by the door. They waited until the fire had ticked and settled. Then they broke the seal with numb fingers, and inside, in that terrible familiar hand, in ink that seemed still wet after however many miles and weeks it had traveled, they found six words:

*The signs are reading true again. I'm coming.*

No signature. None needed. Maren had signed Bram's whole childhood; she hardly needed to sign a letter too.

Bram sat for a long time while the house woke around them—doors opening, Juniper humming in the kitchen, the ordinary music of the family they had chosen—and stared at the letter, and felt the torn-out pages of their own ledger rustling somewhere in their chest.

And beneath the fear, quieter, stranger, almost worse: the thought that arrived unbidden, in Maren's voice, in Maren's cadence, *gently, child, gently*—

*What signs?*

Bram looked down at the ledger open on the desk, at yesterday's neat entries, at the twelfth bottle entered to the house account pending claim. Then, slowly, they pulled the great index volume toward them—the master catalogue, the record of every impossible thing the Gildenscale had ever bought or sold or held in trust—and began, with hands that would not quite steady, to search the entries for anything, *anything*, that might be called a sign.

It was while turning the pages near the back, past the borrowed years and the bottled storms, that they found the entry they had no memory of making, dated today, three lines long, in handwriting that was almost, but not quite, their own:

*Tonight. It begins tonight.*
