## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The Verrin estate smelled of dust and old money, which was to say it smelled of both in equal measure, and neither had the decency to admit how much of each there was.

Bram stood at the top of the grand staircase with a lantern in one hand and a ledger book under their arm, and they let themselves feel, for exactly three breaths, the particular pleasure of a room that belonged to no one anymore. The Gildenscale had seized the estate that morning—debts unpaid, debts compounded, debts stacked like stones until the whole house of Lord Aldous Verrin came down—and now everything inside it was inventory. Everything. The chandeliers. The hunting prints. The stuffed heron under glass in the study, its glass eye catching the lantern light like a coin dropped into dark water.

Numbers never lied. That was what Bram loved about them, what they had loved about them since before they could name the feeling. A chandelier was worth eleven crowns and four silver bits whether or not anyone wept over it. A heron was worth nothing at all, but Bram would enter it anyway, because a ledger with gaps was a ledger with shame in it.

They had learned that from Maren, in another life. *A record is a promise,* she used to say, tapping the page with her gnarled finger. *You make it, you keep it.* Then she had tried to make Bram into something more than a keeper of promises, and Bram had run, and here they were, three years gone, keeping promises for strangers who had become something else entirely.

* * *

The morning had been Osk's doing, mostly. He'd rowed them across the harbor at first light while the fog still lay on the water like a sleeping animal, and he'd talked the whole way, because Osk always talked the whole way.

"Verrin's grandfather made his fortune off a salvage claim," Osk said, hauling on the oars. "Pulled things up from the Sundered Sea. Officially, anyway." He said *officially* the way other people spat.

"Unofficially?"

"Unofficially, half the shore-folk families with old money have a cellar full of things that swam away from them. You'll find out when you catalogue the cellars."

"I don't do cellars," Bram said. "I do shelves."

"You'll do whatever's down there, Brightwater, because I'm not going down, and Juniper won't fit through the door, and Pell will pretend his hip is bad." Osk grinned. His teeth were crooked and salt-yellow and Bram found them oddly comforting. "You're the youngest. That's the whole law of salvage."

It wasn't really the law of salvage. It was the law of the Gildenscale, which was a different thing: everyone did what they were best at, and what nobody wanted went to whoever couldn't argue fast enough. Bram had stopped arguing months ago. There was something in the work—the slow, patient naming of things—that settled them. Every object entered into the book was an object that could not be lost, could not be lied about, could not simply vanish the way people vanished, the way Bram had vanished from Maren's cottage on a night much like this one, leaving only a note and a stolen pair of boots.

*Item: one note. Item: two boots, worn. Item: one apprentice, fled.*

Maren would have entered it that way. Bram sometimes suspected she had.

* * *

The library took up the whole east wing, two floors of it, with a gallery running around the upper shelves and a ladder on brass wheels that shrieked when it moved. Bram spent the better part of the day there and was glad of it. Libraries were honest rooms. They told you exactly what a person had cared about, and Lord Verrin's grandfather had cared about the sea—not the living sea, with its herring runs and its ferry traffic, but the old sea, the drowned one, the Sundered Sea itself. Shelf after shelf of salvage reports. Charts of coastlines that no longer existed. Journals kept by men who had walked the mudflats at low tide and written down what they found there: doorframes, altars, coins with faces worn to smudges.

Bram catalogued them all. *One hundred and fourteen volumes, maritime interest. Condition: fair to poor. Water damage throughout.*

It was near dusk when they found it.

It was shelved wrong, which was the first strange thing. Tucked spine-inward between a farmer's almanac and a treatise on eel-weirs, as if someone had hidden it deliberately—or as if it had hidden itself, which was a thought Bram refused to think and thought anyway. They pulled it out because a ledger with gaps was a ledger with shame, and turned it over in their hands.

Leather, once green, now the color of old kelp. No title on the spine. No title on the cover either, only a stamped device that Bram's thumb found before their eyes did: a circle, broken, with a line drawn through the break. The mark of the Sundering. Every child on the shore knew it. It hung over the harbor gate; it was branded on the pilings where the ferries tied up; it was scratched into the lintel of the Gildenscale's own door by hands nobody could account for.

Bram opened it standing up, the way they opened everything, one hand braced against the shelf.

The pages were vellum, thick and soft, gone the color of weak tea at the edges. The early chapters were dull enough—a chronicle of the war's end, dates and treaties, the names of signatories in a clerk's careful hand. Bram skimmed. Cataloguing was skimming; understanding could come later, if the thing proved valuable enough to matter.

Then the handwriting changed.

It changed gradually, the way weather changes, so that Bram was several pages deep before they noticed the script had loosened, grown larger, less certain of itself. And the ink had changed too. Where the early entries were iron-gall black, crisp as cut glass, these later ones were brown—ash-brown, faded, the color of something written in burnt wood and water. As if the writer had run out of ink and kept writing anyway. As if the writer had known, somehow, that ordinary ink wouldn't hold what came next.

Bram turned the pages faster. The chronicle had become something else. Not history. Prediction. Lines like:

*In the hundredth year after the breaking, the sea shall reach for what it lost.*

*In the hundred-and-tenth year, a winter shall come early, and the harbor shall know it by its gulls.*

And then—

Bram stopped.

The page was warm.

Not sun-warm. The library had gone cold with the failing light; Bram's fingers were stiff with it, their breath ghosting faintly in the air near the high window. But the vellum under their palm was warm the way a cheek is warm, the way a stone is warm long after an animal has left it. Warm as if the page had recently been alive.

Bram looked down at what their hand had uncovered, and read it twice, and read it a third time because the first two readings could not possibly be correct.

*On the eve of the third frost of the hundred-and-twelfth year, the keeper of accounts shall open the book that opens the war.*

Below it, in the same ash-brown script, smaller, almost an afterthought, almost a whisper:

*He will know the date. Let him decide what knowing costs.*

Bram stood very still in the cold library of a dead man's house, holding a dead man's prophecy, and felt the strangest sensation rise in them—not fear, not yet, though fear was circling somewhere below like a shark under a swimmer. Curiosity. Pure, clean, professional curiosity, the kind they felt about any entry that didn't balance. Because the date was wrong. Prophecies gave years, seasons, portents. This one gave a date, a specific date, an *eve*, and Bram was a keeper of accounts, and keepers of accounts checked things.

They checked.

They set the codex down on the reading table—gently, though they could not have said why gentleness seemed suddenly important—and took out their own ledger, and turned to today's date, which they had written that morning in their own neat hand at the head of the Verrin inventory.

The third frost of the year had come last week. Everyone in Saltmere had remarked on it; Pell had grumbled about his joints; Osk had said the herring were late and late herring meant a hard winter. Which meant, by any ordinary counting, that tonight was the eve of the third frost of the hundred-and-twelfth year.

Bram counted again. Counted the years from the Sundering, which every schoolchild learned, which was carved over the harbor gate beside the broken circle. Counted forward. Counted the frosts. Checked the almanac on the shelf beside them, the farmer's almanac the codex had been hiding behind, as if it had been leaning on it for support.

Tonight.

Tonight was the date. Tonight, this evening, this very hour gathering itself outside the high windows—the eve of the third frost of the hundred-and-twelfth year, the night the codex said a keeper of accounts would open the book that opened the war.

Bram laughed. It came out wrong, thin and quick, the laugh of someone alone in a large house at dusk.

Because it was nonsense. Of course it was nonsense. A century-old book, written by some doom-saying monk or salvage-mad Verrin, happening to land on tonight—it was coincidence, and Bram dealt in coincidences daily. What were the odds that any given page named any given date? Small. But what were the odds across a hundred years and hundreds of pages? Larger than comfort allowed, and besides, the *keeper of accounts* part—well. Bram was a keeper of accounts. So was every clerk in every counting-house on the coast. So had been the author, probably. People wrote about what they knew. A scribe who kept ledgers would naturally imagine doom arriving through ledgers. It meant nothing. It was a mirror, not a window.

That was the sensible reading. Bram was very good at sensible readings. They had needed to be, ever since Maren, since the lessons, since the gift—the thing Maren had called *reading true text*, the thing Bram had spent three years pretending was only a good memory for handwriting. When Bram looked at words, truly looked, the true ones rang. Forged contracts sang flat. Lies in letters sat crooked on the page like badly hung doors. And true prophecies—

Bram had read exactly one true prophecy in their life, at fifteen, in Maren's cottage, and had refused to tell her what it said, and had begun saving money for boots that same week.

So they did the dangerous thing. They stopped being sensible, just for a moment, just privately, just between themselves and the warm page. They looked at the ash-brown words the way they looked at words when it mattered, with the gift open and listening.

The words rang.

All of them. Even the small whisper underneath. Especially the small whisper underneath. *Let him decide what knowing costs* rang like a bell struck underwater, deep and slow and terribly clear, and beneath the ringing Bram heard—or imagined they heard, and would spend many sleepless nights deciding which—the faintest suggestion of a second voice under the first, the way a bell's bronze holds the memory of the mold.

The page was still warm. Warmer, maybe. Or perhaps that was Bram's hand.

* * *

They did not take it.

That was the discipline, and the discipline held. Guild rules: nothing left a seizure site without entry, valuation, and Mistress Vael's mark. A book of prophecy was beyond valuation, which meant it was above Bram's authority, which meant Bram's job was to carry it downstairs, log it, lock it in the strongroom, and say nothing dramatic to anyone.

They managed most of that.

The logging went fine. *Item: one codex, vellum, untitled, device of the Sundering. Provenance: Verrin estate, east library, shelved anomalously. Contents: chronicle and prophecy, author unknown. Condition: fair. Note: page warmth reported, cause unknown.* Bram wrote the note in their flattest hand, the hand they used for things they didn't want anyone to read closely. Vael read everything closely. That was rather the point of Vael.

The strongroom was down three steps behind the auction floor, a low stone chamber that smelled of tallow and cold iron, and Pell kept the keys. He was there when Bram brought the codex down, mending a lamp wick by candlelight, and he looked up with his old eyes—eyes the color of driftwood, eyes that had seen the last of something, though Bram had never asked what.

"That's a heavy one," Pell said, meaning the book, meaning more than the book. Old doorkeepers develop a sense for weight.

"It's a book, Pell."

"Mm." He took it from Bram's arms and weighed it anyway, professionally, the way he weighed everything that came through his door. "Warm," he said, and handed it back. "You hold it. I'm not touching a warm book. Nothing good was ever warm that shouldn't be."

"Pell—"

"Strongroom's yours till morning. Lock it, lose the key down your shirt, and go home, boy." He said *boy* the way he said everything, without checking whether it fit, and Bram had long ago stopped correcting him. "And eat something. You've got the look. The look you get when numbers stop adding up."

Bram locked the strongroom. They did not lose the key down their shirt. They stood outside the door for a long moment with the key in their fist and the cold of the stairwell rising around them, and they listened—to the building settling, to the harbor bells marking the hour, to their own heart doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing.

Then they went back up, and got their coat, and stepped out into the evening that the codex had named.

Saltmere at dusk in early winter: lamplighters working the quay, gulls arguing over fish heads, the smell of tar and bread and cold coming off the water. Ordinary. Thoroughly, aggressively ordinary. Bram walked through it and felt it press against them like a crowd pressing against a door, ordinary on every side, and underneath it all the single extraordinary fact walking with them, invisible, warm, patient:

Somewhere behind them, locked in a stone room, a century-old book lay open to a page that knew about tonight. That knew about *them*. That had waited—how long? a hundred years? longer?—shelved spine-inward behind an almanac, for the one person whose trade matched its terrible little phrase to pull it out and read it on the exact evening it described.

Coincidence, Bram told themselves, turning down the lane toward the boarding house where they rented a room with a view of a wall. Coincidence, coincidence. The word was a rope bridge over a gorge and Bram crossed it fast, not looking down.

But at the corner, where the lamplighter's flame caught the frost beginning to feather along the iron railings—the third frost's eve, keeping its appointment with the world—Bram stopped. Just for a moment. Just long enough to notice that their hands, in their pockets, curled around the strongroom key, were trembling.

And just long enough to hear, beneath the gulls and the bells and the ordinary noise of the surviving world, the tide going out.

Further out than it should. Further out than it ever went.

Bram stood in the lamplight and watched the harbor begin to empty itself, silently, steadily, like a bowl tipping toward something far away, and thought—with dread dressed carefully, carefully, in the borrowed clothes of curiosity—of a drowned sea waiting at the bottom of the world, and of a warm page, and of a line of ash-brown script that had not finished saying what it came to say.

Tomorrow, they would tell the others.

Tonight, Bram walked home fast, and did not look at the water, and dreamed anyway.
