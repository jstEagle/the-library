## 3. The Gildenscale Gathers

* * *

Mistress Vael had a way of listening that made silence feel like an auction.

She sat at the head of the long table in the counting room, hands folded over a ledger she was not reading, and let Bram's words hang in the lamplight like lots awaiting bids. *Tonight's date. In ash-brown ink. A hundred years old.* The codex itself lay between them on the green baize, closed now, as though it might be embarrassed by what it contained.

Vael did not touch it. That was the first thing Bram noticed, and the thing they would remember longest: Mistress Vael, who handled bottled storms bare-handed and once sold a king his own childhood back to him at a markup, would not put her fingers on the book.

"A date," she said at last, in the voice she used for opening bids—level, pleasant, and utterly without warmth. "In a dead man's book."

"It's not a dead man's book," Bram said. "I checked the estate inventory twice. It came from the Harrowgate seizure. No author listed. No provenance before forty years ago."

"Everything has provenance, apprentice. Most of it is simply well hidden." She turned the codex toward herself with one fingertip, the way you'd turn a card you suspected of cheating. "And you're certain of the ink?"

"Ash-brown. Iron-gall goes black or rust-red. This is neither. I've never seen its like." Bram hesitated, then gave up the detail they'd been hoarding since midnight, the one that had finally driven them from bed. "The page was warm."

The lamp guttered, though no door had opened.

"Warm," Vael repeated.

"Like a hand had been resting on it. Like—" Bram stopped, because the next part sounded mad even inside their own skull. Like the page had been waiting to be read.

For a long moment Vael said nothing. Then she smiled, and it was her auction-floor smile, the one that meant she had seen something valuable and was already calculating how much it would cost her to keep it. "Well," she said. "I suppose we'd better wake the others."

Bram blinked. "You believe me?"

"Apprentice Brightwater, I have spent thirty years selling impossible things to people who want them very badly. Do you know what I have learned?" She rose, smoothing her coat of deep oyster-grey silk, and the lamplight ran along it like water along a hull. "That the impossible keeps excellent books. It always leaves a record. It simply prefers we find it late." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at the codex as if it were a rival bidder. "Wake Osk first. If the sea is involved—and something this old is always involved with the sea—I want to know before breakfast."

"And if it isn't the sea?"

Vael's smile thinned to something older and less comfortable. "Then it's worse, and we'll need everyone anyway."

* * *

Osk came down the harbor stairs at low tide, which was wrong, because Osk never came down at low tide. Low tide was when the harbor told its lies—mudflats and rope-ends and the stink of everything the water wanted hidden. He read the water at high tide or not at all.

But Vael had sent a boy with a message, and the boy said *the keeper of accounts*, and Osk had stood in his room above the chandlery with his boots half off and felt something cold walk up his spine on small feet.

So here he was, at the wrong hour, crouched at the edge of the Gildenscale's private slip while the others waited above him, reading the mud.

He didn't like what the mud said.

It wasn't anything so grand as an omen. It was smaller than that, meaner. The barnacles on the slip's pilings had all died facing the same direction—outward, toward open water, their little calcified mouths open as if gasping. Barnacles didn't turn. Barnacles committed to a wall and stayed. But these had turned, every one of them, like a congregation following a preacher who had walked out of the church mid-sermon.

Outward. Away from shore. Toward the deep water past the harbor bar, where the charts went blank and honest sailors stopped pretending they knew the bottom.

"Osk." Vael's voice from the top of the stairs. "The book."

"I'm coming." He climbed slowly, joints announcing themselves the way they did when weather was coming, though the sky was clear and cold and blue as a lie.

In the counting room, Bram showed him the page. Osk read it the way he read water—not the words first, but the whole surface of the thing, the pressure of it, the way the ash-brown script seemed to sit slightly *above* the paper rather than in it. Then he read the words.

*On this night the ledger opens. On this night the keeper of accounts shall know their name.*

His hands were steady. He was proud of that. Forty years reading tides taught a man to keep his hands steady while his stomach dropped.

"You've seen something," Vael said. Not a question.

"The barnacles have turned," Osk said. "All of them. Facing out." He watched Bram's face do something complicated—young and frightened and trying not to be either. "And there's more, but I'll say it once, in front of everyone, so nobody can accuse me of whispering." He looked around the counting room—at Vael carved from silk and iron, at Bram clutching the codex like a shield, at the empty chairs waiting for Juniper and Pell. "Thirty years ago, the winter I was learning my trade, my master pulled me out of bed at dawn because the harbor current had reversed. Ran backward for a full morning, out past the bar, toward the ruins. He made me memorize the date. Said a tide-reader who forgets a reversed tide deserves to drown."

"Why?" Bram asked quietly.

"Because three weeks later," Osk said, "the first ship burned in the Sundering Straits, and the war that broke the world started again for eleven days before someone bought peace back at a price nobody wrote down." He tapped the codex once, with one finger, and took the finger away quickly. "My master kept the date in his own ledger. I inherited it. I never told any of you. There never seemed a reason to frighten people with arithmetic."

Vael's eyes narrowed. "And the date, Osk."

He made himself say it. "Three days from now."

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water. And into it, from the doorway, came Juniper's voice, bright and careless and just slightly too fast:

"Well. Isn't that lucky I stayed up making tea."

* * *

Juniper had been awake for two days, which was normal, and had spent them forging a bill of sale for a haunted mirror, which was also normal, and had heard every word through the counting-room door because the Gildenscale's doors were older than its secrets and leaked sound like sieves.

She carried the tea tray in like a peace offering and set it down among the ledgers, and while the others drank she looked at the codex—really looked, the way she looked at documents, which was a different way than other people looked at anything.

Others saw a book. Juniper saw a *job*.

The paper was right—old rag stock, soft-edged, foxed in the corners the way paper foxes only when it has honestly lived a century. The binding had been re-sewn at least twice; she could see the ghost-holes of earlier thread. But the ink. The ink was the masterpiece or the crime, depending. Ash-brown, sitting proud of the page, unbroken by time. Nobody's ink did that. Ink married paper or it fled it. This ink was *visiting*.

"Somebody wrote this recently," she said, "in ink made to look old. Or—" and here her stomach performed a slow roll, because the second option was worse, "—or somebody wrote it a century ago in ink that doesn't age, which means they weren't writing to be read. They were writing to be *found*. Those are different trades entirely."

"Meaning what?" Bram asked.

"Meaning forgery, properly done, is an act of love. You make a thing look true because you want someone to believe something kind, or profitable, or necessary. But prophecy forged to be found later—" she turned her cup, didn't drink, "—that's not love. That's a trap with a hundred-year fuse. Somebody built this room, this exact room, and waited for us to walk into it."

"Then why does the page feel warm?" Pell asked from the corner.

Pell had come in without anyone noticing, the way he did everything, a big grey man moving quiet as snowfall. He stood by the door with his cap in his hands, and he was looking at the codex with an expression Juniper had never seen on him, and she had catalogued every expression Pell owned during nine years of watching him admit strangers through the guild's door. He looked like a man hearing a song from a country he'd sworn never to return to.

"Pell?" Vael said gently.

"That ink," he said. "Ash-brown. My grandmother had a psalm-book written in that color. Down in the Sundered villages, before the war took them, there was a whole trade in it—sea-ink, they called it. Made from things that come up in nets after storms. Things that shouldn't be in nets." He set his cap on his knee. "The priests banned it. Said text written in sea-ink couldn't be unwritten. Whatever got written, *stayed* written."

"Then whoever wrote our codex," Osk said slowly, "meant it to stay."

"Yes," said Pell. "And I'm asking you all, as the oldest fool in this room, to consider what kind of mind writes a thing it knows cannot ever be erased."

Nobody considered it out loud. Everybody considered it silently. Juniper watched their faces do it—Vael going still and appraising, Osk going grey, Bram going white as the baize—and thought, with the strange clarity of the sleepless: *we are a family, and families are just people who know exactly where to stick the knife, and haven't yet.*

She said, "We should vote."

* * *

They voted the way they did everything: badly, loudly, and with total commitment.

Vael argued first for selling it. "A codex that makes grown tide-readers go pale? There are collectors who would pay us the guild's weight in gold, and we could be gone over the mountains before whatever it predicts comes due. I did not build this house to die in it."

"We don't know what it predicts," Bram said. "One line. One line about a keeper of accounts. That's me, apparently, congratulations to me, I'd like to return the honor."

"You're not the only keeper of accounts in this building," Vael pointed out, with the ghost of a smile.

"I'm the only one whose *name* fits the cipher. I checked. Twice. At three in the morning. I checked your name too, Mistress Vael, and Osk's, and—" Bram stopped, ears going red, and Juniper hid her grin in her cup. "Anyway. We don't know enough to sell. Selling blind is how you get robbed."

Osk argued for burning it. "Sea-ink can't be unwritten, fine. But paper burns, and whatever stays written stays written *somewhere else*, somewhere we're not. I've seen one reversed tide in my life and I did not enjoy the sequel. Burn the book, salt the ashes, and let's all take up fishing."

"And if the book is the only warning we get?" Bram shot back. "If there's a second page, a third, instructions, a way *out*? You burn the map because you don't like the country?"

"Maps don't bleed," Osk said darkly, and refused to explain, and everyone remembered later that he'd said it.

Pell argued, in his slow way, for doing nothing at all. "Every trouble I've survived—and I've survived most of the troubles on this coast—I survived by being too dull to notice until it passed. Prophecy wants witnesses. Starve it."

"That's not wisdom, old man, that's stubbornness with a beard," said Vael.

"My beard has outlasted wiser heads than yours, madam."

And Juniper—Juniper, who had been composing her argument for an hour, who had planned to argue for forgery, for copying the codex and selling the copy and keeping the truth—heard herself say none of it. What she heard herself say was:

"Bram found it. Bram's name is in it. Let Bram decide."

The table went quiet. Bram looked up from the codex with the expression of someone handed a live coal.

"That's not how votes work," Vael said.

"It's how *family* works," Juniper said, and immediately wished she could forge the sentence out of existence, because the word *family* landed in the middle of the table like a dropped glass, and everyone stepped around it carefully for a moment.

It was Pell, of all people, who picked it up. "Girl's right," he rumbled. "Book chose the lad. Lad chooses for us. That's the oldest law there is, older than your auctions, madam, older than your tides." He nodded at Bram. "Go on, then, keeper. What's your account?"

Bram looked down at the codex for a long time. The lamplight moved on the ash-brown letters, and Juniper, watching, could have sworn the ink sat a fraction prouder of the page than it had an hour ago—as if it were listening. As if it liked being discussed.

"We investigate," Bram said finally. "We don't sell it, we don't burn it, we don't hide from it. We find out what it is, who wrote it, and what it wants. All of us. Together." A breath. "Because if some hundred-year-old trap was built for this guild—for *us*—then I want to look it in the eye before it springs. And if it's real—if tonight really was written down before any of us were born—then I want to know what else is in these pages. Because a book that knows dates..." Their voice went very quiet. "A book that knows dates might know endings. And I'd rather read mine than be surprised by it."

Silence. Then Vael said, "All in favor," and four hands rose, and hers rose last and highest, because Vael never lost gracefully and never lost completely either.

"So moved," she said. "Osk—the harbor. First light. If the water's turning, I want to know which way and how far. Juniper—paper, ink, everything you know about sea-ink, and quietly. Pell—double the watch, and nothing comes through that door tonight that I haven't approved personally." She gathered the codex into her arms at last, touching it fully for the first time, and Juniper saw her knuckles whiten. "Bram. With me. You and I are going to read every remaining page of this thing together, and we are going to do it *now*, before whatever wrote it decides we're taking too long."

They dispersed. Chairs scraped. The lamp was trimmed. And at the door, pulling on his coat, Osk paused and looked back at the counting room—at the family scattering to their tasks like sparks from one struck flint—and said, mostly to himself:

"First light, then. God help us, I hope the water's lying."

Above the harbor, the sky was going the color of old pewter, and the tide, far out past the bar, had already begun to run the wrong way.
