## 4. What the Tide-Reader Saw

Osk Vane had been reading the water for sixty years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above all others: the sea never lied, but it did whisper, and only fools mistook a whisper for silence.

He rose before the gulls did, as he always did, wrapping himself in the oilskin coat that smelled of kelp and old rain, and let himself out through the Gildenscale's salt-cellar door with the particular care of a man who had spent three decades perfecting the art of leaving rooms without waking anyone. The guild slept on above him—Vael dreaming of auctions not yet won, Juniper curled around her ink-stained brushes like a cat around a kill, Pell snoring in the doorway room where no door had ever truly closed since his wife died, and Bram somewhere in the attic with candlelight still bleeding under the floorboards, because that child never seemed to sleep so much as pause between ledgers.

Osk loved them all with the fierce, wordless love of a man who had never been good at saying things aloud. Which was, perhaps, why the sea had chosen him. Water didn't need words either.

The harbor at dawn was his cathedral.

He took the steps down to the stone quay two at a time, then stopped, because his knees had opinions about stairs these days, and descended instead with the dignity of age. Mist lay on the water like a sheet drawn up to the chin. The fishing boats knocked gently against their moorings, hulls speaking to pilings in the low wooden language of harbors everywhere. Somewhere behind him the town was still dark, chimneys cold, lamps guttering in their brackets. This hour belonged to him alone—to him and to whatever the tide intended to say.

He knelt at the edge of the quay, as he had every morning for longer than most of the guild had been alive, and dipped his bare hand into the water.

The ritual was simple. It had always been simple. He let the sea run over his palm and between his fingers, and he listened—not with his ears, which were mostly decorative now, but with the older sense that lived somewhere behind his sternum, in the place where his mother had told him his blood remembered being brine. The tide-reader's gift was not magic, whatever the guild's clients believed when they paid for his readings. It was attention. Sixty years of attention, distilled into a single act: *pay attention, and the water will tell you what it is doing.*

What the water was doing this morning was wrong.

Osk knew it before he consciously knew it, the way you know a room has changed before you can name what's missing. His hand registered it first—the pull against his fingers running the wrong direction, faint but insistent, like a current trying to remember an older path. Then his ears caught it: the sound of the harbor was off by a fraction, the lap of water against stone arriving a half-beat late, as though the sea itself were hesitating.

He pulled his hand back and looked at the water properly.

The tide was going out. That much was ordinary; dawn tides ebbed, everyone knew that. But the ebb was wrong in its bones. It moved too smoothly, too purposefully, without the stutter and swirl of water negotiating with a harbor's stones and sandbars. And it was pulling *hard*—harder than any ebb Osk had ever felt in this harbor, harder than the spring tides of his youth, hard enough that the mooring lines of the fishing boats had gone taut as harp strings, all of them straining in the same direction at once.

All of them straining seaward. All of them straining toward the same point on the horizon.

Toward the Sundered Sea.

Osk stood very still. Mist beaded on his white eyebrows. Behind him, the town went on sleeping, and above him the Gildenscale's windows stayed dark, and he felt suddenly, terribly like the last awake man in a burning house who has just noticed the smell of smoke.

Because he had seen this before.

* * *

He had been nineteen the first time. Nineteen, and already orphaned twice over—once by the fever that took his parents, once by the conscription that took his brother—and working the herring boats out of a harbor town whose name he had not spoken in forty years because the town no longer existed. The war had seen to that.

In the last winter before the war—the true war, the Sundering War, the one the histories called ancient as though enough centuries could make a thing forgivable—he had knelt on a quay exactly like this one and put his hand in water exactly like this water, and felt exactly this pull. Backward. Purposeful. Toward the deep places where the drowned ruins of the Sundered Sea lay scattered like the bones of a broken promise.

He had told no one.

That was the arithmetic he had carried for six decades, the sum that never came out clean no matter how many times he worked it. He had been nineteen and frightened, and a boy who tells people the sea is pulling wrong gets called mad, or worse, gets called a sea-lover—a collaborator, a sympathizer with the folk below—in the winter before a war, and boys called that did not always survive to see spring. So he had kept it. He had knelt on that quay feeling the water strain toward catastrophe and said nothing to anyone, and eleven days later the first beacon fires had lit along the coast, and the war had come up out of the deep like a thing that had only been waiting for permission.

His brother had died in the third year of it. Drowned, ironically, though not by sea-folk—by a river crossing, in the mud and panic of a retreat, dragged down by his pack and his armor while men who might have helped him ran past. Osk had never learned whether the pull he'd felt that morning had meant anything at all. Whether the sea had been warning him, or summoning something, or simply turning over in its sleep. He had never learned, and the not-knowing had grown scar tissue over the years, thick and numb, until he could almost pretend it wasn't there.

Almost. On quiet mornings, in the space between the last star and the first light, he could still feel it: the weight of a secret kept by a boy who hadn't known what secrets cost.

And now the water was pulling again.

* * *

He stayed on the quay until the sun cleared the headland, watching. The mist burned off slowly, reluctantly, and what it revealed made his stomach tighten: the water in the outer harbor had gone glassy and strange, flat in a way open water never is, as if something vast beneath had smoothed it deliberately. And the color was wrong. Harbor water here ran green-grey over sand, honest water, workmanlike water. This had a darkness in it, a blue so deep it read as black, welling up from beyond the harbor mouth like ink dropped in milk.

The fish would know. Fish always knew first. He made a note to walk past the market later and listen to what the fishermen complained about—empty nets, strange catches, things dredged up from depths that shouldn't have been dredged. The sea spoke in a hundred dialects and fish were its clearest speakers.

But he already knew what it meant. That was the cruelty of it. A younger man might have hoped he was mistaken, might have spent the day constructing innocent explanations—unusual winds, a seismic shudder, the moon in some rare configuration. Osk was seventy-nine years old and he had spent those sixty years learning the sea's grammar, and there was no innocent explanation for a tide that flowed backward toward the ruins of a drowned civilization. There was only one sentence the sea could be saying, and it was the same sentence it had said sixty years ago, in the last winter before everything burned:

*I remember. I am going back.*

He climbed the steps to the Gildenscale slowly, letting the cold seep out of his joints, rehearsing words in his mouth the way a man rehearses a confession. Because that was what this would be. He understood that with terrible clarity. Last night Bram had come down from the attic pale as paper, clutching a century-old codex with a date written in ash-brown ink, and the guild had voted—argued, really, argued like the family they were—to investigate. Vael had assigned him the harbor. *Read the water, Osk. Tell us what it says.* She had said it lightly, the way she said everything, auctioneer's confidence draped over genuine worry.

She had not known she was asking him to confess.

* * *

The kitchen of the Gildenscale smelled of barley porridge and burnt honey, which meant Juniper had attempted breakfast again, which meant breakfast would be an adventure. Osk found them all gathered in the long room: Pell already up and in his chair by the window, hands folded atop his cane, watching the street with the vigilance of a man who had spent fifty years watching doors; Juniper cross-legged on the table itself, because chairs were for other people, her fingers stained three shades of black; Vael standing at the hearth in her good coat even at this hour, because Vael dressed for negotiations the way other people dressed for weather; and Bram at the corner table with the codex open before them, guarded by one hand the way a child guards a wounded bird.

They looked up when he came in. Five faces. His family, assembled out of wreckage and luck and Vael's terrifying talent for collecting strays.

"Well?" Vael said. "The harbor. What did it say?"

Osk hung his oilskin on its peg. He took his time about it. He poured himself tea he did not want, because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and they were not shaking yet, but he knew the tremor was in there waiting for its moment.

"The tide's running backward," he said.

"Backward?" Bram looked up. "Tides go in and out. That's—"

"In and out, yes. In and out." Osk set the cup down. "This isn't going out. Out is the sea breathing. This is—" He searched for it, the way he searched the water, patiently, letting the right image surface. "This is the sea inhaling. Drawing everything toward one place. Every mooring line in the harbor was pulled taut at dawn, all pointing the same way." He met Vael's eyes. "Toward the Sundered Sea."

The silence that followed had texture. He heard Pell's cane shift against the floorboards. He heard the fire tick in the grate. He heard Bram's breath catch—that quick, sharp intake of someone connecting a page of text to a fact of the world.

"That matches," Bram said quietly. "The codex. The passage before the dated line—it talks about the water remembering. 'When the sea turns its face to the grave it dug, the account comes due.'" They looked down at the book. "I thought it was poetry."

"Prophecy usually is," Vael said, "until it starts keeping appointments." Her voice was level, auctioneer-smooth, but Osk had watched her run a hundred sales and he knew the tell: her thumb was moving against her forefinger, counting something invisible. She counted when she was afraid. "How bad, Osk? Give it to us plain."

Osk looked at his tea. Then at the people he loved. Then he did the thing he should have done sixty years ago on another quay in another life.

"There's more," he said, "and I've owed it to you for a while, though I didn't know how much until this morning." He drew a breath that hurt on the way in. "I've seen this omen before. Once. When I was nineteen. The winter before the Sundering War began. Same pull, same direction, same wrongness in the water. I knelt on my father's quay and felt the sea drawing itself back toward the deep places, and I knew—I *knew*, the way I know this morning—that something was coming. And I told no one." His voice wanted to crack and he refused it permission. "Eleven days later the beacons lit. My brother died in the third year. And I have spent sixty years telling myself a frightened boy couldn't have changed anything, and sixty years not quite believing it."

Nobody moved. Juniper had gone still in the way she only went still when forging—total absorption, total control—and Pell's old eyes held something that was not judgment, which somehow was worse than judgment would have been. It was recognition.

"You were young," Pell said at last, gravel-voiced. "We were all young once, and stupid, and alive anyway. Mostly."

"I'm not asking forgiveness," Osk said. "I'm telling you because Bram found a date in a dead man's book, and the sea is pulling toward the ruins, and I will not keep a second secret through a second war. Whatever this prophecy wants, whatever's coming—we face it with everything on the table. Everything. That's the only way this family survives what's coming. No more private knowledge. No more protected fears." He looked at each of them in turn. "Starting with me."

Vael exhaled slowly. Her thumb had stopped counting.

"All right," she said. "Then we do what we do best. We investigate. Bram, the codex stays on this table and nowhere else. Juniper—" a flicker of a smile, grim as winter, "—start thinking about what a forgery of a prophecy looks like, in case we need to sell someone a lie. Pell, the cellar routes. Osk—" she paused, and something in her face softened by a degree, the degree she allowed herself, "—read the water twice a day now. Morning and dusk. If it changes, if it pulls harder, if a single fish swims the wrong way, I want to know before the gulls do."

Osk nodded. The confession sat in his chest like a stone finally set down after sixty years of carrying, and the space it left behind was strange and light and a little frightening, the way an empty room is frightening when you've lived in a full one.

It was Bram who broke the moment, because it was always Bram, the keeper of accounts, who noticed the entries no one else saw. They had turned back to the codex, and their finger had stopped on the page, and the color drained out of their face by slow degrees, like a tide going out.

"Osk," they said. Their voice was very small. "You said eleven days. Between your omen and the beacons."

"Eleven days," Osk confirmed. "Give or take a morning."

Bram turned the book around and pushed it across the table so he could see. Below the ash-brown date—the date that had been *last night*—the faded script continued, in a hand that trembled slightly, as though the writer had been cold, or afraid, or both:

*On the eleventh day, the sea shall turn its face to the grave it dug, and the keeper of accounts shall know the reckoning has begun.*

Osk read it twice. Outside, far off across the water, a gull cried once and fell silent, as if the whole morning were holding its breath.

"Counting from last night," Bram whispered, "that makes today the first."
