## 5. The Prophecy Names Its Own

Bram spent the rest of that first day pretending the codex was just another entry in the inventory.

*One book, bound in grey calfskin, provenance seized estate of Aldous Wren, condition poor, contents heretical, value—*

They sat with the quill hovering over the *value* column for a long time. The lamp guttered. Downstairs, the Gildenscale went about its evening the way it always did: Pell's boots on the stair, slow as a tide coming in; the clink of Osk's tide-glasses being rinsed; Juniper's voice, low and musical, talking to herself the way she talked when she was working, copying someone's hand so faithfully she became them for an hour or two at a stretch.

Numbers never lie. That had been the first lesson Bram had taught themselves, alone, in the three years since they'd run from Maren's cottage with nothing but a satchel and a name they were half-sure wasn't theirs. A ledger is honest even when no one else is. Two bottles of storm, minus one sold to the widow Corvane: one bottle of storm. A year borrowed against a future harvest, accruing interest at a fair rate: arithmetic you could hold in your hands like a warm stone.

But the codex refused arithmetic. It had a date written in ash-brown ink — *last night's date*, which should have been impossible, and was worse than impossible because it was also *accurate* — and beneath it a line about a keeper of accounts, and the keeper of accounts, whatever else the phrase might mean, meant this guild, and this guild meant—

Bram set down the quill. Their hand was not steady enough for entries.

"You're doing the thing," said Juniper from the doorway.

Bram hadn't heard her come up. She leaned against the frame with an apple in one hand and her sleeves pushed to the elbow, forearms still ghosted with the iron-gall stain that never quite washed out. She looked, as always, like someone who had stepped out of somebody else's portrait and decided to stay.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at a page like it owes you money." She bit the apple. "Vael says supper's on. Osk caught eels. Actual eels, from actual water, which he claims proves the harbor's healthy, which I personally doubt."

"Juniper." Bram's voice came out smaller than they intended. "I need your eyes."

She stopped chewing.

They showed her the codex by lamplight, and Bram watched her face do what faces did when they met true text: first the flicker of recognition, then the narrowing, then the long stillness of someone checking their own craft against someone else's. Juniper could forge anything — deeds, decrees, the seal of the Harbourmaster's office, once, memorably, a bishop's dying confession — but she could not forge this. She'd told Bram that years ago, drunk on plum wine, staring at a bottled fog with something like reverence: *You can copy a thing until it's perfect, but you can't make a thing true. Truth has weight. Paper knows.*

"This isn't a forgery," she said now, quietly. "Obviously."

"I know."

"No — I mean it isn't *anything*. Look at the ink." She tilted the page toward the flame. In its light the ash-brown script seemed less written than *deposited*, each letter sitting on the surface of the paper like silt after a flood. "Ink dries into paper. This hasn't dried. It's been drying for a hundred years and it's still wet somewhere underneath. Whoever wrote this—" she hesitated, and Bram watched a forger's certainty waver for the first time, "—whoever wrote this didn't finish writing it. Or it's still being written."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, Pell dropped something heavy and swore with great feeling.

"There are other pages," Bram said. "Sealed. Look."

The later pages of the codex were stuck fast, fused along their edges as though the book had wept glue, or as though the words inside were not yet finished arriving. Juniper ran her thumb along the seam and pulled back sharply.

"It's warm."

"I know."

"Bram." She looked up, and for a moment there was nothing of the joker in her face at all. "What does the line say? Exactly. Word for word. Don't paraphrase. Paraphrasing is how lies get comfortable."

"*On the eleventh day, the sea shall turn its face to the grave it dug,*" Bram recited, "*and the keeper of accounts shall know the reckoning has begun.*"

"And before the date? What comes before?"

Bram turned back through the brittle leaves. The earlier pages were legible, if you had the gift for reading true text — and Bram had learned long ago that they did, that the letters rearranged themselves for them the way shy animals approach a patient hand. They had never told anyone the full extent of it. Not Vael. Not Osk. Not even Juniper, who was closest to a friend as the guild allowed, because friends were people you could lose, and Bram had already lost everything once.

But tonight they read aloud, because two sets of ears were better than one, and because the alternative was carrying it alone.

"*First the frost shall fall out of season.*" Bram's finger traced the faded lines. "*Then the sea shall turn its face to the grave it dug. And in that winter shall rise the Sunderer, who was sundered once and shall be whole again, who carries the shore in one hand and the sea in the other, and whose coming rekindles the war the victors buried.*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has texture.

"The Sunderer," Juniper repeated. "Who *was* sundered once. And shall be made whole again."

"That's what it says."

"And the war — the old war? The one with the drowned city?" Her voice was carefully light, the way it got when she was frightened. "That war ended a hundred years ago. Everyone says it ended."

"Everyone says a lot of things ended." Bram thought of the ledger shelves below, of the strange cargo that passed through the Gildenscale's hands — relics salvaged off the Sundered coast, sea-glass that whispered, a helm in the deep cellar that nobody would store next to anything else. "Ending a war and burying it aren't the same trade."

Juniper took the codex from them — carefully, the way you handle something asleep — and read the passage again herself, lips moving. Then she laughed, one short, unhappy note.

"'Who carries the shore in one hand and the sea in the other.'" She shook her head. "It's a riddle. Prophecies love riddles. It doesn't have to be literal. It could be anyone who stands between the two worlds. Anyone who—"

Her voice stopped.

Bram watched it stop, watched the exact moment the meaning arrived behind her eyes, and felt the same arrival happen in their own chest, cold as water finding a crack.

Because everyone in the Gildenscale stood between worlds. That was the whole trade. Vael, who bargained with things that had no business existing. Osk, who read the moods of water like other men read weather. Juniper, who wore other people's names the way other people wore coats. Even old Pell, who kept the door between the street and the impossible.

"Half-and-half," Juniper said softly. "That's what we are. All of us. Shore-folk who deal in sea-things. Sea-things that live on land." She set the codex down as if it had grown teeth. "This prophecy doesn't name a person. It describes a *job description*. And half this household qualifies."

"Not half," Bram said. "All."

They looked at each other across the lamplit table, and Bram understood, in that moment, that something had changed in the room that neither of them could take back. An hour ago this had been a study, and Juniper had been Juniper, and the worst thing in the world was an unbalanced ledger. Now the codex lay open between them like a trap sprung shut, and every quality Bram loved in these people had turned, quietly, into evidence.

*Who was sundered once and shall be whole again.*

Osk, who had come up out of the water as a child and never spoke of where from.

Vael, who had built a self out of auctioneer's polish so seamless that even she might not remember what was underneath.

Juniper, who forgot herself so thoroughly in other people's hands that sometimes, some mornings, she asked Bram to remind her what her childhood dog had been called.

And Bram themselves, running from a mother who wanted their gift, carrying a name they weren't sure they owned.

"Supper," Juniper said abruptly. "We should go down. We should — eat eels. Like normal people. Who are not named in century-old books."

"Yes," Bram said.

Neither of them moved.

* * *

Supper at the Gildenscale was usually the best hour of the day. Long table in the main hall, benches worn smooth by generations of traders, a fire in the great hearth that Pell claimed had burned continuously since before the war — "which is why the house is lucky," he'd say, "and why none of you are allowed to spit near it." Osk cooked the way he did everything: precisely, silently, without any visible pleasure that he nonetheless clearly felt. Vael presided. There were rules to supper, unspoken but absolute: no business, no ledgers, no prices. For a few hours the merchants of impossible things were simply people who ate together, argued about the fire tax, stole bread off each other's plates.

Tonight, Bram noticed everything.

They noticed that Osk served the eels headless — which he always did — but that he did it facing away from the table, at the sink, and that his shoulders stayed high and tight as a man braced against cold water.

They noticed that Vael had dressed for supper, actually dressed, in the grey silk she saved for negotiations, and was laughing a little too readily at Pell's story about the bishop and the goat, her rings clicking against her glass in a rhythm Bram had never heard from her — quick, nervous, like rain on a window.

They noticed that Juniper sat directly across from Bram, which she never did — she preferred the corner seat, back to the wall, forger's instincts — and that she kept glancing at the codex-shaped absence on the table above, where Bram had left it locked in the study, and then quickly away, as if checking whether anyone else had noticed her noticing.

They noticed that Pell had set the table for five, and there were four of them, and that he had put the fifth plate out anyway, at the end, the way he did every night, because — he'd said once, when Bram asked — "a house should always be ready for whoever's coming." Bram had found it sweet, three years ago. Tonight it looked like an omen setting its own place.

"So," said Vael, refilling her cup with a hand that was perfectly steady, which was itself suspicious, because Vael's hands trembled only when she let them. "Osk tells me the tide's gone strange. And our young archivist tells me my new acquisition is a book of prophecy. And nobody," she smiled brilliantly around the table, "has brought either of these matters to me directly. Which means we're all being very polite, and very afraid, and eating eel about it."

"Mistress Vael—" Bram began.

"Bram." She cut them off gently, and her eyes, when they fixed on Bram, had the flat shine of an appraiser's lens. "Tell me about the Sunderer."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Pell stopped chewing. Osk turned from the sink, slowly, dish towel still in his hands.

So Osk had told her. Of course he had. Vael was the guild; you told Vael. Bram felt something twist — not jealousy exactly, but a small cold proof that the family had channels of trust that did not include them, that they had been here three years and were still, in certain currents, the newest water.

"It's a figure in the codex," Bram said carefully. "A destroyer. One who breaks the peace between the sea-folk and the shore. The prophecy says the Sunderer will reignite the old war."

"And the codex gives a description." It was not a question. Vael had read the book too, then, or part of it — or Osk had described it to her. Either way, she was ahead of Bram, and Vael hated being behind more than she hated losing money.

"'Who was sundered once,'" Bram quoted, watching every face at the table, "'and shall be whole again. Who carries the shore in one hand and the sea in the other.'"

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Outside, far off, the harbor slapped against pilings with a sound that seemed, tonight, less like water than like slow applause.

"Well," said Pell at last, into his cup. "That's half the coast. Half the *world*, if you count marriages."

"Pell." Vael's voice was soft.

"I'm saying," the old man persisted, stubborn as driftwood, "that a riddle like that fits anyone you want it to fit. That's the trouble with prophecies. They're written wide on purpose. You could hang that description on the harbourmaster. On the queen. On any smuggler ever worked both sides of the strait. It's a net with holes big enough to drive a cart through, and the only fish it's guaranteed to catch is the one what reads it too close."

"A net catches what it catches," Osk said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. He had returned to the table at last and stood at its head, dish towel folded over his arm like a servant who had once been something else. His pale eyes — eyes the exact grey-green of deep water, eyes that Bram realized they had never once seen surprised — moved from face to face.

"You all felt it," he said. "The moment the description landed. Each of you thought of yourselves first. Don't lie; it's written on you like tide-lines." He laid the towel down. "I'll save us the courtesy of pretending otherwise. When I heard 'carries the sea,' I thought of me. I came out of that water. I don't remember how. I don't remember why. Some mornings I wake with salt in my mouth and a word in my head that isn't a word in any language I know, and it feels like the last piece of a sentence, and I am *terrified*, most days, of hearing the rest of it."

The honesty of it landed on the table like something dead. Bram's chest ached. They loved him for it and hated the codex for making him do it.

"And I," said Vael, after a moment, with the air of a woman paying a toll, "thought of myself. Because I have spent thirty years building a woman out of nothing, and a woman built out of nothing is, by definition, someone who was sundered once." She lifted her cup. "To self-knowledge. May it cost less than advertised."

"I thought of me," Juniper said, too fast, and then, cornered by four pairs of eyes, gave a shrill little laugh. "Well — obviously. You all know what I do. I'm other people all day long. Sometimes I'm not entirely sure what's left over when I take the hands off. If the book wants someone who was broken into pieces and might get put back together wrong—" she spread her stained fingers, "—I'm practically the exhibit."

"And you, Bram?"

It was Pell who asked it. Old Pell, doorkeeper, keeper of the fire, who missed nothing that crossed his threshold. He asked it kindly, which made it worse.

Bram looked down at their plate. The eel had gone cold and oily. *Who was sundered once.* They thought of a cottage in the hills, and a woman with clever hands and a voice like a latch, and a night three years ago when Bram had climbed out a window rather than become whatever Maren needed them to be. They thought of the name on the guild rolls — *Bram Brightwater, apprentice, keeper of accounts* — a name chosen from nowhere, adopted, assembled. A self built out of escape.

"I thought of myself too," they said. "Everyone here did. That's the point. That's what the book *wants*. It wants us looking sideways at each other over supper instead of looking at it."

"Clever," said Vael. "And true. And completely useless, because knowing a trap is a trap doesn't lift your foot out of it." She set down her cup with a click. "Tomorrow we convene properly. Full council. We treat this codex as what it now is — the most dangerous item this guild has ever held — and we decide what to do with it. Until then—" her gaze swept the table, auctioneer's gavel in her eyes, "—nobody reads alone. Nobody decides alone. And nobody," a beat, delicate as a knife laid on silk, "concludes that the prophecy is about *them* and acts on it privately. Are we understood?"

Nods all around. Sincere, Bram judged, in four different ways and to four different degrees.

Pell gathered the plates. Osk banked the fire. And Bram, climbing the stairs to bed past the study door, felt the pull of the codex through the wood like heat from an oven — and made themselves walk past it, and lay in the dark listening to the house settle, thinking:

*Five of us. Five descriptions that fit. And a book that's still being written.*

Below, faint through the floorboards, they heard Juniper's footsteps cross to the study. Pause. Cross away.

Pause.

Cross back.

And stay.

Bram lay staring at the ceiling, telling themselves that Juniper was only checking the lock, only touching the cover, only being Juniper. Telling themselves that love and suspicion could not share a bed. Knowing, with the terrible clarity of the sleepless, that the prophecy had already done its first work: it had taken a family that shared everything and given each of them, silently, a room of their own to hide in.

Downstairs, the fifth plate sat washed and gleaming at the end of the empty table, waiting for whoever was coming.

Ten days remained.
