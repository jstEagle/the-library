## 6. Letters from the Hedge

The letter came on a morning so cold that the harbor had grown a skin of ice along its edges, thin as breath on glass, and Bram was warming their hands over the brazier in the counting room when Pell's knock came at the door—two soft raps, then one, the guild's old signal for *something you won't like*.

"Rider left this at the gate," Pell said, holding out a square of grey paper as though it might bite. "No seal. Just your name."

Bram's name. Not *the keeper of accounts*, not *Gildenscale*, not any of the half-dozen false names they kept filed away for exactly such occasions. Just *Bram*, written in a hand they knew better than their own—the hand that had taught them theirs, every loop and crossbar corrected with a dry quill-tap against the knuckle until the letters stood straight as fence posts.

They did not take it at once. Pell waited, patient as furniture, his old face folded into something between sympathy and curiosity.

"You know who it's from," he said.

"I know whose hand it is." Bram took the letter. It weighed almost nothing. Things that weigh almost nothing are often the heaviest things in a room; Bram had learned that in ledgers long before they learned it in life. "Thank you, Pell."

He lingered a moment longer, the way he always did when he wanted to ask something and knew better than to ask it, then shuffled off down the corridor, his boots whispering on the stone.

Bram sat back down at the counting table. The brazier ticked and settled. Around them, the Gildenscale went about its morning: somewhere above, Vael's voice rose and fell through the rehearsal of an auction patter; somewhere below, Juniper's pen scratched in the scriptorium, making lies that looked more true than truth ever managed to. The sounds of home. Bram held the letter over the brazier's warmth for a moment—not to open it, just to feel it—and then broke the plain fold.

*Child.*

Not even *dear child*. Maren never wasted ink on tenderness when there was business to be done.

*You will have seen the signs by now, or you are no student of mine, which we both know is a lie neither of us can afford. The tide runs wrong. The frost came early and came hungry. And if you have found what I think you have found—a book older than it has any right to be, warm where paper should be cold—then you already know why I am writing.*

*I have read them too. The signs. I have been reading them since before you were born, though I did not understand until this winter what I was reading toward.*

*I am coming to Saltmere. Do not run. You have gotten fast, I grant you—three years is long enough to grow quick and clever—but you cannot outrun this. No one can. Not even you, with all your gift for seeing what is truly written.*

*When I arrive, show me your hands.*

That was all. No signature. She had never needed one; her handwriting was a signature, was a summons, was a wall coming down. Bram read it three times, and by the third reading their eyes had stopped moving and were simply fixed on the last line while the rest of the room went quiet around them.

*Show me your hands.*

Three years, and she opened with the same words she had used to open every lesson of Bram's childhood. Show me your hands, and Bram would hold them out palms-up, and Maren would turn them over, inspect the nails, the calluses, the ink stains, as though reading a text written in skin—and perhaps she had been. Perhaps that was exactly what she had been doing all those years, and Bram had been too young, too eager, too *grateful* to see it.

The letter slipped from their fingers onto the counting table. The brazier popped. Above, Vael's auction patter reached a practiced crescendo and fell away into applause from no one.

Bram put both hands flat on the wood, palms down, and pressed until the bones ached, as if they could pin themselves to the present tense.

* * *

The hedge-witch's cottage had smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and, underneath both, of salt—though the sea was two days' walk from Maren's door, and Bram had never once thought to wonder about that until much later, when wondering had become the only thing they had left.

Maren had taken them in at seven years old, or so the story went; Bram's memories of before were a wet grey blur, a road, someone crying. A hedge-witch collecting a stray child was common enough in the upland villages. What was not common was what Maren did next, which was teach the child to read.

Not ordinary reading. Any child could learn ordinary reading. Maren sat Bram down at her scarred oak table with a page of a hymnal and said, *Read me what it says,* and Bram read the printed verse aloud, and Maren said, *Now read me what it says,* and Bram, confused, read it again, and Maren struck the table hard enough to make the lamp jump and said, *Again. Look again. What does it say?*

And Bram had looked, really looked, the way you look at water when you're trying to count the fish beneath it, and the page had—shifted. The way heat shifts the air above a fire. Underneath the printed verse, in letters that were not ink but something older, something that seemed to sit *behind* the paper rather than on it, another line waited:

*We bind the sea with songs of shore, till it forgets the shape of war.*

Bram had recited it without understanding, and the silence afterward had lasted so long they'd looked up afraid they'd done something wrong, and found Maren staring at them with an expression Bram would spend the next decade learning to name and still hadn't, quite. It wasn't pride. Pride was warmer. It was closer to hunger—the look of a woman who has walked a long way in the dark and finally sees a light she'd begun to believe was a story.

*True text,* Maren called it, when she finally explained. The layer beneath all writing, where things remembered themselves. Every book had it, if it had lived long enough, if enough hands had touched it, if anything true had ever passed through it. Most people went their whole lives without knowing the world kept records beneath its records. But a reader of true text—true readers were born maybe once in a generation, maybe once in ten, and they could not be made, only found.

*Found,* Maren said, and looked at Bram, and smiled, and Bram was nine years old and basked in it like sunlight.

It took six more years for the sunlight to show its other face.

* * *

The quarrel, when it finally came, was about a ledger.

Not one of Bram's—they didn't keep ledgers then, that habit belonged to the Gildenscale, to later, to safety—but one of Maren's, an old thing bound in green leather gone black at the corners, which she kept locked in the bottom of her herb chest and which Bram, sixteen and curious and trusted, had never once been tempted to open. Until the night Maren forgot to lock the chest. Or—Bram understood now, turning the memory over the way you turn a stone to see what lives under it—until the night Maren *left it unlocked*, because she had decided her apprentice was ready, and had chosen a stranger's way of saying so.

Inside the ledger, in Maren's own hand, were accounts. Not of herbs sold or charms mended. Of readings. Dates, places, and beside each one, in a column Maren had headed simply *yield*, notes in her cramped, exact script: *Boy at Millbrook, age 11, read a christening record. True line concerned a debt owed by his grandfather. Yield: the mill changed hands.* *Woman of the fen villages, read her mother's funeral roll. True line named a living man among the dead. Yield: the man fled; his lands forfeit to the parish; the parish tithes to us.* Page after page. Years of it. Bram's own name appeared too, in entries going back to their first lessons, each reading logged, each yield calculated, as though Bram were not a child at a table but a well being drawn from, and the water measured.

At the bottom of the last filled page, in fresher ink, a single line:

*The codex requires a true reader. When the signs converge, the child reads the book. All else follows.*

Bram had not confronted her. That was the part they could never forgive themselves for, in the small hours, in the honest dark—that they had not thrown the ledger on the fire and demanded answers, had not shouted, had not cried. They had closed the green ledger, returned it to the chest, and gone upstairs, and lain awake listening to Maren's kettle ticking below, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the person who had fed them and taught them and corrected their letters with a dry tap of the quill had also, all along, been keeping accounts on them.

They ran three days later, in the hour before dawn, with a satchel of clothes and the hymnal—the only book they took, though they couldn't have said why, except that it was the first place they had ever seen true text, and some part of them must have known they would need to remember how it began.

They walked for a month. They starved a little. And then, on a grey afternoon in the port city of Saltmere, half-dead and hiding in the doorway of a shuttered shop, they had watched a procession come down the street carrying a palanquin on which rode a glass bottle the size of a piglet, and inside the bottle a storm turned slowly on itself like a cat settling, lightning folding into lightning, and a woman in auctioneer's black leaned out from the shop door beside Bram and said, "You're blocking my light, and you're looking at my merchandise like you're pricing it. Either of those can be arranged to your advantage."

Vael. Who had fed them that night. Who had asked no questions for a week, and then asked all of them at once, and liked the answers, or at least found them useful. Who had given Bram a desk and a ledger and said, *Numbers don't lie, and neither do you, I suspect, which makes you rare in this building.*

Belonging bought with secrecy. Bram had known the price when they paid it. They had simply believed—three years of believing it—that the purchase was final, that nothing from before could follow them across the threshold of the Gildenscale.

The letter on the counting table said otherwise.

* * *

They told the council that evening, because the guild had rules and rule the first was *no secrets that touch the whole house*. They set the letter in the middle of the supper table, between the bread and the lamp, and let them read.

Osk read it twice, his broad weathered face going still in the way it did when the water showed him something he didn't like. Juniper read it once, fast, and looked at Bram with an expression caught between fury and fear. Vael read it slowly, the way she read contracts, hunting the clause beneath the clause.

"'Show me your hands,'" Vael quoted at last. "What does that mean?"

"It's how she started lessons." Bram kept their voice level. Their hands, under the table, were fists. "She'd inspect them. Nails, calluses, ink. I used to think it was affection."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was checking her investment."

Juniper pushed back from the table. "Then we don't answer the letter. We don't open the gate. One witch shows up asking after prophecy, right after we find a codex that names one of us—" she stopped herself, but everyone heard the end of the sentence anyway, *names one of us*, the suspicion that had been sitting at their suppers for a week now like an extra guest nobody had invited.

"She trained them," Osk said quietly. "If anyone knows what this book is, it's her."

"If anyone knows how to *use* Bram, it's her," Juniper shot back.

"That," said Vael, "is precisely why she comes through the front door instead of the wall." She tapped the letter once with one lacquered nail. "A woman who wanted to take something from this guild wouldn't announce herself. This is either honesty or strategy, and I've built a life on telling those apart, and I genuinely cannot." She looked at Bram. "Which means it's your call, keeper. She's coming for you. Do we bar the door, or do we hear her out?"

Every face turned to Bram. The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the window, and outside, far off toward the harbor, the ice creaked as the tide moved beneath it—backward, Osk had sworn that morning, backward, toward the drowned ruins, though Bram had made him promise not to say it aloud again until they understood more.

Bram looked down at their own hands, lying open on the table, ink-stained, callused, three years free of anyone's inspection.

"When does the frost break the roads?" they asked.

"Another week," said Pell, from the doorway, where he had been listening the way he listened to everything. "Maybe less, this winter."

"Then she'll be here within the month." Bram drew the letter back across the wood and folded it along its original creases, carefully, the way you close a book you intend to open again. "We leave the gate unlocked."

Juniper made a sound low in her throat, halfway to a word, and didn't finish it. Osk reached over and squeezed Bram's shoulder once, hard, and let go. Vael simply nodded and rang the little bell that ended council, and the family scattered to its evening work, and Bram sat alone at the table with the lamp burning down.

*Show me your hands.*

They flexed their fingers in the lamplight, watching the shadows move. Somewhere north, past the frozen roads, a woman who had taught them everything and accounted for every lesson was packing a bag—or sharpening something, or praying to something, or all three—and the worst of it, the part Bram could not say at supper and barely admitted to the dark, was that some traitor corner of their heart was glad.

Because Maren had read the signs. Maren, who had spent a lifetime reading what lay beneath the world's writing, had looked at this same winter, this same wrongness, and reached for the one person she had ever known who could see true text.

Whatever was coming—and the codex upstairs, warm as a sleeping animal, promised that something was—Maren believed Bram could face it.

Bram only wished they could decide whether that belief was a blessing or a bill come due.

Above them, faintly, through the floorboards, they heard the codex humming.
