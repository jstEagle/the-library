## 7. The Buyer with Grey Eyes

Mistress Vael had sold wonders for thirty years, and in that time she had learned one truth above all others: the worth of a thing was never in the thing itself, but in the wanting. A bottled storm was only glass and weather until someone stood before it, hungry, remembering a summer that nearly killed them. A widow's borrowed year was only sand and sorrow until you watched her count out coins she could not afford, because what price was too high for one more season of her husband's laugh? Vael's gift—the one that had built the Gildenscale from a rented stall on Fishmonger's Row into the shadowed grandeur of Saltspire House—was not magic at all. It was the ability to see wanting, to weigh it like an assayer weighs gold, and to name its price without flinching.

She was weighing it now.

The stranger sat across the auction table from her in the Green Room, where Vael conducted all business that required discretion. The room was designed to unsettle: walls of dark salt-cured timber, a single lamp burning whale-oil that gave off more smoke than light, and behind the buyer's chair—though he could not know this—the mounted skull of a leviathan calf, its empty sockets aimed at the back of his neck like a warning from a previous century of bad bargains.

He did not look unsettled. He looked, if anything, amused.

He was young, or young enough to matter, dressed in grey wool so fine it seemed woven from fog itself, cut in no style Vael recognized from shore or sea. His hair was the color of wet slate. His eyes were grey as well—a grey so pale it was nearly silver, nearly nothing—and they moved about the room with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory of his own.

"You'll forgive my directness," he said, "but I'm told the Gildenscale does not waste time."

"We waste other people's," Vael said pleasantly. "It's cheaper."

The smile he gave her was thin and correct. "Then let us be efficient. I wish to purchase the codex recovered from the Corvane estate. The prophetic codex, bound in eel-skin, three hundred and eleven pages, water-damaged at the corners." He recited it as though reading from a card. "I am prepared to offer four hundred crowns."

Vael did not move. Four hundred crowns was a fortune by any honest measure; it was the Gildenscale's operating capital for two years, Pell's pension thrice over, Juniper's freedom from debt to every counterfeiter in the Lower City. It was also, she calculated in the space between one heartbeat and the next, roughly forty times what the codex would fetch at open auction, where its provenance was murky and its contents, as far as any appraiser had yet determined, were the ravings of a drowned madman.

Which meant the stranger knew precisely what the codex contained. Or believed he did.

"Four hundred," she repeated, letting the number hang in the smoky air like something dead and bloated. "For a book of ash-brown scribbling and drowned-man nonsense. You must think me a very poor merchant indeed, Master...?"

"Corvane."

The name landed in the room like a dropped knife. Vael kept her face still, but inside, cold water began to move. Corvane. The seized estate whose effects Bram had been cataloguing when the codex surfaced—a shipping family, old money, old salt, wiped out in a single generation by debts and storms and, rumor said, worse things than either. The family had been extinct, or near enough, for decades.

"I understood the Corvane line ended," Vael said.

"Lines end," the stranger said. "Waters do not." He folded his hands upon the table. They were elegant hands, but Vael noticed—because she noticed everything, because noticing was the whole of her trade—that the skin between his fingers was faintly webbed, a translucent membrane no wider than a sliver of moon. Most people would have missed it. Most people would have doubted their own eyes even if they hadn't.

Vael had sold things to sea-folk before. She had never sold anything to one who walked so comfortably on land.

"The codex is not for sale," she said.

"Everything is for sale. That is the creed of your guild, is it not?" He tilted his head, and the lamp-smoke curled around him as though it too were curious. "I have studied your rules, Mistress Vael. Three inviolable laws: nothing traded under false pretense, nothing traded twice, nothing traded that belongs to the dead. I admire them. Very few institutions survive on principle alone." His pale eyes settled on her at last, and the weight of them was physical, like a hand laid flat against her sternum. "So I will make you an honest offer, under false pretense of nothing: five hundred crowns. And I will tell you why I want it, which no buyer in your experience has ever done."

Vael poured herself wine. She did not offer him any. Her hands were steady, which pleased her; thirty years of practice had bought her that much at least. "Speak, then."

"The codex contains a prophecy concerning the Sundering of the Sea," he said. "The war between the folk below and the folk above. The war your histories call ancient, concluded, myth." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Your histories are wrong. The war was never concluded. It was *paused*. Sealed beneath a bargain struck in blood, and bargains, Mistress Vael, are simply transactions awaiting renegotiation." He leaned forward slightly. "The codex tells when the seal breaks. It names the figure through whom it breaks. And I am here to ensure that when it breaks, the right hands are holding it."

"And whose hands are those?"

"Mine."

* * *

Afterward, Vael could not have said how long the interview lasted. Time in the Green Room behaved strangely when the stakes grew teeth; she had seen buyers haggle for six hours over a love-charm and sellers surrender heirlooms in eleven seconds. But eventually the stranger rose, adjusted his grey sleeves, and left his card upon the table—a card of grey stock, unmarked except for a single embossed symbol: a wave, broken in half, the halves turned away from each other.

"The Remnant's mark," Vael said quietly, when the door had closed behind him. She said it to the empty room, to the leviathan skull, to herself. She had heard the name perhaps three times in her life, always in whispers, always attached to words like *treason* and *extinction* and *the old blood rising*. The Remnant were supposed to be a story told to frighten children of both peoples: the heirs of the sea-folk's war faction, the ones who had refused the sealing bargain, scattered and diminished and biding. Stories did not send buyers with four hundred crowns and webbed fingers.

Stories did not know that Bram Brightwater had found tonight's date inked in ash-brown script.

That was the detail that frightened her most, and she turned it over now as she climbed the stairs to the counting room, where the lamps burned late and the ledgers lay open like sleeping animals. The codex had been in the Gildenscale's possession for six days. Six days, and only five people alive knew it existed: herself, Bram, Osk, Juniper, and Pell. Yet the stranger had named it—described its binding, counted its pages—before offering a single coin.

Either the Remnant had eyes inside Saltspire House, or they had eyes on everything, everywhere, and there was no meaningful difference between the two possibilities. Both meant the same thing: the net was already drawn, and they were already in it.

In the counting room, she found Osk awake, as she had known she would. He sat by the window with his boots off and his bare feet in a basin of harbor water he'd carried up himself, reading the tide-lines in the ripples the way other men read tea leaves. He looked up when she entered, and whatever he saw in her face made him go still.

"You met him," Osk said.

"You know who he was?"

"I know what he smelled like." Osk lifted his feet from the basin, and the water in it went flat and dead-still, which water should never do indoors. "Deep water. Old water. Water that remembers being angry." He dried his feet slowly, not looking at her. "There's more. There's something I've been meaning—" He stopped. Started again. "The tide's been wrong, Vael. Pulling backward, toward the ruins. I've seen it once before. A long time ago. Right before—" He swallowed the end of the sentence, but Vael finished it for him, silently, in the privacy of her own skull: *right before the last winter of the old peace.*

"Not tonight," she said gently. "Tonight we vote. Tomorrow you confess everything, and I mean everything, Osk—I won't have secrets surfacing midwinter like drowned men." She softened it with a hand on his shoulder, the way she softened everything, because a leader's kindness was just strategy wearing a warmer coat. "Bring it to the table. That's what the table is for."

Osk nodded, and Vael turned to leave, and then paused at the door.

"Osk. If the codex names one of us—if what the stranger said is true, if there's a Sunderer coming—what do we do?"

Osk was quiet a long moment. Outside, the harbor wind moaned against the shutters like something grieving.

"We do what families do," he said finally. "We hold on tighter."

* * *

Vael slept badly, which was rare, and dreamed of the sea, which was rarer. In the dream she stood upon the frozen shore of the Sundered Sea itself, a place she had never been and never wished to be, watching figures walk out onto the ice: a boy with ledgers under his arms, a woman with ink-stained fingers, an old man carrying a door key the size of a sword. Behind them the horizon cracked like a plate, and from the crack came a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard—a vast, patient grinding, the sound of a bargain coming due.

She woke before dawn with her heart hammering and her mind already working, because fear was fuel if you refined it properly, and Vael had spent thirty years refining.

By lamplight she took out the guild's oldest ledger—older than her tenure, older than Pell's doorkeeping, a ledger begun by the Gildenscale's founder and maintained by every master since—and turned to the page of standing instructions written in the founder's own cramped hand. At the bottom, in ink faded to the brown of old rust, was a line Vael had read a hundred times and never once understood until this moment:

*When the deep comes asking, sell it nothing. Show it everything. Let it see how many of us there are.*

She closed the ledger. Outside, the first snow of the long winter was beginning to fall, early and wrong, fat flakes descending with the slow deliberation of ash.

Pride, Vael thought, pulling on her coat. Pride was the armor she wore best; she had forged it herself, link by link, from thirty years of refusing to be small. She had stared down magistrates and smugglers, dragon-toll collectors and the Dowager of the Salt Marshes, and she had never once lowered her price. She would not lower it now. Whatever came asking—grey-eyed, deep-blooded, patient as pressure—Saltspire House would show it nothing but a locked door and a raised chin.

And yet.

And yet beneath the pride, in the place where she kept the things she did not say aloud, fear moved like a current. Because the stranger had known the codex. Had counted its pages. Had spoken of the seal and the Sunderer as one speaks of household matters. And because—she admitted it now, in the dark, where honesty lived—he had not seemed like a man buying a book.

He had seemed like a man collecting on a debt.

Vael banked the lamp and went down to wake the others. There was a vote to call, a family to gather, a war to prevent—and if preventing it proved impossible, then there was a price to be set, and Vael Almeida, mistress of the Gildenscale, keeper of impossible things, intended to be the one who set it.

The snow fell harder. Somewhere beyond the harbor bar, far below the grey and thickening water, something old turned over in its sleep, and waited to hear what the shore-folk would decide.
