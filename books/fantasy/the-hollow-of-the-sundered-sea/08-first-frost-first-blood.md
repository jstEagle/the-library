## 8. First Frost, First Blood

Pell had kept the door of the Gildenscale for forty years, and in all that time he had learned to read the weather the way other men read letters—slowly, with his lips moving, and never quite believing it until it was too late.

So when the frost came down Saltcrown Hill on the ninth morning of autumn, silvering the cobbles and killing the widow Marrow's geraniums in a single night, Pell stood at the guild's threshold with his tea going cold in his hands and felt something old and cold turn over in his chest. Frost on the ninth of Harvestmonth. In his boyhood, that would have been an omen worth a copper to any fortune-teller worth her salt. In his old age, he knew better: omens did not need telling. They arrived on their own schedule, punctual as debt collectors, and they always came for what was owed.

He touched the doorframe—the same worn oak his own master had touched before him, back when the Gildenscale had been three rooms above a chandlery—and went inside to wake the household.

The frost did not lift by noon. It did not lift by evening either. By the second day the harbor ice had grown thin grey teeth along the pilings, and Osk came up from the tide-stairs shaking his head, wringing seawater from his sleeves as though the sea itself had offended him.

"Backward," he said, when Vael asked. He said it quietly, but the counting room had gone quiet enough that quiet carried. "It's still pulling backward. Not all the time. Like something breathing."

"Breathing," Vael repeated, in her auctioneer's voice, which could make even dread sound like a lot beginning at reasonable reserve.

"Or remembering," said Osk, and would say nothing more about it.

Pell said nothing either. But that night, after the lamps were trimmed and Juniper's pen had gone silent behind her door and Bram's candle burned its small stubborn light over the ledgers, Pell climbed to the attic where the guild kept the things no one bought anymore, and took down a box he had not opened since before Bram Brightwater had ever knocked on the Gildenscale's door.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay a soldier's coat. Grey wool, salt-eaten, a hole punched through the left breast no bigger than a plum stone. Pell sat with it across his knees for a long while, in the cold that was creeping down through the roof tiles weeks too early, and let himself remember.

Not the war itself—he had spent forty years not remembering the war itself, and he saw no reason to break the habit now—but the cost of it. That much he permitted. The cost was the part you carried.

He had been nineteen when the sea broke over the shore towns, and twenty-two when the last of it drained back, and between those two numbers there had been a winter so long the almanacs simply refused to print it. The Sundering Winter, the histories called it, though histories were written by people who hadn't eaten their boots. Three years of frost. Fish frozen mid-river. A king drowned in his own harbor trying to flee south. And the dead—not the soldiers first, either; the soldiers went early and quick, mostly. It was the ones after, the ones in the towns, the ones who starved elegant and slow, who taught Pell what a long winter actually was. It wasn't weather. It was arithmetic. Every week of ice was so many mouths, and every mouth was a ledger entry, and somewhere at the bottom of that ledger was a line with your own name on it, waiting patiently to be written.

His brother Aldous had a name like that. Pell had carried the coat home for him because there was no one else to carry anything home.

* * *

On the fourth day of the unnatural frost, Vael summoned the household to the counting room, and Pell watched her tell them what the tide-reader had already told him alone: that a second fragment existed. That a bookbinder in Greyharbor—a cousin of a cousin, one of the guild's far-flung eyes—had come into possession of loose leaves matching the codex's hand. Ash-brown ink, the same warm paper, the same century-old certainty. And that she was willing to trade, for a price the guild could meet, if someone came quickly.

"Quickly is doing a great deal of work in that sentence," said Juniper, arms folded, ink stains to the elbow as always. "Greyharbor is six days' ride in good weather. In this?" She nodded at the window, where the frost had climbed halfway up the glass like something trying to get in.

"In this," Vael agreed, "it is four days' ride and worth every hour of it. We do not leave prophecy fragments lying about in market towns, Juniper. Not when we know what the first one has already cost us." Her gaze flicked—to Bram, sitting straight-backed at the ledger desk with their pen still in hand, pretending very successfully not to be the subject of the sentence. To Osk, staring at the floor. Back to the middle distance, where auctioneers keep their secrets.

"I'll go," Pell said.

Every head turned. He had expected that. At seventy-one, he moved like a ship at anchor, slow and deliberate and impossible to shift without effort, and everyone in the room knew his knees made the stairs an argument.

"You'll do no such thing," Vael began.

"I know Greyharbor. I ran cargo through Greyharbor before you were born, girl, and half the town still owes me favors they're ashamed of. The bookbinder will open her door for me and bolt it for anyone younger." He held up a hand before the objections could organize themselves into a chorus. "And I won't go alone. Sella and Tam go with me. Two riders and an old man in a cart full of hides—that's nobody. That's a tanner and his grandchildren. Nobody looks twice at a cart full of stinking hides."

Sella and Tam were the youngest of the guild's field agents, quick and light and loyal, and Pell loved them both with the specific tenderness of a man who had buried enough people to know exactly how much each love was going to cost him someday. He told himself, on the road out of the city with the frost smoking off the fields, that he was bringing them because they were good at this. He did not tell himself the other thing, the truer thing: that the guild was a family, and families in stories always sent the children away from danger, and Pell had stopped believing in stories the year he'd dug Aldous's coat out of the mud.

He should have brought them anyway. He should have left them both behind, locked in the cellar with the pickled eggs and the bottled fog, safe as books. There are nights afterward, many nights, when he lies awake and arranges the journey differently in his head, sends them back at the crossroads, goes on alone with the cart and his bad knees and his forty years of knowing better. But memory is a coward's country, and Pell had lived there long enough to know its borders by heart. You cannot garrison the past. You can only pay its taxes.

* * *

They reached Greyharbor on the evening of the third day, ahead of schedule, because the frost had hardened the mud ruts into something almost like road. The city smelled of coal smoke and fish rot and, underneath both, a smell Pell had hoped never to smell again: the particular mineral stink of deep cold, cold coming up out of the ground instead of down from the sky. Cold like that had a direction. Cold like that came from under the world.

Maren's cousin's cousin turned out to be a small sharp woman named Odile Vetch, whose shop smelled of glue and leather and old paper, and whose hands shook only slightly as she drew the fragment from a false-bottomed drawer.

"Three leaves," she said. "Loose. They were sewn into the binding of a psalter I bought at estate sale, if you can believe it. Hidden. Whoever hid them wanted them found eventually, or wanted them never found, and I've decided I don't want to be the one who decides which." She looked at Pell with the frank appraisal of a tradeswoman. "You're older than your reputation, Master Doorkeeper."

"My reputation is also older than me," Pell said. "We've gotten used to each other."

But when she laid the leaves on her counter and Pell bent close, the words swam in front of his old eyes, and it was Tam—young Tam, sharp-eyed Tam, who read everything, signs and ledgers and the faces of strangers—who leaned in and went suddenly, terribly still.

"Pell," Tam said. "This isn't the same text."

"What do you mean?"

"The codex Bram found—it was a whole page, a whole passage. This is—" Tam's finger hovered over the ash-brown script without quite touching it. "This is marginalia. Notes. Someone wrote these in the margins of the original and then cut them out." A pause. "One of them says *the keeper bleeds first.*"

The shop went very quiet. Outside, the wind came down off the hills carrying the smell of under-the-world cold, and somewhere in the street a dog began to howl and could not seem to stop.

"Pack them up," Pell said. His voice sounded like someone else's voice, an old man's voice, cracked and careful. "Wrap them in oilcloth. Sella, saddle up. We ride tonight, in the dark, and we don't stop until the city walls."

Odile wrapped. Sella saddled. And Pell, standing in the doorway watching the empty street, felt the frost reach through the gap under the door and touch his boot, and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *this is how it started last time. Not with armies. With a scrap of paper and a dog howling.*

* * *

They made eleven miles before the ambush.

Pell counted them afterward, the way he counted everything, because counting was the only prayer he trusted: four of them, maybe five, in the trees above the ford at Wrenwater, where the road dipped toward the frozen river and the moon went behind cloud just often enough to be convenient. They wore no colors. They moved like professionals. And they were waiting—not for the cart, Pell understood even as the first arrow took the lantern off its hook, but for whoever carried the fragment. Someone had known. Someone had sold the errand before the cart ever left the city gate, and Pell filed that knowledge away in the place where he kept the war, to be opened later, at a price.

"Sella! The river!" he shouted, and the night came apart.

He would remember pieces. Everyone remembered pieces; whole memory was a luxury of people who hadn't been there. The arrow through the lantern. Tam's horse screaming. Sella, quick Sella, faithful Sella, putting herself between the riders and the old man and the cart, sword out, shouting words the wind took. The ice of the ford groaning under hooves. The fragment—where was the fragment—Tam had it, Tam had tucked the oilcloth packet inside his jacket, Tam was off his horse and running for the treeline, running the wrong way, running to draw them off, young and stupid and brave in exactly the way Pell had been at nineteen and had sworn never to encourage—

An arrow took Tam in the back at the edge of the trees.

Pell did not remember deciding to move. Forty years of doors, forty years of standing between the Gildenscale and whatever knocked, and his body knew its work even when his mind had gone white and ringing. He got to Tam somehow, across the ice that cracked and complained beneath his weight, and dragged him behind a fallen tree while the horses fought and Sella shouted and the ambushers, finding their prize contested and their position given away, melted back into the dark with the professionalism of men paid by results.

Then it was quiet, except for the river muttering under its ice, and the dog—some farm dog miles off, or perhaps only the wind—howling and howling.

Tam died in the space of an hour, which was longer than he needed and shorter than Pell wanted. The arrow had gone deep. There was nothing for it, nothing in the saddlebag kit, nothing in the world, and Tam knew it before Pell could find the lie to tell him. Young Tam, who read everything, read his own blood soaking the frost and smiled up at the old man with lips going grey.

"Hurts less than I thought," Tam said. "Is that—does everyone know that? Should we tell people?"

"We'll put it in the newsletter," Pell managed, and Tam laughed, and the laugh cost him something he didn't have, and then there were no more jokes because there was no more Tam to make them.

But he had held onto the packet. Even dying, even at the end, his fist stayed closed around the oilcloth like a doorkeeper at his post, and it was Pell, kneeling in the frost with his hands red to the wrist, who finally pried the fingers loose—one by one, gently, the way you close a ledger entry—and unwrapped the scrap and held it up to Sella's shaking lantern-light.

Ash-brown ink. Century-old certainty. Four words, in a margin cut from a larger truth:

*the keeper bleeds first.*

Below it, smaller, in the same hand, a line that none of them would understand until later, that would sit in Pell's memory like a splinter working toward his heart:

*and the account is settled in kind.*

* * *

They rode back through three days of hardening frost, Sella driving the cart, Pell holding the reins and saying nothing, the body wrapped in sacking in the back among the hides because there was nothing else to do and nowhere else to stop. Once, at dawn, Sella asked him whether he believed in omens, and Pell considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"I believe in costs," he said at last. "Omens are just costs arriving early. Somebody's always keeping the books."

At the gates of the city, the guards waved them through without a second glance—a tanner's cart, two tired travelers, nobody. Inside, the streets were iced to a shine, and people moved carefully, the way people move in a city that has begun, without admitting it, to be afraid.

Vael met them at the door of the Gildenscale. She took one look—at Pell's face, at the cart, at the shape under the sacking—and the auctioneer fell away from her like a cloak dropped, leaving only a woman standing in a doorway with frost coming down the hill behind her.

"Pell," she said.

"One dead," he said. "The fragment's intact. And Vael—" He stopped, because the next thing was the hardest thing, harder than the ford, harder than the hour with Tam's hand going cold in his. "Someone sold us. Before we ever left. They knew the road, they knew the timing. There's a seller in this house or near it, and until we know who, I'd count the spoons."

He watched the words land in her, watched her pride take the blow and her fear absorb it, and understood, with the weariness of his years, that this was how it had gone the last time too. Not with trumpets. With a scrap of paper and a dead boy and a family looking at one another sideways across a supper table, wondering which face was the mask.

That night the household buried Tam in the yard behind the guild, in ground too hard to dig properly, with Osk reading the tide and Juniper crying silently and furiously at the same time and Bram standing apart, white-faced, holding the scrap of ash-brown paper as though it might burn them—or as though, Pell thought, watching them, they had already begun to suspect whose blood those four words were talking about.

*The keeper bleeds first.*

Pell stood at the graveside longest, as was his right and his custom, and let the grief settle into the place where he kept the rest of it, the great cold warehouse of it, Aldous's coat and the Sundering Winter and every name in every ledger he had ever closed. Grief was inheritance, in the end. The oldest thing any of them owned. You inherited it from the dead the way you inherited a house: you didn't choose it, you couldn't refuse it, and sooner or later you found yourself living in its rooms.

When he finally came inside, stamping the frost from his boots, he found Vael waiting in the hall with a lamp, her face set in lines he had not seen on her since the night she'd bought the guild's freedom from the harbor tax-men with nothing but her voice and a forged deed.

"There's a rider at the gate," she said. "Arrived an hour ago. Says she's expected." A pause, and something moved behind her eyes—wariness, curiosity, the beginnings of an auctioneer's appraisal. "She says her name is Maren, and she's come about the book."
