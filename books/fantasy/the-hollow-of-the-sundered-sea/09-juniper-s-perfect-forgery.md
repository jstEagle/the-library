## 9. Juniper's Perfect Forgery

The trick to a perfect forgery, Juniper had always said, was not in the ink. Anyone could match ink. You could grind the same oak galls, steep them in the same rainwater, add the same bitter pinch of iron salts, and still produce something that a careful eye would catch in a heartbeat—because paper remembers, and hands remember, and time is the one material no forger can buy.

So she had learned to steal time instead.

She was doing it now, in the back room above the Gildenscale's counting floor, with three candles burning down around her and a dead woman's prayer book open on her lap. The book had belonged to a lighthouse keeper who had died forty years ago on the Saltmere rocks, and its pages were soft as moth wings, yellowed at the edges the way only genuine neglect can yellow them. Juniper had bought it honestly from the keeper's granddaughter, who had wept a little and taken the coins anyway. That was the part of the trade nobody put in the songs: that every forgery began with someone's real grief, and you had to hold it gently while you took it apart.

She peeled a leaf of the old paper free at the gutter, where the binding would hide the wound, and laid it flat beneath her lamp. Then she set the codex beside it—the true codex, the one with tonight's date written in ash-brown script and a line about *the keeper of accounts* that had turned their supper table into a battlefield of glances—and began, as she had begun a hundred times before, to become someone else.

Not someone else, exactly. Someone *elsewhen*. That was the real craft. A forger did not imitate a hand; she inhabited it. She had to think in the rhythm of a century-dead scribe, feel the ache in his wrist, know which letters he hurried through when his candle was guttering and which he loved enough to slow down for. She had spent two days with the codex's original pages before touching anything, learning the prophet's habits the way you learn a lover's. He pressed too hard on his downstrokes. His ascenders leaned east, like grass in a wind off the water. He crossed his t's with a single impatient flick, except when he was writing words he feared—then the crossbar went on slow and careful, like a hand laid on a wound.

He had been afraid of very many words.

Juniper dipped her pen—a quill she had cut from the same breed of goose, cured in smoke because she'd found scorch marks on the codex's margins and deduced the man wrote near a fire—and touched it to the stolen page.

*In the winter of the long frost,* she wrote, in his hand, in his fear, *the Sunderer shall come among the shore-folk wearing a borrowed face, and—*

She stopped. Her own breath had gone strange in her throat.

Because here was the thing about prophecy that no one had warned her, back when she was a girl learning to fake seals and signatures in a smuggler's den on the Corrow coast: prophecy is not like other text. Other text sits still and lets you do what you like to it. Prophecy *pushes back*. It has weight, the way deep water has weight, and when you write it—even false prophecy, especially false prophecy—you have to believe your way through every word, or the belief shows. A lie written without conviction is just ink. A lie written with total conviction becomes something else entirely, something with a pulse, and Juniper had discovered long ago that she could do this—that she could make herself believe absolutely, completely, for exactly as long as the sentence required, and then stop.

Most forgers couldn't. Most forgers either couldn't summon the belief or couldn't put it down again afterward. Juniper could do both. It was, she sometimes thought, the only honest thing about her: she was a very good liar precisely because she never confused the lie with herself.

She finished the line. It read beautifully. It named a Sunderer with a borrowed face, born under a false name, marked by a scar on the left hand—details she had invented whole, details designed to point at anyone but the people she loved, details that Vael would sell to the grey-eyed buyer for a fortune in guild credit and a season's grace. It was good work. It might have been her best work.

And it was warm under her fingers. Not lamp-warm. *Body*-warm, like a page that had been held in a sweating palm.

Juniper set down the pen and looked at her hands. They were steady. They were always steady; that was the first thing they taught you in the den, that a shaking hand tells the truth your mouth wants to hide. But there was a smudge of ash-brown ink along the side of her right palm, and she had ground no such ink tonight. She had used ordinary black.

She looked at the codex. Its pages lay closed and innocent, and yet she could not shake the certainty—absurd, baseless, absolute—that somewhere inside it, a line had changed. That her false prophecy had reached into the true one the way a root reaches through a wall, and something over there had shifted to make room.

"Don't be stupid," she said aloud, to the empty room, in the voice she used for frightened apprentices. It worked on apprentices. It did nothing for her.

* * *

She worked through the night, because the deadline was real: the grey-eyed buyer had given Vael until the week's end, and Vael had stood in the counting room with her auctioneer's smile bolted on so firmly it looked painted, and said, "We will consider the offer," which in Gildenscale meant *we need time to forge something*, and everyone had known it, including, Juniper suspected, the buyer himself.

The plan was simple, the way all dangerous plans are simple. Sell the Remnant a codex. Not the true one—a sister to it, aged convincingly, prophesying a Sunderer who matched no one in the guild, sending the Remnant's hunters chasing a ghost with a scarred left hand across half the coastline while the guild found the drowned archive Osk's backward tide kept pointing toward. Buy a season. Maybe two.

Simple. Except that forging prophecy required believing it, and by the third night, Juniper noticed something wrong with her memories.

It started small. She was aging a fresh batch of pages in the smoke box—she used a mixture of beechwood and dried kelp, because the codex's original leaves smelled faintly of the sea when warmed, and she had spent an entire afternoon with her nose in the book like a hound establishing that fact—when she caught herself humming a tune she didn't know. A child's skipping song, in a language she almost recognized. She stopped. She did not know any children's songs from anywhere. She had grown up in the smugglers' den on the Corrow coast, and the den's music had been work chants and warning whistles, and no one had ever skipped.

Except—hadn't there been a girl? A girl who taught her rhymes behind the fish racks? Juniper reached for the memory and found it, whole and vivid: salt wind, the girl's chapped lips, the way the rhyme ended on a note that made your chest hurt. She could smell the fish racks. She could feel the cold of the cobbles through her knees.

But the den had no fish racks. She had drawn the den a hundred times in her head over the years—it was a cellar, a low stone cellar with a trapdoor and a rope ladder, and she had slept in a hammock between two support beams, and there was no street outside it, no cobbles, no fish racks, no girls.

Two memories. Both hers. Both complete. Both certain.

She sat very still in the smoke-smelling dark and understood, with the particular clarity that comes at the worst possible moment, what believing does. For three nights she had believed herself a dying prophet writing of war and sacrifice, and belief is not a coat you take off; it soaks through. Somewhere in the making of her false codex, some part of her had needed a childhood that fit the hand she was forging—a childhood near the sea, near fish racks, near a girl who sang—and her mind, obliging craftsman that it was, had simply *made one*. Or found one. Or—

Or taken one. From somewhere. From the true codex's warmth, from the prophet's leaking centuries, from whatever lived in those ash-brown lines and wanted, perhaps, to be believed in too.

"That's not how memory works," she told the room.

The room, generously lit by three candles and one lie, declined to answer.

* * *

On the fifth day she took the finished forgery down to show Bram, because if anyone in the guild could tell her whether she had broken something, it was the keeper of accounts—the person whose reading of true text was so clean it hurt to watch, who saw documents the way Osk saw tides, straight down to the silt.

Bram turned the false codex over twice, ran a thumb along the smoked edges, sniffed the warmed page, and was quiet for a long moment.

"It's perfect," Bram said finally. "It's better than perfect. I've handled books older than this looks." A pause. "Juniper. What did it cost?"

"Nothing costs nothing. You know that better than anyone."

"I mean *you*. What did it cost *you*?"

And here was the trap she had walked herself into: a good liar lies fluently, instantly, and Juniper opened her mouth to say *nothing much, a few sleepless nights* and found that she could not. Not because the lie was hard, but because she genuinely did not know whether it would be a lie. She had woken that morning reaching for her mother's face and finding two mothers—one gaunt and sharp-voiced in the den, one soft and sea-smelling and singing—and no way to choose between them, no way even to ask which question was the right one.

"Ask me in a week," she said, which was the most honest sentence she had spoken in years, and hated how it sounded.

Bram looked at her the way Bram looked at ledgers that wouldn't balance—not accusing, just attentive, patient as arithmetic. "You know the rule," Bram said quietly. "Everything in the ledger gets entered eventually. Even the prices nobody wants to pay."

"Especially those," Juniper agreed, and smiled her workshop smile, the one that had gotten her out of four arrests and one marriage, and carried her forgery upstairs to wait for the buyer.

That evening she wrapped it in oilcloth and tied it with the kind of careless knot a desperate seller uses, and rehearsed her trembling hands and her widow's story, and everything was ready, and everything was fine.

It was only when she blew out the candles that she noticed the skipping song was still in her head, and that she now remembered *who had written it*.

A prophet. A hundred years dead. A man whose hand she knew better than her own mother's—one of her mothers, anyway—who had composed it for a little girl on the Corrow coast, behind the fish racks, in a life Juniper had never lived.

She stood in the dark with her heart going like a trapped bird, and downstairs, faint through the floorboards, came Pell's voice calling her name—urgent, ragged, the voice of a doorkeeper bringing bad news up from the door.

"Juniper! Juniper, you'd best come down. There's been trouble on the salt road—and they're saying—" a breath, ragged as torn cloth— "they're saying it's one of ours they found."
