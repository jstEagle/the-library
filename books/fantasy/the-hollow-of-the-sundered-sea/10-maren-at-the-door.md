## 10. Maren at the Door

The knock came at dusk, three soft raps like a hand asking permission rather than demanding entry, and every hair on Bram's arms rose before the sound had finished dying in the wood.

They had been balancing the week's ledger at the long table—bottled fog out, a widow's borrowed year returned with interest of two remembered afternoons, the columns neat as stitches—and their pen stopped mid-figure, leaving a small blue comet frozen above the page. Pell looked up from his chair by the door, where he spent most evenings now, one hand resting on the iron bar that could seal the Gildenscale against an army. His eyes went to the door, then to Bram, and something old and sad moved through his face like a fish under ice.

"You're expecting no one," he said. Not a question. He had learned to read Bram's ledgers almost as well as Bram did, and there was nothing in tonight's book about visitors.

"No one," Bram said. Their voice came out smaller than they intended.

The knock came again. Three soft raps. Patient. The patience was the worst part. Soldiers knocked hard; debt collectors knocked hard; only one person in all the world knocked as though she had all the time the sea and sky would allow her, and expected you to come to *her*.

Bram set down the pen. Their fingers were not quite steady, and they hated that, hated that three years and eleven hundred miles of running should count for so little against a sound.

"I'll answer it," Pell said, heaving himself up.

"No." Bram stood. "It's for me."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can smell the hedge."

That wasn't quite true—or it was true in a way that had nothing to do with noses. Maren's magic smelled, if smell were the right word at all, of cut thorns and rain about to fall and bread left too long in a cold oven. It had soaked into Bram's childhood the way smoke soaks into wool, and no amount of washing had ever gotten it out, because Bram had begun to suspect there was nothing underneath it to wash.

Pell hesitated, then drew back the bar and opened the door a hand's width, keeping his broad body in the gap like a doorframe himself.

A woman stood on the step with snow gathering on her shoulders. She was smaller than Bram remembered, or perhaps the world had simply grown larger since they'd last stood across from her. Her grey hair was braided in the same careless rope-braid she'd worn every day of Bram's early life, and her coat was patched at both elbows, and her boots—the same cracked brown boots—were crusted with road salt from a journey far longer than any honest traveler would have made in this season. She carried nothing. No pack, no staff, no satchel of bottles and bones. Just herself, and the cold, and that terrible patient stillness.

"Bram," Maren said.

Not Brightwater. Not apprentice. Just the name, the small one, the one only she had ever used, and hearing it was like pressing a thumb into a bruise Bram had convinced themselves had healed.

"Mistress Almeida is not receiving callers," Pell said, which was nonsense, since Bram Almeida was standing right there and everyone knew it, but Bram understood: he was offering them an exit, a fiction to hide behind if they wanted it.

Maren smiled at him over the gap in the door. It was a soft smile. It had always been a soft smile. That was the trap of it, Bram thought. A hard woman you could refuse. A soft one—

"They may call me Maren," she said. "I've come a long way, and I don't intend to stay long, and I won't ask anyone here for anything except what I've already asked." Her eyes found Bram's over Pell's shoulder. "I asked the road to bring me. It did. Now I'm asking my student to let me in out of the snow."

*Student.* Three years gone and she reached for the word like it was still hers to give.

Bram crossed the floor. They felt every step, as though the boards might remember them being dragged across similar floors once, small feet bare, learning which creaks meant Maren was awake and watching. They pulled the door wide.

The cold poured in. The snow swirled. Maren did not move.

"Well?" Bram said. "Come in, then. Before Pell changes his mind."

* * *

She sat where she was put—at the far end of the long table, hands folded, steam rising off the cup of nettle tea Osk brought her without being asked. Osk, who trusted the water and little else, studied her the way he'd study a strange current: carefully, from a distance, noting the direction of pull. Juniper hovered by the hearth pretending to mend a shirt that had needed no mending in years. Vael had not appeared at all, which meant she was listening from behind the curtain of the stairwell, and Bram knew it, and suspected Maren knew it too.

"So," Bram said, when the silence had stretched past bearing. "You read the signs."

"I read everything," Maren said mildly. "It's what I raised you for."

The words dropped into the room and lay there like stones. Bram watched Juniper's needle stop. Watched Osk's cup pause halfway to his mouth.

"That's not—" Bram began.

"It's exactly what happened," Maren said, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not here to pretend otherwise, Bramble. Pretense is a luxury for people who aren't out of time." She opened her coat—not reaching for anything, just showing the inside pocket, the gesture of a woman demonstrating she carried no weapon—and drew out a folded paper, worn soft at the creases. "May I? Or will your guild make me say my business in front of the whole household?"

"The whole household *is* my business," Bram said. "Say it."

Something flickered in Maren's eyes—approval, maybe, or grief wearing approval's face. "Three weeks ago the wells in my village went bitter. Not fouled—bitter. Like tears. The hens stopped laying. The river below the hill ran clear as glass but the fish wouldn't enter it. And the hedge roses bloomed out of season, black ones, which they do—" her voice caught, very slightly, and steadied— "which they do when something old is waking up. So I sat down with my books, and I cast what I know how to cast, and every reading came back pointing east. Toward the sea. Toward the Sundered Sea." She laid the paper flat on the table and turned it so Bram could see. It was a drawing, done in charcoal gone shiny with handling: a spiral, broken in seven places, each break marked with a rune Bram half-recognized from the codex's margins. "And every reading came back pointing at you."

"At me," Bram said flatly.

"At what you can do." Maren's hands stayed flat on the table, open, harmless. Deliberately harmless. Bram knew how much effort deliberate harmlessness cost. "Your gift. True text. You can look at any written thing and see what it actually says—not what the writer wanted it to say, not what the ink pretends, but the truth underneath. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what it means, in a winter like this one, when someone has edited a prophecy?"

The word *edited* landed harder than Maren could have known. Bram kept their face still. The drowned archive. The truth Osk and Bram had pulled up from beneath the Sundered Sea, wrapped in oilcloth and lies, hidden even now under the floor of the salt cellar. Half a truth told. If Maren knew what they were carrying—

"We have a fragment," Bram said carefully. "A second piece of the codex. We can't fully read it. The script resists."

"Because it's been tampered with." Maren nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. "Then I'm useful. That's why I came. Not to drag you home—you'd go sideways if I tried, you always did. I came to help you read what the Remnant doesn't want read. And then I'll leave, if leaving is what you want." She picked up her tea. Her hands, Bram noticed, had age spots now. New ones. When had she gotten old enough for new spots?

"And in exchange?" Vael's voice came from the doorway, silk over steel. She must have decided the eavesdropping was done its work. "Hedge-witches are practical folk. Nothing travels a hundred leagues in winter for free."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. "In exchange," she said, "I get to stand in a warm room with my student for one evening, and sleep knowing the person I trained for the worst winter of this age isn't facing it alone." She drank. "There. Is that transaction enough for a Gildenscale ledger? Write it down. One evening, warm room, paid in full."

Nobody laughed. But something in the room loosened anyway, the way a knot loosens when you stop pulling at it.

Juniper brought her a heel of bread and honey without a word. Maren accepted it like a queen accepting tribute, and ate like a starving woman trying not to show it, and Bram looked away because looking was suddenly unbearable.

* * *

Later—much later, when Osk had gone up and Juniper had mercifully followed, and Vael had withdrawn with a promise to "continue negotiations in the morning"—the house settled into the particular quiet of a building full of people pretending to sleep. Snow ticked against the windows. The fire had burned down to a red heart. And Bram sat at the table with the charcoal spiral between them, and Maren sat across from her student as she had sat across from them a thousand nights, and the years fell away until Bram couldn't tell whether they were twenty-two or twelve.

"The fragment," Maren said. "Before I sleep. Show me."

"Why the hurry?"

"Because the tide is wrong, Bramble. You know it's wrong. Your tide-reader knows it's wrong. Whatever the sea is doing out there, it's doing it faster than we are." She spread her palms on the table. "Show me, and by morning you'll know more than you know now. That's the whole offer. No hooks. No lessons. No—"

"Show me your hands first."

The words were out of Bram's mouth before they understood they'd said them, and then they heard them, heard whose voice they were spoken in—hers, Maren's own, from a thousand childhood evenings: *show me your hands first*, before every lesson, before every working, before Bram was allowed to touch ink or chalk or the thin bone-handled knife used for cutting sigils into candle wax. *A reader's hands are the reader's whole estate,* she used to say. *Let me see what state my estate is in.*

Bram had fled that. Had run three years and eleven hundred miles to escape being inspected, appraised, held up to the light like a bottle on a shelf. And here they sat saying her sentences back to her.

Maren went very still.

"I beg your pardon?" she said quietly.

"Your hands," Bram said, and their voice was steadier than they felt, or perhaps it simply sounded that way over the pounding of their heart. "Witch-craft leaves marks. Ink in the knuckle-creases, burn scars from the wax-work, the callus on the middle finger from the bone knife. If you want to handle our fragment—if you want to touch anything in this guild—I want to see what you've been doing on the road east. What workings you've cast. What prices you've paid." They leaned forward. "You taught me that readings always come with a cost, Mistress Almeida's teacher. So show me yours."

For a heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—the kitchen was silent as a held breath.

Then Maren laughed.

It was not the soft laugh from the doorstep. It was short and startled and genuinely delighted, and it cracked something open in Bram's chest that they immediately, furiously tried to close again.

"Oh, well met," Maren said, pressing a hand to her mouth. "*Oh*. Three years away and the first thing you do is turn my own rule on me." She shook her head, and the delight faded into something older and wearier and truer. "No. I don't think I'll show you my hands."

"Then no fragment."

"I understand." She didn't move to leave. She folded her hands on the table instead, deliberately, so that Bram could see them—see them *not* shown, palms down, fingers laced, every mark hidden. It was such an exact inversion of the old ritual that Bram's throat tightened. "But you should know what you're refusing, Bramble. My hands are ruined. There's ink in them that won't come out because I wrote things this autumn that shouldn't be written by anyone, and there's a scar on my left palm from a price I paid to learn where you'd gone, and there's—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "There's a mark on my right wrist that matches one in your codex. The seventh rune of the broken spiral. I didn't put it there. I woke up with it the night the wells went bitter, and it hasn't faded, and I think—" 

Her voice did not shake. Bram almost wished it would. Steadiness, from Maren, had always meant the worst things.

"I think the prophecy isn't finished writing itself," Maren said. "And I think some of us are already pages in it."

The fire shifted. Outside, far off, so faint it might have been imagination, came a sound Bram had learned to dread in the last weeks: the harbor groaning, the water pulling backward, dragging itself toward the drowned ruins as though the Sundered Sea were inhaling.

Maren tilted her head toward the sound, and for the first time since the doorstep, Bram saw fear cross her face—real fear, unhidden, gone in an instant but unmistakable.

"That's twice tonight I've heard it," she said softly. "At midnight and now. It's getting stronger. Whatever your fragment says, whatever your archive says—we have days, Bramble. Maybe less." She rose, stiff-kneed, and gathered her coat around her. "Keep your hands and I'll keep mine. But tomorrow, when the sun's up and your guild is watching, I'm going to ask you again. And tomorrow, I hope you're brave enough to be frightened with me."

She paused at the foot of the stairs, where Vael had indicated the guest room with a look rather than words.

"For what it's worth," Maren said, not turning around, "the braid suits you. You wore your hair like mine when you were small. I never told you to. You just did."

And she climbed the stairs, and her door clicked shut, and Bram sat alone at the long table with the charcoal spiral and their own two hands—unmarked, unread, refused—shaking slightly above the ledger.

It was only when they finally blew out the lamp that they noticed the codex, shut and locked in its case since morning, lying open on the sideboard across the room.

Open to the page with their date on it.

And the faded ash-brown script beneath it, which no one had touched, had darkened—fresh as wet ink, glistening faintly in the ember-light, one new line added where no line had been that morning:

*The keeper bleeds first — and the witch holds the blade.*
