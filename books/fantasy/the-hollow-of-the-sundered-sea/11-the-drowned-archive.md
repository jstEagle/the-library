## 11. The Drowned Archive

* * *

The tide went out before dawn and did not come back.

Osk stood on the shingle at the edge of the harbor with his boots in the wet sand and his hands in his pockets and watched the sea walk away from him like a dog called home by a stranger's whistle. It was not a spring tide. It was not a storm tide. It was the water itself turning its back on the shore, sliding backward over the flats in long silver sheets, draining toward the horizon where the drowned ruins of the Sundered Sea lay under forty fathoms of cold green memory.

He had seen it once before. He had been nine years old, and his grandmother had gripped his wrist hard enough to bruise and said nothing at all, which from her had been worse than screaming. Three weeks later the first ships burned.

Now he was fifty-one, and he had told no one for six days, and the guilt of it sat in him like a swallowed stone.

"You could have woken me," Bram said behind him.

Osk didn't turn. He'd heard them coming down the shingle — they still walked like an apprentice, quick and apologetic, as if the beach might object to their presence. "You needed sleep."

"I needed to know." Bram came to stand beside him, breath fogging in the grey air, a ledger bound in oilcloth slung across their back like a weapon. They were twenty years old and carried books the way soldiers carried blades. "Six days, Osk. You watched this happen for six days and you said nothing."

"I said something." He heard how thin that sounded even as he said it. "I said the tide was strange."

"The tide is not strange. The tide is *leaving*." Bram crouched and pressed two fingers into the wet sand, into the ripple-marks the retreating water had left, marks that should have been smoothed away by now by any returning wave. There had been no returning wave. "In the codex, the marginalia says the sea withdrew for three days before the Sundering. Three days of backward water, and then the war came up out of the deep like a fever."

"Your point?"

"My point," Bram said, standing, "is that we have perhaps three days, and you've spent one of them being polite about it."

Osk almost smiled. Almost. That was the thing about the keeper of accounts — they had a way of stating a man's failures plainly enough that they became instructions instead of wounds. "There's a channel," he said finally. "Under the old breakwater, where the smugglers used to run silk past the customs cutters. Pell knows it. I know it. At low water — at what used to be low water — there's a gap in the reef wide enough for a diver, if the diver doesn't mind cold that bites through bone."

"And past the reef?"

Osk looked out at the exposed seabed, acres of it, glistening and wrong: stranded starfish curling their arms against the air, an anchor chain trailing off toward the dark like a rope thrown down a well. Beyond the flats the water pooled in the deeper channels, and in those pools the light did something it shouldn't. It bent downward. As if the sea were looking at something below itself.

"Past the reef is the Hollow," Osk said. "And in the Hollow, if my grandmother's stories were worth the salt she paid for them, is the archive of the Sundered Sea. Everything the sea-folk wrote before the war. Everything the shore burned when we won." He paused. "Everything except what they couldn't find."

Bram was quiet for a moment. When Osk finally turned to look at them, they had the expression they got over a ledger that refused to balance — not fear exactly, but the fierce attention of someone who has found the error and means to hunt it to its den.

"Then we go now," Bram said, "while the water is still ashamed to come back."

* * *

The cold took Osk first, the way it always did, sliding through wool and oilskin and skin as though all three were the same thin cloth. He swam ahead with the lamp lashed to his belt, its flame burning low and blue in the waterproof glass, and Bram followed at his shoulder, kicking steadily, the oilcloth bundle strapped tight between their shoulders so their hands stayed free.

Above them, through ten fathoms then twenty, the surface was a ceiling of hammered pewter. Below them the Hollow opened like a throat.

He had dived wrecks before. He had pulled drowned sailors' rings off their fingers for grieving widows and never once felt he was robbing anyone. But this was different. This was descending into a city. The ruins rose around them out of the gloom — towers of pale coral-locked stone, streets running in graceful curves no human mason would have chosen, doorways tall and narrow and arched like fish spines. Kelp forests hung from the rooftops in slow golden banners. A school of silver fish poured through a window like smoke through a keyhole.

Bram touched his arm and pointed. Carved above the doorway the fish had come through, worn nearly smooth but still legible to a reader of true text: a line of script in the old sea-tongue, spiraling like current written down.

"What does it say?" Osk asked, and immediately felt foolish, because Bram couldn't answer underwater.

But Bram had come prepared, as Bram always came prepared. They produced a slate and chalk from the pouch at their belt, wrote quickly, and turned it:

*"ALL WHO ENTER, ENTER AS GUESTS OR NOT AT ALL."*

"Hospitable people," Osk murmured, and the words came out as a stream of bubbles, and both of them laughed — badly, wildly, the laughter of two small warm creatures in an enormous cold place — and the sound of it seemed to please the ruin somehow, or so Osk chose to believe, because the current shifted then, gentle as a hand at his back, and drew them onward and down.

Down past a plaza where statues stood with their faces eroded to featureless calm. Down past a library whose shelves had long since dissolved into silt, though the reading alcoves remained, carved with cushions of stone soft as sea-sponge. Down, down, following the backward pull of the water, until the lamp's blue circle found a door.

It was not grand. That was the first thing Osk noticed, and the thing that convinced him more than any carving could have. Every other structure in the drowned city reached; this one only endured. A low arch set into the base of a hill of stone, sealed with a slab of black glass, and upon the glass, in fresh bright script that could not possibly be fresh and could not possibly be bright after a hundred years underwater, a single word in the common tongue of the shore:

*REMEMBER.*

Bram hung in the water before it, hair drifting like ink, and Osk saw their lips move — reading, always reading, tasting the word — and then saw their eyes go wide.

"It's addressed to us," Bram said when they surfaced in the air pocket beyond, gasping, hauling themselves onto a ledge of shell-white stone inside a chamber the size of Vael's auction hall. The air was cold and mineral and impossibly breathable, and the lamp-light spread strangely here, filling the whole space without shadow, as if the room itself remembered how to be lit. "Not to sea-folk. Not to divers. To *us*. To whoever came looking. Someone wanted this found, Osk. Someone wanted it found by *shore-folk*, a hundred years after the war, by whoever the water sent."

"Or the water sent us because the Remnant will send someone worse," Osk said, dragging himself fully onto the ledge, lungs burning, heart hammering with more than exertion. "We're not the only ones who can read a backward tide."

"No," Bram agreed quietly. "Which is why we take everything and leave nothing."

They stood, wringing seawater from their sleeves, and looked out across the chamber.

The archive was not shelves. Osk had expected shelves; he supposed everyone expected shelves. Instead the walls were lined with niches, and in each niche rested a single sheet of some material he had no name for — not vellum, not papyrus, thin as fish-scale and translucent as sea-glass, and every sheet glowed faintly with its own preserved light, the way jellyfish glow, the way the sea glows at night when it is thinking. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. A civilization's whole testimony, filed in the dark like lanterns waiting to be needed.

"Oh," Bram said. Just that. Just a single syllable, and Osk heard in it the sound of a person meeting the largest number they would ever count.

They worked for hours. There was no way to know how many — time moved strangely in the Hollow, stretched like taffy — but Osk sorted while Bram read, passing sheets from niche to the flat stone table at the chamber's center, and Bram read each one with their lips moving and their eyes widening and narrowing, and slowly, sheet by sheet, the shape of the truth assembled itself in front of them the way a wreck assembles itself out of silt when you brush the centuries away.

Treaties. Trade ledgers — real ones, honest ones, kept by sea-clerks in a script so beautiful it hurt. Letters between shore-harbors and sea-courts, friendly ones, affectionate ones, complaining about tariffs and weather and a shared festival of lights held every midsummer on the shallows. A hundred years of neighbors.

"This isn't right," Bram said, somewhere in the middle of it, not looking up. "The histories say the sea-folk broke the peace. That they sank the grain fleet at Corrow Bay without warning. That's why the shore rose up. That's the whole reason. My mother sang songs about it."

"Keep reading."

"I am keeping reading." Bram's finger stopped on a sheet near the center of the table, and Osk leaned close, and together they read the account of Corrow Bay.

It was not an attack. It was a rescue gone wrong — the grain fleet had been sinking in a sudden squall, hulls stove in on a shoal that appeared overnight, and the sea-folk had risen to save the drowning crews, and the shore-garrison had seen shapes in the storm-water and loosed the ballistae. The dead of Corrow Bay had drowned tangled in nets meant to save them. And the account ended with a line that made Bram's hand shake on the slate:

*"We do not answer. We do not strike back. We sign the mourning-treaty and we wait for them to learn our names. If they do not learn our names, let the record show who chose the war."*

"They waited," Osk said hoarsely. "Gods below, they *waited*. They had the whole ocean and they waited."

"And then someone edited the record." Bram was moving fast now, pulling sheets, cross-referencing, the apprentice-ledger-keeper rising up in them like a second heartbeat. "Look — the mourning-treaty copies, the ones the shore keeps, the ones in every temple archive from here to Corrow — they say the fleet was sunk *without warning*. Deliberate. But the original—" they held two sheets side by side, glowing faintly, one older than kingdoms, "—the original says *shoal*. Says *storm*. Says *we tried to save them*. Someone rewrote it. Someone on the shore, after the war, rewrote the cause so the war would have a villain, and the villain would be them, and the victors would be clean."

The anger came up in Osk slowly, the way deep water comes up a diver's legs — pressure first, then pain, then a roaring in the ears. He thought of his grandmother's silence. Of the songs. Of the war-dead lists carved into the harbor chapel wall, every name a child of the shore, and not one line anywhere saying *and this is what we did to start it.*

"They didn't just win the war," he said. "They won the *story*."

"And the prophecy—" Bram had gone very still. In the lampless, shadowless light of the archive, their face was pale as the shell-stone. "Osk. The codex. The prophecy of the Sunderer. Who wrote prophecies? Victors write prophecies. Victors write the future the same way they write the past." Their voice dropped. "If they edited the treaty, they edited the prophecy. And if they edited the prophecy—"

"Then the Sunderer may not be what it says it is."

Bram nodded once, slow, and began gathering sheets with hands that were no longer shaking but very, very careful, the care of someone handling something that could detonate. "We take the originals. All of them. The treaty, the Corrow account, the council minutes — there are council minutes, Osk, *minutes*, they voted on whether to fight and the vote was *no* — and anything that mentions the prophecy's authorship. Because I promise you, somewhere in this room, there is a sheet that says who held the pen."

* * *

They found it last, as the important things always seem to arrive last, tucked in the final niche at the far end of the chamber — a niche apart from the others, smaller, set at the height of a kneeling person's hands.

The sheet within was different from the rest. Older. The glow in it was ash-brown, faded, the exact color of the script in the seized codex, and when Bram lifted it free the whole archive dimmed for a moment, every sheet flickering like candles in a draft, as if the room had drawn breath.

Bram read it in silence. Osk watched their face and saw awe leave it, and saw what replaced the awe, and wished he hadn't.

"Say it," he said.

Bram's voice came out flat and precise, ledger-flat, the voice they used when numbers were the only thing holding them upright. "It's the author's note. The prophecy of the Sunderer wasn't written by the sea-folk at all. It was written *after* the war, by the shore chancellery — commissioned, dated, signed. 'Let a prophecy be made,' it says, 'so that the peace is kept not by trust, which failed, but by fear, which does not.' They invented the Sunderer, Osk. They invented a doom and sewed it into the future like a curse into a hem, so that no one — shore or sea — would ever dare open the old wound again." Bram looked up. "It was never a warning. It was a *lock*. And locks don't care who they crush."

"And the edit?" Osk asked, though he already knew, the way you know a tide has turned before you see it turn. "The part they changed. The part they hid."

Bram hesitated. For the length of one long breath, Osk watched them weigh something — he saw it happen, saw the scales move behind their eyes, saw the keeper of accounts decide, briefly, terribly, what to enter and what to omit.

Then Bram turned the sheet toward the light, and Osk read the struck-through line beneath the commissioning signature, the line some clerk a century ago had scraped half away and hoped the sea would keep:

*"…and the Sunderer, who must pay the price willingly, shall not be condemned but mourned—"*

The rest was scraped to nothing.

Above them, far above, through forty fathoms of green water, the sea shuddered — a long slow groan through the bones of the drowned city, the sound of a tide remembering its way home. The backward pull faltered. Somewhere overhead, the water began, at last, to return.

"We need to be out of the Hollow before it closes," Osk said.

Bram folded the author's note into the oilcloth bundle with the rest, next to the codex, next to the fragment that read *the keeper bleeds first*, and for a moment their hand stayed pressed flat against the bundle as if holding a wound closed.

"Yes," Bram said. "And Osk — when we get back, when the guild asks what we found — there's a part of this they can't hear yet. Not because it isn't true. Because it's too heavy to carry until we know what it means."

Osk looked at them — at the child-apprentice who kept their secrets in columns and their fears in margins — and understood that the first lie of the archive had just been told, quietly, in a drowned room, before either of them ever surfaced.

The water was coming back. They swam for the light.
