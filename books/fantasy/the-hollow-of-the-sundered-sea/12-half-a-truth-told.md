## 12. Half a Truth Told

The salt cellar had never held a council before. It was built for barrels and brine-pickled eels, for the guild's less legal inventory, and it smelled of it—of old tides and older secrets—but tonight Pell had scrubbed the long table clean, set out every candle the Gildenscale owned, and called it a war room. Bram counted the candles as they came down the stairs: eleven. They always counted things when their hands wanted to shake.

The archive lay in the center of the table, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with deep water. Even now, hours after Osk and Bram had hauled it up from the drowned ruins, it wept. A thin ring of seawater had gathered beneath it on the scarred wood, and no one had wiped it away. It looked, Bram thought, like the table itself was sweating.

They had dried what could be dried. The pages of the original record—*the true record*, Bram's mind kept insisting, though they were learning to distrust that phrase—had been laid flat on racks by the stove, weighted with smooth stones from Juniper's collection. The ink had survived a century underwater better than any ink had a right to. It was not ink at all, Bram suspected. It was something older, something that had been written to be read by people who lived beneath the sea, and it did not know how to fade.

Maren sat at the far end of the table. She had asked to sit in on the council, and Vael had said yes before Bram could find a reason to say no, and so the hedge-witch who had raised them—the hedge-witch they had run from three years ago with nothing but a stolen satchel and a heart full of grievance—now watched the proceedings with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral. She had not spoken since coming down. She was waiting. She was very good at waiting. Bram remembered that much about her, and hated how warm the memory was.

"Right," said Vael, and the room went quiet the way rooms only go quiet for Mistress Vael, auctioneer of the impossible, whose voice could sell fog to a fisherman. "We've all seen the pages. We've all had a day to be frightened privately. Now we're frightened together, which is the Gildenscale way." She let the joke land and die. "Bram. You read it first. You read it best. Tell us what the sea has been hiding."

Every eye turned. Bram had rehearsed this on the stairs. They had rehearsed it the way Juniper rehearsed a forgery—not the words, but the truth underneath the words, the part that had to hold weight if anyone pressed.

So they told the truth. Most of it.

"The prophecy is real," Bram began, and their voice came out steadier than they'd hoped. "Not real the way the codex was real—a copy, an interpretation, a thing passed through a hundred hands until it meant whatever the holder feared most. This is the source. Written before the Sundering, or during it, by scribes of the shore alliance. And it has been *edited*."

Juniper leaned forward. Of everyone at the table, she understood editing best; her whole craft was the honest lie, the document that was false in every particular except the ones that mattered. "Edited how?"

"Struck lines. Replaced lines. There are three places where the hand changes—where the script goes from the old formal bookhand to something quicker, harder, written over a palimpsest. Someone scraped the vellum and wrote new words where old words used to be." Bram touched the nearest page, careful of the water still bleeding from its edges. "The codex we found in the estate—that was a copy of the edited version. Everyone who has read this prophecy in the last hundred years has read the doctored text."

"And the doctoring says?" Osk asked. He sat with his back to the wall, as always, his tide-charts pushed aside to make room. His eyes were red-rimmed; he had not slept since the dive, since he had stood in the drowned archive and seen with his own eyes the place his grandfather's stories had come from.

"The doctored version names a Sunderer," Bram said. "A figure who will reignite the war between sea and shore. That part is true—it's in the original too, in the same hand as everything else. But the original says more." They paused. This was the edge of the cliff. This was where the rehearsal ended and the fall began. "The original explains *why* the line was changed. The scribe who altered it left a marginal note. It says—" and here Bram made themselves quote it exactly, because numbers never lied and neither did quotations, "—*'struck for morale; the troops will fight poorly for a sacrifice.'*"

Silence. The candles guttered in a draft from the stairwell, and eleven flames bent together like a field of grass.

"A sacrifice," Pell said at last. The old doorkeeper's voice was gravel dragged over gravel. He had been a boy during the last winter of the last war; he had carried the dead on carts. "Meaning the Sunderer isn't the one who breaks the peace. The Sunderer is the one who *ends* it. Again."

"That's what the margin implies," Bram said carefully. "That the role was reframed as villainy because no army marches well behind someone meant to die."

"But it doesn't say who," Vael said sharply. "The original doesn't name the Sunderer?"

"No. It describes them. 'The keeper of accounts, whose blood opens the last page.'" Bram spread their empty hands. "Which is why the Remnant wants the codex, and why they want us afraid. Fear makes people sell. Fear makes people run. Fear makes people do stupid, brave, expensive things."

It was a good speech. Bram knew it was a good speech because they had built it the way Juniper built a forgery, seam by seam, and every seam held. Vael nodded slowly. Pell grunted, which from Pell was applause. Osk stared at the weeping archive and said nothing, which was unlike him, but he had been saying nothing all day and Bram had begun to dread the shape of his silences.

Only Maren moved. She uncrossed her hands, folded them again, and looked at Bram across the length of the table with those grey-green eyes that had watched them learn letters on a kitchen floor, and Bram felt the look go through them like a fish-gutter through a herring.

Because there was one more line. One more struck line, in the deepest layer of the palimpsest, under the sacrifice note, in a hand even older than the editors'. Bram had found it at dawn, alone, with the lamp turned low, and had sat with it for an hour while the harbor froze outside the window, and then had copied it onto a scrap of paper and burned the scrap and told no one.

The oldest line said: *the keeper gives freely, or the sea takes all.*

Freely. Not bled out by an enemy's blade at midwinter, not murdered into martyrdom. *Gives.* Which meant the Sunderer had to choose it—which meant the Sunderer had to know—and Bram had spent the whole day carrying that word around inside their chest like a swallowed coal, warming themselves on it and being burned by it in turns. If they told the guild, the guild would spend the rest of the winter looking at each other, measuring each other, wondering who would be asked to give. If they told the guild, Vael would start planning a way around it, because Vael always planned a way around it, and the ways around it would get people killed. If they told the guild—

No. The lie was already told. All that remained was to keep telling it well.

"Bram." Maren's voice, quiet, cutting the silence like a keel. "You've read deeper layers than you're showing. I can see it in your hands."

The cellar went very still. Bram kept their face the way Juniper had taught them to keep a forged seal—relaxed, unremarkable, boring. "I've read everything there is. What you see in my hands is three years of ledger work and bad sleep."

Something flickered across the hedge-witch's face—hurt, maybe, or respect, or the particular grief of a teacher watching a student surpass the lesson. "Of course," she said softly, and folded her hands again, and said no more. But Bram saw her eyes drop, once, to their fingers resting on the table, and stay there a moment too long, the way she used to look at their palms before lessons, reading the calluses like a ledger of everything they'd touched.

*Later*, Bram told themselves. *Survive the council first.*

* * *

The argument started, as arguments at the Gildenscale always did, over money.

"We sell it," said Vael. "Or rather, we don't—we *auction* it. Announce the sale. Let the Remnant and the crown and every collector and crackpot bid against each other. While they're bidding, we move the real archive somewhere no one will ever—"

"It's not a vase, Vael." Osk hadn't raised his voice; he rarely needed to. "It's a war. You can't put a war on a block and take ten percent."

"I've put stranger things on blocks."

"You haven't put *this* on a block, because this one bites."

"Children," Pell rumbled, without heat, the way he'd been saying it for forty years, and both of them subsided a half-inch, which was all either of them ever gave.

Bram sat at the table's end and took minutes, because taking minutes was what they did, because the scratch of the pen gave their hands a job and their heart a hiding place. *Motion: public auction of the recovered archive, proposed by Vael. Seconded by no one.* They wrote it down anyway. The ledgers never lied, and if Bram wrote down what was said instead of what was true, well—the ledgers didn't lie, but ledger-keepers could choose what went into them. They were learning that distinction very well this week.

Juniper spoke next, and her voice was strange—flatter than usual, worn at the edges. Since the forgery, since the false codex she'd built for the Remnant, she had been like this: present in body, absent in some essential room of herself. "Does it matter what we decide?" she said. "They already have soldiers in the city. Grey Eyes doesn't negotiate; he collects. Whatever we vote, we should vote fast and then start packing."

"We are not running," Vael said.

"We ran from the last war fine," Pell said mildly. "Well. Some of us ran. Some of us buried people."

"Pell—"

"I'm not arguing, girl. I'm remembering. There's a difference, and the difference is that remembering doesn't pretend to be a plan." The old man laid one knotted hand flat on the table, beside the ring of seawater. "Here's my memory, since we're spending them freely. Last time the sea rose, this harbor burned for nine days. I was seven. My mother tied me to a roof beam so I wouldn't wash away, and the rope is the reason I'm alive, and I have hated ropes ever since, and I joined a smuggling crew at twelve because smugglers' boats don't carry rope. Nine days of burning. And at the end of it, nobody won. The sea-folk went down into their ruins and pulled the door shut behind them, and the shore forgot them on purpose, because forgetting was cheaper than fighting. That's what the Sundering was. Not a victory. An agreement to stop." He looked around the table, at each of them in turn, last of all at Bram. "And now we've dug up the paperwork of the agreement, and everyone wants to know what it says, and I'll tell you what it says. It says somebody, someday, would have to pay to keep the door shut. Paperwork always says that. Everything costs. That's the first rule of trade and the last rule of war."

Bram's pen stopped moving.

Nobody else seemed to hear it. Vael was already talking about safehouses; Osk was muttering about the tide turning backward again at moonrise; Juniper had gone back to staring at her own hands as though checking them for alterations. Only Maren was looking at Bram, and only Maren had heard what Pell had said, and Bram watched the understanding move across her face like weather.

She knew. Or she guessed. Or she simply knew Bram—knew the set of their shoulders, the pause before an answer, the specific quality of silence that Bram produced when they were holding something hot. Three years of running, and the hedge-witch could still read them like weather-glass.

*Bram,* she mouthed, silently, across the candle-smoke. Not a question. A summons. *After.*

* * *

The council broke near midnight with no decision, which Vael declared was itself a decision—"we buy time, we watch the water, and nobody does anything heroic until we've slept"—and the family dispersed to their beds and their watches and their private fears. Bram stayed to bank the candles, pinching each flame with wet fingers, counting as they went. Eleven. Ten. Seven. Four.

One.

In the single remaining light, Maren crossed the cellar and stood beside them, close enough that Bram could smell the hedge on her—dried nettle, beeswax, woodsmoke, the exact smell of a childhood they had spent three years trying not to remember.

"You struck a line tonight," Maren said quietly. "Out of the record. Out loud."

"I told them everything that matters."

"No one tells everything that matters. That's the first lesson of true text, and I taught it to you myself, so don't look at me like I've accused you of heresy." She reached out—not for Bram's face, not for their shoulder, but for their hands, the old gesture, the pre-lesson gesture, *show me your hands*—and Bram stepped back before they could decide whether to allow it, and the hurt that crossed Maren's face was so brief and so total that Bram had to look away from it, down at the candle, at the small brave idiotic flame.

"There's a line," Maren said. "Under the editor's note. Under the sacrifice. In the oldest hand."

The candle flame swayed. Bram kept their eyes on it.

"If there were such a line," they said, "and I had read it, and I chose not to put it in front of five frightened people the night they learned the world might end—would that make me a liar?"

Maren was quiet a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone soft in the old way, the teaching way, the way it had been over the kitchen table with the good lamp lit, and Bram hated how much of home was still stored in that sound.

"It would make you a keeper," she said. "Keepers choose what the record holds. That's the whole terrible job. I only ever wanted to teach you how heavy the choosing gets." She turned toward the stairs, then paused, her back to Bram, her shadow long across the salt-stained wall. "But hear me, child, because I'll only say it once. A half-truth held out of love is still a debt. And debts, in this guild above all places, always come due—with interest."

Her footsteps faded up the stairs. Bram stood alone in the salt cellar with one candle and the weeping archive and the word they had swallowed at dawn, sitting in their chest where they had hidden it, burning a hole straight through:

*Freely.*

Outside, far off toward the harbor, the ice groaned—a long, cracking sigh, the sound of the sea shifting in its sleep. And beneath it, fainter, almost imagined, Bram thought they heard the backward pull of the tide beginning again, dragging the water out past the breakwater, toward the drowned ruins, toward the shut door, toward midwinter.

They pinched out the last candle and climbed into the dark, and upstairs in the ledger room, they opened a fresh page and wrote, in their smallest, steadiest hand, a single entry with no date and no amount:

*One secret, held. Value: unknown. Interest: accruing.*

Then they closed the book, and locked it, and put the key in their pocket, and tried to believe that a locked ledger could keep anything safe—including, most of all, from the people who trusted them enough to believe they'd told the whole truth.
