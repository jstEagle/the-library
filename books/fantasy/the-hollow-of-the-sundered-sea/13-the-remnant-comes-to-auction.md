## 13. The Remnant Comes to Auction

Mistress Vael had been an auctioneer for forty years, and in forty years she had learned one truth above all others: a room is a weapon, and the person who controls its rhythm controls everyone standing inside it.

She was thinking about that now, standing at the tall window of her office above the auction floor, watching the street below. Snow had come early this year—Pell said the gulls were flying wrong, Osk said the tide was pulling backward, and both of them were right, which was the trouble. The city wore its first white like a borrowed coat, too soon, ill-fitting. And down there on the cobbles, where the lamplight pooled yellow against the drifts, six soldiers in grey oilcloth stood in a loose half-circle around a man with grey eyes.

They had not knocked. That was the insult of it. They had simply arrived, the way weather arrives, and taken up their positions as though the Gildenscale were already theirs.

Vael let them stand in the cold a while longer. Let them shift their weight. Let their breath fog and thin and fog again. Every minute she made them wait was a minute she spent reminding herself—and them—who owned this room, this street, this hour. She finished her tea. She set the cup down without a sound. She chose her earrings: long drops of sea-glass, pale green, the color of shallow water over sand. Armor came in many forms. Tonight hers would be jewelry, posture, and a voice trained to make grown merchants weep over lots they could not afford.

Then she went down to open her doors.

* * *

The man with grey eyes gave his name as Corvane, though Vael suspected it was no more his true name than "Mistress Vael" was hers. Names were inventory here; she knew better than anyone how cheaply they sold and how dearly they cost. He stood in the entry hall with snow melting off his shoulders into puddles he did not glance at, and behind him his soldiers filled the doorway like a wall of wet slate.

"You refused my offer," Corvane said. It was not a question. It was barely even an accusation. It had the flat quality of a man reading terms aloud from a contract he believed already signed.

"I refuse many offers," Vael said pleasantly. "It's most of my business."

"Last time I offered five thousand crowns for the codex."

"And last time I told you it wasn't for sale." She smiled at him, the smile she used on bidders who had reached the limit of their purses—the smile that said *I am not mocking you, I am merely informing you of your position.* "You seem to have brought soldiers this time instead of coin. I'll note the change in your method of payment."

Corvane's mouth tightened, but his eyes stayed still, and it was the eyes Vael feared. Grey as winter water, grey as the drowned ruins far out past the harbor bar, grey as the pages of a book that should have crumbled a century ago. He looked at her the way creditors look at debtors—not angry, not yet, but patient in a way that promised the anger would be thorough when it finally arrived.

"The Remnant," he said, "does not forget what it is owed. The codex belongs to us. It was written by our ancestors, sealed by our ancestors, lost by our ancestors' carelessness. You are holding stolen property, Mistress Vael. I am offering you the chance to return it honorably before honor stops being offered."

"Stolen," Vael repeated, savoring the word like a wine she intended to spit. "From whom? The estate of a dead man whose heirs never claimed him? A book that sat in an attic under pigeon droppings for sixty years while the Remnant forgot it existed?" She turned her head slightly, letting the sea-glass catch the lamplight. "If it was precious to you, you let it rot. If it rotted, it was never yours in any way that matters. Possession, dear sir, is nine-tenths of the law, and the tenth part is the part I keep."

Behind her, she heard Pell's boots on the stairs—he had come up from the door the moment the soldiers appeared, moving with the unhurried shuffle that was itself a kind of performance, because Pell could move very fast when he chose and wanted no one to know it. Old Pell, seventy if he was a day, with hands like knotted rope and a memory full of smuggler routes that had never appeared on any chart. He positioned himself near the door without appearing to position himself anywhere at all.

"We could take it," one of the soldiers said quietly. Not a threat, exactly. An inventory. Six of them, armed, against a guildhouse full of forgers and tide-readers and one old doorkeeper. The arithmetic was simple, and the soldier clearly thought it favored him.

Vael let the silence stretch until it began to hurt him. Then she laughed—a short, genuine laugh, the kind she could not fake, which was why she spent it so rarely.

"You could try," she said. "And then you would learn what the rest of the city already knows: that the Gildenscale has friends in strange places, debts outstanding in stranger ones, and a cellar full of bottled storms that I would take a great deal of pleasure in uncorking over your heads. Bottled storms, soldier. Do you know what happens when one breaks? Neither do I. Nobody does. That's rather the point of them." She spread her hands. "So. We can do this the loud way, in which case everyone loses something and I lose least, or we can do this my way, in which case you might leave here with something worth having after all."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Whatever he had expected from the guild's auctioneer, it was not this—this calm, this relish, this refusal to be frightened by men with swords. He had bought obedience all his life, Vael guessed. He did not know what to purchase when the seller declined to sell.

"What way is that?" he asked.

"An auction," said Vael. "A public one. Tomorrow night. Open to every buyer in the city. And on the block—" she paused, and here she allowed herself the smallest cruelty, the pause of a woman who knows exactly how much a pause costs, "—a certain prophetic codex will be offered for sale. Genuine. Authenticated by my own appraisers. Starting bid: one crown."

Corvane's eyes flickered. For the first time since entering, he looked uncertain, and uncertainty in a man like this was more dangerous than anger—but also more useful.

"You'd sell it?"

"I'd *auction* it," Vael corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is me. Come tomorrow night. Bring your soldiers. Bring your crowns. Bid like everyone else." She stepped closer, close enough that he could smell the salt-and-cloves oil she wore in her hair, close enough that her voice dropped to the register she saved for closing rooms. "Or don't come, and spend the rest of your life wondering which of the hundred buyers in that hall walked away with your birthright. But understand this, Corvane of the grey eyes: the Gildenscale does not give. The Gildenscale *trades*. Everything has a price. Even prophecy. Especially prophecy."

She watched him swallow it. Watched the arithmetic move behind his face—soldiers versus storms, force versus auction, the certainty that a public sale meant witnesses, meant scrutiny, meant the Remnant's name spoken aloud in front of the whole city's buying class. He had come expecting to frighten a shopkeeper. He was leaving having agreed to attend a party.

"Tomorrow night, then," he said at last.

"Wear something warm," Vael said sweetly. "The winter's coming early. Everyone says so."

* * *

The moment the door closed behind them, the guildhouse changed key.

Vael climbed back to her office and found them already gathering—Bram with the ledger hugged to their chest like a shield, Juniper perched on the desk edge with ink-stained fingers laced together, Osk dripping meltwater from a walk along the seawall, Pell standing in the doorway because Pell always stood in doorways, as though doors were furniture designed around him.

"They took the bait," Vael said. "Or they think they did. Either way, we have until tomorrow night, and tomorrow night is going to be the loudest, longest, most magnificent lie this city has ever watched me tell."

Juniper grinned, sharp as a paper cut. "What are we selling them?"

"Nothing," Vael said. "We're selling them everything. A full evening's auction, dozens of lots, a packed hall—and somewhere in the middle of it, the codex goes on the block, and I run the bidding up and up and up until the whole room believes it's real, and then—" she snapped her fingers, "—the lot is withdrawn. Reserve not met. Seller's discretion. Perfectly legal, perfectly ordinary, happens at auctions every week. Corvane cannot protest without revealing why a century-old book matters enough to send soldiers for. He is trapped by his own secrecy, and I intend to charge him rent for every hour he spends in that trap."

"And while the whole city watches you perform," Bram said quietly—they always spoke quietly, as though numbers might overhear and be embarrassed—"we move the archive."

Vael turned to them. "Yes. While I hold the stage, the real cargo goes out the back. The archive from the drowned vault, the fragments, everything that proves what the prophecy actually says. If the Remnant takes the guildhouse tomorrow—and they may, whatever happens in that hall—they'll find a codex-shaped hole and a great deal of worthless wonder."

"Not worthless," Juniper objected. "I've been working all week. Wait till you see the lots."

That was the other half of the plan, and Vael loved it with the pure love of a showwoman watching another artist work. Juniper had forged wonders. Not documents tonight—*wonders*. A vial of mermaid's grief, labeled and waxed and faintly, heartbreakingly luminous. A letter written in frost that melted as you read it. A key that opened nothing but fit every lock it touched, which Juniper swore was harder to forge than any lock at all. A jar of the last summer of a village that had drowned, the air inside it still warm. Each one fake. Each one flawless. Each one destined to make the hall gasp, bid, and believe—so that when the codex appeared among them, wrapped in the same velvet, lit by the same candles, the belief would already be in the room, waiting for it.

"Osk," Vael said. "The routes."

Osk had gone still, the way he went still whenever the sea entered a conversation. "Pell's old ways," he said. "The cut behind the tannery, the culvert under the fish market, the stair that isn't a stair down to the low tunnels. They haven't been used in twenty years. Some of them have fallen in. But the tide's running backward right now—it'll be running backward through the culverts too, which means the water levels will be wrong in ways nobody who doesn't read water would notice." He glanced at Pell. "Can you still find them in the dark?"

Pell's answer was to push open the door and say, "Bring a lamp and follow me, boy, and mind the third step, it's been rotten since before your mother was born."

* * *

They moved the archive at midnight, in the blue hour between the last cart and the first watch.

Vael insisted on seeing them off herself, though Bram argued she should sleep—argued in that careful, ledger-keeper's way of theirs, all logic and no volume, and Vael pretended to consider it and then put on her boots anyway. Some things an auctioneer does not delegate. Standing at the mouth of Pell's tunnel, holding the lamp while four of her people loaded a waterproof chest onto a sledge, she felt something she had not let herself feel in weeks: fear, yes, but underneath the fear, a fierce and stubborn pride. This was her family loading their history onto a sled in the dark because she had asked them to trust her with a day of theater. They had not hesitated. Not Bram, who catalogued courage the way they catalogued everything else—one line, one number, one unblinking entry. Not Osk, who had confessed his guilt about the omens only days ago and now carried the archive like a penance. Not Juniper, whose hands shook slightly as she sealed the chest, though whether from cold or from the fraying the forgery had left in her, Vael could not tell and did not ask. There were questions you saved for spring, if there was a spring.

"Low tunnels to the salt gate," Pell murmured, tapping the wall twice where a chalk mark hid under grime—an old mark, his own mark, from a life he rarely mentioned. "Then the kelp-walk out past the breakwater. There's a boat. There's always been a boat. The trick is knowing which night it's where it ought to be." He squinted at the black water visible through the grate ahead. "Tonight's such a night. I checked."

"Of course you did," Vael said.

"Forty years of checking," Pell said. "It's the checking that keeps you alive. The bold ones drown."

Bram paused at the tunnel mouth, one hand resting on the chest, and looked back at Vael with an expression she had learned to read the way she read a bidder's eyes: something being decided, silently, in private. "Tomorrow night," Bram said, "when you withdraw the lot—Corvane will know, within a minute, that he's been played. What then?"

"Then he does one of two things," Vael said. "He either swallows his pride and leaves to report that the guild humiliated the Remnant in public—which buys us days—or he stops pretending to be a buyer and shows us exactly what he is." She met Bram's eyes steadily. "Which is why the archive leaves tonight, and why you—" she touched Bram's shoulder, briefly, the way she touched the gavel, "—will not be in the hall tomorrow. You'll be elsewhere. Don't argue. You're the keeper of accounts, and I've read enough prophecy by proxy to know that keepers bleed first. I won't hand them the chance."

Bram didn't argue. That frightened Vael more than anything else that night. Bram Brightwater arguing was a storm in a teacup; Bram Brightwater agreeing quietly was someone deciding the cup might break.

"Go," Vael said. "All of you. And Pell—if the boat isn't where it ought to be, you come back, you hear me? Nothing in that chest is worth any of you. Nothing in this world is."

Pell snorted. "Tell that to the chest."

* * *

The next evening the Gildenscale burned like a lantern.

Every candle in the house was lit. The hall smelled of beeswax and spiced wine and the particular electric scent of two hundred strangers deciding whether to trust each other. Vael stood at the rostrum in her second-best gown—the best was for wars and weddings, and she hadn't decided which this was—and worked the room the way a musician works an instrument, lot by lot, voice rising and falling, drawing bids out of people who had come intending only to watch.

Juniper's wonders performed beautifully. The vial of mermaid's grief fetched forty crowns from a shipping magnate who wept, actually wept, and later swore to anyone who'd listen that he'd held it to his ear and heard the sea mourning. The frost-letter sold three times before it fully melted, each buyer convinced they alone had captured its final words. The jar of drowned summer went for a sum that made even Vael blink, purchased by a widow who said, quite sanely, that she knew it was false and did not care, because false warmth was still warm. Vael loved that widow instantly and forever. That was the secret doctrine of the Gildenscale, the one she never wrote in any ledger: the impossible things were never really for sale. Only the believing was. And believing, unlike everything else, was inexhaustible.

Corvane sat in the fourth row, flanked by his soldiers dressed, poorly, as gentlemen. He bid on nothing. He watched everything. His grey eyes tracked the codex from the moment Juniper's assistants carried it out on its cushion of velvet—tracked it with the fixed hunger of a man watching his own heart being handled by strangers.

Vael felt his attention like weather pressure and smiled wider for it.

"Lot forty-one," she announced, and the hall hushed, because word had traveled, because word always travels, because she had made sure of it. "One codex, bound in sealskin-embossed leather, approximately one hundred years old, recovered from the Harrowgate estate. Prophetic content authenticated. Ladies and gentlemen—history, on the block. Who will open at one crown?"

"One," called a grain merchant, just to be in the game.

"Five!" someone else, drunker, braver.

"Twenty," said a voice from the left, and Vael's stomach tightened pleasantly, because that voice belonged to a buyer she had cultivated for exactly this purpose—a rival of the Remnant, though the hall didn't know it, a woman whose interest in old prophecies was genuine and whose purse was deep.

"Fifty," said Corvane.

There it was. The hall rippled. Two hundred people leaned toward the grey-eyed man in the fourth row, and Vael let the ripple build, let the whispers climb, let the story write itself in real time across the faces of the crowd: *who is he, why does he want it, what does he know.* Every rumor was a brick in the wall between Corvane and the quiet violence he'd planned. A man bidding in public is a man who has agreed to rules.

"One hundred," countered the woman on the left.

"One-fifty," Corvane said, jaw tight.

"Two hundred," the woman said, and now they were truly dueling, and the hall was roaring softly, and Vael raised her gavel with the timing of a lifetime—

"—Two hundred and fifty," Corvane said, hoarse now, past sense, past strategy, spending Remnant gold like a drowning man spending rope—

—and Vael brought the hammer down on the *withdrawal*.

"Lot forty-one," she said, into the stunned silence, with the crisp authority of forty years, "is withdrawn. The reserve was not met. Seller's discretion. Thank you all for bidding."

For one heartbeat the hall simply did not understand. Then it understood, and the noise that rose was not disappointment but delight—the crowd smelled the theater in it, the audacity, the sheer magnificent nerve of withdrawing the most anticipated lot of the season at its peak. Somewhere in the back, people started laughing. Somewhere else, people started asking questions. Everywhere, people started telling each other stories about the night the mysterious grey-eyed bidder lost a prophet's book to a seller's whim.

Corvane stood up. His chair scraped. His soldiers' disguises suddenly fit worse than they had an hour ago.

"Mistress Vael," he said, crossing the floor with the terrible calm of a man who has stopped caring who watches, "where is the codex?"

"Gone," Vael said, and it was even true. "Sold, delivered, or destroyed—I'm afraid the Gildenscale guarantees provenance, not whereabouts. That's rather the nature of impossible things. They're impossible to hold on to."

He stared at her. Around him, the hall kept chattering, drinking, bidding on the remaining lots, oblivious or pretending to be, and Vael understood suddenly and completely that she was standing on a stage with the boards already smoking beneath her feet. The performance had worked. The performance had also ended. There was a moment in every show—that moment, the one every performer knows—when the applause is loudest and the exit is narrowest and the mask weighs most.

She finished the auction. Of course she finished the auction. She sold the last lot, a forged compass that pointed at whatever the holder missed most, to thunderous applause, and bowed, and blew out the candles one by one like a woman ending an ordinary evening.

But when she climbed the stairs to her office and looked out the window, the six soldiers were back on the cobbles. And there were more of them now. Twelve. Twenty. Standing in the snow with their grey oilcloths whitening, patient as winter, and at their center a lantern-lit table where Corvane sat writing something by hand, slowly, in a book of his own.

Vael recognized the shape of what was coming the way sailors recognize a squall line: not by the wind, which hadn't arrived, but by the light, which had already gone wrong.

Down in the hall below her, her family was laughing, counting takings, dividing Juniper's takings, alive and warm and victorious. Above her, the snow kept falling on the men who had come to end all of it.

Vael poured herself a glass of the good brandy, the bottle she kept for funerals and miracles, and drank to whichever one was climbing her stairs.
