## 15. Maren's True Lesson

The hedge-witch's cottage had smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke. The Gildenscale smelled of salt, ink, and old paper, and Bram had spent three years convincing themselves that this second smell was home.

So when the knock came at the guild's back door on the fourth night of frost—three slow raps, then two, then one, a rhythm Bram had not heard since they were fourteen years old—their body knew it before their mind did. Their hands went still over the ledger. Their breath went shallow in the way it does when a door you thought was sealed swings open from the other side.

Pell answered it, because Pell always did, and his voice carried down the corridor with the careful courtesy he saved for strangers who might be dangerous. "We're closed to trade after dark, madam."

"I'm not here to trade." Maren's voice was lower than Bram remembered. Rougher. Like a path worn deeper by weather. "I'm here for my apprentice."

*Not your apprentice*, Bram wanted to shout down the hall. *I unmade that word myself, letter by letter, the night I left.* But they didn't shout. They sat very still in the counting room and listened to their own heart conduct its small, traitorous drumroll.

Footsteps. Vael's voice now, silk over steel: "Mistress Maren of the Thornhedge Road. We know your reputation. The Gildenscale honors all guests who honor our rules."

"Your rules," Maren said, and there was something almost like amusement in it, dry as autumn leaves. "I've read about your rules. I've read about everything you do here. That's rather why I've come."

Bram stood before they decided to stand. Their chair scraped. In the corridor, three conversations stopped at once.

They walked out into the entry hall where lamplight fell yellow on wet stone, and there she was: smaller than the figure that haunted Bram's nightmares, broader in the shoulder, her grey hair braided back in the practical style she'd used for chopping herbs and, occasionally, for other things. Her coat was travel-stained. Her eyes were the same—dark brown, nearly black, patient as soil—and they found Bram across the room the way a root finds water.

"Hello, little ledger," she said.

"I have a name," Bram said. It came out steadier than they felt. "You gave it to me. You don't get to replace it."

"No," Maren agreed. "I suppose I don't." She looked around the hall—at Vael standing like a drawn bow, at Osk dripping harbor-water by the stairs, at Juniper half-hidden behind the doorframe with her hands full of forgery tools she'd been carrying for no reason anyone could name. At old Pell, who had gone very quiet, because Pell remembered the last war and knew what hedge-witches had done in it. "You've built something here. It looks warm. Warm things burn fastest when people want them to."

"Is that a threat?" Vael asked.

"It's a forecast," Maren said. "I deal in those. May I come in out of the cold? I'll even pay the door-fee. I know you charge one."

"We do," Pell said slowly, "for strangers."

"Then I'm glad we're not strangers," Maren said, and set a coin on the table that made Pell's eyebrows climb: an old silver piece, minted before the Sundering, worth more than the door had ever charged anyone. "Consider it rent for the evening. I need to speak with Bram. Alone, if your rules allow it. If they don't allow it, alone anyway, but I'd rather not be rude."

Vael looked at Bram. It was, Bram realized later, the first time the auctioneer had ever deferred to them in front of the whole guild—a small surrender, or perhaps a test. Bram lifted their chin.

"The counting room," they said. "Ten minutes. Door stays open."

"Door stays open," Maren agreed pleasantly. "I raised you to be careful with doors."

"You raised me to be afraid of them."

"Yes," Maren said, without a flicker of denial. "That too."

* * *

The counting room was Bram's territory the way a den is a fox's: every surface stacked with ledgers, every ledger cross-referenced, every number reconciled twice. Bram sat behind the desk because sitting behind the desk helped. Maren took the stool across from it, the one clients used, and folded her hands, and for a long moment neither of them spoke while the open door let in the murmur of the guild pretending not to listen.

"You got my letter," Maren said finally.

"I got your letter."

"And you didn't answer."

"There wasn't anything to say."

"There was a great deal to say," Maren said. "Three years' worth. But you never were one for words that couldn't be counted." She glanced at the shelves, at the spines of the ledgers, and something moved through her face too quick to name. Pride, maybe. Or grief wearing pride's coat. "You catalogued all this?"

"Bottled storms. Borrowed years. A widow's fog, returned last spring with interest."

"A widow's fog," Maren repeated softly. "You used to hate fog."

"People change."

"People are changed," Maren said. "That's different, and it matters tonight." She leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the lines around her eyes, deepening them into something Bram had never seen on her before and needed a moment to recognize: weariness. Not the performative sternness of lessons. Actual, bone-deep tiredness, the kind Bram had only ever seen in mirrors. "Bram. I'm going to tell you why I came, and you're going to want to interrupt, and I need you not to. Can you do that? For ten minutes. You were always good at counting time."

Bram said nothing, which was answer enough.

"When you ran," Maren began, "you took nothing but the clothes you wore and the primer I made you. Do you remember the primer?"

Bram's hand moved without permission to the inner pocket of their coat, where a slim, oilcloth-wrapped book had lived for three years against their ribs. They hated that their body confessed so easily. Maren saw, and nodded, unsurprised.

"The primer teaches true text," she said. "Writing that cannot lie. Most text lies, Bram—all of it, constantly, the way most people lie, kindly or cruelly or just carelessly. Words drift from what they mean. Records decay. History is written by whoever held the pen longest. But true text—" her finger tapped the desk, once, hard, "—true text is the thing itself. Unalterable. Self-verifying. There are maybe six people alive who can read it fluently, and fewer who can write it, and I spent eleven years teaching you because you were the best I ever found. Because you were the *only* one I ever found."

"Why?" The question tore out of Bram before they could stop it. "Why does it matter? Why did it matter enough that you—" Their voice cracked and they hated that too. "Why did I matter enough to be locked in a cottage on the Thornhedge Road for eleven years, copying characters until my fingers bled, never allowed past the garden gate, never allowed—"

"Because of the codex," Maren said.

The word landed in the room like a dropped weight.

"You've found it by now. Of course you have—it's how these things work, prophecy has a sense of theater. The seized estate. The ash-brown script. The page warm under your fingers." She watched Bram's face and whatever she saw there confirmed it. "Then you know it's real. And you know what it names. The keeper of accounts. The Sunderer. The war that sleeps under the Sundered Sea, waiting for someone to wake it."

"The guild is investigating," Bram said stiffly. "It's handled."

"It is not handled. It will never be handled, because prophecies aren't problems, they're *sentences*—they're already written, they're only waiting to be served." Maren's voice stayed level, but her hands had tightened in her lap. "I read the signs six months ago. The tide running backward. The frost coming early. The same omens from the last winter before the Sundering—I found them in records older than any guild, older than any kingdom, and I did what I always do with text that lies. I went looking for the version that doesn't."

Bram's mouth had gone dry. "And?"

"And I found it." She reached into her coat and drew out a packet wrapped in waxed linen, and set it on the desk between them without opening it. "A fragment. One of several scattered when the codex was hidden, a century ago, by people who hoped scattering would be the same as destroying. It isn't the final page. But it's close to it. And it's written in true text—which means no one can read it except us."

The lamplight hissed. Somewhere in the guild, Juniper laughed at something, bright and sudden, and the sound cut through the walls and was gone.

"Read it," Maren said, and pushed the packet across the desk.

Bram's hands were steady. That surprised them. Three years of running, and when the moment finally came, their hands behaved like a clerk's hands, because that was what they had made themselves into: someone whose hands did not shake, because numbers never lied and neither, anymore, did they. They unwrapped the linen. Inside lay a scrap of vellum, brittle and sea-stained, crowded with characters that shimmered faintly the way heat shimmers over a road—not moving, exactly, but refusing to be quite still, the way truth refuses to be quite comfortable.

Bram read.

They had thought the reading would be like the old lessons: slow, effortful, character by character, Maren's voice correcting them at every stumble. Instead it was like falling into warm water. The primer had kept the shape of the language alive in them through three years of disuse, and the true text opened to their eyes like a lock recognizing its key:

*When the frost comes early and the tide runs home,*
*the keeper of accounts shall bleed upon the book,*
*and the book shall open to its final page.*
*And the final page says this:*
*that the Sunderer must die willingly at midwinter,*
*and the blade that kills the Sunderer*
*must be the blade of the one who shaped them.*

Bram stopped. Read it again. The characters did not change. True text never changed; that was the whole horror of it.

"The one who shaped them," Bram said. Their voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking through the open door. "Shaped. That's not *raised*. That's not *taught*. Shaped is what you do to—"

"To a blade," Maren said quietly. "Yes."

The room tilted. Bram gripped the desk's edge and the wood was solid and real and did not help at all.

"You," they said. "The final page. It's you. You're the one who—"

"I'm the one who kills the Sunderer," Maren said. "If there is a Sunderer. If the prophecy is served instead of broken. Yes. I've known since midsummer. I've had since midsummer to make my peace with it, which is more than you've had, and I'm telling you now because you deserve to hear it from me and not from a scrap of vellum, and because—" her voice frayed, just slightly, the first thread come loose, "—because I am so tired, Bram. I have been carrying this since midsummer alone, and I am not strong enough to carry it alone anymore, and I never should have tried."

Bram stared at her. The woman who had raised them. The woman whose lessons had been iron and whose silences had been longer than the winters. The woman they had fled barefoot through the frost with a stolen primer against their ribs, telling themselves for three years that anywhere was better than the Thornhedge Road, that freedom was worth any price, that they had escaped.

*Escaped.*

The word turned over and showed its underside.

"You trained me," Bram said slowly. Each word placed like a coin on a scale. "Eleven years. True text. No other craft—no healing worth the name, no weather-work, none of the hundred things a hedge-witch knows. Just reading. Just *me, reading*. You wouldn't even let me copy false text for practice. You said it would spoil the ear."

"It would have."

"Why?" Bram's voice rose. "Why me, why true text, why *eleven years*—"

"Because the codex's final page is true text," Maren said, "and it cannot be opened until the keeper bleeds on it, and once it opens, someone must read it correctly, all of it, every clause, every condition, every escape written into it by whichever frightened hand wrote it a century ago. If it's misread—if one character is mistaken—the sacrifice fails, or worse, it *succeeds wrong*, and the war comes anyway with no seal on it at all. The world gets one reader for that page, Bram. One. And I could not do it myself."

"Why not?"

"Because I can't write true text." She said it flatly, without shame, the way she'd once announced the price of a charm. "I can read it. Slowly. Painfully. With errors. And errors kill. So I searched for thirty years for someone who could do what I couldn't. And then a child came to my door who could see the difference between a true record and a false one *without being taught*—who cried the first time I showed them a forged deed, not because they were sad, but because the falseness *hurt their eyes*—"

"I was *six*," Bram whispered.

"You were six," Maren agreed. "And you were the answer to a question I'd been asking since before you were born. So yes. I trained you. I trained you for this. Every lesson, every locked gate, every winter of copying characters until your fingers bled—it was all for the final page. I told myself it was also for you, that true text would keep you safe, that a rare gift is armor. Some nights I even believed it." She met Bram's eyes, and did not look away, and Bram understood suddenly that this—this not looking away—was the hardest thing she had ever done. "But the truth is the truth, and you of all people deserve it without varnish: I built you for a purpose. The way you build a boat for a crossing. And I have regretted it every day since you ran, because the running proved what I'd refused to see—that boats, if they're lucky, become something else. Something that chooses its own water."

The counting room was silent. Through the open door, the guild murmured on—Vael's low instructions, Pell's slow footsteps, the ordinary music of a family that had taken Bram in without asking what they'd been made for.

Because that was the difference. That was the whole difference, laid bare under the lamp like a ledger opened to audit. The Gildenscale had taken them in and asked nothing. Maren had taken them in and asked *everything*—had asked it before they could speak, had spent their entire childhood paying a debt they'd never incurred, shaping them toward a death they hadn't consented to. Not their own death. Worse. The death of *someone else*, at the edge of a blade Maren would have to lift, and Bram would have to read the page that ordered it, correctly, perfectly, or the whole world would pay for their mistake.

Horror rose in Bram slowly, the way floodwater rises—quiet at first, then all at once everywhere. Not horror at Maren. Horror at the shape of their own life, seen whole for the first time. Every choice they'd prided themselves on—the flight, the guild, the ledgers, the numbers that never lied—even these bore fingerprints. Even their rebellion had been rehearsed in advance, a chapter in someone else's book. *The keeper of accounts.* They had run three thousand miles from the Thornhedge Road and landed precisely where the prophecy had filed them, like a document returning to its correct drawer.

"I'm not a boat," Bram said at last. Their voice was very quiet. "Or I didn't used to be. I don't know anymore. That's what you've taken from me—you understand that, don't you? Even if I break the prophecy. Even if I tear out the final page and burn it. I'll never know which parts of me were mine. The reading. The loving of clean numbers. Maybe even the running. You put your hands in the clay so long ago that I can't find the place where your hands end and I begin."

Maren flinched. It was a small thing, quickly mastered, but Bram saw it, and some cold, clear part of themselves noted it with satisfaction, and a larger, warmer part grieved at the satisfaction.

"No," Maren said. "You're right. And I won't insult you by apologizing cheaply. An apology that costs nothing is false text, and you'd see through it faster than anyone alive." She stood, slowly, joints cracking like the frost outside. "So here's what costs. The fragment stays with you—you read it better than I ever will, and you'll need it. And when the final page opens, if it opens, I will ask you—*ask* you, Bram, with words, out loud—to read it. And if you refuse, I will accept the refusal, and I will go to midwinter alone, and I will fail alone, and the consequences will fall on my head and not yours. That is the only unmaking I can offer. The rest—" she spread her hands, empty, "—the rest you'll have to do yourself. Amend the record. You're the only one who can."

She walked to the door. Stopped. Did not turn around.

"For what it's worth, little ledger—and it isn't worth much, I know the exchange rate on my own words—I didn't only train you for the page. Somewhere in those eleven years, against all my intentions, I taught you everything I know about surviving winters. And you survived three of them without me. Whatever I made, you've outgrown the making." A pause. "The guild's lucky. Though they don't know yet how lucky, or what's coming for them."

"What's coming for them?" Bram asked.

Maren turned her head, just enough that Bram could see her profile against the lamplight from the hall—the sharp jaw, the weathered cheek, the eyes that had counted omens for thirty years.

"The Remnant," she said. "They've been hunting the fragments longer than I have. And tomorrow, or the day after, they're going to stop hunting and start taking. Ask your grey-eyed buyer what she offered Mistress Vael, and then ask yourself what happens when an offer like that gets refused twice." She pulled her hood up. "Count your people, Bram. Count them carefully. While you still know what each of them is worth."

And she stepped out into the frost-dark, leaving the door open behind her, and Bram sat alone in the counting room with a dead woman's vellum in their hands and a single thought ringing in their skull like a struck bell:

*Everything I am was written by someone else.*

*Which means—*

The thought arrived whole, terrible and luminous, before they could stop it:

*—which means somewhere there may be a hand that can rewrite me.*

Bram looked down at the fragment, at the shimmering, unlying characters, and for the first time in their life they did not read the text.

They read the margins.

And in the margins, faint as breath on glass, in a hand they did not recognize, someone a century ago had written four words in ordinary, lying, human ink:

*the book can be amended.*
