## 16. The Keeper Bleeds First

The snow had stopped lying about what it was.

For three days it had come down soft and apologetic, dusting the rooftops of Saltreach like flour on a baker's hands, and the city had pretended—had wanted so badly to pretend—that this was ordinary winter arriving fashionably late. But on the fourth morning the sky went the color of pewter and held there, and the cold came down like a verdict, and every gutter in the Gildenscale quarter froze into a row of small white fangs. The long winter had stopped being a rumor. It was a fact now, and facts were Bram's native country, and even they could not find a kind way to enter this one into the ledger.

Bram wrapped the codex in oilcloth, then in wool, then in a scarf Juniper had knitted badly and Vael had worn ironically for a season before declaring it *structurally sincere*. Three layers against the cold and against everything else. The bundle went inside their coat, over their heart, which was where Bram had decided important things ought to be carried. They had read enough of the prophecy by now to know that carrying it anywhere else was a luxury the world no longer offered.

"You're going out *again*," Pell said from his chair by the door. He said it the way he said most things these days—half statement, half accusation, all love.

"The archive fragment Osk marked in the drowned charts," Bram said, tugging their gloves on. "The second half of the Ash Codex. If the Remnant gets it before we do—"

"If the Remnant gets it, we'll fight them with wit and spite, same as always." Pell's hands were folded on his cane, and they trembled, and he looked at them trembling as though they belonged to someone he'd been introduced to once at a party. "Bram. Boy. Listen to an old man who has buried more friends than he can count on one winter's worth of fingers. Nothing that must be carried through snow at dawn has ever turned out to be worth it."

"That's not true," Bram said. "You carried me through snow once. First night I came to the guild. You told me."

Pell's face did something complicated, the way old faces do when memory and dread shake hands. "And look what grew out of it," he said softly. "Go on, then. Go. But you come back through my door before the lamps are lit, or I'll come looking, and I'm too old to run and too mean to die."

Bram kissed the top of his head—he smelled of pipe smoke and beeswax and the particular patience of doorkeepers—and stepped out into the white morning.

* * *

Saltreach in deep cold was a city holding its breath. The harbor had frozen at its edges, a lacework of grey ice creeping inward from the pilings, and the gulls sat hunched along the seawall like an audience waiting for something terrible to begin. Bram moved quickly, head down, counting steps the way they counted everything—it was four hundred and twelve paces from the guild door to the chandler's stairs where Osk was supposed to meet them, and they had counted three hundred and six when they realized the street was too quiet.

Not empty. Too quiet. There is a difference, and Bram had learned it the way one learns most true things: afterward, when it was nearly too late.

The fish market should have been shouting. The chandlers should have been swearing. Instead there was only the creak of ice in the harbor and the sound of their own breath and—there, behind them, muffled by snow—the soft deliberate fall of boots keeping pace.

Bram did not run. Running was what prey did, and whatever else the prophecy had made them, they refused to be prey. They walked. They counted. At three hundred and twenty they cut left down Tanner's Lane, where the walls narrowed and the snow lay unbroken, so that anyone following would have to leave footprints, would have to *be accounted for*. Numbers never lie. Numbers had never once lied to Bram Brightwater, and it was numbers that told them, in the flat honest voice numbers use, that there were four sets of boots behind them and one set ahead, waiting, at the mouth of the lane.

They had walked into a sentence, and they were somewhere near the middle of it.

"Bram Brightwater." The voice came from the figure at the far end of the lane—a woman in a grey seal-hide coat, her hair bound back with cord, her eyes the color of weathered slate. The grey-eyed buyer. The Remnant. She stood with her hands open at her sides, which was somehow worse than a drawn blade. "You've been very difficult to arrange a meeting with."

"I keep a busy calendar," Bram said. Their heart was going like a drum in a procession, but their voice came out level, and they were privately astonished by it.

"So I hear. Ledgers, inventories, auctions." She took a step forward; the boots behind Bram took a step forward too, closing the sentence from both ends. "We only want the book."

"Which book?" Bram asked, because stalling was also a form of accounting—every second entered, every second spent.

The woman smiled without warmth. "You know which book. The one that names you."

The words landed in Bram's chest like stones dropped down a well. *Names you.* They had suspected. They had lain awake in their narrow room above the counting floor suspecting, turning the codex's phrases over and over—*the keeper of accounts shall open the way; the keeper bleeds first; by the keeper's hand the page is made legible*—and each time they had found some other meaning, some other keeper, because hope is a forger more skilled than Juniper could ever dream of being.

"I don't know what you mean," Bram said, and reached for the codex through three layers of cloth, purely by instinct, the way a hand goes to a wound.

The woman saw. Of course she saw.

"Take it," she said—not to Bram, to the others—and the lane erupted.

* * *

Later, Bram would try to remember the fight in order, the way they tried to remember everything, and fail. Memory after blood is an unreliable ledger. What remained were fragments: the first man coming off the wall of the cooperage with a boathook; Bram ducking under its arc and driving their shoulder into his knees; the snow taking them both; the shocking warmth of blood that turned out to be their own, welling from a slice along their scalp where a ring or a buckle had caught them. The second attacker was faster, a knife-man, economical. The third hung back with a hooked pole and simply waited, patient as tide, letting the others spend Bram's strength while he saved his own.

Bram fought the way they did everything: precisely, stubbornly, and without any gift for it whatsoever. They had never been trained. Maren had taught them herbs and letters and how to hold still while the truth passed through them—not how to break a grip, not where to put a fist. They bought seconds with desperation and spent them badly, and knew they were spending them badly, and kept spending anyway, because the bundle was over their heart and the bundle was the point.

The knife-man got the coat open on the fourth exchange. Bram felt the blade glance off the wool, off the oilcloth, and then—cold, then hot, then a strange distant burning like a candle held too close—into them, below the ribs on the left side, shallow but real, the kind of wound that means *I could have gone deeper and chose to ask a question instead.*

"*Enough*," the grey-eyed woman said, and the lane stopped.

Bram was on one knee in the trampled snow. The codex bundle had come loose from their coat and lay in the slush between them and the woman, three layers of sincerity darkening with red. Bram's blood. Their own blood, soaking into the wrapping of a book written in ash a hundred years ago by people who had edited history to flatter themselves.

The woman crouched, took the bundle, weighed it in her hand. Then she looked at Bram—at the wound, at the blood on the snow, at the way Bram's hand pressed instinctively against their side—and her expression changed. Not to cruelty. To recognition. Which was worse.

"Oh," she said quietly. "You didn't know."

"Didn't know what," Bram managed. The world was tilting in a slow, expensive way.

"The final page." She rose, holding the codex the way one holds a key one has searched a house for years to find. "It can't be opened by hands. We've tried. Fire, force, prayer—we tried all of it, and the ash-script stays shut, and our scholars tore their hair, and our queens despaired. Because it isn't locked to a hand, keeper of accounts. It's locked to *blood*. Yours. The blood of the one who keeps the accounts of impossible things, spilled in the book's defense." She tilted her head, studying Bram almost gently. "You bled for it just now. Did you feel it? Under the wool? Did the pages go warm?"

And Bram—God help them, God help anyone whose whole life is noticing things—realized that they *had* felt it. A heat against their chest in the moment after the knife, a pulse of warmth like a struck match, which they had attributed to fever or fear or the body's ordinary treacheries. Not fear. Recognition. The book knew its keeper the way a lock knows its key: at the exact moment the key begins to turn.

"No," Bram said aloud, because saying it made it a lie they could later correct. "No, that's—that's not—"

"You're the keeper," the woman said. "You've been the keeper since the night you opened the estate ledger and found your own date written in ash. The prophecy didn't choose you last week, child. It's been choosing you since before you were born. And now it has what it needs." She tucked the codex inside her coat. "We don't need to kill you. Why would we? You'll open the page for us yourself, willingly or otherwise, the next time you bleed near it. All wars need a door, and you—" she smiled again, that warmless smile— "you're the door."

She turned to go, and Bram, kneeling in the blood-warm slush with a hole in their side and their life's work walking away in a stranger's coat, did the only thing their nature permitted.

They laughed.

It startled everyone, including Bram—a short, cracked, unmistakably real laugh that steamed in the freezing air. The grey-eyed woman paused.

"What," she said, "is funny?"

"Bookkeeping," Bram said. "All my life I thought it was the dullest gift in the world. My teacher said I had a talent for true text and I thought, wonderful, I'll spend my years checking sums for smugglers. And it turns out the entire fate of two peoples turns on whether a page opens." They pressed their hand harder against their side and watched red seep between their fingers, and beneath the terror, beneath the cold, something in them shifted and set, like a keel finding water. "You think I'm a door. Fine. Doors swing both ways."

The woman studied them a long moment. Whatever she saw made her frown faintly, the way a chess player frowns when the board does something unsanctioned.

"Come find us when midwinter comes," she said. "Or don't. Either way, keeper, the sea remembers what's owed." Then the four of them were moving, up the lane, over the wall, gone into the white like ink absorbed into paper, leaving only bootprints that the falling snow was already, diligently, entering into the record and striking out.

* * *

Osk found them eleven minutes later—or nine hundred seconds, though Bram, drifting against the chandler's wall with their coat pressed to their wound, had lost the habit of counting for the first time since childhood. Osk came around the corner at a dead run, took one look, and made a sound Bram had never heard him make, a raw syllable with no craft in it at all.

"Tide's wrong," Osk said, dropping to his knees in the snow, hands hovering over the wound without touching. "Tide's been pulling backward since dawn, I was coming to tell you, and then I heard—" He stopped. Tide-readers are not men of many words, but his face said the rest: *I heard fighting, and I ran toward it, and I was late.*

"They have the codex," Bram said. Each word cost a coin they could ill afford. "Grey-eyes. Four of them. Took the book."

"We'll get it back. Vael will—Juniper can—"

"No." Bram caught Osk's wrist. Their glove was slick. "Listen. Listen to me, Osk, because I finally understand the shape of it and I need it witnessed before I faint or die or worse, start making sense of it slowly." They breathed. The breath whistled. "The final page doesn't open to force. It opens to my blood. Spilled defending it. That's why 'the keeper bleeds first.' Not a threat. An *instruction*. The prophecy wrote me into itself the way you write a tide-table—because that's just when the water arrives." A laugh shivered out of them, thin and unhinged. "I'm not a victim of the prophecy, Osk. I'm a *component*."

Osk stared at him. Snow gathered on his shoulders and in his lashes and he did not brush it away. When he spoke, his voice was very low and very steady, the voice he used reading water. "Then we stop being components. We change the reading."

"You can't change a tide-table."

"No," Osk agreed. "But you can build a seawall. Can you stand?"

Bram considered the question honestly, the way they considered all questions of account. "If you count standing generously."

"Good. Because Pell said before the lamps are lit or he comes looking, and I have seen Pell come looking." Osk got an arm under Bram's shoulders and hauled, gentle as a crane, and together they stood, two figures leaning into the wind in a lane full of ruined snow, and began the long four hundred and twelve paces home.

Something had changed in Bram with every step. The terror was still there—it lived in the wound, in the wet warmth seeping steadily through their shirt—but it had stopped being a flood and become a current, and currents, unlike floods, have direction. They knew what the Remnant wanted now. They knew what the page required. They knew that every drop of their blood was a key turning in a lock built a century ago by liars who had edited the war to suit the winners.

And if the prophecy had entered them into its books—

Well. Bram Brightwater had been amending other people's entries since they were fourteen years old. Let the ash-script think it held the pen.

Halfway home, the bleeding slowed. Halfway home, Bram noticed something else, something they would examine later, in the lamplit safety of the counting floor: beneath the wool, against their skin, the ghost of a warmth that was not their own. The codex, miles away now in a stranger's coat, and still they could feel it—faint as a pulse through a wall, patient as tide.

It was waiting for them.

Let it wait, Bram thought, and leaned harder on Osk, and counted the paces home.
