## 17. Juniper's Confession

The salt cellar had not been meant for prisoners.

It had been meant for pickled herring and barrels of brined eel, for the guild's winter stores and Vael's infamous plum brandy that could strip varnish from a door. But the door was oak and iron now, and the little window near the ceiling had been shuttered with a plank, and the lamp they'd left her burned low enough to make shadows of everything. Juniper sat on an upturned crate in a room that smelled of old fish and older secrets, and she thought: *this is what it is to be forged over.*

Three days. Or four. She had lost count somewhere between the second interrogation and the moment Bram had looked at her through the bars—not with hatred, which would have been bearable, but with that terrible open grief, like a ledger left out in the rain, every line running.

*Her forged codex was found among the enemy's things.* That was all anyone had needed. The evidence had been tidy as a receipt: grey-cloaked Remnant soldiers with Pell in irons, and tucked into their commander's satchel, a codex in Juniper's own hand—the false one, the beautiful one, the one she had bled her memories into page by page until she no longer knew which parts of her childhood were hers and which were ink.

She understood why they believed it. She almost believed it herself.

That was the thing about forgery that no one warned you about, when you apprenticed to the craft. You did not merely copy. To make a lie convincing, you had to live inside it while you worked—you had to *believe* the prophecy as your pen moved, feel its weight, its certainty, let it settle into your hands the way true text settled into Bram's. And belief, once invited in, did not leave politely when the work was done. It stayed. It rearranged the furniture. It whispered, in the small hours, that perhaps the memories you were so sure of—the ones from before, the ones no one else could corroborate—were things you had written rather than lived.

Juniper had been forging since she was nine years old. She had forged letters of passage and bills of lading, seals and signatures, a whole dead duke's correspondence once, forty pages of it, and slept soundly after. She had never been afraid of her own skill before this winter.

Now she sat in a pickle cellar and could not swear to her own name's history.

* * *

They came at lamplighting. She heard them on the stairs—two sets of feet, one heavy and reluctant, one light and quick—and knew them before the key turned. Pell's tread, and Bram's.

Pell came first, filling the doorway, his great grey beard uncombed, his knuckles scarred from three days of demanding her release in language that had made Vael wince. He would not look at her. He stood by the door like a man standing watch over something he was ashamed to guard.

Bram came in slowly, carrying the lamp, and set it on a barrel between them so that the light fell evenly, honestly, the way Bram liked light to fall. Their bandaged forearm was visible where their sleeve had ridden up—the wound from the ambush, the keeper's blood, though only Bram and Maren yet knew what that meant. They looked tired in a way that went past the body.

"Vael says I get one hour," Bram said. "She says if I come out convinced, we reopen the question." A pause. "I told her I wasn't coming down here to be convinced. I'm coming down here to listen."

"You came down here because you're the only one who reads true text," Juniper said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended, scraped raw from disuse. "You want to watch my face while I talk and see if I'm lying."

"Yes," Bram said simply. "That too."

There was no point being offended. There was no point being anything, really, except honest—which was its own bitter joke, given her trade. She folded her hands in her lap and found they were shaking, and made herself look at them instead of at Bram, because looking at Bram hurt.

"Ask me," she said.

"I don't have questions," Bram said. "I have one. Everyone has the same one. Did you sell us?"

"No."

"That's not—" Bram stopped. Breathed. Started again, gentler. "The route was sold, Juniper. The archive is gone. Pell spent two days in chains. Someone gave the Remnant exactly what they needed, and every thread leads back to you. If the answer is no, then tell me what the yes actually looks like. Tell me all of it. Don't curate it. Don't make it pretty. I've spent three years reading ledgers; I'll know if you round the numbers."

Juniper laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing in it. "You want the ugly version."

"I want the true version."

And there it was, the word, sitting in the lamplight between them like a coin on a table. *True.* The thing she had spent her whole life counterfeiting. The thing Bram carried in their fingers like a second heartbeat.

So she gave it to him. Gave it to them. She gave it raw.

* * *

"It started with the forgery itself," she said. "The false codex. You remember—I said I could do it in a fortnight, and Vael said do it in ten days, because the Remnant's buyer was coming back and Vael wanted the sale done before he grew suspicious or impatient, whichever came first. Ten days. Do you know what ten days of forging prophecy does to you? No. Of course you don't. You read true text; you've never had to build a lie big enough to stand in.

"To forge it right, I couldn't just copy the style. Style can be checked. I had to make it *feel* prophetic, and prophecy feels like certainty. So I wrote it as if I believed it. Every session, I'd sit down and say to myself: this is real, this came from the drowned archive, this is the voice of the sea speaking through whoever wrote it, and I am only the hand. And I'd write. And it worked—it worked so well that when Osk read it, he went pale, and Osk doesn't go pale. When Vael read it, she said, 'Gods, Juniper, I half believe you.' That was the compliment. That was the *point.*

"But here's what I didn't tell anyone. Belief takes payment. I don't know how else to describe it. It was like the codex drank from me while I fed it. Small things at first. I'd finish a page and reach for a memory to steady myself—my mother's kitchen, the smell of the bread—and it would be... smooth. Blank in the middle. Like a wall someone had whitewashed. I thought I was tired. Ten days of work, sleeping four hours a night, of course the shelves felt bare.

"By the last day I couldn't find my mother's face at all. I could find the *idea* of her—a shape, warm, gone. My ninth birthday, the one I've told a hundred times, the one with the magpie and the stolen pie—I reached for it and found I was holding a description instead of a memory. Words. My own words, the ones I'd used to tell the story, arranged in the order I always told them. I couldn't tell whether I remembered the day or just the telling.

"And then—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, because Bram had asked for ugly— "then I realized I couldn't prove any of it had ever happened. Everything from before the guild. Before Vael bought me off a ship in Corvess harbor, fourteen years old with a satchel of forged papers and no surname I trusted. All of it was behind glass now. I could see it but I couldn't touch it. I had built a perfect forgery of a prophecy by spending the one thing I owned outright: my past."

Pell shifted by the door. The floorboards creaked under his weight, and Juniper heard him breathe out slow through his nose, the way he did when he was keeping his temper on a leash.

"So when the grey-eyed man's people came back with the offer—not the codex offer, the other one, the quiet one, the note slipped under the door of the Salt & Anchor addressed to 'the Forger'—I was already half-hollowed. Already doubting everything I remembered, including what I remembered about *them.* Including whether the guild's warmth was real or another thing I'd written. And the note said: we know what the forgery cost you. We can give it back. Your memories, restored, whole, guaranteed. The price is small. A route. Old smuggler ways. Pell knows them; everyone knows Pell knows them; they're hardly secret, they're just unfashionable."

She stopped. Made herself keep going.

"I didn't sell the route to hurt anyone. I want that on the record, even if the record is a pickle cellar and two people who may never forgive me. I sold it because I wanted to be *whole*, and they dangled wholeness in front of me like meat before a starving dog, and I was so tired, Bram. I was so tired of reaching into myself and finding pages torn out. You can't imagine what it's like to doubt your own childhood. To stand in your own head and not know which walls are original construction.

"But that's not the confession. That's the excuse, and I promised you no excuses. Here is the confession:

"The note asked for the routes. I didn't give them the routes. I gave them something better—or worse—I gave them *Pell*. I told their contact where Pell would be, when, and with what, because Pell was the one carrying the archive out along those routes, and I reasoned—gods help me, I *reasoned*—that if the Remnant took Pell, they'd take the archive, and the exchange would happen fast, clean, one prisoner for one price, and no one else would ever need to know I'd been the hinge. I told myself I was containing the damage. I told myself I alone should bear the risk, because I alone had already paid part of it. I told myself Pell was the toughest of us, the oldest, the one who'd survived the last war, the one who'd laugh at a cell and walk out of it in a week.

"I never asked Pell if he agreed to be spent that way. I never asked anyone. I decided, alone, in the dark, with a hollowed-out heart, that I got to choose which of us was collateral. And that—that—is what I did. Not the route. Me. I made myself the author of our family's worst night, and I dressed it up as sacrifice because sacrifice was the only story I still knew how to write convincingly."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, the guild house creaked against the wind, and the long winter pressed at the walls like a patient animal.

* * *

Silence held the cellar for a long time. Juniper watched Bram's face across the lamplight, watched them turn her words over the way they turned entries in a ledger, testing each figure, looking for the place where the columns failed to sum.

They didn't fail. That was the horror of it. Every number added up.

"Pell," Bram said quietly. "You're hearing this."

"I'm hearing it." Pell's voice was gravel dragged over stone. He still hadn't looked at her. "Two days in irons. They broke two of my fingers asking who else knew the routes. I told them nothing, partly from stubbornness and partly because—" he paused, and his jaw worked— "partly because I couldn't imagine who'd have sold it. Couldn't imagine it was someone in the house. That was my mistake too, maybe. Loving this family so hard I couldn't see it clearly."

"I'm sorry." It came out of Juniper before she could dress it in anything. "Pell, I am sorry. Not the polite kind. The kind that sits in your chest like swallowed anchor-chain. I chose you because I thought you were unbreakable, and that's its own kind of cruelty—deciding someone's strength means their suffering doesn't count. Your fingers. Your two days. The fear you must have felt, chained in a Remnant hold, wondering which of us—"

"Don't," Pell said. "Don't narrate my fear for me. I'm old, girl, not fragile. I was afraid, yes. And angry. And then, somewhere around the second bowl of their rotten barley soup, I was mostly *bored*, which is the truest state of the aged soldier. What I want to know isn't whether you're sorry. Sorry is cheap; I've heard sorry from a hundred condemned men. What I want to know is this: when they offered to give your memories back—did you believe them? Truly? Because if you believed them, then you believed they could hand you a past, and a past handed to you by the enemy is a past they wrote. You'd have traded everything you are for a forgery of yourself. Their forgery. In their hand. With their edits."

Juniper stared at him. Of all the blows struck in this cellar, that one landed deepest, because it was true in a way she hadn't let herself see.

"They'd have written me," she whispered. "I'd have become their document."

"Aye," Pell said, and finally, finally turned his head and looked at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were not cold—they were old and weathered and full of something that might have been pity wearing anger's coat. "Which is why I'm not going to vote to put you out, whatever Vael decides. You made a forger's error, girl. The worst one there is. You forgot that anything given to you complete and whole and painless is counterfeit. Real things cost. Real memories cost. Yours cost you at the writing desk, fair and square, and they're *gone*, and that's the true price, and you paid it. The Remnant's version would've cost you nothing and been worth less. You nearly traded gold for gilt. That's not treachery. That's craftsmanship failing at the worst possible moment."

"That's a generous reading," Bram said.

"It's an accurate one," Pell said. "I've known treachery. I fought beside traitors in the last war, watched men sell bridges and battalions and their own mothers' graves. Treachery looks away from the cost. This one—" he nodded at Juniper— "this one counted the cost wrong, but she counted. There's a difference. Thin as vellum, maybe. But I've staked my life on thinner."

* * *

Bram picked up the lamp. Moved it closer, so the light fell full on Juniper's face, and she understood what they were doing—reading her, the way they read text, looking beneath the surface words for the true hand underneath.

"One more question," Bram said. "The memories you lost. Can you feel where they were?"

Juniper frowned. "What do you mean?"

"When you reach for your mother's kitchen. Is it blank? Or is it *blank like something was removed*?" Bram's voice had gone careful, precise, the ledger-voice, the voice that noticed a column one digit off. "Because there's a difference between a page that was never written and a page that was cut out. One leaves nothing. The other leaves a scar in the binding."

Juniper reached inward, the way she had reached a hundred times these past weeks, hating herself a little more each time. Down past the guild years, which were solid, crowded, real—down to the white space where her childhood had been.

And there, at the edge of the blankness, she felt it. A roughness. A texture like torn vellum, like the ghost of a stub where a page had been razored close to the spine. Not empty. *Excised.*

"Oh," she said, very softly. "Oh, gods. It's cut. It's cut out. I can feel the knife-marks."

"Then it wasn't the forgery," Bram said, and their voice shook, and Juniper saw their eyes go wide and bright in the lamplight with something that was not accusation at all. "Or not only. Juniper—listen to me. I've handled a hundred forged documents. Forgery doesn't cut memories out of a living mind. Nothing we do cuts memories out of a living mind. Something *took* them. Something took them before, or during, or—" they stopped, and Juniper watched the thought arrive in their face like weather. "The codex. The true codex. It took payment from me too, the night I opened it. I told no one. My hands were warm on the page and afterward I couldn't remember the hedge-witch's garden, the one I grew up in, and I thought I was just tired."

Pell made a noise like a door slamming. "Both of you," he said. "Both of you paid, and neither of you told the house. This family keeps ledgers of impossible things and forgets to enter its own losses in them. That ends tonight."

And then, in the lamplight, in the fish-smelling dark, Pell did the thing Juniper would carry longer than the chains, longer than the broken fingers, longer than the shame. He crossed the cellar, took her by both shoulders—gently, so gently, minding the bruises the Remnant had left—and pulled her up off the crate and into his arms, beard and wool and pipe-smoke and the enormous slow heartbeat of him, and said into her hair:

"You stupid, brave, foolish girl. Next time you decide to spend one of us, you ask first. We're not stock. We're family. Family gets a vote."

"I'm sorry," Juniper said into his shoulder, and this time the word was not cheap, because it was not the end of anything. It was the beginning of the long work of earning it back.

Bram stood watching, lamp in hand, and did not hug her, because Bram was Bram—but they set the lamp down on the crate beside her, deliberately, within her reach, and said, "When Vael calls the vote, I'm speaking for you. And after—" they hesitated, and their bandaged arm twinged, and they pressed it without seeming to notice— "after, I need you at my table. Tonight. Both of you. Because I found something in the codex's margins today, something in faded ash-brown script that I couldn't read until now, and I think—Juniper, I think it's in *your mother's hand.*"

Juniper's breath left her all at once. Her mother. The woman whose face she could no longer find. Whose kitchen was a whitewashed wall. Whose handwriting might be waiting, all this time, in the margins of the book that had started everything—

"The vote comes at dawn," Bram said, pulling the cellar key from their belt and turning it in the lock, opening the door onto the stairs and the lamplit world above. "But the table is lit now. Come up. Bring what's left of you. We'll start there."

Juniper rose on legs that felt like a forger's worst nightmare—uncertain, provisional, drawn in pencil—climbed the stairs past Pell, past Bram, toward the light.

And on the top step, she felt the torn edge of herself catch once more on something sharp and true: the knowledge that whatever waited in those margins, it had been written by a hand she loved and could no longer picture—and that the next page of her own story would have to be written fresh, in her own hand, witnessed by the family who had chosen, at dawn, to keep her.
