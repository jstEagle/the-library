## 18. The Last Page of Winter

The wound on Bram's palm had stopped bleeding twice that day, and both times Maren had made them open it again.

"It wants to be fresh," she said, not unkindly, kneeling at the worktable in the back room of the Gildenscale where the codex lay open beneath three lamps and a circle of salt that Juniper had poured with hands that only shook a little. "True text drinks what's freely given. A scab is a kind of lie."

"You could have said that before," Bram muttered.

"I could have said a great many things before." Maren did not look up from the page. Her hair had gone almost entirely white since the last time Bram had stood this close to her—three years, and it might have been thirty—and her hands, those hands she had once held out wordlessly before every lesson, asking to see Bram's palms the way other teachers asked for homework, were folded now and quiet on the table's edge. She had aged into something softer than the woman in Bram's memories, or perhaps Bram had simply run far enough to forget her edges. Either way, she was here. She had crossed a whole winter to be here, and she would not say why until the page was open, and Bram was beginning to understand that whatever she was withholding was heavier than anything she'd already confessed.

*The final page names me,* she had told Bram two nights ago, in the kitchen, over tea neither of them drank. *I am to kill the Sunderer. That is why I taught you to read true text, Bram. Not because I loved you. Because someone had to.*

Bram had carried that sentence around for two days like a stone in the boot. Now they crouched at the table and looked at the codex—the seized estate's prize, the book that had started everything, its vellum gone the color of old tea, its binding stitched with thread that Oswin swore was spun kelp—and tried to feel nothing at all, because feeling too much made the true text swim.

"Again," Maren said.

Bram pressed their reopened palm flat against the final leaf.

For a moment there was only paper: fibrous, dry, faintly sweet with the rot of centuries. Then the warmth came, as it always came now, rising through the skin like sunlight through shallow water, and the blank surface began to move.

It did not change so much as *admit*. The letters were there all along, Bram understood—had always been there, pressed down into the vellum the way footprints are pressed into snow, invisible until the light comes slantwise. Their blood was the slantwise light. The ink surfaced slowly, ash-brown, each letter arriving like a fish rising to feed, mouth first.

And Bram, keeper of accounts, reader of true text, read.

*When the sea stands still and the year stands shortest,
the Sundered Sea shall ask its price.
One shall offer the throat willingly,
one who keeps the count of all things lent and lost,
and the war shall sleep another hundred winters
bound in the blood of the offering.
But if none offers, or if one offers unwilling,
then the seal breaks downward, and the drowned rise,
and the shore will learn again what the deep remembers.*

Bram read it aloud because Maren asked them to, and because reading it aloud made it real in the way numbers are real, and because some part of them still believed—if belief is the right word for a thing that feels like falling—that saying a thing plainly enough can shrink it.

It did not shrink it.

The room was very quiet when they finished. Outside, the harbor had frozen in long gray teeth all the way to the breakers; the tide had been standing still for six days, exactly as the verse promised, and the whole city spoke of nothing else. Fishermen burned candles on their decks. Children dared each other to walk on the ice. And here, in a guildhouse full of stolen wonders and borrowed years, five people stood around a dead man's prophecy and heard the meter tick toward midwinter.

*Midwinter is in nine days.*

"Well," said Pell, from his chair by the door, where he had insisted on sitting even though his ribs were still strapped from the Remnant's cellar. His voice was gravel over old grief. "At least it's plain. I've sat through a hundred auctions where less was said with ten times the words."

"Pell." Vael's voice was warning and plea at once.

"No, let me say it." He leaned forward, knuckles like driftwood on the chair's arms. "Someone dies. Willingly. At midwinter. Or the drowned come up and every soul on this coast pays instead. That's the ledger. I've read enough of Bram's ledgers to know a balance when I hear one. There's no line here for negotiation."

"There is always a line for negotiation," Vael said, but her voice cracked on *always*, and everyone heard it.

Juniper had not spoken since the reading began. She stood at the edge of the salt circle, arms wrapped around herself, and when Bram glanced at her they saw her lips moving—copying, they realized. Even now, even shattered and half-remade, she was memorizing the text, letter by letter, the forger's reflex so deep in her that it survived the loss of her childhood. Perhaps it had *eaten* her childhood. Perhaps that was the trade the forgery had taken, paid in installments she hadn't known she was making.

"It doesn't name anyone," Juniper said at last. Her voice was thin but steady. "I've checked. Twice. The earlier fragments—the ones we bled for, the ones people died for—they named 'the keeper of accounts.' But this page says only *one who keeps the count of all things lent and lost*." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "That could be any of us. That has *always* been able to be any of us. We keep counts. It's what we do. Vael keeps the auction book. Osk keeps the tide tables. Pell keeps the door-log. I keep—" she faltered—"I kept the samples of every hand I ever forged. We are all keepers of counts. This prophecy wrote itself a net wide enough to catch whoever walked into it."

"And it caught me first," Bram said quietly. "*The keeper bleeds first.* That was Pell's scrap. That was the agent who died holding it. And I bled, and the page opened. It's me, Juni. It's been me since the night I found the date in ash."

"No," said Osk.

Everyone turned. The tide-reader stood by the shuttered window, and he looked worse than any of them—worse than Pell's broken ribs, worse than Juniper's frayed self—because Osk had spent six days watching the sea hold its breath, and a man who reads water learns to read dread.

"You don't get to decide alone," he said. "That's what you're all circling. Bram bleeds, therefore Bram decides. No. The verse says *the guild must choose*. Listen to it again—*one shall offer*. One. It doesn't say which one, and it doesn't say the one who opened the page gets to volunteer for the rest of us. We vote. We've always voted. That's the Gildenscale. That's the whole point of us."

"The Gildenscale trades in impossible things," Vael said. "This may be the most impossible thing we've ever handled. Which means it goes on the block. Which means—" She stopped. Everyone watched her stop. Mistress Vael, who had faced down soldiers and sea-witches and the grey-eyed buyer without blinking, put one hand flat on the table as though the floor had tilted. "Which means we must decide, by vote, whether one of us dies. And I find, standing here, that I do not know how to conduct that auction."

"Then don't conduct it," Maren said.

She had barely moved all evening. Now she rose, slow as a tide herself, and Bram saw for the first time how heavily she carried whatever the final page had written about her—how it bent her shoulders the way a yoke bends an ox.

"Let an old woman tell you what she knows," Maren said. "I have read this text longer than any of you have been alive. I trained myself to read true text forty years ago for this purpose, and when my own eyes failed the task, I found a child whose gift ran deeper than mine, and I trained that child instead." Her gaze found Bram's, and it did not waver, and it did not apologize. "You may hate me for it. You should, probably. But hear the rest. The verse says *willingly*. That word is doing more work than any other word on the page. True text does not waste words. A willing offering binds the seal for a hundred winters. An unwilling one breaks it. Which means the Remnant cannot force this. Which means their only path to war is to make sure no one offers—or to trick someone into offering falsely. And which means—"

"—that whoever offers has to mean it," Bram finished. Their voice sounded far away to their own ears. "All the way down. No despair dressed up as duty. no sacrifice made to spare the others guilt. It has to be *true*."

"Yes," Maren said. "And child, you are the only person in this room who can read whether an offering is true. Including your own."

The silence after that was the worst silence Bram had ever stood inside. It had texture. It had weight, like deep water. Somewhere above them the house creaked against the wind, and somewhere below, in the vaults, the bottled storms murmured in their glass the way they always did when the weather outside argued with the weather inside.

* * *

They did not vote that night. Vael refused to bring it while the lamps were low and the fear was high, and she was right to—Bram watched her marshal the refusal the way she marshaled a bidding floor, with grace and absolute authority, and loved her for it, and hated the tenderness of loving her, because tenderness was going to make everything harder.

So they dispersed to the small rituals that held them together. Pell insisted on walking the perimeter despite his ribs, and Osk went with him, and their two voices drifted up through the floorboards, low and continuous, the oldest sound in the house. Juniper shut herself in the scriptorium—not forging, she'd sworn off forging, but copying, copying, copying the prophecy onto clean paper in a fair hand, because she said someone should own a version of it that wasn't trying to kill them. Vael sat in the counting room with the auction book open in front of her and did not write in it for two hours.

And Bram went down to the salt cellar, where the ledgers lived, and took out the oldest one—their own first inventory, the night three years ago when a runaway with blistered feet and a head full of hedge-magic had been handed a pen and told, *numbers never lie, little one; start there and maybe you'll learn to trust something else.*

Maren found them there. Of course she did. She had always been able to find Bram; that was one of the things Bram had run from.

"You're going to offer yourself," she said. Not a question.

"The verse fits me. The blood fit the page. The date in the codex—" Bram closed the old ledger gently. "I found tonight's date in ash-brown ink three seasons ago, Maren. Before the frost. Before the Remnant. Before any of it. Whatever this book is, it's been waiting for me specifically. I'm not going to make the others carry it."

"Mm." Maren lowered herself onto the stool across the table, joints cracking like ship's rigging. For a long moment she just looked at Bram, the way she used to look at a difficult text before touching it. Then she said, "Read me. Right now. Read my intent, the way I taught you. Tell me what I want."

"That's cruel."

"Everything true is cruel. That's why most people prefer lies. Read me."

Bram sighed and reached across the table and took Maren's hands in theirs—the gesture reversed, teacher's hands held by student's—and let the gift rise.

Reading a person was like reading waterlogged paper. Most people's surfaces were blurred; their intentions soaked through layer upon layer until nothing stayed legible. But Maren had spent a lifetime making herself legible to true readers, and underneath sixty years of discipline Bram found the stratum they expected: resolve, iron-cold, the shape of a blade raised. And beneath that—

Grief. Old, calcified, load-bearing grief. And beneath *that*, so deep Bram nearly missed it, a wish so simple it hurt:

*I don't want it to be you. I never wanted it to be you. I would take it myself if the page would take me, and it won't, and that is the cruelest thing the true text ever taught me.*

Bram let go. Their eyes stung.

"You've been trying to open that page yourself," they said. "Since you read the signs. That's why you really came—not to kill the Sunderer. To *be* the offering, if the book would let you. And it wouldn't."

"No." Maren's voice did not shake. Sixty years of practice. "It wouldn't. I've bled on that page eleven ways, child. It wants what it wants. I am the hand that kills; I am not the throat that's offered. Believe me, I have cursed the grammar of it every night for a year."

"Then help me do it properly," Bram said. "If it must be done, teach me how to mean it."

Maren looked at them for a long time. And then—for the first time in Bram's memory, in memory at all—the hedge-witch bowed her head, and her discipline cracked, and she wept, silently, the way people weep who learned young that tears cost extra.

"I'll teach you," she said, "to mean it. And every day between now and midwinter, I'll also teach you every loophole in that verse I've found in forty years of hunting. Because I am a selfish old woman, Bram Brightwater, and I did not cross a frozen country to bury the best thing I ever made."

* * *

They worked for six days.

Maren knew the true text the way Pell knew smugglers' coves: every current, every sandbar, every place where the wording thinned. She showed Bram how the verse bound itself—*another hundred winters* echoing the original sealing, how the seal required a *count*, how the phrase *all things lent and lost* was older than the guild, older than language's politeness, reaching back to whatever first transaction had sundered sea from shore in the deep past. She taught Bram the difference between meaning a thing and wanting to mean it, and how true text could taste the gap between them.

And every day, the guild gathered, and every day, Vael called the question she dreaded, and every day, someone moved to table it. Osk wanted to dive the ruins again, to search the drowned archive for an amendment clause—there had been one edit before; there could be others. Juniper combed her fair-hand copy for ambiguities the way she once hunted flaws in banknotes. Pell offered himself twice, formally, and was voted down twice, furiously, and sat through both votes looking like a man being robbed.

On the sixth night—the night before midwinter eve—the house was silent, and Bram sat alone at the worktable with the codex, their palm scarred and re-scarred, and read the verse one final time before morning.

And that was when they saw it.

Not in the prophecy. Beside it. In the margin, in a hand so faint it might have been breath—a single line of true text, hidden deeper than the page itself, visible only because six days of Maren's lessons had taught Bram to read not just ink but *pressure*, the ghost-weight of a stylus that had written and been scraped away:

*The count can be amended by the counter, at equal price.*

Bram stared at it. Their heart did something complicated and painful.

Equal price. The counter. Whoever kept the count could rewrite the terms—if they paid as much as the verse demanded. Not a life for a life. Something worth exactly as much as dying. Something the true text would weigh and find equivalent.

Bram thought of the ledgers downstairs. Thought of what they had traded away already, these past weeks, in small unrecorded installments—sleep, safety, the easy assumption that tomorrow resembles today. Thought of what a person *is*, when you subtract everything.

They did not tell the guild that night. They told themselves it was because the margin note might be a trap—the Remnant had forged prophecy before; the codex had passed through enemy hands. They told themselves it needed testing first, alone, with Maren, before it was offered to five people who would try to shoulder it themselves.

Both things were true. And beneath both, quieter and truer than either: Bram Brightwater, keeper of accounts, had finally found an entry they could not bear to post publicly, and so, for the first time in three years of honest numbers, they kept it off the books.

Outside, the frozen sea gleamed under a fat white moon, holding its breath.

Tomorrow was midwinter eve.
