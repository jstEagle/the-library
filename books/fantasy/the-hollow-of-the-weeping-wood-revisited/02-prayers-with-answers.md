## 2. Prayers With Answers

The road to the Weeping Wood was a road nobody took.

Casper had walked it for an hour past the last of the tanneries, where the city gave up pretending to be beautiful and became mud and smoke and the carcasses of old barges rotting along the canal. Beyond that, the cobbles simply stopped, as if Vhalen itself had drawn a line and said *this far and no farther*. Past the line there was only packed earth, frost-silvered in the early light, running straight toward a wall of trees so dark they looked less like a forest than like a hole cut into the morning.

She kept replaying the night before.

* * *

It had been near midnight when she left the guildhall, ledger-clutch tight under her arm, Corvane's warning still ringing in her ears like a struck bell that refused to finish dying. *Some pages are burned for a reason, Vasquez. Curiosity is how scribes become entries.* She had meant to go home. She had meant to sleep, or at least lie awake in the honest dark of her own room instead of the dishonest dark of the archive.

But the market district had been on her way, and the market district at midnight was a different country — awnings lashed down, braziers banked to coals, the fishmongers' stalls smelling of salt and ice. And huddled in the lee of the shuttered chandler's shop, wrapped in a coat that had been gray once and was now the color of surrender, there had been an old man she knew by sight if not by name. Everyone knew him. He sat at the mouth of Tanner's Row every day with his palm out and his eyes down, and every day the city walked past him like water around a stone.

He was praying.

Not loudly. That was what had stopped her — the *quietness* of it. Most beggars in Vhalen had learned the arithmetic of prayer: you made it audible enough that the pious felt their virtue itch, but not so loud it became a demand. This man had abandoned even that. His lips barely moved. His hands were folded in his lap like a finished account, and he was whispering to no one, to the cold air, to whatever listened when nothing did:

*"Just the girl. Just let my girl come home from the coast roads. I don't need coin. I don't need warmth. Just let the coach come through the pass tonight, just this once, just her."*

Casper had heard ten thousand prayers in her years among the ledgers. The Guild recorded them all — that was half its business, the tithe-rolls and votive-tallies, prayers logged like debts because that was precisely what they were. She knew their grammar. She knew that prayers were wishes wearing humility as a disguise.

This one was different. There was no disguise in it at all. It was naked as a bone.

She had stood in the shadow of the chandler's awning, telling herself she was only resting her feet, and watched the old man finish. He crossed himself the old way — wrong way, the way the pre-Guild faithful had done it, thumb to brow and then to breast, a gesture the liturgy manuals called obsolete — and then he settled back against the wall and closed his eyes, and Casper had thought, with the flat professional pity she wore like armor: *there is no coach coming through the pass. The pass has been snowed shut for nine days. Everyone knows it.*

Everyone knew it. Which was why, when the bells of Saint Ossuin's — the dead god's own church, deconsecrated two generations ago, its bell tower now used for fire alarms — began to ring at half past three in the morning, she was still standing there, unable to make herself leave.

She was not the only one who woke. Doors opened up and down Tanner's Row. Lamps bloomed behind shutters. And through the crowd that gathered, stumbling and swearing and wrapping blankets over nightclothes, came a girl of perhaps nineteen, mud to her knees, frost in her hair, leading a mule loaded with winter fish, weeping without any idea she was doing it.

*"The pass opened,"* Casper heard her say, over and over, to anyone who would listen. *"The pass just — opened. The snow was twenty feet deep and then it wasn't. It was like walking through a doorway. There was a smell, like—"*

*"Like what?" someone asked.

*"Like sap,"* the girl said. *"Like the trees were bleeding. Like something sweet and green and alive had decided, just for me, to be warm."*

The old beggar did not cheer. He did not weep, either, though his face did something complicated and terrible, the face of a man whose whole theology had been *nothing listens* and who had just been proven wrong in front of God and everyone. He simply looked up — past the crowd, past the rooftops, toward the black line of the Weeping Wood at the city's edge — and nodded once, the way a debtor nods at a creditor.

Casper had checked the Guild's records in her head as she stood there, the way other people said calming prayers. No licensed intercession had been filed. No votive-debt discharged. No miracle-license stamped, sealed, entered, taxed. The pass had opened for one girl because one old man had asked, and there was no receipt.

In Vhalen, everything had a receipt.

* * *

Now she stood at the tree line, and the trees stood over her, and neither of them moved.

Up close, the Weeping Wood was worse than it was from the city walls. From a distance it was merely dark. Up close it was *wet*. Every trunk glistened, beaded with resin that seeped from a thousand small wounds in the bark and ran down the grooves in slow, unbroken threads — not amber, exactly, but close, catching the dawn light and holding it the way an eye holds light. The ground drank it. The air tasted of it, sweet and green and faintly funereal, like flowers arranged for a funeral that kept being postponed.

And the shrines were glowing.

She counted four of them in the first hundred yards, little stone alcoves set into the roots of the great trees, each one older than the Guild, older than the city wall, their carved gods worn nearly featureless by centuries of rain. In the liturgy manuals these were listed as *dormant sites*, historical curiosities, safe to visit, safe to ignore. They were not dormant. Each alcove exhaled a warmth she could feel from six feet away, a hearth-warmth, a living-room warmth, and inside each one the worn stone face of some forgotten god seemed — she made herself look, made herself be precise about it — *relaxed*. As if it had been holding its breath for forty years and had finally, recently, let it out.

Casper took out her notebook. Her hand was steady. She noted that her hand was steady, which was either professionalism or shock, and wrote:

*Site 1. Shrine active. No license. No filing. No tax stamp. Sap flow increased beyond all survey baselines. Ambient temperature elevated 4 degrees against season.*

Then, after a moment, in smaller letters:

*I am afraid.*

She crossed it out. Then she didn't. Some entries deserved to stand.

"You're writing it down."

The voice came from above and behind, and Casper spun, pen up like a knife, which was the most dangerous thing she owned and therefore, in her hand, genuinely a weapon.

A woman sat in the fork of a root as thick as a rowboat, eight feet off the ground, watching her with an expression of mild professional interest. She was perhaps fifty, weathered brown, dressed in the travel-stained remnants of vestments — Casper recognized the cut immediately, the high collar and the sunburst hem of the Order of the Quiet Flame, the priestess caste that the Guild had absorbed, defanged, and pensioned off a generation ago. Around the woman's neck hung a chain with nothing on it. The absence was louder than an amulet would have been.

"Writing it down," the woman repeated. "Recording. Cataloguing. You're Guild."

"I'm a scribe," Casper said. "Who are you?"

"Sefa." The priestess dropped from the root with more grace than her knees should have permitted. "Formerly of the Quiet Flame. Currently of nowhere, which is a parish with excellent views and terrible pay." She nodded at the nearest glowing shrine. "You've been counting the shrines. How many have you found warm?"

"Four."

"There are eleven." Sefa's eyes were pale gray, the color of ash after the fire's gone through. "There were eleven forty years ago, too, on the night they went cold. I've spent four days walking this wood confirming that they've woken again, and I'll tell you what I told myself on the first night, when I thought I was dreaming: write it down. Because when the Guild comes — and they will come, they always come — someone will need to have written it down."

"The Guild already knows," Casper said. "Or part of it does. There are pages missing from the debt-ledgers. Entries burned. My master—"

"Your master." Something moved behind Sefa's gray eyes, too quick to name. "Corvane."

Casper went very still. "I didn't say his name."

"No," Sefa agreed softly. "You didn't." She turned and walked deeper among the roots, and after a heartbeat's argument with herself that she lost, Casper followed.

The priestess's camp was tucked into the hollow between two colossal trunks — a lean tarpaulin, a cold fire ring, a bedroll, and, sitting cross-legged on the bedroll with the absolute gravity of a small emperor, a child.

The child was maybe seven years old, thin, dark-eyed, with hair cropped short in the practical style of an orphanage or a fever ward. She was drawing in the dirt with a stick. Casper glanced down at the drawing and felt the ground tilt gently beneath her professional feet.

It was a ledger page. Columns, rules, figures — crude, childlike, but unmistakably a ledger page. And in the margin, rendered in careful stick-strokes, a small burning page with a small stick-figure scribbler beside it.

"That's Wren," Sefa said quietly. "Don't stare. She hates being stared at. Don't lie to her, either. She can tell."

"Can she."

"Wren," Sefa said, "tell the scribe what you drew yesterday."

The child didn't look up. "A lady burning a paper in a tall room. She had ink on her fingers. She was scared of the paper." Wren's stick paused. "She's scared of lots of papers."

Casper's mouth had gone dry. "Yesterday."

"Mm." Sefa lowered herself onto a root, folding her hands, and for a long moment she simply studied Casper with the weary tenderness of a woman triaging a wound she'd seen before. "Forty years ago, the gods of this wood went silent. Not dead — the Guild's own founding charters call them *sequestered*, which is a lovely accounting word for *imprisoned*. The covenant that bound them was written in blood and memory, and the men who wrote it needed a vault that couldn't be robbed and a seal that couldn't be forged. So they wrote it somewhere no thief would ever think to look."

"Where," Casper said. It was not quite a question.

"In people," Sefa said. "In heads. In the one archive that burns when you do." Her gaze dropped, briefly, to Casper's ink-stained fingers, and Casper felt the look land somewhere below her ribs, in a hollow she had never been able to name — the ache she'd carried all her life, the sense of a room in herself with the furniture missing.

"The seal is failing," Sefa went on. "The shrines are waking. The prayers are being answered. And the child who anchors the covenant — the living key, the last knot in the binding — sleeps thirty feet from your campfire and draws tomorrow's pages in the dirt." She leaned forward. "So, scribe. You came here following a miracle. Tell me: when you found the burned page in your master's ledger, whose handwriting was in it?"

The wind moved through the Weeping Wood, and a thousand threads of sap shivered on a thousand wounded trunks, and somewhere overhead a branch groaned like a ship changing course.

"I don't know," Casper whispered.

"Liar," said Wren, without looking up, and drew another column.
