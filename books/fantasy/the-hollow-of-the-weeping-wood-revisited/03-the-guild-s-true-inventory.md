## 3. The Guild's True Inventory

The vault beneath the Ledger Guild of Vhalen had no door that locks could describe.

Casper had copied the guild's inventories for eleven years, and in all that time the sub-basement appeared on no ledger, no manifest, no tax roll. It existed the way debts existed — officially nowhere, practically everywhere. She knew of it only because Corvane kept its key on a cord around his neck, and because once, when she was nineteen and feverish, he had murmured in his sleep at his desk: *not below, never below*, and she had pretended not to hear, the way a scribe pretends not to read what she copies.

She came at the turn of the third watch, when the guild slept like a thing fed.

The stairwell behind the ink stores went down farther than the building stood tall. That was the first impossibility. The second was the cold — not winter-cold but *ledger*-cold, the dry chill of paper that has been kept too long by someone who loves it. Her lantern threw her shadow up the stairs ahead of her like a warning she chose to walk past.

At the bottom, iron shelving ran into dark on both sides. And between the shelves, row upon row, sat crates.

Not coin crates. Not deed boxes. Crates of glass.

She lifted the lid of the nearest one and her breath left her the way breath leaves when a name is called from a crowd you thought empty.

Jars. Dozens of them, packed in straw, each one small enough to hold a fist, each one glowing with a faint amber light that moved — that *swirled* — like something alive and dreaming. Each jar wore a paper label, written in the precise hand of a guild archivist:

*MEMORIA DEBITORUM — HALE, MARGIT. 3 UNITS. PAID IN FULL.*

*MEMORIA DEBITORUM — OKONWE, TAM. 1 UNIT. PARTIAL.*

*MEMORIA DEBITORUM — BREN, ILSE. 12 UNITS. DEFAULTED.*

Memories. Bottled memories, inventoried like tallow, taxed like salt. Casper stood very still while the vault's silence pressed against her ears, and she understood, with the terrible clarity of a woman reading her own death warrant in beautiful calligraphy, what the Ledger Guild actually traded in.

The old gods did not want gold. Gold meant nothing to things that predated mining. They wanted *lives* — not the years themselves, necessarily, but the living of them. The taste of them. The proof that a person had been someone. And the guild, in its wisdom, had found a way to bottle the proof.

She moved down the shelves because moving was better than thinking. Hale. Okonwe. Bren. Names she knew — Okonwe had been a dockmaster who vanished from the rolls six years ago; everyone said he'd gone south. Bren, Ilse — there was a memorial tile for her in the Lantern District, paid for by *grateful neighbors*. Grateful neighbors. Paid in full.

Then a crate near the back wall, older than the others, its wood gone gray, its label faded almost to nothing.

*V.*

Her hands were shaking so badly she nearly dropped the lantern. The lid came away and there they were: three jars, nested in rotted straw, glowing a warmer color than the rest — honey-gold shot through with threads of green, like sunlight through leaves.

VASQUEZ, ADELA. VASQUEZ, ADELA. VASQUEZ, ADELA.

Three units. Her mother's name. But the handwriting on the labels —

She knew that hand. Of course she knew it. She saw it every morning in the mirror of her own ledgers, in the slight forward lean of her letters, the way her *a*'s closed too tight. She had written those labels. She had labeled jars of her own mother's memory with hands that did not remember doing it, in years that had been cut out of her like pages razored from a book.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to the vault, to whatever was listening. "No, no —"

The first jar was warm through the glass. When she raised it to the lantern light, something inside turned toward her — not liquid, not smoke, but a shape of attention, the way a sleeping dog turns its head at a familiar footstep. And for half a heartbeat, less, she smelled rain on hot stone and heard a woman laughing and felt, impossibly, *held* — small arms around a neck that was not hers anymore, a voice saying words in a language the guild had no alphabet for —

The memory touched hers and recoiled, the way a hand recoils from a stove.

Because there was nothing in her to receive it. Where the matching piece should have lived, she was smooth. Hollow. Sealed over like healed skin over a wound that should have killed her.

She sat down hard on the floor among the crates, the jar clutched to her chest, and the vertigo took her properly then. Eleven years. She had eleven years of herself, maybe twelve — fragments before, fog after — and she had always assumed grief was simply what childhood felt like from the far side. Everyone's early years thinned. Everyone's mother blurred.

But mothers did not blur into *nothing*. Mothers did not leave a daughter with a hole shaped exactly like a life.

And the entries in the debt-ledger, in her own handwriting, from years she couldn't account for — she had written those sitting right here, or somewhere like here, labeling pieces of her family as inventory. Someone had made her do it. Someone had made her file away her own self and then locked the drawer.

"Corvane," she whispered, and the name tasted like ash and interest accrued.

* * *

Flashback fragment — unmoored, undated, arriving the way returned property arrives:

*A kitchen with copper pots. A woman singing off-key, flour on her dark forearms. "Adela, mi vida, if you spill that again I will write it in your ledger." Laughter. Outside, the sound of the Weeping Wood — that strange soft weeping the sap makes in summer — and the woman going quiet, going to the window, her hand flat against the glass. "They're listening tonight, little one. Be careful what you ask for."*

*A knock. Men in gray with ledger-chains at their belts. The mother's face changing — not fear exactly. Calculation. Bargaining-face. The face of a woman about to spend something expensive.*

*"How much?" Adela's mother asked. And a gray man answered, "Everything she'll owe us. All of it, signed now, collected later." And small hands — her own small hands — being pricked with a stylus, a drop of blood-ink on parchment, and the smell of hot wax, and her mother whispering, "Forgive me, this buys you *time*," though time for what, time for whom —*

The fragment ended where all her fragments ended: at a wall of sealed-over nothing.

* * *

"You should have taken the stairs more quietly."

Casper did not scream. Later she would be proud of that. She turned, jar still against her chest, and found Corvane standing at the foot of the stairs in his nightclothes and boots, the vault key hanging bare against his throat, his face lit from below by her lantern into something older than his fifty-some years.

"How long," she said. Her voice came out level. That surprised her most. "How long have I been in these crates?"

"Sit down."

"I'm sitting."

He almost smiled. Then he saw which crate was open, and which jar she held, and the smile died before it finished being born.

"That one isn't yours," he said quietly.

"It has my mother's name on it. In my handwriting."

"Yes." He came closer, slowly, the way men approach wounded animals or lit fuses. "It does."

"Tell me."

"Casper—"

"*Tell me.*" The jar flared green at her breastbone and neither of them remarked on it.

Corvane lowered himself onto an upturned crate across from her, and for a long moment the only sound was the faint, patient swirl of bottled lives. When he spoke, he spoke like a man reading a verdict he'd memorized decades ago.

"Your mother came to us forty-one years ago. No — thirty-nine. You were four." He rubbed his eyes. "There was a bargain available, in those days, for people who knew to ask. A large withdrawal against a large collateral. She needed the city to survive something. You don't need to know what. What you need to know is the terms: the guild would extend the loan, and the gods would wait, and the collateral securing it would be—" he gestured, helpless, at the jar in her arms, "—her daughter. Collected in installments. As required."

"As required," Casper repeated.

"The covenant is fraying. It has been fraying since before you were born. Every year the seal weakens, and every year the guild must feed it something to keep the old ones sleeping. Memories are... acceptable fare. Painless, mostly. Renewable." He looked at the shelf of names. "We tell ourselves renewable."

"And mine?"

"Yours were the reserve." His voice dropped. "Collateral of last resort. Your mother's bargain bought Vhalen twenty extra years, Casper. The whole city. Every market bell, every wedding, every child born under our walls — purchased on the security of your childhood. We've drawn against you eleven times. Tonight would have been the twelfth."

The word *twelve* landed in her like a nail. Twelve withdrawals. Three jars visible. How many more, elsewhere, unlabeled? How much of her was already spent, and on what, and did any of it matter — did the beggars in the Lantern District know they walked through streets bought with her fourth summer?

"I didn't consent," she said.

"You were four."

"I mean *now*. The drawings. The labels. My own hand, Corvane. You made me archive myself."

"We made you *willing*," he said, and had the decency to look ashamed of it. "A debtor who fights the collection bleeds the product. Memory resists extraction from an unwilling mind — it curdles, spoils, comes back wrong. So we took the resistance out first. Small adjustments. You've never questioned a missing evening. Check your ledgers. There are gaps you've explained to yourself beautifully."

The vertigo returned, worse. Every quiet night she'd attributed to exhaustion. Every morning she'd woken with ink on her fingers and no memory of writing. She had been a good employee. She had been a *compliant asset*.

"So arrest me," she said. "Or whatever you do to scribes who open crates."

Instead, Corvane reached into his nightshirt and drew out a second key — small, brass, unremarkable, the kind that opens a clerk's desk drawer. He set it on the floor between them and slid it across with two fingers.

"The east gate key. And a letter of manumission, unsigned, in my desk — this opens that desk. Take the letter, take the jar if you must, and be on a barge before dawn. Go south. Never touch a ledger again." He stood, joints cracking, suddenly looking every year of a man who had fed a god with other people's Tuesdays. "In exchange: silence. You saw an old cellar and dusty bottles. Nothing more."

"Why?" It came out cracked. "Why would you give me anything?"

"Because the twelfth drawing is scheduled for next week," he said, "and I have signed eleven orders in my life, and I find I cannot sign the one that takes the rest of you. Because I am old and tired and a debtor like everyone else in this room." He paused at the stair. "And because your mother made me promise something, the night she signed. She said: *when she finds out, let her choose.*"

He climbed three steps, then stopped, not turning around.

"One more thing, Casper. The Wood began weeping again Tuesday. Real tears. The shrines are warm. Whatever your mother bought with you — the twenty years — I think they're spent." He looked back, finally, and his eyes were wet. "I think the collection has come due, and it isn't the guild coming to collect."

Then he was gone, and Casper Vasquez sat alone in the dark among the bottled lives of Vhalen, holding a piece of her mother in one hand and a stranger's mercy in the other, knowing exactly where she had to go — because if the gods were waking, then somewhere at the edge of the city, in the hollow beneath the Weeping Wood, someone had to be asking why.

She pocketed the brass key, tucked the jar marked *VASQUEZ* inside her coat, and went up the stairs toward the door that led out of the guild — and toward the trees that had started, impossibly, to answer prayers.
