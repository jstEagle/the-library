## 4. A Child Who Remembers Everything

Sefa woke before dawn to the sound of Wren talking to the fire.

Not *to* the fire — that was a thing children did, chattering at coals as if the flames listened. Wren's conversations were different. The fire answered.

She lay in the hollow of the great root where they'd made their camp for three nights now, watching the small figure crouched at the ember-pit, her breath fogging in the cold. The Weeping Wood rose around them like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and from every branch the sap seeped slow and silver, catching the last starlight. The old texts called it *the grief of the gods made visible*. Sefa had spent eleven years as a priestess of the Concordant Flame and had never once seen the Wood weep. Now she could not make it stop.

"Tell it no," Wren was saying, in the small, reasonable voice she used for negotiations no child should have to conduct. "You can't have my dream. It's mine. I dreamed it."

The embers flared, sulked, dimmed.

"Good," said Wren, satisfied, and poked the fire with a stick like any ordinary orphan in any ordinary city, and Sefa's chest ached with a tenderness so sharp it was nearly fear.

She rose quietly and went to sit beside her. The cold came up through the ground the way it always did here, as though the earth itself were trying to remember what warmth was. Sefa fed the fire a knot of dry pine and said, "What did it want?"

Wren glanced up. Her eyes were the color of rainwater over river stones — pale, unsettling, old. "My dream. I dreamed about my mother again, and it said if I gave it the dream it would make her real. Or find her. It wasn't clear. They're never clear." She frowned into the flames. "That's how you know they're gods and not people. People are clear when they want something."

Sefa had been trained in doctrine for eleven years, had recited the Litanies of Separation until her voice went hoarse, had believed — had *needed* to believe — that the old gods were gone, sealed beyond the veil by the covenant, their hunger locked away from the soft world of mortals. The Concordant Flame taught that the sealing had been mercy. She had never questioned it until the night the Flame refused her kindling and a voice like a landslide had said, *we remember you, little wick, and we remember what your sisters took.*

"They can't make your mother real," Sefa said. "They bargain in what they don't have. That's the oldest rule there is."

"I know." Wren's chin lifted. "But it keeps asking. It's been asking since the market. Everything's been asking since the market." She turned those rainwater eyes on Sefa, and the question in them was worse than any accusation. "Did I do something wrong? Before, they never talked to me. Now they never stop."

Sefa had no answer that wasn't a lie, and she had sworn — after the Flame, after her disgrace, after the long gray year of walking — never to lie to this child. So she said, "I think something broke, little bird. And I think the crack runs through you."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she leaned her head against Sefa's shoulder, and they watched the fire behave itself, mostly, and the Wood wept its silver tears around them, and Sefa did not sleep again.

* * *

The woman came back at midmorning.

Sefa heard her before she saw her — the particular tread of someone who walked cities for a living, boots finding the cobbles and then the root-paths with a bookkeeper's precision. She'd seen her yesterday at the Wood's edge, a scribe in Ledger Guild gray with ink stains to the second knuckle, standing at the boundary stones with an expression Sefa recognized from the inside: the look of a person whose world had developed a crack and who could not stop pressing her thumb against it.

"You came back," Sefa said, not rising from her seat among the roots. Wren was off gathering fallen shrine-offerings — she did that, tidying the abandoned altars of the old gods with the fierce domesticity of a child arranging a dollhouse — and Sefa was glad of it. "I told you the Wood wasn't safe."

"You told me a lot of things." The scribe stopped at the edge of the shrine-clearing. Casper Vasquez — Sefa had read the name off her guild badge yesterday, along with her indenture-seal, which was the sort of detail a disgraced priestess learned to read the way sailors read weather. "None of them explained how a beggar's prayer for bread was answered four hours after he whispered it. I checked. The baker's boy swears the loaves appeared in the cooling rack between one bell and the next. There's no ledger entry. There's *no explanation*."

"And that frightens you."

"It *offends* me," Casper said, and then, as if hearing herself, she flushed. "Everything in Vhalen has a ledger. Every debt, every credit, every miracle anyone's ever sold. If something happened, it was *paid for*. That's how the world works. That's how it's worked for forty years."

"Forty years," Sefa repeated softly. "You say that like you were there."

Something crossed the scribe's face — quick, involuntary, like a shadow of a bird over water. "I'm twenty-six."

"I know." Sefa rose, brushing bark from her robe. Up close the woman looked worse than yesterday — sleepless, hollowed, her eyes rimmed red. But it was more than exhaustion. Sefa had spent her priesthood tending the grief of others; she knew the geography of a wound when she saw one. This was not grief. This was absence. "You walk like a woman missing something she can't name. You flinch when people mention their mothers. You counted the shrine-offerings yesterday — I watched you — but you counted them in *pairs*, like you were balancing columns no one asked you to balance. And when Wren spoke to you, you looked at her the way a starving woman looks through a baker's window."

Casper went very still. "You don't know anything about me."

"No," Sefa agreed. "But I know the shape of the hole. I've seen it before." She hesitated. Eleven years of doctrine said *say nothing*. Eleven years of disgrace said *what do you have left to lose?* "I've seen it in a mirror."

The wind moved through the Wood, and the sap wept faster, silver threads sliding down the black bark. Somewhere deeper in the trees, one of the old shrines — a bowl of cracked granite older than the city — began, very faintly, to glow.

Casper stared at it. "That wasn't doing that yesterday."

"Things here do more every day." Sefa wrapped her arms against a cold that had nothing to do with weather. "You want your explanation, scribe? Sit down. It's long, and it's ugly, and it ends with the world ending — slowly, the way a debt ends a debtor."

* * *

She told her most of it, there among the roots, while Wren wove crowns out of shrine-flowers and pretended not to listen, which meant she was listening to every word.

"Forty years ago," Sefa said, "there was a covenant. The oldest one. The gods of the first world — the hungry ones, the ones your Guild pretends never existed — were sealed beyond the veil. Not killed. *Sealed*. And the seal wasn't stone or sword. It was an accounting. Every prayer unanswered, every miracle withheld, every debt the gods were owed — all of it was written into a single binding, a ledger of refusal, and the ledger was anchored to a living soul. A person. Someone who carried the covenant in their own flesh, so it could never be burned or stolen or lost."

Casper's voice came out strange. "What happened to it?"

"It broke." Sefa watched the woman's hands, which had gone white-knuckled in her lap. "Forty years ago, almost to the season. The records of my order call it the Unbinding, and they don't say how or why, because the ones who knew died or were silenced. But I'll tell you what I know that they don't." She leaned forward. "The seal didn't shatter. Ledgers don't shatter. They get *unbalanced*. Someone removed something from it — or something was taken — and the covenant has been running on a deficit ever since. For forty years the gods have been pressing against a door held shut by a lock with a piece missing. And now the door is opening. Not all at once. A prayer here. A miracle there. The Wood weeping. The shrines warming." She gestured at the glowing granite bowl. "The gods aren't returning, scribe. They're *collecting*."

"And the child?" Casper's eyes went to Wren, who was placing a flower crown on the head of a moss-covered god-statue with tremendous solemnity. "You said the crack runs through her."

"Wren is what my order would have called a *resonant*. A soul born in the shape of the covenant — the way a bell is shaped to a note. When the seal was whole, she'd have lived and died an ordinary orphan with an odd talent for getting what she asked for. Now?" Sefa's throat tightened. "Now she's the clearest window the gods have into this world. They speak *through* her shape. Every prayer she prays lands like a hammer on the broken part of the lock. She's not doing anything wrong. She's simply *louder* than the rest of us. And every day she gets louder, and every day the door opens a little wider, and one day — soon, if no one mends it — it won't be a door anymore. It'll be a wound."

Casper was silent for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had the flatness of someone reading a figure she didn't want to be true. "The Guild. The Ledger Guild of Vhalen. They keep the debts. If the covenant was a ledger—"

"Then your masters have been keeping the lock on the door of the world," Sefa said, "and they've known for forty years that it's broken."

The scribe stood up so fast she swayed. "No. No — I've audited their books. I *am* their books. There's nothing — there are debts, ordinary debts, coin and contract—"

"And impossible things," Sefa said quietly. "You've seen the entries. Don't lie to me, Casper Vasquez. I've watched you press your thumb against the crack in your own world for two days now. What did you find in your ledgers that you can't explain?"

The woman's mouth opened. Closed. Sefa watched her decide — watched the exact moment the bookkeeper's instinct to conceal lost to the starving look through the baker's window.

"Entries," Casper whispered. "In my own handwriting. From years I don't remember. Three years of my life, gone, and my master burned the page when I asked, and there are crates in the vault, I've seen them, crates of—"

She stopped herself, shaking. Sefa rose and, before she could think better of it, took the woman's ink-stained hands in her own. They were cold. They were shaking. They were the hands of someone who had been carrying a weight alone for a very long time, and Sefa knew that weight, knew it down to the grain, and hated that she recognized it.

"You feel hollow," Sefa said. "Like something was scooped out of you and the edges healed over wrong. I know. I have the same hollow. Mine is where my faith used to be. Yours—" she hesitated, then said it, because the woman deserved the truth even if it cut, "—yours might be where the missing piece of the covenant used to live."

Casper stared at her. The wind rose through the Wood, and every tree wept at once, silver and ceaseless, and far off in the deep hollow beneath the roots something vast turned over in its sleep and murmured a name.

Not Wren's name.

Casper's.
