## 5. What Corvane Owes

I did not knock on Corvane's door. I had learned that much from the ledgers: debts were always collected in person, and always unannounced.

The rain had followed me from the Wood, thin and needling, and it had soaked through my coat by the time I climbed the guild stairs to the master's study. Below me, the counting floor hummed with its evening arithmetic — quills scratching, coins singing against brass trays, a hundred clerks pretending the world was only numbers. I had been one of them for eleven years. I had been one of them for exactly as long as I had been anyone at all.

Corvane's door was unlocked. That was the first wrong thing.

He sat at his desk with the lamp turned low, and before him, laid out with terrible neatness, were three glass jars. My name was etched on each one. *VASQUEZ,* in the guild's formal hand, and beneath it, in smaller script, dates I did not remember living through.

"You went into the vault," he said. Not a question. He didn't look up. "I told the door-wardens to admit you. I've been waiting two days for you to find the courage."

"Or the desperation."

"Those are the same thing, in my experience." He gestured to the chair across from him. When I didn't move, he said, "Sit, Casper. You'll want to be sitting."

I sat. The jars caught the lamplight and threw it back warped, the way light moves through water. Up close I could see that they weren't empty. Something pale drifted in each one — not liquid, not smoke. Shapes. A girl's hand holding a wooden horse. A doorway painted blue. A woman's laugh, if laughter could be bottled, and apparently it could, because I knew that laugh. I knew it the way you know a word in a language you no longer speak.

"Tell me what I'm looking at," I said.

"Inventory." He folded his hands. Corvane had always had still hands; I'd admired them once, the way an apprentice admires anything done well. "You audited the debt-ledgers. You found the entries in your own hand. So you already know the guild's books don't balance. What you don't know — what almost no one alive knows — is *what* we owe, and *to whom*, and how long we've been paying."

"Corvane—"

"Twenty years ago," he said, "this city should have died."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something, and the sound came up through the floor like a voice from underwater.

"You've heard the story they tell in taverns. The plague year. The famine that followed. The winter when the granaries emptied and the river froze to its bed." He turned one of the jars slowly with one finger. "What the taverns don't tell you is that the plague was not a plague. The famine was not a famine. They were *foreclosures*, Casper. The old gods held the note on this city — on every city — and forty years ago the covenant that kept the accounts in order was broken. When the seal failed, the debts came due. All of them. At once."

"And the guild—"

"The Ledger Guild was never a merchant house. We were the covenant's clerks. The middlemen between the realms and the things that dreamed them. When the covenant broke, we did what clerks do. We negotiated." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and was mostly grief. "The gods were dying, you understand. Starving. A god with no worshippers is a fire with no wood, and they had been sealed away from their faithful for centuries. But they could still *hold*. And what a dying god wants, more than prayer, more than sacrifice, is to remember what it was to be believed in."

The shape in the middle jar turned over. A blue door. I knew that door. I had never seen it, and I knew it.

"Memories," I said. My voice came out flat. "You sold them memories."

"We *sold* memories, yes. Bought from the desperate, harvested from the condemned, and — in certain cases — taken as collateral." He looked up at me then, and his eyes were the same tired grey they had always been, but they were not the eyes of my master anymore. They were the eyes of a man reading a bill he could never pay. "Your mother came to us twenty years ago, Casper. She was not desperate. She was *precise*. She had learned — I never learned how — that the gods would accept a structured payment. A lien against the future. She offered them the childhood of her daughter: every memory from birth to her seventh year, held in trust, redeemable at maturity. And in exchange, the gods would stay their hand on Vhalen for twenty years. Twenty years of harvests. Twenty years of children who didn't starve."

The room tilted. I gripped the arms of the chair.

"Twenty years," I said. "That was—"

"Last autumn," Corvane said quietly. "The note came due last autumn. And your mother, may she rest wherever she is, structured a second instrument before she died. A renewal. Secured against the same collateral — the same three jars — but with a new term. Which is why the Wood has only begun answering prayers *now*, and why the seal is failing *now*, and why you found your own handwriting in ledgers covering years you cannot remember. You were the guarantor of your own debt, Casper. Every entry in those books that isn't in your living memory is in your *dead* one. In the jars."

I looked at the jars. The girl with the wooden horse. The blue door. The laugh.

"Is she in there?" I asked. "My mother? What she looked like? What her voice—"

"First jar," he said. "Everything up to your fourth year. The other two run to your seventh."

I had spent eleven years not missing something I could not name. I had told myself the hollowness was grief for parents I barely recalled, or ambition with nowhere to go, or simply the shape of a life that had never quite fit. And all that time, the missing thing had been *labeled*. Filed. Shelved in a vault beneath my feet, accruing interest.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "You burned the ledger page. You warned me off. Why open the vault door now?"

"Because the renewal is failing too." He rose and went to the window, and the rain wrote its grey arithmetic down the glass. "The gods took the collateral and the term both, and they are still starving, and the seal is still breaking, and there is nothing left in this guild's coffers to buy another twenty years. The Wood is answering prayers, Casper. Do you understand what that means? It means they have stopped *waiting*. It means the foreclosure has begun. When it completes, Vhalen goes the way of the plague year — but permanently. Every debt collected at once. Every ledger in this city closed by a hand that doesn't read."

"Then what do we do?"

"*We* do nothing." He came back to the desk and did something I had never seen him do. He unlocked a drawer, took out a key, and set it on the wood between us. It was black iron, old, and it was warm — I could feel the warmth of it from where I sat, like a hand held near a hearth. "That is the ledger key. The master key to the covenant accounts. It has not left this drawer in forty years. With it, a person could enter the hollow beneath the Wood. Could reach the covenant-stone. Could — if the old texts are to be believed, and I have spent forty years praying they are not — *reforge* the pact."

"Then give it to the Guildmaster. Give it to the—"

"The Guildmaster is dead. Has been for a decade. I have been signing his name." He said it without drama, the way he signed everything. "There is no *we*, Casper. There is a disgraced priestess camped in the Wood, a child the gods answer too readily, and a scribe who is the only living soul whose name appears on both sides of the covenant. You are the debtor and the collateral and, if the texts are right, the only hand that can rewrite the terms." He pushed the key toward me. "Take it. Take the road east out of the city tonight, and keep walking, and don't stop until Vhalen is a story you heard from someone else. The gods want their payment, and you are the last coin in the vault. If you're not here to be collected, perhaps the debt dies with the debtor."

"And the city?"

"The city has had twenty years it should never have had. That is not nothing. That is *everything*, to the people who lived them." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and I understood — finally, fully — that Corvane was not my enemy. He had never been my enemy. He was a clerk who had spent his whole life making payments on a debt he hadn't incurred, with currency he hadn't stolen, and he was tired down to the marrow. "I am asking you to live, Casper. It is the only selfish request I have made in forty years of service."

I picked up the key. It was warm as a living thing.

"You said my mother was precise," I said.

"Yes."

"Then she didn't buy twenty years just to have me run from the collection." I stood, and the jars caught the lamplight, and the girl with the wooden horse seemed to turn her head. "You taught me that a debt is a promise with a date on it. Well. The date is here."

"Listen to me—" He reached across the desk, and I stepped back, and something in his face broke open, all the forty years of it at once. "You don't know what's down there. The last god who keeps the accounts — Ossuin — he will offer you *everything you lost*. He will make the offer gently. He will make it *kindly*. And you will want it more than you have ever wanted anything, because I have seen what a person looks like carrying a hollow that size." He pressed his palms flat to the desk. "I am begging you. As your master. As the man who has signed your mother's name on renewal papers every year since she died, and hated every signature. *Run.*"

"I'm a scribe," I said. "I don't run. I *amend*."

I put the key in my pocket, and I took one of the jars — the first one, the one with my mother's laugh sealed in glass — and I carried it to the door.

"Casper."

I stopped. The rain was louder out here, against the corridor's high windows.

"The vault," he said behind me. "There's a fourth jar. Unlabeled. I never had the courage to open it, and I never had the courage to destroy it. If you're going to do this — whatever this is — you should know what else she left you."

I looked back at him. He was an old man at a desk with three jars and a confession, and for the first time in eleven years I saw him exactly as he was: another entry in a ledger that had never once balanced.

"Tell me where," I said.

* * *

The sub-vault was colder than the main one, and older, and the dark down there felt less like an absence of light than like a presence of patience. My lamp found the shelf on the third try. The fourth jar sat where forty years of dust said it had never been moved.

There was no label. But when I lifted it, the glass was warm, and inside, drifting like a held breath, I saw not a memory but a *message* — my mother's handwriting, suspended in the pale, written on nothing, addressed to no one and therefore to me:

*If you are reading this, the term has ended and you have chosen not to run. Then hear the only thing I could not put in any ledger: the covenant cannot be reforged with a whole soul. It must be given freely, in whole or in part — and the gods will tell you the parts they want. Do not believe them. They have never once told the whole price. Find the priestess. Trust the child. And whatever the god who keeps the receipts offers you —*

The rest was blurred, dissolved into the pale like ink in rain, and no amount of tilting the jar brought it back.

*Whatever he offers you,* I finished silently, *don't.*

I tucked the jar under my arm beside the first one, and I climbed out of the dark with two pieces of my mother and a black iron key, and above me the city went on counting its coins, all of them spent, all of them owed, while eastward at the city's edge the Weeping Wood stood weeping sap in the dark — and, for the first time in forty years, *waiting for an answer.*
