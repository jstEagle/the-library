## 6. The Road Beneath the Roots

**CASPER**

The shrine-door was not a door.

That was the first thing Casper understood, standing before the great root that rose from the Weeping Wood's heart like the rib of some buried giant. It was bark, and it was wood, and it was also — when the light touched it wrong — a page. A vast, vellum-pale surface veined with grain that might have been handwriting, if handwriting had been grown rather than written.

Corvane's ledger key hung against her breastbone on a cord, warm as a held coin. She had not decided yet whether she carried it as a weapon, a ransom, or an apology.

"Tell me again why the child opens it and not the disgraced priestess," she said. "Or the scribe with the stolen key. I have credentials. I have, arguably, a criminal record now."

Sefa crouched at the root's base, arranging a ring of white river-stones with the fussy precision of a woman who had done a thousand rites and been thanked for none. "Because doors are promises, and Wren is the promise's name."

"That's not an answer. That's a hymn."

"It's both." Sefa looked up, and in the wood's green half-light her face was older than her years and younger than her grief. "The covenant was sealed through a living anchor. A person, not a stone. When it broke, the breaking lived in someone too." Her eyes flicked to Wren, then — briefly, unwillingly — to Casper. "Some doors don't open for keys. They open for kin."

Wren stood at the edge of the stone circle, small hands buried in the pockets of a coat two sizes too large, watching the root with the flat patience of a child who had learned that adults would explain themselves eventually or not at all.

"It doesn't want to be knocked on," Wren said. "It wants to be *remembered at*."

Casper's mouth went dry. "Remembered at."

"Like this." Wren stepped into the ring of stones, tipped their head back, and spoke — not loudly, not dramatically, but in the clear, unembarrassed voice of a child reciting something learned by heart.

"Once there was a girl who lived above a chandler's shop on Vesper Lane, and her mother smelled of tallow and rosemary, and on feast days she sang the second verse wrong on purpose to make the girl laugh—"

The root *shivered*.

Casper did not hear the rest, because her own heartbeat had become a drum in her ears. Vesper Lane. Tallow and rosemary. The wrong verse. She had never told anyone that. She had never *known* that — not since the guild took it, not since she'd woken at fourteen with a past smooth as a scraped page and a master telling her the fire had taken her family, take this pen, child, ink is a kinder inheritance than grief.

But Wren knew. Wren, who remembered everything, was reading her like a ledger.

Sap beaded along the root's pale surface, welling slow and thick, and where each drop fell it struck the ground and did not soak in. The ground *opened*. A seam of darkness spread beneath the stones like ink dropped in water, widening, breathing out a smell of deep earth and old rain and something sweeter underneath — candle smoke, rosemary.

The door was open. The door had been open since Wren said *rosemary*.

"Down," Sefa said simply, and went first, staff in hand, into the dark.

* * *

**SEFA**

The stairs had not been built. Sefa knew construction when she walked on it — she had spent nine years in the temple of Vhalen learning the geometry of sacred spaces before they took her vestments and her name from the rolls. These steps were *grown*, spiral and root-lace, and they descended farther than the city's foundations had any right to permit. Above them, the Weeping Wood went on weeping. Below them, something waited that had been patient for forty years, which was, for a god, barely a held breath.

Wren climbed down ahead of her, sure-footed, humming. Casper came last, and Sefa heard what the scribe probably thought she hid: the small, ragged hitch in her breathing every few steps. The sound of a person carrying something breakable and being afraid to look at it.

"You knew her mother," Sefa said, when the spiral widened into a landing. Not a question.

Wren answered anyway. "I remember her for her. That's what I'm for."

"Wren." Sefa kept her voice gentle. "That's not what you're *for*. That's just what you can do. There's a difference, and I'll thank you to remember it when the gods come asking."

The child shrugged, the shrug of someone who had been told they were a vessel so often they'd stopped hearing it. Sefa's chest ached. She had sworn, when she took Wren in — off the temple steps, out of the snow, before her disgrace was even cold — that no one would ever use this child the way the temple had used her. And here they were, walking the child down into the dark to open doors, because no one else could.

The road beneath the roots was not dark for long.

Light came up to meet them, pale and slow, and Sefa stopped on the last step because what she saw made her think of the flooded crypt beneath the old temple, where the water lay so still it held perfect reflections of things that were no longer there.

Time pooled here. There was no better word for it. The hollow's floor was shallow water that was not water, and standing in it, waist-deep and glowing faintly, were *scenes*. A kitchen. A chandler's shop. A woman's hands kneading dough, a girl of six on a stool beside her, both of them laughing at nothing the light could show. They stood in the flooded dark like figures in a reliquary, and they flickered when the ripples touched them.

"Those are mine." Casper's voice came out stripped of all its scribe's irony. She had waded forward without seeming to decide to, and her hand was hovering over the scene of the kitchen, not quite touching, the way one hovers over a flame or a wound. "Those are — Sefa. Those are my memories. The ones they bottled. They're *here*."

"The hollow keeps what the covenant kept," Sefa said. "And the covenant kept everything the guild took. It was the collateral. The vault upstairs is just the warehouse." She said it flatly, because there was no kind way to say it. "The gods have been living on the interest."

Wren had gone still. That was the thing about the child that frightened Sefa most — not the power, but the stillness that came over them when the remembering was heavy. Wren stood at the edge of the flooded floor, looking at the woman in the kitchen, and said, very quietly:

"She's going to die in the winter. The mother. And the girl won't understand why the guild came, only that they took the remembering of her away so she wouldn't hurt. But hurting is how you know you loved somebody. If they take the hurting—"

"*Wren.*"

"—then they took the loving too." The child looked up at Casper with terrible, gentle eyes. "I'm sorry. You asked me to remember you. That's what's in you."

Casper stood in the pooled light with her hand still hovering, and Sefa watched her decide — watched the moment land on her like weather, watched her shoulders take it. She had seen people break in this wood. She had seen people run. Casper Vasquez did neither. She lowered her hand into the scene, into the light that was not water, and the kitchen flickered around her fingers like a candle in wind, and she said, in the voice of a woman reading her own sentence aloud:

"Then we'd better make the guild give it back. All of it. The hurting too."

* * *

**CASPER**

They walked through her childhood.

There was no other honest way to put it, and Casper had sworn, somewhere on the stairs, that she would be honest with herself if it killed her — because the guild had spent twenty years teaching her that a thing not written down had not happened, and she was done living by that rule.

The scenes stood in the shallow light like stalls in a night market. She walked the lane between them and touched what she dared. A festival, herself at nine with sugar on her chin. A schoolroom, a teacher's kind hand. A fever, her mother's palm cool on her forehead, rosemary and tallow, *there now, there now*. Each one flickered under her fingers and poured a little of itself back into her — not the whole memory, not the bottled weight of it, but the *shape*. The way a beggar outside a bakery knows bread by smell alone.

It should have been joy. Some of it was. But between the scenes, in the gaps where the pooled light ran dark, other things stood too. Things she had not bottled. A debt-collector's shadow on the door. Her mother's face, drawn and older than it should have been, signing something by candlelight. A guild seal pressed into wax like a wound.

"She bargained," Casper said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "My mother. She didn't sell me. She *pledged* me. There's a difference in the ledger law — a sale ends it, a pledge continues it. She bought something with me. Twenty years of something."

Sefa had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she knew the answer and was deciding whether Casper could survive hearing it. Wren answered instead, because Wren always answered.

"Twenty years of the city not falling down," the child said. "The covenant broke when your mother signed. It was supposed to break all the way. She gave the gods the sweetest thing she had — your remembering — to buy them staying asleep a little longer." A pause. "The twenty years are nearly spent."

The dark between the scenes deepened as they walked, and Casper understood that the light was thinning because they were descending through her own history toward its oldest layer, and beneath the oldest layer there was no more her. Only the thing the pledge had been *for*.

Her foot found the edge of the flooded floor where it shelved downward, and the water-that-was-not-water closed over her boot, cold as a page left open in winter.

Then the whole hollow *breathed*.

Every scene flickered at once. The kitchen, the festival, the fever — all of them guttered like lamps in a draft, and from the deep below came a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears: a slow, vast shifting, like a ledger the size of a mountain being turned to a new page.

Sefa's hand closed on her arm. "It knows we're here."

Wren was smiling. That was the worst part. The child stood in the failing light with their face lit from below, and the smile was not a child's smile at all — it was old, and patient, and *glad*.

"He's been waiting so long," Wren said. "He kept *receipts*, Casper. He kept receipts for every prayer nobody answered. He wants to show you his accounts."

The dark below them opened like a book.
