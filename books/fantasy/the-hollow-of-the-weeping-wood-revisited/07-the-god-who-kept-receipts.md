## 7. The God Who Kept Receipts

The dark opened into a hall, and the hall was a ledger.

That was Casper's first thought, and it shamed her — that even here, at the root of everything, her mind reached for columns and margins. But the thought would not leave. The cavern stretched before them in long parallel shelves of stone, rising floor to unseen ceiling like the racks of the guild's archive, and every shelf was packed with documents. Not paper. Something older and paler, like bark stripped thin as vellum, curled into scrolls and bound with sinew. They filled the hollow the way water fills a well: completely, patiently, without any visible end.

And it smelled like the Ledger Guild. That was the detail that made her throat tighten. Dust and tallow and the faint vinegar tang of old ink. Forty years of her life had been spent breathing that smell, and here it was again, underground, in a place no scribe had ever been supposed to find.

Lamps burned along the aisles — small clay lamps, each with a flame that gave light but no heat. There were thousands of them. Perhaps tens of thousands.

"Careful where you step," Sefa murmured. She had Wren by the hand now, and her grip on the child looked less like comfort than like anchoring. "Ground like this remembers being worshipped. It doesn't always like being walked on."

Casper didn't answer. She was reading.

It was instinct, or compulsion, or whatever the guild had ground into her during ten years of indenture — her eyes went to the nearest scroll, to the label inked along its spine in a script so precise it could only have been written by something that never hurried. The script wasn't Vhaleni. It wasn't anything she knew. And yet she understood it the way she understood the shape of her own hands.

*Petition of Maro the fisher, offered at the shrine of the drowned road, asking that his daughter's fever break. Received. Not granted.*

Below it, a second line: *Cost of silence: one spring, one daughter's wedding, one grave dug too early.*

"He really did keep receipts," she said aloud. Her voice came out strange, flattened by the weight of the shelves.

"Not receipts," said a voice from the far end of the hall. "Accounts."

He was seated at a desk.

Of all the things Casper had imagined finding beneath the Weeping Wood — monsters, thrones, the writhing body of divinity — she had not imagined a desk. It was a plain thing of dark wood, scarred by centuries of pen-knives, exactly like the desk Corvane kept in his office, and behind it sat a figure that took her mind several long moments to resolve into a shape she could bear.

He was old the way mountains are old, which is to say he did not look aged at all; he simply looked like he had been there before the concept of youth. He wore robes the color of wet slate, and his skin was gray-brown bark over something that moved slowly beneath, and his eyes —

His eyes were the eyes of a clerk who has worked a double shift for six hundred years. Tired. Meticulous. Kind, in the way that precision can be kind.

Three lamps sat on the desk before him, and beside them, stacked in neat towers, scrolls. So many scrolls. They rose past his shoulders on either side, and more lay open under his hands, and Casper understood with a lurch that these were different from all the others in the hall. These were current.

"You are the debt-auditor," Ossuin said. His voice was quiet, dry, like a page turning. "Vasquez, Casper. Indentured, Ledger Guild of Vhalen, term of service eleven years, three months, nine days." He looked up at her. "You check the guild's sums against the world. You have found forty-two discrepancies since your appointment. You reported thirty-nine."

"The other three?" Sefa asked sharply.

"She kept them." The god turned his gaze to her, and it was unbearable, being seen through like that. "In a notebook sewn into the lining of her left boot. Because some discrepancies felt like they belonged to someone who couldn't afford to have them corrected." He folded his hands. "You see why I wanted to meet you. I have been keeping accounts alone for four hundred years. I recognize a colleague."

Wren made a sound — half gasp, half laugh — and pressed against Sefa's side. The child was staring at the lamp flames. "They're prayers," Wren whispered. "Each lamp is a prayer nobody answered. He lights them so they don't go out."

"It seemed cruel otherwise," Ossuin agreed. "To receive a plea and do nothing with it. Even receiving it changes its weight." He gestured, wearily, at the endless shelves. "Every petition ever offered to my kind. Every one that arrived and met silence. I logged them. What else does a god do when the covenant binds him out of answering? He keeps records. Someone must be able to say, at the end, *this much was asked of us, and this much we failed to give.*"

Casper's chest hurt. She thought of the debt-ledgers upstairs, of the entries labeled *impossible things*, of Corvane burning that page in his brazier with hands that shook. She had believed she was auditing a fraud. She had been auditing an apology four hundred years long.

"Why show us this?" she managed. "Why now?"

"Because the seal is failing, and when it fails, the accounts come due." He said it the way Corvane announced quarterly closings. "My siblings will return unbound, and they will not return as accountants. They have been starving for worship for four centuries, and starving gods do not read terms. Unless—" and here he paused, and Casper watched something move across his ancient face that she recognized from mirrors, from Corvane's office, from every negotiation she'd ever witnessed: the moment before the offer.

"Unless the covenant is reforged," she said.

"At the stone below this hall. Yes. A soul freely given, whole or in part, written into the binding." He nodded slowly. "I know what you carry, scribe. I helped draft the original terms. Your mother bargained twenty years for this city, and paid with your childhood, and the guild has held those years in glass ever since, drawing on them as collateral." He slid open a drawer.

What he lifted out made the world tilt.

Three jars. Small, palm-sized, stoppered with wax gone amber with age. Inside each one swirled a faint luminous haze — and Casper knew, knew the way she knew the smell of the archive, what the haze was. She had inventoried crates of them in the vault. She had never once imagined holding her own.

"These are yours," Ossuin said. "Not copies. Yours. Your mother's face when she sang. Your father teaching you letters on the kitchen table with flour dusted on the boards. The name your grandmother called you, the one that was never written anywhere. The dog. The winter the river froze and the whole street skated. Everything the guild took, everything Corvane guards, everything you have been grieving without knowing what you grieved." He set them on the desk between them, gently, the way one sets down a sleeping child. "Take them. Drink them back. Walk out of this hollow whole."

"And the reforging?"

"Will fail. My siblings will pour through the crack, and the city above will learn what four centuries of hunger looks like." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "But you — you will be yourself. Complete. For however long completeness lasts. Is that not worth—"

"No," Sefa started, but Casper raised a hand.

She was looking at the jars.

Here is what no one tells you about grief with no object: it doesn't ache. It hums. It sits in you like a room you're forbidden to enter, and you press your ear to the door sometimes, and you hear nothing, and that nothing becomes the shape of your whole life. Casper had lived thirty-one years with a humming in her chest and had built a self around the hole in herself the way a tree builds around lightning-scar. She had told herself she didn't miss what she couldn't remember.

She was lying. She had always been lying.

Her mother's face. She did not know her mother's face. She knew she'd had one; she knew everyone did. But there was no image, no voice, no warmth — just a word, *mother*, floating unanchored in the dark like one of Ossuin's lamps.

And here it was. Three small jars. Reachable.

Wren touched her sleeve.

Casper looked down. The child was watching her with those too-old eyes, and their expression held no judgment at all — only a terrible, gentle understanding.

"I remember everything," Wren said softly. "Everything that ever happened to me. Do you know what that's like? There's no room left for new things. Every night I lie down and all my yesterdays pile on me like stones." The child's small hand tightened on her sleeve. "If you take the jars and go, I'll understand. Truly. But then I'll be the one who remembers you leaving."

The lamps flickered. Thousands of unanswered prayers, guttering in the dark, waiting.

Ossuin waited too, hands folded, patient as shelf-stone. "There is no wrong answer, scribe," he said. "There is only the answer you will live inside."

Casper reached out.

Her fingers stopped a breath from the first jar — close enough to feel a cold radiating off the glass, cold like snowlight, like the frozen river she couldn't remember skating on. Her hand was trembling. Somewhere in that trembling hand was a girl who had a mother's face to hold onto, and the girl was screaming, and Casper loved her so much it felt like dying.

She closed her fist, empty, and drew it back.

"Write me a receipt," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "For what I'm refusing. I want it in your best hand. I want it dated."

Something moved through the god's tired eyes — she would spend a long time deciding whether it was sorrow or respect.

"As you wish," said Ossuin, and reached for a blank scroll. "Though be warned. The stone below does not accept refusals as payment. When we descend, it will ask you for a soul, whole or in part, and it will not offer alternatives."

"Then I'll bring one," Casper said. "Terms of my own drafting."

Far down the hall, beyond the last lamp, a stair of living root began to glow — and with it, faint and growing, came the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep, beginning to wake.
