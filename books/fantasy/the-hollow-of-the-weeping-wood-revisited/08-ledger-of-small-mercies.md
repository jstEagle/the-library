## 8. Ledger of Small Mercies

Sefa had spent nine years learning to measure time in small, practical units. Not hours—hours were for people with futures. Minutes. The time it took a kettle to boil. The time it took a lie to leave her mouth and reach a child's ears. The time between a god's name being spoken and the world remembering it had once been holy ground.

She was measuring in seconds now.

The shrine-door behind her had begun to sing, and nothing that sang like that was safe.

"Again," she said, and her voice came out steadier than her hands. "From the first line. Don't rush it."

Wren sat cross-legged on the hollow floor, small hands pressed flat against the packed earth, and recited the closing verse of the Doorkeeping Litany. The words were old—older than Sefa's order, older than its disgrace, older than the covenant that had made such litanies necessary. As the child spoke, the light in the hollow steadied. The walls, which had been weeping threads of gold, slowed their bleeding. Somewhere far above, the Weeping Wood's endless drip of sap fell quiet, like a held breath.

Sefa counted. She had learned to count the way other priests had learned to pray.

Forty seconds of stillness. Then the gold threads shivered and resumed.

"It's not holding," Wren said. Not a question. The child never asked questions about things like this; she simply stated them, the way she stated the names of dead birds she had never seen, or the contents of a stranger's pockets. "I can feel it slipping. It's like—like trying to carry water in my hands, and there's a hole in my hands."

"There's no hole in you," Sefa said. "There's a hole in the world. You're just the only one standing where the water comes through."

It was a kind thing to say, and it was a lie, and Sefa had gotten very good at telling the two apart, because for nine years they had been the same words in her mouth. *The gods are gone. The silence is holy. Your prayers go nowhere, child, and that is a mercy.* She had said that last one to Wren a hundred times, and every time, the child had looked at her with those too-old eyes and known it for what it was.

The seal had been failing for forty years. Everyone in the old orders had known it, the way a family knows a father is dying and arranges the furniture around it. What no one had known—what Sefa was only now understanding, standing in the flooded gold light of the hollow with a scribe and a child and a god's ledger waiting below—was how fast the end would come once it started.

It had started an hour ago.

* * *

They had been an hour into the descent when the first of the returned prayers came through.

Casper had heard it first—the scribe had a scribe's ear for language, and she had stopped mid-step and said, *That was Vhalen dialect. Market district. Someone just prayed, and it landed.* Then a second prayer. Then a dozen, overlapping, tumbling down the root-tunnels like coins spilled down a well shaft, and each one that landed made the walls glow a little brighter and the air a little sweeter and the hollow a little less like a place and a little more like a throat.

"Divinity doesn't return gradually," Sefa had said, and hated how her voice shook. "It was dammed. Dams don't leak politely. They—"

The word *break* had been taken from her by the sound of the third shrine-door along the passage shattering into singing dust.

That was when they had run. Not walked, not descended with dignity—run, down through the pooling light, past Casper's memories hanging in the air like lanterns (a woman's hands kneading bread; a door painted blue; a name being sung), past Wren clutching Sefa's sleeve and reciting the Litany under her breath like a rope hand over hand, down to the antechamber before the covenant-stone, where the last shrine-door stood between the flooding divine and the deep places where Ossuin kept his accounts.

And then Casper had said the thing Sefa had been dreading since the market district, since the first impossible dawn, since the beginning.

"Someone has to hold the door. And it can't be her." The scribe had looked at Wren, then at Sefa, and Sefa had seen her do the arithmetic—had watched her arrive, line by line, at the only answer. "You're the priestess. You know the door-rites."

"I was defrocked," Sefa said. "Stripped of office. My order voted."

"Did they strip the knowing?"

No. They hadn't. That was the cruelty of disgrace; it took your name off the rolls and left everything else, the way amputation leaves the ache.

So here she was. Alone at the shrine-door, with a kettle's worth of minutes boiling away, and the last relic of the Order of the Quiet Threshold in her hands.

It was small. That was the first thing people always said, when the order had still had people. The Threshold Stone—a chip of the original doorstone from the first shrine ever raised, no bigger than a knucklebone, worn smooth by nine hundred years of priests' thumbs. It had no power of its own. That was the doctrine. It was only a *focus*, a thing to pour attention through, the way a funnel pours water. The order had carried it out of the burning of their last temple in a beggar's cup, and had carried it through forty years of exile, and Sefa had carried it through nine years of hiding, and it was warm in her palm now for the first time in her life.

Warm meant it was working.

Warm meant it was dying.

* * *

"Say it again," she told Wren, and Wren said it again, and Sefa pressed the Threshold Stone against the shrine-door and felt the Litany travel through her arm like current.

The door was not a door the way doors are doors. It was a *decision*—the old gods' decision, at the covenant's making, that some things would stay out and some things would stay in. The stone in Sefa's hand was a fragment of that decision, and when she pressed it to the seal and spoke the rites, she was not holding a door shut so much as *agreeing* with a choice made nine hundred years ago, adding her small yes to the vast original one.

The gold light pressed back. It did not push like wind or water. It pressed like *attention*—like being seen by something enormous and gentle and utterly without malice, which was worse, because you could fight malice. What could you fight in something that loved you?

And it did love them. That was the horror Sefa had never been able to explain to the guild scribes, to Corvane, to anyone who thought of the old gods as creditors or jailers. The divinity flooding the hollow was not angry. It was *grateful*. It had been locked out of the world for forty years and it was coming home to everything it had ever loved, and it would unmake whatever it touched, because that was what love did when it was this old and this large and had forgotten the shape of small things. It would unmake Wren. It would unmake the covenant-stone. It would unmake the careful, bounded, mortal world that the covenant had built precisely so that small things could go on being small.

The door held. The light pooled against it like water against glass, and Sefa stood in the shallows of it and felt it lapping at her boots, her shins, her knees, and where it touched her, her old scars ached with a sweetness that was almost unbearable.

*It knows me,* she thought. *It remembers my order. It's trying to say my name.*

She did not let it. She fed the Threshold Stone another verse of the Litany, and another, and did not listen.

* * *

The problem—the logistical problem, and Sefa clung to logistics the way drowning people cling to spars—was fuel.

The Litany had forty verses. The door-rites required them to be spoken in order, through the stone, without pause. Sefa knew all forty. Wren knew all forty. But Wren could not stand at the door, because Wren was the anchor, and the anchor had to stay back from the flood, and besides—

Besides, Wren's power was destabilizing. Sefa could see it in the way the child's shadow moved wrong, lagging a half-second behind her body, then running a half-second ahead. Every prayer that landed in the world above came down the hollow and pooled in Wren, and Wren was filling up like a cup under a tap with no drain. The child's recitation of the Litany helped the door, but it cost her—each verse drew the flooding divinity through her small body on its way to the seal, and each pass left her a little brighter, a little less solid, a little more like the lanterns of memory that hung in the upper tunnels.

"Verse thirty-one," Sefa said.

"Verse thirty-one." Wren's voice had harmonics in it now. Two voices, then three. "The threshold is not a wall but a *welcome* that remembers its limits—"

The stone flared. The door held. Sefa's arm burned from fingertips to shoulder, and she looked down and saw the light had reached her waist, and where it had touched her hands, the skin had gone smooth—younger, the scars of her defrocking fading, the calluses of nine hard years softening. It was healing her. It was *unmaking* her, gently, toward some ideal version, and in an hour it would unmake her all the way back into light and warmth and nothing.

Fine. Fine. That was the trade. She had made her peace with trades nine years ago, in the ashes, holding a beggar's cup with a stone in it.

The real problem was the arithmetic. Forty verses, and Wren could manage maybe twenty before the light in her got dangerous. The door needed all forty, sustained. Which meant—

"Verse thirty-two," Sefa said, and then, because the child's shadow had gone entirely white: "Stop. Wren. Stop there. That's your last one."

"But the door—"

"The door is my problem." Sefa knelt, took the child's face in her hands—carefully, carefully, the hands that glowed faintly now, the hands that were already becoming something else. "Listen to me. You're going to go down to Casper. You're going to tell her the seal bought her until moonrise, no more. She needs to know the covenant-stone won't wait for a better moment, because there isn't one. Can you remember that? *Moonrise.* Say it back."

"Moonrise," Wren said. Her three voices made it sound like a chord.

"And Wren." Sefa pulled the beggar's cup from her pack—the cup that had carried the Threshold Stone through fire and exile and nine years of disgrace—and pressed it into the child's hands. "Take this. It's not worth anything. It's just—it held things. That was its whole life, holding things, and it was good at it. Be like the cup."

Wren looked at the cup, and then at Sefa, and did the thing that broke Sefa's heart most efficiently of all the child's many talents: she understood. Completely. Without drama. She saw the arithmetic Sefa had done, and she saw the answer, and she knew that arguing was a waste of minutes, and she was, gods help them all, *her* child, raised on logistics.

"Verse thirty-three is the hard one," Wren said. "The one about the keeper who stays."

"I know it," Sefa said. "Go."

The child went—ran, small feet slapping the hollow stone, her too-white shadow streaming behind her like a banner—and Sefa turned back to the door, alone, with thirteen verses left and a knucklebone of a stone going hot as a coal in her palm.

* * *

She spoke them slowly. That was the discipline; that was the whole of the Quiet Threshold's teaching, the thing the order had died preserving: *a door is held by patience, not strength.* She gave the stone each verse at the pace of a kettle boiling, and between verses she let herself think the thoughts she had not permitted herself in nine years.

She thought about the night her order fell, and how she had run not with the relics or the scrolls but with the cup, because someone had to carry the small thing, and she had been the youngest, and that was what the youngest were for.

She thought about the first morning she'd found Wren in the wood, a starving child praying to a silent sky with perfect faith, and how Sefa had lied to her—*the gods are gone, it's a mercy*—because the truth was that the gods were listening and could not answer, and a child who knew that would go mad with grief. She had lied to protect her, and the lie had held for three years, and then the wood had started answering, and every answer had been a small accusation.

She thought about Casper Vasquez, who had walked into their camp with a scribe's posture and a debtor's eyes, and how Sefa had recognized the hollow in her instantly, because she knew that hollow from the inside—it was what disgrace carved in you, the space where your name used to be. And she thought: *she's down there now, about to pay something terrible, and she doesn't know yet that the terrible thing and the right thing are the same thing, and when she finds out, she'll need someone who has already made that trade to have made it first.*

So that when Casper looked for a precedent, someone would have left one.

The stone was white-hot now. The light was at her chest. It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part—it felt like being *approved of*, like the warmest hearth and the fullest table and every prayer she'd ever whispered being answered at once, *yes, yes, we heard you, we always heard you, we were only waiting to be let in.*

"Verse thirty-eight," Sefa said, and her voice had harmonics in it too now, and she found she did not mind. "The keeper who stays is not lost but *addressed*—"

The door groaned. Held. The gold pressed close, and somewhere in it, unmistakably, something spoke her name—the name from before, the priestess-name, the one the order had struck from the rolls—and it said it the way you say a word you've been keeping in your mouth for forty years.

"Not yet," Sefa told it, and gave the stone verse thirty-nine.

* * *

She had one verse left, and perhaps ten minutes, and she spent four of them sitting down, because her legs had decided independently that the sacrifice could proceed without spectacle and her body agreed.

From here she could almost see down the passage, to where the covenant-stone waited in the deep dark, and she imagined Casper standing before it, ledger key in hand, doing her own arithmetic. *A soul freely given, whole or in part.* The gods would take a life. They would take a memory. They would take whatever was offered without flinching, because they had forgotten the shape of small things, and it would be left to the small things to teach them—left to a scribe, a defrocked priestess, a god-touched child, to show the vast loving flood what a *bounded* gift looked like.

"Verse forty," Sefa said.

The last verse was the shortest. It had only five words, and the order had never written them down; they were passed mouth to mouth, priest to priest, and Sefa had carried them nine years without speaking them aloud, saving them, the way you save the last of anything.

She pressed the Threshold Stone to the door one final time. It was no longer a stone; it was a coal, a seed of dawn, a small decision burning in her palm.

"The threshold holds because *I* hold."

The stone gave itself entirely to the door, and the door drank it, and the seal blazed white-gold down the whole length of the hollow—a pillar of held breath, of agreed decision, of *no, not yet, we are not finished*—and Sefa felt the last relic of the Order of the Quiet Threshold pass out of the world and into the hinge of heaven, and she laughed, quietly, because it had taken nine hundred years and it had finally been spent exactly as intended.

The light reached her heart.

She had time for one more thought, and she spent it, as she had spent everything, practically: *Moonrise. She has until moonrise. Someone should be counting.*

Far below, in the deep dark before the covenant-stone, Casper Vasquez heard the seal blaze and felt the hours it had bought settle over her like a debt called in—and turned to face the stone, and the god, and the price.

The door held.

For now, the door held.
