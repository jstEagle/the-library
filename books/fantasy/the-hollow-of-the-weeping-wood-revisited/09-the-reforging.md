## 9. The Reforging

The covenant-stone was smaller than a debt should be.

Casper had imagined something vast — a monolith, a mountain's knuckle, a thing that required scaffolding and hymns and the labor of generations. Instead it was a waist-high slab of grey granite sitting in a pool of still water at the bottom of the hollow, its surface worn smooth as an old ledger page, its edges rounded by centuries of hands that had come here to give something away.

Ossuin stood beside it. The last old god did not loom; he had never loomed, not even in his own stories. He was a tall figure of dusk and dust, his outline soft as charcoal rubbed with a thumb, and where his face should have been there was only the suggestion of one — the way you remember a face from a letter rather than a meeting. Around his neck, on a cord of woven root, hung a ledger of his own: small, cracked, swollen with use.

"You've been counting," Casper said.

"Every prayer." His voice was dry paper turning. "Four hundred and eleven thousand, two hundred and six unanswered since the seal went up. I kept the accounts because someone had to. A debt unrecorded is a debt denied, and I would not have them denied. Not even by me."

Above them — impossibly far above, through roots and stone and the pooled dark of the hollow — Sefa was holding the shrine-door against the returning flood of divinity, spending her order's last relic hour by hour. Wren was somewhere in the light of her, glowing like a struck bell. Moonrise. Someone should be counting. Casper was counting now. She could feel the hours draining through the stone beneath her feet like water through a cracked jar.

"Tell me the price," she said. "All of it. No rounding down."

Ossuin turned the little ledger in his hands. "A soul," he said. "Freely given. That is the whole of the law and it has never changed. Whole or in part. The first covenant was sealed with the gods' surrender freely made — we gave up our claim on the world, and the world gave up its claim on us, and the stone held the terms between. When your guild began selling memory-debts, they were not inventing anything. They were counterfeiting us. Taking souls in pieces and calling it commerce." He paused. "You know this already. You read it in Corvane's ledgers."

"I want to hear it from the party owed."

"Then hear this too." The god's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "Whole is death. The soul departs the body entire, and the body follows, and the binding holds for another age. In part —" he lifted the ledger, "- in part, the giver chooses what is taken. And what is given cannot be reclaimed. Not by me. Not by Ossuin, keeper of receipts. Especially not by me."

"And if I take your bargain instead." She said it flatly, because she had been carrying it since the chamber of bottled memories, turning it over like a coin she couldn't stop biting. "My memories back. Intact. All three jars' worth, plus whatever else was skimmed off me over the years. And I walk away, and the seal fails, and the returning gods unmake whatever they touch."

"The world ends messily," Ossuin agreed. "But *you* end whole. You would remember your mother's voice. You would remember why the rain smelled the way it did when you were seven. You would remember being loved." He let that stand in the dark between them. "I am not offering it cruelly, scribe. I am offering it because I have watched four hundred thousand prayers go unanswered, and I know exactly what an unanswered life weighs. You have been robbed longer than anyone living. No one would blame you."

"No," Casper said. "That's the trick of it. No one would."

She walked to the stone.

Up close she could see the old terms cut into its face, shallow as breath-fog on glass, in a script older than the Ledger Guild, older than Vhalen, older than the idea of writing things down so they could be held to later. But underneath the old script, fainter, there were other marks — hundreds of them, thousands, small tallying scratches like the ones she made in margins when an account refused to balance.

"What are these?"

"Prayers," Ossuin said. "The ones that got through anyway. Through the seal, through the guild's theft, through everything. People keep praying, Casper Vasquez. It is the one debt they refuse to stop paying." He came to stand beside her, and his hand — when it settled on the stone — left no mark at all. "The reforging requires a new signature. A soul, freely given, written into the terms. Wren can open the door but she cannot sign; she *is* the door. Sefa has already given what priesthood leaves to give. Which leaves—"

"Me. It always left me. That's why my mother bargained, isn't it? Why Corvane skimmed my childhood into jars and called it collateral. They weren't protecting me from the covenant. They were keeping me *available* for it." Her laugh came out wrong, jagged. "I've been an entry in arrears my whole life."

"Yes," said Ossuin, who kept honest books.

The water around the stone was perfectly still, and in it Casper could see herself reflected, and behind her reflection — layered under it, like ink bleeding through cheap paper — she could see the others. Fragments. Her returned memories, walking loose in the hollow since the seal began to fail: a woman singing over a cradle. Rain on a tin roof. Her mother's hands folding a letter. A name spoken softly, *Casper, Casper, little scribe*, in a voice she had spent twenty years not knowing she missed.

They drifted close, those fragments, drawn to her like moths to the one lit window. She could almost feel them against her skin. Warm. Patient. Hers, if she reached.

Moonrise. Maybe four hours. Maybe less.

"Here's my problem with both offers," she said. "Yours gives me back myself and costs everyone else everything. Death gives the world its wall and costs me everything, and worse — it costs *them*. Sefa and Wren would carry a corpse out of this hollow and call it victory and never once sleep properly again. I've audited enough grief to know how it compounds." She flexed her fingers, the way she did before a long entry. "So I'm going to propose different terms. And before you object — you told me yourself. In part, the giver chooses what is taken. The law hasn't changed. So the law will hold me."

Ossuin went very still. Gods, she thought, are just debts that learned to walk, and this one was doing arithmetic behind his absent face.

"Name them," he said.

"My name." She heard her own voice steady itself, professional, the tone she used when reading bad news aloud to men who thought numbers wouldn't break their hearts. "Not my life. Not my soul entire. My *name* — Casper Vasquez, daughter of Maren Vasquez, debtor, orphan, scribe of the Ledger Guild of Vhalen. Every memory tied to that name. Every year signed for. The guild bought pieces of me; I'm selling the rest of the account. All of it, into the stone, as the new binding. Freely given."

"And what remains?" Ossuin asked quietly. "If the name goes?"

"A body. A mind. Whatever's underneath the ink." She swallowed. "Someone with hands that still know how to write, and no reason at all to write anything. Unclaimed. Unledgered. No god, no guild, no covenant able to call on her, because there will be no *her* to call on — only a woman the books have never heard of." She looked up at the place where his face wasn't. "You said what's given can't be reclaimed. Not even by you. So make sure, old god. Make absolutely sure. Because if some clerk of yours ever finds a loophole and hands me back my name, the seal comes apart with it."

"There is no loophole," Ossuin said. "There never was. That was always the tragedy of us." He opened his small cracked ledger to a fresh page, and the page drank the dark and gave back light. "Speak the terms. I will witness. The stone will hold them better than I ever held anything."

* * *

She took out her pen.

It was a guild-issue nib, third-grade iron, chewed at the cap from years of thinking. It had written ten thousand entries in service of men who sold children's yesterdays by the dram. It seemed right that it should write the last one.

She knelt at the stone, and the old script warmed under her palm like a living thing acknowledging a colleague, and she began.

*I, who was called Casper Vasquez,* she wrote, and felt the first syllable of herself loosen like a knot giving way, *do here enter these terms freely, under the old law, in part and not in whole.*

The fragments in the water pressed closer. Her mother's voice. The rain. The cradle-song. She let them gather at her shoulder without turning her head, because if she looked at them directly she would stop, and stopping was the one entry she could not afford.

*Let the seal be remade not in surrender nor in sale, but in choice. Let the realms be bound by this: that what was taken may never again be taken, only given. Let every debt to the old gods be answered or forgiven, and let the accounting end.*

Behind her, far above, the shrine-door groaned. Hours, then. Minutes, maybe. She wrote faster.

*In payment I yield: my name. My history. Every memory bound to either. Let them pass into the stone and become the wall between the worlds, and let no power under sky or root restore them, for they are spent, and spent is spent.*

Her hand was shaking now, and tears were dropping onto the granite and beading there like sap, like the Weeping Wood's own grief borrowed for the occasion. She didn't wipe them away. Even weeping was evidence, and evidence belonged in the record.

*And let this last line stand above all others:* she wrote, and her heart slammed once, hard, like a fist on a table, *that the woman who remains owes nothing to anyone, belongs to nothing, and is free.*

The pen scratched the final stroke.

The stone drank it.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the obscene part. She had expected tearing, burning, some grand invoice of agony to justify the price. Instead it was like setting down a load she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine. The name went first — she felt it leave the way you feel a word leave your mouth, except it never arrived anywhere. Then the years peeled away in order, newest to oldest, each one folding closed like a finished book: Corvane and the burning page, the beggar's answered prayer, the vault of jars, the road beneath the roots, Sefa's crooked smile, Wren's impossible gravity —

No, she thought, no, wait, I'm still using those, I need—

But the terms were the terms. She had written them. She had chosen them. That was the whole point, the only point, the thing she would have wanted remembered if remembering were possible: *she chose.* No one offered these terms. She had drafted them herself, in the dark, at the bottom of the world, and signed them clean.

The last thing to go was not a memory at all. It was smaller than that. A feeling — the particular warmth of being known by two people camped among tree-roots, of belonging to a story larger than a ledger line. It lingered while everything else drained, and it looked at her, in the way feelings can look, and it did not say goodbye.

It said: *we'll find you.*

Then the stone flared white, and the hollow filled with the sound of a vast account closing — not with a slam but with a sigh, the sound of four hundred thousand prayers finally marked *answered*, and the water rose gently around her knees, warm as bathwater, and Casper Vasquez went out like a lamp whose oil was not spent but simply, at last, unowned.

When the light faded, a woman was kneeling alone at a smooth grey stone in a pool of still water, holding a chewed pen she did not recognize, in a place she had never seen, in a silence that felt less like emptiness than like a room after guests have gone — swept, and waiting, and strangely kind.

She looked down at the pen. At the stone. At her own hands, which were inkstained and steady and entirely unfamiliar.

Somewhere above her, faintly, through miles of root and rock, she heard a child begin to cry — and then stop, and then laugh, and then call down into the dark in a voice cracked with hope:

"*Hello? Is somebody down there?*"

The woman who had been nobody's debtor stood up, and turned toward the sound, and began, without knowing why, to climb.
