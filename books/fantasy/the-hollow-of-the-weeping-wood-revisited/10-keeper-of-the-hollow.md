## 10. Keeper of the Hollow

The Wood had stopped weeping by midwinter, and by spring it had begun to laugh.

That was how Wren thought of it, anyway — the sound the branches made when the wind came through, a dry rustling like pages turning. Sefa said that was fanciful. Sefa said a great many things were fanciful, and then she would look up into the silver-leaved canopy with an expression that suggested she believed every word of her own dismissal and was simply too proud to admit it.

Wren sat on the shrine-door's threshold, knees drawn up, watching the woman in the hollow below.

She was down among the roots again, as she always was at this hour, kneeling on a flat stone with a ledger open across her thighs. She wrote with a stub of charcoal because she claimed ink froze in the hollow's breath, though Wren had seen ink work fine down there. She just liked the charcoal. She liked the way it smudged, she said. She liked things that left marks you could half-erase.

Her name was not Casper.

Wren knew this the way he knew the covenant-stone's weight, the way he knew which prayers were his to carry: absolutely, and without being able to explain it. The woman had no name at all, really. The ledgers called her *Keeper*, in a hand that was hers and yet written by no one. When travelers came down from Vhalen with questions — and they still came, fewer each season, but they came — she told them, "I keep the accounts here," and if they asked what accounts, she smiled in a way that made them stop asking.

"Is she writing about us?" Wren asked.

Sefa looked up from mending a sandal strap. The priestess had gone grey at the temples this year, which Wren suspected was less age than arithmetic; she counted everything now, hours and candles and steps, as if time were a debt she intended to pay down personally. "She doesn't write about people. She writes about debts."

"She used to."

Sefa's hands went still. Above them, the leaves turned their pages.

* * *

They visited twice a month, or once a week when the roads were kind. It had been Sefa's idea at first — *she saved us all, the least we owe is company* — but somewhere over the long winter it had stopped being obligation and become something with its own gravity. They brought bread from the market district, where the beggars' corner had become something like a chapel. They brought news: the Ledger Guild had reopened under new masters who burned nothing and audited everything, and old Corvane had retired to a cottage two days east where he grew bitter herbs and, according to rumor, apologized to his garden daily.

And they brought stories.

That was Wren's favorite part, though he'd never say so. Sefa told the stories, mostly, in her dry priestess voice, sitting cross-legged on the root-shelf while the Keeper worked below. Stories about a scribe named Casper Vasquez who broke into a guild vault and found three jars with her own name on them. About a woman who walked through her own memories in the dark beneath the world and did not flinch. About a scribe who stood before a god who kept receipts of every unanswered prayer and was offered back her whole stolen self — and chose instead to sign away her name so that everyone else could keep theirs.

"She sounds brave," the Keeper said, the first time Sefa told it. She didn't look up from her ledger. Her charcoal moved steadily, column after column, numbers balancing against numbers. "Or foolish. Those often wear the same coat."

"She was both," Sefa said. "Mostly both."

"And what happened to her? This Casper?"

Wren had held his breath. He remembered — he remembered *everything*, that was his curse and his purpose, he remembered the covenant-stone blazing white, remembered her hand moving across it, remembered the exact moment her name left the world like a candle pinched out between wet fingers. He remembered her turning around afterward with a face wiped clean and asking, politely, *who are you all, and why are you crying?*

"She's still here," Sefa had said quietly. "Right in front of you."

The Keeper had considered this for a long moment. Then she had gone back to her columns. But that night, when Wren crept down to check, he found she had copied the story into the margin of her ledger in tiny, careful letters — not the facts, Sefa's telling of it, word for word, as if the words themselves were entries worth keeping.

* * *

By summer, some of the stories had begun to feel familiar to her.

It started small. Sefa mentioned the guild vault, the smell of it — cold stone and lamp oil and the sweetish rot of bottled years — and the Keeper said, without thinking, "The third shelf sags. On the left." And then she frowned at herself, because she had never been told that, and Sefa went very still, and above them the leaves turned and turned.

Another time, Wren dropped a honey-cake down the root-shelf and she caught it without looking, left-handed, exactly the way a person catches something when their right hand is busy holding a pen. "You write with your right," Wren observed. "You catch with your left."

"Don't you?"

"No," Wren said. "Nobody does. That's a scribe's trick. You learn it carrying ledgers up ladders."

She looked at her own hands for a while after that. Then she laughed — a short, startled sound, like something escaping — and ate the honey-cake, and said, "Well. Whoever I was, she was practical."

But it was the small things that undid her, Wren thought. Not the vault, not the tricks. It was the evening Sefa hummed an old hymn while washing cups, one of the disgraced order's melodies, and the Keeper's humming joined in on the harmony line before she seemed to know she was doing it. She stopped. The water dripped. Somewhere overhead a wood pigeon complained about the state of the world.

"I know that song," she said.

"It's very old," Sefa said carefully. "No one sings it anymore."

"Someone taught me." The Keeper pressed her wet hands flat against her skirt. "I can almost see — there was a window. Rain on it. Someone was singing to make the counting go faster, and I was small, and—" She shook her head hard, like a dog shedding water. "Nothing. It's gone. Isn't it strange, how you can miss something you never had?"

"Yes," Wren said, before Sefa could stop him. "It is. But it comes back. Bits of it. If you're patient. If people keep telling you."

She looked at him for a long time. In the hollow's green light her eyes were the same eyes he remembered from the covenant-stone — tired, sharp, kind underneath the sharpness like a blade wrapped in wool. She didn't remember him. She didn't remember any of it. But sometimes, when he prayed — and he still prayed, carefully, sparingly, the way you handle a blade someone might take the wrong way — sometimes she would glance up at the same moment, as if something in her had heard.

"Tell me another one," she said, settling back onto her stone. "About her. The scribe. Tell me the part where she's afraid."

"There's a lot of that part," Sefa warned.

"Good," said the Keeper. "Fear balances the ledger. Everything else is padding."

* * *

Autumn came, and with it the first pilgrims in numbers — not the furtive questioners of spring but real pilgrims, walking the road from Vhalen with offerings of bread and buttons and, once, an entire wheel of cheese that made the Keeper laugh until she had to sit down. The shrines along the road glowed warm again. The gods were quiet now, properly quiet, bound by a covenant written in a nameless hand, and what they gave, they gave freely, because the accounting was finally honest.

On the eve of the anniversary — none of them said *of what*, but all of them knew — the three of them sat together under the great root arch while rain came down soft as ash outside. The Keeper had finished her ledgers early. She sat with her hands empty in her lap, which was rare, and looked out at the rain.

"Do you ever think," she said, "that she got the worst bargain of anyone?"

Wren's heart stumbled. "What do you mean?"

"A soul freely given, whole or in part — those were the terms, yes?" She glanced at Sefa, who nodded slowly. "Everyone keeps saying she was generous. She gave up her name, her history, her *self*. But think about what she actually bought." The Keeper's voice was steady, lecturing, the voice of someone reading columns aloud. "A city. A child. A priestess. Twenty years, forty, however long the covenant holds. And all it cost her was—" she gestured vaguely at her own chest— "*this*. Whatever this was going to be. She'll never know if she'd have been happy. She paid with the only coin she couldn't count."

"You could count it for her," Wren said softly.

She turned to look at him. Rain drummed on the leaves above, patient as a pen scratching.

"What do you mean?"

"You keep her ledgers perfectly. You kept her stories in your margins. You catch with your left hand and you harmonize with disgraced hymns and you knew the third shelf sagged." Wren took a breath. He was eleven years old and god-touched and he had carried the covenant in his blood since before he could walk, and he had never once been afraid to say a true thing, so he didn't start now. "Maybe she didn't lose herself. Maybe she just — filed herself somewhere safe. And maybe we're the ones holding the key, if we keep telling her until the ink fades and only the truth is left."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain.

Then the Keeper reached out and took his hand — left hand to right hand, scribe's habit, though she couldn't have known why it felt natural — and squeezed it once, firmly, the way you press a seal into wax.

"Well," she said. "Then we'd better be thorough. Start from the beginning. And don't skip the parts where she's afraid."

Above them, the Weeping Wood turned its thousand silver pages, and somewhere in the hollow below, in a ledger no one had written in yet, a fresh page waited — blank, unclaimed, ready for whatever name love might finally give back.
