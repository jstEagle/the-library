## 1. The Girl Who Spoke Before Kingdoms

The storm came down the Thornfell passes an hour before dusk, the way storms always did in late autumn—sideways, mean, and full of stones. Casper Lockridge watched it swallow the valley from her survey cabin's single window, and she did what she always did when weather closed the high roads: she banked the fire, checked that her maps were dry and hidden, and settled in to be no one at all.

That was the trick of surviving ten years in these mountains for the Crown. Not strength. Not cleverness, though she had plenty. The trick was unremarkability. A woman alone in a cabin was a fixture, like a cairn or a dead tree. Guild riders passed her door without slowing because there was nothing there worth a second glance. She had built herself into the landscape, stone by careful stone, until even she sometimes forgot there was anything underneath.

The knock came at full dark, three blows soft as falling snow.

Casper went still over her supper of barley and salt pork. Nobody knocked on her door softly. Riders banged. Smugglers whistled the two-note signal off the eastern crag. A soft knock was either a fool or something that didn't want to be heard, and both were trouble.

She took up the long surveyor's knife she used for trimming map edges and opened the door a hand's width.

A child stood on her step, half-drowned, barefoot, maybe eight years old or nine—the storm made it hard to tell age. Her hair hung in black ropes. Her lips were blue. Behind her the rain came down in silver sheets, lightning stitching the peaks white, and yet the girl stood perfectly straight in it, as if the wind were a rumor she'd declined to believe.

"Get in," Casper said, because whatever else she was—a spy's lure, a trap, a ghost—the child would be dead by morning otherwise, and Casper had spent enough years selling safe passage to know exactly what death by mountain looked like.

The girl crossed the threshold without a sound. That was the first wrong thing. Bare feet on rough pine boards should have slapped or squelched. Hers made no noise at all, like the cabin had gone quiet around them out of courtesy.

Casper shut the door against the storm and turned to look at her properly. Small face, sharp-chinned, eyes so dark they seemed to hold the room's light rather than take it. There were scratches on her arms—old ones, healing ones—and mud dried gray along her shins, but no frostbite, which after a night in this weather was its own impossibility.

"Where are your people?" Casper asked. She kept her voice flat, the voice she used with guild clerks. "Who sent you?"

The child looked at her. And spoke.

The words were nothing Casper knew. They moved like water over stones, all vowels and soft endings, rising and falling in a pattern that wasn't question or answer but simply *speech*, ancient and unhurried. It sounded the way the mountains looked at dawn before anyone named them.

Casper had grown up in the lowlands garrison towns. She could swear in four tongues and count in two more. She had sat through lectures at Thornfell Keep while scholars dissected every language the Crown had ever conquered, catalogued, or crushed. She knew what Old Imperial sounded like, and High Lowland, and the trade-pidgin of the coast. She knew the name of what this was without knowing a word of it, the way you know a face from a wanted poster.

Wealdish. The tongue of the forest the kingdoms burned.

Dead a hundred years. Dead with everyone who spoke it, burned into ash alongside the trees and the villages and the songs, so thoroughly that the guild's own histories called the language *unrecorded*—as if a whole people had never bothered to say anything worth writing down.

"You can't," Casper said stupidly. "No one speaks that."

The girl said it again, patient, and this time Casper heard the shape beneath the sound: *shelter*. Or perhaps *hearth*. Or perhaps something older than either.

She fetched bread and the last of the honey, and the child ate like someone who remembered eating was a thing bodies did, mechanically, without pleasure. Casper worked while she watched—drying the girl's hair with a linen rag, setting out a blanket—because hands busy with small kindnesses left the mind free to do its real work, and her mind was running cold and fast.

*Options.* That was how she thought of everything. A smuggler's habit, a survivor's discipline. Every situation was a map with routes on it, and you picked the one that kept you alive and invisible.

Route one: turn the child out. Let the storm finish what it started. Casper examined that road honestly and found she couldn't walk it, which irritated her profoundly.

Route two: feed her, warm her, and at first light walk her down to the ferry at Greywater and hand her to Bren with coin enough to lose her somewhere south. Clean. Anonymous. The kind of kindness that cost nothing and left no mark.

Route three existed too, and she refused to look directly at it. Route three involved the reports she'd read last month in the guild archive, copied in her own neat hand for Vane's signature: *anomalies in the eastern woodlands, flora flowering out of season, travelers reporting song where no settlement stands.* Route three involved why the Crown paid good gold for men who could read a landscape, and what the Crown might pay for a child who *was* one.

She was still refusing to look at route three when the candle did it for her.

There were three candles burning on her work table, stubs of tallow she kept for drafting. As the girl finished the bread, murmuring again under her breath—that same flowing speech, a lullaby perhaps, or a list, or a prayer—the flames *leaned*. All three of them, together, bending toward the child like wheat toward sun, though the air in the sealed cabin was dead still.

Casper stopped breathing.

The flames stretched, elongated, and their color shifted—from tallow-yellow to a green so deep it was almost black, the green of light coming up through water, of moss on the north side of stones, of the old paintings in Thornfell's chapel that showed the Weald before the burning. The girl lifted her head and looked at the candles the way you'd look at old friends across a crowded room, and spoke one more phrase, gentle as closing a book.

The flames straightened. Turned ordinary. Went on burning as if nothing had happened.

Casper's hands were shaking. She put them flat on the table to stop it, and felt the grain of the wood, and the cold, and her own heartbeat—all the solid, mappable world asserting itself around her.

Ten years. Ten years of being furniture, of keeping every secret in a separate locked drawer, of making sure the Crown got accurate maps and the smugglers got safe roads and Vane got his loyal instrument and nobody ever got *her*. And in one night, sitting across from a wet child eating her honey, every drawer in her had been forced open at once.

Because she recognized the feeling. That lean of the flame. She'd felt it herself, once, twelve years ago, bleeding in a ravine with a broken leg, certain she'd die unmourned and unmapped—until Aldous Vane found her, and she had leaned toward him like a flame toward warmth, and he had carried her out, and she had spent a decade repaying a debt whose interest she never finished calculating. Things bent toward what they needed. Candles. Women. Whole kingdoms, maybe.

And something out there in the dead forest needed this girl badly enough to send her walking barefoot through a storm.

The child set down the crust, wiped her mouth, and looked at Casper with those dark holding-light eyes. She pointed one small finger—not at Casper's face, but at the rolled charts stacked in the corner behind the lockbox, the private ones, the ones no living soul knew existed.

Then she spoke a single word. One syllable, round and heavy, and this time Casper didn't need a scholar to feel the meaning settle over the room like weather:

*Mine*, the word said. *Yours. Ours. The thing that is owed.*

Outside, the storm broke apart all at once, the way mountain storms do—no tapering, just silence rolling down the valleys like a dropped curtain. In that silence Casper heard, very far off and very faintly, something in the eastern woods that might have been wind through leaves, except that every leaf within forty miles was a century dead.

She looked at the child. The child looked back, waiting, unhurried, as if she had walked out of a burned kingdom and a hundred years of silence and could certainly wait out one frightened woman.

"All right," Casper said quietly, to the girl, to the candles, to the listening dark. "You're mine tonight."

She banked the fire and lay down between the door and the child, knife in hand out of habit, and told herself the arrangement was tactical.

It took her a long time to sleep. When she finally did, she dreamed of a forest she had never seen, green beyond all reason, and a road running through it drawn in ink that wept.

At dawn she was awake before the light, and already packing—and she hated herself a little for how fast her hands moved, and for knowing, with a cartographer's certainty she could not talk her way out of, that the first thing tomorrow's sunrise would bring was a rider on the pass below. She'd seen the guild's survey flags moving east for a week now, patient as ants.

The hunt had started without her. The only question left on any map she owned was whether she'd be standing in its path—or drawing it.

* * *

She was saddling the mule when she saw the smoke signal rise off Greywater ridge: two columns, then three. Bren's warning code. *Riders on the water road. Come armed, come unseen.*

Casper looked down at the girl standing in her doorway, feet bare on the frozen ground, unbothered by either.

"Well," Casper said, swinging up into the saddle, "let's go find out what you are."
