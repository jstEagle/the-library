## 2. Ink and Obligation

The summons came on the third morning after the girl arrived, riding in the belly of a guild courier's satchel like a knife wrapped in velvet.

Casper found it wedged in her door, though no rider had come up the switchback trail—she'd have heard hooves on the scree. The envelope was heavy cream stock, sealed with the Thornfell compass rose pressed in black wax, and it bore her name in a hand she knew too well: Aldous Vane's, all sharp ascenders and patient downstrokes, the handwriting of a man who believed that anything worth doing was worth doing precisely.

*Surveyor Lockridge. Your presence is requested at the Keep within the fortnight. Matters of the Crown's maps require your particular eye.*

*Matters of the Crown's maps.* Eleven years she had worked for the Guild of Royal Cartographers, and never once had Vane wasted ink on pleasantries he didn't mean. *Particular eye.* It meant trouble. It meant something had appeared on someone's charts that shouldn't be there—or worse, something that should be there and wasn't.

Behind her, inside the cabin, the girl sat cross-legged on the floor with Casper's spare cloak pooled around her shoulders like a nest. She had not spoken since dawn. She rarely spoke at all, and when she did, the words came out in that dead language—the Wealdish tongue, the language of a forest that had been ash for a hundred years—and every time, the candle flames leaned toward her like men craning their necks to hear a secret.

Casper had spent two days telling herself the child was a coincidence. A refugee, maybe, from some valley clan that had kept the old words alive the way mountain folk kept old grudges: out of habit, out of spite. Two days of telling herself that the way the fire bent when the girl sang under her breath meant nothing more than a bad draft.

Two days was apparently all the universe intended to grant her.

She turned the summons over. On the back, in smaller script, a single line: *Come alone. Come quickly.*

"Damn you, Aldous," she said quietly.

The girl looked up. Her eyes were the gray-green of lichen on north-facing stone, and they fixed on Casper with an attention no seven-year-old should possess—as if she could read the wax seal's meaning straight through the paper.

"You stay here," Casper told her. "You don't open the door for anyone. If anyone comes, you go out the back and down into the ravine, and you wait where I showed you. Do you understand me?"

The girl tilted her head. Then, in that voice like wind through dead branches, she said something soft and low in Wealdish, and the lamp on the table guttered—bowed, actually, the flame flattening sideways as if in agreement.

"I'll take that as a yes." Casper pulled her surveyor's coat from its peg and shrugged into it, feeling the familiar weight of it settle over her shoulders like armor. For eleven years this coat had made her invisible. A woman in a guild coat with a transit case and a roll of charts was furniture; nobody remembered her face, nobody questioned her presence, nobody looked twice. She had built her whole life on that principle. Useful to everyone. Loyal to no one. Noticed by no one at all.

She was beginning to suspect those days were over.

* * *

Thornfell Keep squatted at the head of the King's Road like a stone toad, all squat towers and slate roofs furred with frost. Casper had visited perhaps forty times in eleven years, and the place never stopped feeling like walking into a mouth.

The guildhall occupied the keep's east wing, and it smelled the way it always smelled: ink, tallow, cold stone, and beneath it all the faint mineral tang of the drying presses. Casper handed her coat to a hall-boy who didn't look at her face—good—and climbed the stairs to Vane's office at the top of the tower.

The door stood open. Of course it did. Vane liked to watch people approach; it gave him time to arrange his expression.

"Casper." He stood at the great map table that dominated the room, backlit by the tall window, and even now—even with dread crawling up her spine—she felt the old pull of him. Twenty-two years ago he had been the guild journeyman who'd fished her, half-frozen and starving, out of a ditch along the Border Road, a fourteen-year-old orphan with nothing but a talent for drawing what she saw and no name anyone would speak kindly. He'd forged her apprenticeship papers himself. He'd taught her that a map was a kind of power, and that power could belong to a girl with no family if she was careful enough and good enough.

He had saved her life. She had never let herself forget it, and he had never let her forget it either—not cruelly, never cruelly, just... present. Always present. A debt with a heartbeat.

"Guildmaster." She inclined her head. "Your courier was efficient."

"My courier was frightened." Vane turned from the window. He was sixty now, silver at the temples, still broad through the chest, with the permanent faint squint of a man who had spent his youth reading terrain by half-light. His eyes found hers and held them with that terrible gentle directness he reserved for people he intended to use. "Sit, please."

She sat. The chair across the map table was the comfortable one; he'd placed it there for her, which meant he wanted her relaxed, which meant she should be anything but.

Vane unrolled a chart across the scarred oak—a regional survey of the Thornfell range, one of hers, three years old, and she noted with professional detachment that someone had marked it up. Red ink clustered in the high valleys south of the Serpent's Spine. Little annotations in a clerk's hand.

"Do you know what these are?" he asked.

She leaned forward and read. *Livestock found unhurt but mute. Moss flowering out of season. Wells tasting of leaf-rot. A shepherd reports hearing singing where no village stands.* Her blood went cold, very slowly, the way a lake freezes—from the edges inward.

"Folk rumors," she said, keeping her voice level. "Mountain people love a story. Half of it'll be weather and the other half will be drink."

"Half of it," Vane agreed pleasantly, "will be weather and drink. But look at the distribution." His finger traced the red marks. "They cluster. They follow the old burn line—the border of what was the Weald before the war. And in the last eight months they have been moving north. Toward the passes. Toward—" he tapped the map, "—your survey territory."

Casper made herself breathe. *Toward my cabin. Toward my door. Toward the child asleep under my spare cloak.*

"What is it you want from me, Guildmaster?"

"What I always want. Precision." He drew a second document from the drawer—an official writ, sealed with the Crown's signet above the guild's rose, twin stamps like a beast with two mouths. "The King's ministry has ordered a full investigation of these signs. They believe—there are those at court who believe—that something survives in the burn lands. Something the war did not finish."

"The war finished the Weald," Casper said. "Everyone knows it. A hundred thousand acres of forest, burned to the root. There's nothing left to finish."

"There is a difference," Vane said quietly, "between dead and sleeping. My predecessors knew it. Why do you think the guild has surveyed the burn line every single year for a century? Not for timber. Not for roads. We have been watching a grave, Casper, because graves are only safe so long as nothing climbs out of them."

He said it without drama, without flourish, and that was the worst part. Aldous Vane did not believe in ghosts. He believed in evidence, in accumulation, in the slow patient weight of data—and he was looking at her with the full force of that patience now.

"These signs began eight months ago," he continued. "But in the last six weeks they've concentrated. Livestock going mute. Wells. Singing." He paused. "And four days ago, a patrol out of Highmoor Fort reported a light in the upper ravines. A single candle, burning in a window of an empty survey cabin. A cabin registered to Surveyor Casper Lockridge."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, in the drafting hall, a pen scratched. Casper heard her own heartbeat instead.

"You've had someone watching my cabin?"

"I've had someone watching *all* the cabins. Routine." He folded his hands. "I'm not accusing you of anything, Casper. I'm asking you, as the finest field surveyor this guild has produced in thirty years, to find the source of these signs and report it to me. Whatever it is. A vagrant. A cult of fire-superstitionists. A traveling singer spreading old songs." His voice gentled further, which she recognized as the most dangerous register he owned. "Or something older. If the Weald has left a seed behind—if anything of it endures—then the Crown must know first. Not smugglers. Not valley clans. Not the kinds of people who would sell such a thing or worship it or break it open to see what shines inside. Us. The guild. Order. That is how we protect people. You know that better than anyone—you've drawn safe roads through country that kills the unwary. This is the same work, Casper. Bigger scale. Find the thing. Map it. Bring it home."

*Bring it home.* In a cage, she thought. In a jar. In whatever iron box the ministry had built in some cellar under the capital, next to the other things the war hadn't finished.

"And if I find nothing?" she asked.

"Then you find nothing, and we close the file, and the mountains keep their secrets another decade." He smiled, and it was the smile from the ditch, twenty-two years ago—the one that had said *I see you, girl, I see what you could become.* "You've never failed me. Don't start with this."

There it was. Not a request. A debt, called in, wrapped in pride and gratitude and twenty-two years of *he saved your life* — and underneath it, unmistakable, the shape of the hunt already running: riders on the passes, patrols at Highmoor, watchers counting candles in windows. They didn't need her to find the child. They needed her to make it *official*. To draw the map that led the iron box to the girl's door.

Because the girl had come out of the burn lands. Because she spoke the dead tongue. Because the flames bent toward her when she sang.

Eight months of signs, moving north. Six weeks of concentration. Four days since the light in her window.

The hunt wasn't coming. The hunt had been circling for months, and Casper had simply been standing in its path without knowing, holding the thing it sought in her own kitchen.

"Of course, Guildmaster," she heard herself say, and her voice was steady, and she hated how steady it was, how easily the old training held. "I'll begin in the southern valleys and work north."

"Sensible." Vane rolled up the chart and held it out to her—her own map, annotated with her own quarry. "Report by courier. Weekly. And Casper—" he caught her hand briefly over the table, his grip warm and dry and strong, exactly as it had been the day he pulled her from the snow, "—whatever you find, you bring it to *me*. No one else. There are people at court who'd want this handled less carefully. Promise me."

"I promise," said Casper Lockridge, who had promised the smugglers safe passage last spring, who had promised the guild precision every year for eleven years, who had promised nothing true to anyone in her entire careful life.

* * *

She rode out of Thornfell Keep as the afternoon light went long and coppery, and she did not take the King's Road. She took the drover's track instead, the one that wasn't on any chart she'd ever sold or submitted, winding down through the pine shadow toward her cabin, and all the way down she turned the problem over like a stone in her hands.

Every route ended the same way. Deliver the girl, and betray the one innocent creature who had ever walked into her life asking nothing. Refuse Vane, and make herself the second thing the guild hunted. Flee, and flee where? Every road in these mountains was a road she herself had drawn, sold, or memorized. She had spent a decade making the world legible. Now the world's legibility was a net closing around her.

By the time the cabin roofline showed through the trees, dusk had settled, and Casper had made one decision, small and enormous: she was not reporting by courier. Not yet. There was one group of people in these mountains who might know what the child was—who had songs and grandmothers and reasons of their own to hate the Crown's maps.

Bren the ferryman owed her favors, and she owed him one back, and between them lay thirty miles of river road and a conversation that would amount to treason.

She reined in at the cabin door and listened. No candlelight showed in the window—but the girl was supposed to keep it lit. Casper's hand went to her knife before her mind caught up, and she pushed the door open onto darkness.

Then the dark *sang*—one low note, wordless, and every candle stub in the room flared alight at once, and the girl stepped out of the shadowed corner with Casper's spare cloak around her shoulders, watching her with those lichen-colored eyes.

On the table, laid out neat and deliberate, sat the girl's only possession: a small carved token of pale wood, shaped like a leaf, from a tree that had been extinct for a hundred years.

It was pointing south. Downriver. Toward Bren's ferry.

As if the child already knew exactly whom Casper needed to find.
