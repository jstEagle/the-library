## 3. What the Smugglers Know

The river knew the way to Bren's ferry even when Casper didn't want it to. She'd spent ten years making herself a woman of roads, and the Greywater had always been the truest of them—unmapped on any chart the Crown held, unwatched by any rider, because no one in Thornfell Keep could imagine that the kingdom's arteries ran through mud and reeds and a man with one good eye.

She brought the child down through the alder stands at dusk, when the light went copper and long and the midges rose in columns over the water. The girl walked without complaint. That was the worst of her, some nights—she never complained, never wept, only watched everything with those grey eyes like rain standing still in the air, and spoke in that tongue that made the candles lean.

Casper had stopped lighting candles.

Three days now since the storm had delivered the child to her door, barefoot and soaked and speaking words older than the stones of Thornfell Keep. Three days of Casper lying awake listening to the girl murmur in her sleep, feeling the words move through the cabin like current through water, bending flame, stirring ash, making the frost on the window crawl into shapes that looked, if she let herself look too long, like leaves. Three days of telling herself she would take the girl down to the garrison at Miller's Ford and be rid of her.

Instead she'd packed bread and dried apples, shouldered her survey satchel, and taken the child toward the river.

Because there was one thing Casper Lockridge had learned in ten years of selling safe passage to men who trusted nothing: when you couldn't read a road yourself, you found someone whose grandmother could.

* * *

Bren's ferry sat moored at the crook where the Greywater bent south, a flat-bottomed barge with a tarred canopy and a cookfire burning in an iron ring on deck. The smell hit Casper first—onion, fish, woodsmoke, wet rope—and something in her chest unclenched for the first time in three days. It smelled like the only kind of home she'd ever had, which was the borrowed kind.

Bren saw them coming from the tiller and didn't reach for the crossbow leaning against the rail, which was its own kind of welcome. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, grey in his beard, missing two fingers on his left hand from a toll-gate that had closed faster than advertised. His remaining eye found the child immediately and stayed on her.

"Yara," he said quietly. "That's not cargo."

"No."

"That's trouble wearing a small coat."

"Yes."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he turned and bellowed up the bank, "Marta! Kill the fire or feed it, we've guests!" and just like that, the decision was made, because with smugglers decisions were never spoken aloud—they were simply cooked into supper.

The crew came down the bank in ones and twos. Marta first, broad-shouldered, flour to the elbows, who'd run contraband salt through three kingdoms before her twentieth winter. Then the twins, Osk and Ossa, all elbows and grins, and old Tam with his bad hip, and last young Piet, sixteen and trying to grow a beard that hadn't committed yet. They were seven souls total, and they were, though Casper would have gutted anyone who said it aloud, the closest thing to family she possessed.

They gathered around the cookfire, and the child stood pressed against Casper's side, watching them all with those still grey eyes. The fire did not bend toward her here—or rather, it did, but so gently that only Casper noticed, flames leaning a hair's width eastward as if listening.

"She speaks?" Marta asked.

"Not our tongue." Casper hesitated. She had rehearsed this crossing the alder flats and every rehearsal had failed, because the truth was a road she'd never surveyed. "She speaks a tongue I don't know either. But I think—I think some of you might."

Silence, except the river.

"Say it plain," Bren said.

"The Weald-tongue."

Old Tam spat into the fire. The hiss was very loud. "Woman," he said, "that word hasn't crossed this water since the burnings. My grandmother sang lullabies in it, and my grandfather told her if the wrong ear caught one note, they'd hang him from the mill beam and make her watch." He looked at the child, and his weathered face did something complicated. "Where'd you find her?"

"She found me. Storm night. At my door."

"And the guild?"

"Not yet. But they will be."

Bren rubbed his jaw. "You're asking us to harbor a Weald-child while Crown riders comb the passes. You know what that is."

"Treason," said Tam.

"Supper," said Marta, and ladled out bowls.

* * *

It happened after supper, the way important things do.

The crew had settled into their evening rituals—Osk and Ossa arguing over dice, Piet mending net, Tam smoking his pipe with the ceremony of a man performing a sacrament—and the child had crept close to the fire, drawn the way children draw to warmth everywhere and always. And then, softly, half to herself, she began to sing.

The fire leaned.

Not flickered—leaned, every flame bending toward her like grass under wind, and the sparks rising from the iron ring drifted not upward but inward, circling her slow as gnats over a pond. Casper felt the hairs rise on her arms. On the far side of the fire, Tam had gone absolutely rigid, pipe halfway to his mouth.

The song was low and winding, syllables falling like water over stones, and Casper understood none of it and yet her chest ached as if she understood all of it—as if the melody were doing the translating, bypassing language entirely and going straight to whatever part of a person remembers being small and safe.

Tam's lips moved. Silently at first. Then, barely voiced, cracked and rusty as a gate in a house nobody lived in anymore, he sang along—one line, two, three—and his eyes were streaming and he wasn't even aware of it.

The child stopped. Turned. Looked at him.

And said, clearly, in words that came out shaped like Tam's own accent, roughened by his cadence, as if she'd reached into his mouth and borrowed his voice: *"Hollow."*

One word. Translated. Given back.

Nobody breathed.

"Hollow," Tam repeated, staring at her. "She said—the song, it's the Lament of the Root, my gran sang it when—" He stopped, pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "The last verse. The singers go down to the Hollow, where the spirit sleeps beneath the spring, and they seal it in with their voices so the burning can't reach it. That's what that verse is. It's not a sad song. It's instructions."

"A map," Casper said, before she could stop herself, and heard her whole life in the word.

"A map," Tam agreed. "In song."

Marta had come to stand beside Bren at the rail. "Songs their grandmothers were forbidden to sing," she murmured. "Half these crews carry fragments and don't know it. A verse here, a refrain there, scattered down the families like seed grain saved through a famine." She looked at Casper. "You knew. You came here because you knew we'd have pieces she needs."

"I came here because I didn't know where else to go," Casper said. It was as close to honesty as she got, and it cost more than she expected. "I came here because you're the only people alive who'd take her in before they asked what she was worth."

Bren grunted. He crouched by the fire across from the child, slow, the way you approach a deer or a debt collector, and studied her. She studied him back, unblinking.

"You know what happens if the guild finds her on this boat," he said—not to her, to Casper. "You know what happens to us. Not hanging. Worse. The guild doesn't hang smugglers who touch forest-things. They burn them, same as they burned the Weald. Make examples."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm not asking." Casper heard her own voice come out level and cold and was surprised by it. "I'm telling you what's coming, so you can cast off and be elsewhere when it does. That's the fair trade. I've never asked any of you for anything but distance, and distance has kept you rich and breathing. Take the distance."

The silence stretched. The child reached out and touched Tam's knee, once, lightly, the way you'd steady something wobbling.

"Gran's name was Ede," Tam said suddenly, to no one, to everyone. "Ede Rootsong, folk called her, joking-like, before the jokes about roots got dangerous. She used to say the Weald wouldn't die while one throat remembered it." He looked up at Casper, and the tears were dry now and his eyes were hard as river stone. "I'm not a brave man, Yara. Never been. But I'll tell you this for free: if the Crown burned a whole forest to kill one sleeping spirit, and now the spirit's sent a child to a smuggler's door—then the Crown is frightened. And frightened crowns pay well for courage, but they pay better for silence, and neither payment keeps a man warm at night."

"So you're in," Marta translated.

"I'm in," Tam said. "God help us."

"Then we're all in," Bren said heavily, "because Tam's the only one of us who can't swim, and nobody lets Tam drown alone." He stood, joints cracking, and pointed his stump of two fingers at Casper. "But hear me, Yara Lockridge. This isn't smuggling. There's no price on this run. Whatever comes of that child, we share it—prison, pyre, or miracle. You don't get to carry her alone anymore. That's how people like us die: alone, being noble."

Something moved through Casper's chest that she had no chart for—a pulling sensation, like a current taking hold of a hull. Ten years she'd kept every soul at oar's length, useful to everyone, loyal to no one, invisible and therefore alive. And these mud-footed criminals had just, in the space of a bowl of stew, made her visible.

"All right," she managed.

"Good." Bren turned to business as if nothing enormous had occurred. "Guild riders are already sweeping the high passes—we passed word along the water today, three patrols between here and Miller's Ford. If they're hunting a forest-sign, they'll work the rivers eventually; water's how signs travel." He squinted downstream into the dark. "Which means we move her tomorrow, before dawn, up the old towpath. And before you ask—no, Yara, that road doesn't go on anything you draw. Some things stay in mouths, not ink."

The child tugged Tam's sleeve. When he looked down, she pointed at the dark mass of the southern hills, where the dead Weald lay beyond the ridgeline, black against the last grey of the sky. Then she said the word again, patient as a teacher, in her borrowed voice and her own strange voice both at once:

*"Hollow."*

And every ember in the fire ring flared bright green for a single heartbeat, like moss, like spring, like a memory of a color, and died back to ordinary orange.

Downriver, faint but unmistakable in the still air, came the sound of hoofbeats on the towpath. Coming north.

Coming fast.
