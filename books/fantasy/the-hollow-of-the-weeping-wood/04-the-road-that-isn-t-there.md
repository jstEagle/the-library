## 4. The Road That Isn't There

The riders came through the pass at dawn, and Casper watched them from the ridge above her cabin with a spyglass she'd bought from a dead man's estate and never once regretted.

Six of them. Guild grey, not Crown red, which meant Vane had sent his own. They moved the way guild riders always moved—too neat, too confident, horses shod for paved roads and hating every stone of the Thornfell scree. She counted their packhorses. Two extra. Room for cargo.

Or for a child.

Beside her, in the shadow of the cabin door, the girl stood very still. She had learned in three days that stillness was survival; Casper hadn't taught her that. The child simply knew it, the way she knew things no one should know—the way she'd known, on her first night, to press her palm flat against the floorboards before the earthquake came, hours before the ground remembered how to shake.

"They're looking," Casper said, mostly to herself.

The girl looked up at her. Then she raised one small hand and pointed—not at the riders, but at the smoke rising thin from Casper's own chimney. Casper had banked the fire at first light. She'd been careful. She was always careful.

But the wind had turned in the night, and the smoke had told on her anyway.

* * *

She'd left the cabin looking like a cabin someone had merely stepped out of. Fire damped, kettle on the hook, her good survey coat hung by the door where any searcher would see it and conclude she'd be back. The girl she wrapped in Bren's oldest wool cloak, hood up, face down, and carried out through the root cellar into the rocks behind the house, where a goat track wound up toward the high shoulder of Mount Cael.

Casper had mapped every official road in the Thornfells twice over. She knew them the way a priest knows scripture—which roads the Crown patrolled, which ones the guild watched, which passes froze first and thawed last. And because she knew them so well, she also knew what she had never drawn: the other roads. The smugglers' roads. The paths that existed only in the memory of people who could hang for using them.

There were three ways south out of the high country that appeared on no chart bearing the guild seal. One ran along the drowned riverbed below Kestrel Falls, usable three weeks a year. One crossed the salt flats, too exposed for anything but desperation. And one—the one she chose now—was the Hollow Road, though no one called it that anymore except Bren, who'd named it as a joke and then stopped laughing when he realized the name fit.

It began at a cairn that wasn't a cairn, two miles east of her cabin, and it followed a shelf of rock along the mountain's flank, narrow enough that a loaded mule had to walk it sideways. It dropped through a fold in the hills that didn't look like a fold unless you'd walked it in fog, and it came out in the birch scrub above the ferry crossing, forty miles from anywhere the guild thought worth watching.

Casper had never inked it. Not on her private charts, not on the working drafts, not even in the coded marginalia she used for the smugglers' fees. The road existed only in her feet and her head, and in the heads of perhaps six other living people.

That was why she'd chosen it. That was why, when she crested the last rise above the birch scrub on the second evening and saw fresh hoofprints pressed into the mud of the hidden path—shod hooves, guild-shod, moving *south*—she felt something in her chest go cold and quiet, like a candle pinched out.

* * *

She made camp that night in a hollow beneath a leaning granite slab, built no fire, and fed the girl dried plums and the last of the smoked trout while she herself ate nothing. The child watched her with those old, old eyes and chewed slowly, and after a while she reached out and touched Casper's wrist, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a fence post to see if it was real.

"I'm fine," Casper lied.

The girl tilted her head. In the dark her eyes caught what little light there was and held it, faintly green, like moss on a north-facing stone. She pointed at Casper's chest. Then she made a shape with both hands—a box, closed—and pressed her lips together.

*I know,* the gesture said. *You have something shut inside you.*

"Stop that," Casper said, without heat. "Eat your plums."

But she lay awake most of the night afterward, staring at the underside of the granite, turning the hoofprints over and over in her mind.

Guild riders on the Hollow Road. It was impossible. It was *impossible*, unless—

Unless someone had drawn it.

And only three people alive could have. Three people had ever seen her private charts: the master copies, the ones she kept locked in the survey chest, the ones with the uninked roads marked in her own cipher, pencil-faint, meant to be erased before any eyes but hers fell on them.

Vane. Who had saved her life at nineteen, pulled her half-drowned out of the Whitemere rapids, given her a trade and a name in the guild rolls and a reason to keep living that wasn't just spite. Who had taught her that a map was a promise, and that the cartographer's first duty was honesty.

Tam Weller, her junior surveyor, who drank too much and worshipped her too openly and couldn't keep a secret from his own reflection.

And Ilsa. Young Ilsa Merren, sixteen years old, bastard-born, sharp as a chip of flint, who copied charts in the guild scriptorium with a precision that made Casper feel, for the first time in ten years, like her own work might be surpassed someday. Ilsa, who had asked her once, shyly, over ink pots: *How do you decide what goes on the map and what doesn't?*

And Casper had answered, because she'd liked the girl, because she'd seen something of her younger self in that hungry, careful face: *A map is a weapon. You decide who you're pointing it at.*

She'd said it as a lesson. Now she lay under cold granite wondering if she'd handed a knife to a stranger.

* * *

They reached the ferry crossing at midday on the third day, and Bren was waiting for them, which meant either that he was very good or that word traveled faster than she did.

"The riders came through two nights past," he said, low, poling the flat ferry across the brown water while the girl sat between them with her hands folded in her lap like a small duchess. "Four of 'em. Asked after a child. Showed a sketch." He glanced back at Casper. "Good likeness."

Casper's stomach dropped. A likeness meant a witness. A witness meant someone had seen the girl—at her cabin, on the road, somewhere—and reported it. Which meant the hunt wasn't guessing anymore. It was tracking.

"Bren." She kept her voice level. "The Hollow Road. Someone rode it this week. Guild-shod horses, heading south ahead of me."

His pole stilled in the water. For a long moment the only sound was the river worrying the hull.

"That road's got maybe eight heads that hold it," he said finally. "Yours. Mine. My sister's. The Corvane brothers. Old Marta, and she's dead these two years, so seven." He looked at her sideways. "And whoever read your book."

"My book is locked."

"Locked books get opened, Casper. You know that better than anyone. You've opened enough of other folks'." He pushed off from the far bank and poled them into the reeds below the willow stand. "Question isn't whether your charts leaked. Question's who wanted gold bad enough, or wanted somethin' else bad enough."

Something else. Casper climbed out onto the muddy bank and stood a moment among the willows, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the ridge.

Vane wouldn't sell her charts. He wouldn't need to—he could order them surrendered with a word, and she'd have obeyed him, once, gladly. Tam couldn't keep his mouth shut about ale debts, but a ciphered chart? He'd sooner sell his teeth.

Ilsa, then. Ilsa, who copied everything beautifully. Ilsa, who had a name that would never appear on a guild roll, never be spoken in the Keep's great hall, never be anything but *the Merren girl*—unless someone with power promised otherwise.

Had Vane found her? Had he gone fishing among the copyists for the one thing Casper guarded best, knowing that hunger made the finest tracing paper?

Or worse—had Ilsa done it for nothing crueler than ambition, tracing the uninked roads onto her own paper because Casper herself had taught her that maps were weapons, and weapons were meant to be *used*?

The girl tugged Casper's sleeve. She pointed downstream, toward the low hills beyond the ferry landing, where the land went soft and grey and forested—the last fringe of the old Weald, dead these hundred years, its burned stumps still standing like gravestones for a mile in every direction.

Then she touched her own chest. And for the first time since Casper had known her, the child's face changed—not wary, not watchful, but *afraid*. She pointed again, harder, at the dead wood, and then she spoke, one syllable in that buried language, and the willow leaves around them trembled though there was no wind.

Bren went pale. "What did she say?"

"She didn't say it to us," Casper said quietly. She took the girl's hand. It was cold. "She said it to *them*."

Downstream, faint on the wind, came a sound Casper had heard only once before in her life, on the worst day of her worst survey: the creak of saddle leather. Many saddles. Coming up the river road.

She looked at Bren. Bren looked at the treeline, at the dead forest standing sentinel over the water, and then back at her, and she saw the exact moment the smuggler in him lost the argument to the man.

"There's an old mine shrine," he said. "Two hours in, off the corpse-road, under the roots of the biggest black oak left standing. My grandmother's people hid there, back in the burning." He swallowed. "Nobody's prayed there since. But the walls remember how."

The girl's hand tightened around Casper's fingers.

Behind them, on the river road, the hoofbeats grew louder.
