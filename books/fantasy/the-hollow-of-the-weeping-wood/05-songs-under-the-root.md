## 5. Songs Under the Root

The mine had no name anymore. The men who'd dug it had called it something in the old tongue—Bren's grandmother had sung it once, when he was small enough to sit under her loom and out of his father's sight—but the Crown's surveyors had struck it from every record a generation back, and names, like people, died faster when nobody wrote them down.

He led Casper and the girl down the collapsed cart-track anyway, past the rotted timber of the adit, into the first gallery where the mountain closed over them like a hand. His lantern threw their shadows huge against the rock. Somewhere ahead, water dripped with the patience of stone.

"Smugglers used this before the war," Casper said behind him. Her voice did what it always did—stayed flat, stayed small, a voice built for not being noticed. "It's not on any chart I've drawn."

"No," Bren said. "It wouldn't be."

The shrine was three galleries in, where the tunnel widened into a chamber the miners must have cut deliberately, because it made no sense for ore. Someone had carved a tree into the far wall—a great spreading thing, roots going down into the floor and branches up into the ceiling until the stone itself seemed to grow them. Generations of hands had polished the bark smooth. At its base, someone long dead had set niches for candles, and though the candles were dust, the wax stains still held the shape of a hundred years of small fires.

Bren set his lantern there, in the oldest stain, because that was where it belonged.

The girl stood very still, looking at the carved tree. She hadn't spoken since the ferry. She didn't speak now. But she lifted her hand and laid her palm flat against the stone, fingers spread, the way you'd touch a door to feel whether someone was home behind it.

Casper watched her do it. Bren watched Casper watch, and saw the mapmaker's face do something maps couldn't record.

"She knows this place," Casper said quietly.

"She's never been here." Bren shrugged off his pack and started unpacking the bread and hard cheese he'd wrapped at the ferry landing. "None of us have. My gran came once, when she was a girl, before they sealed the shafts. But knowing isn't about being somewhere, is it? That's what she's teaching me, this one." He nodded at the child. "Knowing is about being *from* somewhere. Even if the somewhere's burned."

* * *

They ate without fire. Casper had argued for one—cold food, cold stone, cold child—and Bren had said no, and for once she hadn't pressed him. He suspected she understood better than she let on: smoke rose, and riders watched the ridgelines, and the guild's men could read a chimney's ghost the way Bren read water.

The girl ate like the starving eat, fast and ashamed of it, and then, fed, she simply folded—down onto the bedroll Bren's sister Maren had packed, curled small as a fern frond, asleep between one breath and the next. Children slept like that only when they trusted the people around them, or when they were too exhausted to do anything else. Bren chose to believe the first.

For a while he and Casper sat in the lantern light saying nothing. It was a comfortable nothing. Ten years he'd ferried her cargo across the Thornfell river, ten years of packages that never went on manifests and passengers who never gave names, and they'd built their whole friendship out of silences like this one. You didn't ask Casper Lockridge questions. You left gaps, and sometimes she filled them.

Tonight she didn't fill them. Tonight she sat with her elbows on her knees and looked at the sleeping girl and then at the carved tree, and finally she said, "Your grandmother. The songs. You said the crew knew fragments. From where?"

Bren turned his cheese rind over in his fingers. He'd known this question was coming since the night the girl had first opened her mouth on his ferry deck and the words that fell out had made old Tam drop an oar into the water.

"You know what my family does," he said. "What we've done, four generations. We move what can't be moved and carry what can't be carried. Tax-stamped brandy. Deserters. Letters that would hang the men who wrote them." He paused. "And songs."

"Songs."

"My gran's mother was Wealdish. Not Wealdish the way the Crown means it—not a border farmer who happened to be on the wrong side of a treaty line. *Wealdish.* Her people lived inside the forest, in the deep stands, the places your maps show as blank." He nodded at the wall, at the carved tree with its roots going down forever. "When the kingdoms burned the Weald, they burned the trees first. Took them two years. But trees are only wood. What they were really burning was the tongue, because the tongue was how the forest kept itself. Every song was a root, every story a seed. Kill the language and the wood stays dead even if a sapling comes up, because nothing's left to tell the sapling what it's for."

Casper had gone very still. She did that—went still the way water goes still, everything underneath still moving.

"So they forbade it," she said.

"They did worse than forbid it. Forbidding just drives a thing underground, and underground things grow strange and strong." He heard his own voice roughen and let it. "They took the children. That was the king's edict—every child of the forest peoples to be fostered in loyal households, raised right, taught proper. My great-grandmother's daughter was taken that way. Never came home. And my great-grandmother—" He stopped. Started again. "She decided that whatever they took, they wouldn't take *all* of it. So she hid the tongue inside the safe things. Lullabies, because nobody examines a lullaby. Work chants. The blessing we say over the dead. Words that sounded like nonsense syllables, like boat-rhythm, like nothing. She taught them to my gran as music, and my gran taught them to my mother as music, and my mother taught them to me as music, and told me—when I was twelve, the same night she told me what our family really ferries—that they weren't music. That they were a garden planted in a cellar, waiting for the sun."

The lantern flame leaned. Just slightly, just toward the bedroll, the way candle flames had bent toward the girl since the first night—the way flames bent toward her now even in sleep, all of them in the chamber tilting a degree off plumb, as if the dark itself had begun to listen.

Casper noticed. Of course she noticed; she noticed everything. She said nothing about it.

"The smugglers' songs," she said instead. "Tam's grandmother's songs. They're the same garden."

"Different cellars. Same gardener's line." Bren smiled, and it hurt to smile. "Half the river families have it and don't know. That was the genius of it. You can't burn a song out of a thousand kitchens. You can't arrest a lullaby. The Weald's people scattered along the waterways like seed on wind, and everywhere they landed they planted a little of the tongue in the only soil they had left—their children's ears. Waiting." He looked at the girl. "Waiting for somebody who could hear it whole."

* * *

She began to dream aloud sometime past midnight.

Not words, at first. Sounds—a low rising hum, like wind finding a valley, like the note a wet finger draws from a bowl's rim. Then the sounds found shapes, and the shapes were the old tongue, flowing soft and unhurried, a child telling her dream to no one, or to everyone, or to the mountain.

Bren understood perhaps one word in twenty. But the twenty were enough to make his skin rise.

*Root,* she murmured. *Under. Green. Wait.*

And then a word he knew entire, because his mother had sung it over his grandfather's grave: *hollow*—but not hollow the way the Crown meant it, not empty. Hollow the way a bell is hollow. Hollow the way a cup is hollow. Made-empty-on-purpose so that something could be poured in.

"Bren." Casper's voice, barely a breath. "Look at the walls."

He looked.

Moss was climbing the carved tree.

Not creeping—climbing, fast as spilled water, green flooding up through the grooves of the carving, filling the bark-lines the old miners had cut, so that the tree on the wall grew living growth the way a wick grows flame. It spread outward from the tree across the bare stone in slow fractal branches, and where it spread, the mine's hundred-year darkness turned soft and breathing. The air changed too. It smelled suddenly of rain on warm earth, of spring, of a world that had never heard of axes.

The girl slept on, murmuring, her small hands curled at her chin, and the moss reached the ceiling and put out tiny pale flowers no one could name.

Casper got up. Crossed the chamber. Knelt—not touching the girl, never touching, but near—and watched her the way Bren had seen her study terrain: reading, measuring, afraid of what the measurements said.

"It's not a vessel," Casper whispered. "Vessels hold things. She's—" He watched her reach for the cartographer's word and fail to find one. "She's a *source*. Everything flows out of her."

"Aye." Bren's throat was tight. "My gran used to end the hidden songs with a line we thought was just a closing verse. *Sleep, and the wood remembers you.*" He looked at the flowering ceiling, at the impossible green, at the child dreaming the forest back into the bones of the mountain. "I think we had it backwards. I think the wood's been sleeping, and she remembers *it*."

Casper knelt there a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and small again, the not-noticed voice, but Bren had ten years of practice hearing what was under it.

"The guild's reports called it forest signs. Movement in dead timber. Sap where no sap should be." She didn't look at him. "I'm supposed to find the source of those signs and report it. That was the summons. That was Aldous asking me himself."

"And?"

"And I rode back to my cabin and lied to the most dangerous man I know, and then I brought her to you." Now she looked at him, and her eyes in the lantern light were the eyes of a woman standing at the edge of a bridge she'd already burned behind her. "So I need to know, Bren. Your family kept the tongue alive for a hundred years in cellars and lullabies. You're the ones who wait. Tell me what the waiting is *for*. Because if I give her up, I want to at least know what I killed. And if I don't—" her jaw tightened— "if I don't, I want to know what I'm carrying her toward."

Bren was quiet for a while. The moss flowers opened, one by one, pale as moths.

"I'll tell you what my gran told me," he said finally. "There's a place in the deep Weald. The songs call it the Hollow—not empty, hallowed, though the Crown's scholars wrote it down wrong and their wrong stuck. The heart of the wood. Where the last of the old spirit went to sleep when the burning came, because a spirit can't die while anything still speaks its name. And the songs say—" he swallowed— "the songs say it doesn't wake by itself. Something has to carry the memory home to it. Something young enough to still believe the words mean what they mean."

Outside, far above them, faint through a hundred fathoms of stone, came a sound neither of them could mistake: hoofbeats. Many. Moving along the ridge road in the dark.

Casper was on her feet before the sound faded, snuffing the lantern, and in the sudden black the moss glowed faintly green, and the girl slept on, dreaming, and the mountain listened.
