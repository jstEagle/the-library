## 6. Vane's Bargain

They moved before dawn, three carts and a mule train, down a road that existed only in Bren's memory and Casper's silence. By the time the sun cleared the Thornfell peaks they were two valleys west of the mine shrine, hidden in the root-cellar of a ruined shepherd's cot that had not sheltered sheep in forty years. The girl slept against Bren's coat like something small and stubborn that had decided, provisionally, to trust them.

Casper did not sleep. She sat at the mouth of the cellar with her back against cold stone and watched the pass below, where the morning fog lay like milk poured over the world.

She was still watching it when she saw him come up the track alone.

No escort. No guild colors. Just one rider on a grey mare, unhurried, his hands open and visible on the pommel the way he'd taught her to approach strangers twenty years ago—*show them your empty hands first, Lockridge; everything else you show after.* He stopped thirty paces out, dismounted, tied the mare to a dead rowan, and stood there in the fog with his breath steaming, waiting.

Aldous Vane had saved her life when she was seventeen, fished her half-drowned out of the Whitefall gorge with a survey rope and no questions asked. He had taught her everything: how to hold a chain-line true, how to read a ridge from its shadow, how to be so quiet and so useful that no one ever thought to look closely at what else she might be. He was sixty now, grey at the temples, broad through the shoulders still, with the patient eyes of a man who had spent his whole career measuring things that did not want to be measured.

"Master Lockridge," he called softly. "May I?"

"You found me," she said. "That answers most questions."

He climbed down into the cellar without invitation, because he never needed one. His gaze went to the sleeping girl, then back to Casper, and there was no triumph in it, no satisfaction. That was the worst part. He looked tired.

"Ilsa," he said.

Casper felt the name go through her like a splinter finding bone. "I don't know what you mean."

"You don't have to lie to me. You never have." He lowered himself onto an overturned crate, joints cracking, and rubbed his face with both hands. "Three people had access to your private charts. I've known that since the copies surfaced in the guild's archive. I also knew it wasn't you—you don't copy anything by hand anymore, your script went mechanical years ago, which is a flaw I regret teaching you out of, incidentally. And I knew it wasn't me." A ghost of a smile. "Which left a frightened nineteen-year-old who wanted a family name more than she wanted air."

"And you used her."

"I gave her what she asked for. The way I once gave you what *you* asked for—a place in the world, when you had none." He said it without cruelty, and that was the horror of Aldous Vane: he could justify anything, and the justification would always be true enough to stand on. "The child, Casper. Where is she? Here, or somewhere safer?"

Casper looked at the girl. Sleeping. Breathing. In her sleep her lips moved around words no living scholar could parse, and near her feet, in the dark of the cellar, something green was quietly growing along a crack in the stone.

"She's asleep," Casper said.

"So I see." Vane leaned forward, elbows on knees, and spoke very low. "Then let us talk while she can't hear us. Because I'm going to offer you something, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide what I am."

* * *

He laid it out plainly, the way he'd once laid out the Whitefall rescue plan—risks first, always risks first, because Vane believed fear honestly named was fear half-disarmed.

"The harvests failed east of the Weald again," he said. "Third year running. Do you know what that does? It doesn't make men reasonable. It makes them hungry, and hungry men burn things. The Crown is holding the eastern provinces together with garrisons and grain tariffs, and both are failing. There are lords talking openly about secession. There are priests saying the famine is punishment for the burning—the old sin coming home—and half the peasantry believes them, which is worse than secession, because belief doesn't negotiate."

"The war ended eighty years ago."

"The war ended. The *sin* didn't." He gestured vaguely northward, toward the black line of the dead forest on the horizon. "We burned the Weald to win those borders. Every road you've ever mapped runs over its grave. And now a child walks out of nowhere speaking its language, and candle flames bend toward her, and moss grows where she sleeps. You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't."

Casper said nothing.

"The guild's naturalists believe the spirit survived the burning," Vane went on. "Dormant. Rooted somewhere deep—the songs call it the Hollow, if the songs can be trusted. If we can take it—if we can bring it under the Crown's keeping—" he caught himself, corrected, "*under our study*, then the same power that made the Weald bloom across a thousand square miles could be turned outward. Fields that don't fail. Rivers that don't flood. An end to famine, Casper. Not this season. Forever."

"You're describing a cage."

"I'm describing a granary." His voice hardened, just slightly. "You think I don't know what it sounds like? I burned villages in that war's last year—I was young, and I told myself it was duty, and I have spent forty years building maps so that the next war would be fought with ink instead of fire. This is ink, Casper. One child, taken gently, kept safely, studied honestly. Against that: every harvest for the rest of history. Weigh it."

"That's not the weighing that matters to me."

"No?" He looked at her steadily. "Then weigh this. I know you have her. Which means the Crown's warrant names you too, as of yesterday—harboring, obstruction, treason, the full litany. I wrote the warrant. Then I delayed its delivery by four days, which is a crime of my own, and if my clerk talks, I hang beside you." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper, set it on the crate between them, and did not push it toward her. "Full pardon. For you, for the ferryman, for every soul on that mule train. Sealed by my own authority, backdated before the warrant. Protection under guild law—which means the Crown cannot touch any of you without coming through me first. And a stipend for the girl's keep, generous, paid quarterly, whether you stay in the guild's service or vanish tomorrow."

Casper stared at the paper. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone cold.

"And in exchange."

"In exchange you bring her to Thornfell Keep. Alive, unharmed, willing if you can manage it. She'd want for nothing—rooms, physicians, scholars who would spend their lives learning her language instead of erasing it. She'd be the most protected child in three kingdoms." He paused, and then, because Vane never lied to people he respected: "And she would never leave. Not until we understand what she is. Perhaps never."

The cellar was very quiet. The fog outside had begun to lift, and light came down through the broken roof boards in pale bars, and in one of those bars dust drifted, slow as snow.

Casper thought: *he believes it.* That was the terrible thing, the thing that made her chest ache. Aldous Vane was many things—ambitious, ruthless in his tidy bureaucratic way, capable of using a bastard girl's desperation like a crowbar—but he was not a liar, not in the places that counted. He believed the spirit could feed kingdoms. He believed the arithmetic was honest. He believed that one stolen childhood weighed against ten million unfailing harvests was not even a bargain but an obligation, and that refusing it would be the vanity of people who had never watched a granary burn.

And she almost said yes.

That was the part she would carry afterward, the part she would not confess to anyone for a long time: that for eleven heartbeats she stood in the dusty light and *wanted* it. The pardon. The protection. The ending of the looking-over-her-shoulder that had been the whole weather of her life since she was fourteen years old and first learned that being invisible was the only safety a mapmaker could buy. Vane was offering her what she had been selling everyone else for a decade—the safe route out. All she had to do was deliver a sleeping child to a cage and call it mercy.

Eleven heartbeats.

Then the girl stirred in her sleep and murmured something soft and liquid, a word with roots in it, and along the cellar wall the crack of stone filled, silently, with a thread of green moss, and Casper understood that the choice had already been made—not by her, but in her, somewhere deeper than the place where bargains were counted.

"Aldous," she said. "Why did you come alone?"

His jaw tightened. "Because I hoped I wouldn't need the escort."

"And if I refuse?"

He rose from the crate, slowly, and looked down at her, and for a moment she saw the man from the gorge again—the one who had thrown her a rope before asking her name.

"Then I ride back to Thornfell," he said, "and I report that I searched this valley and found nothing. And I will keep reporting nothing for as long as I can. But Casper—" his voice dropped, and there was real grief in it now, plain and unbought— "there are others in the guild who are not me. Magister Corven has his own copies of your charts, and Corven believes the spirit should be taken *now*, while it is weak, and that the vessel is expendable once it's bled of language. When Corven finds her—and he will find her, he's already bought the loyalty of half my riders—it will not be rooms and physicians. It will be a table and knives."

He picked up his hat from the crate. The pardon stayed where it was, lying between them like a coin neither would pick up.

"Bring her in, and I can protect her inside the machine. Keep her out, and you're protecting her from me only until something worse comes down that road." He tipped his head toward the track, the fog, the world beyond it. "Think on which enemy you'd rather she have."

He climbed out of the cellar and untied the grey mare, and Casper stood at the lip of the ruin watching him go, hating him, loving him, understanding him completely, which was somehow the worst of the three.

Behind her, the girl woke, and said—in the old tongue, but with a cadence Casper now almost knew, a rising note like a question, like a warning—

and far off to the north, past the ridgeline, past the passes, the dead trees of the Weeping Wood began, all along their black miles, to shiver.
