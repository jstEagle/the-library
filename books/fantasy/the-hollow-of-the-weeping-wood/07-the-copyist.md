## 7. The Copyist

The copy of Casper's private chart was good.

That was the thing that kept her awake in the loft above Bren's ferry house, the girl sleeping warm against her side, breathing the slow breath of a child who trusted the dark. The forgery was *good*. Not guild-standard—the line weights were wrong for Thornfell's drafting room—but good in a way that meant the hand that made it had studied Casper's work for years. Had learned her shorthand. Her hatching. The particular way she curled a contour around a ridge like a finger hooked over a shoulder.

Three people had ever seen those charts whole: Vane, Ilsa Fenwick, and herself.

Vane wouldn't need to copy them. He'd simply take them. Which left a sixteen-year-old apprentice with ink-stained cuffs and a bastard's name, sitting in the archive at Thornfell Keep, tracing roads into existence that had never been drawn on any Crown map—and could never be allowed to exist on any Crown map.

Casper lay in the dark and did the arithmetic she'd spent ten years refusing to do. If Ilsa had copied the smuggler's road through Graywater Pass, she'd done it from memory or from the one night two winters ago when Casper, drunk on plum brandy and loneliness, had spread her private folio open on the archive table and let the girl marvel at it. *You draw the world like it's alive,* Ilsa had said. *Like it might bite.*

Casper had laughed then. She wasn't laughing now.

Because the guild riders sweeping the passes weren't moving like men following rumor. They were moving like men following *lines*. Roads drawn in a hand they recognized. And there was only one road left unmarked anywhere between here and the Hollow—one road Casper had never committed to paper, not even drunk, not even lonely. The ferry road under the river. Bren's road.

If Ilsa knew about it anyway, she hadn't gotten it from the charts.

She'd have to get it from Casper.

* * *

Casper went back to Thornfell Keep alone, which was either the bravest or the stupidest thing she'd done since the child arrived, and she'd stopped keeping score on that particular ledger years ago. She went in through the postern as the surveyor returning from fieldwork, mud to the knee, pack full of honest readings from the eastern ridges. Nobody looked twice at her. That was the point of Casper Lockridge: nobody ever looked twice.

She found Ilsa where she always found her, in the archive's back alcove, bent over a copying table with a magnifier clipped to her eye and three lamps burning against the perpetual dusk of the windowless room. The girl didn't hear her come in. Of course she didn't; Casper had taught her that trick too—*walk on the outside edge of your foot, weight forward, let the floorboards do your lying for you.*

Ilsa was copying a boundary charter. Innocent work. But beside her elbow, half-tucked under a blotter, was a corner of paper Casper would have known blindfolded by touch alone: her own rag stock, her own iron-gall brown, her own private watermark pressed into the sheet—a tiny thorn, off-center, that she'd begun stamping into her personal stock the year after Vane saved her life, because a woman who survives by being invisible learns to sign her invisibility somehow.

"Leave the blotter," Casper said.

Ilsa went white. Then gray. Then, with a courage Casper hadn't expected and would remember later with something dangerously close to pride, the girl pulled the sheet out and laid it flat on the table, face up, like a confession.

"I can explain."

"You copied the Graywater road."

"And the Kestrel cut, and the ford below Harrow Mill." Ilsa's chin came up. Her hands stayed flat on the table, deliberately visible, the way you hold still when you know someone has a knife. "All of them."

"Why."

"You know why."

"Say it anyway."

Ilsa swallowed. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped out of its defiance into something rawer. "Fenwick isn't my name. You know that. Everyone knows that. *Bastard of the tannery row*, they called me before the guild took me in. Master Vane said—" She stopped. Started again. "He said if I brought him something worth a commendation, he'd petition the heralds. A legitimation. My father's name. He told me who my father is, Casper. He told me, and it's a name that opens doors, and he said all I had to do was copy some old charts."

"Some old charts," Casper repeated softly. "Charts that put riders on a smuggler's road the night we were moving a child the guild wants to burn out of the world."

"I didn't know about any child." It came out fast and true. "I swear it on whatever you want sworn. Vane said the roads were compromised, that smugglers were selling passage to Wealdic sympathizers, that the copies were for internal security. Internal *security*, Casper, that's what he called it. I thought—I thought I was helping clean up the guild. I thought I was finally going to be *somebody* instead of nobody's daughter."

Casper stood very still. The lamps hissed. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the archive floor and passed on, and both of them listened until the sound died.

"You were helping," Casper said at last. "That's what makes it unforgivable. If you'd sold the charts for coin I could hate you cleanly. But you did it because a man with power offered you the one thing you've starved for since birth, and you believed him, because why wouldn't you? Why wouldn't anyone?"

Ilsa's eyes were wet now, and furious about it. "Are you going to turn me in?"

"To whom? Vane?" Casper almost smiled. It felt like an old scar stretching. "No."

"Then what are you going to do?"

What was she going to do. Kill her, was the honest answer, or the answer a smarter woman would arrive at. A dead apprentice couldn't copy anything else. A dead apprentice couldn't tell Vane that Casper had confronted her, couldn't mention the ferry road, couldn't send riders down into the dark under the river where a mute child slept among people who sang forbidden songs. Every survival instinct Casper owned—every instinct that had kept her alive through ten years of double-dealing—stood at attention inside her chest and reported, clearly and coldly: *remove the risk.*

And then Casper looked at the girl properly. Really looked.

Sixteen. Ink to the second knuckle. Shoulders held like someone who'd learned early that standing small invited kicking. That hungry, watchful way of looking at a person's hands instead of their face, because faces lie but hands tell you what's coming. Casper knew that posture from the inside. She'd worn it at fifteen, in a survey camp full of men who thought a girl with no family name was a resource rather than a person, and she'd been saved—not by mercy, not by justice, but by Aldous Vane deciding she was useful, which is a different thing entirely and takes years to learn to tell apart.

Ilsa was being made useful. Casper knew exactly how this story ended, because she'd read it in her own life for twenty years and never once found a page where it turned out well.

"No," Casper said aloud, answering the question and the instinct together. "I'm not going to do what he'd expect me to do."

"What—"

"Listen carefully, because I'm only saying this once." Casper leaned across the table, close enough to see her own reflection doubled in the girl's eyes. "In three days, maybe four, Aldous Vane is going to stop needing you. He'll get what he wants or he won't, and either way, the moment a bastard apprentice becomes a liability instead of a ladder, he will drop you so fast you'll hear the wind. He's done it before. Ask him someday about a surveyor named Wren. No—don't ask. He'll lie beautifully."

"But the name—"

"There is no name coming, Ilsa. There was never going to be a name. He showed you a door and it's painted on the wall." She watched it land, watched the girl flinch like something struck. "I'm sorry. I'd rather you hate me now than thank me never."

Ilsa sat down slowly on the copying stool, as though her bones had been quietly removed. For a long moment she said nothing. Then, in a voice scraped nearly hollow: "What did you give up to become what you are?"

"Everything," Casper said. "That's the lesson. Write it somewhere he'll never find it."

She straightened. Took the copied charts off the table, folded them once, and fed them into the lamp flame, holding each until the fire licked her fingers before letting the last corner go. Ash drifted down onto the boundary charter like black snow.

"The ferry road," Ilsa said suddenly. "There's a road under the river. Isn't there. That's why you're here—you're checking whether I know about it."

Casper said nothing, which was an answer.

"I haven't copied it," Ilsa whispered. "I don't know where it goes. But Vane asked me last week if I'd ever seen charts of the lowlands east of the mill. He's looking for it, Casper. He's already looking."

The cold arrived in Casper's spine on schedule, patient and total. She nodded once, filed it, turned for the door.

"Casper." The girl was on her feet. "Why are you letting me go?"

Casper paused with her hand on the frame, in the lamplight's failing reach, and gave her the only truth she had that was worth carrying.

"Because someone once decided I was more useful alive than dead, and I've spent ten years paying interest on that decision. Consider yours paid." She stepped into the corridor's dark. "And because mercy is the one thing I've never put on a map. Seemed time I drew something new."

Behind her, faintly, she heard Isla whisper, *Wren,* testing the name of another ghost, learning how heavy names got.

* * *

The snow started an hour past midnight, fat and windless, the kind of fall that erased roads as it landed. Casper stood at the ferry house window watching it bury the landing stage and thought: *a gift.* Snow like this bought days. Riders lost tracks; riders lost nerve; the river road under the ice became the only road in the world, and only Bren's crew could read it.

Then she saw the light.

A lantern, far up the far bank, bobbing along the ridge trail—the ridge trail that ended nowhere, served nothing, and existed on no map Casper had ever drawn or permitted to exist. Someone was walking it anyway, unhurried, sure-footed, following a line through the dark as though the line were written down somewhere and he'd read every word.

She didn't need the light to know who walked like that. She'd have known the stride across a burning field.

Vane was already on his way down to the water. And behind his lantern, small and reluctant, moved a second figure—too slight for a rider, too steady for a prisoner, walking with ink-stained hands jammed deep in a coat pocket.

Casper's mercy, walking toward her through the snow, with the man who'd taught them both everything they knew.

Downstairs, the child woke and began, softly, to speak.
