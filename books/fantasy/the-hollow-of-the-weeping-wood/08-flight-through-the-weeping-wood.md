## 8. Flight Through the Weeping Wood

The cart left the smugglers' camp an hour past midnight, when even the moon seemed to be looking elsewhere.

Casper drove. Bren sat beside her with a crossbow across his knees and his youngest brother, Tam, wedged between them for warmth. In the bed of the cart, under oilcloth and sacks of dried apples, the child slept—or did not sleep; it was hard to tell with her anymore. Her eyes had taken to staying half-open in the dark, and sometimes Casper caught her watching the stars as though reading them.

Behind them rode four more of Bren's people on horses too thin to be worth stealing, which was exactly why they'd survived this long. Behind *them*, somewhere down in the black folds of the Thornfell passes, were guild riders. And behind the guild riders, patient as winter, was Aldous Vane.

"You're sure about this road," Bren said. Not a question. A man asking the dark to be kind.

"No road goes where we're going," Casper said. "That's the point."

She meant it literally. There was no road into the Weeping Wood—hadn't been for a hundred years. The Crown had burned every track, felled every bridge, salted the earth along the borders so thoroughly that the maps she'd inherited from her predecessor showed the forest as a single gray stain labeled in fading ink: *STERILE. UNPRODUCTIVE. FORBIDDEN.* The cartographers' guild had never once sent a surveyor inside. Why measure a corpse?

But corpses could still be crossed, if you didn't mind what touched your boots on the way.

The old drover's path began where the last of the grazing land gave up—a seam of bare rock that no shepherd would bother climbing—and narrowed into something only goats and desperate men would call a trail. Casper took it slow, one hand on the brake lever, listening to the cart's axle complain. She had walked this route twice in her mapping years and never inked it, never spoke of it, never let herself think of it while anyone was near. It was hers the way a heartbeat is yours: private, essential, invisible.

Now she was spending it like coin. Everything was being spent.

"Light behind us," Tam said suddenly, twisting around.

Casper looked back. Far below, threading through a valley like a lit fuse, torches. Six points of flame. Maybe eight. Moving fast, and moving *together*, which told her these weren't smugglers or border reivers. Guild riders drilled in formation even at night.

"They've found the path," she said quietly.

"They've found *a* path." Bren cocked the crossbow with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "Question is whether they know where it goes."

"It goes where we go. So it doesn't matter."

Ahead, the first dead trees rose out of the dark like burned pillars holding up nothing.

* * *

Everyone in the borderlands knew the story, though nobody told it above a whisper anymore. A century ago the Weald had covered the whole northern shoulder of the mountains, and the people of the Weald had spoken a tongue older than any kingdom's charter. The Crown had wanted the timber, then the land, then simply the winning. The war lasted eleven years. The burning of the forest itself lasted three days. Old songs said the smoke was visible from the capital, two hundred leagues south, and that the king declared a feast day that his own priests refused to bless.

What remained was a hundred square miles of standing deadwood—trunks charred black but never fallen, as if the fire had killed them without the courtesy of knocking them over. Nothing grew there. Grass wouldn't take root at the edges. Birds flew around it. Even the smugglers, who feared nothing that bled, gave the Weeping Wood a wide berth and paid extra tolls to those who'd carry goods around it rather than through.

Casper had skirted its western edge a dozen times. She had never entered.

Until now, with torchlight blooming behind her like an infection.

"Keep the child's face uncovered," she said over her shoulder.

Bren glanced at her. "Thought we were hiding her."

"From them, yes. From—" She didn't have the word. "Whatever lives in this place now. She's not cargo, Bren. If anything in here remembers what was done to it, I want it to see *her*, not oilcloth."

He held her gaze for a moment, then reached back and pulled the covering away from the girl. The child blinked up at the dead trees sliding past overhead, and Casper would have sworn—would swear it later, under oath if anyone had dared ask—that the girl smiled.

The first tree moved when they were thirty feet from it.

There was no wind. Casper knew this because she had spent ten years learning to read wind the way priests read scripture; it was the first thing she checked each morning and the last each night. There was no wind, and yet the great black trunk shuddered, a long creaking groan rolling down its height like a door opening in an empty house, and its branches—bare, brittle, dead a century—lifted. Just slightly. Just enough.

Then the sap came.

It ran down the charred bark in slow silver threads, catching the starlight, and Casper heard Tam make a small strangled noise beside her. The drops fell from the low branches onto the cart bed, onto the oilcloth, onto the child's outstretched hand, and where they landed they did not run off. They sank in. Her skin drank them.

"It's weeping," the child said.

Not in the old tongue. In plain Border speech, flat and clear, the way a person states a fact they find unremarkable. Casper's hands went tight on the reins.

"Can you understand me?" she asked.

The girl tilted her head, considering, the way she considered everything—as though language were a garment she kept trying on. "Understand is a small word," she said. "I remember you. You drew the ground before it was cut."

"I drew maps."

"You drew *wounds*," the girl said, "and called them clean lines. But you left some places blank. Blank is a kind of mercy. That's why the wood lets you pass."

More trees were waking now, ahead and behind, a ripple of movement running through the forest like a shiver through a pelt. The groaning built until it wasn't separate sounds anymore but one continuous note, deep and grieving, and the sap fell everywhere, silvering the path, beading on Casper's sleeves, running warm—not cold, *warm*, like something newly alive—down the back of her neck.

Every instinct she owned screamed at her to turn the cart around. Ten years of survival lived in that scream: *be small, be unnoticed, be nowhere interesting*. She had built her whole life on the principle that the world forgave you if you never touched anything.

She drove deeper into the Weald.

"Torchlight's closer," Bren said grimly. He turned in his seat. "Doran! Maren! Drop back to the bend and slow them however you can. Don't kill guildmen unless they force it—we may all want pardons someday."

"And if Vane's with them?" Doran called back.

Bren was quiet a moment. "If Vane's with them," he said, "then killing him won't help either."

The two riders peeled away into the dark. Casper heard the hoofbeats fade behind her, and hated how much comfort it cost to send friends backward toward blades so she could keep driving forward into a forest of weeping ghosts.

* * *

The fight, when it came, was short and ugly and happened mostly behind her.

She heard it—the crash of a horse screaming, the crack of the crossbow, shouts in guild cadence answered by smuggler curses—and every fiber of her wanted to turn, to look, to know. Instead she watched the path and drove, because the child's hand had closed around her wrist, small and strong, and the girl said, "Forward. The wood is watching the front of you. Someone has to be."

The groaning of the trees changed pitch. Deeper. Angrier. Casper realized the forest could feel the riders at its edge—could feel men with iron and fire entering the place where fire had already done its worst—and the sap was falling faster now, thickening, and the branches were no longer merely lifting. They were reaching.

Something exploded behind her—a green flash, a smell like crushed leaves and hot copper, a sound that was less a noise than a pressure—and a horse shrieked in a register no healthy animal finds. Shouting. Then a voice she knew, hoarse and furious: *"GO, CASPER!"*

"Bren—"

*"DRIVE!"*

She drove.

Tam was crying silently beside her, fists pressed to his eyes, and she put one arm around him without taking her gaze off the path. Behind them the sounds folded into the general groan of the wood, and the darkness closed like water over a stone, and Casper Lockridge—who had always known exactly where everyone was, who had made a career and a life out of knowing—drove blind into the heart of the dead forest and did not know what she had lost.

* * *

They stopped at dawn's gray rumor, where the trees stood widest apart, and waited.

An hour passed. Two. The sap had hardened on everything, a faint silver glaze, like frost that had decided to stay. The child wandered among the trunks, touching them one by one, and where her palm rested the char flaked away to show pale wood beneath, alive, breathing.

Then Casper heard footsteps, uneven ones, and Bren came limping out of the treeline with Tam's older sister Marta supporting him, and Casper saw the bandage before she understood it, and understood it before she could stop herself from looking.

His right hand was gone at the wrist. Wrapped in sacking, soaked through, tied off with a bowstring. His face was gray as ash but steady.

"Doran?" she managed.

"Rode east with Maren. Both whole." He lowered himself against a trunk with a grunt, and the tree—she saw it plainly—leaned toward him, just a little, the way a dog leans against a trusted leg. "Vane was there. Front rank. He called my name, Casper. Knew the family line, knew my grandmother's toll-road. He offered us all crowns' pardon right there in the dark." Bren laughed, wet and bitter. "Then he saw the girl in the cart light and stopped offering."

"The hand?"

"A guildman got close. I got his sword-arm. His friend got mine." He shrugged with his whole body except the ruined arm. "Fair trade, by smuggler sums. And here's the thing, Casper—" he leaned forward, and his eyes were bright with fever or fury or both, "—the wood fought *for* us. Branches came down on guild riders. Roots came up under their horses. It knew which side we were on. It knows whose side you're on."

Casper looked at the child, standing among the pale-wounded trees with silver tears drying on her cheeks.

"There's no side to be on yet," Casper said. "We're still running."

"No." Bren shook his head slowly. "Running's what we were doing in the mountains. This—" he lifted the bandaged stump toward the endless waking dark around them, "—this is going home. For her, maybe. For the wood, certainly. Question is whether it's going home for *you* too, or whether you're still just drawing lines and calling them roads."

Far off behind them, faint but persistent, came the ring of axes.

They hadn't stopped at the forest's edge. Vane had brought axes.

Casper closed her eyes and felt the map in her mind—all of it, every route, every secret, every blank space she'd ever left as mercy—and understood, with terrible clarity, that there was exactly one place left to go.

"The Hollow," she said.

The child turned. Smiled again, sad as sap.

"Yes," the girl said. "Mother's almost awake. She'll want to know what you're willing to give her."
