## 10. Where No Road Goes

The Weald did not forgive the Crown's armies so much as it forgot them.

Casper heard the stories the way she now heard everything—secondhand, at a distance, from travelers who came to the forest's edge and turned back shaking their heads. The Third Legion had marched in from the east with surveyors and fire-carts and a general who believed, as generals do, that terrain was a matter of patience. They had walked for six days through country that should have taken two. On the seventh day they had emerged on the same meadow where they'd started, their maps useless, their compasses spinning like drunkards, and the trees—green now, impossibly green, a hundred years of ash undone—watching them from every side with that patient, root-deep attention the old wood had never lost.

The general had ordered a second attempt. The forest had rearranged itself around the column like water around a stone, and this time it had let them out three miles further from where they began, which everyone understood to be a courtesy rather than a mercy.

After that, the Crown stopped sending armies into the Weald. It sent letters instead, and then envoys, and finally—Casper learned this from Bren, who heard it from a toll-keeper who owed him a favor—a delegation of scholars, unarmed, carrying gifts of seed-grain and iron plows, asking politely if the forest might be willing to discuss borders.

The forest had not yet answered. Casper suspected it was enjoying being asked.

* * *

She lived now in a house that existed on no map, because there were no maps anymore—not hers, not the guild's, not any that told the truth about the Thornfells or the Weald or the hundred hidden roads between them. She had fed all of it to the spring in the Hollow: ten years of ink, every secret pass, every smuggler's shortcut, every careful line she had drawn to make herself indispensable and therefore invisible. The paper had gone down into the dark water like leaves, and the water had taken it, and what rose after was not silence but a sound—the Weald waking, a groan and then a green rush, sap running in trees dead for a century, the whole forest weeping and laughing at once.

She had thought, in the moment before she let the maps go, that she would feel emptied. That she would stand there afterward with nothing inside her, the way she had felt as a girl in her father's house, useful to no one, belonging nowhere.

Instead she felt light. It was the strangest thing she had ever learned: that the weight she had carried for ten years had never been keeping her alive. It had only been keeping her small.

The cabin by the pass was gone—burned, probably, or simply absorbed; the forest was thorough about such things. She lived now in a longhouse in one of the returning villages, built by hands that remembered how to build, among people whose grandmothers had sung forbidden songs and whose children were learning them again out loud, in daylight, without flinching. Her name here was just Casper. Not Lockridge of the Crown survey, not the woman who could get you over the mountains for a price. Just Casper, who fixed roofs and mended fences and sat in the evenings with a child on her knee, teaching her letters.

The child spoke plainly now. That was the word everyone used—*plainly*—as though before she had been speaking in riddles, when really she had been speaking in memory, in the accumulated voice of everything the Weald had been and lost and kept. Now she said *bread* and meant bread. She said *Bren* and meant the ferryman, who visited twice a season with his nine-fingered grip and his booming laugh and stories that got longer every time. She said *stay* and meant it, and Casper stayed.

Her name, the child had finally given them, was Wren. She had chosen it herself, from a bird she'd seen in the new branches, small and brown and unremarkable and utterly unafraid.

* * *

On a bright morning in late spring, Casper spread a sheet of fresh paper on the longhouse table and set a stick of charcoal beside it.

Wren looked at the paper the way she had once looked at Casper's candle flames—with that same wary, hungry attention, as though the blank white surface might speak to her in a language older than kingdoms.

"You don't have to," Casper said. "If you hate it."

"I don't hate it." Wren climbed onto the bench. She was seven now, or the Weald said she was seven, though Casper privately suspected the forest rounded generously. "I'm deciding."

"Deciding what?"

"What a map is." Wren picked up the charcoal, turned it over in her small fingers. "Bren says you used to draw roads. He says your roads were better than the Crown's. He says the smugglers still talk about them, even though they're gone." She frowned. "He says it like it's sad."

"It isn't sad." Casper had rehearsed this answer for months and still didn't quite believe it. "Roads are promises, Wren. This road goes here, that road goes there, and if you follow it, it will hold. I made ten years of promises to people I mostly didn't like, and I kept every one of them, and it nearly hollowed me out." She tapped the blank paper. "So before you draw anything, decide what you're promising."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she bent over the paper and drew a single line—a wandering, confident line that started at the bottom edge and went nowhere in particular, curving like a river that hadn't decided yet.

"That's not a road," Casper said.

"No." Wren added a second line beside it, then a third, and Casper saw what she was doing: she was drawing the Hollow, the way the paths had braided around the spring, except she was drawing them as they were *now*, moving, growing, the way no cartographer could ever fix them on paper. "It's the forest. It doesn't stay still, so my map can't either. Bren says the soldiers' maps lied because they drew things that don't move like things that do." She glanced up, suddenly fierce. "I want to draw true ones."

Casper's throat tightened. She looked away, out the window, at the green wall of the Weald at the village's edge—at the trees that leaned toward the house sometimes when they thought no one was watching, the way sunflowers lean toward light.

"There's no trade for that," she said gently. "No guild will certify a map that changes. No king will pay for a chart he can't use to move an army."

"I know." Wren went back to her lines, charcoal whispering. "That's why nobody's ever drawn one."

And there it was—the thing Casper had spent ten years failing to understand, handed to her by a seven-year-old with charcoal on her fingers. Every map she had ever made had been made for someone else's purpose. Someone else's army, someone else's cargo, someone else's hunger for control. She had been the best liar in the Thornfells precisely because she believed maps were tools, and tools belong to whoever holds them.

But a map drawn for its own sake—for love of the thing mapped—that was different. That was a song. That was a grandmother singing quietly in a kitchen, keeping something alive that no empire could burn.

"Show me how you'd mark the spring," Casper said, and pulled the bench closer, and for the rest of the morning they drew together, and Casper taught her nothing at all, which was the best lesson she had ever given.

* * *

Bren arrived at midsummer with the ferry season high and a crate of river pears balanced on his hip, and found them both under the big oak at the village edge, surrounded by Wren's drawings—dozens of them, pinned to the grass with stones, each one the same forest seen differently, shifting, alive.

"Well," Bren said, setting down the pears. He looked at the drawings a long time. His missing hand—he still refused a hook, said the stump told truer stories—rested easy against his belt. "The guild sent another envoy last week. Down at the crossing. Young woman, plain clothes, very polite." He grinned. "Asked a lot of questions about whether anyone round here had skill with charts."

Casper went still. "Did she give a name?"

"She did." Bren watched her carefully. "Ilsa. Said she'd left the guild. Said she wanted to learn honest mapping, if such a thing existed." He shrugged. "I told her the honest kind was new. Only one teacher for it, far as I knew, and she was particular about students."

"Bren."

"I didn't tell her where. I'm not a fool, Casper Lockridge, whatever my grandmother said." He lowered himself onto the grass, groaning theatrically, and took a pear. "But I'll say this. Vane's finished. The Crown cut the guild's funding the day the Third Legion walked in a circle for a week. Half the cartographers have gone into ship-charts and the other half are drinking themselves stupid in Thornfell town. Whatever hold that man had on anyone—" he bit the pear, chewed, swallowed— "it's broken. And the girl he promised to legitimize? There's nothing left to legitimize her *into*. She's got nothing to lose and a head full of your methods, and she's out there looking for the one person who ever showed her mercy instead of a knife."

Casper said nothing. She looked at Wren, who had paused her drawing and was watching them both with the frank, unhurried attention of the Weald itself—as though she already knew how this would end and was simply waiting for the adults to catch up.

"She copied my charts once," Casper said finally. "For a name. For a place to belong." She picked up one of Wren's drawings—the first one, the single wandering line—and smoothed it flat against her knee. "I let her go because I saw myself in her. Maybe that was mercy. Maybe it was just delayed payment."

"And if she finds you?"

Casper looked out at the forest, green and vast and rearranging itself gently, endlessly, around everything that entered it. She thought about the blank paper on the table that morning. About promises, and who they belonged to.

"Then I'll teach her," she said. "What a map is."

Wren smiled, picked up her charcoal, and began a new line—one that ran off the edge of the page and kept going, past the paper, past the table, into a country no one had ever charted, where no road went and none was needed, because the way forward had finally learned to walk itself.
