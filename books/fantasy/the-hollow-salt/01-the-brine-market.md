## 1. The Brine Market

The salt came in on the morning wind the way it always did, fine as flour and patient as sin, settling into the cracks of Hollowmere's market square until the whole town gleamed like something bleached. Ulyana Thackeray had lived her entire twenty-six years inside that whiteness, and she had learned to read it the way sailors read weather. Salt on the tongue meant a dry day, good for trading. Salt in the eyes meant the flats were breathing, exhaling their long mineral sigh across the valley, and on those days people's memories sat loose behind their faces like coins in a torn pocket.

Today was a breathing day.

She felt it before she saw it — that faint static at the back of her skull, the whisper of other minds brushing against hers like moths against lantern glass. Magic didn't work in Hollowmere. Everyone knew that. The brine killed it dead; charms curdled, spells slid off the air like water off wax. But Ulyana's gift wasn't magic, not exactly. It was older and quieter than that, a thing that lived in her blood rather than her breath, and the salt only slowed it. Never stopped it. She'd tested that truth often enough as a girl, pressing her palm to a sleeping cousin's brow and pulling out the memory of a stolen apple just to watch him cry about it later without knowing why.

She'd been beaten for that. She'd learned better.

Her rules now were carved into her like the market ledgers were carved with debts: *never steal pain, never leave the valley.* Two rules, simple as a knife. Pain cut wrong when you pulled it free — left wounds in both the giver and the taker, jagged edges that festered. And outside the valley, she'd been told by her grandmother, the gift would turn on her, flood her, drown her in every mind within a mile until there was nothing left of Ulyana but noise. Whether that was true she'd never tested. She intended never to test it.

"Thackeray girl." Old Maret Pulsen waved her over from his pickle barrel, gap-toothed and cheerful. "You looking for work or looking for trouble?"

"Coin," she said. "Same as ever."

He laughed and spat into the brine ditch. "Then you're in luck. Widow Corren's been asking after you since dawn."

Ulyana's stomach did its small familiar drop. Widow Corren paid well. Widow Corren also wept, and weeping made everything harder.

* * *

Sella Corren sat beneath the awning of her late husband's stall, which her sons still stocked out of habit or grief — no one in Hollowmere could tell the difference anymore. She was a small woman folded into herself like a letter someone had given up trying to send, and when she saw Ulyana coming she straightened with the desperate eagerness of the bereaved everywhere.

"You'll do it, then? You said maybe, last week. You said—"

"I said I'd think on it." Ulyana sat across from her, keeping the barrel between them. "Tell me what you want taken."

"The face." Sella's hands twisted in her apron. "I can't — it's going. Every morning it's going a little more. I wake up and I know his name, Tam, Tam Corren, forty-one years my husband, but the *face*, girl, the face is like water through my fingers. Yesterday I thought he had brown eyes. His eyes were grey. Grey like the flats after rain. How can I forget what color my own husband's eyes were?"

Because grief eats memory the way salt eats iron, Ulyana thought, and said nothing of the kind. "And you want me to take it from you entirely?"

"No!" Sella flinched. "No. That's not — the others said you could do it gentle. Copy it, like. Leave mine where it is but make a... a second one. So when mine goes, I'll have yours to look at."

A copy. Harder than a theft, gentler too, the way surgery was gentler than amputation only if the surgeon knew her trade. Ulyana looked at the widow's chapped hands, at the stall stacked with jars of pickled herring that Tam Corren had brined himself, and felt the old loneliness shift in her chest like ballast.

"I'll need an hour," she said. "And two silver. One now, one when you've seen it's done."

Sella pressed the coin into her hand like it burned her. "Bless you. Bless you, Thackeray girl."

*Don't bless me,* Ulyana thought. *I'm the reason you have to pay for your own husband's face.*

But she took the coin.

* * *

The copying itself was quiet work, done in the shade behind the tannery where the smell of hides covered the smell of the flats. Sella closed her eyes. Ulyana laid two fingers against the widow's temple, light as a moth landing, and sank in.

Memory was a country, and everyone's country was different. Sella Corren's was small and warm and terribly neat — a cottage of the mind with everything in its place, except that one room stood empty now, swept clean of its occupant, and Ulyana understood without being told that this was where Tam had lived. She found him at last in the margins, in the corners: a grey-eyed man laughing over a net, a grey-eyed man cursing at rain, a grey-eyed man asleep with his mouth open while herring brine dripped onto the floor and he didn't care, he didn't care, because forty years of marriage meant you could be ugly in front of each other and call it love.

Ulyana gathered the face carefully, the way you'd lift an egg from a nest. She took the eyes first, then the set of the jaw, the laugh-lines, the way his hair went white at the temples in a pattern like frost on a window. She wrapped it all in stillness and drew it out along her own nerves, and it settled into her mind with the weight of all the others — the hundreds of borrowed faces and voices and moments she carried, a hoard she couldn't spend and couldn't put down.

Then, gently, she pressed the copy back into Sella's empty room, setting it there like furniture returned to a house.

Sella opened her eyes and wept immediately, which Ulyana had expected. What she hadn't expected was the widow grabbing her wrist with surprising strength.

"He's there," Sella whispered. "Oh — oh, he's *there*, I can see the stall from here, I can see him laughing at me, you wicked girl, you wonderful wicked girl—" She pressed the second silver into Ulyana's palm and kissed her knuckles, and Ulyana sat very still and let her, because there was no rule against being thanked, only against enjoying it too much.

"There's a condition," Ulyana said, when the widow's grip loosened. "When you don't need it anymore — when you're ready to let even the copy go — come find me. I'll take it back. No charge."

"Why would you want it back?"

Because I already carry too many faces that aren't mine, Ulyana thought. Because someone has to hold them. Because if I don't, who will?

"Sentiment," she said aloud, and smiled, and hated how easily the lie came.

* * *

The rest of the afternoon passed in small trades. A farmer wanted the memory of his father teaching him to plow, copied for his son who'd never met the man. A boy of twelve, saving coppers in a sock, wanted the taste of honey-cake from the festival three years back because his mother said he'd been too sick that day to eat any — Ulyana lifted it from a baker who owed her a favor and sold it cheap, watching the boy's whole face light up like a lamp. Small mercies. Small coin. The hoard in her head grew by two memories, and she kept careful count, because the count mattered. Grandmother had drilled that into her before she died: *count them, girl, count them every night, because a thief who loses track of her thefts is a thief who gets caught.*

By the time the sun leaned west and the market began to fold itself up — awnings lashed, barrels rolled, the pickle-man singing his off-key closing song — Ulyana was tired in the particular way that came from carrying other people's joy around all day. And underneath the tiredness, humming like a struck string, was tonight.

Dain.

She caught herself smiling at the thought and scowled to cover it, which fooled no one, least of all the fishwife who elbowed her companion and giggled. Dain Merrow, cartographer, who had arrived eight months ago to map the valley's ever-shifting brine channels and simply — stayed. Who laughed with his whole body. Who had asked her, three days ago, blushing like a boy, whether she might consent to dine with him at the Lantern House, and who had given her a token: a small compass worked in brass, its needle frozen forever pointing south toward the flats.

*"So you always know which way home is,"* he'd said, *"even standing still."*

She'd almost read him then. Almost let her gift slide across the surface of his mind, quick and casual, the way she checked every stranger who showed interest in her. It was the sensible thing. It was the safe thing. But he'd looked at her with such open, uncomplicated warmth that she'd felt something in her chest crack slightly, some wall she'd spent years mortaring, and she'd thought: *not this one. Let this one be real.*

Let this one be real. As if wanting it could make it so.

She was still turning the brass compass over in her fingers, needle glinting, when she felt it.

Not the salt-breath static. Not the loose-pocket looseness of minds on a breathing day. This was different — a silence, a held breath, a hole in the noise shaped exactly like a person. Ulyana had spent her life reading the psychic weather of Hollowmere, and she knew every kind of absence: the drunk's blackout, the child's blankness, the dead's great nothing. This was none of those. This was *deliberate*. Someone was shielding themselves from her, which meant someone knew there was something to shield against.

She turned, slow, casual, a woman idly watching the market close.

He stood at the edge of the square near the dried-up fountain, a tall figure in a traveler's dust-grey coat, and he was watching her. Not the idle appraisal strangers gave the Thackeray girl — not curiosity, not suspicion, not the furtive hunger of someone considering hiring her. This was attention, focused and patient, the attention of a man studying a specimen. He was perhaps fifty, lean, with a scholar's stoop already forming in his shoulders and a face like a closed book.

And chained to his left wrist — actually chained, iron links running up under his sleeve — was a book.

It was a thick thing, bound in leather gone dark with age, and it hung against his hip the way a weapon hangs. Even at thirty paces, even across the noisy closing market, Ulyana felt it. Or rather, she felt nothing from it, and the nothing was deafening. Everything in Hollowmere gave off some faint hum of mind or matter — people, animals, even objects soaked in long use. The book gave off a void, a cold spot, a silence with *edges*.

The stranger met her eyes.

He didn't look away, as a polite man would. He didn't stare, as a rude one would. He simply held her gaze for three heartbeats, the way one scholar acknowledges another across a lecture hall, and then he inclined his head — barely, precisely, a bow measured to the degree — and turned and walked away down the lane toward the inn, the chained book swinging against his leg with a soft, rhythmic clink.

Ulyana stood alone in the emptying square with the compass in her hand, its needle pointing south, south, always south, toward the flats, toward home, toward the one direction she had sworn never to go.

The wind came off the brine and breathed across her neck, cold as a finger.

She had a date to keep. She made herself walk toward the Lantern House anyway, counting her stolen memories like rosary beads, and telling herself — firmly, reasonably, in her grandmother's voice — that strangers with chained books passed through Hollowmere every few years, and that a man who bowed to her from thirty paces was no business of hers.

She counted twice, and got a different number both times.

That had never happened before.
