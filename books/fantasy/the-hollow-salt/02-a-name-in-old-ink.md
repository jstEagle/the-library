## 2. A Name in Old Ink

The stranger did not follow her home that night. Ulyana made certain of it.

She took the long way from the market, past the tannery where the vats breathed their sour breath into the dark, down Cooper's Lane where the gutters ran white with salt-rime even in summer, and doubled back twice through the fish-smokers' alley until she was satisfied that no shadow clung to her heels. The man with the chained book had watched her lift the widow's memory — had watched her do it with the particular stillness of someone taking notes behind his eyes — and Ulyana Thackeray had survived twenty-six years in Hollowmere by treating stillness as a warning, the way sailors treat a wind that stops too suddenly.

She locked her door. She sat with her back against it for a long while, listening to the house settle around her, and told herself the cold crawling up her spine was only the draft under the boards.

*He was just a scholar,* she thought. *Scholars come through on the salt road twice a season, buying specimens nobody wants, asking questions nobody answers.*

But scholars did not chain books to their wrists. And scholars did not look at a memory-thief mid-theft with something like recognition, or worse — with something like *grief*.

She slept badly, and dreamed of brine.

* * *

He found her two mornings later at the drying racks above the flats, where the coarse salt came up from the pans in great grey slabs and lay in the sun like sleeping animals. It was the one place in Hollowmere where no one lingered — the work was done by contract crews before dawn, and by midmorning the racks belonged to gulls and to whoever wanted silence. Ulyana went there when she needed to think, which lately meant every day since the market.

"You have excellent instincts," the stranger said, "but you checked your back trail, not your front."

She turned. He stood at the end of the rack-row with his hands folded, a lean man gone grey early, dressed in the travel-worn blacks of some southern college she didn't recognize. The book hung from his left wrist by a fine chain of dull silver links, its cover scarred leather gone the color of old blood. Up close he smelled of ink and road dust and, faintly, of something medicinal — camphor, maybe, the way libraries smell when they are dying.

"You're trespassing," Ulyana said. "The flats are guild property."

"The flats," he said, "are many things. Guild property is the least of them." He took one step closer, unhurried, the way a man moves when he has already decided the outcome. "Ulyana Thackeray."

Not a question. Her name in his mouth sounded wrong, the way her own voice sounds wrong played back to her — familiar shapes, foreign weight.

"Whoever told you that name sold you a lie," she said. "There's no Thackerays left in Hollowmere."

"There's one," he said gently. "You've spent your whole life making sure no one counts."

The gulls screamed somewhere over the pans. Ulyana measured the distance to the rack-end, to the drop beyond it, to the knife she kept in her boot out of habit rather than need. He saw her measuring. He stopped moving entirely and raised both hands, palms out, and the chain on the book swung and caught the light.

"My name is Emrys Vale," he said. "I am — I *was* — third chair of the Order of the Quiet Page, in Corvane. I've traveled eleven hundred miles to speak with you, and I would rather do it standing here in the sun than any other way you might force me to. So let us be civilized, and let me show you why I came."

"I don't want whatever you're selling."

"It isn't mine to sell." His hands went to the book, and Ulyana watched him work the chain free of a clasp at his cuff — watched, and felt the hair rise on her arms, because the clasp had no keyhole and yet it opened at his touch like a mouth. "It never was."

The book fell open across his palms.

* * *

Later — much later, when she could bear to order the memory instead of letting it lie in her mind like wreckage — Ulyana would understand that everything hinged on what happened in the next thirty seconds. Vale held the book open toward her but angled away, so that she could see only the paper's edge and not the page, and he said, in the tone of a physician delivering a diagnosis:

"Before I show you this, know three things. First: this book predates your grandmother's grandmother. Second: it has been in my order's keeping since before the order had a name. Third—" and here his voice did something complicated, grief and apology braided together, "—third, I am sorry. No one should learn this standing up."

Then he turned the book.

The page was vellum gone amber with age, ruled in faded iron-gall ink, and at its head, in a hand that looped and slanted like water finding its level, were words in Old Meric that Ulyana could barely parse and yet understood whole:

*And in the fortieth year of the quiet, the Salt-Bearer shall stand upon the brine, born of the Thackeray line, and the god beneath shall know her step. Forty days hence she shall choose, and the valley shall live or be unmade by the choosing.*

Below the verse, filling the lower half of the page, was a drawing.

A woman's face, rendered in fine crosshatch by an artist who had loved his subject or feared her — Ulyana couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing was its own kind of horror. High cheekbones. A mouth set hard at one corner, the way hers set when she was lying. Hair bound back for work. Eyes that looked directly out of the century at her, patient and appalled.

Her face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance, not a cousin's echo. *Hers*, down to the small scar through her left eyebrow she'd earned falling off the drying racks at nine years old, down to the crooked tooth visible at the corner of the drawn mouth because the artist had drawn her almost smiling.

"That's impossible," she said. Her voice came out flat and far away, as if someone else were using it.

"Yes," Vale agreed quietly. "That is very nearly the technical term for it."

* * *

Here is what Ulyana Thackeray knew about herself, the complete inventory, the whole of it: she had been born in Hollowmere; her mother had died of lung-rot when she was seven; her grandmother had raised her on salt-fish and silence and died four years ago leaving her a narrow house and a sealed cellar she'd never opened. She stole memories because she was good at it and because coin was coin, and she had three rules — never steal pain, never steal from children, never leave the valley — and she had believed, with the simple faith other people gave to gods, that those rules kept her safe. Small life, small footprint. Invisible.

And now a dead man's ink said otherwise. Said she had been *written*. That some scribe a hundred years gone had put her face on vellum and her death on a calendar, forty days, and then closed the book and chained it shut and waited for her to be born.

The vertigo came on slowly, the way floodwater comes — not a wave but a rising. She gripped the drying rack and the salt slab under her palm was warm from the sun and utterly ordinary and that ordinariness was the only thing holding her to the world.

"You're pale," Vale observed. "Sit down if you need to."

"Don't tell me what I need." But she sat anyway, on the rack's low crossbeam, because her knees had opinions of their own. "This is a trick. A forgery. You found some woman who looks like me and—"

"Look at the marginalia," Vale said. "Bottom right."

She looked. Beneath the sketch, in smaller script, cramped as though the writer had been running out of room or courage: *She will not believe it. None of them ever do. Show her the scar.*

Ulyana's hand rose to her eyebrow without her permission.

"How," she whispered.

"I don't know how," Vale said. "I have read this book for thirty years and I don't know how, and neither did the twelve readers before me, and I suspect the person who wrote it didn't either. Prophecy is not knowledge, Mistress Thackeray. It is a wound in time, and we are all of us bleeding through it." He crouched to her level, careful, keeping the book between them like a lantern. "But I know what the order believes it means, and I know what they sent me to do about it, and those are two different things, and only one of them is why I'm really here."

Every instinct she owned told her to run. Every instinct she owned also told her that a man who had walked eleven hundred miles to open a chained book in a salt field was not a man who could be outrun — not because of his legs, but because of what he carried.

So she did the other thing. The dangerous thing. The thing she was actually good at.

"Keep talking," Ulyana said, and let her gift rise.

* * *

Stealing a memory was nothing like the stories. There was no trance, no touch required, no glittering thread drawn from the ear. It was closer to listening — to leaning close against the thin wall of another person's attention and hearing what played on the far side. Surface thoughts were easiest, mere murmurs through plaster. Deep memories took patience, a slow sinking, like wading into cold water inch by inch until the cold closed over the crown of the head.

Vale's surface was disciplined, nearly silent — a scholar's habit of mind, thoughts filed and shelved. But discipline is not silence, and beneath the shelving Ulyana heard the hum of a single page held constantly in recall, worn smooth as a river stone from handling.

She sank toward it.

Through the plaster. Through the shelving. Down into the cold.

And there it was: the page, rendered in perfect detail inside his mind, every line of the verse, every stroke of the crosshatching — and more than the page. Around it, layered like sediment, were thirty years of reading it: footnotes in six hands, arguments conducted in candlelit rooms, a vote taken in a chamber smelling of tallow, the order's ruling read aloud in a voice like a struck bell — *the Salt-Bearer must not reach her fortieth day; her death re-seals what her birth unseals* — and Vale himself, younger, raising his hand against the ruling, and being overruled, and volunteering for the journey anyway, and the private thought he had carried eleven hundred miles like a stone in his boot:

*I will see her face first. If she is what they say, I will decide with my own eyes whether the world needs her dead.*

Ulyana surfaced gasping.

Vale was watching her with an expression she had seen once before, years ago, on a widow whose husband's face she had just lifted — the expression of a man who knows exactly what has been taken and lets it happen anyway.

"You read me," he said. Not angry. Almost relieved.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you would. Words can be forged." He tapped the book, once, softly. "A stolen memory cannot. Now you know I'm not lying, and now you know what my order intends, and now, Mistress Thackeray, you have perhaps forty days to decide whom you trust — knowing that everyone who has ever spoken your name in prophecy has already decided for you."

The gulls wheeled silent over the pans. Far off, the town bell rang the noon hour, and the sound crossed the flats the way sound always did in Hollowmere — flat, thinned, arriving late, as if even noise moved carefully here.

"One question," Ulyana said. Her voice was steady. She was distantly proud of that. "The verse says *born of the Thackeray line.* My grandmother's cellar has been sealed since before I was born. My family kept *something* down there, and I never asked what, because asking felt like opening a door I couldn't close."

Vale closed the book. The chain sang softly as it gathered.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "you should ask."

* * *

That night she did not sleep at all. She sat in the narrow house among her grandmother's furniture — the good chairs, the covered mirrors, the salt dish on every sill — and listened to the cellar breathing beneath the floorboards, a silence so dense it had texture, and understood for the first time in her life that she had never been hiding in Hollowmere.

She had been kept. Like something in a jar.

At dawn she took the crowbar from the shed, and the sealed door at the bottom of the stairs waited for her the way the drawn face had waited — patient, appalled, and hers.
