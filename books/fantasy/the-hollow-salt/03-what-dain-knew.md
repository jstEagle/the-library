## 3. What Dain Knew

The Lamplighter had been a counting house once, back when Hollowmere counted anything worth counting, and its windows still held the tall, narrow proportions of ledgers. Ulyana arrived early because she always arrived early — a thief's habit, to know a room before it knew her — and chose the corner table beneath the mounted antlers of an elk no one alive had shot. She ordered nothing. The boy who worked the tap knew her by sight and brought the dark tea without being asked, sweetened with honey because he believed, wrongly, that she had a sweet tooth.

She checked the salt dish on the sill. Full. Her grandmother's rule, kept past all reason: *salt in every window, salt in every door.* As a child Ulyana had thought it was against spirits. Later she'd understood it was against travelers — against anything that might come in carrying more than it could hold.

Dain came in on the hour, shaking rain from his coat, and the whole room adjusted itself around him the way rooms did. He had that quality. Cartographers were rare in Hollowmere — there being, officially, nowhere else to map — and he wore his strangeness lightly, laughing at his own trade. *I map the valley,* he'd told her the first time, deadpan. *It takes me a week. It's very detailed.*

He sat across from her, and for a while it was easy. That was the thing about Dain; it was always easy. He told her about the surveyor's marks he'd found on the western flats, stones older than any road, arranged in a pattern he refused to call a spiral until she made him. He stole a piece of her bread and she let him, which was itself a kind of theft, and they argued cheerfully about whether the northern hills were hills or the shoulders of something sleeping. He had a way of looking at her when she talked — not the hungry look men gave the memory-thief, not the careful look townsfolk gave a Thackeray, but the plain interested look one person gives another, which in Hollowmere was rarer than magic.

Ulyana had rules about this too. Never read someone you wanted to keep.

But the candle between them guttered when the door opened behind him, letting in a gust and a figure who left again immediately, and Dain's eyes flicked to it — tracked it — with a precision that had nothing casual in it. And in that half-second, unasked-for, unwanted, his surface rose up at her like water through a crack.

She had learned young that her gift had no manners. Strong feeling pushed through skin like heat through wool. Most people leaked ordinary things: hunger, lust, the tune stuck in their heads. What poured off Dain now was not ordinary. It was *counting*. He was counting the exits. He was noting that the tap-boy had gone into the back. He was thinking, in a flat professional cadence that did not belong to the man who'd just been joking about sleeping hills:

*—confirmed, same face as the sketch, report by the next crossing, the order wants verification before the forty days run—*

The tea went cold in her mouth.

*Forty days.*

Vale's book. Vale's voice. *The Salt-Bearer, born of Thackeray line.*

Dain was still talking — something about the eastern flats, about how the brine ate the iron out of survey stakes — and Ulyana watched his mouth move and felt the evening peel away from her like paint from a wet wall. Every easy moment reassembled itself into evidence. How he'd appeared eight months ago, just after the first rumors of a scholar asking questions. How he never spoke of where he'd come from, only of where he was going, always west, always *someday* west. How he'd asked, once, idly, whether she'd ever want to leave the valley — and how she'd laughed and said the flats saw to it nobody left, and how he'd looked at her then, just for a breath, the way a man looks at a lock he is deciding whether to pick.

"You've gone quiet," Dain said. He smiled. God help her, the smile was real. That was the worst discovery of the night — that some part of him was real, and she could not tell anymore which part, and neither, she suspected, could he.

"Say the word *forty*," she said quietly.

His face did what faces do when they are managed: nothing, beautifully. "Forty?"

"And *order*. And *sketch*."

The lamplight stood very still between them. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan clattered, absurd and domestic. Dain set down his cup with exaggerated care, and when he looked up, the charm was still on his face but it had gone thin, like frost on a window you could see the room through.

"How much did you get?" he asked. Not *what do you mean*. A professional question. An honest one, finally.

"Enough."

"Ulyana—"

"Who sent you?"

He exhaled through his nose. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and the salt dish on the sill threw a small white shadow in the candlelight. "Does it matter?"

"It matters more than anything has ever mattered. Who sent you."

"The Order of the Quiet Page." He said it plainly, wearily, like a man setting down a pack he'd carried too far. "Vale's order. Vale himself wrote the commission — though I doubt he told you that either. They found references in the prophecy to a living descendant of the Thackeray line. They needed confirmation she existed. That she was—" he gestured at her, at the teacup, at the whole small warm room, "—*this*. Ordinary. Contained."

"Contained," Ulyana repeated. The word tasted of the cellar under her grandmother's house, of the sealed door she hadn't opened yet, of every salt dish on every sill of her childhood.

"That's not how I thought of it. At first." Dain leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something rawer. "I was sent to look at you and write down that you were harmless. Eight months, Ulyana. Do you know what I wrote instead? Nothing. I kept writing nothing. *No sign of the subject exercising anomalous ability. No indication of prophetic significance.* I lied in official correspondence for you. I taught myself your valley's survey so I'd have a reason to stay. Whatever you think tonight is—"

"What I think," she said, "is that you knew my death was written down somewhere before I did. You sat across from me and joked about sleeping hills and you knew there was a date."

"I didn't know how to tell you."

"You didn't *want* to tell me. Telling me ends the assignment." She stood. Her chair scraped, and two tables of drinkers glanced over and away, trained by generations of valley silence to notice nothing. "Was any of it real? Answer carefully, cartographer. I'll know."

And he flinched — actually flinched — because he knew exactly what she was offering to do, and he knew she could, and for one naked moment he was terrified she would take everything and leave him hollow on the floor of the Lamplighter. Then he mastered it, and said, with terrible gentleness:

"All of it was real. That's why I'm telling you the rest now: hunters are coming. Vale defected — or claims to have — and the order doesn't send one man for a confirmation twice. Staying small won't save you. Staying anywhere won't. I'm sorry. I should have burned the commission the day I met you."

She wanted to strike him. She wanted, worse, to believe him. Both wants sat in her chest like stones in the same pocket.

"Get out of Hollowmere," she said. "Not tomorrow. Tonight. If I ever see you again I will read you down to the floorboards, and I promise you, Dain, whatever the order built you out of, there won't be enough left to map."

He nodded slowly. At the door he stopped, and without turning around, said: "You kept the token."

Her hand went to her collar before she could stop it — to the little brass compass he'd given her their second month, a surveyor's keepsake, its needle fixed forever on a point southwest of anywhere. She'd worn it every day since. She'd told herself it meant nothing.

"Sentimental," she said coldly.

"No," Dain said. "It points at the drowned temple. I gave it to you because I hoped someday you'd ask me why." And he went out into the rain, and she watched through the ledger-tall window as he crossed the square without once looking back, which was either the discipline of a spy or the heartbreak of a man, and she could no longer tell the difference, and that was the wound she would carry longest.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the canal, past the shuttered market where yesterday she'd lifted a widow's grief for coin and called it honest work. The rain had stopped. The flats beyond the last houses lay silvered under a breaking moon, vast and white and patient, and for the first time in her life Ulyana looked at them and did not see safety.

She saw a lid.

*Staying small means staying safe.* Her grandmother's catechism, her mother's, hers. But Vale had found her anyway. The order had found her anyway. She had been observed her whole life by people she'd never seen, named in a book older than her grandmother, dated like a jar of preserves — and the man she'd let closest had been one of the observers, and the seal everyone trusted was, if Vale was honest, failing.

At her gate she stopped. The compass hung warm against her collarbone, its needle steady on the southwest, on the drowned temple she had never heard of until an hour ago.

Inside, the narrow house waited. Beneath the floorboards, beneath the covered mirrors and the salt dishes, the sealed cellar door waited too — patient, appalled, and hers.

Ulyana went to the shed and took down the crowbar.
