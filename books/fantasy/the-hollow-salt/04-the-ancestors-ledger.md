## 4. The Ancestors' Ledger

The cellar key had not left her grandmother's neck in forty years, and now it hung around Ulyana's own, warm from her skin, heavier than any key had a right to be.

She had waited two days after Vale's departure—two days of pretending the market still mattered, of selling back a fisherman's memory of his drowned son at an honest price, of catching herself staring at the brine flats as if they might blink. Two days of the word *Thackeray* turning over and over in her skull like a stone in a tumbler, wearing itself smooth, growing nothing but smaller.

On the third night she took a lantern, a pry-bar, and the key, and went down into the part of the house no one spoke of.

The door to the cellar was oak, iron-banded, and painted over so many times it had become less a door than a suggestion of one. Her grandmother had kept it locked since before Ulyana could walk. *Salt stores,* the family said. *Nothing down there but salt.* In Hollowmere that was a plausible lie; the whole valley was built on salt the way other towns were built on graves.

The key turned with a sound like a tooth breaking.

Cold air breathed out at her, air that had been holding its breath for decades. It smelled of paper and lamp-oil and, beneath both, faintly and impossibly, of blood gone old in stone.

* * *

> **From the ledger of Mereth Thackeray, Warden Third Rank, entry dated in a year no calendar in Hollowmere now counts:**
>
> *The last of the town's memories goes under tonight. Brine takes what blood cannot hold. By morning, Hollowmere will believe we came here fleeing the wars, poor pilgrims, lucky in our ignorance. Let them believe it. Belief is cheaper than grief, and grief is what killed the world outside.*
>
> *My hands shook doing it. I record here, for whatever warden comes after me: my hands shook.*

Ulyana stood on the third step and did not go down for a long moment.

The lantern light reached only so far—a floor of packed earth, shelves along one wall stacked with jars of salt gone grey with age, and against the far wall a desk that looked less like furniture than like an altar someone had grown tired of maintaining. On the desk lay books. Not many. Seven or eight, their spines cracked, their covers stained dark in patterns that might have been water damage.

Might have been.

She told herself it was water damage, and went down.

* * *

The first book she opened was a household ledger, ordinary enough—prices of mutton, quantities of lamp oil—and then the entries changed mid-page, the neat merchant's hand giving way to something tighter, angrier, written by the same fingers.

> **Entry, undated:**
>
> *They are calling him a god of war again. The priests outside the flats say Vessik came with fire and we hid from him. Good. Let them say it. A war-god can be feared. A memory-keeper cannot be forgiven.*
>
> *We did not steal his war. We stole everything else.*
>
> *He held every memory of every soul who worshipped him—the dead remembered through him, the living comforted by being known. When the armies came he would not choose sides because he carried sons on both sides of every line, and to unmake either army was to unmake the mothers' remembering of them. So the war ground on, and the kings blamed him for not ending what only they could end.*
>
> *Our family ended it. Our way. We cut him open where he sat in his drowned temple and we drank his divinity out of him like poison from a wound, and with it we took the memory of the war itself—every battle, every grave, every reason—and we poured it into the earth and salted the earth over it.*
>
> *He lives. That is the worst sentence I will ever write. He lives below, hollowed, holding nothing, and the valley above him holds everything he lost.*
>
> *We did not flee magic. We are the reason there is none here. The brine is not our shelter. It is his shackle, and we are its locksmiths.*

Ulyana read it twice. Then a third time, moving her lips without sound, the way the illiterate do, except she was not illiterate; she simply could not make the words become meaning fast enough to satisfy the horror climbing her throat.

Her family. Her name. The name stitched inside every coat she owned, carved on the market stall her mother had rented, spoken with respect in Hollowmere as the name of people who had *come from somewhere else*, who had survived something, who were owed the small deference refugees receive.

They had not fled anything.

They had done this.

* * *

> **Entry:**
>
> *The gift passed to me today, as it passes to the eldest daughter of each line. I felt it come in like weather. I can lift a man's sorrow out of him now with a touch, sell it, bury it in the flats where it feeds the lock. My mother says this is mercy. She says we are Hollowmere's grief-eaters, that we keep the town light-hearted by taking what weighs it down.*
>
> *I know what we are. We are thieves keeping a stolen house. Every memory I pull from a mourner's head does not vanish—it goes DOWN. It goes to HIM. We feed the god we emptied with the leavings of the town we blinded, and the brine grows thicker every year, and the lock holds.*
>
> *And when the lock fails—because locks fail, all locks fail—there will be one born of our blood who can give it all back. The prophecies outside the flats already whisper of her. Salt-Bearer, they call her. As if bearing salt were a blessing and not a sentence.*
>
> *I pray she is never born. I prayed the same for myself, once.*

Ulyana closed the ledger very carefully, the way you close a coffin.

Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have. Some part of her—the part that had spent ten years lifting grief out of strangers' eyes and calling it kindness—had already known. Had always known. You do not develop a talent for unmaking people's pain out of nowhere. Gifts came from somewhere. Hers had come from below, filtered up through salt and shame and forty generations of women telling themselves stories about refuge and flight.

Every widow whose husband's face she had sold back to her. Every price haggled, every coin taken, every rule—*never steal joy, never leave the valley*—every rule was not ethics. It was maintenance. Never steal joy, because joy fed the lock poorly. Never leave the valley, because a warden who leaves her post—

A warden who leaves her post lets the gate swing wide.

She thought of Vale's book chained to his wrist. Of her own face drawn in century-old ink. Of a death dated forty days out, precise as a debt collector's letter.

*The bearer chooses which hollow fills,* some second line had supposedly said. She hadn't believed in prophecy. Prophecy was for people whose lives hadn't been small enough to check.

Now she knew why her life had been kept small. Smallness was the cage. The cage was the point. And she had been raised inside it by a grandmother who kissed her forehead every night and never once said *I'm sorry*, because you don't apologize to the lamb while you're fattening it.

"No," Ulyana said aloud, to the cellar, to the dark. "No, she loved me."

The dark did not argue. The dark just smelled of blood gone old in stone.

* * *

There was one more book, set apart from the others, wrapped in oilcloth and bound shut with wire. It took her several minutes and the pry-bar to open, and when she did, she understood why it had been sealed, because it was not a record at all.

It was a confession, written across a lifetime, in a hand that deteriorated page by page from elegance to scrawl.

> **The last entry of the last warden before me — Aldith Thackeray:**
>
> *Child, if you are reading this, then the forty days have begun, and I am dead, and you are angry. Be angry. Anger is honest. Everything else we handed down is a forgery.*
>
> *Listen to me. There are two futures written for you and both were written by cowards. One says you die and the seal re-knots. One says you live and the seal breaks and HE RISES, and the world remembers its war all at once, every grave opening in every mind on the same morning, and no one survives knowing that much grief.*
>
> *But there is a thing the prophets outside never understood, because prophets only ever count to two. The god was not a weapon. He was a KEEPER. He was made of what people entrusted to him. He rose in wrath in the old tellings because wrath is all a robbed thing has left.*
>
> *Give him back what we took. Not the war—we never had the war, it's in the salt, it's gone—but what YOU have. What your line has hoarded. Every lifted memory, every sold-back sorrow, every scrap of other people's lives we've carried like coin in our pockets. He was a keeper. Fill him, and he will not need to rise. He will be able to rest. And resting things dissolve.*
>
> *It will cost you everything you are. I won't pretend otherwise. But child—you were never allowed to be anything anyway. At least this way, the spending of you buys something true.*
>
> *I was too afraid. My mother was too afraid. Somewhere back there, a woman was brave enough to help commit the crime and too cowardly to undo it, and we have all been her ever since.*
>
> *Don't be her.*

Ulyana sat on the cold earthen floor with the confession open in her lap until the lantern burned low.

Above her, the house creaked its familiar night sounds. The market would open in six hours. The widow from Tuesday would come back wanting another hour of her husband's voice. Dain—Dain, whose face she could not currently look at even in memory—was somewhere across town carrying orders she hadn't yet fully unwrapped.

Forty days. Fewer, now, by three.

She looked around the cellar at the grey jars, the altar-desk, the seven books of her inheritance, and she understood at last what she was looking at. Not a storeroom. A prison guardhouse. Her whole childhood had been conducted in the wardens' quarters, above the cell, and everyone she had ever loved had been either jailer or prisoner.

She snuffed the lantern, climbed the stairs, and locked the door behind her—then stood a moment with the key in her fist, wondering why she bothered.

Because tomorrow, someone was coming to kill her for a prophecy she'd verified with her own gift. And the only person alive who might help her understand the other side of that prophecy was the scholar with the chained book—who was, she now realized, the first outsider in a hundred years to walk into Hollowmere asking questions about Vessik.

Which meant the order that sent him already knew what her family had buried.

Which meant they were coming.

Far below her feet, deeper than the cellar, deeper than the roots of the valley, something that had held nothing for a hundred years shifted in its sleep—or in its waking—and the salt in the jars on the shelf rattled once, softly, like teeth beginning to chatter.

Ulyana pressed her palm flat against the floorboards and felt, faint and unmistakable, a pulse.

It was counting.
