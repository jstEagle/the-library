## 5. Forty Days, Fewer Now

Emrys Vale had lied to better people than Ulyana Thackeray, and he had done it with a steadier voice.

That was the thought that kept him awake in the rented room above the Brine Market, staring at the water stain on the ceiling that resembled nothing so much as a spreading bruise. He had told her the book named her. He had shown her her own face sketched in ink a century old. But he had not told her the rest of it — the part that mattered, the part that had made him steal the Codex of Hollow Salt from the Vault of Nine Candles and run south through three kingdoms with his order's hounds at his heels.

The part where they wanted her dead.

He turned the thought over the way he'd once turned pages, gently, as if gentleness could change what was written there. *Let the bearer be unmade before the fortieth day, and the seal shall drink deep of her ending.* The passage was elegant in its cruelty, the way prophecy always was — no knife required, only arithmetic. Kill the girl before the date, and the god beneath the valley would slumber another century. Let her live past it, and the seal would crack like river ice in spring.

His order had voted. Nine candles extinguished, one by one, until only his remained lit. He could still hear Prior Cassian's voice, mild as milk: *One life against ten thousand, Emrys. Even you cannot call that murder.*

Even you. As if he were the unreasonable one. As if he hadn't spent thirty years memorizing the Codex and understood what the others refused to see: that the seal was already failing, that killing Ulyana Thackeray would be pouring one cup of water into a draining sea. The prophecy offered two endings — sealing or shattering — but both assumed the valley held. It didn't. Something beneath the brine was waking on its own, patient and enormous, and the Thackeray girl was not the lock's weakness. She was the key someone had finally come looking for.

He rose before dawn and dressed in the dark, out of old habit. The room smelled of tallow and salt-cured fish, and through the shutters he could hear the market assembling itself below — awnings snapping open, the wet slap of fish on cutting boards, the particular hush that fell over Hollowmere at first light, as though the whole town were listening to something underground.

Perhaps it was.

* * *

He found her at the widow Maren's stall, because of course he did. Three days now he had watched her work, and he had begun to understand that watching Ulyana Thackeray was like watching weather — she moved through the crowd with a competence so total it looked like carelessness. She laughed at the right moments. She touched elbows, adjusted shawls, leaned close enough to breathe the same air as her marks. And when she stepped back, they were lighter by one memory and richer by whatever coin she'd named, and they smiled at her, grateful, not knowing what they'd paid.

Today the widow Maren pressed a folded paper into Ulyana's hand and gripped her wrist afterward, holding on a moment too long. Vale was close enough to hear the old woman say, "It's clearer than it's been in years. I can almost smell his coat again." Almost. The word hung between them like smoke.

Ulyana saw him then. She always saw him; that was the trouble with thieves, they noticed watchers. Her chin lifted a fraction, and her eyes — gray as the flats after rain — went flat and assessing.

"Scholar." She crossed to him, coin-purse hidden somewhere in her layers of clothing. "You're still here. I'd have thought your order recalled you."

"My order believes I'm dead," Vale said quietly. "I let them believe it."

Something shifted behind her eyes. Not fear — calculation. He watched her decide to hear him out, and hated himself a little for how easily she decided it, and hated his order more for making this conversation necessary.

"Not here," she said. "The drying sheds. No one goes there till midday."

* * *

The drying sheds stood at the valley's eastern edge, where the brine flats began their white glitter toward the horizon. Racks of salt-fish hung in rows like sleeping bats, and the air was thick enough to chew. Ulyana stopped in the aisle between racks, arms folded, and waited.

Vale set the Codex on a salt crate between them. The chain that had bound it to his wrist lay coiled beside it, cut three days ago in a creek bed far to the north. The book looked smaller out in the open air, its vellum cover scuffed, its brass corners green with age.

"You showed me my name," Ulyana said. "You showed me my face. You did not show me everything, Scholar, and I've been in this business long enough to know that a half-told truth is just a lie wearing its good coat. So. Tell me why you really came."

He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it on the road, in taverns, in the dead hours before dawn. None of the rehearsals survived her stare.

"The passage about the Salt-Bearer has a second clause," he said. "My order has known it for a hundred years. They've been waiting for a Thackeray daughter to be born in the prophesied season. Waiting to kill her."

The words came out level, which surprised him. Ulyana did not move. A fish rack creaked overhead in the wind off the flats.

"They believe your death re-seals the god," Vale continued. "Before the fortieth day from when the Codex was opened. Which was eleven days ago." He let that land. "Twenty-nine days, Ulyana. That is what I stole the book to tell you. Not that you're named in it. That there are people crossing mountains right now with knives and a clear conscience, coming to make sure you die on schedule."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind moving through the hanging fish, a low hollow note like breath across a bottle's mouth.

"My grandmother said our family kept something," Ulyana said at last. Her voice was very quiet. "She never said what. She said we were wardens, and that warden was a word for gravedigger who got fancy ideas." She looked down at the Codex. "And your order wants to add me to the grave."

"Yes."

"And you?" Her eyes came up, sharp as a filleting knife. "Why does a man burn his own life down to warn a thief he met four days ago?"

Because I read the whole book, he wanted to say. Because I found the marginalia in a dead woman's hand saying *the seal drinks, the seal drinks, we gave it our memories and it is still thirsty*. Because every instinct I have says killing you will not close the wound, only widen it.

What he said was: "Because I've spent my life guarding a truth my order uses as a weapon. I'd rather be wrong with you than right with them."

She studied him for a long time, and he felt the strange prickling at the base of his skull that meant she was reading him — riffling through his surface thoughts like a merchant checking coins for clipping. He let her. There was nothing left in him worth hiding.

"All right," she said finally. "I believe you're telling the truth. That's not the same as trusting you." She picked up the Codex and weighed it in her hand. "Twenty-nine days. What do we do?"

"We—" 

He never finished. The wind changed.

It came off the flats in a gust that should have smelled of nothing — the brine flats carried no scent, no magic, no memory, that was their whole purpose — but this wind smelled of ozone and crushed thyme and something older underneath, something like a battlefield left out in the sun. Ulyana's head snapped toward the eastern horizon. Vale followed her gaze.

A figure was walking the flats.

Not stumbling, not crawling the way travelers did when the valley drank their magic — walking, upright and unhurried, boots leaving prints in the crusted salt. As Vale watched, the figure raised one hand and light gathered in the palm of it, gold and deliberate, a small captive sunrise. Magic. Held, not lost. The first traveler in living memory to cross the brine without being emptied.

"He's not losing it," Ulyana breathed. "Nobody crosses and keeps—"

"I know." Vale's mouth had gone dry. "The seal isn't drinking anymore. It's not strong enough." He turned to her, and the deadline that had lived in his head for eleven days suddenly had a body, a shape, a golden light walking toward them across the white. "Ulyana. If the seal can't strip a traveler's magic, then it can't hold what's underneath much longer either. Your order's arithmetic is wrong. Killing you won't re-seal anything. There's nothing left to seal *with*."

The figure reached the valley's edge and stopped, standing at the boundary stone where the salt gave way to packed earth. Up close he was young, travel-worn, a mage's sigil stitched at his collar — and his eyes, when they found Ulyana, went wide with recognition that made no sense, because he had never seen her before.

But Vale had seen that look before. It was the look of a man seeing a portrait step out of its frame.

"You're her," the traveler said. His voice carried strangely in the dead air, doubled somehow, as if something vast were speaking an instant behind him. "You're the one the book promised. Lady, I've walked nine days to find you, and the whole way the salt was whispering your name."

Behind him, far out on the flats, the ground shimmered — a heat-haze, except the morning was cold.

Ulyana looked at Vale, and all the wariness in her face had burned away into something harder and more frightened and more alive.

"Twenty-nine days," she said. "I'm starting to think we don't have twenty-nine hours."

Far beneath their feet, so deep that no one heard it, something turned over in its sleep — and the salt flats, for the first time in a century, began to sing.
