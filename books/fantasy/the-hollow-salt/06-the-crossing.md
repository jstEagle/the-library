## 6. The Crossing

They left Hollowmere by the drover's gate, where the palisade had rotted to a knee-high suggestion of itself, and Ulyana did not look back at the town because she was afraid of what looking back would cost her. Fear, she had learned, was a currency like any other. Spend it wrong and you'd be poor by morning.

Vale walked beside her with the prophecy book still chained to his wrist, the chain wrapped now in sacking to muffle its sound. He had broken the chain himself that afternoon—she'd heard the snap of it from the next room and felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was knotted. A man unchained from his own scripture. It didn't make him trustworthy. But it made him interesting.

The flats began a half mile past the gate. From the ridge they looked the way they always looked at dusk: pale as scalded skin, cracked into polygons wide as wagon wheels, each one edged in darker salt like the mortar between terrible bricks. The locals called it the White Silence. Children were warned off it with stories about travelers who walked out singing and came back mute, or didn't come back at all. Magic couldn't cross the brine. Everyone knew that. Everyone had always known that, in the way people know things they have never once thought to question.

Ulyana had questioned it exactly once, at nine years old, standing at the flat's edge with her grandmother's hand heavy on her shoulder.

*Why can't it cross?*

*Because we don't want it to,* her grandmother had said, and then had gone very still, as if the answer had escaped her rather than been given. She had never answered anything again so honestly in her life.

Now Ulyana stood at the edge again, twenty-three years old, with a dead woman's memory of her husband's face still folded somewhere behind her ribs along with forty other stolen things, and a scholar who smelled of ink and fear, and forty days minus five on her own life.

"The crossing takes until dawn," Vale said. "If the seal holds. If it doesn't—" He stopped. Adjusted the sacking on his wrist. "There is no account of anyone crossing since the valley closed."

"Comforting."

"I wasn't attempting comfort. I was attempting accuracy. They're frequently at odds." He glanced at her sidelong. "You could still turn back. The order's hunters will come for me first. I'm the defector. You're only the evidence."

"That's the most generous thing anyone's said to me all week."

"It wasn't generosity. It was arithmetic." But there was something underneath the dryness, some grain of feeling he hadn't managed to grind smooth, and she found herself absurdly grateful for it.

She stepped onto the salt.

* * *

The first hour was nothing. The second hour was cold—the flats gave back no warmth after sundown, and the wind came across them unbroken from whatever lay beyond, carrying a smell she had never encountered: not brine, but something older and greener underneath it, like the ghost of a sea that had died here. The third hour she spent cataloguing what she carried, because it was the only inventory that mattered.

Forty-one memories. Not her own—not one of them. The widow's wedding day. A shepherd boy's first snow. A drunkard's single lucid hour watching his daughter sleep. Forty-one small stolen suns, kept warm in the dark of her, waiting to be sold back or bartered away. She had told herself for years that she was a shopkeeper, nothing more. That memories were goods. That holding them was no different from holding coin.

Out on the flats, with nothing between her and the horizon and the wind worrying at her coat hem, the lie curdled.

They were getting louder.

It started as a pressure behind her eyes, like a headache assembling itself out of parts. Then the widow's wedding day began to *speak*—not in words, not yet, but in sensation. The smell of beeswax and crushed grass. A hand tightening around hers. Joy, real joy, pressing outward against the inside of Ulyana's skull like a moth against glass. And beneath it the others, waking one by one, forty-one voices murmuring in a language made entirely of other people's lives.

"They're louder out here," she said. Her voice came out cracked.

Vale looked over. In the starlight his face was all angles and shadow. "The brine dampens resonance. Inside the valley you're insulated—from magic, yes, but also from everything you carry. Out here—" he gestured at the dark plain ahead, "—there's nothing between you and it. Nothing between any of us and anything."

"You knew this would happen."

"I suspected. There's a difference, and I've built a career on pretending otherwise."

She almost laughed. It came out as something closer to a sob, and she swallowed it down, and kept walking.

* * *

At the flats' midpoint, the land remembered.

She felt it come up through her boots before she understood it—a tremor, not in the ground but in the ground's *memory*, and her gift, her curse, her splinter-of-something reached down without her permission and touched it.

And the march began.

She saw them the way she saw stolen memories: whole, immediate, hers-for-a-moment. A column of figures crossing this same salt a century ago, except then it wasn't salt—it was shallow water over dark earth, and the column stretched back beyond sight. Men and women in grey marching cloaks, faces set like doorframes, and at their head a family whose name rang in her blood like a struck bell. Thackerays. Her people. They were not fleeing. Fleeing people look over their shoulders. These looked only forward, and their forward was a drowned temple far out in the world, and what they carried back with them—in wagons, in chains, in vessels of lead and grief—was a god.

Not a captive god. A *hollowed* one. She saw it in the way the marchers' faces flickered when they passed over this spot: a flinch, a shame swallowed fast, the look of people who have done an enormous thing and agreed together never to name it. Behind them the water shivered with something vast trying to follow and unable to remember how.

Ulyana dropped to her knees on the salt crust and vomited until there was nothing left, and then kept heaving anyway, her body trying to expel a century.

"Ulyana." Vale's hand hovered near her shoulder, not landing. "What did you see?"

"They were smiling," she said. "Some of them were *smiling*. They'd won. They'd ended the war and they were bringing home the peace and they were *smiling*, and under the smiles—" She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. Salt crusted her lips; it tasted of nothing, which somehow was worse than any taste. "Under the smiles they knew. They knew exactly what they'd done. My grandmother knew. That's why she said *we don't want it to cross*. We. Not they. She counted herself in."

The memories inside her had gone quiet during the vision, hushed like mourners, and now they resumed—and out here, past the midpoint, past whatever invisible line marked the deep heart of the old seal, they weren't murmuring anymore. They were *loud*. The widow's joy blazed. The shepherd boy's snow fell in her chest, actual cold, actual wonder. The drunkard wept his one clean hour directly into her spine. Forty-one lives insisting on themselves, on being felt, on being *real*, and Ulyana knelt on the white silence of the flats and understood, finally, with her whole body, what she had been doing all those years in the market. Not shopkeeping. Hoarding. Walking around Hollowmere stuffed full of other people's hearts and calling it a living.

She wept. She let herself, because there was no one to see but Vale and the stars and whatever slept beneath the salt, and because the crying was at least her own—her one un-stolen possession tonight.

When it was done, maybe ten minutes, maybe an hour, she got up. Her knees ached. Her throat burned. The horizon ahead was indistinguishable from the horizon behind, but Vale pointed at a smudge of darkness low in the north and said, "The highlands. The temple lies beyond them," and she nodded, and they walked.

* * *

Dawn found them at the flats' far edge, where the salt gave way in a ragged seam to grey-green scrubland, and the world changed.

Ulyana felt it happen like stepping through a curtain of warm water. One stride she was numb, sealed, the valley's long silence wrapped around her like cotton. The next, magic poured into her—not as spectacle but as *sense*, a new organ opening, overwhelming as sound after deafness. The scrub grass hummed with slow green attention. The wind had opinions. The distant hills watched her pass with the patient interest of old animals. And every one of her forty-one stolen memories turned toward that flood like flowers toward sun, blazing, singing, *alive*—

—and something else flooded in with it. Something enormous, lying far away and far below, turning over in its sleep. For one instant she felt its attention find her, brush across her like a searchlight, and pause.

It recognized her. Or it recognized what she carried. The splinter. The piece of itself that had walked out of it a century ago and been handed down, thinned, generation to generation, until it lived behind the eyes of a thief in a salt-locked town.

Then the attention withdrew, sinking back into the deep places, and Ulyana stood shaking at the edge of the world with Vale's hand finally, actually, on her shoulder.

"What was that?" she whispered.

Vale was very pale. "That," he said, "was the reason my order wants you dead in thirty-five days." He looked north, toward the hills, toward the drowned temple, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and certain in a way she hadn't heard from him before. "It knows you're coming, Ulyana. And I no longer believe we're the ones choosing to go."
