## 7. The God Who Was Robbed

The drowned temple announced itself the way grief announces itself: not with a roar but with a silence that had weight.

Ulyana felt it before she saw it—a hush pressing against her skin like cold water, deeper than the quiet of the brine flats, deeper even than the cellar where she'd found her grandmother's ledger. Outside the valley, sound behaved strangely. Every footstep rang. Every breath echoed back to her carrying things it shouldn't have: the memory of her mother's kitchen, the taste of salt bread, the widow's face as Ulyana had lifted her husband's smile and left her holding nothing. The land here remembered everything, and it made sure she did too.

They came over a ridge of black stone at dusk, and there it was below them—a temple sunk to its shoulders in a lake of glass-clear water, so still it looked less like water than like another sky lying on its back. Columns rose from it in ranks, each one carved with faces. Thousands of faces. Men, women, children, soldiers, priests—every one of them rendered mid-expression, laughing or weeping or shouting, as though the temple's builders had understood that memory was not a thing you kept in jars but a thing you carved into stone so it could outlast the flesh.

At the center of the sunken roof, half-submerged, lay a figure.

Ulyana stopped walking.

Even at this distance, even through the failing light, she knew what she was looking at. A god, carved colossal and reclining across the temple's spine like a sleeping man on a rooftop, his stone hands cradled against his chest. But the hands were wrong. The wrists had been broken off—sheared cleanly, deliberately—and where the head should have been bowed in rest, the face had been *hollowed*, scooped out from brow to chin, leaving an empty basin of stone that the lake water pooled in like tears that had nowhere else to go.

"That's him," Vale said quietly. He stood beside her, thin and gray after six days of flight, his chained book hanging heavy at his hip. "Vessik."

"He looks—" She couldn't finish. *He looks like something done to,* was what she meant. Not a conqueror. Conquerors were carved standing, weapons raised, enemies beneath their heels. This figure was carved *receiving*. His open palms, even broken, were angled upward. The posture of someone who had spent eternity taking burdens from other people and holding them.

"The order's texts call him the War-God," Vale said. "The Devourer of Nations. The reason your ancestors fled into the valley and sealed it." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had gone careful, the way one speaks near a wound. "I used to believe that too. Then I read the book chained to my wrist instead of the ones they allowed us."

He unclasped the chain—the first time she'd seen him do it willingly—and let the prophecy book fall open in his hands. But he didn't read from it. Instead he reached into his coat and drew out a second book, small, water-swollen, bound in cracked leather gone green with age.

"What is that?"

"Found it in a ruin outside Vailmoor, twenty years ago. A priest's journal." He handed it to her. "The last priest of Vessik. Written during the war. I've never dared show it to anyone from the order." His mouth twisted. "Or anyone at all, really. You're the first."

Ulyana took it. The pages were soft as cloth, the ink faded to the color of old bruises. She began to read, standing on the ridge in the dying light, and the world began to tilt beneath her.

*The god does not make war,* the priest had written. *The god remembers war, and in remembering, ends it. That is what the old ones understood. No soldier can march to battle once Vessik has taken his memory of why he fights. No king can summon a host whose grievances have been carried away. We bring our hatreds to the temple and leave them in his hands, and we go home light, and we do not come back for them.*

*But the kings have learned what we are doing. They say we are disarming them. They are right. Blessed be.*

She turned the page. Her hands were shaking.

*They came at dawn. Not an army—an order of mages, hooded, carrying chains of white iron. They say the Thackerays lead them. I write this in the crypt while they break the doors above. If any find this: know that they will not kill the god. Killing a god makes martyrs, and martyrs remember. They mean to hollow him. To take his divinity out of him piece by piece and leave the shell. They mean to steal remembering itself.*

*And they will win. And in fifty years no one will recall there was ever a war, because no one will recall there was ever anything to forget. May whatever follows us be kinder than what came before.*

The page ended there. The next entries were in different handwriting—cramped, triumphant, official. Ulyana recognized the seal stamped beside them because she'd seen it pressed into wax on the first page of her grandmother's ledger: a closed gate above crossed keys. The mark of House Thackeray.

*Day 1. The extraction proceeds. The vessel holds the god's substance without rupture. The valley site has been chosen; the flats will be saturated with his essence, diluted past sensing, until the whole region becomes a lock of which he himself is the key-stone. What better prison than his own power, turned inward?*

*Day 9. Remarkable discovery. The essence, properly distilled, may be grafted into living bloodlines. Wardens must carry a portion of the lock within themselves. The gift passes mother to daughter. It is not theft if we are also the cage.*

*It is not theft if we are also the cage.* Ulyana read the line three times. Somewhere behind her ribs, something that had always been steady went very still.

Her gift. Her *only* gift, the small crooked talent that had kept her fed for fifteen years—that had defined every rule she lived by, every boundary she'd drawn around her life like a fence around a grave. Never steal pain. Never leave the valley. Lift a memory gently, sell it back kindly, keep your head down and your hands quick and maybe the world would stay small enough to survive.

It wasn't hers. It had never been hers. It was a splinter of a god's stolen power, bred into her bloodline like a brand into cattle, so that her family could use the victim's own flesh as the bars of his cell.

"They didn't flee the war," she said. Her voice came out flat, strange, belonging to someone else. "They *ended* it. By robbing the one being alive who could end wars forever. And then they built a paradise on top of him and called the robbery protection."

"Yes," Vale said simply. "That's why the order wants you dead, and why I want you alive. Because the prophecy doesn't name a hero, Ulyana. It names a *key*. And keys don't get to choose what locks they turn—unless someone gives them the choice." He looked down at the hollowed god in the water. "Your ancestors never gave it to anyone."

* * *

They descended to the temple as the last light failed, wading into water that rose only to their knees despite the depth it promised—the lake held some enchantment of its own, or perhaps the god's残 remnant power still bent the world politely around his resting place. Up close, the columns' faces resolved into individuals. Ulyana walked between them slowly, reading them the way she'd once read travelers in the market: a girl laughing with her whole body thrown back; an old man mid-word, eyes bright with a story; a soldier whose carved expression was not rage but *relief*, shoulders dropping, sword point-down, as though this column remembered the exact moment his war left him.

Every face had been defaced. Not destroyed—*emptied*. Someone had come through this temple with tools and patience and scooped the expressions out, leaving smooth blank ovals, the way Ulyana left widows smooth and smiling and holding nothing.

Except one.

Near the temple's heart, half-drowned, one column still bore its face intact: a woman, arms raised, not in worship but in offering, holding up toward the hollowed god a shape that might have been a lantern or might have been a heart. Beneath her feet, worn nearly illegible by water and centuries, ran an inscription. Vale knelt and brushed the silt away with his sleeve, and Ulyana read it aloud, haltingly, in the dead liturgical tongue her grandmother had taught her without ever explaining why:

*"We gave him everything we were afraid to carry. He carried it so we could be light. Hollow not his house, hollow ours instead—he was never the burden. We were."*

Ulyana crouched beside the inscription for a long time. The water moved faintly around her knees, though there was no wind.

"I've been selling people their own memories," she said. "Fifteen years. I told myself it was mercy. Renting grief back to the grieving, coin by coin." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And all that time I was carrying a splinter of *him*. Every memory I lift—I'm doing what he did. Taking burdens. Holding them. Except my family took his hands away and put the burden-holding into blood, generation after generation, so no one would ever have to worship again. So no one would ever have to *thank* anyone. We just—absorbed the miracle and called it a trade."

"You didn't choose it," Vale said.

"No." She stood. Water streamed from her skirts. "Neither did he. That's rather the point, isn't it?"

She turned to look at the great reclining figure one more time. In the dark, with the stars doubled in the still lake, the hollowed face seemed almost peaceful—as if emptiness, given enough centuries, could learn to imitate rest. But she had spent fifteen years learning what hollowed things felt like from the inside. Widows who smiled too readily. Soldiers who'd sold their worst days and couldn't remember why they flinched at drums. Emptiness didn't rest. Emptiness *waited*.

And beneath the waiting, so faint she might have imagined it, she felt something she'd felt only twice before in her life: once in her grandmother's cellar, and once the night the seal first cracked and a traveler crossed the flats with his magic intact. A pull. Gentle, patient, immense—not hunger, not wrath. Something more terrible than either.

*Recognition.*

The god's scattered essence, soaked through a hundred miles of brine and bred into four generations of Thackeray blood, had finally been brought close enough to its own stolen self to know what it was looking at.

Ulyana pressed her palm flat against the woman's column, against the offered lantern-heart, and spoke to the empty face above the lake.

"I know what they took," she said. "All of it. I've been carrying pieces of you my whole life without knowing your name." The pull deepened, and far out across the water, the drowned columns began—one by one, very softly—to hum. "And I think I'm the only person left who can give it back."

Behind her, Vale went rigid. "Ulyana," he said slowly, "the humming—it's not coming from the temple."

She listened. He was right. The sound was rolling in from the east, from the direction of the flats, from home—deep and rhythmic, like something vast turning over in its sleep. Like a lock, after a century, beginning to recognize the shape of its own key.

And beneath the humming, carried on water that should have been silent, came another sound: hoofbeats. Many of them. Riding hard.

"They found us," Vale said. "God help us—they found us, and I don't think the god is the only one waking up tonight."
