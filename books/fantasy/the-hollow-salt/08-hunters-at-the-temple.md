## 8. Hunters at the Temple

Vale had spent thirty years learning to read danger in small things: a candle burning too steady, a door left ajar where habit said shut, a bird going quiet. In the drowned temple, the warning came as water.

The flooded nave had been still all night, black and mirror-calm, reflecting the drowned god's broken columns so faithfully that Ulyana had twice reached down to touch them. Now, near dawn, the surface shivered. Rings spread from the far archway, one after another, deliberate as footprints.

"Someone's wading," he whispered.

Ulyana looked up from the ledger she'd been transcribing by candlelight, her face hollow-eyed and salt-streaked from three days of reading her ancestors' crimes. "The flats are impassable. You told me—"

"The seal is dying." He was already moving, snuffing candles with his fingers, gathering the loose pages of prophecy they'd copied. "Which means the order no longer needs permission to cross."

He heard them before he saw them: the particular wet rhythm of boots moving through ankle-deep water, unhurried, disciplined. Six, maybe seven. The Order of the Quiet Page did not send its hunters to negotiate. It sent them to leave nothing that could be read.

* * *

They had known this hour would come. Vale had counted on three days; they'd been given two. He pressed the copied pages into Ulyana's hands and steered her toward the stair at the temple's rear—the old processional way that climbed out of the flooded foundations toward the valley's underdark, toward Hollowmere itself.

"There's a door beneath the town," he said. "Your grandmother's cellar connects to it—I read it in the ledger, the wardens' passage. The brine won't stop you now. Nothing will."

"And you?"

"I'll be along shortly."

She gave him the flat look of someone who had spent her life detecting lies in the faces of strangers. "You're a terrible thief, Emrys. You steal truth and then hand it back."

"Go," he said gently. "Please."

She went—three steps up the stair—and then the first hunter came through the archway with a lantern raised, and everything happened very quickly after that.

* * *

Vale had never been a fighting man. His weapons were citation and patience, marginalia and long study. But the temple was his, in a way—he had spent eleven years of exile mapping its drowned geometry, and he knew which pillars held and which were rotten to the core, which causeways ran shallow and which dropped into forty feet of black water.

He shouted the first hunter's name—Brother Cassel, who had once shared his cell at the seminary, who had wept over the same forbidden texts—and when Cassel hesitated, struck the lantern from his hand into the water. Darkness bloomed.

Then he ran, splashing loudly, deliberately, drawing their eyes away from the stair, away from the girl whose death they believed was arithmetic: one life weighed against a world sealed safe.

"The prophecy is wrong!" he called into the dark. "Read the second line! Read what your own masters burned!"

"Brother Vale." Cassel's voice, sad and level. "You were the best of us. That's why it hurts."

"That's why you should listen to me."

A crossbow bolt answered him instead, cracking off a pillar an inch from his ear. So much for the best of us.

* * *

What saved him, at first, was Dain.

Vale heard the fight before he understood it—a hunter's cry from the eastern gallery, the ring of steel on stone, a body dropping into water. Then a second cry. He risked a glance around his pillar and saw a figure moving along the high walkway with a cartographer's grace, precise and economical, putting hunters down not with fury but with terrible efficiency, like a man crossing items off a list.

*Dain.* The boy had come after all. Sent, surely—to redeem himself, to prove his loyalty by delivering the Salt-Bearer's head—but somewhere on the flats between Hollowmere and this drowned place, he had chosen differently. Vale allowed himself one breath of hope. Hope was a luxury he rarely permitted himself; he spent it now.

"Dain!" he shouted. "The stair! She went for the stair!"

Dain didn't answer. Below, the hunters regrouped, four of them converging on the stair with the grim patience of men who knew the geometry better than they wished to. Their leader—a woman with a white scar bisecting her jaw, Sister Ondrey, whom Vale had last seen presiding over the burning of the seminary's library—raised two fingers.

Two hunters broke for the stairs. Two came for him.

* * *

He fought the way a scholar fights: badly, cleverly, with everything the room offered. He toppled a rotten pillar between himself and his pursuers and felt the whole gallery groan. He led them onto a causeway he knew would fail, and it failed, taking one hunter down into the deep with a sound like a swallowed scream. The other—Cassel again, always Cassel—caught him by the collar at the edge and held him there, both of them teetering over the black water.

"It doesn't have to be her," Cassel said quietly. Water dripped from his beard. His eyes were tired in the way only believers grow tired. "It could be you, Emrys. Confess your part. They'll let the penitent choose the manner."

"The penitent also chooses the manner of everyone else's dying," Vale said. "No thank you."

He drove his elbow into the old wound Cassel carried from the border wars, twisted free, and scrambled toward the stair—which is when he saw the thing that would decide everything.

Sister Ondrey had gotten past Dain. She stood at the base of the stair itself, crossbow leveled at Ulyana's retreating back, at the pale shape climbing through the dark toward the door that led home.

And Dain leapt.

Vale would remember it always, because he was a scholar and memory was his trade: the arc of the young man's body against the lantern-light, blade reversed, taking Ondrey's shot meant for another. The bolt went into him below the ribs—not clean, not kind, the kind of wound that drowns a man slowly in his own blood—and he fell with her, both of them crashing into the shallows, and when they rose only Dain rose, bleeding, driving Ondrey back step by step until she retreated into the dark to count her losses.

"She's through!" Dain gasped, clutching his side. "Vale—the door—she made the door—"

Four hunters left, and Vale among them, and one wounded boy standing between the order and everything the order feared. Cassel had recovered and was coming. The others were coming. And Ulyana Thackeray was beyond the door now, descending into the roots of Hollowmere alone, carrying every stolen grief of her life and the truth of her bloodline, walking toward the god her family had gutted.

Vale looked at Dain, gray-faced and swaying, and at the stair, and did the only arithmetic he had ever trusted.

"Sit down before you fall down," he told the boy, and turned to face the stair.

* * *

He met them on the ninth step, where the passage narrowed and the drowned temple's cold breathed up from below. Cassel first, lantern high, sorrowful as ever.

"Don't," Cassel said.

"You've read the book," Vale said. "The real book, not the censored folio. Tell me you've read the second line."

"There is no second line."

"There is. Your masters cut it out and burned the page and told you the ash was holy." He backed upward, step by step, buying height, buying time. Behind him, far above, he could hear Dain dragging himself after Ulyana, slow as a tide. "The bearer chooses which hollow fills. Do you understand what that means? She isn't the key to Vessik's cage. She's the *hand on the door*. Kill her and you don't re-seal anything—you just take away the only person alive who can choose mercy."

"Then she can choose it dead," said the hunter behind Cassel, and threw something.

Vale caught the flash of it late—a knife, low, aimed not at his heart but at his knee, the mark of a professional who wanted him alive for questioning. He stepped off the stair instead.

It was, he reflected in the half-second of falling, a very scholarly decision. The water below the ninth step was the deep water, the forty-foot black where the drowned god's oldest altar lay, and if any place in the world still held a residue of Vessik's divinity, it was there. He did not fear it. He had spent thirty years studying a god, and in the end he chose to meet one.

Before the water took him, he shouted upward, into the dark, knowing sound carries strangely in the temple's throat, knowing she might hear, needing her to hear:

"Ulyana! The second line! *The bearer chooses which hollow fills!* Not the god's hollow—*yours!* Choose what fills yours! Choose—"

The water closed over him like a page turning.

* * *

Down, and down. Cold took him first, then the ache in his lungs, then something else—something vast and patient brushing against his mind like a whale passing a diver. Not anger. Not wrath. A question, older than the war, older than the theft:

*What do you carry for me?*

Memories, Vale thought, sinking. I carried memories. Mine, and hers, and a hundred grieving souls'. Let them be enough. Let her be enough.

He thought of the widow in the Brine Market whose husband's face Ulyana had lifted for coin. He thought of Dain bleeding on the stair. He thought of a girl sketched in ink a century before her birth, holding a book chained to a stranger's wrist, and how she had stolen even that from him—her own death—rather than accept it as a gift.

*Choose,* said the vastness, gentle as deep water.

*I choose,* Vale thought, as the darkness filled him at last, *that she lives to choose.*

Above him, muffled by forty feet of silence, he heard the door at the top of the stair slam shut, and the sound of one wounded man dragging himself after the Salt-Bearer into the roots of the world—toward the hollowed god, toward the choice no prophecy had imagined, toward the price she did not yet know she would have to pay.

The water was warm now. Or perhaps that was only the last of him, leaving.

Far above, through the drowned temple's throat, faint as a heartbeat, something ancient began to stir—and wait.
