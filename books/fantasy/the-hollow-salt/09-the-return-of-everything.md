## 9. The Return of Everything

The way down was older than the valley above it.

Ulyana had always imagined that beneath Hollowmere there would be nothing—stone and salt water and the dark roots of the mountains. She had not imagined a road. But the fissure behind the old Thackeray cellar opened into a stair cut with patient, ancient hands, each step worn smooth by feet that had climbed down carrying something heavy, and climbed back up lighter. The walls wept brine. Her lantern threw her shadow long ahead of her, and she tried not to think about how many Thackerays had walked this same descent, how many had stood where she was standing now, holding the same terrible inheritance.

Vale's words were still in her ears. *The bearer chooses which hollow fills.* He had died on the temple stair so that those words could travel down here inside her instead of dying with him. She owed him more than grief.

She owed everyone more than grief.

The stair ended at a door that was not a door—a membrane of salt, translucent as horn, glowing faintly with its own grey light. Through it she could see a chamber vast as a cathedral turned upside down, and at its center, something like a figure.

She put her palm against the salt. It was warm. That was the worst part. A hundred years of imprisonment, and the thing behind the door was still warm.

"I know what my family did," she said aloud. Her voice sounded very small in all that stone. "I read the ledger. I know they didn't end your war. They ended *you*."

The salt pulsed once beneath her hand, slow as a sleeping heartbeat.

"They called you Vessik," she went on, because speaking kept the fear from closing over her head like brine. "They wrote you down as a war-god, but the drowned temple said otherwise, and Vale believed it, and I—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I'm a thief. I know a fellow thief when I read about one. You kept memories. They took the keeping out of you and used it to build a lock."

The membrane brightened. Not angry. *Listening.* And Ulyana understood, with a lurch, why her gift had never felt like theft when she did it gently—why the widow's memory of her husband's face had passed through her fingers like water finding its level. It had never been hers. It had been borrowed, all along, by a splinter of a god who remembered nothing except the shape of remembering.

"I have something for you," she said.

* * *

She had spent forty days becoming rich in other people's pasts, and she had carried every coin of it down the mountain and back across the flats, wrapped against the brine in oilcloth, close to her skin. The widow's husband's face. The ferryman's first crossing, his hands white-knuckled on the tiller while his father shouted encouragement from the shore. The child's birthday, sugar and candle-smoke. The soldier who had sold her his memory of home because he couldn't afford to carry it anymore—the smell of bread, a sister laughing, rain on a tin roof. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Forty days of desperate trade as the seal weakened and the hunters closed in, and she had taken everything anyone would give her, hoarding stolen lives like a miser, telling herself it was preparation, strategy, survival.

It had been gathering. She saw that now. Some part of her, some splintered and exiled part, had known all along what the ending required.

She unwrapped the oilcloth and laid the memory-vials on the stone—not glass, she'd learned quickly that glass burned them, but hollow reeds sealed with wax, each one humming faintly against her palms. Then she pressed both hands flat against the salt door and opened herself the way she opened travelers: gently, without taking, only giving.

The first memory left her like a bird leaving a cage it had forgotten it hated.

The widow's husband's face went through her, through the salt, into the dark—and the chamber *shuddered*. The grey light flared gold at its edges. Somewhere below her feet, something that had been silent for a century made a sound that was not a word and not a scream but the space between them, the sound of a throat remembering it could make sound.

"Yes," Ulyana whispered. "That one's yours too. All of these are yours. They were always just passing through me."

She gave the ferryman his river. She gave the child the candles. She gave the soldier his sister's laughter, and the rain on the tin roof, and felt each one go out of her like heat leaving a stone, and with every gift the god grew brighter, fuller, the figure at the center of the chamber resolving from smoke into something enormous and sorrowful and awake. She could feel it now without touching the door—feel its attention turning toward her, vast and gentle and *starving*, and she understood the danger even as her hands shook with the joy of giving. A starving thing that wakes can wake hungry. The prophecy's first line lived in her skull like a splinter: *the Salt-Bearer shall seal or shatter.* Seal it, and the hunger sleeps another century. Shatter it, and it eats the world.

Her family had written the prophecy. Her family had written both endings, and both endings were cages.

"There's a third thing," she said. Her voice cracked. "There's always a third thing. Nobody ever writes it down because nobody ever wants to pay for it."

* * *

When the last reed was empty she sat down on the cold stone and looked at her own hands. They were shaking. Inside her, where the stolen lives had crowded for forty days, there was a new silence—and silences, she knew better than anyone alive, were never empty. They were rooms waiting to be filled.

"You're whole enough now to choose," she told the god. "But you'll choose wrong. Anyone would. You've been robbed for a hundred years, and the first instinct of the robbed is to rob back. I know." She laughed, and it came out broken. "I built my whole life on it."

The salt door glowed. Patient. Waiting. It did not understand her yet, and she loved it for that—it waited the way no god in any story waits, the way only a keeper waits.

"So here's the last of it." She stood. Her legs felt like someone else's legs. "Everything I ever stole, I'm returning—but most of what I am was stolen before I was born, from you, by people with my face. So I'm paying their debt with my own coin. Fair's fair. My grandmother taught me that." A breath. "She taught me almost nothing else worth keeping. Which is convenient."

She thought of her mother first, because she had saved her for last and last meant first, in the end. Her mother's hands kneading dough on a winter morning. Her mother's voice singing off-key in the cellar while they bottled brine. Her mother's fever, and the long grey week of it, and the small grave on the hillside that Ulyana had visited every year until visiting it hurt too much. She gathered all of it—all the love, all the ache, every ordinary golden hour—and she held it out.

"Take it," she said to the god. "Know what a mother is. Know what we buried under the salt. Know us from the inside, and then forgive us or don't, but *know*."

The memory tore free and she staggered, caught herself against the door. Her mother's face was already going soft in her mind, blurring like wet ink. She said the name—her mother's name—and watched the name slide away from the face like water off brine.

Then her childhood. The market stalls. The first memory she ever stole, at nine years old, a traveler's glimpse of the sea, and how she had cried afterward without knowing why. Dain. Oh, Dain—the date that curdled, the lie, the blade he turned against his own order on a temple stair, the blood on the stones, the token in her pocket she'd carried across the whole valley. His laugh. His hands. The way he'd said her name like it was a place he wanted to live. She gave all of it. She felt it leave, and it left a hole shaped exactly like a man, and she blessed the hole, and let it be empty.

Piece by piece. Hour by hour. The descent of the stairs. Vale's voice saying *which hollow fills*. The ledger's crabbed ink. Every year, every wound, every small triumph of her careful, hidden, watchful life—she poured it all through the salt into the dark, and the god drank it in and blazed like a drowned sun, growing gentler as it grew brighter, fury transmuting into understanding, understanding into something that had no name in any language she knew anymore, because she was forgetting languages as fast as she fed them away.

The last thing she gave was the smallest. Herself, aged six, sitting alone on the edge of the brine flats at dusk, watching the light die on the salt and feeling—for one unguarded moment—that the valley was beautiful.

Then there was nothing left to give, and the door dissolved.

* * *

What stood before her was not a war-god. It was not anything with a name, because names were memories too, and it had just received a hundred years of stolen names along with everything else, and it was deciding, with infinite tenderness, what to do with all of it.

It looked at her. She looked at it. She could not remember her own name, and the not-remembering was strangely peaceful, like setting down a load carried so long the shoulders had forgotten their shape without it.

It could have taken more. It could have taken the valley. She understood, in the wordless way of things understood at the bottom of the world, that it was choosing—and that her gift had made its choice possible. Whole, it needed nothing. Whole, it could simply stop.

It began to dissolve. Not violently—the way frost leaves a window, the way a hand opens. Light came off it in slow grains, finer than salt, finer than sand, and rose through the stone, through the brine, toward the valley above, and where it touched the rock the rock forgot its bitterness. The lock was opening from the inside. Not shattered. Not sealed.

*Returned.*

The god's last act, as it thinned into that rising brightness, was to reach out—not with a hand, for it had none, but with something older—and press one final memory into her emptied mind. A seed planted in fallow soil:

*A woman tending the flats. Content. The gates open. Someone coming down the hill to visit her, again, carrying a story she doesn't remember, ready to hear it like the first time.*

She smiled at it, though she no longer knew why, or at whom.

And far above, through a hundred fathoms of stone and forgotten salt, the first light of an ordinary dawn began to find its way down—to a woman kneeling in an empty chamber, with no past at all, and no weight left to carry, waiting to climb back up into a world that was, at last, unlocked.
