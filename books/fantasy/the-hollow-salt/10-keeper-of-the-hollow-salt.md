## 10. Keeper of the Hollow Salt

The morning Hollowmere opened its gates, Dain Merrow stood at the eastern palisade and watched a hundred people walk out of the valley for the first time in living memory, and not one of them asked why the gates had ever been shut.

That was the strangest part. Not the gates themselves—though they stood wide now, their great salt-stained timbers thrown back against the walls like wings, their chains dissolved to red rust that crumbled at a touch—but the absence of the question. He had expected weeping, or terror, or at least the furious confusion of people waking from a long sleep. Instead there was only a kind of mild wonder, the way a man might feel stepping into a room he'd forgotten existed in his own house. *Oh,* their faces said. *There was a door here all along.*

An old woman pressed her palm flat against the open gate and laughed.

"Rusted clean through," she said to no one. "Hasn't been shut in years, I expect. Funny how you stop seeing a thing."

Dain said nothing. His hands were still bandaged where the hunters' blades had found him on the temple stair, and his ribs ached with every breath, and he had learned in the past weeks that some truths are not meant to be handed to people who cannot hold them. The valley had woken whole but hollowed—the war gone from every ledger, every lullaby, every grandmother's warning—and what remained was peace without provenance, a house swept so clean no one remembered there had ever been dust.

He walked down through the town as the sun climbed. The brine flats gleamed beyond the last row of houses, stretching white and rose-gold toward the horizon, and even from here he could see her: a small figure moving along the crystallized shallows with a rake over her shoulder, tending the salt pans the way she had tended them—or something like them—all her life.

Ulyana Thackeray. Except the name meant nothing to her now. Nothing did.

* * *

He had found her three days after the god dissolved, when the shaking in the earth stopped and the light came up strange and clean over the flats. She had been sitting at the edge of the pans, barefoot in the shallow brine, looking at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. Which, in every way that mattered, they did.

"Hello," she had said, when he approached. Polite. Curious. A little wary, the way anyone is wary of a limping stranger with blood on his coat. "Are you lost? Travelers usually come through the gate, not across the flats."

"You came across the flats," he said. "Three nights ago. With me."

She had frowned—not frightened, only puzzled, the way one puzzles at a dream that won't quite resolve. "Did I? I don't remember it." She looked down at her hands again. "I don't remember much of anything, if I'm honest. Waking up here, on the salt. There's—" She paused, searching. "There's a shape where things should be. Like footprints in snow after the snow's melted. You can see where something stood."

Dain had knelt in the brine beside her, his wounds screaming, and told her the first true thing he could find.

"My name is Dain," he said. "I'm a cartographer. And I think—I think I loved someone who looked very much like you."

She studied him for a long moment. Then, softly, almost shyly: "I'm sorry. I wish I could be her."

And that was how it began.

* * *

The valley changed faster than he thought possible. Without the seal, the brine lost its hunger within days. Travelers crossed the flats and kept their magic; a hedge-witch from the eastern passes set up shop by the market fountain and cured a child's fever before anyone thought to be alarmed. Salt harvested from the pans tasted of nothing but salt—clean, ordinary, useful. Merchants came through the open gates blinking in the sunlight, asking why so fertile a valley had no trade roads, and the people of Hollowmere shrugged and said, cheerfully, inexplicably, *we never needed any.*

The Thackeray house stood empty at the end of Lantern Row. Dain went inside once, alone, and found the cellar door standing open, the ancestors' ledger lying face-down on the floor where Ulyana must have dropped it. He read enough to understand what she had carried out of that room and up through the dark beneath the temple: that her family had not fled magic but murdered a god; that the valley's peace was built on a theft; that every generation of Thackerays had been born wardens of a prison and never told why. He closed the ledger and left it for the town to find or not find. Some inheritances should burn. This one, he suspected, would simply fade, like everything else the valley had chosen to forget.

Only she had paid the full price. Only she remembered nothing at all.

* * *

He visited her on the flats most evenings, when the light went long and the salt pans turned to hammered copper. She had taken work with the harvest crews—they gave her a cot in the drying-house and never asked where she'd come from, because in Hollowmere no one asked such questions anymore—and she was good at it, patient and deft, reading the weather in the crust of the pans the way she had once read grief in the faces of strangers.

"I keep having this feeling," she told him one evening, raking slow lines through the shallows while he sat on the dike above. "Like I used to know things about people. Just by looking at them." She shook her head. "Silly."

"It isn't silly."

"No?" She glanced up, and the low sun caught her eyes, and for one unguarded moment Dain saw something move behind them—a shadow of the woman who had stolen a page from a scholar's mind, who had banished him with her chin high and her heart breaking, who had given away her mother's face and her childhood and him, all of him, to make a broken god whole enough to die gently. Then it passed, and she was just a salt-harvester with tired shoulders and no yesterday.

"What was she like?" Ulyana asked. "The woman you loved. You've never said."

Dain looked out at the flats. He had told her pieces, over the weeks—the market, the widow's memory, the scholar with the chained book—but never the whole shape of her, because the whole shape of her included the way she had erased herself, and he did not yet know whether that truth was a gift or a wound.

"She was sharp," he said. "Funnier than she let anyone see. She had rules she lived by—never take pain, never leave home—and then she broke both of them in the same week, for reasons that were right." He smiled despite himself. "She was angry at me for a long time, and she had every reason to be. And in the end she did the bravest thing I've ever witnessed, and nobody in this valley will ever know she did it."

Ulyana had stopped raking. She stood hip-deep in the brine, watching him with an expression he couldn't name—something between longing and vertigo, a woman pressing her face against a window fogged from the inside.

"That sounds like a story worth telling properly," she said. "Start at the beginning. The market. Tell me all of it."

So he did. He told her about the eve of their date, and the widow whose husband's face she lifted for coin, and the stranger watching from the shadows with a book chained to his wrist. He told her about Emrys Vale, and the forty days, and the prophecy that named her twice—seal or shatter—and how she had refused both. He told her about the crossing, and the drowned temple, and Vale holding the stair with his dying breath. He told her about Vessik, the memory-keeper god, robbed of everything he was, and about the woman who walked down into the dark carrying every stolen thing she'd ever taken plus everything she owned, and laid it all at the god's feet like an apology a century overdue.

By the end the sun was down and the stars were out and Ulyana was crying without knowing why, tears running salt-salt-salt down her face into the pans below.

"I don't remember any of it," she whispered. "But my chest hurts like I do."

"That's how I know you're still her," Dain said.

* * *

He came back the next evening, and the next. Sometimes she asked for the story; sometimes she just wanted company, and they talked of small things—the price of salt in the lowland towns, the hedge-witch's terrible singing, whether the rains would come early. Once she took his hand as they walked the dike, casually, as though they had always done it, and neither of them mentioned it, and he thought: *begin again. However many times it takes. Begin again.*

Because that was the choice she made, each time. He saw it clearly now, clearer than any prophecy had ever seen. The book had offered two endings—seal the god, shatter the god—and she had written a third: *fill the hollow.* Give the robbed god back his robbery. Pay with the only coin that mattered. And what remained afterward was not a ghost or a martyr but a woman, alive and breathing and empty, standing in the shallows of a freed valley, who each evening heard the tale of who she had been and each evening chose, freely, without debt or prophecy or bloodline, to keep listening. To keep beginning.

Justice didn't need her to remember. It only needed her to stay.

* * *

On the first day of autumn, Dain came down to the flats carrying two things: a satchel of drawing paper—he had begun mapping the trade roads that would soon run east from the open gates, the first honest work he'd done in a year—and a small wooden token, carved in the shape of a compass rose, worn smooth by her thumb in another life. He had kept it through everything. It seemed right that she should decide what became of it.

She was waiting for him at the edge of the pans, and something in her posture made his heart stumble. She wasn't raking. She was holding her hands out in front of her, palms up, staring at them, and the brine around her ankles was glowing faintly, softly, the way water glows when moonlight catches it—except there was no moon yet, and the light was coming from her skin.

"Dain," she said, and her voice was steady but her eyes were wide. "Something happened today. A traveler came through the gate, sick with fever, and I touched his hand to help him up, and I—" She swallowed. "I knew his daughter's name. I've never met him. I don't know how I knew it."

The glow pulsed once, gentle as a heartbeat, and faded.

Dain stood very still on the dike, the token heavy in his pocket, and understood what he was looking at. Not the god returned—Vessik was gone, truly gone, dissolved into the wide world's magic like sugar into warm water. But a splinter. A seed. Whatever she had poured into the hollow had left something behind in the vessel: not memory, not yet, but the *shape* of memory. The gift, growing back from the root.

"The story," she said. "Tell me the story again. All of it. And this time—" she looked at her shining hands, and then at him, and there was something new in her face, something fierce and familiar that made his throat tighten— "this time, don't leave anything out. I want to know exactly who I was. Because I think I'm becoming her again. And this time, I'd like to choose it on purpose."

Dain sat down on the dike beside her, in the last of the light, and opened his mouth to begin.
