## 2. The Sigil on My Skin

I did not sleep so much as lie awake negotiating with my own conscience, and losing.

The map lay beneath the loose floorboard under my cot, wrapped in a spare shift that smelled of lamp-oil and old ink. Every few minutes I told myself I would take it to Elder Cassian in the morning and confess everything—the stranger at the door, the blood on his lips, the words *thirteen turns* spoken like a coin pressed into a beggar's palm. And every few minutes another voice, smaller and meaner and much more honest, reminded me that Elder Cassian would burn it before breakfast, and then a man would have died for nothing, which seemed like poor stewardship of a death.

I was, after all, a scribe. We are trained from childhood to believe that nothing written should be destroyed lightly. It is practically doctrine. Somewhere in the Rule of the Valley there is a line about how the burning of a book is a small murder, and I had always assumed this was hyperbole invented by people who had never had to copy out the same harvest-prayer four hundred times.

So: the map stayed under the floorboard. The stranger stayed dead on the scriptorium step, carried off by the temple watch before dawn. And I stayed awake, listening to the valley breathe its windless, magicless breath around me, waiting for the feeling of having done something terrible to arrive.

It never quite did. What arrived instead, sometime past midnight, was heat.

* * *

It began in my wrist. Not pain, exactly—pain has manners, pain announces itself—but a warmth like a coal held too close, growing closer by degrees. I sat up in the dark and pushed back my sleeve and watched, with the detached interest of a man watching someone else's disaster, as a mark rose through the skin of my inner wrist like a bruise blooming in fast motion.

It was not a bruise. Bruises do not have geometry.

Lines drew themselves in fine black strokes, precise as anything I could have made with my best quill, and I am very good with a quill. A circle first, then a ring of small characters inside it—not letters of any alphabet I knew, though they *wanted* to be known, the way a word sits on the tip of your tongue. Then a central figure like an eye crossed with a key. When the last stroke settled, the whole thing pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat deciding whether to bother.

"Well," I said aloud, to my empty cell, because saying things aloud is how I keep from screaming. "That's new."

I tried washing it off. I tried scrubbing it with pumice until my wrist went pink and offended. I held it over the candle flame until the heat became genuinely alarming, and the sigil simply absorbed the light, drinking the glow the way dry ground drinks rain, and gave me back a faint smug warmth in return. Marks, as a rule, do not have expressions. This one managed it anyway.

At some point—I want to say it was out of scholarly rigor, but let us be honest, it was panic dressed in a scholar's robe—I fetched the map from under the floorboard and unrolled it by candlelight.

The blood-ink was gone. Where yesterday the valleys and roads had been drawn in rust-brown lines that seemed still wet, now the parchment showed only blank vellum, smooth and innocent as a lied-about childhood. Every drop of it had gone somewhere. I looked at my wrist. My wrist looked back, if a wrist can be said to look.

"You couldn't have asked," I told it. "There's a whole rite for taking on a mark. There's consent involved. There's paperwork."

The sigil pulsed. I chose to interpret this as an apology. It was easier than the alternative.

I thought about the stranger's face as he died—grey as ash, eyes going in and out of focus, and yet in his last lucid moment he had looked at me with something terribly like relief, as though he'd been carrying a stone up a mountain for years and had finally found somewhere to set it down. He hadn't grabbed the nearest person. The scriptorium door opened onto a courtyard used by a dozen brothers and sisters at all hours. He had waited, I realized now, for *me*. He had looked at me coming across the flagstones and chosen.

Which raised a question I was not remotely ready to answer: how does a dying man know a debt-bound copyist in a hidden valley is the right person to inherit a burning secret?

I rolled up the blank map, put it back under the floorboard, and lay down again. The sigil glowed faintly against the dark like a banked fire. Sleep, when it finally took me near dawn, was the deep unconsciousness of the truly doomed.

* * *

They came at midmorning, while I was copying the ninety-third iteration of a prayer for gentle rain, which given recent events struck me as darkly funny.

Brother Halloway found me first—he always found me first; he had a nose for trouble the way pigs have a nose for truffles—and leaned over my desk with the careful casualness of a man who has rehearsed looking casual. Halloway was one of Elder Cassian's enforcers, which in the valley meant he mostly enforced quiet and tidiness, but he wore his authority like a coat two sizes too big and was forever shrugging it back onto his shoulders.

"Oscar." A pause, weighted. "Elder Cassian would like a word."

"Of course." I blotted my page with steady hands. I would like it noted, for the record, that my hands were steady. Inside, something was doing a frantic little dance. "Is this about the ink requisitions? Because I only asked twice, and the second time Sister Vey said—"

"It isn't about ink."

"Ah," I said.

The elder's study smelled of cedar and cold tea, as it always did. Elder Cassian sat behind his desk like a stone that had learned to disapprove. He was not unkind—Cassian had taken me in at nine years old, fed me, taught me letters, and never once asked about the family that left me at the border stones—but kindness in Cassian came pre-wrapped in duty, and duty today was sitting prominently on the desk between us in the form of a folded grey cloak.

"This was found near the body," he said. "The watch believes it belongs to the man who died on your step."

"I wouldn't know."

"No," Cassian agreed mildly. "You wouldn't. Which is why I am asking rather than accusing. The man came here deliberately, Oscar. Across the border stones, where nothing magical can survive, where no road leads unless you know exactly what you're looking for. Men do not make that journey by accident, and they do not make it to die politely on our doorstep." His eyes were pale and patient and missed nothing. "Did he give you anything?"

My wrist burned. Not painfully—a warmth, insistent, like a hand tugging a sleeve. I kept both hands flat on my knees and thanked every saint of the scriptorium that sleeves were long and elders were old.

"He spoke," I said. "Two words. 'Thirteen turns.' Then he died."

Cassian was silent long enough that the silence developed texture. "Thirteen turns," he repeated, tasting it. "Turns of what, I wonder."

"The moon? A wheel? A spitted goose?" I offered a smile. It landed somewhere in the middle distance and died there.

"Search his effects," Cassian said—not to me; to Halloway by the door—"and search the scriptorium. Gently. If the man left something behind, I want it found and brought to me. Whatever it is, it doesn't belong in this valley, and it will not stay in it." His gaze returned to me, and for a moment the patience dropped away and I saw underneath it something I had never seen in him before, and it frightened me more than any anger could have.

He was afraid.

"Go to your cell, Oscar. Remain there."

I remained there for approximately eleven minutes, which I felt demonstrated admirable compliance.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing the only home you've ever known: it takes almost no time at all. I had lived twenty-two years in that valley and it took one bundle—one shift, one spare quill, one hoarded heel of bread, one blank map that I told myself I was bringing only so it could be properly disposed of beyond the stones, and even as I thought it I knew I was lying to myself with the fluency of a professional.

The border stones stood in the western pass, seven great grey monoliths older than the temple, older than the Rule, worn smooth by weather that never quite touched them. Beyond them the pass fell away toward the lowlands, green and ordinary and enormous. I had seen the outside world exactly once, from this spot, at nine years old, on the day I arrived. I had spent thirteen years not thinking about it, which is a full-time occupation and leaves little room for anything else.

Behind me, down the hill, voices. Lanterns. Halloway's unmistakable stride, the one that sounded like a man applauding himself with his feet.

My wrist was on fire now—truly fire, white and climbing—and as I stumbled between the first pair of stones I understood, in the way one understands a falling thing just before it lands, that the sigil was pulling. That it had been pulling all along, patiently, and that the stranger's dying gift had never once considered asking my permission because it had never once doubted my answer.

I crossed the line between the stones.

Magic touched me.

I don't have better words than that, and I am, again, a professional with words. It was like being noticed. All my life I had been the sort of person the world's eye slid past—invisible, debt-bound, safe—and now something vast and ancient and utterly indifferent turned its attention upon me the way the sun turns upon a patch of ground, without malice, without mercy, complete. It poured through the sigil and up my arm and into my chest and I made a sound I will deny to my grave, half sob and half laugh, and fell to my knees on the far side of the stones with the whole weight of existence resting briefly, horribly, wonderfully on my spine.

And underneath the terror—shamefully, unmistakably, irreversibly—was joy.

Because it was *real*. The valley was warm and safe and dead, a hearth with the chimney sealed, and this—this roaring, sickening, endless *more*—was alive. Thirteen years of copying other people's prayers in a place where no prayer was ever answered, and here, on my knees in wet grass, something had finally, finally answered *me*.

Lantern-light bobbed at the top of the pass. Halloway's voice, sharp: "There! By the stones—stop!"

I got up. My legs shook. My wrist blazed like a lantern of its own, and I hid it in my sleeve out of pure habit, and then—laughing breathlessly at my own absurdity—pulled the sleeve back and let it shine, because I was beyond the stones now, and hiding was a valley skill, and I had just resigned.

"Sorry, Brother Halloway!" I called up the hill, giddy and appalled at myself in equal measure. "Tell Elder Cassian the ink requisition can wait!"

Then I turned south, toward the lowlands, toward the capital I had only ever copied onto maps, following a road I could not see and a dead man's instructions I could not read—yet—with thirteen turns ticking somewhere in my burning skin like a clock learning to count.

Behind me, the valley's safety fell away like a coat I'd outgrown.

Ahead, the dark was enormous, and for the first time in my life, so was I.
