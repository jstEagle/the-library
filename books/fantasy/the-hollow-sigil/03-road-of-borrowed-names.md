## 3. Road of Borrowed Names

The first thing I learned about the world beyond the border stones was that it smelled like onions.

Not the holy, incense-and-vellum smell of the scriptorium. Not the cold mineral hush of the valley air, which had been scrubbed of everything but prayer for as long as anyone could remember. Onions. Frying in fat, in a blackened pot over a fire that crackled with an ease I found frankly suspicious. In the valley, fire was something you asked permission to light.

"You going to stand there gawping all night, or are you going to eat?"

The woman behind the pot was broad as a door and twice as weathered, her forearms dusted with flour and her gray hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of bacon rind. She'd fished me out of the ditch outside Tallowmere three days ago, half-starved and full of questions I couldn't answer, and had not once asked any of her own.

"I can pay," I said, which was a lie. I had four coppers and a temple token that no one outside the valley would honor even if I'd been fool enough to show it.

"Mm." She ladled stew into a tin bowl and shoved it at me. "That's what the last one said too."

I sat down on an upturned crate by the fire and ate, because my body had stopped consulting my pride somewhere around the second day of walking. Around us, the caravan settled into its evening shapes—wagons drawn into a ring, oxen lowing, children chasing each other between the wheels while their mothers pretended not to watch. A dozen families bound for the capital, for work or luck or simply because anywhere was better than where they'd been.

I had told them my name was Tam. It was the first name I'd ever chosen for myself, and I turned it over in my mouth sometimes like a coin I wasn't sure was real. *Tam*. A scribe's name, small and unremarkable. A name that wouldn't follow anyone home.

"Tam," Maren said now, in exactly the tone she used when she knew I was lying about something but hadn't decided whether it mattered. "You've got ink on your collar again."

I looked down. Of course I did. Old habits die harder than temples do.

* * *

It was Maren who taught me the caravan's rules, mostly by violating them herself. Rule one: don't ask where people came from. Rule two: don't ask where they're going. Rule three: the cook's word is law, except when it contradicts the driver's, in which case both are wrong and the oxen decide.

She talked the way other people breathed—constant, warm, requiring no response. About her late husband ("a good man who died doing what he loved, which was arguing with a horse"), about her daughter in the capital ("writes letters full of words I have to sound out, so I know she's clever"), about the price of pepper ("robbery, plain robbery, and I say that as a woman who has committed actual robbery").

In exchange, I did sums for her. Accounts, portions, the arithmetic of feeding forty mouths on roads that ate money like locusts. It was honest work, the first I'd ever done that wasn't measured in prayers per page, and I found—to my considerable alarm—that I didn't hate it.

It was on the fourth morning, balancing her ledger against a sack of dried plums, that I discovered what the sigil could really do.

Maren kept her accounts in a little grease-stained book, and half her entries were in a cipher her husband had invented—"so the tax men stay honest," she said, meaning *so we could cheat them*. I'd been squinting at it for an hour when the sigil on my wrist warmed, gently, like a hand laid over mine.

And the cipher opened.

Not translated—*opened*, the way a locked door opens when you suddenly remember you were always holding the key. The numbers rearranged themselves in my mind into plain figures. And beneath them, fainter, I felt something else: the ghost of her husband's hand moving across these pages years ago, the little flourish he made on his sevens, the smudge where he'd laughed and dragged his sleeve through wet ink.

"Oh," I said aloud, before I could stop myself.

"What?"

"Nothing." I closed the book. My heart was hammering. "Your plums cost more than you think they do."

"They always do," Maren said darkly, and took the book back without suspicion, and I sat there in the wagon's shade feeling like a man who has discovered he can walk through walls and hasn't yet decided whether that makes him free or monstrous.

Because it wasn't just ciphers. Over the next two days, testing carefully, shamefully, I found that the sigil read *everything*. A merchant's contract, with all the clauses meant to be missed. A letter a young drover was reading and rereading by the fire, from a girl who loved him and hadn't said so. A tattered primer belonging to one of the children, in which someone—her mother, I guessed, from the careful, self-taught letterforms—had copied out the alphabet in the margins, learning alongside her daughter in secret.

Every written thing, laid open to me. Every secret that had ever been trusted to paper.

I was a scribe. I had spent eleven years copying other people's prayers, and I had believed—I had *needed* to believe—that ink was sacred precisely because it was honest. That what was written down was what was true.

The sigil didn't care what I believed. It just read.

* * *

It was the notice that changed things.

We'd stopped at a crossroads market called Fenlow's Rest, a sprawl of carts and canvas that smelled of wet wool and frying fish, and Maren had sent me to buy salt while she haggled over a wheel. I was passing the message post—the great board where towns nailed up notices, lost dogs and missing persons and prices of grain—when the sigil flared hot against my pulse.

I stopped. Turned. Read.

The notice was fresh, the ink barely a day old, and it bore the seal of the capital: a crown above an open book. The drawing was crude—a face rendered by someone working from a description rather than a likeness—but the description itself was precise enough to turn my stomach.

*A scribe, age indeterminate, of unremarkable appearance. Answers to unknown name. Marked upon the left wrist with a sigil of unknown provenance. Last seen fleeing the temple valley. Considered dangerous. Considered—*

Considered *what?* The next word had been scraped off the board, deliberately, by someone with a knife and strong feelings. But the wood held its scars, and the sigil read those too, whispering up through the gouges like a voice through a floor:

*Considered stolen property.*

I stood in the market crowd with salt in my hands and my own face described on a board ten feet away, and I understood, with terrible clarity, that the elders had not wanted the map burned out of piety. They had wanted it burned because it was *mine*—because whatever slept under that blood-inked sigil belonged to someone, and that someone had hidden it in a valley where magic cannot follow, and the temple had spent my whole life guarding a vault without knowing what was inside.

A boy beside me was reading the same notice. He glanced at me, glanced at the drawing, glanced again.

I smiled at him, bland as milk, and walked away at exactly the pace of a man with nothing to hide, and only let my hands shake once I was around the corner.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep. I climbed out of the wagon and found Maren awake by the banked fire, mending a shirt that had clearly been mended before, stitching over patches over patches like sediment layers.

"Can't sleep either?" she said, without looking up. "Sit, then. Talking's free."

I sat. For a while neither of us said anything, which was its own kind of company. Then, because I am a coward in most things but apparently not this one, I asked:

"Maren. What do people say about the king?"

Her needle paused. "Huh. Funny question."

"Funny how?"

"Funny that everyone asks it eventually, on this road, and nobody asks it at home." She bit her thread and set the shirt down. "What do they say in your—wherever you're from?"

"Very little. We prayed for his health. That was mostly it."

"Well." She poked the fire. Sparks went up and died among the stars, which seemed presumptuous of them. "They say he hasn't spoken aloud in two years. Not a word. Everything comes by edict now, written and sealed, read out by heralds. They say—" she dropped her voice, though the nearest wagon was thirty feet off and its occupants snoring like a landslide, "—they say the last thing he said out loud was at his sister's funeral. Just her name. Once. And then nothing after."

"That could be grief," I said. "People go quiet with grief."

"People do," Maren agreed comfortably. "Kings don't get to. That's the whole trouble with kings. Grief is a luxury, like sleeping in." She looked at me sidelong, firelight catching the deep lines of her face. "But here's the part I don't like, Tam, and I'll thank you not to repeat it. The edicts contradict each other. Grain prices up one week, down the next. A tax forgiven, then collected double. My girl writes that the palace heralds read proclamations in different voices—one week the king loves the river guilds, next week he's drowned their charter. Same seal. Same hand, they say, on every page."

"Same hand," I repeated. Something cold walked up my spine. *Records altered mid-page, in the same hand, dated years apart.* No—that was ahead of me; I hadn't seen the archive yet. But the phrase snagged in me like a hook in wool.

"Handwriting's a funny thing," Maren went on. "My husband used to say you can forge a man's signature easy enough, but you can't forge his habits. The way he crosses his tees. The words he reaches for when he's tired." She picked the shirt back up. "Whoever's writing these edicts knows Aldric's hand perfect. Better than perfect. Like it grew there."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"And what do you think?" I asked quietly. "About the king?"

Maren was silent long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, her voice had lost its performance, gone down to the plain timber underneath.

"I think a man who stops speaking out loud has either nothing left to say, or something sitting on his tongue that won't let him." She threaded her needle. "And I think whoever's writing in his name is very good at it, and very hungry, and I'm glad my business is onions."

I laughed—actually laughed, a rusty sound I barely recognized—and she looked pleased with herself, and for a moment the road was just a road and the night was just a night, and I was a cook's assistant named Tam with salt-stained hands and no history worth a wanted notice.

Then the sigil pulsed against my wrist. Not the warm reading-pulse. Something sharper. Insistent. Like a finger tapping glass.

I looked down. In the firelight, faint as breath on a window, new lines were surfacing in the skin of my forearm—not the original sigil, but writing beneath it, rising slowly the way bodies rise in water. Words in a hand I was beginning to know the way Maren knew her husband's habits: the dead archivist's hand, the hand that had bled a map into being.

Three words surfaced, and stopped, waiting for the rest:

*If you are reading this, you must reach the archive before the thirteenth—*

The rest sank back beneath my skin, unfinished, and the sigil went still as a held breath.

Thirteen turns, the stranger had whispered. Thirteen turns of what?

I looked east, where the road ran toward the capital and the capital's crown-topped towers, invisible still, but no longer unimaginable. Two hundred miles of open country between me and an answer, and somewhere behind me, someone had already described my face to strangers.

"Tam," Maren said, watching me with eyes far sharper than her flour-dusted softness suggested. "You look like a man who's just remembered an appointment."

"You could say that," I managed.

"Well." She threw another stick on the fire, practical as sunrise. "Best not be late, then."
