## 4. The City That Remembers Wrong

The capital announced itself to us three days before we saw it, the way a fever announces itself before the rash.

First came the traffic. Maren's caravan swelled from a dozen wagons to a river of them—farmers hauling early apples, pilgrims in mismatched festival colors, a man transporting an entire cage of doves who spent the whole journey complaining that the birds were eating him out of house and home. Then came the banners. They hung from every lamppost and bridge along the last stretch of the King's Road, deep blue silk shot with silver, and on each one the same device: a crown above an open book.

"The Seal of Aldric the Learned," Maren said, catching me staring. She said it the way you'd recite something you'd memorized as a child and never once examined. "He opened the royal library to common folk, you know. First king in six generations to do it."

"Did he?" I asked.

"That's what the banner says." She shrugged her enormous shoulders and went back to arguing with the dove man about tolls.

I had learned by then not to push Maren when she quoted things. She was a woman built entirely of proverbs and stew, warm as a hearthstone and about as given to questioning herself. But I noticed—and I want it recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head—that she'd told me two weeks earlier, on a cold night outside Harrow Ford, that King Aldric had closed the royal library to commoners after a fire. She'd been quite definite about it. She'd even said her cousin worked there and lost his position.

When I mentioned this, gently, over breakfast, she frowned at her porridge for a long moment.

"Well," she said finally, "one of those is true."

"And which one?"

"I don't know." She looked up at me with the frank, untroubled honesty that made traveling under a false name beside her feel less like a crime and more like a game. "But I'll tell you what I *do* know, scribe. My cousin exists. Or existed. And one of these days somebody's going to explain to me why I can't hold onto which side of him is true."

The sigil on my wrist gave a single, slow pulse against my pulse-point, like a second heart clearing its throat.

* * *

We came through the Southgate at midmorning, into a city so loud I could taste it. Copperleaf, they called it—the City of Ten Thousand Bells—and it earned the name. Bells marked the hours, the markets, the births, the executions, the arrival of dignitaries, and apparently, this week, the king's birthday.

Except the king's birthday, according to a baker I spoke with while Maren haggled over caravan space near the Tallowmarket, had been celebrated last month. Lavishly. With a parade.

"He has two?" I asked.

The baker, a round woman dusted to the elbows in flour, gave me the look reserved for idiots and foreigners. "Everyone gets one birthday a year, love. His Majesty just gets... well." She lowered her voice, though the street around us roared on. "Some years he seems to have more than others. You get used to it. There's cake either way."

I bought a roll I didn't want, mostly because she seemed proud of it, and walked away chewing on more than bread.

Because here was the thing I kept noticing, the splinter under the skin of the whole glittering city: everyone loved their king, and no two of them could agree on *why*. The baker praised him for ending the grain tax. A carter ten feet away cursed him for imposing it. A student on the steps of the Chancery told me Aldric the Learned had founded the great public schools; his classmate insisted the schools predated him by a century and the king merely restored them. Both cited edicts. Both cited dates. Both were certain.

And the edicts themselves—pasted up on the Chancery wall in beautiful formal script, which of course I stopped to read, because I am a scribe and proclamations are my native habitat—read like a conversation between two men who hated each other:

*By order of the Crown, the tithe on river-fish shall be remitted, that the poor may eat.*

Dated the first of Last Seed.

*By order of the Crown, the tithe on river-fish shall be levied at double rate, that the treasury may be filled.*

Dated the ninth of Last Seed.

Same month. Same seal. Same gorgeous, careful hand—I stood close enough to touch the ink, and the hand was identical down to the flourish on the final letter. One man had written both. Eight days apart. Either the king had changed his entire philosophy of fish in a week, or—

Or something was wearing him, and hadn't yet learned to keep its story straight.

My wrist burned. Not painfully; the sigil had settled into a kind of weather by then, a barometric pressure behind the skin that rose whenever written lies were near. I pressed my sleeve down over it and made myself walk away before I could be seen reading the same proclamation four times with an expression the city watch might find interesting.

Maren found me at the wagon an hour later, sitting very still.

"You've got your thinking face," she said. "The one where you look like you're proofreading the world and it keeps failing."

"It does keep failing," I said.

She sat down beside me on the tailboard, which groaned but held, and handed me half an apple without comment. We watched the bells ring the noon hour—nine of them, staggered, because no one could agree which tower was authoritative.

"Maren," I said. "Why did you come to the capital? Really. You could have stayed with the caravan all season."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "My cousin. The one at the library." She turned the apple core over in her fingers. "I got a letter from him, two years back. Saying he'd found something wrong in the records and was going to fix it. And then I got another letter saying he'd never written the first one, and would I stop worrying, everything at the archive was fine, better than fine, the finest in the world." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Two letters. Same handwriting. Different men inside them. So when a green-eyed scribe with a haunted wrist asks to come to Copperleaf, I come to Copperleaf."

I hadn't told her about the sigil. I still haven't, properly. But Maren had a way of knowing things sideways, the way you know rain is coming from the smell rather than the sky.

"The archive," I said. "The royal one. Would your cousin's name open doors?"

"No," she said. "But yours will. Scribes are scribes everywhere, love. Ink is the one country with no borders."

* * *

The Royal Archive of Copperleaf occupied a wedge-shaped palace of white stone between the Chancery and the old temple district, and it smelled the way my whole childhood should have smelled: paper, leather, lamp oil, and dust settling on knowledge like snow.

Getting in took less effort than it should have, which was itself a warning I failed to read. I presented my temple credentials—genuine documents, at least; my forgery skills were honest ones, in that I'd only ever forged permission slips for myself—and the under-librarian, a nervous young man named Pell whose eyebrows appeared to be attempting escape, waved me through with barely a glance.

"Scribe Winterbourne, of the Valley Temple," I said.

"Oh, thank goodness," said Pell. "A valley scribe. You people don't... " He hesitated. "You don't remember things oddly, do you?"

"I'm sorry?"

"Nothing. Nothing. Third gallery, past the map room. Mind the ladder on the left, it bites." He was already retreating. "Records requests go through the morning clerk. If the morning clerk isn't available, don't wait for him. He's been unavailable for some time."

"For how long?"

Pell's eyebrows performed their final migration. "That depends," he said delicately, "on which record you consult."

The third gallery was exactly what I'd hoped for and therefore exactly the trap I should have expected: the complete civic record of the kingdom, year by year, bound in blue calfskin, indexed in a hand so beautiful it made my teeth ache. I requested the volumes covering the last six years—the span since the letters in the dead archivist's map had gone silent—and carried them to a reading table by the window, where the light was good and honest.

For an hour, everything was fine. More than fine. It was the happiest hour I'd spent since leaving the valley, hunched over the flowing record of a living country: harvest tallies, court appointments, a lawsuit over a disputed goose that ran to eleven pages and ended in reconciliation. I copied excerpts with the pure, uncomplicated joy of my trade, and if the sigil murmured occasionally—a faint warmth when I passed over a date, a flicker at a name—I dismissed it as indigestion of the soul.

Then I reached the fourth year of the record, and the goose lawsuit, and I stopped.

I had already read this page. An hour ago. In the second volume, dated a full year later, a different dispute had cited this exact case as precedent—*Harrow v. the Tallowmarket Guild*, eleven pages, resolved by shared custody of the animal. But here, in its original volume, the case ran only four pages and ended with the goose being awarded outright to Harrow.

I checked the citation. I checked it again. I checked it a third time with the specific, trembling care of a person hoping to find themselves wrong.

Same case number. Same parties. Two different outcomes, a year apart, both entered into the permanent, inviolable civic record of the realm.

My hands were steady. I want that noted. Whatever else happened in that gallery, my hands were steady, because twelve years of temple training teaches you that a scribe's hands are the last thing allowed to panic.

I turned back through the volume, slowly now, reading like a forger reads—looking not at what was written but at how. And there, on the corrected goose page, I found it. A seam. Invisible to any ordinary eye: the ink matched, the hand matched, the parchment aged uniformly. But the *rhythm* was wrong. Every scribe has a heartbeat in their letters, a spacing that drifts and returns like breath. This page breathed perfectly. Too perfectly. Uniform as fence posts, line after line, in a way no living hand sustains across four hundred words.

Someone—or something—had rewritten the page. In place. On old parchment. In ink that had learned to be old.

I pulled other volumes. Once I knew the rhythm to listen for, I heard it everywhere. A tax ledger with a revised figure that breathed too evenly. A court appointment reversed mid-reign, the new entry wearing the old date like a stolen coat. A death record for a librarian—name smudged, cause listed as "fever"—entered in a hand identical to entries made thirty years before the man could possibly have died.

The same hand. Across decades. Across generations of official record. One hand, patient and perfect and utterly without a heartbeat, threading itself through the memory of the entire kingdom.

The sigil on my wrist was burning now, steadily, and when I looked down—carefully, casually, shielding it with my sleeve—I saw the mark had changed. The lines of it had shifted, rearranged themselves into something almost legible. Almost a word.

Almost a *name*.

And beneath my fingers, the open death record—the smudged name, the impossible hand—had begun, very faintly, to glow at its margins, as if the page remembered being something else and wanted, desperately, to say so.

I blew out my reading lamp, closed the volumes with a softness that cost me everything, and got up from the table with the unhurried gait of a man who had definitely found nothing at all.

Behind me, in the emptying gallery, a voice that sounded exactly like under-librarian Pell called out, cheerfully:

"Find everything you were looking for, scribe?"

I turned. The gallery was empty.

The lamps were all still lit.

And on the desk where I'd been sitting, someone had laid out a fresh sheet of parchment, a pot of ink, and a pen, arranged with terrible neatness—as if inviting me to sit back down and write whatever version of today they preferred.

The sigil flared white-hot against my wrist, and for the first time since the stranger died at the scriptorium door, I heard it speak—not in words, but in certainty, the way you know your own name:

*They know you're here. Run.*
