## 5. What the Crown Wears

The palace kitchens took me in the way large institutions take in anything useful and slightly damaged: without questions.

I had come to the capital with a scribe's credentials, a forged letter of introduction, and Maren's parting gift of a meat pie that I was fairly sure contained more gristle than meat. The credentials did their work. Within two days I was copying inventory lists for the Master of Stores—a man with ink-stained cuffs and the mournful expression of someone who had once dreamed of commanding armies and now commanded barrels of salted herring—and within four days I had learned that the servants' corridor behind the great hall led, if one turned left at the tapestry of the Huntress, directly past the doors of the royal presence chamber.

I learned this from a laundry maid named Bett who had the cheerful indiscretion of a woman who had worked in palaces long enough to find them boring.

"The king doesn't hold audiences anymore," she told me, snapping sheets on the line like she was trying to whip them into obedience. "Not real ones. He sits there while the Lord Chamberlain reads things *at* him. Sometimes he nods. Sometimes he doesn't. Nobody can tell what makes the difference."

"Perhaps he's shy," I offered.

Bett gave me the look reserved for new staff who were either very stupid or very funny, and hadn't yet decided which. "Shy. Aye. Kings are famously shy." She snapped another sheet. "You're the new scribe. Winterbourne. You've got ink on your ear."

I did. I always did. It was the one thing about myself I'd never managed to forge convincingly.

* * *

Inside King Aldric's mind, there was a room.

He had built it himself, in the early days—back when the thing wearing his crown was still learning the shape of him, back when he still believed that somewhere inside his own skull there might be a door it couldn't open. The room had no windows. It had a chair, and a table, and on the table a single candle that never burned down because Aldric could not remember how candles worked anymore, only that they should burn.

Through the walls of the room, muffled as snowfall, came the sounds of his life continuing without him.

Today they were sounds of court. The rustle of silk. The Chancellor's voice, pitched in that careful, honeyed register the man used when lying. And underneath it all—the sound Aldric had come to dread most, the sound like a spoon scraping the bottom of a jar—his own voice, answering.

*Yes,* said the king's mouth, out there beyond the walls. *The eastern levy shall stand.*

*I never agreed to any eastern levy,* Aldric thought, uselessly, from inside his room. He thought it every day. It changed nothing. Thinking, he had discovered, was not the same as being heard. He was a man shouting down a well and hearing only his own echo come back wrong.

That was the worst of it—not the watching. He had made his peace with the watching, mostly. The Vessel wore him the way a glove is worn: completely, patiently, with attention to every finger. When it smiled with his mouth, the smile reached his eyes, because the Vessel had studied his eyes. When it wept at his daughter's memorial—which it had attended twice now, having forgotten between the first and second that it had already grieved—the tears were perfectly calibrated.

No. The worst of it was the forgetting. Each morning Aldric woke in his candlelit room knowing slightly less than he had known the morning before. He had written names on the walls once, in the early months. His wife. His daughter. His mother. The words had faded like breath on glass, and he could not remember what writing was, or how to do it again.

But some things stayed. Some things the Vessel could not digest, the way a body cannot digest a cherry stone.

He remembered a valley where magic could not follow.

He did not know why he remembered it. He did not know why, of all the lost furniture of his mind, that one fact remained—stubborn, unexplained, glowing faintly in the dark of his room like an ember that refused its nature. But he held onto it. He sat in his chair and he held it, and outside, the king who was not him went on signing edicts that contradicted each other week to week, and Aldric thought, with the last of his defiance:

*Someone will come from the valley. Someone always comes from the valley eventually.*

He did not know why he believed this either. Belief, it turned out, was another cherry stone.

* * *

Bett's gossip got me as far as the antechamber.

This required a small crime, which I committed with the trembling competence of a man who had spent his life committing only paper crimes. The Master of Stores required a copyist at the afternoon petition session—something about reconciling the grain tallies against the Chamberlain's figures, which, Bett had noted with a snort, was like reconciling a cat against a bowl of cream. So I carried my board and my ink through the left turn at the Huntress tapestry, past the guards who looked through scribes the way they looked through furniture, and into the antechamber of the royal presence, where petitioners waited on benches to be heard by a king who mostly wasn't listening.

I told myself I was only observing. That was the scriptorium training talking: *observe, record, do not touch.* But the sigil on my wrist had other ideas. It warmed against my skin as I entered, faint and insistent, like a cat pressing its head against your hand, and when I glanced at the petitioners' faces, I began to *read* them.

Not their papers. Them.

It came in flickers, half-second glimpses, the way you catch your reflection in a shop window without meaning to. A farmer clutching a deed saw, in my presence, a memory of his father teaching him to plow. An old woman petitioning for her son's pardon carried, folded small behind her eyes, the memory of his birth. The sigil drank these things passively, greedily, and showed them to me whether I wanted them or not.

And then the doors opened, and the king came in, and every flicker in the room bent toward him like grass toward wind.

* * *

Aldric felt the sigil before he understood it.

It came through the walls of his room like sunlight through shutters—one thin blade of warmth across the floor, impossible, wrong, *wonderful*. Nothing warm entered his room. The Vessel kept it cold; cold kept him quiet.

He stood up. His legs remembered how, barely.

*There,* the warmth said, or seemed to say. *There you are.*

For the first time in two years, King Aldric pressed his hands against the walls of his own mind and pushed.

* * *

The king crossed the presence chamber, and I watched the court bow, and I watched something else besides—something I would have missed entirely if the sigil hadn't been humming under my skin like a struck bell.

For exactly three seconds, the king's face *changed*.

It was subtle. The courtiers saw nothing; they were busy bowing. But the mask slipped—or rather, something behind the mask surged forward, the way a drowning man's hand breaks water. The king's eyes found the room, truly found it, moved across the petitioners with desperate speed, and stopped.

On me.

On my wrist, specifically, where the sigil pulsed beneath my sleeve like a trapped moth.

His mouth opened. The court waited for wisdom. The Lord Chamberlain leaned in, quill ready.

"*Herring,*" said the King of the Realm, distinctly and with tremendous feeling.

A pause. "*The herring levies,*" the Chamberlain corrected smoothly, "shall be reviewed, majesty, yes—"

"No," said the king. And then, quieter, with an urgency that raised every hair on my arms: "*Count them.*"

The Chamberlain smiled the smile of a man managing a difficult relative. The audience continued. Petitions were heard and dismissed in the king's voice, in that flat reciting tone Bett had described. But I had seen it. I had *felt* it through the sigil—a pressure, a knocking, a man's whole self thrown against a locked door from the inside.

*Count them,* he had said. To me. Or to whoever wore the sigil. Count the herring. Count the tallies. Count *something*, because counting was what scribes did, and somewhere in the numbers—

My hands were shaking so badly I nearly dropped my ink pot.

* * *

Afterward, I hid in the records office and counted the herring.

It was absurd work and I did it feverishly, cross-referencing the stores ledger against the Chamberlain's requisitions, and what I found made my blood go cold and then hot and then cold again. The tallies didn't merely disagree. They disagreed *impossibly*. Barrels that existed in spring had never existed by autumn. Whole shipments signed for in the king's own hand—dated, sealed, witnessed—had no corresponding entry anywhere, as though the ledger itself had been edited by someone who could reach backward through time.

Or forward. Or sideways. I thought of the archive, the altered pages, the same hand dating itself years apart, and I understood that whatever wore the crown did not merely consume memories. It *revised* them. It fed, and then it tidied up after itself, and the tidy-up was worse than the feeding.

The old woman's son would not simply be forgotten. There would come a day when he had never been born.

* * *

In his room, Aldric felt the warmth withdraw and nearly wept.

But it had left something behind. In the place where the light had touched the wall, he could see—for the first time—faint letters. Old ones. His own handwriting, from the early days, from the month he still had hands that obeyed him:

*If anyone reads this: I am here. I am still here. Do not let them tell you the king is dead.*

He stared at it until the letters faded. Then he sat back down in his chair, beside his candle that did not burn, and he held his ember of a valley, and he waited.

*Count them,* he had said. He did not remember saying it. But he felt lighter, the way a man feels after shouting down a well and hearing, at last, not an echo—but an answer.

* * *

I found Maren that evening at the Drowned Bell, the tavern where she'd been staying, and I told her everything: the king's face changing, the word *herring* delivered like a prayer, the ledgers that ate themselves. She listened while shelling pistachios with alarming efficiency, and when I finished she was quiet for a long moment.

"You're going back in," she said. Not a question.

"I have to see it," I said. "What it does. When it feeds."

Maren cracked a shell, considered the nut, and handed it to me—an odd gesture, almost ceremonial, the cook's equivalent of a knight's blessing. "Then listen," she said. "Courtiers lie. Ledgers lie. But nobody lies with their whole face. Watch the faces, little scribe."

So I went back. Through the Huntress tapestry, past the bored guards, into the long gallery where the evening court gathered like moths around a lamp that gave no light. And there I saw it.

The Vessel—it had a name now, in my head, though I never spoke it aloud—stood beside a young courtier, a girl with quick eyes and a nervous laugh, and laid a hand upon her shoulder in what looked, to everyone watching, like paternal kindness.

The sigil screamed.

Not loudly. Silently, the way a tooth screams. Through my wrist I felt it happen: the girl's memories pouring out of her like water from a tipped jug—her childhood summers, her first love, her mother's voice singing—all of it flowing up through the king's fingers, into the king's smiling face. And the smile *brightened*. It brightened the way a lamp brightens when fed.

The girl laughed at something the king said. Her eyes had gone slightly distant. By morning she would not remember why she had ever loved music.

Pity, which had been growing in me since the antechamber, crystallized all at once into something harder and more useful. Aldric was not dead. Aldric was *buried*—buried alive, conscious, holding one ember in the dark—and the thing above him was eating his kingdom one memory at a time, and no one in this glittering room could see it except a debt-bound scribe with a dead archivist's mark burning on his wrist.

I turned to leave, to find Maren, to plan—and found my path blocked by the Lord Chamberlain, whose smile did not reach his eyes.

"Master Winterbourne," he said pleasantly. "The king wishes to see you. Alone."
