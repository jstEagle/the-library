## 7. Thirteenth Turn Rising

*Oscar*

Three nights.

The words kept arriving in my head the way bad news always did—first as arithmetic, then as weight. Three nights until the thirteenth moon slid fully into eclipse and whatever binding held the Vessel to King Aldric's body became permanent, unbreakable, a marriage of parasite and throne that no rite could annul. I had learned this from a dead woman's letters written in cipher on the back of a map drawn in blood, which was, I reflected, a truly miserable way to receive one's schedule for saving the kingdom.

I sat in the back room of Maren's cousin's cookshop with the letters spread across a flour-dusted table, and I tried very hard not to think about how much I had come to rely on the smell of onions and woodsmoke to feel safe.

"You're doing the thing," Maren said, setting down two bowls of stew that smelled like forgiveness after a long day. "The scribe thing. Where you stare at a page like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money," I said. "Everything in this city owes me something. That's rather the problem."

"Drink your stew."

I drank my stew. It was good, because everything Maren made was good, and she cooked the way other people prayed—with total commitment and an assumption that someone was watching. She'd taken me back in without a single question when I'd turned up on her doorstep hollow-eyed from the archive, though I suspected the questions were banked somewhere behind her ribs, waiting for a cold night.

"The guard will come at moonrise," I said.

"And you're sure he can be trusted?"

"He can't be trusted at all. That's what makes him useful. A man who knows exactly how much he's worth to the palace has no illusions left to sell." I wiped my hands. "Besides, he hates the Vessel more than he fears it. I've read his service record. All forty pages of it, including the parts they tried to burn."

Maren snorted. "You're a terrifying little creature, Oscar Winterbourne. Has anyone told you?"

"My temple elders, frequently. Usually while burning my work."

There came a knock at the back door—three slow raps, then one fast, exactly as arranged—and Maren let in a man who filled the doorway like bad weather. Ser Dain Halvard, formerly of the king's own guard, currently of nowhere in particular. He had a soldier's flat way of looking at a room, cataloguing exits first and faces second, and when his eyes landed on the sigil at my wrist, uncovered because there was no longer any point hiding it, he went very still.

"That's the old mark," he said. "The archivists' mark. My grandmother had it tattooed over her heart. Said her mother had served them, before the purge."

"Your family kept archives?"

"My family kept *secrets*," Dain said, sitting heavily. "Same trade, worse pay." He looked at the letters, the map, the scattered evidence of a crime older than either of us. "Tell me what you need, scribe. And then tell me honestly how likely we are to die, because I've done the dying-for-a-king thing once already and found it overrated."

"Moderately likely," I admitted. "Improving by the night."

He nodded as if I'd quoted scripture. "Good. Hate a liar."

We planned until the candles guttered: the servant corridors Dain still knew by heart, the guard rotations he could predict because he'd written half of them himself before his disgrace, the moment on the third night—the deepest hour of the eclipse—when even the Vessel would have to be still, drawing itself inward, completing its feeding. That was our door. Narrow, dark, and guarded by nothing but timing.

It should have felt like hope. Mostly it felt like holding a lit candle in a high wind and pretending your hands weren't shaking.

* * *

*Dain*

In my years wearing the king's colors I had learned that palaces do not sleep. They only pretend to, the way old soldiers do—one eye open, listening.

So when I walked my usual circuit past the barracks at dawn and saw the new notices going up, paste still wet, I stopped pretending.

They were memory-notices. I'd seen the prototype once, in the Vessel's early days, before anyone understood what it was doing. A sheet of paper that wasn't paper, ink that moved if you looked too long. You couldn't help reading it. The words reached into your own head and pulled out something you'd given the record-keepers years ago—your oath of service, your confession at induction, the small honest truths people tell clerks and forget they've told.

Mine would be easy to write. *Ser Dain Halvard, dismissed for drunkenness and grief. Weeps on the anniversary of the fire. Blames himself.* They wouldn't even have to invent the shame. They'd just hand it back to me, polished and public, until every tavern in the capital knew the sound of my worst night.

And that was the trap of it, see. The Vessel didn't need to hunt the scribe with wanted posters and blades. It needed only to make every friend a liability, every confidence a weapon, every kindness something you'd regret having offered. It was turning the city against itself using the city's own recorded heart.

By midday there were three of them posted at the market square, glowing faintly like things seen through water. A fishwife stood before one, her face draining as the notice recited her late husband's debts in her own voice. A young clerk was on his knees in front of another, begging it to stop, though it said nothing cruel—it simply repeated him, faithfully, endlessly, the way a mirror repeats you when you wish it would look away.

A boy near the fountain caught my sleeve. "Soldier, is it true? What the walls are saying about the harbor master?"

"What are they saying?"

"Things he only told the tithe-clerks. Things nobody should know." The boy's eyes were bright with fear and something worse—appetite. The notices fed on being read, and the city was hungry.

I walked away quickly and did not look back, because I had a meeting at a cookshop, and because I already knew what I would find there: two people who trusted me, deciding whether to trust me anyway.

That's the arithmetic of treason, in the end. Not whether you're loyal. Whether the person next to you believes you are, on the day it matters.

* * *

*Oscar*

The traps found us on the second night, and they found us through the gentlest door imaginable: Maren's own ledgers.

She kept accounts for half the street—loans, favors, debts of eggs and coin and apology. It was the most honest document in the district. So of course that's where the Vessel struck. Sometime during the day, without anyone touching it, her ledger book began to speak.

Not aloud. Worse. Anyone who opened it read their own entry—every debt they owed her, every kindness she'd extended and quietly recorded, laid out in their own handwriting. The cobbler opened it and found a page itemizing how Maren had fed his children through the winter, with a note in his own hand: *never repayable*. The widow Anselm found her grief catalogued like an inventory. People came to the cookshop all evening, not angry but *ashamed*, standing in the doorway holding the ledger at arm's length like a dead animal, asking Maren why she'd written such things down, why she'd made their small dignified fictions visible.

"They're not cruel entries," Maren told me, gray-faced, wiping down a counter no one was sitting at. "That's what I keep telling them. They're kind entries. I wrote them so I'd remember who needed what. But nobody wants to be remembered that precisely."

"I know," I said softly.

Because I did. That was the whole horror of the sigil-bearer's life, laid out in flour and onions: to be recorded is to be held. The Vessel understood this better than any of us. It didn't destroy bonds between people. It *illuminated* them, until the light became unbearable.

"We should burn the ledger," Maren said.

"No. Burn nothing. That's what it wants—to make us ashamed of every record, every tie, until we cut them ourselves." I put my hand flat on the book. "It's isolating us. Me especially. Every ally becomes a page it can turn."

"Then stop reading my mind through my soup pot and tell me the plan again."

I told her. Dain would take us in through the laundry galleries on the third night. The sigil would open the archive-vault beneath the throne room where the original binding was kept. And I would do the rest, alone, in the dark, at the exact hour the moon gave up its light.

"And after?" Maren asked, and her voice did something careful. "After you tear this crown off a king. After the valley's protection breaks and magic comes flooding into a place that's never had to defend itself. After all that. What happens to the scribe who made it happen?"

I hadn't answered that question yet. I suspected the answer was a whole other book.

Before I could try, the knock came—three slow, one fast—but wrong, too early, too loud. Dain slipped inside smelling of smoke and river water, and his face told the story before his mouth did.

"They know about the cookshop," he said. "Not you two specifically. The *pattern*. Someone's been reading the city's memories backward—finding everyone who's gone quiet lately, everyone meeting in back rooms. There's a notice with my face on it three streets over. It's reciting my court-martial." He laughed, short and ugly. "It gets one detail wrong. Says I wept. I didn't weep. I laughed. But by tomorrow the whole garrison will remember the weeping version, because the wall says so, and the wall is more reliable than I am."

"Dain—" Maren started.

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't defend me, don't thank me, don't stand near me in daylight. Listen. This is how it wins, scribe. Not by catching us. By making each of us the weakest link in the other's chain. So we go tonight. Tomorrow. Not the third night."

"The rite won't work before the eclipse—"

"Then we get you *into position* tonight, and you wait in the dark under the throne room for a day and a night with that thing burning on your wrist like a beacon, praying it doesn't notice you before the moon does." He leaned across the table, and up close his eyes were bloodshot and absolutely certain. "Or we stay clever, and spread out, and safe, and lose. Choose."

The sigil chose for me. It flared against my wrist—hot, insistent, alive—and for a moment I felt what it felt: the Vessel turning its attention toward us like a vast eye opening beneath the city, patient, amused, already reading the last page of our story.

"No," I heard myself say. "Not tonight. Tonight it expects panic. Panic is a pattern too." I looked at Maren, then at Dain, and hated how much I loved them both, how much that love now weighed. "Tomorrow night, at the change of watch. And until then, we separate. No messages. No meetings."

"And if one of us is taken?" Maren asked quietly.

I thought of her ledger, of the widow's shame, of the boy at the fountain hungry for other people's secrets. I thought of chapter eight of a stranger's confession, which I had read alone at midnight and never told either of them: *the binding requires a willing memory freely surrendered, and the Vessel has always preferred to take them unwilling.*

"If one of us is taken," I said, "the others run, and finish it, and don't look back. That's the price of the page. We all agreed to pay it the day we met."

Nobody agreed now. Nobody argued either. That silence was the truest thing spoken all night.

Dain left first, vanishing into the fog off the river. Maren pressed a wrapped bundle into my hands—bread, cheese, a knife I pretended not to recognize as hers—and said, "Come back hungry," which was her way of saying *come back*, which was her way of everything.

I walked out the front like any late customer, hood up, heart hammering, and made it exactly four streets before I felt the sigil go cold.

Not warm. Not burning. *Cold*, like ice laid against the pulse, and underneath it, faint as a voice through a wall, something speaking in a cadence I almost recognized. It took me three steps to place it, and when I did, my stomach dropped through the cobblestones.

It was reciting the archivist's confession. My map. My letters. In her voice.

Someone had given the Vessel her memories. Someone had opened her grave.

And the voice was coming from the alley ahead—where a figure waited in the dark, holding out a folded piece of paper, and saying, in the archivist's stolen cadence:

"Thirteen turns, scribe. But you should hear what she left out."
