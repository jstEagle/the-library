## 8. The Price of the Page

Maren had cooked for caravans through three wars, two plagues, and one wedding that turned into a brawl over a disputed goat, and she had learned this much: you can smell a betrayal coming the way you can smell milk turning. Not sour yet. Just... off.

Dennick was off.

He'd been off since the night he told them about his family—wife dead in the Vessel's first purges, twin daughters taken to the palace nurseries and never returned. He'd told it flat, the way men tell things they've rehearsed until the grief wears smooth like a river stone. And Oscar had reached across the fire and squeezed his wrist and said something gentle and true, and Maren had watched Dennick's face while it happened, and what she saw there wasn't comfort.

It was hunger.

"You're staring at your stew like it insulted your mother," she said now, ladling him a second helping anyway. Kindness cost her nothing. It was the cheapest thing she owned and she gave it away by the barrel. "Eat. Tomorrow we walk into a palace full of people who can eat memories, so tonight we eat actual food while we've got teeth."

Dennick ate. That was the trouble with Dennick—he always did exactly what you asked. A soldier to the marrow. Soldiers follow orders, and somewhere between the safehouse and the third watch of the night, someone had given him new ones.

Maren knew because she counted spoons.

It was an old caravan trick. You laid out the tinware before bed, and if anything moved in the night—a spoon shifted, a knife turned edge-inward—you knew hands had been where hands shouldn't be. She'd done it every night since they'd started planning the eclipse run, mostly out of habit, partly because Oscar slept like a scholar (badly, hunched over papers, muttering about marginalia) and Maren figured somebody in this conspiracy ought to stay awake.

The spoon nearest Dennick's bedroll was turned edge-inward. And Dennick's boots were gone from beside it.

She lay still in the dark and listened. Below the safehouse, in the alley, a whistle—three notes, soft as a dove. Palace signal; she'd heard the guards use it outside the archive walls. Then footsteps on the stair, more than one pair, moving with the particular care of people who have been told *take them alive*.

"Maren." Dennick stood in the doorway, backlit, sword drawn but pointed at the floor, which she supposed she should be grateful for. His voice cracked right down the middle. "Don't fight them. Please. They said—they said they could give them back. Elenna and the girls. Everything they took. I'd remember their faces again. I'd—" He stopped, because there is no good way to finish that sentence.

"Sit down and eat your stew," Maren said, which was not brave, exactly. But it was hers.

They came up the stairs anyway. There were six of them, and their faces were wrong—not cruel, just *smooth*, like pages someone had wiped clean and hadn't gotten around to writing on again. The Vessel's work. Maren had seen that smoothness once before, on a spice merchant in the lower city who'd cheerfully sold her saffron at a loss and couldn't say why.

Oscar came awake halfway down the hall, and Maren heard the exact moment they understood—the small sound they made, half gasp, half apology, as though being captured were some discourtesy they'd committed against the world.

"Run," Maren told them, calm as stirring soup. "Back window, over the wall, don't look back. That's an order from your elders, scribe, and I'm older than everyone in this building."

"They'll wipe you," Oscar said, and their voice broke on the word *wipe* like a plate dropped on stone. "Maren, they'll take—"

"Then I'd best make my last ones good ones." She gripped Oscar's shoulder, brief and hard, flour-dusted fingers leaving pale prints on the borrowed cloak. "Listen to me. Whatever they put in my head after tonight, whatever stranger is wearing my face tomorrow—she likes her stew thick, she swears at goats, and she would burn down a palace for you. If any of that leaks through, you come find me. Understood?"

Oscar was crying. Silent, furious crying, the kind that hates itself for happening. "I only met you on a road."

"And I only fed you because you looked like a strong wind would take you." Maren smiled, and meant it, and pushed them toward the window with both hands. "Go be the thirteenth turn, little scribe. Someone has to."

The last thing she saw of Oscar Winterbourne was a shape going over the wall into the dark, and the last thing she thought, quite distinctly, was: *well. That's the best batch I ever raised.*

Then the smooth-faced guards were on her, and one of them held a thing like a candle made of gray light, and Maren closed her eyes and kept cooking, in her head, one final meal—for a table of people she could no longer name, in a life she was about to stop remembering, with salt enough for anything.

* * *

Oscar ran.

They ran the way they had learned to run everything in the past weeks—badly, breathlessly, with the map's blood-ink burning behind their eyes and the sigil searing their wrist like a brand fresh from the forge. Behind them the safehouse lit with lantern-light and shouting, and then, worse than shouting, silence. Maren's silence.

*Don't think about it,* Oscar told themselves, taking the drainage culvert Dennick had shown them, the one gap in the palace's outer ring. *Don't think about her flour-dusted hand. Don't think about the stew. Don't—*

But thinking was all they had. Thinking was the whole terrible gift of a scribe's mind: everything written down, nothing erased.

The culvert stank of the river and let out beneath the old granary, and from there the palace rose against the eclipsing sky like a wound in the world. Above its tallest tower the moon hung three-quarters swallowed, the thirteenth turn nearly closed, and Oscar felt the sigil pull toward it the way water pulls toward a drain.

There were fewer guards than there should have been. That frightened Oscar more than many guards would have. The Vessel wasn't defending the palace.

It was setting the table.

The servants' stair was open. The archive door, which had taken Oscar three forged credentials and a bribe to enter a month ago, swung at a touch. Every barrier they'd catalogued, every obstacle they'd spent nights mapping—gone, or moved aside, arranged with the neatness of a place setting.

*It wants me here.* Oscar's feet slowed on the marble. *Why does it want me here?*

Because of the letters. Because of what they'd found in the archivist's hidden cache, three nights ago, by candle stub: *the binding requires a bearer willing to unmake themselves.* The Vessel needed a sigil-bearer to attempt the rite. Needed them to stand in the throne room under the eclipsed moon and begin pouring themselves out—because a rite half-finished doesn't free a king. It empties a vessel. Both vessels. And then there would be nothing left in Aldric's body at all, no parasite, no king, no one, and the crown-born thing could simply move into a hollowed house and shut the door.

*It's not trying to stop me. It's trying to help me fail.*

Knowing didn't slow Oscar's feet. That was the cruelest part. Because Aldric might still be in there—buried, watching, two years of screaming behind his own eyes—and knowing the trap and walking into it anyway was the only move that left anyone alive at the end of the board.

The throne room doors were tall as trees and opened at a breath.

Inside, candles burned in their hundreds, though no servant had lit them. The eclipsed moon fell through the great window in a blade of bruised silver, and at the end of the long room, on the throne, sat King Aldric—or the thing wearing him—and it did not rise, did not call the guard, did nothing but *smile* with the king's handsome, exhausted face.

"Scribe," it said, in a voice like pages turning. "You came. How faithful. How *predictable*. Do you know, I have eaten the memories of four thousand souls, and do you know what I've learned?" It tilted the king's head. "No one ever believes they are the price. Not the soldier, not the cook, not the child. Everyone thinks the ledger balances somewhere else."

Oscar's hand found the sigil on their wrist. It was hot as a coal now, and through its heat they could feel the rite already waiting—feel, with terrible clarity, what it would take. Not blood. Not years of study. *Memories.* The fuel of the unbinding was the bearer's own past, poured out page by page, and the sigil showed them the order of the burning as plainly as a table of contents:

*First, the debts. Easy. Let them go gladly.*

*Then the temple. The scriptorium. The prayers copied ten thousand times until the words lost meaning.*

*Then the valley itself. The border stones. The safety.*

*And last—* Oscar's breath caught— *last, the childhood. What little of it remained. The smuggled child's face. The mother's voice singing in a language the archives claim is extinct.*

The rite would not kill them. That was the horror of it. It would leave them standing, breathing, *blank*—a person-shaped book with the pages pressed smooth, exactly like Maren, exactly like the courtiers, exactly like the king. They would forget why they'd come. They would forget whom they'd mourned.

They would forget Maren's hand on their shoulder, saying *go be the thirteenth turn.*

"I hesitate," the Vessel observed pleasantly, watching them from the throne. "They always hesitate. Shall I show you something to hurry you along? I keep a copy of everything, you know. Your cook, for instance. Would you like to see what she remembers now? Nothing. Not even the color of your cloak. She asked me—I mean, the guard asked me for his daughters, and I gave him those, and in exchange I took her, and oh, scribe, she went so *gently*. People always do, at the end. Like sleep. Like soup going cold."

Something in Oscar went very quiet and very cold, colder than the valley border stones, colder than ink.

Because the Vessel had made its mistake. It thought hesitation was fear. It thought Oscar stood at the edge of the rite the way Dennick had stood in that doorway—hungering, wavering, purchasable.

But Oscar stood there doing sums, as scribes do. Weighing pages. And they understood at last what the dying archivist had known in the scriptorium doorway with blood on his lips: that the binding had never required a hero. It required a *record-keeper* willing to erase their own record—and trust that someone, someday, would write it down again.

"The thirteenth turn closes in an hour," Oscar said aloud, and their voice did not shake, which surprised them both. "So here is what I remember, and what I choose to give: the debts. The temple. The valley. All of it—gladly."

They stepped forward. The sigil blazed. The candles bowed.

"But the last page," Oscar said, reaching the foot of the dais, looking up into the stolen face of a king who might still be screaming inside it, "the last page, monster, stays mine."

The Vessel's smile flickered—just once, just slightly, like a spoon turned edge-inward in the night.

And high above, the eclipse began to close its final quarter.
