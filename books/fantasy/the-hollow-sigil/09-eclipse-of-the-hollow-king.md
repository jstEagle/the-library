## 9. Eclipse of the Hollow King

The moon began to die at the stroke of midnight, and the throne room did nothing to stop it.

Oscar stood in the shadow of the great doors—doors they had opened with a stolen keyring, a forged seal, and the particular confidence of someone who had spent eleven years being overlooked on purpose. The eclipse crept across the moon's face like ink spreading through wet paper, and the light that fell through the high windows turned bruise-colored, then red, then a deep arterial dark.

The Vessel sat on the throne wearing Aldric's body, Aldric's crown, and an expression of patient hunger. It did not look surprised to see them.

"Scribe," it said. It had learned to use Aldric's voice beautifully. That was the worst part—the voice was warm, tired, human. "You've come a long way from your valley."

"Not far enough," Oscar said. Their wrist burned. The sigil pulsed there like a second heartbeat, and beneath their feet the marble floor had begun to sweat pale light, every memory ever recorded in this palace rising toward the surface like fish coming up to feed. "I've come exactly far enough."

"You're going to try the unbinding." The Vessel tilted its head—Aldric's head—and something almost like curiosity moved behind the king's grey eyes. "With what? You have no archive. No blood rite. No army. You have a dead woman's handwriting on your arm and a cook waiting outside who cannot remember your name."

"That last one," Oscar said quietly, "was a mistake I intend to correct."

* * *

They had planned this in Maren's kitchen three nights ago, back when Maren still knew them, back before the guard Corvin sold them all for the memory of his daughters' laughter. The plan had been simple in the way plans are simple at two in the morning over onion soup: get into the throne room, start the unbinding, survive.

"The sigil eats memories," the archivist's letters had said. *It must be fed by the bearer. Give it what you can bear to lose.*

So Oscar knelt at the center of the floor, in the old binding circle the first archivists had cut into the stone and the Vessel had never thought to scour away—because why would it? No one living remembered it existed. Except one dead woman, and one scribe with her map burning against their skin.

"Here we go," Oscar whispered, and pressed their palm flat to the circle, and let the sigil open like a mouth.

* * *

The first thing it took was the ledger.

Eleven years of debt, itemized: candle wax, vellum, the fine for the broken window in the novices' dormitory, the interest compounding like mold. Oscar felt each line lift off them and go down into the stone, and with it went the particular posture of a debtor—the slightly bowed shoulders, the habit of standing where the light wouldn't find you, the arithmetic always running in the back of the skull. The weight left. Oscar gasped, lighter and terrified by how much of themselves that lightness had been built on.

The Vessel rose from the throne. "Stop," it said, and this time the warmth was gone from Aldric's voice, and what remained was older and colder and very interested.

The second thing the sigil took was the copying. Ten thousand pages of prayers Oscar had written without understanding, in a valley where magic couldn't follow and neither could questions. They felt the muscle-memory of the quill leave their hand, and the careful smallness of their script, and the rule they'd lived by: *write what you are told, sign nothing, want nothing.*

"No," said the Vessel, and struck.

* * *

It didn't strike with force. It struck with memory—Aldric's memory, poured into Oscar's mind like scalding water.

Oscar stood in a rain-soaked field at nine years old, holding a sword too big for him, while his father—the old king—said *again* in a voice like gravel. Oscar stood in a sickroom watching his mother's breath slow and stop while physicians argued about Latin. Oscar stood at his own coronation hearing the crowd cheer and thinking, with perfect clarity, *they are cheering the crown, not me, and someday the crown will agree.*

And under all of it, the oldest one, the one the Vessel saved for last because it was the sharpest: Aldric at twenty-three, finding the first archivist's letters about the Vessel, reading what slept beneath the palace—and choosing to burn the letters and say nothing, because he was young and the throne was new and the thing had been quiet for a century.

*I looked away,* that memory whispered. *This is my fault. Everything after is my fault.*

"That's not yours to give me," Oscar said through their teeth. Their knees were on cold stone; the eclipse above was nearly total; the sigil drank and drank. "That's his. And he's still in there."

"He is *dinner*," the Vessel said, and now it wasn't bothering with the king's face at all—the flesh rippled, the eyes went black and lidless, the crown sat on something that had only borrowed the shape of a man. "He has been dinner for two years. And when the moon finishes dying, little scribe, so will you."

It came down the dais steps toward them, and the whole room's memories rose with it—the walls bleeding recorded grief, centuries of courtiers' terror, and it wore all of it like armor.

Oscar did the only thing left. They gave the sigil more.

* * *

They gave it the invisibility.

This was the hard one. This was the one they'd built brick by brick for eleven years: the art of being nobody. The way to stand in a doorway so guards' eyes slid past. The trick of answering questions with just enough blandness to end conversations. The practiced blankness of the face, the forgettable name spoken softly, the entire architecture of a self designed to be skipped over like a dull page.

The sigil took it all. Oscar felt it peel away and understood, distantly, with something between horror and hilarity, that they were erasing the only survival skill they had ever mastered, in front of the most dangerous creature in the world, on purpose.

But underneath the invisibility—under the debts and the copying and the careful smallness—there had always been one thing the temple hadn't put there. One thing the archivist hadn't given them. One thing even the Vessel, which ate memories whole and wore them as clothing, could not counterfeit.

Because the Vessel could show Oscar Aldric's worst night. It could show them their own forged life. But it could not show them the truth, because the truth had happened to *them*, alone, and no record of it existed anywhere except inside them.

The night the stranger died, Oscar had held her hand, and she had looked at them—not past them, not through them, *at* them—with her last strength, and said, "Thirteen turns," and pressed the map into their hands. And in that moment, for the first time in Oscar's life, someone had chosen *them*. Not their pen. Not their obedience. Them.

Nobody had made that memory. Nobody had written it down. It was simply true, and true things, the first archivists had written, *are the only ink the sigil cannot drink, for it feeds on what is recorded, and love unrecorded is beyond its appetite.*

"I know one thing you can't eat," Oscar said.

The Vessel paused, one clawed hand raised, the eclipse a thin ring of fire around the moon above.

"It isn't in any ledger," Oscar went on, standing, though their legs shook. "It isn't in your archives. You can't wear it, or copy it, or feed on it. A dying woman chose me. *Me.* Not the bloodline. Not the binding. Me." They raised their burning wrist, and the sigil flared white. "And everything else I am—I'm giving it to you. Take it. Choke."

They fed the sigil the last of the forged life—all of it, the whole eleven years, every safe gray day—and the circle beneath them ignited, and the unbinding came up through the palace like dawn.

* * *

What happened next, Oscar would spend the rest of their life failing to describe properly.

The memories tore free. Two years of the king's stolen self ripped out of the Vessel in a torrent—the taste of Aldric's wedding wine, his daughter's first steps (she had died young; the grief hit Oscar like a wave), his private jokes with a gardener, his terrible singing, his fear of deep water, his hope, his stubbornness, his ordinary human days, thousands upon thousands of them, pouring home to the hollow place where they'd been kept.

The Vessel screamed with a hundred stolen voices at once. It reached for the memories to hold them—but they weren't records anymore, they were *true again*, and true things slipped through its claws like water. It shrank. The crown slid sideways on a head that was becoming less and less shaped like anything. The black eyes fixed on Oscar with something that, in a lesser creature, might have been called fear.

"You are *nothing*," it hissed. "I took everything. What is left of you?"

Oscar smiled—actually smiled, breathless, half-laughing, tears cutting through the dust on their face.

"Everything that's mine."

And they pulled.

* * *

The eclipse ended the way an argument ends when one side finally runs out of words. Moonlight—ordinary silver honest moonlight—came flooding back through the high windows, and in it the Vessel came apart, unraveling like a bad manuscript, its stolen shapes sloughing off it layer by layer until what remained was small and dark and ancient and furious, a coiled thing of pure hunger with nowhere left to feed.

The binding circle closed over it like a book.

Silence. Dust settling in the moonlight. On the throne, a man in a rumpled shirt lay crumpled, breathing—breathing!—his chest rising in great ragged pulls, his hands opening and closing as if remembering how hands worked.

Oscar staggered to him and knelt.

"Aldric," they said. "Your Majesty. Can you hear me?"

The king's eyes opened. They were grey and unfocused and human, and brimming, and he stared at Oscar's face for a long moment with the desperate concentration of a man trying to recall a word that has just escaped him.

"You," he croaked. His voice was wrecked, rusted from two years of disuse. "I know you. I don't—" His brow furrowed; he pressed a hand to his temple. "I knew you. When you were—" He stopped. Stared harder. Something moved behind his eyes, some door creaking open onto a room full of dust and sunlight and a child smuggled out in a laundry cart.

"Later," Oscar said gently, and found, to their surprise, that they meant it—that whatever was buried in the king's restored memory about a child and a valley and a crime, they could face it tomorrow. Tonight they had erased themselves down to the bone and discovered the bone was enough. "First we're getting you out of that chair. And then—" they glanced at the doors, behind which somewhere a cook with a hole where a friend should be waited, unaware, "—then I'm calling in a favor."

Aldric blinked at them. "Who *are* you?"

Oscar Winterbourne—debtor no longer, invisible no longer, author of their own record at last—helped the mortal King of the realm to his feet.

"My name," they said, "is Oscar. And we're going to go get my friend her memories back."
