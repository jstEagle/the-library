## 10. A Hand Unbound

Dawn came to the capital the way it always had, Oscar supposed, but for the first time in two years the king was awake to see it.

Aldric sat propped against his own throne's steps, wrapped in a blanket that Maren—hoarse, hollow-eyed, and insisting she was perfectly fine—had fetched from a servant's closet three corridors away. He looked smaller than his coins. That was Oscar's first thought, absurdly. On every stamped copper in the realm, King Aldric's profile stared out with a jaw like a cliff face. The man himself had a jaw more like a friendly hill.

"You're staring," he said. His voice was rust and honey, unused.

"Forgive me, Majesty. I'm a scribe. I stare at things until I understand them."

"And what do you understand?"

"That your coiners flatter you terribly."

The laugh that came out of him startled all three of them—it came out crooked, half-sob, half-hiccup, the laugh of a man whose body remembered joy before he did. He pressed a hand over his mouth. "I haven't—" He stopped. Started again. "It wore my face for two years. I watched from inside, like a man drowning under ice, watching someone skate across me. I don't know what my face does anymore. Apparently it laughs."

"It's a good laugh," Maren offered from her perch on an upturned bread basket. "Bit rusty. Needs oiling."

"Maren," Oscar said warningly.

"What? He's a king, not porcelain." But her hands were shaking around the cup of tea someone had pressed into them, and everyone saw it, and nobody mentioned it. That was the agreement they'd all made without saying it aloud: *we will not name what was taken from you yet.* Her memories of the last month—the road, the caravan, Oscar—were gone as cleanly as ink lifted off vellum. What remained was a cook who'd woken up in a strange palace with a stranger's sigil-scar fading on her wrist and no idea why her chest ached when a certain scribe walked into the room.

She'd introduced herself twice now. Both times, Oscar had shaken her hand and said, "Oscar Winterbourne," as if for the first time, and both times something behind her eyes had flickered like a lamp in wind.

*Imperfect,* the archivist's letters had promised. *Memory returned through sigil-work is a beginning, not a restoration. It returns the way spring returns—not all at once, and never to the same garden.*

Oscar was learning to live in that word. *Beginning.*

* * *

By midmorning the throne room had filled with the kind of people who appear after any disaster: guards who'd sworn loyalty to a thing wearing their king, courtiers recalculating decades of flattery, and one very confused chamberlain holding two contradictory edicts in each hand like a man asked to juggle knives he'd personally sharpened.

"Sire," the chamberlain said, "the grain tariff decree of last week contradicts the grain tariff decree of the week prior, and both contradict the—"

"Burn them," Aldric said wearily. "All of them. Everything issued in the last two years. Start the ledgers fresh."

The chamberlain went pale. "Burn the *records*, sire?"

"Yes."

"But—" The man clutched the scrolls to his chest as though Oscar might snatch them. "Sire, a record is not merely paper. It is proof. It is memory made durable. If we burn—"

"He knows," Oscar said quietly. "Believe me. He knows exactly what records are worth."

The chamberlain looked at Aldric. Aldric looked at Oscar. And then, because kings apparently could delegate anything, even grief, Aldric said: "You'll want a royal archivist, then."

"I'll want several things first," Oscar said. "But yes."

They cleared the room eventually—all but the three of them—and when the doors shut, Aldric rose, steadied himself on the throne's arm like an old man, and crossed the floor to stand in front of Oscar. Up close, the hill-jaw and the tired eyes and the faint silver threading his temples, he looked less like a coin and more like a person, which Oscar decided was probably the highest compliment a king could receive.

"They tell me you fed it your own life," Aldric said. "Your memories. To pull its teeth."

"Some of them." Oscar flexed their right hand. The sigil was nearly faded now, a pale ghost of a scar shaped like nothing at all—or everything, depending on the light. "I gave it the temple years. The copying. The debt. Twenty years of being small and quiet and invisible." A pause. "I find I don't miss them, which is either very healthy or a sign I should talk to someone."

Aldric smiled—a real one this time, lopsided. "And what do you remember instead?"

"Birds. Rain on the border stones. My mother's hands—I think they were my mother's hands—flour to the wrists. Things from before the valley. Small things. Enough." Oscar met his eyes. "Enough to know I was stolen once already. I wasn't going to let a crown do it a second time."

"No," Aldric said softly. "I imagine not."

He straightened as much as his diminished frame allowed—which wasn't much, and he knew it, and there was something almost funny about a king trying to be regal while wrapped in a servant's blanket.

"Ask me for anything," he said. "Land. Title. A purse heavy enough to make a door swing open anywhere in this kingdom. Ask, and it's yours. You've earned more than I can give, but I'll spend the rest of my life trying."

Oscar had known this moment was coming since the eclipse. They'd rehearsed answers on the walk back from the throne dais—witty ones, noble ones, ones that would have sounded fine written down. None of them survived contact with the actual moment.

"I want Maren's memories back," Oscar said. "All of them. The month on the road. Me. All of it."

Aldric blinked. "That's... not land."

"No."

"Not a title. Not gold."

"She forgot me." Oscar's voice did something embarrassing on the second word, and they pushed past it. "The Vessel took her to hurt me, and I got her back, but only the part of her that existed before we met. She looks at me like I'm a stranger, and I *was* a stranger to her for twenty years and I'm not going back to that. So. That's what I want."

For a long moment the king said nothing. Then: "It can't be done cleanly. You know that. What the sigil returned to you came back ragged. Hers would too."

"I know. Ragged is fine. Ragged is a beginning." Oscar managed a smile. "I've recently become very fond of beginnings."

* * *

The working took place that evening in the archive, because it seemed right—among the shelves where the archivist had spent his life guarding truth from a hungry crown. Candles. Chalk. The old letters spread out like a surgeon's tools. Oscar's wrist bare above the chalk circle, the ghost-sigil waking reluctantly under their blood, one last favor asked of a dying magic.

Maren sat across from them, arms crossed, deeply skeptical.

"So to be clear," she said, "you're going to cut your hand, mumble at a book, and put memories into my head. Memories I don't have. Of a person I've apparently known for a month."

"Yes."

"And these memories are of... what, exactly? Because if this is some scheme to make me believe I owe you money—"

"Maren."

"—because I've fallen for that before, in a caravan outside Duncarrow, a man named Piet—"

"*Maren.*"

She grinned. Then the grin faltered, and for a heartbeat something raw showed underneath—the ache she couldn't source, the grief with no object. "Just do it," she said quietly. "Whatever it is. I'd like to stop feeling like I'm missing a tooth I keep checking for."

The working was gentler than Oscar expected. Perhaps because the Vessel was gone; perhaps because the sigil, spending its final strength, chose kindness. Warmth pooled from Oscar's palm into the space between them, and images rose—shared, doubled, seen from both sides at once:

Rain on the caravan canvas. Maren's stew, aggressively over-peppered, defended as a regional tradition. The wanted notice with Oscar's face, and Maren tearing it down and using it to wrap fish. The night Oscar confessed the whole mad truth of the map and the sigil, and Maren had listened, and believed them, and said, "Well. Someone has to keep you fed."

When it was over, Maren sat blinking in the candlelight, tears running freely down her face and an expression of profound irritation.

"Well?" Oscar asked, terrified.

"Well, *now* I remember why I found you so irritating," she said, wiping her nose with her sleeve. "You eat like a heron. You apologize to furniture. And you—" Her voice caught. She laughed wetly. "You stayed. When everyone else would have run. You stayed."

"Someone had to keep you fed," Oscar said.

She grabbed them by the collar and hugged them hard enough to creak ribs, and neither of them said anything else for a good long while, because some records are kept better in silence.

It wasn't everything. Some pieces were missing—the name of the inn outside Correnvale, half of one argument about poetry. Edges soft, colors slightly wrong. A beginning, not a restoration.

But Maren held Oscar at arm's length and said, "Right. Now introduce yourself properly. I want to hear how you'd have told it."

"Oscar Winterbourne," Oscar said, and meant every syllable. "Formerly a scribe. Currently unemployed. Future uncertain."

"Oh, we'll fix that," Maren said darkly. "Nobody survives an eclipse with me and stays unemployed."

* * *

Aldric kept his word about the rest. The valley's border stones stood silent and ordinary now—their protection had died with the sigil-magic, and the elders' frantic letters arrived weekly, demanding explanation, then aid, then, finally, grudging acknowledgment that perhaps a valley which had hidden a stolen child for twenty years owed the world a little honesty.

Oscar wrote back once, formally, in a fine clear hand: *The debt registers of the Temple of the Quiet Valley are hereby settled in full. Seek payment elsewhere. — O.W., Royal Archivist.*

The title still fit strangely, like borrowed boots. Aldric had granted it with a rueful smile—"You'll guard memory better than the crown ever did"—and Oscar intended to earn it. There was so much to rebuild. Altered pages to restore. Lies, decades deep, to be dug out gently and replaced with truth. Somewhere in the work, Oscar suspected, waited the full story of a child smuggled out of a palace, and a mother with flour to her wrists, and whether she had lived, and where. Grief for later. Hope for now.

On the morning they left, Aldric met them at the city gate himself, unannounced, blanket-less, trying very hard to look kingly and mostly looking like a tired uncle seeing off favorite relatives.

"The road east is safe as roads go," he said. "Which is to say, not. Take the guard escort—"

"We'll take the caravan route," Maren said. "Safer. Better stew."

"There is no better stew than mine," Oscar said, "and I include yours in that assessment."

Maren gasped in theatrical horror. "Returned memories and you use them *against* me—"

Aldric watched them bicker all the way to the gatehouse, and when Oscar glanced back, the king was laughing—that crooked, rusty laugh, getting smoother with practice—and raised one hand in farewell.

The road ran east out of the city, past the milestone, past the last rooftops, into country green and wide and utterly unwritten. Oscar walked it with ink-stained fingers, an empty satchel waiting to be filled, a friend at their shoulder arguing passionately about pepper ratios, and no debt, no border stone, no crown, no chain.

Ahead, the world lay open as a blank page.

Oscar took out a fresh sheet, just to feel it, and began to write.
