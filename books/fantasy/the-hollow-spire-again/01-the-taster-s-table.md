## 1. The Taster's Table

The trick to surviving a poisoned breakfast is to arrive before the poison does.

Emrys Sandoval had learned this in their first year of service, when they were nineteen and still flinched at every silver spoon. Now, seven years on, they arrived in the queen's kitchens while the coals were still being raked and the bread was still warm enough to steam, and they stood at the taster's table—a plain oak thing, scarred by decades of knives—while the cooks moved around them like water around a stone.

Nobody looked at Emrys. That was the point. A food-taster who drew the eye was a food-taster who drew questions, and questions were how people in Cindral Vale ended up in the river with their pockets full of gravel. So Emrys had spent years perfecting the art of being furniture: the flat brown hair pinned without ornament, the servant's grey that matched every other servant's grey, the posture of a person perpetually waiting to be told what to do next. They had even cultivated a small, apologetic cough, deployed at exactly the right moments to make themselves seem slightly pathetic. Pathetic people were invisible. Nobody envied the pathetic.

"Almond cakes," said Mistress Halloway, the under-cook, setting down a plate. "Honey from the southern apiaries. The pears are new."

"The pears are always new," Emrys said, and Halloway almost smiled. It was the closest thing either of them had to friendship, this exchange of ritual complaints, and Emrys guarded it carefully. Friendship was another thing that drew the eye.

They began, as always, with the honey. A single fingertip, touched to the comb, then pressed to the tongue.

Here was the thing no one in the kitchen knew: Emrys could not taste it.

Not entirely. There was a numbness at the center of the tongue, a smooth grey patch like a cobblestone worn blind, where flavor simply refused to live. It had grown over the years, grain by grain, dose by dose. The physicians called it *acclimation*. The taster corps called it *the coin*, because you paid for your immunity one small piece of yourself at a time. Emrys called it nothing at all, because naming it meant thinking about it, and thinking about it meant remembering the first time they'd felt it spread—the morning after the nightingale broth, when the taste of rosemary had gone missing like a bird flown from an open cage.

What remained to them was the edges. Salt at the tip. Bitterness along the sides, sharp as a slap. Sweetness, faintly, like hearing music through a wall. Enough to work with. Enough to survive on.

The almond cake carried only bitterness at its edge—almonds always did—and beneath it, nothing. Emrys chewed, swallowed, counted to sixty. The kitchen went on around them. Somewhere above, the queen would be waking, pulling the bell-cord, expecting her breakfast to be safe because a forgettable person in grey had already eaten it.

Sixty seconds. No burning throat, no creeping cold in the fingers, no metallic bloom behind the teeth. Emrys nodded once, and the tray went up.

* * *

Back in their rooms—two chambers above the tannery lane, chosen precisely because no one chose to live above a tannery—Emrys opened the ledger.

It was not an official document. The official ledger lived in the corps' offices, bound in green leather, and recorded each tasting in a clerk's neat hand: date, dish, result. Emrys's private ledger recorded other things. It was small, cheaply bound, and written in a shorthand of their own devising, because some truths were safer encrypted.

*Thirdmonth, 14th.* Honey, almonds, pears. Clean.
*Thirdmonth, 13th.* Venison pie. Bitterness at the crust's edge—not poison, just carelessness. Noted to Halloway; she'll be cross.
*Thirdmonth, 11th.* The wine. *Again.*

Emrys sat back and looked at that last entry for a long moment. The wine was the problem. Twice now there had been something in the queen's evening wine that Emrys's tongue caught but could not name—not one of the forty-one poisons catalogued in the corps' training manual, not hemlock or foxglove or the slow grey rot that had killed old Taster Bren two winters past. Something subtler. Something that slid off the numb center of the tongue and left only a whisper at the edges, like a word spoken too quietly to parse.

Forty-one poisons survived. Emrys ran a thumb down the tally marks in the ledger's front pages, column after column, each one a morning they had not died. It should have been a comfort. Lately it felt more like a debt accruing interest.

They closed the book and hid it beneath the loose hearthstone, then turned to the second business of the day, which was also the secret one.

The charms took shape under their hands the way bread took shape under a baker's: without thought, without ceremony. A knotted cord for a fisherman's wife whose husband feared deep water—three knots, each tied with a breath and a word in no language anyone living spoke. A pinch of salt and iron filings sewn into a linen square for a midwife. A slip of paper bearing a sigil that wasn't quite a sigil, folded eight times, for a clerk who wanted his gambling debts to stay unlucky for other men.

Magic had been outlawed for seven years. The Edict of Unmaking had come down in the queen's first year—no spells, no charms, no tongues spoken outside the sanctioned liturgy—and the purges that followed had been thorough enough that most people in Cindral Vale genuinely believed magic was gone, burned out of the world like a fever. What they didn't know was that it hadn't burned so much as gone underground, into tannery lanes and fish markets, sold in whispers for coppers by a forgettable person in grey.

Emrys didn't think of themself as a criminal. They thought of themself as a tradesman serving an underserved market. The knots worked—weakly, imperfectly, the way a candle works compared to the sun—but they worked, and the people who bought them slept better, and no one was ever the worse for it. That was the story Emrys told themself, anyway, on the nights the story needed telling.

After dark, the customers came. The fisherman's wife first, hood up, coins palmed. Then the midwife, who stayed to gossip about which noble house was quietly falling apart. Then, near midnight, a boy of perhaps fifteen who wanted a charm against conscription and paid double because he was ashamed to want it. Emrys sold him the cheapest one and lied that it was strong.

By the time the last customer left, the candle had burned to a stub, and Emrys stood in the doorway watching the fog roll up the tannery lane, thinking about the wine again. Thinking about the whisper at the edge of the tongue. *A word spoken too quietly to parse.*

They did not yet know how literally true that would prove.

* * *

The knock came at dusk the following day.

This was wrong in itself. Emrys received no visitors at dusk. Dusk was when the watch changed and the lanes filled with bored soldiers looking for entertainment; dusk was when a charm-seller locked their door and became, again, nobody. The knock was soft—two taps, a pause, one tap—and it came from low down on the door, at the height of someone small.

Emrys stood very still in the middle of the room, listening.

Two taps. Pause. One tap. Again.

They crossed to the door and put their eye to the crack. The lane was empty except for fog and the tannery stink and, standing on their step, a child.

A girl, Emrys judged, though it was hard to say—she was thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a coat far too big for her, with the hood drawn up so that all that showed was a pale face and two dark eyes that did not blink nearly often enough. She was maybe eight years old. Maybe ten. Children who had starved a while stopped aging honestly.

Emrys opened the door the width of a hand. "Whatever you've been told I sell, I don't sell it to children. Go home."

The girl did not go home. She did not speak, either. She simply looked up at Emrys with those unblinking eyes, and then she raised one small hand and held out something between finger and thumb: a scrap of cloth, dyed a blue so deep it was nearly black, embroidered at its corner with a symbol Emrys had never seen and yet recognized instantly, the way one recognizes one's own name shouted across a crowded room.

Their mouth went dry. "Where did you get that?"

The girl opened her mouth—and closed it. Opened it again. Her throat worked. Nothing came. She pressed a fist to her chest, hard, twice, and her face twisted with a frustration so fierce it was almost anger, and Emrys understood: she couldn't speak. Or she couldn't speak anything that would help.

Then she did speak.

The words came out low and rough, half-voice, the way sound comes from throats long unused. Three syllables, four, five—a sentence, rising and falling in a rhythm Emrys's ears had never heard and yet some chamber behind their ears knew intimately, the way a body knows the rhythm of a mother's heartbeat heard before birth.

Emrys stepped back. The door swung wider. Fog curled across the threshold like something with intent.

"That's—" Their voice cracked. They tried again. "That's not a language. That's nothing. There's no such language anymore."

The girl tilted her head. And she said it again, slower, deliberate, the way one speaks to a child or a fool or someone one desperately needs to understand—and this time, in the middle of the sentence, in the middle of a string of sounds that belonged to a country erased from every map and memory and record in the kingdom, one word landed on Emrys's tongue like a key finding its lock.

*Tastes.*

No—that wasn't it. It was close to *tastes*, shaped like it, kin to it. But it meant something wider. Someone who tastes. One who takes into the mouth on behalf of another. One who stands between a body and its poisoning.

The girl pointed one grimy finger directly at Emrys's chest.

Emrys Sandoval, royal food-taster, seller of small charms, master of being forgotten, stood in their own doorway and felt seven years of careful invisibility tear down the middle like wet paper. Because they had understood her. Without study, without translation, without knowing why—they had understood her, the way you understand a language you were sung to sleep in before you could walk.

And somewhere far below the city, beneath foundations older than the crown, Emrys felt—or imagined they felt—something answer. A hum, at the very floor of hearing. Like a tower of doors, waking.
