## 2. A Language Without a Country

There is a particular art to hiding a child in a room full of spices, and I will confess it took me three attempts to get it right.

The first attempt was behind the sacks of saffron, which failed because Cora sneezed—once, small and devastating, like the world's most expensive accusation. The second was in the cold cellar, which failed because the child looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *reasonable objection*, and I found I could not argue with it. The third attempt worked: a hollow between the pepper racks and the false wall where I kept my charm-stock, dark and dry and smelling of cloves, which is apparently as good as a blanket to someone who has slept in worse.

"Stay," I said, in the way one says it to a dog or a debt. "Quiet. No one comes here before midmorning."

Cora looked at me and said something in that language again—the one that had slipped under my door at dusk like water under a wall—and then, seeing my face, pressed their lips together and nodded.

I want to be clear about something. I have spent seven years perfecting the craft of not noticing things. It is the entire foundation of my livelihood. A food-taster who notices too much becomes a person of interest, and persons of interest in this city end up either promoted or buried, and the promotion is worse. So when I say that I spent the whole night lying awake cataloguing every reason to open my door and hand the child over to the watch by dawn, understand that I was doing it professionally. Diligently. The list was long and persuasive.

The child had said a word on my doorstep—a single word, repeated twice, patient as rain—and some locked room in the back of my skull had opened its door and answered.

I did not sleep so much as I lay there being haunted in installments.

* * *

Master Ivo kept rooms above a chandler's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, which tells you everything about how far a man can fall from a university chair. He had been the Crown Linguist once—before the Edict, before the purges—and now he made his living authenticating forged genealogies for merchant families desperate to prove they'd been noble longer than they had. It was honest work, comparatively, and he did it drunk.

I went to him because he was the only man in the capital who might recognize a language instead of flinching from it, and because he owed me. Three years ago I had quietly sold him a charm against gout that actually worked, and had charged him half rate, and had never mentioned it to anyone. In this city, discretion is the only currency that never devalues.

He opened the door looking like a man who had been arguing with his own reflection and losing. His eyes were rimmed red, his collar had last met a washboard during a previous reign, and when he saw me he sighed the sigh of a man whose day has just gotten measurably worse.

"The taster," he said. "If you've come about the gout, it's back."

"I've come about a word."

"A word." He stepped aside anyway, which was the whole conversation, really.

His rooms smelled of tallow and old paper. Every surface held books, and every book had been defaced—not torn, but scraped, pages sanded blank in strips, spines re-titled in dull bureaucratic lettering. I had seen that kind of damage before, in the archives beneath the palace, where the taster corps keeps its records. Someone had gone through this man's life with a knife and left the bindings standing, the way you leave a house standing after you've taken everything out of it.

I set down the slate I'd brought. On it, in chalk, I had written the sounds as best I could hold them—the child's word from the doorstep, and two more phrases Cora had murmured while settling into the spice hollow, half-asleep, the way children murmur prayers they don't know are prayers.

Ivo read them. Then he read them again. And I watched something happen to his face that I had previously only seen happen to other people's faces: the color draining in stages, like wine through a cloth.

"Where," he said, and stopped. Cleared his throat. Started over. "Where did you hear this?"

"You'll identify it first."

"Don't give me orders in my own—this is High Vaeli." He said it fast, like setting down something hot. "Court register of Vael. The formal speech. Not the market tongue—*High* Vaeli, the language of petitions and oaths and—" His hand drifted toward a shelf and stopped short of touching anything. "It was outlawed alongside the magic, taster. Same edict. Speaking it carried the same penalty as casting. Do you understand? They burned the grammar books and then they burned the grammarians, in roughly that order, because the books were cheaper."

"And now?"

"Now nothing. Now it doesn't exist. That's rather the point of the exercise." He sat down heavily on a stool that complained. "The census came first—I helped build it, God rot me, I built the questionnaire myself, *what lullabies does your mother sing, what words do you use for salt and door and home*—and then the removals began. Speakers were relocated, the records said. Resettled. There was a village north of here called Vaelsmouth, renamed within a season, and everyone in it simply—ceased, in the administrative sense. You cannot purge a country, taster, so you purge the evidence of it. Maps, records, names. And the language last, because the language lives in people, and people are stubborn."

He was quiet for a moment. The chandler's boy was shouting prices in the street below.

"So no," Ivo said finally. "A native speaker of High Vaeli should not exist. A child speaking it fluently, with court inflections, is not merely improbable. It is impossible in the way that a ghost is impossible—meaning only that our theory of the world is incomplete."

"It's real," I said. "The child. About eight years old. Mute except for that. Came to my door after dusk."

"Came to *your* door." Something sharpened in him, the scholar waking up inside the drunk. "Of all the doors in this city. Why yours?"

"I don't know."

"You're lying."

"I'm a food-taster, Master Ivo. Lying is the least of my vices, and the only one I practice daily." I stood. "How much to keep asking questions?"

"For you? Nothing." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "For the answers I already suspect? Everything you have and several things you don't. Go home, Emrys Sandoval. Burn your slate. If anyone asks, you came here about gout."

"Gout's incurable."

"So is knowing a dead language. We all carry something." He walked me to the door, and just as I crossed the threshold he caught my sleeve, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "One thing. The word on your slate. The one the child repeated."

"Yes?"

"It isn't a greeting. It's a title. An old one." He wouldn't look at me. "*The one who tastes.* It's from the Spire liturgies—the tower records, the ones nobody's supposed to remember exist. Whoever taught that child to speak taught them to come find you specifically. Sleep well."

* * *

I didn't.

I went home through streets gone blue with lamplight, past shuttered windows with their watchful slits, and let myself into my rooms, and checked the spice hollow, and Cora was asleep exactly where I'd left them, curled against the pepper racks with one hand closed around a dried bay leaf like a talisman. I put a blanket over the child and sat down on my stool and did not sleep.

Somewhere past the smallest hour of the night, I did dream.

I dreamed of a kitchen that wasn't mine—low-ceilinged, stone-floored, with a window that looked out on terraces cut into a hillside, olive trees going silver in wind. A woman stood at a stove with her back to me, and she was humming, and I knew the tune the way you know your own pulse, without ever having learned it. She turned, and her mouth moved, and the words arrived inside me already translated:

*Come away from the window, little taster. Some things see you seeing.*

And I answered—in the dream I answered, easily, fluently, my mouth shaping sounds I have never studied, never heard spoken in this kingdom's waking hours—and what I said was:

*I'm watching for the tower, Mama. The one that hums.*

The woman laughed. The kitchen filled with the smell of bread and something bitter underneath it, medicinal, familiar—

I woke with the taste still in my mouth. Bitter. Almond-bitter, the exact note I catalogue every morning when I sip the queen's breakfast and wait to see if my tongue goes numb. Seven years of that taste, swallowed in small measured doses, building my immunity one breakfast at a time.

In the spice hollow, Cora was awake and watching me across the dark room, eyes catching what little light there was.

The child opened their mouth and spoke—one soft phrase in that outlawed tongue—and this time I didn't need a slate or a linguist. The meaning rose in me like water finding a crack, unbidden, unhired, *remembered*:

*You dreamed it too.*

I sat very still. My hands were steady—that much the poison-training gives you, hands that never shake, a taster's pride—but everything under them was shaking hard enough to rattle.

Because here is the thing about learning a language. Learning feels like effort. Like climbing. You reach for each word and haul it up. What happened in that dream, what happens every time Cora speaks now, is the opposite. The words are already there. They've always been there, packed away somewhere below the numbness, below the ledger, below the careful forgettable surface of Emrys Sandoval, royal property, immune to everything.

Someone buried a language in me. And someone paid to keep it buried.

I got up, lit no lamp, and pulled out the one document I was never supposed to question: my own hiring record, the taster corps' contract, stamped and sealed seven years ago. My surname printed neat in the clerk's hand.

*Sandoval.* A good southern name. Unremarkable. Forgettable.

Except that in a dream-kitchen on a hillside, a woman who hummed in a dead tongue had called me by another name entirely, and I could almost—almost—hear it.

At dawn I would go to the archives. At dawn I would start pulling threads.

But first I sat in the dark with a child who shouldn't exist, in a room above a city that had murdered a country, and listened to my own heartbeat say the only Vaeli word I'd never needed translated.

*Mama. Mama. Mama.*
