## 3. What the Ledger Doesn't Say

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in government offices. It isn't peace. It's the silence of paper pretending it has never been wrong about anything.

The Taster Corps kept its records in the undercroft of the Hall of Provisions, three floors below the kitchens where I earned my keep, in a room that smelled of dust, tallow, and the faint vinegar tang of old ink. I had been down there perhaps four times in seven years—once to sign my indenture, once to renew it, twice to collect hazard pay after particularly eventful dinners. Hazard pay. As if swallowing poison were a trade like any other. In a sense, I suppose it was mine.

I told myself I was down there for the same reason as always: to check the ledger against my own count. A taster who doesn't keep private tallies is a dead taster. The crown's clerks recorded what they *believed* we'd survived; my own book recorded what had actually gone into me. The discrepancy between those two numbers was, over the years, the only honest thing in my professional life.

But that was the excuse. The truth was simpler and worse. Cora had said a word in her sleep the night before—*sarneth*—and when she said it, something behind my ribs had answered in a voice I didn't recognize as mine. I needed to know if anyone else had ever felt that. And the only place the crown kept its people was in ledgers.

So: dust, tallow, vinegar. I lit the lamp, found the corps registry, and began at the beginning, which is where all lies are best hidden, because no one ever reads back that far.

* * *

The registry was organized by year of induction, each taster given a page: name, surname, district of origin, the oath of service in cramped clerk's hand, and then columns upon columns of substances administered, dates, dosages, outcomes. Outcomes being either *tolerated*, *illness*, or—in the early years—a small neat dash that meant nothing at all, because a dash required no explanation and no widow's pension.

Seven years ago. That was the date everyone in the capital knew without knowing why. Seven years ago the Edict of Cleansing outlawed magic, dissolved the College of Charms, burned the grimoires, and quietly scrubbed one entire country out of existence. Vael. I knew the word now the way you know a scar you've stopped looking at—Cora's muteness had made sure of that, and Master Ivo's horrified confirmation had made it worse. High Vaeli, he'd called her language. The tongue of a nation that, according to every map in this building, had never been.

I turned pages backward through the years. Five years ago. Six. Six and a half. The names scrolled past: Aldous Merrin, Jena Cotteril, Bram Fenwick. Ordinary names. Capital names. Names with no music in them.

Then I reached seven years ago, and the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. Not the way handwriting changes when a clerk grows tired or a new hand takes up the pen. It changed mid-page, mid-column, as though someone had torn out everything before and started fresh. The entry for the third month of that year began halfway through a sentence about dosage schedules for a man named—there was no name. Just a gap in the register column, a pale rectangle where the paper hadn't yellowed at quite the same rate as the paper around it. Pages removed. Carefully, recently enough that the replacement sheets were cut from a different batch. I held the leaf up to the lamplight and saw the ghost of writing pressed through from the missing sheet beneath: numbers, mostly. Dosages. And one word, half-legible, in a clerk's hurried script:

*Vaeli.*

My mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with my profession. I have tasted forty-one poisons. I know every shade of numbness my tongue can produce. This was a different kind entirely—the numbness of a man realizing the floor he's standing on is a lid.

I kept reading. There was nowhere else to go; the room had narrowed to the size of the page.

Every taster inducted after that torn-out month shared something. At first I thought it was coincidence, because coincidence is the last comfort a drowning man reaches for. Sera Vane. Tomas Ilvane. Maren Ashvale. Dessa Korrigan. Emrys Sandoval. I read my own name and felt it like a stranger's. Then the next: Pell Amari. Juno Serath. On and on, year after year, right up to the present roll.

Vane. Ilvane. Ashvale. Korrigan. Sandoval. Amari. Serath.

Ivo had shown me a fragment of the linguistic census once—reluctantly, guiltily, the way a man shows you the knife he used to dig his own grave. Vaeli surnames, he'd said, were distinctive. Soft endings. Liquid consonants. *-ane, -ale, -ari.* The purge had used them as markers. Speakers were catalogued, tracked, "resettled." Resettled being the crown's word for a thing I would not write down even in my own private book.

Every taster hired since the Cleansing carried a Vaeli name.

I sat down on a stool that creaked like an accusation and did the arithmetic I very much did not want to do.

The immunity program was famous—or infamous—throughout the palace. Tasters dosed from childhood with trace poisons, building resistance, becoming living instruments so that the queen need never fear her plate. Noble work. Necessary work. I had told myself versions of that story for seven years, and the versions had gotten smoother with practice, the way all comforting lies do.

But trace poisons don't build immunity in a vacuum. They build it in *bodies*. Specific bodies. And the crown had spent seven years selecting those bodies by surname—by bloodline. Gathering up the scattered survivors of a murdered country and feeding them, grain by grain, dose by dose, a slow pharmacological extinction. Not the sword. The sword makes martyrs, and martyrs make songs. This was quieter. This was a spoon.

We weren't being protected. We were being *finished*. Every tolerance I'd built, every poison I'd survived and been paid hazard wages for, was another layer of the trap closing around whatever Vael still lived in my blood. The program wasn't saving us from poison. The program *was* the poison—administered by our own grateful hands, logged in our own careful ledgers, thanked for with our own bowed heads.

And the worst of it—the part that made me put my head between my knees and breathe like a man surfacing—was that it had worked on me completely. I had loved this work. I had been *proud* of it. Forgettable Emrys Sandoval, who asked no questions, who kept his head down, who was so very good at being nobody. Nobody was exactly what they'd ordered.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above me, the palace went about its morning: cart wheels on cobbles, the kitchen bells, a scullery boy singing off-key about a milkmaid. The world continued, indifferent, and I understood that this was how the crown had done it—not with cruelty but with indifference, with paperwork, with the great soft weight of everyone simply not asking.

I copied the names into my private book. All of them. If they meant to erase a country twice, then someone ought to at least keep a list of the second erasure while it was happening. It wasn't much of a rebellion. It was a start.

* * *

Captain Sere found me at dusk, which I should have expected, because Captain Sere found everyone at dusk—it was practically her signature, the way other people had signatures on documents and she had signatures on fear.

She was waiting by the spice stores when I came down to feed Cora, leaning against the doorframe with the relaxed posture of a woman who had never once in her life been relaxed. Her gray uniform was immaculate. Her eyes were the color of a locked drawer.

"Emrys Sandoval," she said, tasting the name like I taste wine. Looking for something underneath.

"Captain." I kept my hands busy with the jar of dried apricots, because busy hands look honest. "Is there a problem with the tasting schedule?"

"There's a question." She let the silence do some work first. She was good at that. "A food-taster. Unmarried, unconnected, famously invisible. And yet in the space of two weeks, this taster visits the undercroft archives for the first time in years. Consults a disgraced linguist under cover of buying charms. And receives—" she paused delicately, "—a visitor. Small. Quiet. Seen by no one official."

My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as professional. Forty-one poisons teach you to keep your face still. "Children come to charm-sellers' doors, Captain. Mothers mostly. Sometimes grandmothers with more hope than coin."

"At night. Alone. To a taster's door." She pushed off the doorframe and walked a slow circuit of the spice store, fingers trailing along the shelves, touching cumin, touching salt, touching everything as if confirming it was real. "You know what I do all day, Sandoval? I listen. To the city. And lately the city has been saying a strange word in its sleep. Do you know what happens to countries that say strange words in their sleep?"

"I'm a food-taster, Captain. Countries are outside my jurisdiction."

Something flickered across her face—amusement, or its taxidermied cousin. She stopped in front of me, close enough that I could smell the iron-and-ink scent the spymistress's office seemed to breed in its occupants.

"Seven years ago," she said quietly, "I made certain choices. I've watched others make different ones since. I'm telling you, as one professional to another, that the ones who chose differently are not available for comment." She straightened. "Enjoy your apricots."

She left. I stood in the dark among the spices, holding a jar, listening to her boots fade up the stairs, and understood that the noose hadn't tightened around my neck yet—but someone had measured me for it.

Behind the sacks of saffron, Cora looked up at me with eyes too old for her face, and said, in the language of a country that officially did not exist, a sentence I couldn't translate but perfectly understood anyway.

It meant: *they're coming.*

I blew out the lamp and started counting the hours until the solstice.
