## 4. The Charm-Seller's Confession

Warm.

That is the first thing. Warmth through the wall of the hiding place, warmth that smells of dust and pepper and something burnt-sweet underneath, like a candle left too long in a jar of honey. The small one — the tall one, the not-child one with the careful hands — has tucked a blanket between the sacks of cloves so the cold stone cannot reach through. The blanket smells of lavender gone tired. It is not a good blanket. It is the best thing anyone has given me since the door closed behind me, and I press my face into it because pressing my face into things is the only sentence I have left.

The tall one does not know I am watching. That is the trick of this house: there are gaps everywhere. Gaps between crates. Gaps under doors. A gap in the floor where a board has been pried up and set back down badly, again and again, by someone who takes things out and puts them back and cannot stop doing it. I have learned the shape of this house through its gaps the way you learn a song through a wall.

Through the gap under the kitchen door, at dusk, I watch the tall one count coins.

* * *

The coins come from people who come after dark. They knock in patterns — two soft, one hard, wait, one soft — and the tall one opens the door only as far as an arm's width, and the arm's width is always exactly enough to pass a small wrapped bundle through and take the coins and say nothing, or almost nothing. The tall one's voice when it speaks to these people is a different voice. Flatter. Lighter. A voice like a coin itself, all edge and shine and nothing inside.

I know why. I do not have words for it anymore — the words went out of me somewhere on the long road, worn away like the heels of my shoes — but I know why the way you know fire is hot without being told. The tall one is afraid. Not of the people at the door. Of the windows across the street. There is one window, third floor, blue curtain, that never opens and never closes and never changes, and every time the door knocks, the tall one looks at it first. Looks at the window before the door. That is what fear looks like when it has been lived in for years: it stops looking at the danger and starts looking at the witness.

I was sent to find someone who would be afraid. That was the instruction, or what remains of the instruction, which is not words anymore but a weight behind my breastbone, a hand pressing down: *find the one who tastes.* I remember hands on my shoulders, many hands or one pair of hands remembered many times, and a voice like a bell rung underwater saying *the one who tastes will know the door,* and then the dark, and the cold, and the road eating my words one by one until all I had left was the weight and the walking.

The tall one tastes. I saw it. In the morning, before the sun was fully up, the tall one stood in a room with a long table and ate small bites off silver plates and waited, counting under their breath, and made marks in a book, and drank something bitter from a flask they kept close to the body like a heart. Their face while they did it was the flat coin-voice made flesh. Nothing moved in it. Nothing was allowed to move.

But afterward — this is the part I keep turning over like a stone with something alive under it — afterward, in the corridor outside, alone, the tall one stopped and put one hand against the wall and stood very still for the length of ten breaths, and then took a small clay vial from their sleeve and touched it to their tongue, and their whole face broke open like ice on a river in spring. Just for a moment. Then the vial went away and the face went flat and the tall one walked on, and no one saw.

I saw. I see everything now. Words were the price of seeing; I would make the trade again.

* * *

The tall one brings me food in the spice room and pretends, badly, that this is a normal thing to do.

The pretending is the tenderest part. The tall one sets down bread and salt fish and a cup of milk — *milk*, for me, a stranger, a smuggled silence in their house — and arranges the plate as if arranging it matters, as if presentation could make any of this ordinary. And then the tall one crouches at the mouth of the crate-gap and says words at me, gentle words, question-shaped words, in the flat country-tongue that everyone here speaks, the tongue with no music in it, and waits for me to answer.

I cannot answer. Not won't. Can't. When I try, the place where words live shows me only smooth walls, a room emptied in the night, furniture gone, floors swept. So I do the other thing instead. I hum.

The humming is not mine. That is important. I want whoever finds me someday, if anyone ever writes this down, to know: the humming is the Spire's. It lives in the tower of doors, the tower that is mostly door, the tower that is a throat the world forgot how to use. Every door in it hums a different note and all the notes together make a chord that holds the sky up — I remember that much, I remember *that* — and when they pushed me out of the last door and it began to seal, the chord bent around the closing like water around a dropped stone, and some of it came with me. It lives in my chest now. It is the last citizen of my country and it is a sound.

When I hum, the tall one goes still. Completely still, the way animals go still, the way the tall one never goes still anywhere else, not at the knocking door, not at the blue-curtain window, not even asleep — I have watched through the floor-gap at night; the tall one sleeps like a soldier, like someone practicing for an ambush. But when I hum, everything in the tall one stops, and the face does the river-ice breaking again, and the eyes get wet and furious about being wet, and the tall one says, once, in a whisper aimed at nobody:

*I know that song.*

Liar, I think, kindly. You don't know it. You *are* it, a little. Something in you is it. The tall one hums back sometimes, badly, three notes wrong out of five, and every wrong note lands in my chest like a hand pressing on a bruise, and I think: *where did you get broken notes of my country's chord? Who took the tower apart and gave pieces of it to you?*

The tall one does not know either. I can tell. The tall one reaches for those three notes the way you reach for a name on the tip of your tongue, and finds smoke.

* * *

There is a bad day. I want to write down the bad day too, because the tall one would want only the good parts written, and that is exactly the kind of lie the tall one tells.

The bad day: boots in the street below, many boots, and a voice calling names through a speaking-trumpet, and the tall one moving through the house fast and quiet, putting out lamps, moving me deeper into the spice room, behind the sacks, a hand on the top of my head pressing me down — gently, gently, but pressing — and the tall one's breath doing a thing I recognize from the road, from my own chest on the worst nights: going small. Making itself take less of the world so the world will take less notice of it.

And then the knock comes, the real knock, the official knock, hard knuckles and no pattern, and the tall one answers the door wearing the flat face like armor, and I hear the whole performance: the bored voice, the small jokes, the offering-up of the ledger, the little laugh placed exactly where a laugh makes officials lose interest. And it works. The boots go away.

And when the door closes, the tall one leans against it and slides down it and sits on the floor of their own hallway like a puppet with cut strings, and puts both hands over their face, and shakes.

I crawl out of the spice room. I should not have. The weight behind my breastbone said *stay hidden, stay hidden* the way it always says, but my legs were younger than my instructions. I crossed the hall and I did the only thing I own: I sat down next to the tall one, shoulder to shoulder against the door, and I hummed the low note, the foundation note, the one the biggest door sings, the one that holds the most weight.

The tall one took their hands away from their face and looked at me. Really looked. And I watched something happen that I had been waiting my whole erased life to see: the tall one looked at themselves. Through my eyes. Saw the flat coin-voice, the practiced boredom, the seven years of making themselves small and flavorless and forgettable — saw it all laid out plain, the way I see it, the way the blue-curtain window sees it — and flinched.

Then, worse and better: the tall one saw the kindnesses too. The milk. The blanket among the cloves. The extra bread hidden under the honest bread. The way the tall one always gives the charm-buyers slightly more than they paid for and calls it measuring error. All the small warm crimes committed against their own survival, every day, in secret, as if some part of them had been leaving a trail of breadcrumbs leading away from the person they'd decided to be.

The tall one cried. Quietly, thoroughly, the way people cry who have not done it in years and are embarrassed by how much was stored up. And I leaned my head against their arm, because that is a sentence too, maybe the truest one I have left.

Afterward the tall one held me at arm's length and studied my face with the expression of someone reading a difficult book, and said, slowly, in the flat tongue, testing each word like a step over ice:

"You weren't sent to me by accident. You knew. Somehow, you *knew*—"

And then the tall one stopped, because the tall one's eyes had gone to the ledger on the shelf — the tasting ledger, the book of poisons survived — and stayed there, and the color left the tall one's face the way tide leaves a shore.

"The tasters," the tall one whispered. "Every taster hired since the war. Every one of us has a name like mine."

The tall one stood up. The tall one took the ledger down with hands that were not steady. And far above us, faint through the ceiling, through the floors, through the foundations of the city itself, I felt it — felt *them* feel it — the great buried door shifting in its sleep, humming one note louder, as if it had heard.

As if it had been waiting seven years for exactly this tremor in the blood of the one who tastes.

* * *

That night the tall one did not sleep like a soldier. The tall one sat up with a lamp and a penknife, prying at the floorboard with the badly-repaired gap, and beneath it pulled out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it, and I smelled it from the spice room before I saw it: old paper, old ink, and under that, unmistakable, the smell of home. Of Vael. Paper made the way we made paper, ink mixed the way we mixed it.

The tall one stared at the bundle as if it might bite. As if it had already bitten, long ago, and the scar was the forgetting.

Tomorrow, the tall one's face said, clear as speech. Tomorrow I take this to someone who can read it.

I pressed my hand flat against the wall and hummed the chord, all five notes, softly, into the dark.

Somewhere beneath the city, the door hummed back.
