## 5. Master Ivo's Bargain

There are three ways a scholar dies in this kingdom. The first is quickly, at the end of a rope, for the crime of having been interesting once. The second is slowly, in a garret, drinking cheap wine and correcting the grammar of tavern signs out of spite. I had chosen the second and told myself it was a choice.

The third way—the worst—is to be needed again.

I knew Emrys Sandoval was coming before the knock came, because I had watched them circle my street twice from the window of my room above the chandler's shop, wearing that magnificent expression of casual purpose that only guilty people can manage. Food-tasters learn to walk like they belong everywhere. It doesn't work on men who spent thirty years teaching young lords how to lie with their faces.

I opened the door before they could knock, because letting them knock would have meant letting them think they had the upper hand.

"Master Ivo," Emrys said. "You look well."

"I look preserved," I said. "Like something pickled against a famine that never comes. Come in before Mrs. Halloway's boy sees you. He reports foot traffic to his aunt, who reports it to the Watch, who report nothing to anyone because the Watch has not solved a crime since the coronation."

They came in. They looked at my room the way people always did—quickly, cataloguing, slightly disappointed. The books were hidden, of course. What remained visible was a man's modest poverty: a bed, a stove, a chair with one leg mended by someone who understood carpentry better than I ever understood anything. And on the table, face-down, a lexicon I had no business owning.

Emrys saw it anyway. Tasters notice things; it is the whole of their profession. You cannot detect henbane in honeyed wine unless you have made an enemy of assumption.

"You kept working," they said.

"Breathing is work, at my age." I sat down heavily. "You've brought me something. People don't visit disgraced linguists to discuss the weather. The weather is my only remaining friend and even she is dull company."

Emrys hesitated, and I will say this for them: their hesitation was honest. Most visitors rehearse their hesitation. This was real—a person standing at the edge of a decision and feeling the drop.

Then they reached into their coat and set a scrap of paper on my table. On it, in careful, unpracticed letters: *sel vetha korrin. sel vetha amai.*

I did not touch it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the dead keep. My hands stayed flat on my knees like a schoolboy reciting abstinence, and my heart went off like a dropped chandelier.

"Where," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, which humiliated us both, "did you hear this?"

"A child said it."

"A child." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "Children don't say that. No one says that. That is High Vaeli. That is the *mourning register*, do you understand? *Sel vetha korrin*—'the door remembers what we carried.' It appears in exactly four surviving texts, all of them burned by order of the Crown, all of which I copied before they burned them, which is why I drink."

Emrys sat down across from me, uninvited, and told me everything.

I should refuse them here. I want you to know that refusal was available. A lesser chapter of my life would have ended with me saying *no, take your paper, take your child, take your dangerous eyes out of my rooms.* But guilt is not a stone you carry; it is a debt that accrues interest, and mine had been compounding for seven years at a rate I could not calculate.

Because here is what Emrys did not know when they walked through my door, and what I told them before the candle had burned a finger's width:

"I designed the census," I said.

They went very still. Tasters are good at stillness. Poisoners must be.

"The purge didn't begin with soldiers," I continued, because once the wound is open there is no dignity in bandaging it halfway. "It began with a questionnaire. Seven years ago, the Crown wanted to know who spoke the old tongues—not just Vaeli, all of them—so the new laws could be enforced 'humanely.' That was the word used. *Humanely.* I built the instrument. A linguistic survey. Innocent-looking questions about grandmother's lullabies, about words for bread and rain and grief. I calibrated it so that even a single inherited phrase would flag a household." I picked up the wine and found it empty, which felt correct. "Three thousand households answered honestly. I know the number because I counted the returns myself. Then I handed the list to the Ministry, and the Ministry handed it to the army, and the army was less interested in linguistics than I was."

The silence between us had texture. It had weight and grain, like wet sand.

"They erased the country," Emrys said finally. "Maps, records—"

"And speakers. Maps are easy to erase. A language lives in mouths, so you remove the mouths." I gestured at the paper between us. "That phrase survived. Which means a mouth survived. Which means my census missed someone, and I find—I find I have never in my life been so grateful to have failed at something."

"Why tell me this?" Emrys asked. "Why confess to a stranger?"

"Because you came to me holding a dead language like a live coal and asking for help, and you deserve to know that the man you're asking helped light the pyre." I leaned forward. "And because I want something. Scholars always want something. It's the last honest thing about us."

* * *

My price was simple, or it would have been simple if it weren't suicide.

"There is a crate," I told them, "in the cellar of the University archive, catalogued as damp-damaged liturgical fragments, value nil. It contains eleven years of my research. The full grammar of High Vaeli reconstructed from pre-purge sources. Phonology, morphology, the mourning registers, the *door-speech*—the ritual language used at thresholds, which is what your child is speaking. If I die, it dies. If it stays in that cellar, it rots. I want it out of the capital. Smuggled to the coast, to a printer in Greyharbor who owes me and fears me in equal measure. Can you do that?"

"I taste food for a living," Emrys said. "Nobody searches a taster. We're furniture."

"You're furniture that knows poisons. Even better." I studied them. They had the look of a person doing arithmetic with their own safety and finding the sum unacceptable but proceeding anyway. I had seen that look before, on braver faces than mine, the night the lists went out. "Then we have our bargain. Your translations for my exile. Though I warn you—" I tapped the paper, and this time I let myself touch it, let myself read the second line properly, "*sel vetha amai*, 'the door remembers what we loved,' and my hands shook, I'm telling you plainly—they shook—" "I warn you that helping you now does not balance the ledger. There is no arithmetic that balances it. Three thousand households. I can translate every sacred text in Vael and it will not make one of them unburned."

"Then why help?"

I considered lying to them. Considered saying something noble about scholarship, about preservation, about the survival of beauty. Instead I told the truth, because the truth was cheaper and I had already spent all my dignity.

"Because I am seventy-one years old," I said, "and I have begun dreaming in a language I helped kill, and I would like, before the end, to be useful to it rather than against it. Call it repentance if you like. Call it vanity. The dead don't care what we call it."

* * *

We worked until the candle drowned in its own wax. Emrys fed me phrases—more of the child's recitations, copied out in that careful hand—and I translated, and each translation landed on the table between us like a small detonation. The recitations were a route description. Thresholds, turns, a descent. *The tower of doors that hums.* Whoever taught this child had taught them the way home in the only tongue the way home would answer to.

It was near dawn when Emrys paused at my door and asked, quietly, whether anyone else knew they'd come to me.

"No," I said.

Which was true when I said it.

But you should know the rest, because I have decided to be a complete document rather than a flattering one. Two days before Emrys knocked, a woman came to my garret. Grey coat, soldier's posture, eyes like audit ledgers. She did not threaten me. Threats are for amateurs; Captain Sere simply sat in my one decent chair and remarked that it must be lonely, a man of my talents with no students, and that the Crown remembered its debts to useful scholars, and that she would call again.

She left no instructions. That was the instruction.

So when Emrys left my room that morning carrying a list of translations and a promise, I stood at the window and watched them merge into the market crowd, and I understood precisely what I had done. I had taken the one clean thing left in my life—my irrelevance—and spent it. Whatever Sere wanted, she now had two threads to pull instead of one, and I was the knot between them.

I went back to my table. The child's paper lay there. *Sel vetha amai.* The door remembers what we loved.

"Then remember this too," I said aloud to no one, and began, with hands that had stopped shaking, to write down everything I had just done—in case someone someday stood where I stood, needing to know exactly how a country gets murdered, and by whom, and in what order.

Outside, the chandler's boy was crossing the square toward the palace, walking quickly.

He had learned, at last, that fast errands paid better.
