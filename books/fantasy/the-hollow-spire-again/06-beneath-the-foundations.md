## 6. Beneath the Foundations

Cora hummed the way other children breathed—constantly, thoughtlessly, a small tuneless thread running through everything. For three days I had assumed it was habit, the way my mother had knitted when she was nervous, except I remembered no mother and no knitting, only the humming now, following me from room to room like a second shadow with better pitch.

It was Master Ivo who stopped mid-sentence over his tea, set down his cup with exaggerated care, and said, "Do that again."

Cora looked at him. Hummed.

"No—" Ivo pressed his fingers to his temples. "The intervals. Sing them apart."

I won't pretend I understood what happened next in anything but fragments. Ivo made Cora hold single notes while he tapped a ruler against the table, made them go higher, lower, stop, start again, until he was pale and gripping the edge of his desk as though the floor had tilted.

"It's not a song," he said. "Or it is, but it's also a map. Melodic cartography—the Vaeli encoded routes into music because paper burns and people forget, but a child doesn't forget a tune. Gods. It's been under our noses for seven years and it's been *humming*."

"Under our noses meaning what, exactly?"

He looked up at me with an expression I'd come to dread, the one that preceded bad news delivered in scholarly tones. "Meaning the route ends somewhere close, Emrys. Somewhere very close indeed. Listen to the last phrase." He hummed it back—a falling figure, three notes descending into a low held tone that seemed to vibrate in the teeth rather than the ear. "That's not a destination. That's a *depth*. The map goes down."

* * *

We went down on the fourth night, which was also the night before the solstice, because I am nothing if not a procrastinator with a death wish.

I told myself the plan was sound. The palace kitchens received their water from the old cisterns beneath the foundations; every taster learns the plumbing of the palace before they learn the faces of its nobles, because poison travels by water more often than by wine. I knew the maintenance grates, the rotation of the under-guards, the hours when the cistern galleries stood empty. Seven years of careful invisibility had taught me one thing thoroughly: how buildings breathe, and when they sleep.

Cora came because leaving them behind meant leaving them alone with Sere's watchers, and I had seen the watcher on the corner opposite my lodging two nights running, pretending to be drunk with a sobriety that would have impressed a temperance society. So: Cora, wrapped in a coat too big, humming so softly it was less sound than intention.

The grate behind the tannery yielded to a crowbar I'd bribed off a mason with three months of stomach-charms. Beyond it, the dark smelled of stone and standing water and something older underneath, a mineral coldness like the inside of a sealed jar.

"This is where you tell me we should turn back," I whispered to Cora, mostly out of politeness, since I'd already decided.

They took my hand instead. Their palm was warm. In the black, that warmth felt like a document certifying that at least one living thing approved of this lunacy.

* * *

The cisterns became galleries, the galleries became corridors, and the corridors became something no surveyor had ever claimed. The masonry changed under my fingertips—rougher brick giving way to fitted stone, fitted stone giving way to seams so fine I couldn't find them with a fingernail. And the seams sang back.

That's the only word I have. When Cora hummed, the walls answered—not echoing, *answering*, a faint sympathetic vibration that rose to meet each note like a dog recognizing its name. The deeper we went, the more insistent the response, until the whole passage around us thrummed with a low chord that I felt in my sternum and my fillings and, I suspect, my immortal soul, if any institution still recognized such a thing.

"Ivo said the Spire was destroyed," I murmured. "Everyone says it. Burned, broken, buried. A lesson."

Cora stopped walking. Looked up at me with those grave dark eyes, and hummed the descending phrase—Ivo's *depth*—and pointed down.

"Yes, thank you, I gathered. Down. I'm walking, aren't I?"

They pointed down again, harder, with the patience of the very young dealing with the very slow, and then pressed both hands flat against the wall.

The wall was warm.

Stone this deep in the earth should have been cold as a creditor's heart. It wasn't. It carried heat the way a sleeping animal does, and under my own palms I felt it: a pulse. Slow. Enormous. Once every ten breaths, maybe twelve, patient as geology.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. Because there are moments when wit fails even me, and this was a cathedral of one.

The Spire hadn't been destroyed. It had been *buried*—deliberately, thoroughly, with the capital built on top of it like a cork in a bottle, like a headstone, like an apology no one would read aloud. The queen's palace, the ministries, the grand avenues where I'd walked invisible for seven years—all of it a lid. All of it weight pressing down on a door.

And we were underneath the weight, walking toward the hinge.

* * *

We found the door an hour before dawn, though dawn was a rumor down here; I judged time by the ache in my knees and the growing certainty that Sere's people would be missing us.

It stood at the end of a chamber that no human hands had built—or rather, human hands had built *around* it, brickwork shoring up walls that predated brick, buttressing a space that didn't need buttressing. The door itself was tall as three men and narrow as a coffin lid, black stone shot through with veins of something that caught Cora's shuttered lantern and scattered it green.

And it was locked. Obviously. But not with iron.

Wards crawled across its surface in High Vaeli script, and here is the humiliating truth: I could *almost read them*. The shapes were familiar the way a face seen once in a crowd is familiar. My eyes traced the characters and my mind supplied meanings a beat later, half-guessed, half-remembered—*blood, key, season, turning*—words rising in me like bubbles from a depth I didn't know I had.

"Blood," I said aloud, testing the translation. "Keyed to blood. Royal blood?"

Cora shook their head slowly. Then they took my hand, turned it palm-up, and touched one fingertip to the pad of my thumb—the callus I have from years of lifting poisoned cups, the spot where the numbness lives deepest—and then to their own chest.

"Not royal," I said slowly. "*Tainted.* Keyed to tainted blood?" I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Seven years of drinking poison for the crown, and the crown never realized they were manufacturing the only key that fits."

Except someone must have known. Someone had removed pages from ledgers. Someone had hired tasters with Vaeli names and fed them slow ruin. Either the wards were keyed against us—

—or we were exactly what someone wanted kept here, alive and numb and conveniently located above a sealed door.

I filed that thought where I file all thoughts that might make me lie down and refuse to get up.

Cora, meanwhile, had found the answer to the second question without my help. They crouched at the base of the door, where a channel had been cut into the threshold stone—a groove, dry now, running the width of the door—and hummed the full melody, start to finish, all of it, louder than I'd ever heard them. The chamber woke. Every surface vibrated. The green veins in the door brightened like veins under pale skin, and in the channel, dust stirred and swirled as if remembering liquid.

And above the door, characters I couldn't read began, very softly, to glow in sequence—one, then the next, then the next—a countdown, or a calendar, or a promise.

The last glyph to light was a circle bisected by a line.

An eclipse.

"The solstice eclipse," I breathed. "Tomorrow night. It opens tomorrow night, for—" I spread my hands helplessly. "For how long? An hour? A minute? Long enough?"

Cora looked at the glowing circle for a long moment. Then they held up one finger.

One hour. Or one chance. With Cora, it amounts to the same thing.

* * *

We were back through the cisterns as the sky went grey, and I'll admit I emerged from the tannery grate feeling less like a triumphant explorer and more like a burglar who'd found the vault empty but heard footsteps upstairs. The city smelled of bread and coal smoke and ordinary morning, and I wanted to kiss it.

Which is, of course, when Captain Sere stepped out of the alley mouth.

She was alone, which was somehow worse than a squad. No armor—just her grey coat, her hands folded loosely, and the particular stillness of a woman who has already decided everything and is merely waiting for you to catch up.

"Emrys Sandoval." My full name. Not *taster*, not *you there*. She'd never used my full name before, and hearing it in the empty street was like hearing a lock turn. "You're up early."

"Insomnia," I said. "Chronic. You should see my physician's bills."

"Mm." Her eyes moved past me—to Cora, small and silent in the oversized coat, watching her with an expression far too old—and then back to my face. To my hands. I followed her glance and felt my stomach drop: my fingertips were grey with cistern silt, and there, unmistakable, a smear of green luminescence along my thumb where I'd touched the door's ward-script.

It glowed faintly. In daylight. Like a signature.

Sere looked at it for a long, long moment. Then she looked at me, and I braced for the whistle, the guards, the end of everything.

She did none of it. She simply said, quietly, "Solstice tonight. The queen expects you at the evening tasting, as usual. Don't be late." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "She counts on you."

And she walked away, coat swaying, leaving me standing in the street with a glowing hand, a mute child gripping my sleeve, and the dawning understanding that I had just been given two choices dressed up as one instruction.

She knew. She had to know—the smear, the hour, the route, all of it. And she'd said *don't be late*.

Either Sere was offering me a rope in the shape of a warning, or she was measuring out enough line to watch me hang. Tonight, under the eclipse, with the door open and the queen's kitchen expecting my tongue in its usual place, I would find out which.

Cora tugged my sleeve and hummed the descending phrase. *Down.* One finger raised.

One hour. One chance.

One night left to decide whose side forgetting was on.
