## 7. An Audience with the Queen

The summons came at midmorning, which was the first insult. Royal business, in my experience, preferred the margins of the day—the gray hour before dawn when a taster can be quietly disappeared, or the blue hour after supper when a charm-seller can be quietly hanged. Midmorning meant the queen wanted an audience, and audiences were for things she wanted witnessed.

"You'll wear the gray," said the page, a boy of perhaps eleven with the flat delivery of someone who had rehearsed this. "Her Majesty's instruction."

The gray. My service livery, undyed wool, the color of ash and of people who have made themselves deliberately difficult to look at. I owned it; I wore it twice a week; it had never once been *instruction*. Somewhere behind that word was Captain Sere, and somewhere behind Sere was my name on a list, and I found I could not guess how far down the ink went.

I dressed in the gray. One does not argue with a summons any more than one argues with weather.

* * *

The queen received me in the small solar, not the throne room—a distinction I catalogued the way a drowning man catalogues water temperature. The solar held a table, two chairs, a window looking east over the lower city where the morning smoke rose straight up in windless air, and Queen Isolde, who was not wearing her crown.

That was the second thing that unsettled me. In seven years I had seen her crowned exactly four times, always from the side of the hall among the servers, forgettable as furniture. Crowned, she was a monument. Uncrowned, with her hair simply braided and her hands wrapped around a cup of something steaming, she looked—there is no kind way to say this—like a tired woman of fifty-three who slept badly.

"Emrys Sandoval," she said. "Sit."

I sat. The chair across from her was closer to hers than protocol allowed, and I understood that too: this was not going to be a reprimand. Reprimands keep their distance.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"Captain Sere has questions about my visitors," I said. "I imagine she's finished asking them."

A flicker at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "Sere reports that you receive a child at dusk. That the child does not speak. That you have since hidden the child among your spice stores like contraband pepper." She set down her cup. "She also reports that you've been visiting Master Ivo, whose library was confiscated six years ago, and that you spent three nights last week walking the cisterns beneath the palace, which no one does unless they are lost, mad, or looking for something."

The floor did not open up beneath me. I noted this as a missed opportunity.

"Your Majesty," I began, and she raised one hand, and the hand alone was enough.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said, "and then you're going to tell me whether I'm wrong, because if I'm wrong, all of this is very embarrassing for both of us." She looked out the window at the smoke. "Seven years ago we ended the war with Vael. You will notice my phrasing. Not won it. Ended it. There is a difference, and the difference is a country that would not surrender and so had to be made to stop existing. Not destroyed—my generals wanted destruction and I denied them—but erased. Maps redrawn. Records burned. Names struck. A language outlawed on pain of the same erasure." Her voice stayed level throughout, the way mine stays level when I swallow arsenic. "Do you know what happens to a language when you kill its speakers, Emrys?"

"It waits," I said, before I could stop myself.

Something moved behind her eyes. "It waits," she agreed. "And seven years later it turns up on your doorstep in the mouth of a mute child, and you understand it, and you don't report it. Instead you feed it, hide it, carry it toward the one door in my kingdom I sealed personally." She turned back to me. "Am I wrong?"

I am, by profession, a liar of the smallest possible denominations—I lie about headaches, about allergies, about whether the soup tastes of bitter almond. Faced with a monarch asking me directly, I discovered I had nothing in my inventory large enough.

"You're not wrong," I said.

* * *

What surprised me most was the relief. I had expected terror and got instead a strange lightness, as though I'd been carrying Cora's existence in my chest for weeks and could finally set it down—even here, even in front of her. I sat in the queen's solar with my treason confirmed and felt my shoulders drop half an inch.

Isolde watched me relax and, I think, understood it better than I did.

"You were born there," she said. Not a question. "In Vael. Before. Your recruitment papers say Ashfall, in the eastern marches, but Ashfall didn't exist until the cartographers invented it, and neither did your parents' names, and neither, if I'm honest, did half the biography we built for you." She leaned forward. "The corps took you because of what you are, Emrys. Vaeli blood carries something in it—we never determined what, some resilience of the body or the spirit—that lets a taster survive doses that kill anyone else. We needed tasters. So we went looking for survivors who didn't know they were survivors, gave them new names, and put them at my table."

"And the doses," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "The immunity program. Building tolerance, year over year."

Was it my imagination, or did her hands tighten around the cup?

"The program has many purposes," she said.

"There is no purpose," I said, "that requires dosing children who were already safe. I've read the ledgers now. I know what the gaps are hiding. Every taster hired since the war carries a Vaeli name, and every one of us is being poisoned slowly enough that none of us can prove it—and the ones whose blood runs truest get the strongest draughts." I heard my own voice go quiet and cold, a register I hadn't known I owned. "You're not breeding immunity, Majesty. You're finishing the war."

For a long moment she said nothing. The steam off her cup thinned and vanished. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, wearier, stripped of the solar and the crown and the whole apparatus of queenship.

"Yes," she said. "Some of it. Not all of it, but more than I will ever be forgiven for, and I stopped asking forgiveness some time ago." She looked at me directly then, and I wished she hadn't, because there was nothing regal in her face at all. Only loneliness, vast and domestic as a piece of furniture she'd lived with so long she'd stopped seeing it. "Do you know what it costs to erase a country? Not the fighting—I could delegate the fighting. The after. Every map, every ledger, every song. And then every year after, making sure it stays erased, because erasure isn't an act, Emrys, it's a chore. It's sweeping a floor that keeps gathering dust. I have been sweeping for seven years and I am so tired." Her laugh was short and awful. "And now a child walks out of nowhere speaking a dead tongue, humming songs under a tower I buried, and everyone who hears her will remember. Not learn—*remember*. Because you don't actually kill a language. It waits in the blood. As well in yours as anywhere."

"So the mercy," I said. "Sere mentioned mercy."

"She would." Isolde rose and went to the window, her back to me, and for a moment she was only a silhouette against the smoke of a city built on top of another country's grave. "Here is what I offer you, and I offer it honestly, which should tell you how much I want to say yes to it myself. Surrender the child. She will be kept—warmly, kindly, I give you my word, and my word on a child's safety is the only coin I have left worth anything. In exchange, you drink a final draught tonight. Not poison. Something older, out of the Spire's own stores. It takes the memories. All of them. You wake tomorrow as Emrys Sandoval of Ashfall, taster to the crown, fifteen years of faithful service, and nothing underneath. No dreams in a dead language. No hum under the floorboards. No grief for a home you cannot name." She turned around. "And in exchange for that, you live honored. A stipend. A house above ground. My personal protection, which in this city is worth more than gold. You live, Emrys, and you live *light*."

The word landed in me like a stone into deep water. *Light.*

Because that was the thing—I knew exactly what she was offering, because I had been buying it in installments my whole life. Forgetting by degrees. The numb tongue, the invented hometown, the careful gray invisibility. I had constructed an entire self out of not-remembering and called it survival. She was offering me the finished version. The complete edition. A life with the weight finally taken out of it.

And God help me, part of me wanted it. Part of me stood right there in that warm solar and thought: *you could sleep. You could sleep without dreaming in Vaeli. You could taste the queen's breakfast and feel nothing but bread.*

"You're lonely," I said quietly. "That's what this is really about."

She didn't flinch, which was worse than flinching.

"You sit at that table every morning," I went on, "and everyone in the room either fears you or performs for you, and once—once—someone sat across from you who remembered the same world you helped destroy. Someone who dreamed in the language you outlawed. And you can't decide whether to keep me or kill me, because keeping me means the dust keeps gathering, and killing me means—" I stopped. "Killing me means you're alone with it. Completely."

"Careful," she said softly.

"You're not offering mercy. You're offering company. A companion for the sweeping—one who's forgotten everything, so you don't have to look at it in their face every morning at breakfast." I stood, slowly, because my knees felt strange. "I love you, Majesty. I think you know that. Fifteen years, and I'd have taken poison for you gladly a hundred times, and I think some small part of you knows that too, and it's why we're having this conversation in a solar instead of a cell." My heart was hammering and my voice stayed steady, which is the strangest trick the human body knows. "But I can't take the draught. And I can't give you the child."

The silence stretched long enough that I could hear the smoke moving against the window glass.

"You understand," she said at last, and her voice had gone back to being the queen's voice, cool and distant and terribly sad, "that if you walk out of here and I do nothing, I am no longer the queen who ended the war. I am the queen who let it restart."

"I understand," I said, "that the war doesn't restart unless you send soldiers after a mute little girl."

"Everything restarts," Isolde said. "That is the curse of ruling. Nothing stays buried just because you were tired." She sat back down and folded her hands, and looked at me for a long moment with something that was almost tenderness and almost a eulogy. "Go home, Emrys. Go home and be with the child. I find I need until the solstice to decide what kind of queen I am."

I walked to the door. My hand was on the latch when she spoke again, and her voice had dropped so low it barely crossed the room.

"For what it's worth—the mercy was real. The cage was real. They're the same thing, and I built them both, and I don't know anymore which one I meant to give you."

I did not turn around. If I had, I might have stayed.

* * *

The corridor outside was full of ordinary noise—boots, distant kitchen-clatter, a guard shifting his weight—and I moved through it feeling like a man walking on the surface of a frozen lake, listening to it crack. Sere stood at the end of the hall, arms crossed, watching me come. She didn't stop me. She didn't speak. She simply stepped aside with a small, deliberate motion, like a door opening, or closing.

When I got home, Cora was waiting in the spice store among the cinnamon and the black pepper, humming the low, patient note that I now knew was not a song at all but a countdown.

Two days to the solstice.

The eclipse would come whether the queen decided or not.
