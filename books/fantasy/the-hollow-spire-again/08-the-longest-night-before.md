## 8. The Longest Night Before

The last honest thing I did that day was eat my own cooking.

Not the queen's — mine. A bowl of barley porridge in my narrow kitchen above the spice stores, eaten standing up, while Cora watched me from atop a flour barrel with the solemnity of a small judge deciding whether to hang me or pardon me. The porridge tasted of nothing, which was fitting. Everything had been tasting of nothing lately, except the words.

Cora hummed while I washed the bowl. The same phrase, over and over, the one that mapped the way beneath the foundations — down through the cisterns, along the sealed street, to the door under the palace where a country nobody remembered still stood waiting behind its wards. Tomorrow night the eclipse would come, and the wards would loosen like an old woman's grip on a stair rail, and either we'd walk through or we'd die trying, which were, if I was honest, the two options I'd been circling all my life anyway.

"You should sleep," I told Cora, in the pidgin we'd built out of gesture and half-learned Vaeli. "Tomorrow's long."

The child shook their head once, the way they did when they meant *you* should sleep but hadn't the words to say so. Then they went back to humming, and I sat down at my table with Ivo's translations spread before me like a losing hand of cards, and I tried not to think about how quiet the city was.

Cities are never quiet the night before something. Dogs know it. Cats know it. Somewhere below us, the whole capital was holding its breath, and I kept catching myself listening for boots on stairs.

They came at the third bell past midnight. Not for me.

For him.

I knew it before I knew it, the way you know rain is coming from the smell of it — through the window over the spice stores I saw lanterns moving in the street below, too many, too orderly, the way soldiers walk when they're pretending to be constables. And across the lane, in Master Ivo's crooked little house with its crooked little chimney, a light went out that had burned every night since I'd known him. Ivo never slept in the dark. He said darkness was where languages went to die.

I was down the stairs and into the cold before I'd decided anything, which is how most of my worst decisions have gone: fully formed, wearing shoes, already halfway to ruin.

* * *

They took him quietly. That was the worst part. Four agents in plain wool, a covered cart, and Master Ivo walking between them with his hands bound in front of him like a man being escorted to his own lecture. He wasn't struggling. He was *talking*. Even now, even taken, he was leaning toward one of the agents and gesturing with his tied hands as though correcting the man's grammar, and I swear on every poison I've ever survived that the agent looked embarrassed about it.

I pressed myself into the shadow of a doorway ten paces away, close enough to smell the lantern oil, and did nothing.

I want to be clear about what that cost. There is a particular arithmetic a taster learns — how much bitterness can be masked by how much honey, how long a body takes to fail against how fast an antidote works. I ran it all in the space between one lantern passing and the next: four agents, one knife on me, a sleeping child upstairs who needed me alive more than Ivo needed me brave. Cora couldn't pass through that door alone without knowing what the words meant. Ivo was the only other tongue in the kingdom that could teach them in time.

But Ivo was also the only other tongue in the kingdom, full stop. Which made him worth taking, and made me next, and made the arithmetic very simple and very ugly.

I let the cart roll away. I stood in the doorway until my legs stopped wanting to chase it, and then I walked back up to my rooms and sat down across from Cora, who was awake, who had heard everything, whose eyes in the dark were the color of the Spire door before it opens.

"They took him," I said, in Vaeli, badly, because some things you should say in the language they belong to. "*Taken. Him.*"

Cora looked at me for a long time. Then the child reached out and touched my hand — the first time Cora had ever initiated contact — and spoke one word. Slowly. Twice.

It wasn't *run*. I knew enough by then to know it wasn't run.

It was *choose.*

* * *

I should explain something about interrogations, since apparently I'm collecting them.

Captain Sere didn't take me at dawn, or at dusk, or in the street with a sack over my head. She sent a page with a note written in her own hand — she has beautiful handwriting, Sere, the kind they must beat into you at spymistress school along with the garroting — inviting me to breakfast. *Come alone,* it said, which was funny, because I'd stopped being alone the moment a mute child knocked on my door, and Sere knew it better than anyone.

So there we were, mid-morning, in a room in the palace I'd never seen, with tea neither of us drank and a plate of little cakes I tested out of pure habit. Old instinct. You spend seven years tasting for a living and you check everything, even the trap.

"Your linguist is comfortable," Sere said. "As comfortable as we can make him. He's cooperating. He always cooperates; it's his tragedy. Did you know he designed the census? Every speaker marked, every household counted. He keeps hoping that if he helps enough people now, the ledger will balance." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "It doesn't balance, Emrys. It never balances. It only weighs."

"You could have just arrested me," I said.

"I could have arrested you weeks ago. I've known since the cisterns." She said it mildly, the way you mention weather. "You leave bootprints in silt, by the way. Very distinctive soles. A taster should be less memorable."

Everything in me went carefully, professionally blank. It's the same face I wear swallowing hemlock extract. "Then why am I drinking your terrible tea?"

"Because I'm going to tell you the truth, and you're going to need somewhere to sit while it lands." She set the cup down. And then Captain Sere, the queen's grey wolf, the woman who had hunted speakers of dead tongues for seven years, said a sentence in flawless High Vaeli.

I won't write it here. Some things aren't for the page. But it was a child's rhyme — a skipping rhyme, the kind girls sing in streets — and she sang it flatly, tonelessly, the way you recite something you've forbidden yourself to feel, and I understood every word, and I understood what she was telling me.

"You're Vaeli," I said.

"My mother sang that to me. My mother, who was erased so thoroughly that when I try to remember her face I get a shape where a face should be." Sere's voice never rose. That was the frightening thing; it never rose. "I was eight when the purges came. An officer found me hiding in a granary and I did the smart thing. I forgot. Out loud. On command. In front of witnesses. I forgot so well they gave me a uniform." She looked at me then, and for one unguarded moment I saw it — not the spymistress, but the girl in the granary, doing sums with her own soul. "That's the offer I'm making you, Emrys. Not the queen's mercy. Mine. Walk away tonight. Take the draught tomorrow morning, lose the language, lose the dreams, keep the pension. Forget, and live. I've done it for thirty years. It's survivable. I'm proof."

"And Ivo?"

"Ivo chose remembering. Look where remembering got him."

"And Cora?"

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, swallowed, gone. "The child is the door," she said. "Doors don't survive wars. Countries come back through doors. I watched it happen once, from inside the granary, through a knothole. I watched my street burn because someone's cousin remembered a song." She leaned forward. "Forgetting isn't cowardice, Emrys. Forgetting is what mercy looks like when it grows up and gets practical. You taste poison for a living. You of all people know that survival is mostly a matter of what you refuse to swallow."

"And what happens to Cora if I swallow your mercy?"

"The queen keeps her word. The child lives. Comfortably. Watched. Empty." She straightened. "You have until moonset. After that I do my job."

I stood. My legs worked, which surprised me. At the door I paused, because I owed her this much — she had shown me the granary, after all, and you don't spit into an open wound no matter whose it is.

"When you sang that rhyme," I said, "your accent slipped."

Her jaw tightened.

"It's a good accent," I said. "Vaeli vowels. You didn't forget everything, Captain. You just buried it deep enough to build a career on top." I opened the door. "I'm not going to do that. I spent seven years being forgettable, and it nearly killed me slower than any poison would have."

"Emrys—"

"Somebody has to remember," I said. "Somebody always has to. Or else the burning never stops, it just changes streets."

I left her sitting there with her terrible tea and her quarter-turned cup and the shape of a country where her mother's face should be.

* * *

Moonset came early, as moonsets do when you're dreading them.

I packed nothing, because packing announces itself. I put Ivo's translations in my coat, the ones he'd finished before they took him, and found — folded inside the last page, in his cramped hand — a note I hadn't noticed before, because Ivo hid things the way scholars do, in plain sight under annotation. Three lines. The first two were instructions: the guards at the cistern gate rotate at the eclipse's first shadow; the ward-stone answers to blood freely given, not blood spilled.

The third line wasn't instructions. It was a single word in High Vaeli, and underneath it, in our alphabet, so I couldn't miss it: *"Say it aloud at the door. It means 'I am still here.' They will hear you. We always hear each other. That is the whole problem with us."*

Old fool. Brave old complicit foolish traitor of a man. I laughed until it turned into something else, and Cora, watching from the flour barrel, tilted their head at me with the patience of someone who has already understood everything and is simply waiting for the adults to catch up.

"We're not running," I told them. "We're going to get him back. Or die in a hole under the palace, which, honestly, feels on-brand."

Cora nodded once, gravely, and slid down off the barrel, and hummed the map.

Outside, the first shadow of the eclipse was still hours away, and the city held its breath, and somewhere in a cell Master Ivo was saying a word aloud to whoever would listen — *I am still here, I am still here* — and for the first time in seven years I stopped calculating, stopped weighing, stopped tasting the odds like a professional.

I picked up the child's hand, and I chose.
