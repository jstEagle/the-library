## 9. The Eclipse at the Spire

There is a particular quality to waiting for your own execution that no one warns you about: it is mostly logistics.

I spent the last hour of the old year checking my pockets like a fussy aunt. Vial of Ivo's tallow-salve for the ward-seams. The ledger pages—my testimony, copied in three hands so no single seizure could kill them all. A stub of candle. Cora's hand in mine, small and dry and steady, which shamed me, because mine was not.

The cisterns were louder than before. Not with water—with people. Sere had pulled her watchers off the streets and packed them below, torchlight bobbing along the sealed galleries like fireflies that had learned to file reports. We moved through the maintenance ways Ivo had mapped from his census days, the ones he'd drawn from memory and guilt, and twice we pressed ourselves into niches while patrols passed close enough that I could smell the tallow on their boots.

Cora didn't tremble. I found that obscene, somehow—a child should be afraid of this. Instead they watched the soldiers with the flat patience of someone who had already survived a country dying, and I understood that what I was about to do, I was doing for a person braver than me. That was either comforting or damning. I hadn't decided.

* * *

We reached the outer door as the moon began to eat the sun.

I want to describe it properly, because everything after happened quickly and this part deserves slowness. The Hollow Spire does not look buried; it looks *waiting*. The door stands thirty feet high in a chamber the masons never touched, black stone veined with silver that catches light the way water catches a blade. The wards across its surface aren't carved—they're written, lines of High Vaeli flowing down the stone in script that moves when you don't look directly at it, patient as a tide.

Cora began to hum.

I'd heard it before, in the spice stores, in the tunnels—the little looping melody that was either a song or a map or both. But here, against the actual door, the hum changed. It deepened. And the script answered, silver brightening vein by vein, and the whole chamber filled with a sound I can only call recognition. The tower knew the tune. The tower had been waiting for someone who still remembered it.

"The eclipse window," I murmured, though there was no one to warn. "Quarter of an hour. Maybe less."

Cora looked up at me and spoke—one of their rare, hoarded words. *"Sandoval."* My name, in Vaeli, where it apparently meant something longer and sadder than it did in the capital. Something like *the one who tastes*.

"Right," I said. "Let's go ruin a perfectly good kingdom."

* * *

The wards rejected Cora first.

It wasn't cruel—it was almost gentle. The child pressed both palms to the stone and the silver flared white and threw them back three feet into my arms, unharmed but ringing like a struck bell. I caught a flash of understanding through the contact: the door wanted a key, and Cora was not the key. Cora was the *hand on the latch*. The key was something else. Something the door would taste.

I knew before I cut my palm. Of course I did. Seven years of the queen's breakfasts, seven years of the immunity program that was neither—seven years of slow, methodical poisoning distilled into a taster's blood, until I was less a man than a record of every toxin the crown had ever fed its enemies. The wards were keyed to royal blood, yes. But they were also keyed to *Vael*, and what the crown had been making, all this time, without knowing it, was a Vaeli key out of stolen blood.

They built the lock and the key from the same prisoners. It was almost elegant. I laughed, and it came out wrong.

"Emrys." Cora grabbed my sleeve, eyes wide, and shook their head hard.

"It's fine," I lied, with the fluency of a professional. "I've tasted worse."

I opened my already-cut palm—Ivo's knife had done its work on the way down—and pressed it flat against the script.

The door drank.

There is no other word. The silver veins went dark as my blood touched them, then flushed red, then gold, and the moving script stilled letter by letter, reading me the way I read the queen's soup: patiently, thoroughly, cataloguing every poison, every suppressed year, every word of Vaeli I'd swallowed and refused to forget. It hurt. I'll say that plainly, because everyone else in this story lies about pain—it felt like being *read*, like having my whole miserable careful life turned over and inspected by something ancient and unimpressed, and when it finished, the door exhaled.

And the chamber changed.

Not the stone—the air. Warm air moved through the seams around the doorframe, smelling of cedar smoke and river water and something green, growing, impossibly alive thirty feet under the palace foundations. The tower's sleeping voice, Ivo had called it. It wasn't asleep anymore. It was a low chord beneath hearing, felt in the teeth, and every hair on my body stood up and sang harmony.

The eclipse reached totality somewhere above us. In the dark, the door opened.

Only a crack. A seam of gold light down the middle, widening by inches, and through it—not darkness, not another tunnel, but *sky*. An honest sky, pale and wide, over rooftops I didn't recognize and a river the color of hammered tin and terraced hills going up and up into morning. Vael. Not erased. Not dead. Just *elsewhere*, behind a door in the dark, breathing.

Cora made a sound I will hear until I die—small and cracked and joyful, a mute child finding their voice at the threshold of home.

That's when the soldiers came.

* * *

Six of them, torches high, Captain Sere at their head—and behind her, held upright between two guardsmen with a split lip and a wild, triumphant grin, Master Ivo.

"You're supposed to be in a cell," I said.

"I bribed a guard with the only currency he respected," Ivo rasped, "which was being insufferable until he opened the door to make me stop." His eyes went past me to the seam of gold and his face did something complicated, grief and awe fighting for the same ground. "Oh. Oh, it's real. Seven years I told myself it couldn't—" He stopped himself. Looked at Sere. "Captain. Your orders?"

"Take the taster," Sere said. "Seal the corridor. No one speaks of what's behind that door."

Her voice was level. Her sword was drawn. But she hadn't moved yet, and her eyes kept going to the light the way a starving man's go to bread, and I thought—there it is. There's the survivor of Vael who chose the crown, looking at the country she traded away.

"Sere," I said. "Come see it. One second. Just look."

"Don't." The word came out raw. She steadied herself. "You think I haven't imagined it? Every night for seven years? I know exactly what's through that door, Emrys. That's why I'm standing here." She raised the sword a fraction. "Step away from it."

"I can't."

"You can. You've spent your whole life stepping away from things. It's your one talent."

"That was survival," I said. "This is different." And I discovered, saying it, that it was true. Somewhere between the ledger and the child and the queen's kind, terrible mercy, I had stopped calculating. "You told me forgetting was survival. Maybe. But somebody has to survive as more than an absence. Somebody has to remember on purpose."

"Guards," Sere said quietly.

They came forward—and Ivo stepped in front of them.

He didn't fight. He was seventy years old with a split lip; he'd have lost a fight to a strong breeze. What he did was worse. He planted his feet, faced the soldiers, and began to speak High Vaeli—loud, formal, cadenced, the recitation cadence of the old census, but inverted, unmade, every marking-phrase turned into an opening phrase. And the tower heard him.

The chord beneath the floor rose. The silver script on the walls—I'd never noticed it covered the *walls*, the whole chamber, the entire buried street—ignited, and the words began to move, to pour, and the sound that came out of the stone was a voice. Deep, layered, many-throated, speaking Vaeli over Ivo's recitation like a wave coming in under a gull's cry. The guards stopped as if they'd hit glass. Two of them dropped their torches. One fell to his knees, and I saw his lips move, shaping sounds he couldn't have known, words rising out of him like water out of a well nobody remembered digging.

Everyone in this kingdom had swallowed Vael somewhere. The tower just gave it back.

"Go," Ivo shouted over it, grinning through the blood on his mouth. "Taster! Take the child and *go*—"

Sere moved. Of course she did—she was the best blade in the palace and she was three steps from me and she had made her choice years ago and meant to keep it. I had one hand on Cora's shoulder, one reaching for the door.

She got within arm's reach. I will always believe she could have taken me.

Instead, at the last stride, her foot came down on the fallen torch, and she stumbled—just half a beat, just enough—and her sword point scraped the floor beside my boot instead of through my chest, and our eyes met over the length of it, and she said, very softly, so no one else could hear:

"*Kesset varo.*"

*I remember too.*

Then she straightened, turned to her frozen soldiers, and bellowed, "The wards are collapsing—fall back to the third gallery!"—and drove them all away from us with the sheer authority of a woman lying fluently.

I didn't thank her. There wasn't time, and she wouldn't have wanted it, and some debts are better carried than paid.

* * *

The door was wide enough now. Beyond it, wind. Real wind, moving grass somewhere out of sight, carrying woodsmoke and bells—bells, a country ringing its own hours—and a light that fell on Cora's face and made them look, finally, like a child and nothing else.

"One passes," I said, because the tower had told me, in that reading of my blood, in words I somehow already knew. "One stays. The door seals for a generation."

Cora looked at me. At the gap. At me again. Then they took the folded testimony from my pocket themselves—the pages I'd written, the ledger gaps, the names of every taster the crown had poisoned—and pressed it back against my chest with both small hands, fierce, refusing.

"No," I said gently. "That's the whole point of you. You're the witness that walks. They can't erase what gets out." I knelt, so we were eye to eye. "Listen to me. When you get there—and you will get there—tell them everything. Tell them a taster remembers. Tell them the door was opened by poison and stubbornness and an old man who couldn't stop talking. Tell them Vael was never gone. It was just quiet."

Cora's eyes were streaming. They grabbed my collar and pulled their forehead against mine and held there, humming the map-song one last time—but slower now, and I understood it had never been a map at all. It was a lullaby. Someone had sung it to them, once, in a country behind a door, and sent them into the dark to find the one who tastes.

"Go home," I whispered in Vaeli, my accent atrocious, my meaning perfect.

Cora stepped through.

For one moment the light took them entirely, gold from crown to heel, and they turned—of course they turned—and lifted one hand. Then the country beyond folded around them like water closing, the great door swung home with a sound like a book shutting, and the silver script dimmed to embers, and the chamber went ordinary and dark and cold.

The bells faded last. Or maybe I just kept hearing them.

* * *

I sat on the cold stone for a long time with my bleeding hand cradled against my chest and the testimony pages safe inside my coat, and above me, faint through thirty feet of foundation, the sun came back from its eclipse and the palace resumed its noise, oblivious, fed, unpoisoned today.

Seven years of tasting, and the truest thing I ever swallowed was that door opening.

Somewhere upstairs, Queen Isolde was learning that her mercy had failed. Somewhere in the third gallery, Sere was deciding what lie to tell her—and whether, this once, to tell the truth instead. Somewhere beyond a door that would stay shut for a generation, a child was telling a resurrected country that it had been remembered.

And I got up, wiped my face, and started the long climb back toward daylight—toward the taster's table, the queen's breakfast, the open secret of my own survival.

A witness is just a poison that works slowly.

I intended to be very effective.
