## 10. The One Who Remembers

The door was open, and the door was closing, and between those two facts stood a child, a coward, and seven years of carefully cultivated nothing.

I had imagined this moment—during the long climb back through the cisterns, during Ivo's voice unraveling into the tower's sleeping throat, during the terrible bright minute when my own tainted blood had done what royal blood could not. I had imagined I would be brave at the end of it.

Here is what bravery actually looked like: me, on my knees in the Spire's doorway, holding Cora's face in both hands while a country breathed on the other side of us.

Because that's what it was. Not a room, not a passage. A country. The door opened onto morning—the real kind, the kind that comes up gold over wheat fields—and air that smelled like rain on stone and woodsmoke and something green and growing, something I hadn't smelled since before I could name smells. Somewhere beyond the threshold, bells. Vaeli bells, with their odd doubled rhythm, tolling a pattern I knew the way you know your own heartbeat: *come home, come home, come home.*

"Only one," the tower had said, in Ivo's translated voice, in the old woman's voice it remembered from before the sealing. "The key spends itself. One passes; the rest wait a generation."

A generation. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. If I sent Cora through, the next opening would come when we were both old or dead. If I went instead—

Cora solved it for me, the way children solve things, by refusing to pretend the problem was complicated. They took my hand, pressed it flat against their own chest, and said—in High Vaeli, in that language with no country, which was now somehow also mine—"*You stay. You remember.*"

"I don't have to," I said, in the same tongue, badly, my accent a crime scene. "There might be another way. There's always another way."

They looked at me the way Sere had looked at me the night before, when she'd said *forgetting is survival* and I'd said *no* — except Cora's version of that look had no bitterness in it. Only patience. Only certainty. A child who had walked out of an erased country, alone, across a kingdom that had outlawed the memory of them, to find *the one who tastes.* They had not crossed all that distance so I could stand here negotiating with a door.

So I did the only thing left to do, the thing I should have understood from the first dusk knock at my door: I made myself useful.

From inside my coat I took the testimony. Weeks in the making—Ivo's census records copied in his cramped scholar's hand, the taster corps ledgers with their seven-year gap, my own account of the immunity program written out three times because my hand shook so badly the first two were illegible. Everything the crown had done, sealed in wax against damp, wrapped in oilcloth. Proof. A record. The one thing a country needs more than its missing children returned: the truth about who took them.

"You give this to whoever keeps your stories," I told Cora. "Whoever they trust. Whoever writes things down."

Cora took it with both hands, solemn as a coronation.

And then—because I am, whatever else I've become, still a merchant at heart, still someone who trades in small charms—I gave them the rest. The copper charm shaped like a spire that I'd never managed to sell. The honey candy from my pocket that had survived everything, even an eclipse. And last, pressed into their palm where no one could take it: the word. My first word, the one that had cracked my anonymity open at the beginning of all this. The word for *remember*, which is also, in High Vaeli, the word for *keep*, which is also the word for *refuse to let go.*

Cora repeated it back to me once, perfectly, the way they said everything—like the language had been waiting inside them the whole time for permission to come out.

Then they hugged me, brief and ferocious, around the ribs, the way you hug someone you intend to find again.

Then they stepped through.

I will not describe what I felt watching them walk into that golden morning. I am a professional. I have tasted arsenic with a steady face. But I will tell you that the bells got louder as they went, and that just before the light swallowed them, Cora turned and raised one hand—not waving, exactly. Saluting. The way you salute a bridge you've crossed, or a fire that kept you warm.

The door closed like a held breath released. The tower hummed down through its frequencies into silence. And I sat on the cold stone floor beneath the palace foundations, alone in the dark, keeping my memories.

That was the cost, in case anyone asks. That's what I chose over the queen's mercy-draught, over Sere's survival, over the soft gray blankness they'd all promised me. I kept everything. The smell of Vaeli rain. My mother's voice singing the doubled-bell song. The taste of every poison, catalogued and cross-referenced, each one a small murder attempted against a bloodline the crown wanted quiet. All of it, mine, permanent, heavy as a ledger I can never put down.

Some people get to forget. Somebody has to remember. It turns out I volunteered.

* * *

They didn't arrest me. I want that noted for the historical record, since apparently I'm going to be part of the historical record whether I like it or not.

Sere found me at dawn, sitting on the palace steps with cistern-water drying in my hair, looking, by her account, "like a man who has been dragged backward through his own life." She stood over me for a long moment. Her hand rested near her sword. Then she sheathed it, sat down beside me on the cold stone, and said nothing for the length of time it takes the sun to clear the rooftops.

"They'll say the Spire was a myth," she finally said. "The queen has already given the order. Officially there was no eclipse anomaly, no disturbance below the foundations, no child."

"I know."

"You could have gone." It wasn't an accusation. From Sere, it was almost tenderness. "The tower would have taken you. You're Vaeli enough. You've earned—"

"I'm the only one who stayed," I said. "That's the whole job now."

She looked at me then, really looked, and I saw the thing underneath her seven years of chosen silence: a woman who had survived by forgetting and had never once slept well. "It doesn't get lighter," she said. "Carrying it."

"No," I agreed. "But it gets familiar."

She laughed—one short, startled bark, the first honest sound I'd ever heard her make—and left me there to face the queen.

Queen Isolde received me in the small morning chamber, without guards, without ceremony. She looked older than she had a month ago. Kingship does that, I suppose, though in her case I suspect it was learning that her mercy-cage had failed, that her taster had chosen truth over comfort, that somewhere under her palace a door had opened onto everything she'd spent seven years pretending wasn't real.

"You sent the child through," she said.

"Yes, Majesty."

"And kept your memories."

"Yes, Majesty."

She studied me the way she studied her food after I tasted it—looking for poison, looking for betrayal, looking for the flaw that would let her dismiss the whole dish. She didn't find one. Finally she said, quietly: "I offered you forgetting. Honored retirement. A life with no weight in it."

"You offered me a smaller cage," I said. "With better upholstery. I've lived in cages, Majesty. I prefer doors."

Another silence. Then—gods help me—the Queen of the Realm laughed too, tired and rueful, and waved me toward the breakfast tray as if none of it had happened. "Taste," she said. "And Emrys? Leave the door open when you go. I find I like hearing the corridor these days. I find I've been very quiet for a very long time."

I tasted. Almond porridge, saffron, no poison. I bowed, and left the door open, and did not die, which by my standards constitutes a triumph.

* * *

Weeks pass. I resume the routine, because routine is a kind of armor and because someone has to taste the queen's breakfast, and because there is a particular irony in being the crown's most conspicuous secret that I have decided to enjoy.

Because that's what I am now. An open secret. Everyone knows. The kitchen girls know—the youngest one leaves extra honey on my tray and won't meet my eye, which is kitchen-girl for *reverence*. The guard captains know. Captain Sere knows most of all, and has taken, without ever saying a word about it, to assigning herself the corridor outside the tasting chamber at odd hours, standing watch over the one witness she couldn't make silent.

The court calls me *the Rememberer*, half-mockingly, the way courts adopt anything they can't kill. I answer to it. I've answered to worse.

And in the taverns—this is the part I love, the part that makes the weight worth carrying—things are stirring. Small things. A ballad singer in the Drowned Rat has started ending his set with a tune in a doubled rhythm nobody can place, and nobody asks him to stop. Someone scratched a spiral—the old Vaeli sign for *door*—into a table in the Salt Quarter, and by week's end there were nine spirals in that tavern alone, appearing overnight like mushrooms. Last Tuesday a wine merchant swore to me, dead serious over a cup of watered red, that his grandmother used to sing a lullaby "from a country that fell off the map," and did I think there was anything in that?

"There's everything in that," I told him, and bought his next cup, and listened to the lullaby twice, and corrected his grandmother's pronunciation in my head, gently, the way you correct someone you love.

They don't know yet what the songs mean. They don't know why the word *Vael* feels like a stone dropped down a well—they haven't heard the splash yet. But words travel. Words are the one cargo no border can search, the one poison I've spent my whole career administering, dose by dose, table by table, tavern by tavern.

Seven years ago, a crown tried to erase a country and nearly succeeded. It failed for one reason: somebody kept remembering. First a mute child with a tower humming in their bones. Then a disgraced linguist doing penance in stolen margins. Then a spymistress who couldn't quite sleep. And now a food-taster with a numb tongue and a ledger of survivals, telling lullabies to wine merchants and leaving spirals where the curious will find them.

One day—next year, or in twenty years, whenever the tower breathes again—there will be a knock at a door, and a child will step through, older now, carrying a testimony wrapped in oilcloth, and they will ask for *the one who remembers.*

I intend to be home. I intend to have the kettle on. And until that knock comes, I will do what I have always done best, better than anyone in this kingdom:

I will keep tasting everything. And I will refuse, one stubborn dawn at a time, to swallow the lie.
