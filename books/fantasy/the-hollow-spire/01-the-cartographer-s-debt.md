## 1. The Cartographer's Debt

The bone remembered the sea before it ever remembered the woman.

Gideon Sandoval held the femur up to the lamplight and turned it slowly, reading the grain of it the way a sailor reads weather. Dense at the shaft, porous near the ends—the bone of a woman who had walked far and carried heavy things. There was a knot of old healing near the knee, a greenstick fracture mended badly in childhood, and when Gideon's thumb passed over that place, the voice came: thin, salt-edged, a girl crying in a boathouse while someone splinted her leg with a barrel stave and told her not to tell her father.

*There you are,* Gideon thought. *Hello, Marta.*

"You can hear her already?" The daughter sat across the worktable with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral she had not yet decided to grieve. Her name was Sela, and she had her mother's jaw—square, stubborn, built for wind. "She's been dead three days. The body's still cold."

"The body doesn't matter." Gideon set the femur down on a bed of wool felt and adjusted the lamp so its light fell clean along the bone's length. "The body is just the house the voice lived in. The voice lives here now." A tap of one fingernail against the shaft, and the lamplight seemed to shiver—or perhaps that was only the voice, stirring. "Bone holds what flesh lets go. That's why we carve it. Not to decorate. To *listen*."

Sela's eyes were red-rimmed but dry, the way eyes get when grief has been rationed for too long and there is none left over for ceremony. "The priest said I shouldn't come here. Said it was corpse-mongering. Devil's trade."

"The priest is welcome to his opinion." Gideon drew the first burin from its roll of oiled leather—a fine hook-nosed tool, worn smooth where twenty years of fingers had gripped it. "Did the priest sit with your mother through the last winter? Did he learn how she liked her tea, or which stories she told twice?"

"No."

"Then he doesn't know what we're keeping. And neither do you. Not yet." Gideon looked up, and let their voice soften into the register they used for the newly bereaved—not pity, never pity, but something adjacent to it, something that sounded like patience because it was. "Tell me about her. Start anywhere. Start with the sea, if you like. She was a cartographer, yes? Before Lowmark?"

Something moved behind Sela's face—surprise, then the small surrender of a person who has been carrying a story alone and has just been offered a second pair of arms. "You knew that?"

"I heard it." Gideon tapped the bone again. "But I want to hear it from *you*. What the bone remembers is true, but it's all truth at once, everything she ever saw, layered like silt. You're going to help me find the surface of it. The parts worth carving deep."

* * *

This was the trade, and Gideon had made a quiet living at it for eleven years in the smuggler's warrens beneath Lowmark, in a cellar workshop that smelled of tallow smoke and chalk dust and the faint mineral sweetness of old bone. The town above was a crooked shelf of buildings bolted to the cliffside like barnacles, all of them leaning seaward as if listening for ships that had stopped coming years ago. Smugglers ran the stairs; smugglers ran the harbor; smugglers ran the crown's customs officer, who took his cut in a back room of the Drowned Lantern and called it taxation. Nobody in Lowmark asked where things came from. It was the town's one virtue, and Gideon had chosen it deliberately, the way a rabbit chooses tall grass.

Because the gift did not hide well. Gideon had learned that early.

It had announced itself at nine years old, in the charnel pit behind the plague-house where the bodies went when no one could pay for burial. Gideon had been sent—children were sent, in those days, small hands for small errands—and had knelt among the stacked bones and heard them *singing*. Not music. Worse than music. Every bone a different voice, every voice mid-sentence, mid-scream, mid-prayer, all of them talking at once, all of them unfinished, and nine-year-old Gideon had stood in that pit with their hands over their ears and understood, with the terrible clarity children reserve for the worst truths, that this thing inside them could never be shown to anyone. Not to the neighbors. Not to the hedge-priest. Certainly not to anyone wearing a crown's colors.

So they had learned to be small. Learned to flinch away from graveyards, to walk wide around battlefields, to keep their hands in their pockets near any pile of bones older than a season. Learned, eventually, the opposite lesson too: that the voices were not a curse if you gave them somewhere to go. A voice trapped in an uncarved bone was a scream in a locked room. A voice carved—its story traced into the bone in the old spiral script, line by line, year by year—was a scream given words, and words could rest.

That was the lie most people believed about the gift, the comfortable one: that bone-carvers spoke to the dead. They didn't. They *listened*. The dead had been talking the whole time. All a carver did was hold the door open long enough for somebody living to hear.

Marta Venn had a great deal to say.

By the second hour, the workshop smelled of bone dust and beeswax, and the femur was beginning to fill. Gideon worked the burin in short, sure strokes, each cut a syllable, each curve a breath. The spiral script was older than the crown, older than the church that condemned it—older, some said, than the maps it so often recorded—and it had no letters, exactly. It had *movements*. A downward stroke that meant water. A doubled loop that meant returning. A jagged break in the line that meant the thing no one wanted to say aloud.

And threaded through all of it, faint as a pulse under the burin's point, Marta's voice: narrating, correcting, remembering.

*"Not the north shore,"* she murmured, as Gideon carved the coastline of her life. *"The sound. We came through the sound, my father and I, the year the herring failed. He carried me on his shoulders through the shallows because I'd broken—"*

"—her leg," Sela whispered. She had gone very still, watching the bone the way people watch a fire. "The barrel stave. In the boathouse. Is that—is that really in there?"

"It's all in there." Gideon blew dust from the fresh-cut line. "Everything she ever was. The bone keeps better records than the crown." A pause, and then, because honesty was part of the craft whether the client wanted it or not: "I should tell you what I can't do. I can't make her talk to you. This isn't a séance. When I'm finished, the carving will hold her—her voice, her memory, the shape of her mind as the bone received it. Anyone who learns to read the script can listen. But she won't answer questions. She isn't *there*, exactly. She's a recording made of a person, not a person made of a recording."

Sela absorbed this with a slow nod. "Then what am I paying for?"

"For the difference between losing someone and losing them *twice*." Gideon met her eyes. "In a hundred years, when everyone who knew Marta Venn is gone, some stranger will hold this bone and hear her laugh at her own jokes and complain about the damp and describe the exact color of the sea off the sound in autumn. That's the debt I owe her. That's what you're buying. Memory with a body."

The lamplight guttered. Somewhere above them, muffled by two floors of smuggler's carpentry, the Drowned Lantern roared with its evening crowd—laughter, a fiddle badly played, the crash of a dropped mug followed by cheering. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Sounds from a world that still believed itself permanent.

Gideon kept carving, and tried not to notice how much they loved it here.

* * *

They finished the last spiral near midnight, and Sela wept at last, quietly, holding the finished femur against her chest like an infant while Marta's voice hummed through the carving—faint, contented, describing the herring run of some impossibly distant spring. Gideon wrapped it in oilcloth, taught Sela the three opening strokes that would wake the voice whenever she wished to hear it, and charged her half what the work was worth, because the dead woman had once drawn maps for refugees fleeing a border war, and debts like that didn't expire.

After Sela left, Gideon banked the lamp low and climbed the narrow stair to check the door.

The Drowned Lantern was emptying. Last-call dregs stumbled out into the fog that Lowmark wore like a shawl, and Gideon stood in the tavern's doorway a moment, breathing the night air—brine, tar, woodsmoke, the sweet rot of the tideflats—and telling themselves, as they did every night, that tomorrow would be the same as today. Carve. Listen. Stay small.

Then they saw the notice.

It was nailed to the tavern door at eye height, four bright new nails in wood gone soft with salt-rot, and the paper was white—blindingly white, unweathered, *new*—which was wrong in itself. Nothing arrived in Lowmark without spending weeks in transit, growing soft and gray and deniable. This paper had come fast. This paper had come on purpose.

Gideon read it twice, though once was enough. Once was more than enough.

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN, AND IN THE NAME OF THE KING'S MOST HOLY PURIFICATION:*

*Let it be known throughout the marches that a Purge of Gifts is declared. All practitioners of the forbidden arts of necromancy, bone-speaking, death-whispering, and kindred abominations are hereby named enemies of the realm. Any who harbor, conceal, or aid such persons share their guilt and their sentence.*

*Let all such creatures surrender themselves to the King's witch-hunters for judgment by fire.*

*There will be no appeals. There will be no pardons. The dead belong to God, and the living who traffic with them belong to the flame.*

Below the text, in ink still glossy, a seal Gideon had only seen on tax writs and gallows notices: the crowned hand, closed.

The fog pressed close. The last drunk wove past Gideon without seeing them, humming. From the harbor came the creak of mooring lines, the lap of black water, the ordinary music of a town that did not yet know.

Gideon's hands were steady. That surprised them. Eleven years of staying small, of walking wide around graveyards, of charging desperate families half-price and keeping the cellar lamp turned low so no light showed through the floorboards—and it ended anyway, ended with four nails and a sheet of white paper, and their hands were steady.

*They'll start in the cities,* Gideon thought, with the cold clarity of a mind that had rehearsed this moment for decades without admitting it. *Then the market towns. Then places like Lowmark, last, because Lowmark is beneath everyone's notice.* Weeks, maybe. A month if the roads were bad. Time enough to burn the carvings that couldn't be allowed to fall into hunter's hands—the confession-bones, the evidence-bones, the ones that named names. Time enough to decide what to carry and what to leave. Time enough to say goodbye to a cellar that smelled of chalk and tallow, to a town that had never asked where things came from.

Time enough to become a fugitive, or ash.

Behind them, in the dark of the stairwell, the workshop waited with its roll of burins and its shelves of finished work—forty voices, fifty, each one a person the crown had decided was contraband. Gideon thought of Marta Venn's femur, wrapped in oilcloth, riding home in a grieving daughter's arms, singing about herring.

*The dead belong to God,* the notice said.

"They don't," Gideon said softly, to the fog, to the paper, to the crowned hand with its fist closed tight around nothing. "They belong to whoever listens."

Far down the coast road, where the cliffs dropped toward the borderlands, a light flickered that was not a ship's lantern and not a signal fire—too white, too steady, moving in formation. Torches. Many torches, traveling fast, weeks ahead of any reasonable schedule.

Gideon watched them for a long moment. Then they went inside, and began, quietly, to count the bones.
