## 2. What the Bones Remember

Gideon counted forty-one bones in the chest beneath the floorboards of their room above the Saltcellar Tavern, and knew each one the way a shepherd knows sheep.

Not by name—bones rarely gave up their names first—but by voice. The knucklebone of a drowned fisherman who sang while the water came in. The finger-bone of a pickpocket who had repented so thoroughly that his ghost had nothing left to confess and mostly complained about the weather. The rib of a woman named Sera Voss, no relation, who had died in childbirth and whose only request, repeated for eleven years now whenever Gideon held her, was to know whether the child had grown tall.

*He's taller than you were,* Gideon had told her once, and she had gone quiet in a way that was not silence but peace, and Gideon had not been able to carve anything for three days after.

Forty-one bones. Forty-one voices. And somewhere down the coast road, torches in formation, coming to make it forty-one pyres.

They worked through the night by tallow-light, because there was no time for sleep and no point pretending otherwise. The carving itself took minutes; the choosing took hours. Every relic Gideon had ever made fell somewhere on a scale from harmless to damning, and the crown's new law did not care about the scale. It cared only that bone remembered, and that remembering was now treason.

The fisherman could stay—he sang of tides and herring, nothing more. The pickpocket could stay. Sera Voss, who asked after her son: stay. But the femur of the smuggler captain who had described, in loving detail, every hidden cove between Lowmark and the border—burn. The jawbone of the old harbor-master who had named, name by name, the officials he'd bribed for thirty years—burn. The small carved vertebra of a girl who had died in the plague wagons and whose voice carried, faintly but unmistakably, the accent of Vael, that country that no longer appeared on any map and whose very syllables were now, apparently, a crime—

That one Gideon held longest.

The girl's name had been Ivet. She had been six. Her voice was thin and bright as a wire, and when Gideon turned the vertebra in their fingers they could hear her counting, endlessly, patiently, the way children count when counting is the only thing left to do. *One, two, three, four.* She never reached a number where she stopped. She simply began again, as if the counting itself were the game, as if the game were all she'd been allowed to keep.

"I'm sorry," Gideon whispered to her. "I'm sorry, little one."

She counted on. The dead did not understand apologies. They understood listening, and listening was about to become a hanging offense.

* * *

Word came up the stairs at dawn, in the shape of Odile Marchetti, Gideon's fence and oldest friend, wearing the particular expression of a woman who has already spent the morning being right about something and hates it.

"They're here." She shut the door behind her and stood against it, arms folded, gray-streaked hair still pinned for sleep. "Three boats at the quay before first light. Not soldiers—soldiers you can bribe. These are the other kind."

"How many?"

"Twelve, maybe fourteen. And one more." Odile's jaw tightened. "He came ashore alone, ahead of them. Gray coat, no insignia. Walked straight through the fish market like he owned the fog. Bought an apple. Ate it standing up. Paid too much and told the boy to keep the change." She paused. "Then he asked for the carver."

Gideon's hands went still over the open chest. "By name?"

"By trade. 'The bone-carver of Lowmark.' Which is worse, somehow." Odile crossed the small room and looked down into the chest, at the forty-one lives laid out like a surgeon's tray. Her face did something complicated and then settled on practicality, which was how she survived everything. "How long to burn what needs burning?"

"An hour. Less, if I don't say goodbye."

"Don't." She said it flatly, without cruelty. "You said goodbye to that harbor-master last winter when you finished him. He knows he was heard. That's the whole sacrament, isn't it? That's what you always say." She caught Gideon's look and softened by a degree, which for Odile was a great deal. "Say goodbye on the road. Say it for the rest of your life. Just be alive to do it."

Downstairs, the tavern was doing its morning pantomime of normalcy—fry-smoke and spilled ale and the fishwives arguing prices—and Gideon moved through it carrying a covered basket like a woman bound for market, which was true enough. Out the back, past the reeking gut-line and the coops, to the old lime-kiln in the cliff-shadow where the tavern's refuse went to burn. It was cold this early. Gideon built the fire themselves, small and hot, feeding it driftwood and coal-dust until the stones glowed, and then sat on a salt-crusted bollard with the basket in their lap and began.

The smuggler captain first. His voice rose out of the bone even before the heat touched it—coves and channels, tide-tables recited like prayers—and Gideon listened one last time, all the way through, because Odile was wrong about one thing: the whole sacrament wasn't just being heard. It was being heard *to the end*. Then the flame found the marrow and the voice frayed, thinned, went to smoke, and Gideon felt it go the way you feel a hand slip out of yours—not pain exactly, but the sudden absence of weight.

The harbor-master next. His confessions came faster near the end, tumbling, urgent, thirty years of bought men and buried ledgers, and Gideon held the jawbone over the fire and wept without drama, the way you weep at funerals for people you barely knew but who deserved better than this.

*I was heard,* the dead man seemed to say, in the last syllable before the burning took him. *Let that be enough.*

It would have to be. It had never once been enough.

Ivet last. Always last. Gideon unwrapped the small vertebra from its cloth and let her count, and counted with her under their breath—*one, two, three, four*—and when the moment came they did not hesitate, because hesitation would have been for Gideon's sake and not hers, and she deserved better than a coward's mercy. The bone whitened. The counting climbed higher and thinner, a note held past endurance, and then—

Silence. Real silence, the kind Gideon had not known since childhood, pressing in like deep water. Somewhere beyond the kiln a gull screamed, and waves worked the shingle, and none of it spoke.

"There," Gideon said to the ashes, hoarse. "Now you're free of us. Now nobody can use you."

The lie tasted like lime and char. Nobody was freed by fire. The dead were silenced twice—once by dying, once by the living's fear—and the second silencing was the only one anyone chose.

* * *

Inquisitor Halvard Crane found them at the kiln.

Gideon heard him before seeing him—or rather, heard *around* him, the way you hear around a missing tooth. The dead of Lowmark murmured constantly, a low tide of gossip and grief, but in the lane behind the kiln there opened a pocket of perfect quiet, moving, deliberate, shaped like a man. Gideon had felt that kind of silence only twice before: once around a dying priest, and once around their own mother, in the last hour before she walked out of their life and down the coast road and out of every story Gideon could carve.

The man who rounded the kiln-wall wore a gray traveling coat, unadorned, and carried no visible weapon. He was perhaps fifty, lean, with a scholar's stoop and a soldier's eyes, and he stopped a respectful ten paces away and inclined his head like a neighbor greeting a gardener.

"You're burning something," he said.

"The tavern's refuse."

"Mm." He glanced at the kiln, at the glow, at the basket on Gideon's lap with its cloth pulled back, and Gideon saw him see the empty wrappings, the ash-flecked linen, and arrive at the truth without hurry. "The harbor-master had a great deal to say, I imagine. They usually do, the ones who lived long. All those names." He said it without accusation, almost wistfully. "You'll forgive me. I've been listening to him for two days. Through the walls of the Saltcellar, where you kept him. He repeats himself terribly."

The bottom dropped out of Gideon's stomach, slow and total, like a boat going down.

Because that was impossible. That was the one thing that was supposed to be impossible. The gift passed blood to blood, rare as a blue moon, and every gifted soul Gideon had ever met had guarded the secret the way drowning sailors guard air—because a gift shared was a gift hunted. No one else could hear what the bones said. No one. It was the single wall between Gideon and the pyre, and this gray man had just strolled through it talking about tide-tables.

"You're one of us," Gideon said. The words came out stripped of everything—no fear, no flattery, just the naked fact.

Crane's expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts before it cracks. "One of you," he repeated. He reached into his coat and drew out an object on a chain: a pendant of dark metal, teardrop-shaped, older than any kingdom Gideon knew. As it swung free, the pocket of silence around him *opened*, and the dead of Lowmark flooded back in—the fishwives' grandmothers, the drowned, the plague-wagons, the whole murmuring tide—and threaded through it, unmistakable, a second current. Voices Gideon recognized. Bones Gideon had carved, miles away, locked in a chest upstairs. The harbor-master, repeating himself. The pickpocket, complaining about weather.

The pendant drank them and poured them into Crane, and Crane listened the way Gideon listened—with his whole body, with hunger—and Gideon understood, with a horror that was almost reverence, that the Inquisition had done the unthinkable. They had taken a gifted child and made the gift a leash.

"It's called a listener's tear," Crane said quietly. "There are seven in the world. I carry one because I earned it, and I earned it because I was born able to hear what you hear, and I spent twenty years wishing I couldn't." He closed his fist around the pendant and the voices cut off, and the silence snapped back around him like a door. "Do you know what the dead want, carver? Truly? You sit in your tavern and call it sacrament, communion, mercy. But I've listened longer than you've been alive, and I will tell you what they want. They want *out*. They want to stop. Every voice in every bone is a soul held at the lip of rest, called back, again and again, by people like us. We don't honor them. We *haunt* them."

"That's not—" Gideon started, and found they had no ending for the sentence, because Crane's face held no madness. It held exhaustion. Twenty years of it. The faces of the condemned always looked certain; Crane looked like a man carrying something heavy up a long stair, who had stopped asking whether the stair ended.

"Ivet," Crane said.

The name landed like a knife between ribs.

"A girl of six, plague-wagon, Vael-born. You carved her vertebra and kept her counting. One, two, three, four." His voice gentled, which was the cruelest thing about it. "I heard her through your walls last night. Do you know what I heard, underneath the counting? Underneath, carver. Where you don't listen, because you love her and love makes us deaf. She isn't playing a game. She's *begging*. She counts because counting is the last prayer she remembers, and she has been saying it for eleven years because no one will let her finish." He stepped closer, and the fog curled between them. "You burned her this morning. Was it grief? Or was it relief?"

Gideon's hands were shaking. The basket trembled on their knees. Because the terrible thing—the unforgivable, unspeakable thing—was that in the moment the flame took Ivet's voice, some small starving part of Gideon had exhaled. Eleven years of a dead child's endless counting, and the silence afterward had felt, for one black heartbeat, like grace.

"Yes," Gideon said, and hated themselves with their whole chest. "Both. It's always both. That's what the gift *is*. If you've got it, you know that. So tell me how burning her served her, Inquisitor. Tell me how the pyre is mercy, when she never once asked for it."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind Crane's eyes, some sediment of doubt stirred up from the bottom. Then he mastered it, the way a man masters a limp.

"The Purge begins today," he said. "Lowmark first, then Thornfell, then everywhere the gift hides. There are lists. Yours is short, which is why I came myself instead of sending soldiers." He reached into his coat again, and Gideon flinched—but what he produced was paper. A folded sheet, sealed with plain wax, no crown upon it. "Every gifted soul on my list gets one hour's warning and one choice: surrender the relics and the gift's use, or run. I am required to offer neither. I am offering both. Consider it the indulgence of a tired man."

"Why?" Gideon's voice cracked on it. "Why warn me? Why any of it?"

Crane looked at the kiln, at the cooling ash, and for an instant he seemed very old, and very far away, as though answering someone who wasn't there.

"Because my sister was on a list once," he said, "and no one warned her." He set the paper on the bollard beside Gideon, turned, and walked back into the fog, and the pocket of silence went with him, and the dead of Lowmark rushed in around the space he'd left like water filling a footprint.

* * *

Gideon packed in twenty minutes and had been packing for eleven years, so the twenty minutes were mostly performance. Clothes, knife, flint, the false-bottomed satchel. Coins sewn into a belt. And bones—only nine now, the harmless ones, the singers and complainers, wrapped in wool and oilcloth. Sera Voss went in last, and Gideon held her a moment at the window.

"He's tall," they told her. "Taller than me. He laughs at bad jokes and he doesn't know you existed, and I'm sorry for that, and I'm taking you somewhere they'll never find us."

Sera Voss said nothing, because the dead never answered questions, but her silence had a texture, and the texture was trust, and Gideon folded her into the satchel like a letter they meant to deliver someday.

Odile met them in the yard with a mule and a face like a slammed door. "North road's watched. South road's watched. That leaves the mountain track through the Serrated Pass, which is watched by nobody because it's watched by *worse* than nobody—rockfall, wolves, and the ghosts of every fool who tried it in winter." She pressed the lead-rope into Gideon's hands and held on a beat too long. "There's a waystation at the pass foot, abandoned since the toll-roads failed. Dry roof. You can reach it by dark if the fog holds."

"Come with me."

"No." She said it fast, before the word could grow roots. "If I vanish the same night as you, Crane reads it as confirmation. Someone has to stay and be boring and innocent and swear up and down that the carver owed them money and skipped town." Her grip tightened. "Besides. Someone has to feed your cat."

"I don't have a cat."

"You do now. Orange. Disgusting temperament. He arrived Tuesday and appointed himself." She let go of the rope, and for one unguarded moment her practicality slipped and Gideon saw underneath it, all the way down: thirty years of buying and selling and keeping secrets together, and this might be the last time either of them stood in the same yard. "Go," Odile said roughly. "And Gideon—if the list reaches me, and it will—I was never your friend. Remember that. It'll hurt less if you believe it."

"It won't."

"No," she agreed. "But say it anyway."

Gideon said it. Then they led the mule out through the fog-thin streets of Lowmark, past the notice on the tavern door with its crowned fist, past the fish-market where a gray-coated man had eaten an apple and paid too much, and took the cliff path north as the sun came up the color of pewter.

Behind them, faint on the wind, they could hear the dead of Lowmark murmuring on—unburned, unlistened-to, waiting—and ahead the mountains stood up out of the mist like the teeth of something patient.

At the first bend in the road, Gideon looked back once. On the quay below, soldiers in crown gray were nailing fresh paper to the customs house, and beside them stood a lean figure in a gray coat, head bowed, fist closed around something at his throat—as if listening.

As if hearing, even now, the bones in Gideon's satchel, singing softly north.

Gideon turned away and walked, and did not look back again until the road bent into the hills—and there, on the high track where Odile had sworn no one would be, a horseman sat motionless against the sky, watching them come.
