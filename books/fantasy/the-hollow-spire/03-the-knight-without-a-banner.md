## 3. The Knight Without a Banner

Ser Maren Kestrel had learned to count her ghosts the way other soldiers counted their wounds: privately, and never out loud.

There were eleven of them now. Eleven voices that lived in the hollow behind her sternum, in the scar tissue of a life she had ruined with one order not given. The loudest was Commander Aldric Vane, who had died at Vael's gates forty years ago wearing her father's crest, and who had not stopped talking since.

*You're sitting too far from the fire,* he said that night, from his usual place—nowhere and everywhere, a voice like wind through a rusted helm. *You'll catch your death, girl.*

"I'm not a girl," Maren muttered to the empty ridgeline. "I'm older than you were when you died."

*That's no excuse for poor campcraft.* A pause, the particular quality of silence that meant Aldric was settling in, making himself comfortable in her skull the way he'd once made himself comfortable at every officer's fire. *And you're brooding again. I can hear it. It sounds like grinding stone.*

"I'm keeping watch."

*You're brooding while facing the general direction of watchfulness. There's a difference. I taught it to you myself.*

She almost smiled. That was the trap of the dead—they knew exactly which of your doors were unlatched. She banked the fire lower instead, feeding it the last dry scrub from the scree slope, and let the mountains of Thornfell close around her like a fist. Below her, somewhere in the black folds of the passes, the smuggler roads wound toward Lowmark and Gravenhollow and all the small dishonest places where a woman without a banner could buy bread without answering questions.

Above her, the stars. The same stars that had hung over Vael on the last night, though she hadn't known it would be the last night. Nobody had known. That was the obscenity of it—the kingdom hadn't ended in thunder. It had ended in paperwork, in sealed orders, in a column of soldiers told to hold position while behind them a country was made to stop existing. And she, twenty-two years old and obedient as a hound, had held position.

*You did what you were ordered to do,* said the ghost who was kindest about it.

*I did what I was told instead of what was right,* she answered, because she was the only one allowed to say so. Even Aldric didn't argue anymore. He'd stopped arguing around year ten. That was its own kind of verdict.

She slept in snatches, sword across her knees, and dreamed of white walls under white fire, and woke before dawn with frost in her hair and the certainty—old companion, older than grief—that today someone was going to die on this road.

She just didn't know yet whether it would be her.

* * *

The mountain road below Thornfell's shoulder was called the Shepherd's Stair, a track cut into the cliff face where switchbacks descended through pine shadow into the river valley. In summer it carried goats, smugglers, and pilgrims avoiding the toll bridges. In autumn it carried nothing but wind and the smell of coming snow.

Maren had taken the high path above it, the poacher's trail she'd mapped over three years of hiding, precisely because it overlooked the Stair. From there she could see anyone using the low road long before they saw her. It was habit now. Three years of being hunted by her own order taught habits deeper than prayer.

So she saw them first.

A crown patrol—six men, royal livery under grey travel cloaks, moving wrong. Soldiers walked patrols in loose order, joking, watching the treeline in rotation. These six moved like a hand inside a glove: tight, silent, spaced for mutual cover. Two carried crossbows already spanned. One carried something else—a long iron brazier on chains, unlit, swinging gently against his back.

A burning cage. Small enough for a person.

Maren went cold in a way the morning hadn't made her. Witch-hunters. Not the village sort with pitchforks and borrowed courage—crown hunters, Inquisition-trained, working the passes. Someone had told them the roads. Someone always told them; fear was the cheapest currency in Thornfell and everyone spent it eventually.

They were converging on a bend in the Stair where the cliff narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for two. And on that ledge, alone, walking with the careful economy of someone used to being small, came a figure in a patched brown coat.

The figure paused at the bend. Looked down. Looked back up the way they'd come—and Maren watched, from two hundred feet above, the exact moment the walker understood the geometry of the trap: hunters ahead, cliff behind, nowhere left but down.

Then the walker did something strange. They reached beneath their coat and drew out something pale, held it briefly against their lips, and Maren—too far to see what it was—saw only the gesture. The gesture of someone kissing a relic. Or listening to one.

*Ah,* said Aldric, in a voice she hadn't heard from him in years. Sharp. Alarmed. *Maren. That one's like you.*

"Like me," she breathed. "What do you mean, like—"

*You know what I mean. The hearing. The dead talk to that one too—I felt it from here like a bell rung underwater. Get down there, girl. Get down there NOW.*

She was already moving. Later she would examine why—why she broke three years of discipline in a single heartbeat, why she abandoned the high ground and cover and caution for a stranger on a ledge. At the time there was no later. There was only Aldric's voice cracking across her mind like a whip, and the memory it dragged with it: another bend in another road, forty years ago, where she had waited for orders while people died.

Not twice. Whatever else she was, whatever she'd become—not twice.

* * *

Gideon Sandoval heard the dead woman say *behind you* four seconds before the crossbow bolt buried itself in the rock beside their head.

The bone-carver dropped flat on the ledge, arms over head, heart slamming. Stone chips stung their cheek. Above, a hunter's voice—flat, procedural, terrible in its calm: *"Marked. Loose again."*

Gideon crawled. The ledge offered no cover worth the name, just a lean of rock and a stunted pine clinging to the cliff like a beggar to a gatepost. Behind, the way back was cut off—two hunters had appeared there as if conjured, crossbows levelled, and between them the man with the brazier-cage smiled the smile of someone doing arithmetic with other people's lives.

"Gideon Sandoval," the man called. He had an Inquisitor's collar hidden under a plain cloak; Gideon could see the silver pin. "Bone-carver of Lowmark. The crown thanks you for making this easy. Come quietly and you'll burn clean, in the proper way, with the proper rites."

*He means it,* whispered the dead woman whose femur lay against Gideon's ribs, wrapped in oilcloth. Her name had been Sorrel; she'd been a hedge-healer in the valley, drowned in the millrace forty years past, and her bones had passed hand to hand until they reached Gideon's bench. She was chatty for the drowned. *He believes every word. That's worse than a liar, love. Liars can be bargained with.*

"I know," Gideon whispered back, and hated how their voice shook. Four years they had survived by staying small—small work, small prices, carvings sold as trinkets, the gift disguised as superstition. Small. Invisible. And here was the whole machinery of the crown's new terror descending on them anyway, because Odile's ledger or the tavern-keeper's fear or some neighbor's envy had finally outweighed Gideon's careful nothing.

Another bolt. The pine took it, needles raining down. Gideon pressed into the rock and listened—for once, desperately—to the dead. The ledge itself had no bones in it, but the cliff did. Old bones, deep ones, a goatherd fallen in some winter generations back, still dreaming his slow dream inside the mountain. His voice was faint, waterlogged by decades:

*...cold... the path, the path goes cold but there's a way, up and over, there's a way...*

"There's always a way," Gideon gasped, "but I can't reach it if—"

If the hunters reached them first. Which they were doing. Boots on stone, measured, unhurried. The Inquisitor's smile widened.

And then the world grew wings.

It happened fast, the way real violence always does—faster than story, all blur and impact. A shape dropped from the cliffs above the far pair of hunters, and it was not graceful; it was a landslide wearing a cloak, all momentum and fury, a tall figure in battered half-plate with a sword that moved like it remembered every drill ever drilled. The first hunter folded around her pommel. The second got his crossbow up and got off a shot that sparked off her pauldron before she took his wrist apart with a short, economical stroke and put him down the cliff-face with a shove that was almost gentle.

"LEFT!" roared a voice that wasn't hers—it came from nowhere, from everywhere, a dead man's parade-ground bark—and Gideon flung themselves sideways on pure instinct as the Inquisitor's third hunter loosed from ten feet away.

The bolt meant for Gideon's spine hit the newcomer's thigh instead.

She staggered. Did not fall. Turned, shieldless, sword low, and faced the remaining hunters with blood sheeting down her greave, and Gideon—who had spent four years learning every flavor of fear—saw something worse than fear on her face. Recognition. She looked at the Inquisitor's collar-pin the way a drowning woman looks at shore.

"Halvard Crane's hounds," she said. Her voice was rough gravel, northern vowels. "Where is he? Is the butcher of Graywater here himself?"

"You're Ser Maren Kestrel." The Inquisitor had stopped smiling. His men were down or dying and he hadn't drawn; he stood very still, and his eyes—pale, lidless, patient—moved from the knight to Gideon and back. "Deserter of the Vael garrison. We wondered where you'd gone to rot." He raised one hand, and his hunters—those still breathing—fell back up the trail. "This isn't the day for either of you. But it's marked. Everything's marked now."

He withdrew. Just like that—up the switchbacks, unhurried, collecting his wounded with the terrible tidiness of a man who has all the time in the world because the world is already his.

Gideon stayed flat on the stones, shaking, and watched the stranger knight kneel in her own blood, probing her thigh with two fingers, swearing with the fluency of a veteran.

"You're hit," Gideon said stupidly.

"I'm aware." She tore a strip from her cloak, bound the wound with quick brutal competence, and then looked up, and Gideon saw her face properly for the first time: mid-forties, weathered, a scar through one eyebrow, eyes grey as slate and haunted as a shuttered house. "You're the carver. Sandoval. The bones speak to you."

It wasn't a question, and lying seemed suddenly obscene, kneeling in the aftermath of someone else's blood. "Yes."

"And you hear them right now. On this ledge." Her jaw worked. "Tell me what they're saying. Tell me—" and here her voice cracked, just slightly, a fault-line in stone, "—tell me if there's a soldier among them. Big man. Northern accent. Says *girl* like it's a title."

The dead woman, Sorrel, murmured: *Ask her her name, love. Ask her who taught her to fight. He won't stop talking about it.*

"He says," Gideon began, and stopped, and started again, because some sentences are doorways you can't step through halfway. "He says you parry like a drunkard and die like a fool if you don't keep your elbow down. He says—he says his name was Aldric. Commander Aldric Vane. He says you owe him thirty years of reports."

Ser Maren Kestrel sat down on the mountain road as if her strings had been cut.

* * *

They built a fire in a hollow off the poacher's trail, well after dark, because neither of them could bear to move farther and both of them needed warmth more than safety. Maren cleaned her wound. Gideon held their hands around a tin cup of nettle tea and watched the knight the way you watch a fire that might spread.

For a long time neither spoke. The pines hissed. Somewhere below, a night bird cried once and gave up.

"Forty years," Maren said finally. "Forty years he's talked to me. Since the day he died. Since the day I stood at those gates and did nothing." She turned the cup in her hands. "I thought I was mad. For years I thought the guilt had cracked something. I confessed it, once, to a chaplain. He prescribed fasting and silence. The silence made it louder." A harsh breath that was almost a laugh. "Then the Purge started, and I understood. All those poor bastards burning in the villages—they hear it too. All of them. It was never madness. It was never even rare. It's just that most people are sensible enough to keep their mouths shut, and I was never sensible."

"The dead don't lie," Gideon said quietly. "That's the first thing you learn. Bones can't lie. They only remember."

"Then he remembers me at twenty-two, standing in the mud, waiting for orders that never came." Maren's eyes were fixed on the flames. "And he remembers what was behind the gates. And he talks about it. Every night. Forty years of it."

"What does he say?"

Maren was quiet long enough that Gideon thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "He says *we have to tell someone.*" Her voice frayed. "Every night, for forty years, he says we have to tell someone what the crown did at Vael, and every morning I wake up and I'm still a coward, and the kingdom is still erased, and there's no one left to tell." She looked up, and her slate eyes found Gideon's across the fire, and there was something naked in them, something that had been alone so long it had forgotten how to be seen. "Until now. Until the dead sent me a bone-carver on a mountain road. What am I supposed to do with that, Sandoval? What kind of God arranges that?"

"No kind I've met," Gideon said. "The dead aren't pious. They're just persistent." They pulled the oilcloth bundle from their coat, unwrapped it a hand's width: the long pale curve of Sorrel's femur, fine characters carved along its length in lines thin as spider silk. "I write them down. Their voices. Their stories. When I carve a life into bone, the voice stays clear—anyone holding it can hear, a little. Anyone gifted, fully." Their thumb traced the carved script. "The witch-hunters call it necromancy. It isn't. It's the opposite. It's making sure the dead get to finish their sentences."

Maren stared at the bone. Then at Gideon. Then she laughed—one short, disbelieving bark, quickly smothered.

"A scribe for ghosts," she said. "A historian with a knife." She shook her head slowly. "I deserted my post while a kingdom burned. You carve its dead so they can't be silenced. And Crane wants us both ash." She looked into the fire, and when she spoke again the parade-ground hardness had returned, but underneath it ran something new, something almost shy. "Come with me. North, through the high passes, away from the low roads. Crane will sweep the valleys. He'll miss the roof of the world."

"I survive by staying small."

"You survived by staying invisible," Maren corrected, "and that's over. He marked you on that ledge. There's no small left for you, carver. There's only together or alone, and alone means he finds you in some cellar in Gravenhollow with a burning cage on his back." She held Gideon's gaze. "I've spent three years hiding from my own ghost. I'd rather spend what's left carrying her with company."

*Her?* Gideon thought, and then understood: she'd said *her* about the ghost, about the haunting itself, the way you'd speak of a wife or a wound.

*Aye,* rumbled Aldric, so loudly that Gideon actually flinched, glancing at Maren to see if she'd heard—she had; her eyes had widened. *Tell her yes, carver. Tell her I'll be quiet. I make no promises, but I'll try.*

"He says he'll try to be quiet," Gideon translated.

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and she pretended they weren't, and both of them let her pretend. "Forty years," she said hoarsely, "and the first mercy anyone's shown that man is a stranger with a knife and a scrap of bone." She stood, wincing at her thigh, and shouldered her pack. "We move at first light. There's an old waystation half a day north, in the pines above the frozen falls—smugglers use it in spring. If we're lucky it'll be empty."

They slept in shifts, back to back, two guarded creatures allowing themselves one shared wall of warmth. And in the hour before dawn, Gideon—drifting at the edge of sleep—heard Sorrel murmur one last thing before the fire settled to coals:

*The waystation isn't empty, love. Something small is sleeping there. Something that smells of smoke that never cools.*

Gideon opened their eyes to the grey beginning of morning, and said nothing, and watched the north.
