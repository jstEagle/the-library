## 5. Smoke Over Thornfell

Maren Kestrel had learned to read smoke the way other soldiers read banners.

It was a skill from her first posting, back when she'd still believed the crown's wars were clean. Smoke told you what burned and how long it had burned and whether anyone had bothered to fight. Woodsmoke rose in fat grey columns, lazy, domestic. Oil burned black and fast, all appetite and no patience. And witch-smoke—she'd learned this only in the last two years, learned it the way you learn a wound—witch-smoke was thin and sweet and wrong, because of what fed it.

The smoke over Gravenhollow was sweet.

She reined the mule to a halt on the ridge above the smuggler city and let the wind push against her face, carrying the smell down into her lungs: char, yes, but under it something like burnt sugar, like honey left too long over a flame. Her hand went to her sword hilt without her permission. Old habits. The sword was a farmer's blade now, bought off a dead man in Lowmark, its edge nicked, its balance wrong for her arm. It would have to do.

"Is that it?" Pip stood in the cart bed behind her, one small hand fisted in the sacking that covered their supplies, the other closed around the ember-stone at their throat. The child's eyes were fixed on the city below with an expression Maren couldn't name—not fear exactly, not wonder. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone seeing a place they'd already grieved.

"That's it," Maren said. "Gravenhollow."

"It's burning."

"Part of it."

Pip didn't answer. Below them, the city crouched in its hollow like something wounded trying to make itself small—a sprawl of slate roofs and rope bridges and stair-cut streets spilling down into a river gorge, half of it veined with the smoke Maren had smelled from three miles out. The eastern quarter, where the warehouses crowded the water, was black. She could see the skeletons of two piers standing in the river like burnt ribs. Above them, on the cliff road, hung a banner she knew too well: the crowned eye of the Inquisition, white on black, snapping in the wind.

"They got here first," Gideon said quietly.

Maren glanced back. Gideon sat at the front of the cart with the reins loose in their hands, watching the smoke with the flat stillness Maren had come to recognize as the bone-carver's armor. They wore grief like a coat worn inside out—all the seams showing, none of the color.

"How long?" Maren asked.

Gideon tilted their head, listening to something Maren couldn't hear—the wind, maybe, or the dead in the cart's cargo, the few carvings they hadn't dared burn. "Two days. Maybe three. The smoke's old in the east quarter. Newer near the temple steps." A pause. "There are bodies under the new smoke."

Of course there were. Maren looked away, at the banner, at the crowned eye staring down the gorge. *We see you,* the eye said. *We see everything.*

"We could go around," she said, though she hated herself for saying it. "South road, cross at the ferry, add four days."

"And walk into whatever checkpoint they've built at the crossing." Gideon shook their head. "No. Gravenhollow is still the way through. Half of it burns; the other half still runs. Smugglers don't stop running because the crown comes calling—they just raise their prices." Their mouth twisted. "I know these people. I owe money to some of them. That used to count for something here."

*Used to.* Maren turned the mule forward again. The words sat in her chest like a stone swallowed wrong.

They descended through pine shadow into the gorge, and the smell grew worse, sweeter, until Pip pulled their collar up over their nose and kept it there. The dead, Maren thought. It wasn't woodsmoke at all, not mostly. Somewhere in that eastern quarter, the hunters had found a cache of relics—or a family of the gifted—and had made an example. She had seen such examples before, in another life, wearing another coat. You stacked the evidence in a warehouse and you burned it and you let the smoke stand over the city for days so everyone understood: *this is what remembering costs now.*

Her commander's voice stirred at the back of her skull, the way it always did when she came near the things she'd done. *You knew what the fires were for, Kestrel. You stacked the wood yourself once, and called it order.*

"I know," she murmured, and Pip looked up at her, and she said nothing more.

* * *

Gravenhollow's western gate was a rusted portcullis nobody had raised in years; you entered the city by wading a culvert that stank of tannery waste, and Maren had done it twice before, in better years, with better company. Now she went first, sword drawn, while Gideon handed Pip down into the dark and followed with the sacking bundle held high. The culvert mouth opened onto Fishgut Alley, and Fishgut Alley opened onto the Long Stair, and the Long Stair climbed the gorge wall in switchbacks past doorways cut straight into living rock—smuggler architecture, a city built by people who expected to be hunted and dug accordingly.

Half those doorways were shuttered. Half the shutters were scorched.

"Odile's house is on Cordwainer Row," Gideon said, low. "Third landing up. If anyone can move us out quiet—river barge, false manifests, papers that say we're wool merchants—it's her."

"You trust her?"

The question hung between them. Gideon's hands stilled on the bundle.

"I trusted her for eleven years," they said finally. "She fenced my first carving when I was nineteen and stupid. She never sold me, not once, not even when the price was high enough to buy this whole row." A breath. "But I watched her weigh things, Maren. All these years. She weighs everything. And lately the scale's been tipping."

Maren said nothing. There was nothing honest to say. She had weighed things herself, once, on a night in Vael's border country when weighing meant walking away, and she had lived with the arithmetic ever since. Trust was a debt like any other. Sometimes the debtor paid. Sometimes the debtor sold you to cover their own accounts.

They climbed. The city was quieter than Maren remembered—too quiet, the particular silence of places where people had learned not to look out their windows. A woman selling eels wouldn't meet their eyes. Two men playing knucklebones in a doorway played without speaking, without laughing, their eyes flicking up at every footstep on the stair. On the third landing, someone had nailed a fresh notice to the rock beside a shuttered shrine:

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN INQUISITION: THE HARBORING OF GIFTED PERSONS IS TREASON. THE HARBORING OF RELICS IS TREASON. INFORMERS WILL BE PAID. SILENCE WILL BE PUNISHED AS COMPLICITY.*

Below the words, in different ink, someone had scrawled: *they took the Tallowmans. all six. even the babe.*

Pip read it. Maren saw the child read it, lips moving, and saw the small hand tighten around the ember-stone until the knuckles went pale. She put her own hand on Pip's shoulder—briefly, awkwardly; she was still learning how to touch people who weren't enemies—and steered them onward before the child could ask what happened to the Tallowmans.

Cordwainer Row smelled of old leather and lamp oil. Odile's house was the one with the green door, and the green door was locked, and when Gideon knocked—three quick, two slow, the old rhythm—there was a long silence, long enough for Maren's hand to find her sword again, before the sound of bolts came, one after another after another, five of them, like a small drumroll of fear.

The door opened a hand's width. One eye, dark and quick, in a face Maren remembered as handsome and now saw as merely careful.

"Gideon Sandoval," Odile said. "Dead, last I heard."

"Rumors of my death keep me affordable." Gideon shouldered gently past her into the dim interior, and Maren followed, and Pip slipped in last, and Odile shot the five bolts home behind them like a woman closing a coffin.

Inside, the house was Odile entire: low-ceilinged, crammed, every surface holding something valuable disguised as something worthless. Candlesticks that were really lockpicks. A lute with documents glued beneath the rosin. Maren stood by the door with her back to the wall, the way she'd stood watch in a hundred rooms, and watched Odile take Gideon's measure across the lamplight.

"The east quarter," Gideon said. "Was it the Tallowmans?"

"The Tallowmans." Odile poured three cups of wine without asking, then hesitated, and poured a fourth, smaller, and pushed it toward Pip without meeting the child's eyes. "Relic-runners, whole family. The grandmother had the gift—could hear her dead husband in his wedding ring, forty years after he drowned. Sweet thing. Harmless." She drank. "Inquisitor Crane found the ring on a corpse he pulled from the river. Traced it back in a day. He's efficient, your Inquisitor Crane. He doesn't torture first, even. He just asks questions in that soft voice, and people answer, because he seems so reasonable, and then the fires start."

Maren's stomach tightened. "Crane. Halvard Crane. He's here himself? Not a proxy?"

"He's here. Lodging at the Saltcellar, with eight witch-hunters and a writ that lets him do anything short of taxing the nobility." Odile set down her cup. "And now you're going to tell me why you've walked into his city, Gideon, so I know how much trouble to be afraid of."

Gideon told her. Most of it. Maren watched the fence listen—the ember-stone, the dragon, the Door, all the impossible freight of it—and watched Odile's face do what faces did when they heard impossibilities: nothing, then everything. When Gideon finished, Odile was quiet a long moment, turning her cup.

"A door out," she said at last. "Out of the crown's reach entirely."

"That's the hope."

"And you want passage. Papers. Quiet."

"I want what I've always wanted from you, Odile. The best work in Thornfell, and my silence about how you get it."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes—something old and tired and fond, and underneath it, Maren thought, something frightened. Frightened people weighed things. Frightened people tipped scales.

"Give me tonight," Odile said. "There's a barge leaving tomorrow, grain run downriver, the master owes me. I'll put you in the hold with the wool bales. But you don't stay here—" she caught Gideon's look and raised a hand, "—no arguments. There's a cellar under the tannery on Rope Walk, disused since the fire. I'll move you there within the hour. If Crane's people knock on my door, they'll find nothing but an old fence and her ledgers."

It sounded reasonable. It sounded safe. Maren stood by the wall and listened to the plan assemble itself and felt the cold weight of her commander's voice settle against the back of her teeth, saying nothing at all, which was somehow worse than anything it could have said.

* * *

The tannery cellar was dry, at least, and dark, and smelled of old lime instead of old blood. Maren took first watch because she always took first watch, sitting on an overturned vat with her sword across her knees, listening to the city breathe above them. Gideon slept fitfully against the far wall, one hand curled around their satchel of carvings. Pip slept curled against Gideon's side like a burr, the ember-stone rising and falling with each small breath, giving off its faint heat that Maren could feel from six feet away, like standing near a banked oven.

Somewhere past midnight, she heard footsteps overhead.

Not many. Four sets, maybe five, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the ground they walked on. Lantern light seeped down through the floorboard gaps in long yellow lines, sweeping, pausing, sweeping.

Maren rose without a sound and crossed to Gideon and put her hand over their mouth, and Gideon's eyes opened already wide awake—bone-carvers, she was learning, slept the way soldiers did, ready to be killed at any moment.

*Soldiers,* said her commander's voice, bitterly. *You'd know.*

The footsteps stopped directly above them. A voice, muffled, asked a question. Another voice answered—soft, reasonable, almost gentle—and Maren's blood went to ice, because she knew that cadence. Every soldier who'd served under the Inquisition knew it. Halvard Crane asked questions the way priests gave absolution, and people confessed to him the way penitents did, gratefully, relieved to finally tell someone.

Then the light moved away, and the footsteps moved away, and Maren allowed herself one breath.

One breath was all she got.

Above them, faint but clear, carried down through lime and stone and floorboards, Odile's voice said: "Yes. Yes, that's them. The bone-carver and the knight and the child. The cellar under the tannery on Rope Walk. That's where I put them."

A pause. Then Crane's soft voice, warm as a hand held over a candle: "And the ember-stone?"

"The child carries it. Never takes it off."

Another pause, longer. When Crane spoke again, there was something in his voice Maren hadn't expected—not triumph, not hunger. Something almost like sorrow, if sorrow could be filed and catalogued.

"Thank you, Mistress Odile. Your amnesty will be honored. Wait here, please. My men will need you to show us the door."

Maren's hand found Gideon's shoulder in the dark and squeezed once. Gideon was already moving, already gathering the satchel, already waking Pip with fingers gentle as a thief's. No time for anger. Anger was a luxury for people with exits.

But as they slipped out through the tannery's drainage tunnel, into the cold and the dark and the reek of the river, Maren looked back once—at the lamplight blooming in the doorway above, at the silhouette of an old woman standing in it, waiting to show the hunters the way—and she thought about trust, and debts, and the exact weight of a human soul on a scale.

Eleven years, Gideon had said. Eleven years, and it hadn't been enough. Nothing underground was enough anymore. The crown had made the whole world a ledger, and every name in it was for sale, and the only question left—the question Maren carried with her into the dark, her hand on Pip's shoulder, the river sliding cold around their ankles—was not *who would betray you.*

It was *how soon, and for how little.*

Behind them, faint through the stone, she heard the first boots descend into the empty cellar, and Crane's voice, soft and reasonable, saying: "Search it anyway. People like this always leave something behind. They always leave something."

And in Maren's pocket, wrapped in cloth, lay the thing they had left behind: a sliver of carved bone, dropped in the rush of escape, holding the voice of a dead woman—and, pressed into its grain, the shape of the road they had taken south.

Toward the mountains. Toward Ashfall.

Toward the Spire.
