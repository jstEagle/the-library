## 6. The Ember That Would Not Die

Pip counted the things they owned the way other children counted sheep.

One knife, too big for their hand. One blanket that smelled of Maren's horse and Maren's sweat and, underneath both, faintly of the lavender oil she used on her sword-belt because a knight was a knight even with her name struck from the rolls. One map — Pip's own, drawn in charcoal on the back of a grain-sack, showing the coastline of a country that did not exist. And one stone.

The stone sat in the pouch against Pip's chest, where it had sat for as long as Pip could remember, which was longer than it should have been possible to remember anything. It was warm. It had always been warm. When Pip pressed it flat under their palm at night, in the dark of whatever barn or culvert or stolen room they'd found, the warmth went through the skin and into the meat of them, and something in the warmth remembered.

That was the word Pip used, privately, without telling anyone. *Remembered.* Not like a coal remembers fire, which is just heat sitting in a thing until it leaks away. This was different. This was the way a song remembers its singer. The stone didn't just hold warmth; it held *attention*, as if somewhere far inside it, something was looking out.

Pip had never told Gideon about the looking-out part.

They told themselves it was because there hadn't been time. There had been so little time since Gravenhollow — since Odile's face going smooth and sorry in the lamplight, since Maren hauling them both out the back of the burning fence-house while the witch-hunters' horns sang down the smuggler streets. But that wasn't the whole truth, and Pip knew it, because Pip was small but not stupid, and being small taught you early that some truths are too heavy to hand to people who are already carrying everything they can carry.

Gideon carried voices. Pip could see it now, having learned to look: the way Gideon's head tilted when the wind moved through dead grass, the way their fingers twitched sometimes as though tracing letters on air. Gideon heard the dead everywhere, all the time, an ocean of them, and never once complained about drowning.

The stone only ever spoke to Pip. And Pip was afraid — this was the truth, the heavy one — that if Gideon heard it too, Gideon would know what it was, and knowing what it was would make it something that could be taken away. Everything Pip had ever loved had been taken away once already. A country. A mother whose face lived behind Pip's eyes like a lamp behind fogged glass. A language that came out of Pip's mouth sometimes in dreams, syllables soft as river-stones, words nobody else alive knew.

So Pip kept the stone quiet, and kept it close, and ran.

* * *

They ran south and up, because Maren said the mountains were the one place Crane's writ grew thin, and because Gideon said nothing at all but walked with their face turned toward the peaks like a plant toward light. They traveled by night along a drover's track that the maps had forgotten, which suited Pip fine. Forgotten roads were honest ones. Nobody built a road unless a place mattered, and nobody bothered to erase a road unless it mattered more.

On the third night the clouds came down to meet them, and the world shrank to a gray bowl of rain and rock, and Maren called a halt at a black seam in the mountainside — a cave mouth half-hidden behind a curtain of ivy gone rust-red with autumn.

"Shelter," she said, in her soldier's voice, the one that meant *this is not a discussion*. "We're wet through and Gideon's limping, and I'll not have either of you die of something as stupid as cold."

"I'm not limping," said Gideon.

"You're limping."

Pip loved them like this, the two of them grumbling at each other in the rain. It sounded almost like what families were supposed to sound like, before the taking-away started. Pip ducked under the ivy first, because being smallest meant going first into dark places, and because the stone had begun, very gently, to warm.

Not its usual warmth. Its usual warmth was hearth-warmth, bread-warmth, the temperature of being held. This was different. This climbed. By the time Pip's eyes adjusted to the cave's darkness, the pouch against their chest was hot enough that they fumbled the drawstring open with shaking fingers, and light spilled out —

— gold, deep gold, the color of the last hour of sunset, and it did not stay politely inside the stone. It reached across the cave walls like water finding its level, and where it touched, the rock *woke up*.

Pip made a sound they'd be embarrassed about later. Behind them, Maren's sword cleared its sheath with a scrape like a held breath, and Gideon said, very quietly, "Pip. What is that. What do you have."

But Pip couldn't answer, because the walls were answering instead.

Letters. No — not letters, not the blocky crown-script from the notices nailed to tavern doors, not even the flowing merchant-hand of Lowmark. These were older. They were carved deep and then worn shallow, cut by claws or by tools no smith alive could make, and they spiraled up the walls and across the ceiling in rivers and tributaries, whole histories of writing, and every line of them was catching fire with the stone's gold light and *burning* without being consumed.

"It's writing," Pip whispered. Their own voice sounded far away. "It's been here the whole time. It's always — the stone does this sometimes, it gets warm near old places, but never —"

"Near old places," Gideon repeated. They had come forward without Pip noticing, drawn the way Pip had known they'd be drawn, and their face in the golden light was terrible and wonderful to see. Gideon looked the way Pip imagined sailors looked at the sea after years inland. "Maren. Put the sword away. Put it *away*. This isn't a trap. This is a library."

"A library," said Maren, who had not put the sword away.

"Older than any library." Gideon raised a hand, slowly, and touched the wall, and the light flared beneath their fingers as though the stone recognized kinship. "These aren't human characters. I've seen fragments — in fences' back rooms, in the collections the guilds pretend not to keep. Dragon-script. Everyone says it's decorative. Everyone says dragons don't write." Gideon laughed, a strange cracked sound. "Everyone is wrong. Look how much of it there is. Look how *long* it goes on."

Pip looked. The script ran back into the mountain farther than the light could follow, gallery after gallery, and the ember-light walked ahead of them down each one like a servant lighting lamps.

And then the stone spoke.

* * *

Later, Pip would try to describe it to themselves, and fail, and try again. It was not a voice in the ears. It was a voice in the chest, in the same place the warmth lived, as though someone had laid their hand flat over Pip's heart from the inside. It was old the way mountains are old. It was tired the way the sea is tired — endlessly, patiently, without any hope or any wish of stopping.

*"Small warm thing,"* it said. *"You brought me home."*

Pip's knees folded. They sat down hard on the cave floor, hugging the stone in both hands, and it pulsed against their palms like a second heartbeat, faster now, quicker, quickening.

"Gideon," they managed. "Gideon, it's talking. It's talking to *me*."

Gideon dropped to their knees beside Pip, and Pip watched their friend's face do the thing it did when the dead spoke — the tilt of the head, the far focus — except this time it happened while Gideon's eyes stayed right here, right present, fixed on the stone in Pip's hands.

"I hear it," Gideon breathed. "Saints above, I hear it, it isn't dead, it isn't —" Their voice broke and reformed. "It's dying. Whatever it is, it's dying, and it's still speaking."

*"I am not an it,"* said the voice, with something that in anything younger might have been amusement. *"Though I have been called worse by better. I am Ashfall. I was witness to the founding of Vael, and I was witness to its ending, and I have been waiting forty years for someone to ask me what I saw."*

The name went through the cave like a bell through water.

Vael. The country that wasn't. The name that had been scraped off maps and out of records and out of mouths, the name that got your door marked if you said it too loudly, the name Pip had been carrying in their mouth since before they could carry anything else. Maren made a noise low in her throat — Pip saw her knuckles whiten on her sword-hilt, and this time she sheathed it, deliberately, as if putting the weapon away from herself rather than from the dark.

"That's not possible," Maren said. "Vael fell forty years ago. Anything that witnessed it—"

"—would be older than any living creature you know of," Gideon finished softly. "Yes."

*"The knight reasons well,"* rumbled Ashfall, and the stone warmed another degree, and high above them, far up the galleries of burning script, something answered the warmth. A glow. Faint, guttering, deep in the mountain's throat — like a candle glimpsed through a keyhole, like a banked fire deciding whether to bother rising again.

And with the glow came the smell. Smoke, yes, but under the smoke something else: hot stone and cold iron and, impossibly, roses — the memory of roses, the idea of a rose garden burned into permanence somewhere in that vast tired chest.

Pip stood up. Their legs shook. They stood up anyway, holding the stone out in front of them the way you'd hold a lantern, the way you'd hold out your hand to something frightened.

"You're the last one," Pip said. It wasn't a question. Pip had been the last of things their whole life; they knew the shape of it from the inside. "Aren't you. You're the last dragon."

The silence went on long enough that Pip thought they'd broken something. Then:

*"I am the last witness,"* Ashfall said. *"Which was always the heavier burden. Beasts die and are done. Witnesses must remain until the story is carried. Forty years I have burned low in this mountain, keeping one flame alive, keeping the record, waiting for—"*

The voice faltered. Actually faltered, the great ancient chest-voice stumbling like an old man on stairs, and the guttering glow far up the passage dimmed, and terror went through Pip like ice water, because they understood suddenly, completely, that this enormous presence was *frail*, that the light in the deep of the mountain was a life, and that it was running out.

*"Waiting,"* Ashfall finished, quieter, *"for a small warm thing who carries my fire in a stone and doesn't know what it is. Child. Where did you find the ember?"*

"I didn't find it," Pip said. "I've always had it. My mother—" The word caught. It always caught. "She gave it to me. She wrapped it in cloth and she said, *carry this and remember Vael, and one day it will remember you back.* I thought she meant — I thought it was just a saying. I thought she was being poetic."

*"Your mother,"* said Ashfall, and the whole cave seemed to lean in, the burning script brightening along its miles of walls, *"was Vaelish."*

"Yes."

*"And she is dead."*

"Yes."

*"Then you,"* said the dragon, and the weariness in the vast voice changed into something else, something that made the hair rise on Pip's arms, something like awe arriving from the other direction, awe flowing uphill, *"are the last free carrier of Vael's living memory. The kingdom was erased from paper and stone and tongue. But fire keeps what paper cannot. Child — that stone is not a keepsake. It is a piece of me. Given willingly, long ago, to your people, so that when the crown burned the records, something would survive that the crown could not burn."*

Gideon made a sound. Pip glanced over. Gideon had both hands pressed flat against the glowing script, and tears were running down their face freely, without shame, the way they cried when a carving was finished and the dead woman's voice was safe in the bone forever.

"It's true," Gideon whispered. "All of it. The purge, the erasure — it wasn't madness, it was *policy*. Someone decided a whole country should stop having existed, and the only reason it failed is standing in this cave." They looked up at Pip, and their eyes were ferocious with grief and wonder in equal measure. "Pip. Your mother didn't give you a trinket. She gave you the *evidence*."

Maren had gone very still by the cave mouth, watching the rain, watching the dark, doing the soldier's work of keeping death out of the room while the rest of them did the harder work inside it. But she said, quietly, without turning around: "If Crane learns what that stone is —"

"He already hunts us," Gideon said.

"He hunts *gifted*. A relic that carries a nation's soul — that's not a fugitive anymore, that's a *campaign*. He'll bring the whole Inquisition. He'll bring armies."

*"Let him come,"* said Ashfall, and for one moment the tiredness burned away and the voice was what it must have been at the founding of kingdoms, vast and molten and absolutely unafraid. Then the moment passed, and the great presence sagged, and the glow in the mountain's depths flickered like a candle in wind. *"Forgive me. I flare and I fade. Forty years is a long time to burn on borrowed breath. Small warm thing — Pip. Come closer to my fire. There is much I must tell you, and less time to tell it than either of us wishes."*

Pip looked at Gideon. Gideon looked at Maren. Maren looked at the rain, and then turned around, and nodded once, the nod of a knight accepting a commission she hadn't asked for.

Pip tightened their grip on the stone — on the ember, on the piece of a dead country that had waited forty years to be found — and walked deeper into the mountain, toward the fading light, toward the last witness of Vael.

Behind them, far below in the wet dark, so faint that only the mountain itself heard it, something else was moving. Boots on stone. Many boots. And a voice, soft and reasonable, carrying strangely up through the rock:

"There's light in the mountain. People like this always leave something behind."

"They always leave something."
