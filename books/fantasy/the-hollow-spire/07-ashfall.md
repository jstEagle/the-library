## 7. Ashfall

The mountain stopped being a mountain two days after we left the cave with the dragon-script.

I don't know how else to say it. One moment we were climbing switchbacks through scree and wind-scoured pine, and the next the rock beneath our boots had changed its nature—not stone anymore but something older, something that had been alive once and had never entirely finished dying. The path was warm. Not sun-warm; there was no sun to speak of, only a bruised grey sky pressing down like a lid. This was warmth from underneath, rising slow as breath, and when Maren knelt and pressed her palm flat against it she snatched her hand back as if she'd touched a sleeping face.

"It's breathing," Pip said.

Pip had not let go of the ember-stone since the script on the cave walls had flared to meet it. They carried it in both hands now, cradled against their chest like a bird with a broken wing, and I noticed that they no longer stumbled on the trail. It was as though the stone knew where we were going and was pulling the child along by some thread too fine for me to see—though my gift felt it well enough, a low hum in my back teeth, in the roots of my hair, in the hollow places where the voices of the dead usually lived.

Because this was different. All my life I had heard the dead through what they left behind: a ring, a rib, a lock of hair, the femur of a woman whose daughter sat across from me in a Lowmark tavern while I carved her mother's story into bone. The dead spoke *through* things. But whatever waited ahead of us was not dead. What waited ahead of us was dying, and the difference rang through me like the gap between a bell struck and a bell cracked—one clear note instead of many tangled ones.

"You feel it too," Maren said. She had fallen into step beside me, and her hand rested on the hilt of her sword out of habit rather than fear; Ser Maren Kestrel did not seem to know how to walk without touching steel. "Don't lie to me, carver. You've had that look since the cave."

"I always have a look."

"Your look says *someone is speaking*. Your look has said it since Gravenhollow burned." Her jaw tightened at the name. Odile's betrayal was still an open wound between us—we did not speak of it, which is its own way of speaking—and every hour since we'd fled the smoke of the smuggler city, Maren had walked like a woman expecting arrows. "So tell me what's saying."

I looked ahead. The pass narrowed into a gorge where the walls rose sheer on either side, black basalt shot through with veins of something that glowed faintly gold when the light shifted—the same color as the script, the same color, I understood suddenly, as the ember-stone sleeping in Pip's arms.

"A voice," I said. "One voice. Old." I hesitated, because the next part sounded like madness even to me. "Tired. It isn't calling for help. It's just—waiting. The way you wait when you've decided no one is coming and you've made your peace with it."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Wind funneled down the gorge and moaned against the stone, and somewhere above us a hawk—or what I took for a hawk—circled twice and vanished behind the ridge.

"My commander used to talk about the mountains east of Thornfell," she said finally. "Before Vael fell. He said the old roads through them weren't built around the peaks." She glanced at me sideways. "He said the peaks were built around the roads. That the kingdom's engineers had a bargain with something that slept under the stone, and the roads went where it allowed them to go."

"And you didn't believe him."

"I believed him." Her voice came out flat. "I just never asked him why he believed it. By the time I could have asked, he was dead, and Vael was a word the crown would hang you for saying." She squared her shoulders. "Keep walking, carver. Whatever's waiting, I'd rather meet it standing."

So we walked.

* * *

The gorge opened without warning into a bowl of stone half a mile across, walled on every side, floored with ash fine as flour. Our footfalls made no sound in it. There was no snow here despite the altitude, no ice, no lichen, nothing green or growing or frozen—only the pale grey powder, ankle-deep, and the strange warm stillness, and at the center of the bowl—

I stopped walking. I am not ashamed to say it. My legs simply declined.

The dragon lay curled at the heart of the ash like a banked fire.

It was enormous—I want to give you the size of it, because size mattered, because the sheer impossible bulk of the thing was part of the grief—but words fail at scale the way they fail at grief. Its body was the length of a ship. Its wings, folded along its flanks, were tattered as banners left too long in weather, membrane gone thin and translucent in places so that I could see the dark bones inside them like ribs through skin. Its scales had been the color of embers once—that much was written in the deeper layers, the ones near the body's heat—but now they were grey and dull and flaking, and whole patches had fallen away to reveal hide beneath that looked less like flesh than like charcoal that remembered being wood.

And it was guttering. There is no better word. Light moved beneath those scales the way light moves under the skin of a dying coal, brightening and dimming, brightening and dimming, in a rhythm that I realized with a lurch was the rhythm of breath—breaths spaced minutes apart, each one a small convulsion, each one visibly, terribly, possibly the last.

"Oh," Pip whispered.

The child walked forward before any of us could move. Maren lunged to catch their shoulder and missed, and I caught her arm instead, and we stood frozen while eight-year-old Pip crossed fifty yards of ash with the ember-stone held out before them like a lantern, like an offering, like a letter finally delivered to the address it had been waiting forty years to reach.

The dragon's eye opened.

It was the size of a cart wheel, gold-ringed, vertical-pupiled, clouded at the edges like a gem going blind, and it regarded the small approaching figure with an expression I had no framework for. Not hunger. Not menace. Something I had seen only on the faces of the very old, in the last hours of the people whose families paid me to carve their stories: recognition without surprise. The look of someone who has waited so long for the end of the waiting that the arrival feels almost like an interruption.

"*You are late,*" said Ashfall.

The voice did not come through the air. It came through the ground, through our boot-soles and our bones, through the ash itself, which shivered and rose in little ghost-plumes around our ankles. And it came through me in another way too—it poured into the hollow places where the dead lived, filled them, overflowed them, and I staggered and dropped to one knee in the warm dust because no voice I had ever carried had been *this loud*, this vast, this patient. The dead whisper. Ashfall resonated. Speaking with a dragon in your skull was less like hearing than like being rung.

Maren's sword was out. Of course it was. She planted herself between the dragon and Pip with her blade raised and her feet braced in the ash, and I watched her take in the impossibility of defending anyone from anything that large, and I watched her decide to do it anyway, and I loved her a little in that moment, the way you love a wall.

"*Put up the iron, knight,*" Ashfall rumbled, with something that might, in a creature less exhausted, have been amusement. "*If I wished harm, I have had four decades to arrange it.*"

"What are you?" Maren demanded. It was the wrong question and she knew it the moment it left her mouth, but questions are armor too.

"I am the answer," the dragon said, "to a question your kingdom spent forty years burying." The great head turned, slowly, joints crackling like green logs in a fire, until that ancient eye found me kneeling in the ash. "*Bone-carver. Get up. Come here. Bring the child. We have little time, and I have spent so much of it already.*"

* * *

We sat in the ash at the edge of the dragon's warmth, close enough to feel it, far enough to see all of it, and Ashfall told us how it ended.

Not all of it. Dragons, I learned, do not narrate the way people do; they do not waste breath—which was, for Ashfall, quite literally true—on sequence or scenery. What came to us arrived in pieces, in images pressed directly into our minds like seals into wax:

A kingdom between the mountains and the sea, terraced and white-walled, its towers crowned with gardens. A people who kept memory the way other kingdoms kept grain—in vaults, in archives, in living minds trained for the work. The Vaelish gift was not one gift but many, and among them were carvers, and the carvers served the dragons, who were not beasts to them but librarians. Witnesses. The dragons held what mortals could not carry for long. Each dragon a vault of a generation's remembering.

Then the crown's armies came over the northern passes, and the reason they came was not conquest. Conquest was the shape of it but not the substance. The substance was a betrayal—a pact broken, a debt denied, something the crown had promised Vael and then decided the cheapest way out of the promise was to make sure no one remained alive who could name it. So: the purge. The burning of records first, then of carvers, then of everyone. The erasure of the name from maps and chronicles and gravestones. Forty years of official forgetting, enforced with fire.

Ashfall had fought. The images showed me that—showed me wings blotting out the sun above a burning city, showed me soldiers falling like wheat, showed me three other dragons, silver and green and storm-grey, falling too. Shown me the moment the last of them died and Ashfall turned from the battle and flew east into these mountains with the only thing left worth carrying: not treasure, not vengeance. Memory. A single concentrated ember of everything Vael had been, drawn out of its own vast archive and compressed into stone.

"The ember-stone," I said hoarsely. My hands were shaking. Beside me, Pip had gone very still, the stone resting in their lap, glowing faintly, pulsing in time—with Ashfall's breath, I realized. With the dragon's failing rhythm. "That's what it is. It's not a piece of you. It's—"

"*Vael,*" Ashfall said. "*All of it that I could hold. The rest I burned through keeping myself alive this long, and even that is nearly done.*" The light beneath the scales dimmed as if in illustration, and for three long seconds the dragon was only a mountain of grey ash-colored scales, and my heart stopped in my chest, and then the glow returned, fainter than before. "*I am a candle, carver. I was a bonfire once. Now I am a candle. And a candle cannot guard anything.*"

"Why us?" Maren's voice was rough. She had not put away her sword, but she had stopped pointing it at anything. "Why show yourselves to us? Why the script in the cave? Why pull a child across half the country?"

"Because the Door still stands."

The words dropped into silence like stones into a well. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"The Spire," Ashfall continued. "The Hollow Spire—you will know it when you see it, knight, for your commander spoke of it truly. It stands in two worlds at once. Half of it in yours, ruined, dismissed, a broken tower in the high passes that no map bothers marking. And half of it in the place where Vael was folded away—not destroyed, never destroyed, only *hidden*, hidden so thoroughly that the hiding became the crime. Between the two halves stands a door. And the door opens for exactly one kind of key."

The great eye turned to Pip.

"*Memory,*" Ashfall said softly. "*True memory. Carried in the flesh, not written in books the crown can burn. The Door does not read paper. It reads the bearer. Someone must stand before it carrying Vael inside them—and the child carries the seed of it. Has carried it since before they could speak. Ask them about the harbor at Sel Maris. Ask them the smell of the terraces in autumn. They have never stood in either place, and yet.*"

"I know it smells like cedar smoke and salt," Pip said.

We all looked at them. The child was staring down at the ember-stone, turning it in their small fingers, and their face wore an expression far too old for it—homesickness, pure and undiluted, for a home that had never been theirs in body, only in blood.

"I know the bells ring wrong on feast days because the big bell cracked the winter I wasn't born and they never recast it, they just tuned the others around it. I know my—" Pip stopped. Their chin trembled. "I know somebody's name. I've always known it. I thought I made it up. Maerlin. Maerlin Vael-Sarr. She was a carver. She's—" The child looked up at Ashfall, and their eyes were swimming. "She's in here, isn't she. In the stone. Everyone's in here."

"*Everyone I could gather,*" Ashfall said gently, and the gentleness of it, coming up through the ground, through the ash, nearly broke me. "*Yes, little one.*"

* * *

Later—how much later I cannot say; time moved strangely in that bowl of ash—Maren walked the perimeter, because Maren processed the world by walking it, sword in hand, checking approaches that had gone unapproached for forty years. Pip had fallen asleep against the warm base of the dragon's foreleg, exhausted past resistance, the ember-stone rising and falling with the vast slow breath. And I stayed sitting where I was, in the warm ash, looking up at a creature that was dying in front of me and asking it the question I had been carrying since the cave.

"Why keep it?" I said. "The stone. The memories. Why not let go? You could have died clean, Ashfall. Instead you've spent forty years starving, alone, holding a country inside you like a wound. For what? Who's left to remember?"

The dragon was silent for so long that I thought perhaps it had slipped into sleep, or beyond it. Then the light beneath its scales gathered, and the eye rolled toward me, and I felt the weight of its attention settle over me like snow.

"*Carver. Tell me what you hear, when you carve a dead woman's story into her bone.*"

I swallowed. "Her. Just—her. The shape of her. What she loved, what she feared, the sound of her laugh if she laughed enough for it to leave an echo in things. It's not a recording. It's more like..." I groped for it. "Like her handwriting. The dead leave handwriting, and I copy it out fair."

"*And when the crown burns your carvings—when the hunters burn the bones—what is lost?*"

"The handwriting," I said quietly. "The person. Again."

"*Yes.*" Ashfall's breath came out in a long plume that was not quite smoke and not quite light. "*Now you understand what was done to Vael. It was not a war. Wars kill bodies; bodies are cheap. What was done to Vael was done to the handwriting. Ten thousand years of human lives—every laugh, every fear, every song, every grave—and the crown reached into history and scraped it smooth, so that no one would ever read it again. So that the debt they owed would be owed to no one.*" The eye closed, opened. "*I am the last hand that still remembers the letters. When I die, if the stone dies with me unwritten, then the erasure becomes total, and permanent, and the crown wins a victory that not even time could have given them.*"

"So you need someone to write it down."

"*I need someone to carry it out. The Door opens to true memory borne in flesh. And the carrying—*" The dragon paused, and in that pause I heard something I had never heard from a voice of any kind, living or dead: reluctance. Fear, even. "*The carrying is not gentle. The stone holds a nation, carver. A mind must be strong to receive a nation and remain itself.*"

My mouth had gone dry. "Who receives it?"

"That," said a new voice, "is what I'd like to know."

Maren had come back around the bowl's rim, ash to her knees, and she stood looking down at Ashfall and me with an expression I was beginning to know well—the look of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like. Behind her, blinking sleep from their eyes, Pip sat up against the dragon's leg, and the ember-stone pulsed in the child's hands, brighter now, warmer now, as if it too had heard the question and was waiting, hopeful and terrible, for the answer.

Ashfall's gaze moved from Maren to Pip to me, and settled, and stayed.

And the dragon said nothing at all, which was the loudest answer I had ever heard.
