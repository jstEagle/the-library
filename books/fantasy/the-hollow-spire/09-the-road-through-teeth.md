## 9. The Road Through Teeth

The Serrated Pass did not deserve its name for its beauty.

Maren Kestrel had crossed it twice in her soldiering years—once in summer, when the switchbacks were merely treacherous, and once in autumn, when the wind came down off the high crags like a blade drawn slowly across the throat. Now it was late in the year and the pass was neither of those things. It was worse. It was quiet.

She walked at the rear of their small procession and counted the silences the way she had once counted her men before a march. Gideon ahead, hood up, staff in hand, favoring the left knee that had been bad since Gravenhollow. Pip between them, small hands buried in the folds of an oversized coat, the ember-stone hanging against their chest on a cord of braided leather, glowing faintly through the cloth like a coal wrapped in wool. And behind them all, Ashfall.

The dragon could not fly. That was the truth none of them said aloud anymore, because saying it made it heavier. Ashfall walked—or rather, dragged herself—on legs that trembled like an old woman's, her wings folded tight against a body gone gaunt beneath scales the color of banked coals. Where she stepped, frost did not form. Where she breathed, the air warmed by a degree or two, and Pip always seemed to find their way to that warmth without knowing they were doing it.

*You keep looking back at me,* Ashfall had said that morning, in that voice like a hearth speaking. *Stop it. I am not dying today. I am merely... negotiating with the day.*

Maren had almost smiled. Almost.

Now, three days into the pass, she stopped smiling altogether, because she had seen the cairn.

It stood at the junction where the pilgrim's trail met the old military road—a stack of stones no higher than her knee, but stacked wrong. Soldiers stacked cairns to mark safe passage; smugglers stacked them to mark water. This one marked neither. It was a marker of the kind bounty crews used in the borderlands: three stones upright, one laid across, and a twist of red thread beneath. *Prey passed here. Prey is ahead.*

She did not break stride. She drifted to Gideon's side as if stretching her legs and murmured, "Don't look left. We're being tracked."

Gideon's jaw tightened, but their pace never changed. "How many?"

"Can't tell yet. But someone read our trail recently enough to leave fresh thread." She glanced skyward, at the low gray ceiling of cloud pressing down on the crags. "And this stretch has no cover for two miles. If they mean to take us, it'll be here."

"Could we turn back?"

"Back is four days to Gravenhollow, which is ash. Forward is the Spire." Maren flexed her sword hand inside its glove. The old wound in her shoulder ached, the way it always ached before violence, as if her body remembered battles her mind wanted to forget. "So we go forward. And we go fast."

Behind them, Ashfall rumbled—not words, just a sound, deep enough that Maren felt it in her boot soles. The dragon knew too. Dragons, Maren suspected, had been reading ambushes since before humans learned to write them down.

They moved.

*

The pass narrowed through the afternoon, walls of gray stone rising on either side until the sky was a ribbon overhead. The old road was paved in places—Vael work, Gideon said, though Maren noticed they only ever said it quietly, the way people spoke of gods they weren't sure were listening. Forty years ago an army had marched this road to erase a kingdom, and Maren had been part of the machinery that sent it. Not on this road. Not on that day. But part of it.

That was the thing about desertion. People imagined it was a single moment—a turning of the heel, a dropping of the sword. It wasn't. It was a thousand moments stretched across nineteen years, each one a small refusal to look directly at what you'd done, until your whole life was built facing sideways.

"Ser Kestrel." Pip had fallen back to walk beside her, the way the child did when the climbing got hard and pride demanded company. "Did you really fight in wars? Real ones?"

"I did."

"Did you win?"

Maren looked down at the small face, chapped by wind, eyes too old for eight years old. Children who remembered erased countries asked questions like that. "Some of them," she said. "Losing isn't always about battles."

"What's it about, then?"

"About what you do after."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. Then: "Ashfall says you carry your guilt like a stone in your boot. They say you should take it out and put it in your hand instead. So you can see it."

Maren's breath caught, just slightly. "Does Ashfall say much?"

"Pip talks to me,*" came the rumble from behind, warm and dry. "*Pip also repeats me to strangers. These are different services.*

"I'm not a stranger," Maren said.

"No," Ashfall agreed, and said nothing more, which from a dragon was a great deal.

The light was failing when Maren saw the second marker—and this time, the thread was new. Bright. Tied within the hour.

She had time to shout exactly one word.

*

They came off the rocks like water finding a crack—six of them, maybe seven, in mismatched leathers with the badges of their old allegiances stripped away, carrying crossbows and boar-spears and the particular confidence of men who hunted things that couldn't fight back. Bounty crews out of the lowland towns, hired since the Purge began, paid by the head for anyone the witch-hunters' lists named. A carver would fetch a fortune. A child who remembered Vael would fetch more.

"*Down!*" Maren roared, and shoved Gideon toward the rock wall as the first bolt hissed past her ear and sparked off stone.

Then the world became the old arithmetic. Distance, numbers, ground, time. Six shooters on the high ledge to the east, two already reloading. Three coming down the scree on foot, spears leveled. The road ahead blocked by a felled tree that hadn't fallen on its own—the trap had been laid for hours, waiting for them to walk into the throat of the pass. Behind, the way they'd come, still open. For now.

"Ashfall!" Gideon's voice cracked across the narrows. "The rear!"

The dragon turned, and even guttering, even starving, she was still a dragon. Fire bloomed from her throat in a ragged, coughing gout—weak by her standards, and by any human standard apocalyptic. The scree slope lit orange. Two of the spearmen fled screaming, their weapons abandoned, hair smoking. The third dropped flat behind a boulder and lived.

But the crossbows on the ledge kept working. That was the trouble with bounty men—they were cowards with patience. Bolts came down in pairs now, disciplined, walking toward them across the road. Toward Pip, who had frozen in the open, arms around the ember-stone, too frightened to move.

Maren didn't decide anything. Nineteen years ago she had failed to move when moving mattered, and every step since had been a rehearsal for this one moment, though she hadn't known it. Her body simply went—across the road, into the open, into the rain of iron—and wrapped itself around the child.

The first bolt took her through the meat of the left shoulder, punching clean through the padding, and the pain was white and clarifying, like cold water. She folded Pip beneath her and dragged them both behind the fallen tree as a second bolt splintered the wood an inch from her cheek.

"Ser Kestrel—" Pip's voice, tiny, pressed against her collarbone.

"I have you," Maren said. "I have you. Stay under."

Through the gap beneath the log she saw Gideon standing in the open, unarmed, doing the thing Maren still did not fully understand—head bowed, lips moving, listening to the dead while the living tried to kill them all. And she saw the lead bounty man come down off the rocks at last, crossbow leveled at Gideon's back, close enough now that the fire couldn't reach him without cooking them all.

Maren drew her sword with her right hand because her left wouldn't answer, rolled over the log, and closed the distance in six strides that her knees would bill her for later.

He was good. He was quick. He was not a knight. She caught his crossbow arm on her forearm, felt the bolt go wild into the stones, and put her shoulder—her ruined, bolt-torn shoulder—into him with everything the fall down the scree had left her. They went down together. His head found the paving. He did not get up.

Silence, then. The kind that rings.

On the ledge above, the remaining crossbowmen weighed their wages against a dragon, however sickly, and a knight, however bleeding, and began withdrawing into the dusk with the professional discretion of men whose contract had not priced in dragons.

Maren knelt on the road, sword point down, breathing in wet, ragged pulls, and watched her own blood drip black in the fading light.

"Maren." Gideon was beside her, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "You're hit."

"It's nothing." It was not nothing. The bolt had gone through, front to back, below the collarbone. She knew the geography of wounds well enough to know it had missed everything that killed quickly and hit everything that hurt for weeks. "Pip—"

"I'm here." The child crawled out from behind the log, face streaked with dust and tears, and did the most remarkable thing: they pressed both small hands flat against Maren's wound, above and below, the way you'd hold a book closed. "Ashfall says hold it tight. Says blood wants to stay in. Blood knows its job."

"That," Maren managed, "is not how medicine works."

"Ashfall is older than medicine."

From the shadows, the great head lowered, and one ancient eye regarded Maren with something she had never expected to see directed at her: not pity. Recognition. *"Nineteen years,*" the dragon said, softly, for a value of soft that still rattled pebbles, *"you carried the stone in your boot. Today you put it in your hand. Feel the difference.*"

Maren felt it. God help her, she felt it.

*

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire small enough to hide, Ashfall's body curved around them all like a wall against the wind. Gideon cleaned and bound the wound with hands that shook only a little, and Maren bore it without sound, because she had been stitched before by men who were drunk and she had screamed less then than she wanted to now, and some habits are armor.

Pip fell asleep against Ashfall's foreleg, one fist still curled around the ember-stone, its glow seeping between the small fingers like a secret.

For a long time, neither Maren nor Gideon spoke. The wind worked at the mouth of the hollow. Somewhere far off, a rock fell, and the pass swallowed the sound.

"You took a bolt for a child you've known nine days," Gideon said at last, not looking up from the bandage work.

"Yes."

"Why?"

And there it was. The question she had spent nineteen years arranging her life to avoid. Maren watched the firelight move on the dragon's scales and understood that she could lie—Gideon would let her, probably, guarded people recognize other guarded people's exits—but the stone was in her hand now, and Ashfall had told the truth about what that felt like.

"Because forty years ago," Maren said, "the crown decided Vael had never existed. And I was there when they made it true. Not at the burning—I was younger, I was posted on the coast, nowhere near it. But the orders came down through my chain of command. My commander, Ser Aldric Voss, refused to sign the muster that sealed the mountain roads. They broke him for it. Stripped his name from the rolls. And the rest of us—" Her voice caught. She let it. "The rest of us signed. Or we looked away while others signed. I looked away. And then, when the last refugees came down out of the hills and the garrisons were ordered to turn them back into the winter, I deserted. I didn't help them. I didn't fight for them. I just... left. Took off my sword and walked south and told myself for nineteen years that leaving was the same as refusing."

Gideon sat very still. In the firelight their face was unreadable, carved, like something out of their own work.

"It wasn't," Maren said. "Leaving was just another way of letting it happen. So when I saw Pip standing in the open with bolts falling—" She exhaled. "I wasn't choosing. I was finally paying. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and I want you to know I know it. Redemption isn't granted. Nobody grants it. You earn it in installments, and I'm nineteen years in arrears."

The wind sighed through the hollow. Ashfall's eye, half-lidded, watched them both.

Gideon tied off the bandage, smoothed it flat with surprising gentleness, and said, "Aldric Voss."

"You know the name?"

"He's one of my voices." Gideon's voice was quiet. "Or he was. A carving came through Lowmark years ago—a finger bone, from a pauper's grave. He talked for eleven nights about a muster he wouldn't sign. About a knight he'd trained who ran south, and how he hoped—how he *hoped*—she'd found somewhere the snow couldn't reach her." Gideon looked up, and their eyes were bright and terrible. "He never blamed you, Maren. In nineteen years of being dead, he never once blamed you. He worried about your boots wearing through."

Maren Kestrel had held a shield line against cavalry. She had carried wounded men three miles through a marsh. She had taken a crossbow bolt that afternoon without crying out.

She wept now, silently, into her unwounded hand, while the dragon breathed warmth over them all and the child slept on, dreaming, perhaps, of a country that was real again for as long as the telling lasted.

*

Near midnight, Gideon woke her for her watch, and Maren took the blade across her knees and stared out at the pass and did not sleep.

It was near dawn when she heard it—not with her ears, not exactly, but in that place behind the sternum where Aldric Voss had lived for nineteen years without her naming him. A feeling, cold and certain, like a hand on the back of her neck.

*They're coming,* the feeling said. *Not the bounty men. The ones behind them. Riders. Many.*

And far down the dark throat of the pass, faint as a star drowning, she saw torchlight.

Forty riders, moving fast.

Crane had found the trail.
