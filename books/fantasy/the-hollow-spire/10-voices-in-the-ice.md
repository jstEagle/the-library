## 10. Voices in the Ice

The mountain killed the road behind us with a sound like a god clearing its throat.

One moment we were crossing the frozen gallery beneath the Serrated Pass's northern shoulder—a corridor of blue ice older than any kingdom, hung with icicles like the ribs of some drowned cathedral—and the next, the whole face of the slope let go. Snow came down first, soft as a lie, and then the rock came down after it, honest and enormous, and the world ended in white noise.

When Gideon dug themselves out of the drift—coughing, one ear ringing, hands bleeding through their gloves—the passage behind them was gone. Sealed. A wall of shattered stone and packed snow where the road had been, rising thirty feet and more, already glazing over with new ice as the meltwater froze in the sudden stillness.

Ahead, the passage continued, narrowing into darkness.

Behind, nothing but burial.

"Pip." The name tore out of them before thought. "Pip—Maren—"

"Here!" Maren's voice, muffled, then clearer. A shape resolved out of the churned snow: the knight on her knees beside a small mound she was excavating with her bare hands, gauntlets discarded, fingers black with cold. "We're here. Both of us. Dig, damn you, dig—"

Gideon dug. They dug until their nails split, until they uncovered Pip's head, then shoulders, the child coughing and spitting and clutching the ember-stone against their chest like a second heart. Ashfall had been at the rear; the dragon's bellow rolled through the ice like thunder trapped in a bottle, and somewhere beyond the collapse they heard the great body dragging itself through the snow, circling, finding no way in.

They were alive. All three of them, alive, in a throat of ice that went forward into the mountain and backward into a wall of stone.

And the temperature was falling.

* * *

"We can't dig through that," Maren said, an hour later. She'd gotten a fire going—small, mean, fed on the splinters of her own spear-shaft's broken fletching—and huddled Pip inside her cloak. "Not without tools. Not before we freeze."

"Then we go forward," Gideon said.

"Forward is down. Look at it." The passage ahead sloped steeply, descending into the mountain's cold heart, and the ice grew thicker with every yard, until the walls weren't stone at all anymore but glacier, ancient and blue-green, lit faintly from within by some refraction of light that had no source Gideon could name. "That's not a road. That's a wound."

"It's shelter," Gideon said. "Stone walls hold warmth better than open air. And Ashfall knows where we went in. If there's another way out—"

"If." Maren said it flatly, but she was already gathering the packs.

They went down.

The cold deepened around them like water. It got into the joints of everything—their boots, their breath, the buckles of their packs, which began to resist the fingers. Pip stopped talking, which frightened Gideon more than the silence of the dead ever had, and pressed close between the two adults, the ember-stone held out in both small hands like a lantern. Its glow was the only warm color left in the world: a stubborn orange, pulsing slow, like something breathing in its sleep.

It was Pip who saw the first hand.

"There," the child whispered, and pointed with their chin, because pointing with a finger would have meant letting go of the stone.

Gideon followed the gesture and felt the floor tilt under them.

A hand, yes—but not flesh. Bone. Pale bone fused into the ice of the wall, fingers half-curled, reaching toward the passage as if the owner had died walking toward exactly where they now stood. And below it, and around it, and beyond it, deeper into the blue dark: more. Shoulders. Helmets. The long straight lines of spears, frozen mid-fall. An entire rank of soldiers embedded in the glacier like flies in amber, preserved in the posture of their last moment, facing outward—facing the way Gideon and the others had come.

"They were holding this pass," Maren breathed. She had gone very still, the way she did only before violence or grief. "Facing the approach. This is a battle line."

Gideon stepped closer. Closer than sense allowed. The cold poured off the ice like a river running uphill, and yet Gideon leaned into it, because beneath the cold they could feel it—that pressure behind the ears, that taste of copper and candle-smoke at the back of the throat, the sensation every carver knew. The presence of the dead, thick as fog.

There had never been anything like this. Never this many. Never this close.

"Gideon." Maren's hand closed on their shoulder. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I have to hear them."

"You don't know whose bones those are."

"I do." Gideon's voice came out strange to them, low and certain. "Look at the helms. Look at the crest on that shield." The device was worn nearly smooth by forty years of ice, but its outline remained: a tower with a hollow center, a spire with a missing heart. "I've carved that sigil twice in my life, Maren. Both times on relics smuggled out of a country that isn't supposed to exist."

Pip made a small sound. When Gideon glanced back, the child was staring at the shield with an expression Gideon had no words for—recognition, and homesickness, and a grief far too old for eight years old.

"That's Vael," Pip whispered. "Those are Vael's soldiers. They're *waiting.*"

* * *

Carving ice was not carving bone. Gideon learned this quickly, kneeling before the wall of the glacier with their knife out and their breath frosting the blade. Ice fought. Ice cracked along lines you couldn't predict, and one wrong stroke would shatter the whole tableau and whatever voice lived in it.

But the gift had never really been about the knife. The knife was only how they listened closely enough.

So Gideon didn't cut. They traced. Fingertip and blade both, following the contours of the nearest soldier—the hand first, then the wrist, then the forearm where the flesh had long since gone but the bone remained, sealed in its clear blue tomb. And they opened themselves the way Odile had taught them never to do all the way, the way that left you lying on your back afterward with someone else's death rattling in your chest.

They opened all the way.

The voices came like a dam breaking.

Not one voice. A hundred. Then more than a hundred—a chorus, layered, overlapping, the last hours of a garrison compressed into a single terrible chord. Gideon gasped and gripped the ice and let it pour through them.

*—hold the line, hold it, the Door must not be found—*

*—they promised us reinforcement from the capital, where are they, where—*

*—the banners are wrong. Look at the banners coming up the pass. Those are our own colors—*

*—it's the crown. Gods forgive me, it was always the crown—*

Gideon's knees hit the ice. Somewhere far away Maren was saying their name, and Pip was crying, and none of it reached them. There was only the chorus, and within the chorus, one voice rising above the rest—clear, disciplined, furious—a commander's voice, and Gideon's knife-hand moved without instruction, tracing the man's jawbone through the ice, focusing, narrowing the flood to a single channel.

*My name is Aldric Vane,* the voice said, *last Marshal of the Garrison of the Hollow Spire, and I make this record knowing no one will hear it, because the men who killed us intend to unmake the country we died for.*

*I was there. Remember that, whoever you are, if anyone is ever able to hear this. I was there when the crown's envoys came to Vael's court and asked for the Door—not to destroy it, as they claimed in their proclamations. To own it. A door between worlds is a weapon such as history has never seen, and the King's ministers wanted it, and Queen Sarelle refused them. Refused to surrender the Spire, refused to give up the Ember that kept it, refused to make her people's memory a thing to be bought.*

*So the crown did what crowns do. They did not declare war. Wars leave witnesses.*

*They sent soldiers in our own livery. They burned our granaries in winter and called it famine. They poisoned the wells at Callowmere and called it plague. And when we finally understood—when I understood—I wrote to the capital, to the King himself, and his answer came back signed by his own hand.*

*He thanked me for my service. And he told me that by spring, there would be no Vael to be loyal to.*

*We held the pass for nine days. Nine days against our own countrymen, because if they took the Spire, they would take the Door, and if they took the Door, they would erase us—not kill us, killing leaves bones, and bones can be read—they would *unwrite* us. Every map. Every record. Every grave. Our children would grow up never knowing there had ever been a place called home.*

*They took the Spire on the ninth day. We fell back here, to the deep ice, the last forty-one of us, and we made our stand at the mouth of the tunnel so that the few who carried the true records—the maps, the histories, the ember-seeds—could flee east through the Door itself. I do not know if they made it. I do not know if anything made it.*

*But hear me, whoever you are. Hear me and carve it somewhere. Carve it in stone, in wood, in your own skin if you must. The kingdom of Vael was not destroyed by dragons or plague or the wrath of heaven. It was murdered by its own king, and buried by its own king, and the Purge you are fleeing—yes, I see you, listener, I feel you at my shoulder like a comrade—the Purge is not a hunt for witches. It is the gravedigger, come back forty years later to make certain the grave stays shut.*

*They erased us because we knew too much. And everyone who carries the gift carries what we knew.*

*So carry it. Carry it farther than we could. And when you speak of us—*

The voice frayed. Gideon clawed at the ice, tracing faster, deeper, chasing the last of it—

*—when you speak of us, say we stood. Say we stood, and say why—*

Silence.

Gideon came back to their body the way a drowning person comes back to the surface—violently, painfully, retreating snow-light and Maren's grip on their arms and Pip's small terrified face swimming into focus.

"You were gone," Maren said. Her voice shook, and Gideon realized the knight had been afraid—for them, of them, both at once. "Four minutes. You weren't breathing. Your lips were going blue and you wouldn't stop—your hands were moving, carving the air, like you were writing something invisible—"

"I heard them." Gideon's teeth were chattering so hard the words barely assembled. "Maren. I heard them. The garrison of the Hollow Spire. Forty-one soldiers who died holding this pass so the records of Vael could escape through the Door." They laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? The crown didn't purge Vael because Vael was wicked. The crown *murdered* Vael. The King signed the order himself. They erased a whole country to hide a betrayal, and the Purge—Crane, the burnings, all of it—it's not about the gift at all. It's about making sure no one can ever *hear* the proof."

Maren had gone the color of the ice. "I was there," she said quietly. "At the end of it. After. My order was posted to the borderlands during the resettlement. We were told we were securing territory against raiders." Her hand drifted to her sword-hilt, the way it always did when the guilt spoke. "There were no raiders. There were villages with names nobody could find in any archive. I told myself the maps were just incomplete." She looked at the frozen ranks of the dead, at the shields with the hollow spire, and something in her face broke and reformed into something harder. "They weren't incomplete. They were *cleansed.*"

"Their final stand," Gideon said. "Right here. Facing the way we came. Holding the line for a retreat that happened forty years ago, and they're still holding it." They pressed their palm flat against the ice, over the marshal's fused hand. "Nobody ever heard them. Nobody ever carved them. They've been waiting in the dark for forty years for someone to listen."

"Then listen," Pip said.

Gideon turned. The child had crept forward, ember-stone raised, and its orange light fell across the glacier wall—and the wall *answered*. Where the glow touched, shapes surfaced in the deep ice that Gideon's eyes alone had missed: further ranks, deeper galleries, and beyond the soldiers, sealed in a pocket of crystal-clear ice like a reliquary, a bundle. Wrapped leather. Straps. The unmistakable silhouette of a satchel built to survive anything, carried by someone who had been ordered to survive anything.

The records. Or part of them. Left behind when the carriers fell.

"It's real," Pip whispered. "All of it. It was always real. Everyone said I made it up, that Vael wasn't anywhere, that I was a liar—" The child's voice cracked, and the ember-stone flared bright enough to throw shadows. "It was *real.*"

Gideon looked at the ice-locked satchel, at the army around it, at the child lit gold at the center of all that blue. Rage had been living in their chest since Lowmark, since the notice on the tavern door, since Crane—rage with nowhere to go, rage that ate its holder. But here, in the cold, it changed. It crystallized. It became something with edges and purpose, something that could be carried instead of merely suffered.

They were going to open that Door. Not to flee. Not anymore.

They were going to open it carrying the truth, and they were going to put the truth down on the other side like a banner planted in enemy ground, and the crown could send every Inquisitor it had and it would not matter, because a story carved into ten thousand ordinary hands could not be burned out of the world.

"We dig them out," Gideon said. "The satchel. Carefully. And then we find the way through, because I am not leaving this mountain without them."

Maren nodded once and reached for her sword to use as a lever.

And that was when the ice beneath their feet rang—a deep, resonant toll, like a bell struck somewhere far below—and from the direction of the collapsed entrance, muffled by thirty feet of stone and snow, came a sound that turned Gideon's blood to glacier-water.

Ashfall, roaring in fury.

And beneath the dragon's roar, thin and human and horribly familiar, carried on some trick of the frozen air:

Horns. Many horns. Marching in step.

Someone had found the other side of the pass. Someone was already digging.
