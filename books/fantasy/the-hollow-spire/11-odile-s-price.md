## 11. Odile's Price

The ice gave them up grudgingly.

Maren worked the sword as a lever for what felt like a season, prying slabs of collapsed snowpack away from a seam in the rubble where the cold air moved—barely moved, a thread of wind thin as spider silk, but moving. Gideon dug with bare hands until their fingers stopped reporting pain and started reporting nothing at all. Pip wedged into the gap and sang, low and tuneless, some counting song from a country that no longer existed, and Gideon chose to believe it helped.

They came out into blue dusk on the far side of the slide, coughing ice dust, Ashfall's roar still echoing off the peaks above them like thunder that had lost its way home. The dragon lay against the mountainside with her wings half-spread, throat smoking, too weak to do more than announce her fury to the sky. The ember-stone at Pip's throat pulsed in sympathy, bright-dark, bright-dark, a heartbeat made visible.

"They're digging from the south face," Maren said. She had her ear pressed to a boulder as if the mountain might gossip. "I can feel the picks through the stone. Half a day, maybe less. They brought sappers."

"Then we go north," Gideon said. "Down into the high valley, cut across before—"

"No."

The voice came from the treeline.

Gideon knew it before they turned. Knew it the way you know a scar—by texture, by the particular ache it raised in old weather. They turned anyway, because knowing a thing and facing it were different countries, and Odile Vash was already walking out of the pines with snow to her knees and a lantern swinging from one hand, and she looked exactly as she had always looked and completely wrong: thinner, greyer, wrapped in a fur coat that had never been hers, wearing the expression of a woman who had rehearsed this moment a hundred times and found every rehearsal insufficient.

"You'll go north straight into the second patrol," she said. "Crane split his forces at the pass mouth. North ridge, south face, and the valley road. He's not hunting you anymore, carver. He's herding you."

Pip stepped behind Gideon's leg without seeming to decide to. Maren's hand went to her sword—not drawing it, just resting there, a question asked politely.

Gideon did not move at all. Moving would have meant admitting their legs had opinions.

"Odile."

"Gideon." She said their name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. Her breath smoked in the cold. "You look terrible."

"You look rich."

The words landed. He watched them land—watched her flinch the way she'd flinched at nothing else, not the accusation in Maren's stance, not the child's stare, only that. *Rich.* The amnesty Crane had bought her with, presumably. A pardon written on paper that smelled of smoke.

"I need to tell you about the valley road," Odile said. "And then I need you to listen, all of it, before anyone decides anything. Can you give me that? Ten minutes of listening. It's more than I deserve. I know it's more than I deserve. Give it to me anyway."

* * *

They made a fire small enough to hide in a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, and Odile talked with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of Maren's tea that she held without drinking, as if warmth were a formality she no longer qualified for.

She told it plainly, which was the worst part. Gideon had expected excuses—the smuggler's arithmetic, everyone has a price, the crown holds all the scales. Instead she told it like a ledger entry, because Odile had always kept honest books even when the goods were stolen.

"They came to Gravenhollow nine days ago. Not Crane himself at first—his people. They burned the Tallowmarket and took forty people to the square, and they said the burning would stop when someone produced the bone-carver who'd been fencing relics through my shop for six years." She turned the cup in her hands. "I told myself I'd hold out. I want you to know that. I sat in my back room and I told myself, *Odile, you have survived three purges and two wars by keeping your mouth shut,* and then they brought out the Ferrow girl—you remember her, the tanner's daughter, sixteen, could light candles with a thought—and they put her on the pyre and lit it, and she didn't scream, Gideon, she just stood there and looked at the crowd like she was memorizing us, and I broke. Not nobly. Not dramatically. I broke the way ice breaks, all at once, with a sound."

"You could have warned me before," Gideon said quietly. "A letter. A whisper. Anything."

"I know."

"You sold me, Odile. You sold Pip." Their voice stayed level, which surprised them; somewhere below the level voice there was a furnace door rattling. "A child. You sold a child to the man who burns children."

"Yes." No flinch this time. She met it head-on, and Gideon understood that she had decided long ago not to be forgiven, that she had built this confession like a coffin and meant to lie down in it. "Yes, I did. And here is what I've done since, and you can weigh it however you like."

She reached into her coat and drew out a folded map, worn soft at the creases, and spread it on the stone between them. Her finger traced the valley north.

"Crane's route. He's taking the main force through the Serrated Pass behind you—he wants you driven forward, down toward the Spire, where he can take you in the open rather than dig you out of another hole. His outriders hold the north ridge tonight, but they rotate at moonset. There's a shepherd's track—" her nail tapped a hairline path west of the ridge line, "—that isn't on any military map. It drops into the Spire valley from above. Hard climbing. The knight can do it. The dragon cannot."

"Ashfall stays," Maren said. It wasn't a question. It was a wound being reopened to check whether it had healed. It hadn't.

"Ashfall stays," Odile agreed gently. "Or you all die on a mountainside, and the dragon dies anyway, and the child dies, and nothing is saved. That's the arithmetic. I'm sorry. I know how much you hate arithmetic."

"And Crane?" Gideon asked. "When he finds the pass empty?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere far below, muffled by miles of stone, the sappers' picks rang on, patient as a heartbeat.

"He won't find it empty," she said. "Because I'm going to be standing in it."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the fire to burn down a log's worth.

"You're going to delay him," Maren said at last. "Alone. With what—an apology?"

"With the truth, mostly. He wants the carver. I'm the one who sold the carver. I'm the thread he trusts." Odile smiled, and it was the old smile, the fence's smile, the one that had charmed a hundred suspicious buyers in a smoky back room, except now it was aimed inward like a knife. "I'll tell him I tracked you further east, that you doubled back toward Lowmark, that you're traveling with a wounded soldier who's slowing you down. I'll make it detailed. I'm good at detailed. And while he decides whether to believe me, he'll have to decide whether to kill me first, and either way, that's hours. Hours is a country, these days. You can hide whole armies in hours."

"He'll kill you," Pip said.

Everyone looked at the child. Pip hadn't spoken since Odile walked out of the trees, and now the ember-stone threw its slow pulse of light across the small furious face.

"He'll kill you," Pip said again. "He burns people. That's the whole thing about him. That's his entire personality."

"Pip—" Gideon began.

"Don't *Pip* me." The child's chin came up, and for a moment Gideon saw it—the thing under the orphan's wariness, the thing that remembered a kingdom. Steel older than the crown. "She sold us. But she came up a freezing mountain alone to fix it, and fixing it means dying, and nobody makes you do that. Nobody can make you do that. So don't you dare pretend it doesn't count."

Odile looked at the child for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone rough.

"That's the fairest sentence anyone's passed on me in weeks," she said. "Thank you."

"It wasn't forgiveness," Pip said. "It was arithmetic."

Gideon laughed—a short, broken sound, startled out of them before they could stop it, and it hurt, laughing, the way ribs hurt after a cave-in. Because the child was right, and worse than right: the child had said aloud the exact shape of the thing sitting in Gideon's chest.

Because here was the truth Gideon had been circling since Odile stepped out of the pines: they were not going to forgive her. Not tonight, maybe not ever. Forgiveness was a door, and some doors you couldn't open because the hinges had frozen, and pretending otherwise would be a lie, and lies between Gideon and Odile had been currency once but were worthless now. And yet—

And yet Gideon heard their mother's carved confession in their memory, the words still fresh as frostbite: *I ran so the hunters would follow me and not the child.* Love that couldn't be called love because it cost too much to name. Debt paid in the only coin left when everything else was confiscated.

Some debts couldn't be repaid. Some could only be paid forward—passed down the line like a bucket in a fire-line, each hand scalded, none of them the hand that started it.

"Odile." Gideon leaned forward. "Look at me."

She looked. Up close, in the ember-light, Gideon could see how much the last nine days had cost her—the sleeplessness printed under her eyes, the tremor in her hands she was hiding by holding the cup.

"I don't forgive you," Gideon said. "I want you to hear that clearly, because you're about to die for us, and you deserve to walk into that with your eyes open. I don't forgive you. Maybe I will someday, on the other side of whatever's coming, if there is an other side. But I see you. I see what you broke yourself on, and I see what you're doing about it, and I—" their voice caught, and they let it, because Odile deserved true things even unforgiving ones, "—I am not going to let it be nothing. Whatever happens on that pass, it will not be nothing. Your books will balance. Not clean. Balanced."

Odile's composure finally cracked. Just a seam—just enough for her to press the back of her wrist against her eyes for one unsteady breath before the fence's mask came back down, smooth as lacquer.

"Sixteen years," she said, "and you still talk like a funeral oration. It's insufferable." She wiped her face briskly, businesslike, and began folding the map. "Moonset's in four hours. The rotation changes then. You should be on the shepherd's track within two, moving quiet. Knight—I marked water sources. The child eats before the climb; cold climbs eat children first." She paused, then added, quieter: "There's a chapel ruin halfway down the track. Dry floor. If the weather turns."

Maren took the map and studied it with a soldier's economy, committing it to memory in the way of people who expected maps to be confiscated. "I'll get them there," she said. "All of them."

"I know you will." Odile glanced at her. "Deserter to deserter, Kestrel—running was the easy part, wasn't it? Coming back is the work."

Maren's jaw tightened. She said nothing. But she inclined her head, a fraction, a salute between two women who understood the same wound from opposite ends.

* * *

They packed in silence. There wasn't much to pack—there never was, anymore—but Gideon took longer than necessary winding the straps, because watching Odile prepare to die was a thing the eyes resisted.

At the edge of the firelight, Odile stopped. She dug in her coat one last time and pressed something into Gideon's palm: a small carving, bone, worn smooth by years of handling. Gideon knew it without looking. Their own early work—one of the first pieces they'd ever fenced through her, a thumb-sized figure of a woman holding a lantern, carved badly, the proportions wrong, the face a smear.

"You kept this?"

"Kept everything of yours." Odile shrugged, embarrassed. "Sentimental habit of fences. We sell other people's ghosts; we like to own one of our own." She closed Gideon's fingers around it. "Listen to it sometime. When it's safe. I used to talk to it, you know. Late nights, closing the shop. Told it things I couldn't tell anyone living." A crooked smile. "If the gift ever comes back to you in some other life—well. Then you'll know everything. Won't you."

"Odile—"

"Don't." She stepped back, out of reach, out of the firelight's claim. "Don't carve me a goodbye. Don't say anything that sounds final. I've spent my whole life trading in endings and I find I've got no stomach for them after all." She turned, then paused, her back to them, the lantern raising her shadow huge against the pines. "Gideon. The Ferrow girl. When this is over—if it's ever over—put her in a bone. Tell them what she looked like when she wasn't afraid. That's the price. That's what I'm buying with this. Promise me."

"I promise," Gideon said. "On my hands. On the gift."

"Good." A breath. "Then we're settled, and I was never good at lingering."

She walked into the trees, south, toward the pass, toward the horns and the picks and the man with the relic that heard the dead, and the snow took her footprints almost immediately, as though the mountain were already helping her keep her secret. Gideon listened—for footsteps, for the gift's faint tide, for anything—but the night offered only wind, and the distant ring of sappers' hammers, and Pip's small hand finding theirs in the dark.

"She's going to be loud," Pip said. "When she dies. Louder than anyone. Even Crane will hear her."

"Yes," Gideon said, and found their eyes were wet, and did not wipe them. "I think that's the point."

They climbed.

The shepherd's track was exactly as cruel as promised—a shelf of stone stitched to the mountainside, ice-glazed, barely wide enough for Maren's boots with the child strapped to her back. Below them the valley filled with moon-shadow; above, the stars wheeled, indifferent and enormous. Gideon climbed with the little bone figure in their fist, its lantern-woman pressing into their palm like a handhold, and did not let themselves look south.

Halfway down, in the black hour before dawn, the wind shifted and carried up from the pass a sound they had been dreading and expecting:

Horns. Many horns. And beneath them, thin with distance but unmistakable, a woman's voice raised high and clear and steady, calling out across the ice—calling Crane's name, calling him east, calling him away—

Calling him a liar, beautifully, with a smuggler's perfect timing.

Odile was buying them hours. Odile was spending herself like coin.

Gideon gripped the carving, whispered *balance, balance*, and kept climbing—down toward the chapel ruin, down toward the valley, down toward the tower that stood in two worlds at once, while behind them the horns changed direction, and the hunt swung east into the empty white, chasing a lie told by a dying woman who had finally, at the very end, kept her books honest.
