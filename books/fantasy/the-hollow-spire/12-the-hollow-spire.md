## 12. The Hollow Spire

The spire appeared the way Pip's dreams did: all at once, and only for them.

They had been climbing since dawn, up a goat-track that wound through black pines, Maren limping in front with her arm strapped to her chest and Ashfall dragging himself along behind like a landslide that had learned patience. The dragon's scales had gone the grey of old ash. Every few hundred paces he stopped, and his breath came out in long sighs that smelled of cold hearths, and Gideon would wait without being asked, because that was the kind of thing Gideon did now, had maybe always done.

Then the trees ended, and there it was.

A tower on the shoulder of the mountain, half-swallowed by ruin. Its western face was broken open, ribs of stone jutting into the sky like the carcass of something enormous. Moss hung off it in green ropes. Birds nested in the wounds. Anyone else would have seen a dead thing, another casualty of some old war the crown didn't talk about.

Pip saw the other half.

The eastern face rose whole and unbroken, pale as milk-glass, veined with lines of gold that caught the afternoon light and held it. Windows arched in shapes Pip knew—knew the way they knew their own hands, though they had never seen them before. A crown-work of slender pinnacles ringed the top, each one carved with a spiral that turned the opposite way from the one below it. And along the base, where the ruined side showed only rubble, the intact side showed doors. Tall ones, inlaid with copper script that moved, slowly, like fish under ice.

"That's it," Pip said. Their voice came out small. "That's Vael."

Maren looked where Pip was looking and saw, presumably, rocks. "It's a ruin, little one."

"It's not." Pip's chest hurt. Not the bad kind of hurt—the other kind, the homesick kind, the ache they'd carried since before they could remember carrying anything. "The broken part is broken here. The rest of it isn't broken anywhere."

Gideon crouched down beside them. Up close, their eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Show me," they said quietly. "Tell me what you see. All of it."

So Pip did. The golden veins, which were roads, or rivers, or both—Vael hadn't needed to know the difference. The spirals, which were the count of years. The copper script, which was a list of names, ten thousand of them, and Pip could read every single one even though the letters were letters Pip had never learned. They read six names out loud before their throat closed, and Gideon put a hand on their shoulder and said nothing at all, which was somehow better than any comfort.

Ashfall lowered his great head until his snout nearly touched the ground beside Pip. One ancient eye, amber going dim, regarded the tower.

"So it stands," he rumbled. His voice was wind through a burnt forest. "I wondered, in the long dark, if I had dreamed it too."

"You were *there*?" Pip asked.

"I was its last wall." The dragon's exhale stirred the moss. "When the crown came over the mountains with fire that eats fire, I stood at the gates of Vael and burned until there was nothing left in me to burn. Then they took my flame away and called it mercy, and erased the country I died for, and told the world it had never been." His eye closed. "But memory is a stubborn animal, little one. It burrows. It waits. It grows teeth."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the tower because there was nowhere better and no time to find it. Maren built a fire with her good hand and grumbled about the elevation, and Gideon sat close to Pip, closer than usual, watching the intact half of the spire with an expression Pip couldn't read.

"You can really see inside it?" Pip asked. "The other world?"

"No." Gideon turned a carving knife over in their fingers—a habit, Pip had learned, the way other people bit their nails. "To me it's just stone. Beautiful stone, maybe. But stone doesn't speak to me here, and stone always speaks to me somewhere." A pause. "You're the only door we have left, Pip."

Pip pulled their knees up to their chin. "What if I'm not? What if I'm wrong? What if I just—" They gestured helplessly at their own head. "Made it up. Made all of it up. Maps of a place that never existed, because nobody wanted me anywhere that did."

Gideon was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was rough. "I've heard a thousand dead voices, Pip. Every one of them real. Do you know how I can tell?"

Pip shook their head.

"Because they don't care what I want to hear. Liars flatter. The dead don't bother." Gideon nodded at the tower. "Whatever's in you, it isn't a story you'd invent. It's too heavy. Stories people invent are light. They carry easy." They smiled, crooked and tired. "You've been carrying yours your whole life. Nobody invents that."

Before Pip could answer, the ember-stone warmed against their chest.

Not hot—it never got truly hot, it just never cooled, a coin of stubborn heat they'd worn so long they'd forgotten what cold skin underneath felt like. But now it *pulsed*, once, twice, like a heart remembering its work. And the copper script on the tower doors began to move faster, fish darting under ice, ice thinning—

"Pip," Gideon said sharply. "Your stone."

"I feel it."

Ashfall raised his head. For the first time in days there was something in his face besides exhaustion. "*Go,*" he said. "Touch the doors. Now. While it wakes."

* * *

The doors were warm.

Not ember-warm—alive-warm, the warmth of a hand pressed to a forehead, of bread, of a body sleeping next to you. Pip laid both palms flat against the copper and the script swam beneath their fingers, rearranging itself, and every name it had shown them slid aside to make room for one more, and the new name was written in letters that glowed.

The new name was Pip's.

Not the name the foundling-house gave them. Not the name the smugglers used. The name underneath all of those, the one Pip had sometimes said to themselves at night like a secret prayer to a god they couldn't name, certain it was nothing, certain it was just a sound they liked. It was on the door. It was the last name on the door. As if the door had been waiting forty years to finish its list, and Pip was the ending of it.

"Oh," Pip said. Just that. Just *oh.*

And then the walls began to remember.

Light poured from the intact face of the tower—not firelight, not sunlight, but something older and gentler, lamplight the color of honey, and inside the honey-light were shapes. People. Moving slow as smoke, walking streets that weren't there, hanging washing between windows that existed only in the other world, and every one of them was translucent and golden and utterly, unmistakably *real.*

"Maren," Pip whispered. "Maren, look—"

"I see it." Maren's voice was strange. She had gone very still, her hand on her sword hilt, staring at the empty air above the rubble with an expression Pip had only ever seen her wear at gravesides. "Saints' bones. I see it. I thought—I thought only you could—"

"The stone," Gideon breathed. "It's showing us. It's letting the tower show us."

The echoes thickened. Sounds came with them now: a market bell, laughter, someone singing a song in a language Pip had never learned but understood completely, the way you understand your own heartbeat. Children ran through the honey-light chasing a ball that wasn't there. An old woman leaned from a window and shook out a rug, and the rug was there, and the window was there, and for one impossible moment the ruin and the memory were the same place and Pip stood in both.

Home.

Not a word anymore. A *place.* Cobbled streets sloping down to a harbor full of white-sailed ships. Terraced gardens climbing the hills, bright with flowers Pip knew the names of—*sorrowbright, duskbell, queen's forgiveness*—names no one alive had spoken in forty years except Pip, alone, to no one. A palace with the same spirals as the pinnacles, turning and counter-turning against a sky that held two moons instead of one.

Pip was crying. They didn't remember starting. The tears came the way the memories did, from somewhere older than knowing, and they didn't wipe them away because wiping away would mean admitting this was a dream, and it wasn't, it wasn't, it had never been a dream—

"Pip." Gideon knelt beside them. Their own face was wet; Pip hadn't seen that before either. "Talk to me."

"I lived here." Pip's voice broke on it. "Or—I would have. If they hadn't—if it hadn't—" They pressed harder against the door. The copper hummed under their palms. "Everyone says I'm nobody from nowhere. Foundling, orphan, stray. But I'm not. I'm *from here.* I remember the smell of the harbor. How do I remember the *smell*, Gideon? I was never born. My country died before I was born and I still remember the *smell of it—*"

"Because you're not from nowhere." Gideon took Pip's hands from the door and held them, both of them, calloused carver's fingers gentle around small ones. "Listen to me. You're not from nowhere. You're the last place it exists. Everything they burned, everything they crossed off the maps—it's all still alive, right here, in you. You're not the orphan of Vael, Pip. You *are* Vael. The last living piece of it."

The echoes swirled around them. The singing woman passed so close that her golden light brushed Pip's cheek like a hand, and Pip felt it, actually felt it, warm and brief as a blessing.

And then the echoes changed.

The market bell rang wrong—fast, panicked, over and over. The laughter cut off. The lamplight curdled toward red, and the shapes in it began to run, and the sky with its two moons filled with smoke, and from everywhere and nowhere came a sound like the world tearing along a seam.

Ashfall made a noise Pip had never heard a living creature make. Low. Endless. Grief with nowhere to go.

"The last day," he said. "It shows the last day."

The golden people were running toward the gates now, toward the shape of a dragon rearing against the burning sky, and the copper script on the doors was moving faster and faster, names scrolling past, thousands of them, and Pip suddenly understood what the list was. Understood what the doors had been waiting to finish.

It wasn't a census.

It was a tally of the dead.

The last name glowed steady under Pip's fingers. Their name. Waiting. Forty years waiting for someone to come home and claim it, and now that they had, the door wanted to know—

The script shifted. Formed a question, in letters of light, in words Pip read without trying:

*Will you carry them?*

Behind them, Gideon drew a sharp breath. "Pip. What does it say?"

Pip looked at the question glowing on the door. Looked back at Gideon, at Maren with her wounded arm and her haunted eyes, at Ashfall guttering like the last candle in a dark room. Somewhere down the mountain, days away but coming, witch-hunters were climbing toward this place with fire that ate fire.

Pip pressed their palm flat over their own name and answered the only way that mattered.

"Yes."

The doors began, slowly, silently, to open—and deep beneath the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep, and every bone in Pip's body knew that opening this door was going to cost them all far more than they had brought to pay.
