## 13. The Mother's Voice

The Spire did not echo. That was the first thing Gideon understood, standing in the entrance hall with dust sifting down through shafts of impossible light. Echoes were the sound of a place forgetting itself, throwing voices back thinner and thinner until they died. The Spire did the opposite. It gathered every sound and kept it.

Gideon had spent thirty years learning to live with the dead murmuring at the edges of their mind — the femur of a drowned fishwife still humming her wedding song, a soldier's knucklebone reciting drill orders into eternity, the small yellowed teeth of infants that whispered nothing but warmth. They had built walls around all of it, brick by brick, the way a man living beside a river builds levees. You never stopped the water. You only decided where it was allowed to go.

Inside the Hollow Spire, there were no levees. There was only the river.

It struck them the moment they crossed the threshold — not sound exactly, not even voice, but *pressure*, the way deep water presses on the ears. Ten thousand years of kept things. Footsteps on stairs that no longer existed. Bread baking in an oven whose stones had been carried away as trophies. A child laughing on a balcony, and the specific creak of the balcony rail under small hands, preserved like a fly in amber. And underneath all of it, woven through it like thread through cloth, a language Gideon had never learned and somehow knew: Vaelish, the tongue of a country that had been unmade, speaking itself endlessly to no one.

"Gideon." Maren's hand on their shoulder, gauntlet cold even through the sleeve. "You've gone white as chalk. What do you hear?"

"Everything." Gideon's own voice sounded far away, thin, a coin dropped down a well. "Everyone. They're not gone, Maren. They were never gone. The Spire has been holding them for forty years like breath held in the chest."

Pip ran ahead, boots slapping stone, and where the child passed the walls *answered* — faint gold light rising in carved channels, following Pip like minnows following a lantern. To Gideon the tower was half-ruin, roofless in places, its grand stair collapsed into rubble they had to climb carefully. But Pip moved through it as though it were whole, turning left at corridors that ended in broken air, waving at doorways that opened onto nothing.

"It's the kitchens," Pip called back, glowing. "The ovens are this way. Ashfall could sleep in the great hearth if she wanted, it's big enough for three oxen—"

Ashfall said nothing. The dragon lay curled in the outer courtyard, too vast now to enter the Spire at all, her scales dull as old pewter, one eye half-open and fixed on the tower with an expression Gideon could not name and did not want to. Longing, perhaps. Or grief so old it had worn smooth, like a river stone.

Maren stayed near the doors — old habit, the soldier in her refusing to be caught without an exit — and Gideon went alone up what remained of the stair, because something was pulling them. Not Pip's laughter, though that drifted down bright and heartbreaking from above. Something else. A single thread pulled out of the weave, and the more they climbed, the more it tightened.

They knew the thread. They had known it all their life, the way you know a scar.

"Mother," Gideon said to the empty stair, and the Spire took the word and gave it back not as echo but as *invitation*.

* * *

She found them — it found them — in a chamber on the fourth landing, a room whose ceiling had fallen open to the sky, so that evening light fell in a long pale column across the floor. The floor was the thing. The whole floor was bone.

Not scattered bones. Worked ones. Panels of it, ivory-pale and honey-dark, fitted together in patterns Gideon's fingers knew before their eyes did: the interlocking spirals of Vaelish carving, the grammar their own hands spoke when they weren't thinking, the style the smugglers of Thornfell called *grave-script* and paid well for and never asked about. This was a masterwork. This was a confession written the only way a carver writes — cut into the dead so the dead can carry it.

And it was signed in the lower corner with a mark Gideon had last seen on a wooden comb, hidden in a child's bundle forty years ago: a wren in flight, wings folded mid-beat.

Gideon knelt. Their knees hit the bone panels and the chamber *woke*.

It came up through their palms where they braced against the floor, up through their shins, their spine, their teeth — a voice, a woman's voice, low and roughened by years of smoke and road-dust, and Gideon knew it the way you know your own heartbeat: without thinking, without wanting, without mercy.

*If anyone finds this, let it be my child. Let it be no one. Let the bone keep it if I cannot say it aloud.*

Gideon's breath went ragged. Forty years. They had rehearsed this meeting ten thousand times in the dark behind their eyelids — the confrontation, the question, the flinging of every bitter word like stones. *Why did you leave me in that alley in Lowmark with a blanket and a carved whistle? Why did you look at me that last morning like I was already a ghost? Why did you run faster from your own daughter than from the hunters at your heels?*

None of the rehearsals had included this: the sheer gentleness of the voice. She spoke the way she used to speak when Gideon was feverish as a small child, close to the ear, unhurried, as though there were all the time in the world and none of it belonged to fear.

*I am Ilse Sandoval. I was born Ilse vaen Corath, in the city of Vael, in the year the bells sang for the comet. I am writing this in the last place I will ever carve, and I am writing it badly, because my hands shake and because some truths resist the knife.*

Gideon looked down and saw it — saw the tremor in the lines, the wavering depth of the cuts, a master carver's hands failing her. Their own hands. Their own tremor, when the grief came too big for the levees.

*You must understand what Vael was, because no one left alive will tell you and the maps have been burned. Vael was not conquered. Vael was sold.*

The words landed like a blade between the ribs. Above, distantly, Pip laughed at something, and the sound fell through the broken ceiling like light.

*The crown came to our council not with armies but with ledgers. Our harbors, they said. Our dragon-roads, our fire-rights, our grain. And when the elders refused — when old Speaker Maerin told the crown's envoy that Vael's fire was not theirs to sell — the envoy smiled, and within the month the hunters came. Not for us. For the gifted. Because a country full of people who can hear their own dead is a country that cannot be lied to about what happens to rebels.*

Gideon's hands curled against the bone. They thought of Crane, of the Purge notices nailed to tavern doors, of the pyres in Gravenhollow. Forty years, then. The wheel had been turning since before they were born. They had simply been too small, too buried in the warrens, too busy surviving, to see what it was grinding toward.

*I had the gift. My mother had it, and hers. When the purges began we fled east with nothing — I carried you, Gideon, you were four, you slept through the crossing of the Serra ford with your cheek against my neck and I memorized the weight of you because I knew — I knew even then — that memory might be all I would ever keep.*

The column of evening light had gone gold, then copper. Somewhere below, Maren's boots paced the entrance hall in slow sentry arcs. Gideon did not call out. They could not have spoken if the Spire itself had asked them to.

*They caught us outside Thornfell. Two hunters and a leashed hound that could smell the gift the way dogs smell blood. And the hunter — his name was Verren, and I have cursed his name for forty years and I curse it now — he looked at you. At a four-year-old asleep on my shoulder. And he said the words I have never been able to carve until this moment:* children carry it loudest.

*He meant he would take us both to the pyre. He meant it almost kindly. That is the worst of it, little bird — the men who burn us are mostly kind men doing arithmetic. One gifted woman, one gifted child, two pyres, or —*

The carving broke here. Not faded — *broken*, a gouge driven straight through several lines by a point pressed hard enough to crack the panel, and Gideon could see the shape of the moment in the wound: her hand slamming down, her whole body refusing the sentence. Then, after, smaller marks. Slower. The carver returning to the work because the work was the only door out of the room she was in.

*There was another way. There is always another way; that is the cruelty of the gift, that you always see it. If I ran loud — if I made myself a prize worth chasing — they would follow me and not you. A grown gifted woman, unbound, unclaimed, running east toward the mountains where the old stories said dragons still lived: that was a hunt worth riding. A child in a blanket by the side of the road in Lowmark was not. A child was a loose thread. They would have searched a day, maybe two, and moved on.*

*So I kissed your forehead while you slept and I put the whistle in your blanket — I carved it that morning, wren-marked, so that someday, if you ever learned to read bone, you would know whose hands had made it — and I stood up and I ran, and I made sure they saw me run.*

Gideon made a sound they had never made before. It was not crying. It was older than crying — the noise a beam makes when a load it has carried for decades finally comes off it, the groan of wood remembering its own weight. Maren's pacing stopped below. Boots on the stair, quick, then stopping at the sound of Gideon's name, once, twice, unanswered.

*I led them three weeks through the hill country,* the voice went on, softer now, the trembling deeper. *I let them see me at ridgelines. I left gift-marked carvings in wayside shrines like breadcrumbs, each one louder than the last, each one a lie telling them the great gifted fugitive of Vael was here, was there, was always just ahead. And when they finally cornered me at the Grey Falls — when I had bought you twenty-three days and a head start into a city of ten thousand hiding places — I stood on the falls' edge and I told Verren that the gift dies with the body but lives in the bone, and that I would leave him such a relic that he would hear Vael screaming in his ears for the rest of his life.*

*Then I jumped.*

The chamber went very quiet. Even the Spire seemed to hold its kept breath.

*The water did not kill me. I wish I could tell you I chose to survive out of courage. It was cowardice, plain and simple — my body would not stop swimming. But I crawled out downstream with cracked ribs and I could not go back, because going back would undo everything, and I could not go forward, because forward was a country where my name was already on pyre-lists. So I became no one. I carved for smugglers under other names. I told myself every year: next year she'll be grown, next year she'll be safe, next year I'll find a way. And the years got shorter and my hands got worse and shame is a patient jailer — it asks for so little, only that you stay where you are.*

*Gideon. Little bird. If you are reading this, then you learned the gift anyway, despite everything, despite the danger of it, and I am proud past all measure and afraid past all endurance. And if I am dead — and I am dead, the stillness of my own voice tells me so — then know this one thing and let it be the load-bearing wall of whatever you build from me:*

*I did not leave you. I spent myself buying you. Every day of silence since has been the price, and I would pay it again, and again, and again, however many times the Spire replays me. That is what mothers are for, in Vael. We are the country's memory when the maps burn. Someone has to hold the shape of love against the dark, or the dark gets to define everyone.*

The last lines were shallow, barely scored, the work of hands that had almost nothing left:

*My hands are done. The wren flies home. Tell my child the bells sang for the comet the year I was born, and that I heard them, and that I believed — my whole life, I believed — that something of Vael would sing again.*

Below, Maren was calling Gideon's name with real alarm now, and heavy wingbeats thudded in the courtyard — Ashfall stirring, the ember-stone's glow bleeding red up through the broken ceiling like a wound in the sky.

Gideon knelt on their mother's confession a long time, forehead pressed to the bone, letting forty years of bitterness dissolve into something that hurt worse and healed deeper. All those nights hating her. All those careful walls built against her name. She had run *toward* the hunters. She had spent herself like a coin thrown into dark water, buying twenty-three days for a sleeping child, and had drowned slowly for forty years rather than surface and spend that purchase.

"I hear you," Gideon whispered to the floor, in Vaelish, a language they had never been taught. "I'm listening."

The Spire, which kept everything, kept that too.

When Gideon finally rose, their legs shook, and their face was wet, and none of it mattered, because from the top of the tower Pip was shouting — high, cracking, terrified — and the light in the walls had turned from gold to a flickering, guttering orange, and Ashfall's roar rolled up through the broken floors like the mountain itself clearing its throat.

Something was wrong with the dragon. Something was wrong with the fire.

And the Spire's walls, all at once, began replaying the sound of Vael's last bells.
