## 15. Halvard Crane Catches Up

The road to the Hollow Spire was not a road. It was a scar.

Halvard Crane rode it at the head of twelve men, and the mountain seemed to know him, the way a body knows the knife that has cut it before. Frost clung to the black stone in patterns that were almost script, almost language, and his relic—the reliquary pendant he wore against his sternum, warm as a swallowed coal—hummed with everything the ice remembered. He had learned, over forty years of hunting, to read the land the way other men read scripture. The land always confessed first.

He had been confessing too, though he would have denied it under oath. For three nights now, in the thin hours before dawn when even witch-hunters slept badly, he had heard her. Not in the relic. In himself. His sister Liss's voice, the one he had silenced eleven years ago in a brazier outside Carrowmere, had begun speaking without any bone or stone to carry it. She said the same thing every time, patient as frost forming: *You burned my voice, Halvard. You did not burn me.*

He told himself it was exhaustion. He told himself it was the altitude. He was an Inquisitor of the Crown, second only to High Inquisitor Vane, and he did not believe in hauntings that came from inside. Hauntings required vessels. That was the whole architecture of his faith: the gift was a disease of objects, and objects could be cleansed. Fire was clean. Fire left ash, and ash held nothing.

He had built his life on the certainty that ash held nothing.

* * *

They made camp in the throat of the pass, where the Serrated Mountains narrowed to a defile wide enough for two horses and narrow enough for ambush. Crane did not sleep. He sat by the fire with his ledger open—the great leather book in which he recorded every gift he had extinguished, every relic consigned to flame, every name crossed out in ink the color of dried blood. Eleven hundred and six entries. He knew the number the way a priest knows his psalms.

Entry nine hundred and forty-one: *Gideon Sandoval, bone-carver, Lowmark. Gift confirmed. Relics destroyed in absentia. Subject fled north.*

He had never actually heard Sandoval carve. But Odile, the fence, had described it before she sold them all—described it with something like reverence, which should have been warning enough. *She sits with the dead the way a midwife sits with the dying,* Odile had said, back in Gravenhollow, before she traded the information for her own amnesty. *She doesn't take their voices. She keeps them. There's a difference, Inquisitor. I've watched you burn things. I don't think you know the difference.*

He had put her words out of his mind. Now, in the firelight, they returned with the persistence of a splinter working toward the skin's surface.

His lieutenant, Brannoc, a heavy man with a face like a closed door, brought him tea laced with something stronger and sat down across the fire without being asked. Brannoc had been with him nine years. Brannoc had watched him burn relics in four provinces and had never once asked what Crane heard while the smoke rose.

"Can't sleep either?" Brannoc said.

"I sleep fine."

"You're sitting up."

"I'm praying."

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment, then away. "Inquisitor. If you don't mind my saying. The men are talking."

"The men talk constantly. It's why they're men and not soldiers."

"They're saying the Spire is haunted. Properly haunted. Not gifted-haunted—haunted. They're saying the whole valley down there hums at night. That birds won't cross it. That old Tallow, who scouted ahead, came back white as milk and wouldn't say why except that he heard singing, hundreds of voices, under the ground."

Crane turned a page of his ledger. "Superstition."

"Aye." Brannoc stared into the fire. "Probably."

Crane heard it then, faint beneath the crackle of the flames—not through the relic, which lay warm against his chest, but through the ground itself, through the bones of the mountain. Singing. Or something like singing. A sound with the texture of many throats, rising and falling in a cadence no living choir would use, because living choirs breathed, and this sound did not breathe. It simply continued, the way a river continues, the way grief continues.

He closed the ledger.

"Inquisitor?"

"We move at first light," Crane said. "Full pace. We take the Spire before midday, before whatever sleeps down there finishes waking."

"And if it's already awake?"

Crane did not answer. It was the first question in nine years that Brannoc had asked him that he could not.

* * *

First light came gray and slow, the sun rising behind the eastern peaks so that the whole pass lay in shadow until nearly mid-morning. They descended in column, twelve riders and a wagon carrying the reliquary chest—three hundred confiscated relics, bones and stones and rings of hair, each one tagged, each one catalogued, each one waiting for the pyre Crane had promised them since Carrowmere.

The humming grew as they descended. By the time the trees thickened into ancient pines bent sideways by centuries of wind, the horses were refusing it. Not spooking—refusing. They stopped, tossed their heads, and stood, and no amount of spur or rein moved them forward past a certain line of moss-covered stones that might once, forty years ago, have been a boundary marker.

Dismounted, then. Crane gave the order without pleasure. Men who walked into a haunting walked slower, and slower men thought more, and thinking was the enemy of everything he had built.

But he was thinking anyway. He could feel it happening, the way you feel rain coming in an old wound. The relic against his chest had gone strange—not warmer, but *denser*, as if the voices inside it were pressing outward against their containment, as if three hundred silenced souls had discovered, all at once, that there was something near enough to hear them. Something vast. Something that answered.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing he could not explain, the thing that had been growing in him since the pass, the thing that made his hands shake on the reins this morning for the first time since his novitiate:

The dead down there were answering each other.

Not echoing. Answering. When the garrison dead sang their death-cadence, something replied—a voice older, deeper, in a tongue his relic could translate only in fragments, and between the fragments came silences shaped exactly like the pauses Liss used to leave when she wanted him to say the thing he was too proud to say. He knew those silences. He had grown up inside them. And now they were arriving from a country that officially did not exist, carried on the breath of people who had been dead for forty years, and they were addressing him personally, or so it felt, or so his cracking certainty insisted, and he could not make it stop.

*You burned my voice,* Liss said again, last night, in the dark of his tent. *You did not burn me. I am still here. I have always been here. You just stopped listening.*

He had built an entire theology on the premise that listening was the sin. That the gift was a corruption, that hearing the dead was how madness entered the world, that silence was safety, that fire was mercy because it ended the temptation. He had burned his own sister's femur with his own hands and called it love. He had gone home to his father afterward and wept, and his father had said, *You did the right thing, Halvard. The hard thing is the right thing,* and Crane had believed him for eleven years because believing was easier than examining what the burning had actually done—which was nothing, which changed nothing, which silenced a vessel and left the voice intact somewhere he refused to look.

Now he was looking. The Spire was forcing him to look.

* * *

They saw it at noon.

It rose out of the valley like a broken tooth—half ruin, half impossible wholeness, its upper reaches dissolving into a haze that was not mist. Brannoc, who had served in three wars and claimed to be immune to wonder, reined in and stared for a full minute without speaking. The intact half of the tower was not ruined at all; it was pristine, its pale stone unweathered, its windows glowing faintly with a light that had no source. It stood in both worlds at once—that was what the smugglers' stories said, what the erased maps' margins whispered—and looking at it felt like looking at a wound in the fabric of agreed reality.

And the sound. The sound was everywhere now. Under the pines, under the snow, under their boots. Hundreds of voices. Thousands. A nation's worth, singing itself back into existence, and threaded through it—Crane would have sworn this on any relic he'd ever burned—threaded through it was a voice he knew.

Liss's voice. Not as it had been in the relic, that small dry whisper trapped in bone. As it had been when she was alive. Full. Warm. Annoyed with him in the particular way only a younger sister could be annoyed, fond underneath the annoyance, *present*. It moved through the flood of Vael's dead like a single candle flame moving through a hall of candles, distinguishable, unmistakable, and it was saying something new now, something it had never said in eleven years of nightly visitation:

*Come closer, Halvard. I'm not the only one in here. Come and see what you've been burning all these years.*

His hand went to the pendant. He pressed it, as he had pressed it ten thousand times, to hear the confiscated dead—to confirm identities, to extract confessions, to build cases. The relic opened, and instead of three hundred scattered whispers he received a single sound: a chorus, coherent, organized, *aware of him*, turning toward him the way a congregation turns toward a latecomer.

They knew his name.

That was not possible. The dead of Vael had died forty years ago, in a purge conducted before Crane took his vows, in a kingdom he had never seen, among people whose names had been scraped from every record in the realm. They could not know him. And yet the chorus parted around a single voice—an old man's voice, cracked and dignified—and the old man said, in accented but clear Common Tongue:

*Inquisitor. You carry our stolen ones in that box on your chest. Three hundred of us, taken from our children's graves, from our wives' hair, from our own fingers. We do not blame you. We blame the crown that taught you burning was holiness. But we ask you—one question, and answer honestly, because we will know if you lie:*

*When you burned your sister's bone, did the silence feel like peace?*

Crane stood in the trampled snow with his men watching him and could not answer.

Because the honest answer was no. The honest answer was that the silence had felt like an amputation. That he had gone home and slept eight hours and woken screaming. That he had burned eleven hundred more since, and every fire had been an attempt to recreate the first silence, and every fire had failed, because the silence was never peace—it was a room with the furniture removed, and he had been living in that room for eleven years, calling the emptiness righteousness.

Brannoc shifted behind him. "Inquisitor? Orders?"

"Hold here," Crane said. "I'm going forward alone."

"That's not protocol."

"No," Crane agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

He walked down through the pines alone, and the voices rose around him like water rising in a lock, and by the time he reached the flat approach before the Spire gates he was drowning in it, willingly, the way a man drowning in memory stops struggling because the memory is of swimming.

The gates were already occupied.

Sandoval stood at the center—thin, gray-faced, hollow-eyed, a carver's hands hanging at their sides like instruments set down mid-use. To their left, a woman in battered armor without insignia, sword drawn, stance low: the disgraced knight, Kestrel, whom his files listed as deserter, whose commander's voice he had heard once in a confiscated ring and catalogued without feeling. To their right, a child. Eight years old, maybe. Small face, enormous eyes, and in the child's cupped hands an ember-stone that glowed like a captive sunrise.

And behind them all, coiled in the shadow of the gate arch, a dragon. Dying. Wings folded like a tent about to collapse. Eyes like banked furnaces, regarding him with an expression Crane had never seen in any creature, human or otherwise: pity.

"So," Crane said. His voice came out wrong, hoarse, smaller than he intended. "Gideon Sandoval."

"Gideon Sandoval," they agreed. Their voice was steady. Whatever ritual they were preparing—or had begun, or were mid-way through, he could feel it in his teeth, a pressure like a struck bell still ringing—they were not going to run. "Inquisitor Crane. You're late. The dead have been expecting you."

"The dead don't expect. The dead are nothing. That is the finding of the Holy Office, and I am its servant."

"Are you?" Sandoval tilted their head. "Then why are your hands shaking?"

He looked down. They were shaking. Both of them, fine tremors, a carver's tremor in a hunter's hands, and he could not stop them, and Kestrel saw it, and the dragon saw it, and worst of all the child saw it, and the child did not look afraid. The child looked *interested*. The way children look at adults who are breaking, with that terrible clear attention that has no cruelty in it at all.

"You hear them," Pip said. Not accusing. Just stating it, the way a child states weather. "Through the wall. You hear them right now, don't you. What are they saying?"

Crane opened his mouth to say *nothing*, and the word would not come, because the walls were saying very much indeed, and some treacherous part of him wanted to say it aloud, wanted it the way a starving man wants bread, wanted to finally, after eleven years, tell someone what he heard.

*Tell them,* said Liss, from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone and the snow and the space behind his eyes. *Tell them what you heard in my bone, brother. Tell them I forgave you before the smoke cleared. Tell them that's the part you've never forgiven yourself for.*

"He hears them," Gideon said quietly, to the knight, not taking their eyes off Crane. "He's always heard them. That's why the burning works on him. You can't hate a thing unless it speaks to you."

"Enough." Crane raised the relic on its chain, the gesture of authority, the gesture he had performed at a thousand arrests, and for the first time in his career the gesture failed him—because raising the pendant meant opening it, meant listening, meant receiving the chorus, and he was already receiving it, had been receiving it since the pass, and the chorus was not evidence anymore. It was testimony. It was a nation standing in the dock and refusing to be silent.

He spoke anyway, because he was still, in the wreckage of everything, an officer with orders. "By authority of the Crown and the Holy Office of the Purge, I place you all under arrest for the crime of retained gifts. Surrender the child. Surrender the stone. Surrender the carver. Do this and the knight and the dragon may yet—" and here his voice did something unprecedented, something it had never done in front of his men, something that made Brannoc, watching from the tree line with field glasses, later swear the air itself had gone wrong—his voice *hesitated*. Mid-sentence. Over the word *may*. Because the sentence continued in his mind, in the template of a thousand identical proclamations, toward *may yet be granted clean deaths*, and for the first time in eleven years the phrase arrived in his mouth tasting exactly like what it was.

Kestrel laughed. Short, harsh, disbelieving. "'Clean deaths.' Gods. You even burn the sentence."

The dragon exhaled, a long slow plume of gray smoke that smelled of cinders and old libraries. "Young hunter," Ashfall said, in a voice like tectonic plates conversing, "we have watched your kind for forty years. We watched the first purge. We watched the maps burned. We watched the names scraped from the stones of our own mountain. We know what you are. But we do not know, yet, what you will choose to be in the next hour. Neither, I think, do you."

Crane stood in the snow between his faith and his sister, and the snow was falling softly, and the chorus was rising, and Liss was saying—I forgive you, I forgive you, I have always forgiven you, the burning was never the sin, the lying to yourself afterward was the sin—

And then the child stepped forward.

Pip walked out past Kestrel's outstretched arm, past the dragon's shadow, straight up to Crane, close enough that he could see the ember-light reflected in the child's eyes, and held up the stone in both small hands as if offering it to him. Offering. Not surrendering. There was a difference, and the child knew it, and the difference was everything.

"This is the last piece of Vael," Pip said. "If you take it, you have to hold it. That's the rule. Anything you take, you have to hold. So hold it." The child pushed it gently against his breastbone, next to the pendant, and the warmth of it sank through his coat, through his shirt, into the skin above his heart, and the stone began—softly, terribly—to speak.

It spoke in the voice of everyone at once. Ten thousand throats. A country. And inside the country, unmistakable, a girl of nineteen laughing at her brother for flinching at thunder, and asking him to hold her hand anyway, and telling him that being afraid together was better than being brave alone, and saying, in the exact cadence of a memory he had spent a decade trying to incinerate:

*Halvard. Whatever you do next—do it knowing I'm real.*

The relic against his chest went quiet. All three hundred confiscated voices, silent, waiting, the way a courtroom waits.

Crane looked down at the child. At the stone. At his own shaking hands, which had burned eleven hundred and six voices and had never once, not once, held anything the way this child was holding this stone—as if a thing could be loved and lost at the same time and both facts could remain true.

"Stand down," he said.

Behind him, from the tree line, Brannoc's voice, sharp with alarm: "Inquisitor?"

"I said stand down. All of you. Drop the torches. Sheathe—" But the order died in his throat, because it was already too late, because Vane's reinforcements had been a day behind him all along, because the sound of a second column was already coming up the pass, drums and steel and the smell of pitch, and Crane understood, with the clarity of a man watching the tide erase his footprints, that his doubt had arrived exactly one army too late to save anyone by merely existing.

The drums grew louder. The child's hand was still pressed against his chest. The chorus swelled toward some final cadence, and Gideon Sandoval, standing in the gate with gray lips and steady eyes, said the words that would end the world Crane had built or die trying:

"It's starting. Inquisitor—you can help us open it, or you can watch us try. But decide quickly. Your king's men won't wait, and neither will the dead."
