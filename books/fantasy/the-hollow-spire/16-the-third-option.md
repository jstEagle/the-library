## 16. The Third Option

The Spire hummed at night.

Gideon had noticed it the first evening, lying on the cold flagstones of the lower hall while Pip slept curled against Ashfall's flank like a kitten against a dying hearth. It was not sound, exactly. It was the memory of sound—the way a bell keeps ringing in the body long after the ear has given it up. The walls of the Hollow Spire were full of Vael, and Vael was full of voices, and the voices never entirely stopped. Even now, with the gift still theirs, Gideon lay awake and listened to a country murmur in its sleep.

They had come up to the Spire's middle chamber to argue. That was what Maren called it. Arguing. As if the thing between them were a dispute over rations, over which pass to take, over whether to trust Odile's map. Gideon sat on the steps of the broken dais with their knife across their knees, turning it over, feeling the worn bone of the handle where their thumb had polished a groove into it over twenty years of work. Maren stood at the arrow-slit window with her arms crossed, watching the dark valley below, where Crane's campfires had multiplied since dusk. Pip sat cross-legged on the floor, the ember-stone cupped in both hands, staring into it as if it were a well and something might climb out.

Ashfall lay in the great doorway, too large now for the hall, her wings folded like a tent fallen in on itself. Her scales had gone the grey of old ash. When she breathed, the breath smelled of cold chimneys.

"There has to be another way," Maren said, for the fourth time that night. "There is always another way."

"There isn't." Gideon said it quietly, and hated how quiet it came out. "You know there isn't."

"The pass north—"

"Is watched. Crane has it. Odile bought us a day, maybe two. One day, Maren. And the Door needs fire, and the fire needs the ember, and the ember needs Ashfall, and Ashfall is—" Gideon stopped. Looked at the dragon. Ashfall's eye, that great amber lamp, was half-lidded, watching them with the terrible patience of a creature that had already done all its grieving and was merely waiting for the arithmetic to finish.

"I heard what she said," Pip said, without looking up. "In the cave. She said the Door drinks fire. She said—" the child's voice caught, steadied, caught again, the way it always did when Pip was trying to be brave in the specific shape of an adult, "—she said it has to be all of it. Not a little. All of it."

"All of it means her," Maren said. "It means we burn her out like a candle and walk through the smoke and call it survival." Her jaw worked. "I deserted once because I wouldn't be part of a slaughter dressed up as an order. I'm not walking through that Door over her body."

"And I'm not letting Crane catch Pip," Gideon said. "Or you. Or her." They nodded at Ashfall. "So we need a third thing. And there is a third thing. There has always been a third thing. I just—" The knife turned in their hands. The bone handle was warm from their grip, and through the warmth, faint as a pulse under skin, Gideon could feel the Spire listening. "I didn't want to say it out loud, because saying it out loud makes it possible, and if it's possible then someone has to decide whether to do it."

Maren turned from the window. "Say it."

Gideon looked at Ashfall. "You knew," they said. "When you told us the Door drinks fire. You knew there was another way to feed it. You were hoping we wouldn't remember."

The dragon's breath went out of her, long and slow, stirring dust from the flagstones in little grey ghosts. "I hoped," Ashfall said, and her voice was a landslide heard from very far away, "that you would be too kind to think of it. Kindness is a kind of blindness, bone-carver. It is the only mercy I have left to offer anyone."

"Someone tell me," Pip said, standing up, clutching the stone to their chest. "Someone tell me right now or I'll—"

"Sit down, little map," Ashfall said gently, and Pip sat.

Gideon rose. Their knees ached. Everything ached; the Spire was full of dead and the dead leaned on the living without meaning to. They walked to the center of the chamber, where the floor still bore Vael's mosaic—a sun with a hole in it, a sun that had been deliberately defaced by someone who loved it, gouged out not by conquerors but by the last Vaeli themselves, so that the crown's iconoclasts would find nothing left to desecrate. Even their vandalism had been an act of love. Gideon thought about that more than they wanted to.

"The Door doesn't actually want fire," they said. "Fire is just the loudest kind of memory. That's what Ashfall told us in the cave. The Door opens to true memory—memory with weight in it, memory that costs something. A dragon's fire is forty years of witnessing, burned into heat. That's why it works. Not because it's fire. Because it's *true*, and it's *heavy*, and it's *hers*."

"Get to it," Maren said, but her voice had gone careful, the way it went careful before bad news.

"There's another kind of memory with weight in it." Gideon held up their hands. Turned them over. The carver's calluses, the old white scar across the left palm where a chisel had slipped when they were fourteen and their mother was still there to bind it. "A carver takes the dead into the bone so the bone can hold their voice. But the old texts—Odile had one, years ago, a thing she wouldn't sell, wouldn't even let me touch twice—the old texts say the first carvers didn't work in bone at all. They worked in *themselves*. The body is the first relic. The body can hold a voice. The body can hold a *country*."

The Spire hummed. Or maybe that was the silence after.

"You're saying," Maren said slowly, "you could carry Vael's memories. All of them. In your own—"

"Body. Mind. Whatever I am." Gideon's mouth was dry. "The Door doesn't care what vessel carries the memory through it. It cares that the memory is true and heavy and *paid for*. Ashfall's fire is paid for with her life. A carver's vessel is paid for with—" They made themselves say it. "With the gift. It burns the gift out completely. Every voice I've ever heard, gone. Every voice I *would* have heard, never. And the memories—" They laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "Ten thousand deaths, Maren. A whole kingdom's last day, all at once, in one skull. The texts say most carvers who tried it didn't come out the other side as anyone at all. Just a body that eats and sleeps and sometimes weeps without knowing why."

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time. The torch Maren had wedged in the wall bracket spat and guttered. Below them, in the valley, a dog barked in Crane's camp, thin and far away, and Gideon thought absurdly of the dogs of Lowmark, of the tavern, of the daughter who had listened to her mother's femur take up her story and had wept without shame. That life was ash now. Most lives were ash, if you waited long enough. The question had never been how to keep things from burning. The question was what you carried out of the fire.

"No," Maren said.

"You don't get to—"

"*No.*" She crossed the chamber in three strides and took Gideon by the shoulders, and her hands were shaking, which they almost never did. "I have watched you carry the dead since the day I met you. Every bone you carve, you carry a little of it with you—I've seen it, I've *seen* it, Gideon, you go grey after every carving, you eat less, you sleep worse, you— and you want to take in a *nation*? You want to pour a country through that—" she tapped Gideon's chest, hard, once, "—and think anything will be left? I buried a commander. I buried a whole company. I know what the dead cost the living, and I am not—" Her voice broke and she mastered it, the way she mastered everything, brutally, like breaking a horse. "I am not standing here again watching people I—watching people die for a country that's already dead."

"It's not dead," Pip said.

They all turned. Pip was standing again—Gideon hadn't seen the child rise—holding the ember-stone out at arm's length, and the stone was glowing, softly, steadily, the way it had glowed in the cave, the way it had glowed every time Vael came near.

"It's not dead," Pip said again, and their voice was furious and wet and eight years old. "It's *right here*. It's in the walls. It's in her." They jerked their chin at Ashfall. "It's in the stone. It's in *me*, maybe, I don't know, I remember things I never saw, I remember *bread*, I remember a street with a fountain, I remember somebody singing and I don't know who she was but I know the song, and everyone says Vael never existed and I *remember bread*, and if Gideon can carry it out of here so it's real somewhere, so somebody can carve it, so it's not just—" Pip's voice cracked clean through. "So it's not just me. I don't want to be the only one who remembers. I don't want to be the only one."

The ember-stone pulsed in the child's hands. And through the Spire, through the walls and the floor and the defaced sun in the mosaic, Gideon felt the dead of Vael go still—all of them at once, ten thousand voices falling silent the way a forest falls silent when something vast passes through it. Listening.

* * *

Gideon knelt down in front of Pip. Their joints protested. They ignored the protest; they were getting good at ignoring protests.

"Listen to me," they said. "I'm going to tell you the truth, because you're the only one in this room who always gets the truth. The truth is I'm scared. Not of dying. Carvers don't get to be scared of dying; we sit with it every day, we carve its voice into bone, it's an old neighbor. What I'm scared of is *forgetting*. I'm scared I'll walk through that Door and come out the other side as a body that eats and sleeps, and I won't remember your name, and Maren will say 'Gideon' and it'll mean nothing to me, and I won't remember the fountain street or the song. That's the price. Not the gift—I can live without the gift, maybe, I think, I hope. The price is *me*."

"Then don't," Pip whispered.

"But here's the other truth." Gideon took the child's hands, ember-stone and all, and folded their own around them. The stone was warm. It was always warm. It had been warm for forty years without a fire to feed it, and Gideon understood now that it was warm because it was *waiting*. Everything in Vael was waiting. "The other truth is that if we do nothing, Crane comes up that mountain tomorrow, and he burns the Spire, and he burns Ashfall, and he takes you, and every voice in these walls goes silent *forever*—not sleeping, not waiting, *gone*, the way he burns every relic he finds. Twice-silenced. And you'll be the only one who remembers bread, and you'll remember it alone, in a cell, or not at all."

Pip's chin trembled. "That's not fair."

"No," Gideon agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing true is."

"Gideon." Maren's voice behind them, low and ragged. "There has to be—"

"There doesn't." Gideon stood and turned to face her, and found that the terror had not gone away—it had settled, the way sediment settles in water, down to a clear cold layer at the bottom of them, and above it something else was forming, something that felt almost like calm. "You want a third option. This is the third option. It's not a good one. It's the only one where everyone walks out and no one is burned. Ashfall lives. Pip keeps their stone. You keep your—your second chance. And I keep—" they smiled, and it hurt, "—I keep whatever I keep. Maybe everything. Maybe nothing. The texts don't say. Nobody's ever done it with a whole country before."

"Nobody's ever *tried*," Maren said, "because it's madness—"

"The whole world is madness right now, Ser Kestrel. The crown burns children for remembering. A dragon is dying in a doorway. A kingdom is holding its breath in the walls. Madness stopped being a disqualification some time ago." Gideon looked past her, to the great doorway, to Ashfall. "You knew about this. Tell me the rest. All of it. If I'm going to do it, I do it with open eyes."

Ashfall's head came around, slow as a season changing. The amber eye fixed on Gideon, and in it Gideon saw reflected the torch, and Maren, and Pip, and themself—small, mortal, brief.

"The rite is called the Taking," the dragon said. "It is not a carving. Carving is a conversation. The Taking is a *door*—you open yourself and do not close. Every voice in this Spire will come through. Not the way they come through bone, one at a time, gently, in their own words. All at once. All of them. The garrison in the ice. The mothers in the granary. The children in the harbor. The ones who ran and the ones who stayed and the ones who betrayed and the ones who forgave. You will be ten thousand people for as long as it takes the Door to drink it—and the Door is *slow*, bone-carver. It savors. It may take a night. It may take three."

"Then I hold the gate," Maren said, and her voice had changed, gone flat and hard and soldier-clean, because this was the one thing she knew how to do with terror: turn it into a wall. "Crane's coming. You said it yourself. If Gideon does this, Gideon does it under guard, and I hold whatever gate needs holding."

"You'll be fighting alone."

"I've been alone my whole life. It's the company I mind." She almost smiled. Almost.

"And me?" Pip's voice, small in the big dark room.

"You," Gideon said, kneeling again, taking the child's face in their scarred hands, "you hold the stone. You stand where I can see you. Because here's the last truth, little map, the one I need you to carry for me in case I can't carry it myself: my name is Gideon. I was born in Lowmark. I carve the dead so they aren't lost. My mother's name was Sera, and she left to save me, and I forgive her—I *forgave* her, I heard her say why, and I need you to know that in case I wake up on the other side of that Door and don't know why there's a child looking at me like I hung the moon. You tell me. You tell me all of it. Every day if you have to. That's your job. That's the whole job. Can you do it?"

Pip was crying now, silently, the way children cry when they've learned already that the world doesn't stop for it. They nodded. Then they threw their arms around Gideon's neck, and the ember-stone pressed warm against Gideon's throat like a second heartbeat, and somewhere in the walls of the Hollow Spire ten thousand dead exhaled at once.

* * *

They made the preparations in the hour before dawn, because Ashfall said the Door drank best at the hour when the living were fewest and the dead were nearest, and because Crane's fires in the valley had begun to move.

Maren checked the gate mechanisms with a soldier's grim thoroughness, counting arrows, testing the old Vaeli counterweights that still worked as if the kingdom had only stepped out for an errand. Pip sat in the center of the mosaic with the ember-stone, humming—Gideon realized, with a jolt, that the child was humming the song, the one from the fountain street, the one nobody alive had taught them. The Spire hummed it back.

Gideon sat on the defaced sun and took out their knife and a length of ordinary pine, cut from a beam in the lower hall, and began, with steady hands, to carve.

Not bone. Wood. The first wood they had ever carved. Because if the Taking worked, there would be no more voices in their ears, ever, and someone would have to remember how to make the dead speak without magic. Someone would have to learn to write it down, to cut it into timber and stone where any passing eye could read it. They carved the first line of Vael's story into the pine, shallow and clear: *We were here. We were real. We remember bread.*

The knife moved. The dead of Vael pressed close, closer than they had ever pressed, ten thousand throats gathering behind Gideon's teeth like a tide behind a sea-wall. Ashfall watched from the doorway with her guttering fire. Maren stood at the gate with her sword drawn, facing down the mountain where torches had begun to climb.

Gideon set down the knife. Closed their eyes. Opened the door inside themselves, and did not close it.

The tide came in.
