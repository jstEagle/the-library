## 18. All the Voices at Once

The Spire breathed around us.

That was the only word for it. The walls of the Hollow Spire rose in two registers at once—cracked grey stone on one side of every corridor, and on the other, pale Vael-marble veined with gold, visible only to Pip, only to memory—and between the two the air moved like lungs. In. Out. A building inhaling ten thousand years of forgetting.

Gideon knelt at the center of it, in a chamber that was half rubble and half throne room, and pressed their palms flat against the floor.

"Say it again," Maren said. She stood at the chamber's mouth with her sword drawn, her back to them, watching the stairwell where torchlight already flickered like a fever. "All of it. Before I go down there."

"If I take the memories into myself," Gideon said, "the Door opens without fire. Without Ashfall dying." Their voice sounded far away to their own ears, as if someone else were speaking through them already. "And when it's done, I'll be empty. No gift. Maybe no—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Maybe no me."

"And if your mind breaks under the weight?"

"Then Pip still gets out. You all still get out." Gideon looked up at her—at the knight who had once run from this same country's burning and had spent three weeks bleeding beside them to earn back the right to stand here. "That's the arithmetic, Maren. It's the only sum that works."

Maren's jaw worked. For a moment she looked younger than her scars, older than her years, both at once. Then she crossed the chamber, gripped Gideon's shoulder hard enough to bruise, and said nothing at all—which was, from Ser Maren Kestrel, the loudest blessing she knew how to give.

She went down toward the gates and the sound of Crane's soldiers.

* * *

Pip would not leave.

The child sat cross-legged in front of Gideon with the ember-stone cupped in both hands, its glow painting their face orange, their eyes enormous. Behind them, faint as breath on glass, Ashfall coiled through the broken archway—the last dragon too vast for the ruin and yet somehow fitting inside it, the way grief fits inside a body. The dragon's scales had gone the grey of old ash. Their breathing came slow as tide.

"You don't have to watch," Gideon said.

"Yes I do." Pip's chin lifted. It was the stubborn set they'd worn since the waystation, since the first map drawn in charcoal on a floorboard: *this is Vael, this is home, you can't tell me it isn't.* "Somebody has to remember you doing it. That's how it works, right? Bones remember because somebody listens. So somebody has to listen to *you*."

Gideon laughed—a cracked, wet sound—and reached out and touched the child's hair. "Then listen well, little cartographer."

They turned back to the floor. Beneath their palms, the Spire waited.

They began to carve.

There was no bone here, so they used themselves. That was the forbidden art, the one the carvers' guild had whispered about and never taught: opening the channel not into a relic but into living flesh, letting the dead pour through and *stay*. Gideon scored the first line into the skin of their own forearm with the bone needle they had carried since Lowmark, since the world was small and warm and only quietly ending. Blood welled. The Spire shuddered.

And the door in the floor of the world opened.

* * *

It did not come gently.

The first voice was a baker. Gideon knew it instantly, knew her the way you know a song heard once in childhood—Marla of the Lower Terraces, who had risen before dawn for thirty years and whose last thought, forty years ago, had been annoyance that the ovens would go cold with dough still rising in them. Her death arrived whole: the heat, the smoke, the strange domestic indignity of it. Gideon took it in. It settled behind their ribs like a swallowed coal.

Then the second voice. And the tenth. And the hundredth.

Ten thousand deaths do not arrive in a line. They arrive as weather.

A soldier named Corvess, drowning in the harbor, thinking of his brother's bad poetry. A queen's archivist sealing the true histories into a vault and walking calmly back into the flames. Children in a schoolhouse singing a counting song in a language that no longer existed anywhere except now, except here, except in the widening furnace of Gideon's skull. Farmers. Whores. Traitors. Loyalists. A dog. Ten thousand dogs. The whole animal country pouring in alongside its people, because Vael had never distinguished, because memory does not sort the living from the beloved beasts.

Gideon's body arched. Somewhere very far away Pip was screaming their name, and Ashfall's voice rolled over them like a mountain learning tenderness: *Hold the line of yourself, bone-carver. Hold it.*

But there was no line. There was no self to hold. That was the price and the mercy of it—that the ritual required annihilation, required Gideon Sandoval to stop being a single person and become instead a *country*, a vessel with ten thousand tenants, and whatever remained when the flooding stopped would be something new and smaller and possibly nothing at all.

They felt their own memories being displaced. Lowmark going dim. Odile's laugh, fading. Their mother's face—

No. Not fading. *Arriving.*

Because among the ten thousand dead, threading through them like gold through marble, came the dead of Vael itself, and Vael remembered everything, and what Vael remembered most recently and most fiercely was its own erasure—and within that erasure, in the last hour of the last day, walked a woman with Gideon's hands and Gideon's stubborn chin, leading witch-hunters away from a hidden child by promising to show them where more gifted children hid.

*I'm sorry,* said the mother's voice, not carved this time but alive, immediate, part of the flood. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, little one. Be small. Be safe. Forget me if you have to.*

*I never could,* Gideon answered, with what was left of their mouth.

And then there was no more answering, because the flood crested.

* * *

Later—if it was later; time had gone the consistency of water—Gideon became aware of another presence in the flood. A living one. A man standing in the doorway of the chamber with his relic raised, his face white as the Vael-marble, weeping without knowing he was weeping.

Halvard Crane had come up the stairs through Maren's defense—through the ringing steel and the impossible sight of Vael's ghost-guard risen to hold their own door—and he had come intending to burn the Spire and everyone in it. But the Spire's walls replayed echoes, and Crane carried a relic that let him hear what the dead heard, and the Hollow Spire was full to bursting with a nation's last day.

He stood in the flood and drowned in it.

Gideon, vast and dissolving, felt him arrive the way you feel a stone dropped into a well. And in the flood, Crane found her. His sister. The voice he had been silencing relic by relic for twenty years, burning each container of her words because hearing her was unbearable and destroying her was the only mercy he understood.

*Hal,* said the sister's voice, mingling now with Marla the baker, with Corvess the drowned soldier, with ten thousand others, until her forgiveness was indistinguishable from a country's. *Hal, look what you're standing in. Look how many of us there are. Did you think you could burn us all?*

"No," Crane whispered. His relic trembled in his grip—an iron lantern-cage, Gideon saw now, really saw, its bars engraved with suppression-script, built to smother voices, to make silence where silence was a kind of killing. "No, I thought—I thought it was kinder. To end it. To end *you*."

*Was it?*

The question went through him like a spear through water.

Crane turned. His soldiers had followed him up the stairs—they stood in the archway now, torches raised, waiting for the order—and Gideon, from the bottomless place where a self used to be, watched him look at them and see, for the first time, not heretics but future voices. Future relics. Future sisters.

"Halvard," said the captain. "The order, sir?"

Crane raised the relic.

The suppression-script flared—not outward, but inward, inverted, turned against the men who had marched behind it for years. The lantern-cage drank their voices, their breath, their heat; two of them fell simply, like puppets with cut strings. The rest broke and fled down the stairs, and Maren's blade sang somewhere below, cutting their retreat into rout.

Which left Crane alone, kneeling, the relic smoking in his hands, at the threshold of a door that was beginning to open.

"It has to stay open," he said. His voice was wrecked, quiet, almost gentle. "Doesn't it. The Door. Someone has to hold it open while they pass." He looked at Gideon—at the thing Gideon was becoming, the vessel glowing with borrowed dead—and something like awe moved across his ruined face. "You're carrying all of them. All at once. How do you bear it?"

*You don't,* Gideon thought, from everywhere. *You just stop being the one who bears it.*

"I hear her," Crane said. "In the walls. I hear her, and she doesn't hate me." He laughed, or sobbed; the sounds had long ago become the same. He crawled to the base of the great Door—the seam in reality that shimmered now like heat-haze, wide as a cathedral, held open by the pressure of ten thousand souls pushing through Gideon's mortal frame—and he wedged himself beneath its edge, iron relic braced against the stone, his body become a hinge.

"Tell my sister—" he began.

The Door's light took the rest.

He died the way he had lived: listening. And in his last moment, Gideon—who was no longer entirely Gideon, who was a country wearing a bone-carver's body—heard the sister's voice rise in the flood to meet him, and wrap around him, and carry him down into the deep water of the dead like a boat finally allowed to come to shore.

* * *

What remained was passage.

Maren came up the stairs bloodied and breathing hard, took one look at the shining seam, and started hauling Pip toward it. Ashfall poured their failing bulk through the archway, and where the dragon passed, the ember-stone in Pip's hands flared once, twice, like a heart deciding.

"Gideon." Pip tore free of Maren's grip and ran back, and pressed the ember-stone into Gideon's bloodied hand, and held on. "Come *with* us. Come *through*. You have to come through."

Through the roaring, through the ten thousand, some small stubborn grain of the person who had once carved a dead woman's story into her femur in a smuggler's tavern found the child's fingers and closed around them.

*Not through,* Gideon tried to say. *I am the door now. I am the hinge and the frame and the road. When the last of you passes, I close it—me, from this side—and Vael comes with me, all of it, into whatever world is willing to be haunted.*

But their mouth wouldn't shape the words anymore. What came out instead was all of them at once—a chorus, a choir, Marla and Corvess and the mother and the schoolchildren and the dogs and the drowned and the burned, speaking with one voice through Gideon's cracked lips:

"*Go. We are coming with you.*"

Maren went pale. Pip went white. And then Pip did the bravest thing an eight-year-old had ever done in either world: they kissed Gideon's forehead, picked up their maps, and walked into the light.

Ashfall followed, wings folded, head bowed, an entire kingdom's witness passing through the seam like a river through a gate.

Maren paused at the threshold. She looked back at the glowing, dissolving figure at the center of the chamber—at the friend she had bled beside, becoming something no longer quite a friend, no longer quite a person—and she drew herself up and gave the salute she had given no banner in fifteen years.

"Ser Gideon," she said. "Of Vael."

Then she stepped through, and the light swallowed her, and Gideon Sandoval—ten thousand strong, utterly alone—turned to the Door, and began, with the last strength of a body that had been a life, to close it behind them.
