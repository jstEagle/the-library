## 20. A Country Willing to Be Haunted

The first thing Gideon learned about the world beyond the Door was that it smelled like rain.

Not the iron-and-ash rain of Thornfell, not the cold mineral drip of the Serrated Pass, but rain that had fallen on grass—on long, uncut meadow grass going silver in the wind, on soil dark and soft as fresh-turned bread. It came to them across a valley so green it looked newly made, as if the land had been waiting all this time for someone to walk out of a door that did not exist and give it a reason to keep growing.

Gideon stood in the doorway's afterimage—the air still shimmered faintly where the Spire had been, a heat-haze with nothing hot beneath it—and breathed, and waited for the voices.

They did not come.

Gideon had known they would not come. They had carried ten thousand deaths through their own body to open the Door; the gift was ash in them now, burned down to the wick and past it. But knowing is a small, dry thing, and grief is a flood. For three days afterward, whenever anyone spoke near them—a cart driver on the border road, a miller's wife selling them eggs—Gideon flinched, half-expecting to hear the words underneath the words, the marrow-truth, the story the bones would tell.

There were no bones talking. There were no bones at all, except the living ones, walking around inside their skins, saying only what they meant to say.

It was the quietest world Gideon had ever stood in. It was almost unbearable.

* * *

They buried Ashfall's passing in the way of travelers who cannot dig deep enough: with words, and a cairn, and Pip's stone.

The dragon had gone east on the third morning, when the mist off the mountains was still lying in the valley like spilled milk. Ashfall's wings had beaten twice—slow, laborious, each one a whole season of effort—and then the old dragon had found the thermal rising off the sun-warmed slopes and simply let go, letting the sky do what the ember-stone no longer could. They watched until the shape became a speck, and the speck became nothing, and the nothing went on being there somehow, in the way of things you have loved.

Pip held the cooled stone against their chest the whole time. When it was over, the child set it on top of the cairn, straightened it twice because it wasn't level, and said, "He wanted to see the sea. He told me once. In the cave. He said dragons used to sleep in the sea-cliffs of Vael and wake up salt-crusted and grumpy."

"Then he's seeing it now," Maren said.

"Do you think it's warm?"

"I think," Maren said carefully, in the voice she used for truths she was still building as she spoke, "that everything he was, he gave away on purpose. That doesn't seem like something that ends up cold."

Gideon said nothing. Gideon had been saying very little. There was a strange economy to a body that had swallowed a country: every ordinary word cost more than it used to. When Gideon finally spoke, it was to Pip, and it was only this:

"He's in here too. All of him. I can't hear him anymore—but I remember everything he ever said. Every word." Gideon pressed two fingers below their own sternum, where the memories lived now, a weight like a second skeleton. "That has to count for something."

Pip looked at the stone a long moment. Then took it back off the cairn and put it in their pocket instead, which Gideon understood completely. Some things are not for leaving behind.

* * *

The borderlands, it turned out, were full of people running from the same fire.

Not the Purge itself—not yet; word traveled slower than fear, but it traveled—they were ahead of that particular tide by weeks, maybe months. What they found instead was the older, quieter exile: Vaeli descendants whose grandparents had scattered when the kingdom was struck from the maps. Farmsteads where the bread was braided in a pattern no one could explain anymore. A chapel where an old woman lit forty candles on the first frost-night and told her grandchildren it was "just how we've always done it." Names worn smooth of meaning—Aelvarren become Elvern, Saelith become Sally—handed down like coins from a country that no longer minted them.

Gideon saw it everywhere, once they started looking. A whole people carrying a homeland the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out: painfully, invisibly, always aware of it when the weather changed.

It was in a village called Harrowmere, three valleys east of where the Door had opened onto nothing, that Gideon first picked up a knife that was not a carver's knife and cut into something that was not bone.

It happened almost by accident. The village headman, a broad, weathered man named Torv with Vaeli blood in his face that he himself did not know the name of, had given them barn space for the winter in exchange for labor. There was a great deal of mending to do. Fences, roofs, the long low byre wall. One gray afternoon Gideon sat repairing a fence post with a drawknife, shaving off rot, and the post's pale inner wood came clean under the blade in long curls—and Gideon stopped, because the grain of it looked like script. Looked like dragon-script, the looping flame-language from the cave walls, the letters Ashfall had taught them to see.

Gideon sat with that a long while. The knife lay in their lap. Somewhere behind the barn, Pip was laughing at something, that bright unguarded laugh that had taken four months and one destroyed kingdom to come back.

Then Gideon turned the post, set the blade to it properly, and began—not to shave it smooth, but to carve. Slowly. Deliberately. The first line of the Lament of the Salt Cliffs, which Ashfall had spoken aloud in the cave while the ember-stone pulsed its slow heartbeat light against the walls. Gideon did not have the gift anymore. Could not hear the words under the words, could not press a palm to bone and receive a life. But memory was not magic. Memory had never been magic. Magic had only ever been the shortcut.

The letters went deep. Gideon made them deep enough to be read with fingers in the dark, the way you'd read a grave-marker, the way you'd learn anything worth keeping.

When Torv came by at dusk and saw the post standing there with its strange looping script, he frowned at it, touched it, traced one loop with a thick cautious finger.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "My grandmother hummed something that sounded like that. She said it was nonsense sounds. She said her own mother told her never to say it outside the house."

"It's not nonsense," Gideon said. "It's the beginning of a song about the sea. About cliffs where dragons slept. Do you want to know the rest of it?"

Torv looked at the post for a long time. His jaw worked once, the way a man's does when something large is moving behind his teeth and he hasn't decided whether to swallow it or speak it.

"Yes," he said. "Gods help me. Yes."

* * *

Winter came down out of the mountains early that year, and Gideon carved through all of it.

Word spread the way word does in farm country—which is to say slowly, thoroughly, and with embellishment. By midwinter there were people coming to Harrowmere from villages Gideon couldn't have found on any map, which was fitting, since most of what Gideon knew wasn't on maps either. They came quietly, in ones and twos, hooded against the cold and against being seen. An old woman who remembered her mother singing a cradle-song in a language she'd been told to forget. Two brothers who had inherited a chest of documents in a script nobody living could read. A girl of maybe fifteen who drew spirals on everything, compulsively, without knowing why, and burst into tears when Pip showed her the spiral-motif on a door-lintel from the Spire and told her, gently, "You're not broken. You're remembering."

And Gideon carved.

Not bone—there was no more hearing in bone, no voices rising out of the marrow, and Gideon had made peace with that the way you make peace with the loss of a limb: incompletely, daily, and with a phantom ache when the weather changed. Instead it was oak and ash and river-stone, slate and antler and the flat gray shingles of the byre roof. The founding of Vael, as the garrison's last survivors had told it in the ice before the ice closed over them. The betrayal—crown envoys swearing friendship at the table while their king's seal moved on orders to burn the archives. The names of the drowned, as many as the flooded memory of ten thousand deaths could yield, which was nearly all of them, carved in columns on the meeting-house wall like a roll call that had finally been answered.

Maren helped with the layout, because Maren had a soldier's sense of walls and sightlines and how a thing should stand so it could be defended. "You're building a fortification," she said one night, watching Gideon work by lamplight, chisel whispering through pine.

"No."

"Gideon. You're putting your history on the outside of buildings. Where anyone can read it. Including anyone who comes to burn it."

"Yes," Gideon said, and blew a curl of shaving off the board. "That's the point. If it's hidden, it can be erased again. If it's on every wall in the valley, then burning it means burning the valley, and the people in it, and the people will know exactly why. I'm done with secrets, Maren. Secrets are what killed Vael. Secrets are what let them strike it off the maps while the whole country was still warm." Gideon set the chisel, tapped the mallet, sent another letter home into the wood. "Let them come with their notices and their nails. Let them try to purge a wall."

Maren was silent for a while. The lamp guttered. Outside, snow ticked softly against the shutter, and somewhere in the house Pip was reading aloud to the girl who drew spirals, both of them bent over a map of a country that existed now in exactly two places: in Gideon's body, and in Pip's careful ink.

"You sound like my commander," Maren said at last. "Ser Aldous. Before the end. He used to say the worst thing the crown ever stole from us wasn't our honor—it was our habit of telling the truth out loud."

Gideon's hands stilled.

"Maren. Your commander. Ser Aldous of the Kestrel company." The name had surfaced from the flood like a fish rising—because of course it had; Gideon carried everything now, every death the Spire's walls had echoed, every ghost Maren had fought beside at the gate. "Tall man. Bad knee. Kept a whetstone in his left gauntlet and hummed when he sharpened. Died at the Vael gate holding the eastern stair."

Maren had gone very still in the way of people who have just felt a hand on their shoulder in an empty room.

"How—" Her voice failed and restarted. "How do you know that? I never—I never told you his name."

"I don't hear him," Gideon said, and it cost something true to say it, and it was also, in a way Gideon was only beginning to understand, a kind of gift. "I never will again. But I remember him. Everything the Spire showed me, everyone who died for that door—he's in me, Maren. All of them are. And as long as I've got breath and a knife, none of them are gone. Not Aldous. Not Ashfall. Not—" the name rose in Gideon's throat like a swallow, painful and necessary, "—not my mother."

Silence. Snow ticking. Lamplight breathing.

"She's in there," Maren said quietly. "Even though you can't hear her."

"Especially because I can't." Gideon looked down at their hands—scarred, capable, ordinary hands, hands that would never again hold a relic and listen to a life pour out of it. "I spent my whole life hearing the dead. And the last thing I ever heard her say was that confession, and then the Door closed, and the silence came down, and I thought—that was the cruelest part. To carry her voice and then lose it. But it isn't." Gideon picked up the chisel. "The cruel part would have been never knowing. Now I know everything. And everything can be written down."

* * *

In spring, Gideon finished the meeting-house wall, and the village gathered to read it.

Not all of them could read—but the letters were carved deep enough for fingers, and those who could read did it aloud, and the ones who couldn't listened, and the old woman who lit forty candles on the first frost-night stood in front of the roll call of the drowned for the better part of an hour, touching name after name, until she found one that matched a song her grandmother had sung, and then she sat down right there on the floor of the meeting-house and wept without shame while the whole village pretended very hard to be busy elsewhere, which is the kindest thing a community can do.

Torv stood beside Gideon at the back. He had learned six lines of the Lament by heart over the winter, mouthing them as he worked, and now he heard them whole for the first time.

"It's a good country," he said at last, meaning Vael, meaning the wall, meaning perhaps both. "Was."

"Is," Gideon said. "That's rather the entire point."

But spring brought other things than gatherings. A tinker came through Harrowmere in the third week of thaw, selling needles and gossip, and the gossip had a hard edge to it: crown riders in the western passes, posting notices on tavern doors. Notices about gifts. Notices about a knight without a banner seen traveling with a gifted carver, a strange child, a dying dragon. Notices bearing the seal of an Inquisitor named Crane—who was, according to the tinker, either dead at some tower in the mountains, or promoted for his services there, depending on who was telling it.

Maren read the description of themselves off the notice's copy with the flat expression she wore for battlefields and bad news. Pip read it too, lips moving, and then looked up at Gideon with a question in their face that they were too brave to ask out loud.

Gideon looked at the wall of the meeting-house—at the names, the song, the spiral motifs, the history of a murdered kingdom standing out loud in the open air where any rider could see it—and felt the old dread stir, and felt something older than the dread answer it.

"Sharpen the knives," Gideon said. "All of them. And send word down-valley. Anyone fleeing the notices—we have walls that need learning, and room at the hearth, and a country here that's going to learn to be haunted properly."

Outside, the wind came up the valley from the east, smelling faintly of open water, and the carved letters drank the light and held it, patient as bones, ready to be read.
