## 22. Ember-Stone, Cold

The mantel above our hearth is made of mountain ash, cut and cured the year I turned fourteen, when Gideon's hands still shook and Maren still woke at every sound of wings. It took three days to fit it, because Gideon insisted on carving something into it first—small figures, a procession of them, marching across the grain like travelers on a road. *So the house knows who lives here,* they said, running a thumb over the tiny faces. *Houses forget otherwise.*

At the center of the mantel, where the carving opens into a clearing, sits the ember-stone.

It has been cold for nineteen years.

I want to be precise about that word, *cold*, because it isn't the cold of winter or of riverbed stones. It's the cold of a thing that has finished its work. A spent candle. A closed door. When I was small I used to press it to my cheek and wait for warmth the way other children waited for festival days, and when none came, Maren would find me sitting on the hearthstone with my knees drawn up and tell me, in her gruff way, that some fires don't go out—they just move somewhere you can't follow. I believed her then because I was eight and she was all I had. I believe her now because I am twenty-seven and I have learned what moving fire looks like. It looks like a room full of children learning to draw a coastline no map remembers.

* * *

The school meets in our front room in the mornings and in the barn when the weather turns. We never named it a school, exactly—it began as two refugee children from the Kellish marches whose parents needed someone to watch them while they worked the new fields, and it became four, and nine, and now there are seventeen of them, ranging from a girl of five who bites to a boy of twelve who asks questions I can't always answer and doesn't like being told *I don't know.*

His name is Tam. He is the reason this chapter of my life exists, if chapters are the right word for the shape a life makes when you look back at it.

It started last autumn, when he found the old maps I keep in the cedar chest under my bed—the ones I drew at eight years old with charcoal stubs and stolen parchment, drawing a country I had never seen but remembered anyway: the terraced orchards above Vael's capital, the white causeway across the Mirrormere, the Spire itself rising out of the mountains like a needle threaded through both worlds. He spread them across the floor and looked up at me with the particular ferocity children reserve for discovering adults have been keeping things from them.

"You drew these?"

"When I was about your age."

"Who taught you?"

"Nobody." I sat down beside him among the maps. "I remembered."

He studied me for a long moment, the way children study adults when they suspect an adult might finally be honest. Then he said, "That's not a real country. There's nothing called Vael anywhere."

And I said, "There is now," which is either a lie or the truest thing I've ever said, depending on the day.

* * *

Here is what I teach them, in the mornings, while the light comes slantwise through the east windows Gideon cut larger on purpose—*so the past can see in,* they claimed, though I think mostly so the plants would stop dying:

I teach them the shape of Vael's borders, which followed the watershed of the Serrated Pass and ran to saltwater in the south. I teach them the names of the rivers, which the crown struck from every record forty years before I was born and which survive now only because a bone-carver heard them spoken by the dead and carved them into wood instead. I teach them the words for *home* in Old Vaelish—*sorenna*—and the words for *door*, which is the same word as *wound*, which tells you most of what you need to know about that country's last days.

I teach them that the kingdom did not fall. That is important. Falling is what happens to things that fail. Vael was erased—unmade on paper, unwritten from treaties, unremembered by decree—and there is a difference, and the difference matters, because erased things can be restored and fallen things cannot.

Tam raised his hand the third morning and asked the question I knew was coming, the one every one of them eventually asks.

"If it was erased, how do we know it was real?"

And I picked up the ember-stone from the mantel—I let them hold it, one at a time; it's the closest thing we have to scripture—and I set it in his palm and told him: "Because a dragon carried its memory inside its own body for forty years rather than let it die. Because a knight who ran away came back and held a gate. Because a carver gave up hearing the dead forever so that the living could remember instead. Real isn't a thing countries are. Real is a thing people do."

He didn't understand all of it. He's twelve. But he closed his hand around the stone carefully, the way you hold an egg or a promise, and I saw that someday he would.

* * *

Gideon is seventy-one now. Their hands, which once could pull a dead woman's whole life out of her femur with a burin and a candle, shake too much for fine work—but not too much for the wooden plaques, the ones that line the road into the village now, each one telling a piece of Vael's story in pictures anyone can read without magic. The miller's children know the plaque of the burning orchards. The ferryman's wife stops every time she passes the one of Ashfall rising over the pass, wings spread against a sky full of smoke, and touches the dragon's eye with one finger. Nobody remembers why they do it. They just do.

The gift burned out of Gideon the night the Door closed. They say they don't miss it, and I think that's mostly true. But sometimes, in the evenings, when the fire is low and Maren has gone up to bed, I catch them listening—not to anything, just listening, head tilted, the posture of a lifetime—and their face does something complicated before they turn back to their carving. You spend fifty years hearing the dead speak through everything you touch. Silence must be its own kind of haunting.

Maren is sixty-eight and still rises before dawn, still walks the fence line, still checks the gate latch twice. She wrote to her old order eleven years ago—not asking pardon, only telling the truth about Vael, the whole truth, the betrayal and the purge and the garrison in the ice. She got back a single letter, unsigned: *We read it. We kept it.* She framed it. It hangs by the door. She says it's the only commendation she ever wanted, and she laughs when she says it, but her hand goes to her sword hilt the way it always does when she's lying.

And Ashfall—

Ashfall flew east the morning we came through the Door, freed at last from the stone that had been keeping him alive, and went to die under open sky as dragons should. I watched him until he was a smudge against the sunrise and then a rumor and then nothing. I have never seen another dragon. Sometimes, on clear nights, I stand in the yard and look east and wonder if the mountains there keep his bones, and whether bones keep anything at all once there's no one left to carve them.

But I know they do. Because I carry his fire's memory myself, in the cooled stone, in the maps, in the mouths of seventeen children saying *sorenna* with their breakfast still on their breath.

* * *

Last evening was the anniversary—the night we came through, though we've stopped calling it anything official; it's just the night the whole household stays up late and Gideon tells the story of the Spire from beginning to end, doing all the voices, even Crane's, especially Crane's, because Gideon believes with their whole scarred heart that even the man who hunted us deserves to be remembered accurately. The children sat cross-legged on the floor. Maren fell asleep in her chair halfway through and woke only for the ending, as she always does. And when it was done, and the fire had burned down to orange coals, Tam—who stayed behind to help stack the chairs, which is his way of asking for more time—stood at the mantel looking at the ember-stone.

"It's really cold?" he asked. "All the way through? Forever?"

"All the way through," I said. "Forever."

He reached out and touched it, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Does it ever—"

And then he stopped talking.

Because under his fingertip—or maybe under mine, I was touching it too by then, I don't remember reaching for it—the stone pulsed. Once. Faint. Warm as a coal found hours after the fire, warm as a hand you'd almost forgotten holding yours, there and gone before either of us could be sure it had happened at all.

We stood very still in the cooling room. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Outside, the wind moved through the mountain ash, and far off, so far it might have been imagination, something that was not quite thunder rolled along the eastern hills.

Tam looked at me with enormous eyes. "Did you feel that?"

I thought of Ashfall under open sky. I thought of a door standing shut in a tower half in this world and half in no world at all. I thought of ten thousand voices that my teacher can no longer hear, alive now only in wood and ink and the mouths of children—and I thought, *maybe the telling works both ways.*

"Go to bed," I said, and my voice wasn't entirely steady. "School starts early. Tomorrow I'll teach you the names of the southern islands."

He went, glancing back twice. And I stood alone by the hearth with the stone in my cupped hands, waiting, watching the gray dawn come up through the east window Gideon cut so the past could see in—

—and felt, at the very edge of sense, faint as breath on a mirror, a second pulse.
