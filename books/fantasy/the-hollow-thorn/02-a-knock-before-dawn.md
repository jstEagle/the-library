## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The storm came up the river valley an hour past midnight, and by the time the first true gusts struck the guildhouse, Yara Thornbury had already closed the ledger and was pretending she hadn't been listening for anything else.

She told herself it was habit. Thirty years of keeping the Guild of Impossible Things' accounts had taught her that storms were good for business — smugglers moved cargo in weather that kept honest men indoors, and desperate people knocked on strange doors in the rain. But there was no shipment due tonight. No client expected. The ledger lay shut on its iron stand in the counting room, its brass clasps cold under her fingertips when she touched them without meaning to, the way a gambler touches a coin.

*Nothing is coming,* she told herself. *You are forty-one years old and you are listening to the wind like a girl.*

The guildhouse had been a granary once, before the founders bought it with money no one could account for, and it still wore its old bones beneath the renovations: beams thick as masts, floors that groaned like a ship in any wind. Yara walked its length out of restlessness rather than purpose, past the trading hall with its long table scarred by two centuries of bargains, past the hearth where the night-fire had burned down to a red eye, past the shelf of ledgers — one for each decade of her service — spines stamped in gold leaf with titles that would have made a bishop laugh. *The Ledger of Small Miracles. The Ledger of Returned Names. The Ledger of Debts Forgiven and Debts Not.*

Her whole life fit on those shelves. She had never quite decided whether that was comfort or indictment.

In the trading hall she found Sef awake, which was unusual; Sef was usually asleep wherever he'd last been paid, and awake only when there was profit in it. He sat at the long table with his boots up, shelling pistachios into a pile and flicking the shells toward the hearth with a marksman's boredom.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said.

"I wasn't trying to."

"Course you weren't." He cracked another shell. "Storm's a bad one. River'll be over the towpath by morning. You want to know what I heard today?"

"No," said Yara, because whatever Sef had heard, hearing it made you complicit, and she had spent thirty years learning exactly how much complicity she could carry.

"Crown riders in Marrowgate. Inquisitor's colors." He flicked a shell into the fire. "Asking questions about the old tongue, they say. About vessels."

Yara kept her face still, which was the first rule of the guild and the only one she had never broken. *We trade in what cannot be owned,* the founders had written, *and therefore we must be owned by nothing — not crown, not creed, not fear.* She recited it to herself the way other people recited prayers.

"The Crown asks about everything," she said. "It's how they justify their budget."

"Mm." Sef smiled his thin smile, the one that showed he knew she was lying and didn't mind, because everyone lied, and lies were just inventory waiting to be priced. "Sleep well, Keeper."

She did not go back to bed. She climbed instead to the small room above the counting room where she slept among the smell of ink and vellum, and she sat by the window watching the storm flail the elms along the lane, and she waited — though for what, she refused to name.

* * *

The knock came in the hour before dawn, when the storm had worn itself down to a steady grey drumming and the world was at its thinnest.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes.

Yara was downstairs before she had decided to move, candle in hand, and she stood in the entry hall with her hand on the bar of the great door and felt her heart going hard against her ribs. The guild's rules were simple about doors: nothing entered after the third bell that had not been contracted before it. She had turned away bleeding men on this threshold. She had turned away a woman carrying a child in the snow, once, and had written the refusal in the ledger in her own neat hand, and had not slept properly for a month afterward, and had never spoken of it.

Three knocks again. Patient. Unhurried. As if whoever stood outside knew, with absolute certainty, that the door would open.

Yara lifted the bar.

The storm pushed in around a small figure standing on the step — a child, perhaps seven years old, perhaps younger, barefoot on wet stone, wearing a shift of undyed wool gone grey with rain. A girl. Her hair was dark and plastered flat to her skull, water running off her in ropes, and she looked up at Yara with an expression that stopped every prepared speech in Yara's throat.

She was not afraid. She was not crying. She was not even cold, or if she was, she wore it the way a queen wears a borrowed cloak — aware of it, unbothered by it. She looked at Yara the way Yara imagined one might look at a familiar house seen after many years away: recognition without surprise.

"You can't be here," Yara said. "The guild takes no unsanctioned—"

The child spoke.

It was not any language Yara knew. She had traded with sailors from nine coasts, with desert caravans, with the hill-folk whose tongue predated the kingdom by a thousand years, and none of them sounded like this. The sounds were older than sound had any right to be — consonants like roots splitting stone, vowels that seemed to arrive a half-second before the mouth made them. And yet, absurdly, impossibly, Yara almost understood. It sat at the edge of comprehension like a word remembered from a dream, warm and specific and gone the moment she reached for it.

The child finished speaking and waited.

"I don't understand you," Yara said, and hated how rough her voice came out. "Child, where is your mother? Where did you walk from?"

The girl tilted her head. Rain dripped from her chin. Behind her the lane ran black and empty to the river, and there were no footprints in the mud — Yara saw that clearly, later, and could not stop seeing it. Bare feet on soaked ground, and no prints coming or going, as though the child had been set down by something that did not touch the earth.

Then Yara felt the humming.

It began low, below hearing, a vibration in the floorboards, in the candle flame, in her own teeth — and then it rose, and she knew it, because she had known it once, twenty years ago, on the one night she had ever drawn her oath-blade in earnest. The blade hung in her quarters upstairs, wrapped in oilcloth, unworn, unsung. Swordspeakers carried their blades naked at the hip or not at all; that was the old law, the law of the twelve, and Yara had broken it so long ago that the breaking had hardened into mere habit. She kept the blade wrapped because a blade that hummed drew questions, and questions drew the Crown, and the Crown drew fire.

But the blade was upstairs, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth in a locked chest, and it was singing anyway.

The sound came through the ceiling like a hymn through a chapel roof. The child's eyes went up toward it, then back to Yara's face, and something in her expression shifted — not fear, but a kind of grave satisfaction, the look of a messenger who has confirmed the address.

"No," Yara whispered, to the child, to the blade, to the night. "No, whatever you are, you have the wrong door."

The child answered in the old tongue, three soft syllables, and this time Yara did understand — not with her ears but somewhere behind her sternum, the way one understands heat or grief. The meaning arrived whole:

*This door. You.*

* * *

Corvane came down at the sound of the blade, as Yara had known he would. The guildmaster was sixty and built like the granary itself, all beams and bulk, with a voice that could quiet a trading hall full of thieves and a face that gave away exactly as much as a locked strongbox. He stood on the stairs in his nightshirt with a lamp held high, and he looked at the child dripping on his threshold for a long, long moment.

"Well," he said.

"She knocked," Yara said. "No prints. She speaks—" She stopped, because saying *the old tongue* aloud felt like opening a ledger she had no right to open. "I don't know what she speaks."

"I do." Corvane's voice was flat. "Turn her away."

"Guildmaster—"

"Rules of the house, Keeper. Nothing enters unsanctioned. You wrote the interpretation yourself, twice, in the margin of the founding text." He came down the stairs slowly, lamp swinging, and Yara watched him look at the child and watched what crossed his face — not confusion, not curiosity, but recognition, swift and quickly buried, the look of a man who has found a debt collector on his doorstep after twenty years of hoping otherwise. "There is a barn two miles east. There is a shrine to the River Mother on the towpath. There is a kingdom full of churches. Any of them will take her in."

"It's storming."

"It's always storming somewhere." Corvane set the lamp on the hall table, and the light steadied, and in its steadiness Yara saw his hands were not steady at all. That frightened her more than the child had. In thirty years she had never seen Corvane's hands shake. "Yara. Turn. Her. Away."

The blade hummed overhead, and beneath the hum Yara felt something else now, something that had not been there twenty years ago: words forming in the vibration, slow as sap rising, a sentence being assembled note by note in a language the sword had not spoken since the night it was forged. It was going to say something. It was taking all its long silence to say it.

And the child stood in the doorway, patient as winter, looking up at Yara with those unafraid eyes, and Yara thought — with a clarity that felt less like decision than like memory, like something already written in a ledger she hadn't opened yet — *she walked through a storm with no footprints to reach my door specifically, and my sword knows her name.*

"Guildmaster," Yara said carefully, "the rules also say we take no contract we don't understand. We don't understand this. Turning her out blind is worse than bad practice. It's reckless."

Corvane looked at her then, fully, and for one unguarded instant Yara saw past the strongbox face into whatever lay behind it, and what lay there was terror — old terror, banked and fed for decades, the terror of a man who keeps a fire alive in a cellar and checks on it nightly.

"Reckless," he repeated softly. "Yes. That's one word for it." He picked up the lamp again. "She does not enter. See that the door is barred. That is a direct order, Keeper, and you have never once disobeyed me, and I am asking you — as the man who taught you the ledgers — not to start tonight."

He climbed the stairs. The lamp-light shrank. The blade's hum faded with him, reluctantly, like a singer asked to wait.

Yara stood alone in the entry hall with the storm breathing in around the child's small shoulders.

The girl looked at the closing dark above the stairs, then at Yara, and said something in the old tongue — four syllables this time, gentle, almost kind. And though Yara could not have translated it under torture, she understood it completely, the way one understands a hand extended in the dark:

*I will wait. I have waited longer than your kingdom. I can wait until you choose.*

Yara closed the door.

She stood with her forehead against the wet wood and listened to the rain, and to the silence on the other side of the door — a silence too complete, too patient, the silence of someone still standing exactly where they had been. She should bar it. Corvane had ordered it. She had never once disobeyed him; obedience was the load-bearing wall of everything she had built, and she knew with terrible precision what happened to buildings when you pulled that wall out.

Overhead, faint through the floors, the blade began to hum again — and this time the hum shaped itself, unmistakably, into the first word it had spoken in twenty years.

It sounded, Yara thought, like a name.

She took her hand off the bar.
