## 4. The Inquisitor's Wax Seal

The rain had followed Dain Marrec from the capital for three days, and he had begun to think of it as a colleague — persistent, humorless, and useful for making people talk. He stood at the window of the Wayhouse of the Gilded Chain in Lowmarsh and watched it fall on the town like a verdict read slowly aloud.

Behind him, his clerks unpacked the traveling office: the portable seal, the wax, the ledgers of open cases, the small iron brazier that smelled, always faintly, of burnt paper. Marrec did not turn around. He was watching a woman cross the square below with a bundle of wet kindling held under her cloak like a stolen child, and he was thinking about vessels.

*A vessel of the old tongue.* The writ from the Lord Chancellor used those exact words, which meant someone above him knew more than they had written down. That was usual. Writs were like wells — you drew up only what the rope could carry, and the rope was always shorter than the depth.

"Ser," said Clerk Ovelin behind him, "the harbormaster is here. And the parish reeve. They came together, ser, which the harbormaster seems to find uncomfortable."

"The harbormaster finds everything uncomfortable. It's why I use him." Marrec turned from the window. He was a lean man of fifty-two years with grey going white at the temples in a way that suggested patience rather than age, and he wore the inquisition's slate-grey coat without ornament except the seal at his breast: a disc of black wax, permanently set, stamped with the crowned thorn that was the Crown's oldest sigil — older than any dynasty now ruling, though no one liked to say so. "Show them in. Tea if we have it. We are guests in Lowmarsh, and guests should be gracious."

"They're frightened, ser."

"Good. Frightened men answer questions quickly. Graciousness costs me nothing."

* * *

The interview took forty minutes and produced three names, one rumor, and a map.

Marrec conducted it the way he conducted all interviews: seated, hands folded on the table, asking each question twice in slightly different words and noting where the answers diverged. The harbormaster, a damp man named Pellwick, told him about cargo that moved without manifests. The reeve, younger and more ambitious, told him about the Guild of Impossible Things — or rather, told him everything *except* its name, circling it the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

"There are dealers here," the reeve said finally, sweating despite the cold room. "In — commodities. Unusual ones."

"Memories," Marrec said pleasantly. "Names. Small miracles. Yes. I've read the file."

The reeve blinked. "You've — then you know—"

"I know what the Crown permits me to know, which is a different thing from knowing." Marrec poured tea for all three of them himself, which unsettled them more than shouting would have. "Tell me about the child."

There it was — the small silence, the shared glance between two men who disliked each other. Marrec let it breathe. Silence was a confession wearing plain clothes.

"A child came off a boat," Pellwick said at last, very quietly. "Or didn't come off a boat. There are stories. Barefoot in the storm, four nights ago. Walking through the floodwater like it was a road. Some say she went toward the tanner's row. Some say she vanished."

"Some say she spoke," the reeve added, "and the words weren't words. Old sounds. A man who heard her said his teeth ached afterward."

Marrec wrote nothing down. He never wrote during first interviews; writing made people edit themselves. He memorized instead, the way he had been trained to do in a school whose name appeared in no catalogue — a school where he had learned forty ways to remember and one way to forget.

"And the guild?" he said. "This covenant of yours. Where does it keep itself?"

Neither man would say it. Of course neither man would say it. The Guild of Impossible Things had survived two centuries of inquisitions by being everyone's supplier and no one's informant, by holding mortgages on half the town's griefs. A widow who had bought back an hour of her dead husband's voice would not testify against the shop that sold it. A thief who had sold his name to escape a hanging would not name the ledger that unmade him.

Marrec understood this perfectly. He did not resent it. Resentment was inefficient.

"Thank you both," he said, rising. "You've been most helpful. One more thing — purely administrative." He took from his case a sheet of heavy paper already bearing the Crown's countersignature, blank but for the heading. "I'll need your marks here, acknowledging that you were interviewed and disclosed what you disclosed. For the record. Nothing more."

They signed. They always signed. Men who signed such papers believed themselves safe from them; Marrec had never disabused anyone of this, because the belief kept towns quiet better than garrisons did.

When they were gone, he stood again at the window while the rain went on falling, and he thought about teeth that ached at old words.

* * *

The memory-dealer's shop was called the Kindly Hour, which Marrec found almost touching. It occupied a narrow building on Tanner's Row between a chandler and a house that sold mourning clothes, and its proprietor was a soft-bodied man named Ambrose Fell whose trade, according to the file, included memories of mothers, first loves, the smell of specific kitchens, and — in three documented instances — entire childhoods purchased from debtors too desperate to know what they were selling.

Marrec arrived at dusk with six sworn men and did not knock.

Fell was at his counter decanting something amber and glowing faintly into a stoppered vial, and he looked up with the expression of a man seeing weather he had predicted. "Inquisitor. I expected you sooner, honestly. The harbor stories travel faster than you do."

"You have a warrant of inspection," Marrec said, presenting it with both hands, courteously, the way one presented a knife hilt-first. "You will open your strongroom. You will produce your acquisition ledgers. My clerks will copy them. If anything in those ledgers references a child, a vessel, the old tongue, or the word *Hollow*, you will tell me everything you know, and I will consider your cooperation when sentencing comes."

"And if I don't?"

"Then sentencing comes differently."

Fell studied him for a long moment. Behind the counter, the little amber lights in their vials seemed to watch. "You're not like the last lot," Fell said. "The ones from the Chancellor's office, ten years back. They shouted. They broke my jars. One of them cried, would you believe it — some memory got loose out of a cracked vial and he lived someone else's wedding for an hour and wept like a bridegroom." He smiled without warmth. "You're not going to shout."

"No," Marrec agreed. "Shouting is for people who haven't finished thinking."

The strongroom yielded ledgers going back nineteen years, meticulously kept — Marrec noted, with professional appreciation, that Fell used a double-entry system and dated everything. His clerks worked by lamplight through the evening while Fell sat in his own shop drinking tea under guard, and near midnight, Ovelin brought the page across with a particular carelessness that Marrec had learned to read as meaning *this matters*.

An entry, eleven days old. *Purchased: one vessel, intact, provenance withheld. Seller: guild intermediary. Price: waived.*

"Waived," Marrec said softly.

Fell's face did something complicated. "A gift, ser. From the guild. They do that sometimes. Goodwill."

"The guild does not give gifts. The guild trades. If they gave you a vessel and waived payment, then either you owed them a debt I should know about—" Marrec let the sentence hang, watched Fell's eyes flick toward a floorboard near the cellar stair, and continued gently, "—or they wanted that particular item somewhere findable. Somewhere a search might plausibly reach. Somewhere it could be *seen to have passed through*."

"I don't know what they wanted."

"No. But you know where it went after it left you."

Silence. The lamps hissed. Outside, the rain had stopped, and in the new quiet Marrec could hear the harbor bells counting the hour — eleven strokes, unhurried, indifferent.

He sat down across from Fell and folded his hands, and when he spoke, his voice was almost kind. "Master Fell, I want to tell you a story. When I was a young clerk, I served under an inquisitor named Halvard Voss. Brilliant man. Ruthless, they said, but that's imprecise — ruthlessness is a blunt instrument and Voss was never blunt. What Voss understood was that fear has a shape, and if you can learn the shape of a man's fear, you need never touch him at all. He dismantled a smuggling ring in Callowmere without a single arrest. He simply visited each member, courteously, and described to each of them exactly what the others had already told him. Most of it was invention. None of them ever learned that." He paused. "Voss died in his bed, honored. I attended the funeral. It rained there too."

Fell had gone pale in the lamplight. "What is it you want me to tell you?"

"The truth. Which is cheaper than the alternative, and lasts longer." Marrec leaned forward slightly. "Where did the vessel go?"

And Fell — because he was a businessman, and businessmen understand arithmetic even when it horrifies them — told him. Not everything. But enough: a buyer from the guild's inner circle, a woman with river-grey eyes, a boat north under a false manifest, and a final destination that Fell swore on his mother's grave he had only heard whispered.

*"Thornbury,"* Fell said. "*Someone called Thornbury keeps the books.*"

Marrec wrote the name down. This once, he allowed himself the pleasure of ink.

* * *

He burned the Kindly Hour at dawn.

Not entirely — that would have been waste, and the inquisition wasted nothing. His men carried out every ledger, every vial, every jar of bottled remembering and stacked them in the sealed evidence wagon under triple lock. Then, with the proprietor standing barefoot in the street wrapped in a blanket, watching, Marrec set fire to the empty shop himself, using a taper from his own case, and stood beside Fell while it burned.

"Why?" Fell asked. His voice was steady. Marrec gave him credit for that.

"As a courtesy," Marrec said. "To you, and to everyone who watches it burn and draws conclusions. The Crown is in Lowmarsh. The Crown is patient. The Crown prefers warnings to funerals — but note, Master Fell, that the warning was still a fire." He looked at the flames eating through the signboard, through the painted hour, through the word *Kindly*. "Your ledgers are confiscated. Your trade is ended. In a year, if the Crown's business concludes cleanly and you have been neither needed nor missed, you may petition to rebuild elsewhere. Consider this mercy. It is genuine, and it is also a leash."

"You could have just arrested me."

"Arrest creates a martyr or a trial, and trials create questions. Fire creates only a story, and stories are easier to govern." Marrec adjusted his coat against the morning chill. "Besides — I promised you consideration for your cooperation. This is it. The difference between what I did and what I might have done. Hold onto that difference. It is the widest margin you will ever be given."

Fell watched the roof beams collapse in a fountain of sparks. "Do you enjoy it?" the man asked suddenly, not looking at him. "The courtesy. The arithmetic. Any of it?"

Marrec considered the question with the seriousness it did not deserve. "I enjoy order," he said. "It is the only thing I have ever enjoyed, and I have never needed more than one."

* * *

That afternoon he wrote his report to the capital, sealing it with hot wax and the crowned thorn, and then — alone in the wayhouse room with the shutters closed — he wrote a second letter that he did not send anywhere, because it was not a report at all. It was habit. Every night for thirty-one years, since the school, since before the school, he had written down the day in a private cipher of his own devising, and every morning he had burned the previous night's page unread.

He did not know why. He had stopped asking why around the twentieth year. Some rituals are load-bearing; you discover which ones only when you try to remove them, and Marrec had never seen the value in testing the walls of his own mind.

Tonight's entry, in the cipher no living soul could read:

*Vessel confirmed real. Child, female, approx. eight years. Passed through Fell (shop destroyed — message delivered). En route to or held by one Thornbury, keeper of guild records. The word Hollow recurs in source material with unusual capitalization and unusual fear. Request from capital specifies capture, condition unspecified, disposal discretionary. Discretionary. Someone is afraid of this child, and someone else wants her handled quietly, and I am the quiet handle.*

*Note: dreamed again of the sea. No reason. Have never cared for the sea.*

He held the page over the candle and watched the cipher curl into black lace, and as it burned he felt — as he felt every morning, so faintly he had catalogued it decades ago as indigestion — the sense of a word sitting just behind his teeth. A word in a language he did not speak. A word shaped like a debt.

Then it passed, as it always passed, and Dain Marrec rose, straightened his coat, and went down to brief his men, courteous and patient and utterly certain, a law with legs and a lantern, walking out into Lowmarsh to find a child who spoke the language underneath the world.

He did not yet know her name. But names, he had always found, were merely the last thing anyone tells you.
