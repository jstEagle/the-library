## 5. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

The forbidden ledgers lived three floors beneath the guildhall, behind a door that had no handle and answered only to blood older than the building.

Yara had known about the vault since she was nine years old, when Corvane had pressed her thumb to the black iron and told her that one day the door would know her better than she knew herself. She had thought it a kindness then, an inheritance. Now, with the child asleep two levels above in the ledger room among rows of recorded miracles, she understood it for what it was: a chain. The door did not open for members. It opened for keepers. And keepers were not permitted to look away from what they found.

Her blood sank into the seam of the iron and the lock thought about it for a long moment — long enough that Yara heard her own heartbeat twice — before the bolts withdrew like something unclenching its fist.

Inside, the air was cold and dry as old paper left too long in winter. Lamps kindled themselves at her approach, wicks catching on nothing, flames standing perfectly still in air that refused to move. The shelves ran back farther than the guildhall's footprint should have allowed. She had counted them once, years ago, and gotten a different number each time. Tonight she did not count. Tonight she walked past the ledgers of small miracles, past the ledgers of names and voices and borrowed hours, past everything she had spent her life believing was the whole of the trade, down to the shelf where the spines turned from brown leather to something darker. Something that might once have been hide.

The oldest books had no titles. They had warnings.

She pulled the first one free and felt the weight of it settle into her arms like a sleeping animal that might wake angry. The cover was stitched vellum over boards of thornwood — actual thornwood, grey and dense and faintly warm, though the vault was cold enough to fog breath. Her fingers remembered the grain of it before her mind placed where she'd felt it before.

The child's hair, when Yara had brushed storm-water out of it, had felt exactly like this.

She sat on the floor, because standing suddenly seemed presumptuous, and opened the book across her knees.

* * *

The early pages were not written so much as grown. Letters rose out of the vellum in ridges of scar tissue, dark brown ink gone nearly black with age. The hand changed every few pages — twelve hands, she would eventually understand, though she could not yet count them, because the script kept shifting between alphabets, some of which used no letters at all but small drawings of moons: full, full, full, and then a gap, and then more fulls, and always after the twelfth a space where a thirteenth moon should have been, drawn instead as a circle of thorns.

She read by lamplight while the vault held its breath around her.

*In the years of the Long Frost, when the world above forgot the shape of its own roots, there were twelve who carried blades that spoke. They were called swordspeakers, and their word was law not because kings granted it but because the world itself had agreed to be bound by it.*

*They went down beneath the thornline, all twelve, into the Hollow where the old things wait, and there they made a covenant. Not a treaty. A covenant — the difference being that a treaty can be broken and a covenant must be paid for.*

Yara turned the page. The ridged letters here were deeper, cut by a hand pressing hard enough to tear.

*The terms were these: the Hollow would keep its thorns below and the realms would keep their roads above, and between them would stand a border maintained not by walls but by words. Twelve oaths, spoken by twelve speakers, renewed at every turn of the moon. Eleven turns of grace, and on the twelfth the renewal, and the covenant would stand so long as there were twelve to speak it and a world willing to hear.*

*And there was a thirteenth thing.*

The lamp flames leaned toward the page, all of them at once, though no draft moved.

*A covenant of twelve is a wheel, complete and turning. But a wheel needs an axle. The thirteenth oath was never meant to be spoken. It was meant to be kept — a living vessel, a soul grown from the root-words themselves, in whom all twelve oaths could rest and remember themselves. The speakers did not create this vessel. They found it, or it found them, or it grew from the place where their oaths touched the soil of the Hollow. The records do not agree, and perhaps the truth is one of those things that changes depending on who is ashamed.*

*What the records do agree upon is this: the vessel was not stolen by enemies.*

Yara's hands had gone still on the page. Somewhere above her, muffled through stone, she heard the guild settling into its night noises — Sef laughing too loudly at something in the common room, the clink of the till, ordinary sounds from an ordinary night. They sounded very far away, and slightly obscene.

*On the night of the twelfth turn, in the four hundredth year of the covenant, eleven of the twelve speakers came to the vessel's keeping-place and took it. The twelfth speaker — the one whose hand wrote these words — refused, and was struck down by her own sisters and brothers, and her blade was broken and scattered, and her name was taken from every record so that even shame could not find her.*

*They did not steal the vessel to destroy it. That is the horror, and I will write it plainly so that no reader may soften it: they stole it to sell it.*

Yara heard herself make a sound. It was small, involuntary, the noise a person makes when stepping on something soft in the dark. The vault absorbed it without echo.

*A buyer had been found. Not a kingdom. Not a power. A company of traders who dealt in things that could not otherwise be owned — memories, names, hours, small impossibilities. They had offered a price beyond reckoning for a living fragment of the oldest covenant in the world. And eleven speakers, who had sworn their lives to maintain the border between the realms, looked at that price, and looked at their oaths, and chose the price.*

*We broke the covenant ourselves. There was no invasion. No dark rising. The thorns came up because we dug the hole.*

The page after that was smeared, as if water had fallen on wet ink long ago, or as if the writer's hand had failed. When the writing resumed it was smaller, cramped, hurried.

*I hid what I could. The vessel could not be returned — the buyers had already moved it, already begun to divide it, to study it, to learn how a soul made of covenant-words could be unbuilt into parts. So I did the only thing left. I spoke a grace into the broken wheel. One turn of the moon's shadow, stretched across centuries — a thirteenth turn that does not end until the vessel is whole again and the twelve oaths are gathered back to it. While the grace holds, the thorns stay mostly below. Mostly. The border bleeds a little more each year; you will have seen the thorned places spreading and been told it is weather, or blight, or the temper of the land. It is none of those. It is a debt accruing interest.*

*When the thirteenth turn completes — when the moon has risen full thirteen times without the covenant renewed — the grace ends. Not with thunder. Grace periods never end with thunder. They end with the quiet withdrawal of a promise, and then the world remembers what it was owed.*

Yara closed the book.

She sat for a long time in the cold with her palms flat on the cover, feeling the thornwood's faint warmth against her skin, and let herself understand, slowly and completely, whose warmth it was.

The Guild of Impossible Things had not been founded by smugglers who stumbled into greatness. It had been founded by oath-breakers laundering their theft into a trade. Every ledger upstairs — every widow's bought-back hour, every thief's sold name, every small miracle priced and stamped and shelved — stood on top of this: the sale of a living covenant, chopped into pieces, its fragments scattered to buyers whose names were themselves worth remembering and therefore had been erased. The guild's wealth was interest on a sin four hundred years old, and she had spent her life keeping its books neat.

She thought of Corvane's thumb pressed to hers, all those years ago. *One day the door will know you.* He had known. He had always known what was down here. He had raised her in a house built on a grave and taught her to call the grave a foundation.

Above her, faintly, the child laughed in her sleep.

Yara climbed the stairs with the book under her arm, and the door sealed behind her, and she did not go to bed.

* * *

The second ledger was easier reading and worse news.

It was a calendar, of a kind — a moon-record kept by successive keepers of the vault, each generation adding its observations in fresher ink. Yara traced the columns with her fingertip: dates of full moons, notes on the thornline's reach, small sketches of places where the border had thinned. A well in some village gone sweet, then bitter, then full of pale roots. A road that grew thorns overnight and had to be abandoned. Children born in the thorned places with eyes the color of deep water, speaking before they could walk.

Speaking languages older than any kingdom.

She found the pattern on her third pass through the columns. Every keeper had noticed it; none had written it plainly, as if naming it might hasten it. The grace period did not run on human time. It ran on lunar time — and the moon's shadow-turn, the thirteenth, was not a fixed span. It shortened whenever the vessel's fragments were moved, or sold, or harmed. Each transaction involving the stolen pieces had shaved time off the world's reprieve. The last keeper to make an entry — sixty years dead, his handwriting shaking — had calculated the remaining grace at roughly four hundred further lunations.

Yara did the arithmetic against the current almanac twice, then a third time with her eyes shut, hoping she had erred.

Thirteen full moons remained. Thirteen risings, and the last one — the one the old keepers had marked in every generation with a circle of thorns — fell on the longest night of the coming winter.

Unless the fragments had been moved recently. Unless someone, somewhere, had traded in a piece of the vessel without knowing what they held, and shaved the grace thinner still.

She thought of the child at the door, barefoot in the storm, arriving precisely when the moon hung fat and white above the guildhall like a coin waiting to be spent. Arriving as if delivered. As if returning.

Not a fragment, she realized, and her stomach turned over. Or not only a fragment. The old book had said the buyers had begun to *unbuild* the vessel — to divide it into parts. But somewhere in four hundred years of trades and resales and deaths and inheritances, the pieces had come back together, the way water finds its level. The child was not a shard of the covenant. The child was the covenant, regathered, walking on bare feet out of the rain to the one door in the world built on the crime against her.

She had knocked. Yara remembered the knock — polite, patient, three small raps. The vessel of the broken covenant had stood in the storm and *knocked*, and Corvane had ordered her turned away, and the guild had almost obeyed, and would call it prudence.

Yara sat in the ledger room with the forbidden book open on her knees and the child curled asleep in the alcove behind the false shelving, and she felt something in her chest that had no name she knew — part grief, part rage, part the specific vertigo of a woman discovering that the floor of her entire life had been a lid.

She had sworn oaths to this guild. She had spoken them with her hand on the great ledger, binding her service to its rules, and she had believed those rules were neutral — a smuggler's code, amoral and clean, taking no sides. But neutrality, she saw now, was the most elegant lie the founders had ever sold. You could not stand neutral on top of a grave. Standing there was a choice, made fresh every day, and the guild had been making it for four hundred years while calling it business.

Her oath-blade lay across her knees. It had hummed for the child. It had spoken — *Thirteenth turn* — its first words in twenty years, and she had filed the moment away as mystery, as oddity, as a problem to be solved on some less frightening day.

It had not been a mystery. It had been a summons. The blade was a descendant of the twelve — she saw that now, saw the whole shape of it — forged from the scattered metal of the speaker the others struck down. The nameless one. The one who wrote the warning and paid for it with her name and her sword. Yara was not a keeper of a smuggling guild. She was the heir of the only honest person in the founding generation, handed a broken sword and a set of cooked books and told to keep both sharp.

Outside the high window, the moon cleared the rooftops, full and pitiless, beginning its climb toward the longest night.

Twelve risings left after tonight. Twelve chances to gather oaths scattered across kingdoms and graves and whatever else four centuries could do to twelve promises. Against her: a Crown that hunted vessels of the old tongue, a guildmaster whose kindness she could no longer trust to have a bottom to it, and a ledger of rules she had served faithfully since childhood — rules that would demand, tomorrow morning, that she surrender the child to whoever asked with proper authority.

Yara looked down at the sleeping girl, at the storm-grey hair and the hands folded neatly as if even in sleep she waited to be told what she was for.

No, Yara thought. Not that. Whatever else the ledger demanded.

Then she opened the forbidden book to its final written page, where the nameless speaker had left one last instruction in a hand gone steady with resolve:

*If you are reading this, then the grace is failing and my blade has found a new voice. Gather the twelve. Begin with the second — it was kept in salt by the only one of us who chose the sea over surrender. And know this: an oath is a debt, and debts can be paid by anyone willing to bleed for them. The covenant does not ask for the worthy. It asks for the willing.*

Yara closed the book, blew out nothing — the lamps extinguished themselves, bowing — and in the dark she laid her palm flat on the humming blade and began, silently, to plan.

Above the rooftops, the first of the thirteen moons slid westward, spending itself.
