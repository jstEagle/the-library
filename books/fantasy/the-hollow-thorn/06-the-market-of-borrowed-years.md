## 6. The Market of Borrowed Years

The road to the Gullet began where the honest roads ended.

Yara had left the city before the fourth bell, Vess wrapped in a fishmonger's oilcloth and strapped to her back like contraband, because that was precisely what the child was now. Corvane's seal had bought them passage through the Saltgate without questions — his seal still carried weight in the lower wards, whatever else it carried — but past the gate there were no seals, only the long gray walk down the drowned causeway to the place the maps refused to name.

Vess did not speak. She hadn't since the vault, since Yara had read those forbidden pages by candle-stub and learned what the guild's founders had done with their clean hands and their careful ledgers. But she pressed her forehead against Yara's spine between her shoulder blades, a small warm weight, and when the wind came off the water she gripped the oilcloth tighter, and that was language enough.

*Thirteenth turn,* the blade had said, and it said nothing now either, but it rode at Yara's hip heavier than steel should be, humming its low note against her thigh like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

The causeway sank as they walked. That was the first thing strangers never believed about the Gullet: that the market literally went under. Twice a day the tide swallowed the old pilgrim road, and twice a day it gave it back, and in the hours between, the merchants of borrowed time set out their stalls on the exposed stones and sold to anyone desperate or curious enough to descend below the watermark of ordinary law.

Yara descended at low tide, when the sea had drawn back far enough to leave the market glistening like something coughed up from a whale's belly.

It was lit by lamps that burned underwater-colored, green and blue, their flames bending strangely in air that still remembered being ocean. Stalls of blackened timber lined both sides of the causeway, canopied in sailcloth gone soft with salt. And everywhere, everywhere, glass.

Glass vials, glass bells, glass eggs no larger than a sparrow's heart. In each one swam a faint light — gold, mostly, though some burned white and one or two flickered a sickly violet — and Yara knew without asking what the lights were. Years. Hours. Whole decades decanted from living bodies and stoppered for sale. A fisherman's stall displayed a row of them graded like pearls: *one year, unblemished; three years, minor weathering; twenty years, taken whole, owner deceased.* At the next table an old woman with a jeweler's loupe screwed into her eye socket held a vial up to a lamp and read the light inside the way a priest reads entrails.

"Fresh stock," the fisherman called as they passed. "Farmer's years. Clean living. They keep better than city years — less rust in them."

Yara kept walking. Vess's grip tightened.

She had dressed for the part, which meant dressing down: no guild colors, no oath-blade visible, though the blade was there beneath her cloak all the same, humming its unfinished note. She wore a smuggler's plain wool and the face she'd learned in her first year with the guild, the one that said *I have money and no imagination*, because in markets like this the dangerous customers were not the ones who looked hard. They were the ones who looked bored.

At the center of the causeway, where the road dipped lowest and the tide would return first, stood the counting house: a dome of driftwood and whalebone strung with ten thousand empty vials that chimed together in the wind like a cathedral of small dead sounds. Beneath it sat the Broker.

No one knew if the Broker had been a man once. What remained was a shape draped in layered gray, seated behind a table of green glass, its face hidden behind a mask of mother-of-pearl carved with the phases of the moon — twelve phases, Yara noted, and felt the blade hum sharper against her hip. Twelve. The old covenant's number. The thirteenth phase had been chiseled off the mask, leaving only a rough scar of shell.

"You carry a swordspeaker's steel," the Broker said. Its voice came from somewhere behind the mask, dry as sand shifting. "That is either very brave or very stupid, coming here."

"I'm told the difference is usually the price."

Something that might have been amusement moved the gray shoulders. "And what does the last of the Thornbury line want with the market of borrowed years? Your guild keeps its own accounts."

"My guild keeps ledgers." Yara set her palm flat on the green glass table. "I need to buy something that isn't for sale. Information. About the old oaths. About where a swordspeaker's vow goes when she dies without passing it on."

Silence ran down the causeway. Not metaphorically — Yara actually heard it, the haggling around them faltering stall by stall, the way sound dies when a name too large for the room gets spoken. A woman selling bottled laughter pulled her canopy closed. Somewhere a child — a real child, a market brat with a pickpocket's quick hands — stopped mid-theft and stared.

"The old oaths," the Broker repeated. It turned its moon-mask slowly toward the crowd, and the crowd remembered its business all at once. When it faced her again, the voice had dropped to almost nothing. "You should sit, Thornbury. Deals made standing up are deals made carelessly, and you will not want to be careless for this one."

There was no chair. There was never a chair at the Broker's table; that was part of the architecture of the negotiation, keeping buyers slightly off balance. Yara stayed standing.

"Everyone knows the swordspeakers are dust," the Broker said. "Twelve blades sworn at the Sundering, twelve graves filled within a generation of the breaking. Their vows didn't die with them. Vows don't die. They *go* somewhere." A gray hand unfolded from the robes, fingers too many-jointed, and tapped the green glass. "A vow is a debt, and debts have weight, and weight sinks. Into stone. Into water. Into bloodlines. Into—" the jointed finger rose and pointed, delicate as a surgeon, at Yara's hip, "—into steel that remembers."

"So you know where the first one went."

"I know where it *is*. Knowing where it is and getting it are separated by a toll." The mask tilted. "You've come to the right market, at least. Everything here is bought with the same coin. You brought years?"

"I brought memory."

For the first time, the Broker went truly still. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath through the chiming vials above.

"Memory," it said softly. "Not time. Time is cheap here, Thornbury. Time we trade by the barrel — I could sell you thirty years of some shepherd's life for the cost of a good horse. But memory..." The jointed fingers laced together. "Memory is time with a *face* on it. Memory is time that knows your name. Do you understand what you're offering? Because I think you walked down that causeway knowing exactly what you're offering, and I find that more interesting than any oath."

"How much for what I need?"

"The location of the first scattered oath costs three years of memory. Taken whole, chosen by me, unrecoverable." The mask leaned closer, and Yara saw herself reflected in twelve moons of pearl. "Or I can take it another way. Three years, but *you* choose what goes. Random extraction is cheaper on the soul — the mind heals around a hole it didn't dig. Chosen extraction leaves a scar shaped like exactly what you lost. Most people choose random." The pearl moons glittered. "Most people are wrong, incidentally. A known grief heals. An unknown one just bleeds."

Yara thought about that longer than she wanted to. Below them, at the causeway's drowned end, the first whisper of the returning tide licked at the outermost stalls, and vendors began folding their awnings with the unhurried ease of people who had raced this water a thousand times.

"Chosen," she said.

"Bold." The Broker unfolded from its seat, taller than it had seemed, robes pooling like poured ash. From within its layers it drew a scale — not brass, not iron, but bone, a balance carved from something's forearm, and Yara did not let herself wonder whose. On one pan it placed a vial of clear glass, empty, waiting. "Three years of memory. Give me the face."

Yara closed her eyes.

This was the arithmetic she had done on the causeway walk, done coldly, done like a ledger entry, because if she let herself do it any other way she would turn around and climb back to the honest world and let the moon run out its turns and let everything drown. She had spent her memories carefully in her head, ranking them, pricing them. Her years in the guild's training yard — useful, but replaceable by discipline. Her first kill, maybe; griefs were heavy but griefs didn't hold information she needed. Certainly not the child. Certainly not the vault, the forbidden pages, the names of the founders.

But the Broker asked for the face, singular. One memory, dense enough to weigh three years.

Her mother.

It surprised her, how quickly the choice arrived, how quietly. She had expected a war inside herself. Instead there was only a strange, practical clarity, the clarity of a merchant reading a bad hand of cards: her mother had died when Yara was nine. Twenty-five years of grief already worn smooth as river stone. The memory was precious the way amber is precious — beautiful, sealed, inert. It taught her nothing anymore. It only glowed.

*It only glows.* She heard her own reasoning and hated it and proceeded anyway, because the moon overhead was running down its turns like sand through a cracked hourglass, and somewhere out in the world a vow lay sunk in stone or water waiting for a swordspeaker who was currently bartering away her childhood.

"The face," the Broker prompted.

"My mother," Yara said. "All of her. Every year I had her. Nine years, compressed — call it the three heaviest. Her face, her voice, her hands. Take the lot."

The empty vial caught the lamplight and held it.

"Kneel," said the Broker.

She knelt on wet stone. Around them the market had gone quiet again, but this time no one pretended to work; they watched openly, the way people watch executions, because that is what a chosen extraction is. The Broker's many-jointed hands settled on her temples, cool as deep water, and Yara heard Vess make a sound behind her — small, wordless, urgent — and she wanted to turn and say *it's all right, little one, this is how it's done, this is the price of the road* but the hands tightened and the world went green as underwater lamplight.

Extraction did not hurt. That was the obscenity of it. It felt like remembering, only backward — like a memory being remembered so thoroughly that it left her entirely, packed and labeled and lifted out. She felt her mother's kitchen one last time: bread smell, rain on the window, a voice singing off-key on purpose because it made Yara laugh. She felt small hands — her own small hands — kneading dough badly. She felt a kiss pressed to her hair, damp from the bath.

*I am Yara Thornbury, and this is my mother, and her name is—*

Gone.

* * *

The tide was coming in when she stood up. She knew this because everyone was packing, fast now, canvas snapping, vials rattling into cases, the green lamps being snuffed one by one along the causeway until only the Broker's table still glowed.

On the table lay a slip of whalebone with characters scratched into it, fresh and sharp. Coordinates. A name half-eroded: *the chapel at Sorrowmere, drowned these sixty years.* The first oath. Kept, apparently, in salt.

Beside the slip, the vial. Inside it swam a light the color of late afternoon through kitchen windows — warm gold shot through with something softer, something that might have been the gleam off a well-scrubbed wooden spoon. Three years, weighed and priced. Her mother's face, floating in glass.

"Standard practice," the Broker said, "is that the seller doesn't look. Looking before the wound closes is how people go mad, trying to remember what they can't."

Yara looked anyway. She owed the memory that much — to be witnessed going.

The light was beautiful. That was the worst of it. It was beautiful and it meant nothing to her. She stared at the gold glow and felt the way you feel looking at a stranger's family portrait in a pawnshop window: aware that someone, somewhere, had loved this, unable to locate the love in her own chest. There was a hole where the warmth should have pooled and the hole was smooth-edged, surgical, complete.

And she was crying. She registered this with distant confusion — tears sliding hot down her face while the market packed up around her — and could find no reason for them anywhere in her body. Nothing hurt. Nothing had been taken that she could name. She was simply grieving, hugely and specifically, with the full force of a loss whose object had been removed, grief like an arrow still flying after the bow had vanished.

"Why," she managed. Her voice came out scraped. "Why am I—"

"Because the heart keeps books the mind can't burn," the Broker said. For a moment the pearl moons regarded her with something almost like gentleness. "You'll do that for weeks. Cry at kitchens. Cry at bread. Cry at songs you'll swear you've never heard. Don't fight it. The body remembers its debts even when the ledger page is torn out." The gray shape began folding itself back down behind the green glass table, becoming furniture again, becoming rumor. "Go collect your oath, Thornbury. And when you spend the rest of yourself — and you will, I can see the spending in you already, you have the look of a woman drawing down her own account — remember that I told you: a known grief heals. Go home someday and choose to learn what you lost. It's the only mercy this market sells for free."

* * *

She climbed the causeway stairs with the whalebone slip clenched in her fist and Vess's arms locked around her neck, and halfway up, above the watermark, above the honest world, she stopped.

"Sef," she said. The guild traitor's name had surfaced during the walk — one of the few usable facts from her research night, a junior factor who'd been asking questions about the vault. She'd need eyes on him while she was gone. "When we reach the ferry, I'm sending word ahead. Someone has to watch the vault while I'm at Sorrowmere."

Vess tugged her cloak. Yara looked down. The child was staring at her face — at the tear-tracks, still drying — with an expression far too old for the small frame, and then Vess reached up and touched Yara's cheek, right where the grief had fallen, and spoke one syllable of the old tongue. Soft. Almost tender.

The blade hummed at Yara's hip, and for the first time the hum resolved into something like a word she couldn't yet translate, and far to the south, past the ferry landing, past the salt marshes, thunder rolled over a drowned chapel that had been waiting sixty years for someone to come and pay its visitation fee.

Yara wiped her face with the back of her hand, cried a little more without knowing why, and walked north into the dark with a stranger's grief and a child's blessing and three fewer years to spend.

The moon, when she found it hanging over the marshes, was fat and silver and nearly full.

Two turns left. Maybe three, if the tides lied.

She intended to make them count.
