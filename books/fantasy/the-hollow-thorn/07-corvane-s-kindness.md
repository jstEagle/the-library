## 7. Corvane's Kindness

Corvane had not slept a full night in thirty-one years, and he had learned to make a virtue of it. The small hours belonged to the ledger vault, when the guild's business was done and the candles burned low and the arithmetic of survival could be done honestly, without an audience. He sat now at the long counting table with the winter accounts spread before him — tallow, salt-iron, three crates of bottled laughter that had gone sour in storage and would need to be sold as grief instead, at a loss — and he let himself, for one unguarded moment, simply listen.

The guildhouse breathed around him. Old timber settling. Rain against the shutters. The river muttering under the pilings. And beneath all of it, so faint he might have invented it, a sound like a struck bell heard through six feet of packed earth.

He knew that sound.

He had heard it once before, on a night very much like this one, thirty-one years ago, and it had cost him every night's sleep since.

Corvane closed the winter accounts. He did not light a lamp. He moved through the dark halls of his own house the way a man walks through a house where someone is dying — quietly, apologetically, knowing exactly which boards would betray him and stepping over each one by memory. Past the dormitory where the young smugglers slept in their rows like spoons in a drawer. Past the chapel they no longer used because prayer attracted attention. Down the narrow stair to the vault door, where he stopped and pressed his palm flat against the iron.

Warm. The vault door was warm, and it should have been cold as riverstone this deep in the earth.

* * *

He had told himself a hundred stories over the years about why he did what he did. That he protected the guild. That he protected Yara, who was worth ten of any other swordspeaker and half again as much trouble. That a man who had done one unforgivable thing could spend the rest of his life standing between that thing and everyone he loved, and call it penance, and almost believe it.

But standing at the warm door, Corvane admitted what he could admit only in the dark, only to himself: he was a coward. He had been a coward since the night of the twelfth turn of the moon, thirty-one years past, when twelve young fools with borrowed swords had stood in a circle under a bleeding sky and decided they deserved a thirteenth share of the world's mercy.

He turned the lock with hands that remembered how.

* * *

The vault was a cathedral of paper. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling in ranks, holding the ledgers of the impossible: names bought back from debtors' prisons, last words preserved in wax cylinders, one hour of a dead man's voice sold twice by mistake and repurchased at scandalous cost. The guild traded in everything the law said a person could not own, and every trade was written down here in Yara's immaculate hand, because Yara believed the ledger made them honest.

It didn't. It only made them legible. There was a difference, and the Crown's inquisitors understood the difference better than Yara ever had.

The child was asleep in the far alcove, curled on the old reading bench among the forbidden folios, wrapped in Yara's own grey cloak. She had pulled three volumes down around her like a nest — Corvane recognized two of them by their spines and felt his stomach drop. *The Founding Accounts.* The ledgers Yara was never supposed to find, and had found anyway, because she was Yara.

And beside her, upright against the bench, her oath-blade standing watch like a soldier who had refused relief for twenty years.

Corvane stood in the doorway for a long time, letting the cold air of the passage mix with the warm air of the vault, watching the girl breathe. She was maybe seven years old. Barefoot even in sleep, even in this season. Her hair was the color of wet ash and her skin carried a faint grey undertone, like moonlight diluted in milk, and when she exhaled, the candles along the shelf nearest her leaned toward her, just slightly, the way flowers lean at the sun.

She was more beautiful than the stories had promised, and that was the worst thing about her. Beautiful things made people brave, and bravery would get everyone in this room killed.

"Thirteenth turn," she murmured in her sleep, in the old tongue that was older than kingdoms, and translated itself somehow into his bones before his mind could object.

Corvane's knees nearly went. He caught himself on the doorframe.

Thirty-one years ago, another voice had said those words — an old woman named Hessa Vail, the last of the founding speakers still living then, dying by inches in a rented room above a dyer's shop while Corvane held her hand and pretended to be a nephew. "You stole her," Hessa had rasped, gripping him with a strength that had no business being in a body that empty. "Twelve oaths made the covenant, boy, and your masters cut out the thirteenth and called it profit. The moon counts to thirteen. When she finishes the count, the Hollow will come to collect, and it will collect from everyone, and it will start with the thieves." She had smiled then, a terrible, gap-toothed smile. "You're all thieves. Every soul who ever took a coin across this counter."

He had told himself she was mad. He had told himself that right up until the moment the Crown's inquisitors started asking after vessels of the old tongue, and the bell began ringing through the earth of the world, soft and patient, like something that knew exactly where it was going and was in no hurry.

Now he crossed the vault, quietly, and knelt beside the sleeping child, and looked at her properly for the first time.

Up close, the wrongness resolved into something almost like familiarity. He had seen that face before — not hers, but its architecture. In the founding portraits, the ones locked in the iron cabinet behind his desk: twelve faces arranged in a circle, and in the center of the circle, painted smaller, a thirteenth figure that the artists had rendered as a shaft of moonlight because no one living knew what to paint.

Someone had known, once. Someone had stolen a person and written her down as a clause in a contract.

"I'm sorry," Corvane whispered, and was startled to find he meant it, and startled further by how little good meaning it did.

The child opened her eyes.

They were pale, the grey of the sky before snow, and utterly awake though she had been sound asleep one breath before. She looked at him without fear, the way she had looked at everything, apparently — Yara's reports were meticulous — and then she reached out one small hand and touched his cheek, right over the old scar there, and said something in the rolling, root-deep language of the Hollow.

He didn't know the words. But he knew the tone. Everyone knew that tone. It was the tone a grandmother uses. The tone of someone looking at a frightened child and finding him wanting.

"Yes," he said hoarsely. "I expect I am."

* * *

He should have woken Yara. That was the sensible move, and Corvane had built a life on sensible moves. Instead he sat down on the floor of the vault, his back against the shelf, his bad hip complaining, and let the child climb into his lap as if he were furniture she had ordered, and he talked to her in a low voice while outside the rain kept up its work against the shutters.

"You can't stay here," he told her. "You understand that? They've come into the city already. Marrec's people. Wax seals on every door in the Sallow Quarter, informants paid in memory-tithe, the whole apparatus turning over like a plowed field looking for you. And when they find you — they won't kill you, girl. Killing is honest. They'll do worse. They'll take you apart to find out what makes you work, and they'll write it all down, and then they'll burn the notes."

The child studied his mouth as he spoke, tracking the words as if language were a trade route she was learning.

"And Yara." He closed his eyes. "Yara will stand in front of you. You've known her four days and she'll stand in front of you, because that's what she is, that's what the oath-blade made her before it ever spoke a word. She'll die for you and think it's duty, and it isn't, it's the same rot that got into me thirty-one years ago except she'll call it love and mean it, and God help me, that's worse."

The girl put her hand flat on his chest, over his heart, and left it there. Warm. Heavier than such a small hand should be, the way an oath is heavier than ink on a page.

"The founders thought they were owed," Corvane went on, quieter now. "That's the part nobody writes down. Not greed, not ambition — entitlement. Twelve miracles between them, twelve perfect oaths, and they looked at the wholeness of the covenant and thought, *we built this, why does the world keep the interest?* So they carved the thirteenth share out and hid it, and the hiding changed it, and it came out the other side as — " he gestured vaguely at her — "*you*. A child. A fragment walking around in a child." He laughed, and it came out broken. "They probably told themselves it was safer that way. Easier to hide. People will forgive you for stealing anything if you tell yourself you're keeping it safe."

The hand on his chest pressed, once. Not hard. Deliberate. Like a signature.

"I know," he said. "I know what I am. I signed the second theft, girl. Did Yara find that page yet? The dispersal order. The night they split you up — no. Not split you up." He corrected himself, and hated how careful the correction was, how lawyerly, after thirty years of practice. "The night they *hid* you. Thirteen pieces, scattered like seed grain, because a whole covenant can be reforged but a scattered one stays lost, they thought. I was twenty-six years old and I countersigned it because Hessa Vail scared me and my masters paid me and I wanted, more than anything in the world, for all of it to be someone else's problem."

The child looked at him with those snow-sky eyes, and Corvane understood, with the particular horror of a man seeing his own reflection in water he'd assumed was too dark to show anything, that she was not judging him.

She was pitying him. And the pity was worse.

"All right," he said roughly. "All right."

* * *

What he did next, he did quickly, before the sensible part of him could file an objection.

He went to the iron cabinet in his study and took out the founding portraits — all twelve, and the center panel with its painted shaft of moonlight — and he carried them down to the vault, and he fed them to the stove that heated the room, one panel at a time, watching twelve faces curl and blacken. Then he took the dispersal ledger, the true one, the one Yara hadn't found because he'd kept it behind a false spine labeled *Tallow, 40 Years*, and he weighed it in his hands for a long moment.

Then he set it in the child's lap.

"If they take this house," he said, "they'll find nothing that points to what you are. No portraits, no orders, no chain of custody. As far as the Crown ever learns, you're a mute orphan Yara took in out of charity, and charity is annoying but it isn't treason." He tapped the false-spined book. "This tells where the other pieces of you are hidden. Twelve locations. I've had thirty-one years to memorize it and I'm telling you now: I never will. Not under torture, not under tithe, not under anything Dain Marrec can imagine, and that man imagines a great deal. This book leaves this vault either in your hands or Yara's, or it burns."

The child hugged the ledger to her chest. Over its cover, her eyes held his.

"There's a condition," Corvane said, and here his voice did something he didn't authorize — it shook. "When this is over. However it ends. When the moon finishes her count and the covenant is mended or the world is ended or whatever happens when arithmetic like yours comes due — someone remembers what I was. Not the guild version. Not the kindly old guildmaster who shielded the innocent child. The true entry. Countersigned. Written down in the ledger in Yara's hand, in the same ink as everything else, so it weighs the same." He swallowed. "I've spent thirty-one years making sure no one could prove what I did. I'd like to spend what's left making sure someone finally can."

The child considered him for a long moment. Then she did something he had not seen her do for anyone, according to Yara's careful reports, not even for Yara herself.

She smiled.

It was a small smile, crooked, with too many teeth in it somehow, and it was the most frightening thing Corvane had seen in thirty-one years, and it was also — and this was the discovery that finished him — kind. Rot and kindness, kindness and rot, grown together like roots in one fistful of earth, indistinguishable, inseparable, both of them real.

She reached up and touched his scarred cheek again, and said one word. Not in the old tongue this time. In plain trade-common, the first word anyone had heard her speak, round and clear and slightly roughened, as though it had traveled a great distance to reach him:

"Ledger."

Then she lay down again among her nest of forbidden books, pulled Yara's cloak over her shoulder, and slept — truly slept this time, breath slowing, the candle flames straightening as she sank under — while Corvane sat on the cold floor beside her until dawn, keeping watch over the one honest thing he had ever done.

* * *

He was upstairs brewing tea he had no intention of drinking when the knock came at the front door. Three measured raps. Patient. Official.

Through the frosted pane of the door, in the grey light of a storm-washed morning, Corvane could see the shape of a man in a long coat, standing very still, and flanking him the shapes of four others, and above them all, held high so there could be no mistaking it, a disc of green wax the size of a dinner plate, stamped with the crowned eye of the Inquisition.

Corvane set down the teapot. He straightened his collar. He arranged his face into the expression of a tired merchant interrupted at breakfast, thirty years practiced, flawless.

Behind him, down in the vault, faint as a bell through six feet of earth, something began to hum.

"Well," Corvane said quietly, to no one. "Right on schedule."

He opened the door.
