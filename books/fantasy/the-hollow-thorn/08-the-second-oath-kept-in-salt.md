## 8. The Second Oath, Kept in Salt

The sea had taken the chapel a hundred years ago and never quite finished the job.

Yara stood at the tideline where the cliffs of Vaelmoor crumbled into gray water, watching the broken bell tower rise from the shallows like a finger pointing at nothing. The tide was going out — it would be out for three hours, maybe four, and the old fisherwoman who had sold her directions for two silver coins and one small memory (the taste of her grandmother's plum jam; Yara had paid it without flinching, though she'd felt the absence all morning like a missing tooth) had been very clear about the arithmetic of the place.

*Three hours out, four hours back in. The chapel drowns twice a day, girl, and it doesn't care what you're doing inside when it does.*

The wind came off the water carrying salt and rot and something older underneath both — a smell Yara had no name for until she realized it was the smell of kept promises. She didn't know how she knew that. Her blade knew, and lately its knowledge bled into hers through the hilt like ink through wet paper.

She checked her gear one last time. Rope, lamp with oilskin over the glass, the ledger wrapped in waxed cloth against her chest where it rode closest to her heart. And the sword — *Oathkeeper*, though she hadn't known its name until the vault, until the child, until everything — hanging at her hip, heavier than any sword had a right to be.

It had grown heavier every day since she'd touched Vess. She had weighed it on the guild's brass scales out of superstition, then out of dread: seven pounds when she'd carried it for twenty years without thought. Nine pounds now. She did not know what it would weigh when this was done, or whether there was a weight at which a person simply could not lift a sword anymore, and she suspected she was going to find out.

"Thirteenth turn," she said to it quietly, the way she'd begun saying good morning.

The blade hummed. Low, patient, like a dog that has decided to trust you but hasn't forgotten the last hand that held it.

She went down the cliff path.

* * *

The causeway to the chapel revealed itself as the tide withdrew, a spine of black stone slick with weed and barnacle, winding out across the exposed seabed. Tide pools glinted on either side of it, each one a small imprisoned world — anemones like clenched fists, a stranded crab marching with absurd dignity toward water that had abandoned it. The sky above Vaelmoor was the color of unpolished pewter, and the light came down flat and sourceless, so that everything looked like a memory of itself rather than the thing.

Yara walked carefully. She had spent her whole life being careful, and only recently understood that carefulness was just fear wearing respectable clothes. But some fears were earned. The seabed around her was littered with things the sea had taken and not digested: a ship's figurehead with its face worn smooth, half a carriage door, ribs of timber that might have been a boat or a house. People had come out here before her. People had miscounted the tide before her too, probably.

The chapel doors were gone. What remained was a mouth of stone, dark water still pooled shin-deep in the nave, draining slowly through cracks in the floor. Inside, the air was cold and mineral and strangely sweet, like the inside of a shell.

Yara lit the lamp and lifted it high.

Pillars marched away into gloom, furred white with calcification. The altar at the far end had become a reef — coral had grown up over it in slow centuries, branching pink and bone-white, and it caught her lamplight and threw it back in fragments, so that the whole sanctuary glittered faintly, like a room full of held breath.

And in the center of the reef, grown into the stone where the priest would have stood, was a woman.

Yara stopped breathing.

At first she thought it was a statue — some pious carving the sea had colonized. Then she saw the hands, and knew no sculptor had ever made anything so precise and so human. The hands were raised, palms open, in the gesture swordspeakers used when swearing witness. The arms were coral to the elbow, branches of rose-pink spreading from the wrists and shoulders and the crown of the head, but the face between them —

The face was still a face. Not flesh anymore, exactly. Something between stone and nacre, features softened by decades of the sea's slow work, but intact. Eyes closed. An expression that was not peace, exactly. It was more terrible than peace. It was the expression of someone who had been given a choice and had chosen, and was still choosing, every moment, forever.

"Oh," Yara said. It came out very small in the big cold room. "Oh, you stayed."

The blade at her hip sang.

Not hummed — *sang*, one clear note that rang off the pillars and came back doubled, and Yara felt it pull against her hip like a hound scenting home. She staggered a step forward, then another, wading through the last of the retreating water, her boots sending ripples across pools where tiny shrimp scattered like sparks.

There were words carved into the base of the altar. The sea had eaten most of them, but the deep-cut letters survived, and Yara knelt in the cold water and traced them with numb fingers:

**HERE STANDS SERRA OF THE TIDE-COUNT, SECOND SPEAKER OF THE TWELVE, WHO WAS OFFERED THE SEA'S MERCY AND DECLINED IT. HER WORD HOLDS. HER WORD HOLDS. HER WORD HOLDS.**

Three times. Carved by different hands, by the look of it — one deep and sure, one hurried, one so shallow it was nearly gone. Three people had come here after her death — after her *becoming* — and each had needed to write it down, as if the fact were too large to hold in memory alone.

Yara sat back on her heels in the flooded nave and let herself feel it, because she had learned in the last weeks that if she didn't let herself feel things they came back later with interest.

Serra of the Tide-Count had stood here while the water rose. That was the story the ledgers hinted at, the story Yara had pieced together from a madwoman's ravings and a grave-goods inventory and Corvane's reluctant, poisoned confidences: when the covenant broke and the twelve oaths scattered, each speaker had been given a choice. Flee and live, oathless. Or stay, and hold.

Serra had held. The sea had risen around her, and she had not moved, and the sea — which is older than kingdoms and honors what it cannot digest — had turned her into this. Coral. Witness. A promise so thoroughly kept that it had become geography.

"I don't know how to do what you did," Yara told her. Her voice echoed strangely off the wet stone. "I've broken small promises my whole life. Little ones. I tell myself they don't count." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I keep a ledger of impossible things and I can't keep my own word about remembering to write to people."

The blade sang again, softer now. Chiding, almost. Or encouraging. With Oathkeeper it was hard to tell the difference, and she suspected that was the point.

She unwrapped the ledger anyway, right there, kneeling in the chapel of the woman who had become a reef. Because rules were rules, and if she was going to learn to keep promises she would start with the ones she already knew how to keep.

*Entry the forty-first,* she wrote, her pencil scratching in the lamplight against the sound of dripping water. *Site of the second oath. Drowned chapel of Vaelmoor. Speaker: Serra of the Tide-Count. Status: kept.*

Underneath, after a moment, she added: *I hope I am worthy of what you're about to give me.*

Then she stood, and drew the sword.

* * *

Taking the oath was not like taking treasure. Yara had imagined — she admitted it to herself now, wading toward the altar with the blade bare in both hands — that she would cut the coral free, or pry loose some relic, some token, some portable piece of Serra's keeping. That was how the guild worked. Everything was extractable. Everything had a price, and once you paid it, the thing was yours.

But when she raised the blade above the coral altar, Oathkeeper refused to fall.

It hung in her grip like a thing swimming upstream. She strained — actually strained, arms trembling, the sword suddenly weighing twenty pounds, thirty — and the blade would not descend. Instead it turned. Gently, irresistibly, the way a current turns a swimmer, until the point faced not the coral but Yara's own chest.

Her heart slammed. She did not let go. Some instinct older than fear told her that letting go would be worse.

"What do you want?" she whispered.

The answer came not in words but in understanding, flooding up her arms the way the tide floods a channel: *Ask.*

Yara looked at the sleeping face of Serra of the Tide-Count. At the open palms. At the carved words — *her word holds* — cut three times by three grieving hands.

"You're not treasure," she said slowly. "You're a creditor."

The blade hummed assent.

So Yara did the thing the guild had never taught her, the thing no entry in the Ledger of Small Miracles had ever recorded, because the guild traded in everything except this. She lowered the sword. She knelt in the cold seawater before the coral woman, laid Oathkeeper flat across her open palms the way a squire presents a blade to a knight, and bowed her head.

"Serra of the Tide-Count," she said, and her voice shook and she let it shake. "Second speaker of the twelve. My name is Yara Thornbury. I'm the last of the order, or near enough, and I've come to gather what was scattered. Your guild — our guild — stole the thirteenth oath. A child. A living covenant. I have her hidden in a vault under a smugglers' den, and I would die before I gave her to the Crown, and I am asking—" her voice cracked, and she pushed through it— "I am asking you to lend me your keeping. Not take. Lend. I'll carry your word with mine until the circle closes, and then I'll bring it back to the sea myself if I have to crawl here on my hands."

Silence. Water dripped. Somewhere far off, the sea breathed against the causeway.

Then the coral began to glow.

It started at the fingertips — a pale rose light moving down the branches like warmth returning to frozen limbs — and spread inward, along the arms, up the throat, until the whole altar burned softly and the nave filled with underwater-colored light, green and pink and pearl. Yara's shadow stretched huge and wavering up the pillars behind her.

The face did not wake. But the mouth moved — barely, a shift in the nacre of the lips — and a voice came that was not sound so much as pressure, felt in the sternum, in the teeth, in the water soaking Yara's knees:

***Who holds my word?***

"I do," Yara said. "Yara Thornbury. Last oath-bound swordspeaker. Whatever's left of one."

***What will you pay?***

And there it was. There was always a price; she should have known; the guild's oldest law was written in her bones even here, even now. She thought of the market of borrowed years, of the coin she'd already spent, of her mother's face gone soft and blank in her memory like a portrait left out in rain. She thought of what else she could afford to lose, and ran down the inventory, and found it shorter than she wanted.

"My memories," she said. "That's the currency. I know. Take what the debt requires."

***No.***

The refusal hit her like a wave, and she rocked back on her heels.

***That price buys nothing here.*** The voice was vast and tired and gentle. ***Memory is what you spend when you sell. Keeping is what you spend when you swear. They are not the same coin. If you would hold my word, you must give me yours — not what you remember. What you will do.***

"I don't understand."

***Swear.***

And Yara, kneeling in a drowned church before a woman who had let the ocean bury her rather than move one inch from her post, understood at last, and it terrified her more than Marrec, more than the thirteenth moon, more than anything, because it was simple.

She gripped the flat of the blade until the edge bit her palm, and she spoke into the glowing dark:

"I swear to carry the second oath with honor until the covenant stands reforged. I swear to guard the child called Vess as my own blood, before guild, before Crown, before my own life. I swear I will not trade what I am given, will not sell what I am trusted with, will not run when the water rises. If I break this, let the blade take me. Let the sea have what the sea is owed."

The words left her and did not echo. The chapel swallowed them whole.

For one long moment, nothing.

Then Serra's coral hand — the real one beneath the reef, the one that had waited a century — closed, gently, impossibly, around Yara's wrist. The touch was cool and hard and kind.

***Witnessed,*** said the voice, and it was smiling now, she was sure of it, ***and accepted, and entered into the record of things that cannot be undone. Rise, little ledger-keeper. You are heavier than you were.***

The light went out like a tide withdrawing.

* * *

Afterward Yara sat on the altar steps, shivering, and watched the blade drink.

There was no other word for it. Oathkeeper lay across her knees, and along its fuller a thread of rose-pink light moved slowly from hilt to tip and back again, the way blood moves under skin, and as it moved the steel changed. Darkened, slightly. Took on at certain angles a nacreous sheen, mother-of-pearl buried in the metal. And grew heavier.

She felt it happen. Not gradually — in increments, like sand poured grain by grain onto a scale. Seven pounds became nine became eleven, and the balance of the sword shifted too, settling deeper, wanting to point at the ground, at the earth, at graves. By the time the light stilled, the sword weighed what a sword weighs when it is also a responsibility.

Yara tried to lift it one-handed and could not. Two-handed, she got it up, barely, and held it before her face, and looked down the length of it at her own warped reflection in the darkened steel.

A stranger looked back. Thinner than she remembered being. Paler. With salt drying white in her hair and a new line between her brows that hadn't been there a season ago. She tried to think of her mother's face and found the place where it had been, smooth and vacant, and grieved without knowing for whom — a grief with no address, wandering her body looking for a door.

"Two," she said aloud. Her voice sounded loud and alone in the emptying chapel. "Two of twelve."

Outside, the light had changed. The pewter sky had gone the color of lead, and the wind had swung around, and when she limped to the chapel mouth she saw the causeway already vanishing — black water sliding up the spine of stone from both ends at once, patient, unhurried, certain.

The fisherwoman's arithmetic. Three hours out, four hours back. She had sworn oaths in between, and oaths took time, and the sea did not care.

Yara wrapped the ledger tight against her chest, slung the too-heavy sword across her back where its new weight dragged at her like a second person clinging on, and started to run.

Halfway along the causeway, with the water at her knees and rising, she saw the boat.

It came out of the murk beyond the point — a long, low hull flying no colors she recognized, oars stroking in perfect silence, which no honest boat ever did. Figures stood in it. Six of them, robed in the gray of the Crown's service, and at the prow a tall man with his hands folded behind his back, watching her approach across the drowning stones with the calm, courteous attention of someone who has already read the ending of the book.

Inquisitor Dain Marrec lifted one hand — not a threat, not yet. Almost a greeting.

"Swordspeaker!" he called across the water, and his voice carried impossibly clear over the wind. "You look burdened. Might I offer you a ride — and a conversation?"

Yara stood in the rising sea, the second oath heavy between her shoulder blades, and understood that the tide was the least of the things closing in on her.
