## 9. Marrec Offers Mercy

Dain Marrec had learned long ago that the best place to catch a smuggler was not at the door of her den but on the road between two safe places — in that thin country of the night where no one was yet guilty and no one was yet free. So he waited for Yara Thornbury in the rain, beneath the leaning eave of a shuttered chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, and he let the water run off the brim of his hat while the city slept its dishonest sleep.

He had been waiting three hours. He did not mind. Patience was the first tool of his trade, sharper than any writ or brand.

The file on Yara Thornbury lay against his ribs inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth: eleven years of the guild's small crimes recorded in a clerk's careful hand. The widow's bought hour. The thief's sold name. Forty-one counts of trafficking in contraband memory, nine of identity theft, one — the entry that had made him sit very still when he first read it — of *possession of an unregistered miracle*. He knew her handwriting from the ledger pages they had seized in a raid two winters past, before he ever knew her face. It was precise handwriting. A hand that believed in rules. That was what made her dangerous, and it was also what made him certain, tonight, that she could be saved.

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the row a cat argued with a drainpipe. And then, without hurry, without any of the wariness he had expected, Yara Thornbury came walking out of the dark with her hood down and her sword at her hip, as if she were simply a woman going home through bad weather.

Marrec stepped out of the eave's shadow and into her path.

She stopped. He watched her take stock of him the way a swordspeaker would — weight, distance, exits — and he watched her arrive, with visible reluctance, at the conclusion that none of it mattered, because there were six of his people on the rooftops and she had already counted them.

"Inquisitor," she said. Rain dripped from her jaw. "You're far from your bonfires."

"That was regrettable business." He meant it. The memory-dealer's shop on Tanner's Lane had burned with more enthusiasm than precision; the man himself had escaped out a back window with nothing but his own name, which was, Marrec supposed, more than most of his customers could say. "I ordered the sign burned. My people are fond of fire. I've spoken to them about it."

"Have you arrested them?"

"No."

"Then we understand each other." She did not reach for her blade. Her hands stayed loose at her sides, and her eyes — grey, level, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep — stayed on his face. "Say what you came to say, Inquisitor. You didn't soak three hours on a street corner to exchange pleasantries."

*Good,* Marrec thought. *Direct. Honest under pressure. This is a woman who can be reasoned with.* He had faced the other kind often enough — the ones who lied beautifully, who made cruelty into a game. Those he broke. But the honest ones, the rule-keepers, the ones who had wandered off the true road by small steps and good intentions — those he could sometimes lead back. And leading them back was, he genuinely believed, the whole point of the inquisition. Not the burning. Never the burning. The burning was only what happened when reasoning failed.

"The child," he said.

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, guarded, gone. There it was. He filed it away with the satisfaction of a man fitting the last piece into a lock.

"There is no child," Yara said.

"There is a child who arrived at your guild door nineteen days ago, barefoot, in a storm, speaking a language that predates every kingdom on this coast. There is a child whom your oath-blade answered like a bell answering its twin. There is a child who is, by every measure my office possesses, a vessel of the old tongue." He let the rain fill the pause. "And there is a woman named Yara Thornbury who has broken guild law for the first time in eleven years of immaculate ledgers in order to hide that child in a vault."

"You have spies in the guild."

"I have patience in the guild. It amounts to the same thing." He tilted his head, studying her. "You're wondering which of them it was. Don't. It doesn't matter, and knowing would only cost you sleep you'll need."

"Why?" The word came out of her flat and hard. "Why does the Crown care about one mute child? Your inquisition burns memory-dealers because stolen memories unmake families. Fine. I understand the law's logic even when I despise its methods. But a child? What threat does a barefoot girl pose to the throne?"

And there it was — the question he had hoped she would ask, because it was the door through which everything else must come.

"May I show you something?" he said, and drew from his coat not the file but a smaller thing: a strip of vellum, old, cracked along one edge, sealed with wax that had once been black and had faded to the color of dried blood. He held it out flat on his palm so the rain would not touch it. "This is four hundred years old. It was found in the foundation stones of the first guildhouse — your guildhouse — when it was rebuilt after the fire of the Interregnum. My office has kept it ever since. Do you know what it says?"

Yara looked at the vellum for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — quieter, careful, the voice of a woman stepping onto ice. "No."

"It is a fragment of the covenant. The old pact between the human realms and the Hollow — the thorned world beneath. Twelve swordspeakers swore it, each upon her blade, each binding a portion of her life to keep the two worlds knit closed. And there was to have been a thirteenth." He turned the vellum slightly, though there was nothing more to see. "A keystone. A living seal. The fragment ends mid-sentence, but scholars agree on what was cut away: the thirteenth speaker was never sworn. She was taken. Stolen before her oath could be given, and hidden — or sold — somewhere in the world of men."

The rain drummed on the eaves. On the rooftops, his people waited, patient as gargoyle stone.

"You believe the child is—" Yara began.

"I do not believe anything," Marrec said. "Belief is for priests. I know what the fragment says, I know what the old tongue sounds like when it is spoken by a throat too young to have learned deceit, and I know that since the night that child entered your guildhouse, the moon has been wrong." He saw her flinch — actually flinch, a small involuntary tightening around the eyes — and pressed gently on. "You've seen it. The halo. The second shadow it casts on clear nights. Thirteen turns, Mistress Thornbury. The moon will complete its thirteenth turn since the child's arrival, and every text my office possesses agrees on what happens then if the covenant remains broken. The Hollow does not stay beneath. It comes up. Through every crack. Through every thorn hedge, every cellar, every grave that wasn't dug deep enough."

"And the Crown's answer," Yara said slowly, "is to burn shops and hunt children."

"The Crown's answer," Marrec said, "is whatever I tell it the answer is. Which brings me to why I am standing in the rain talking to a smuggler instead of dragging her to the cells." He put the vellum away and let his hands hang open at his sides — deliberately, visibly empty, a gesture he had practiced until it looked like honesty. "Here is my offer. Listen to all of it before you refuse."

She said nothing. Rain ran down her face and she did not wipe it away.

"Surrender the child to me. Tonight, quietly, without a trial, without the guild's name entering a single document. In exchange, I lose this." He drew the file from inside his coat — the oilcloth bundle, eleven years of her life in a clerk's hand — and held it where she could see it. "I lose it completely. A clerical error. A fire in the archive, perhaps; my people are fond of fire, as I said. The Guild of Impossible Things ceases to exist in the eyes of the law. No raids. No arrests. No chains. You go back to your ledgers and your small miracles, and the Crown goes back to pretending your kind doesn't exist, which suits everyone."

"And the child," Yara said. Her voice was very steady. "What happens to the child in this generous arrangement of yours?"

"She comes to the Citadel. She is studied. Protected. Fed, clothed, kept warm—"

"Kept in a cell."

"Kept in *safety*," Marrec said, and heard the steel come into his own voice before he could soften it. He made himself soften it. "Mistress Thornbury. I am going to speak plainly, because I think you are a woman who respects plainness, and because I believe — I truly believe — that you have not thought past the size of your own courage. What is your plan? Keep her in a vault? Run? Run where? If the moon fails on its thirteenth turn, there is no *where*. There is no vault deep enough, no border far enough. The Hollow does not conquer, it does not negotiate, it does not grieve. It grows. I have read the accounts of the last breach — the Thornwood, four centuries ago, before the covenant. Whole valleys. Ten thousand souls, and not one body found, and the trees that grew afterward bore fruit that whispered. Is that what you want for her? For your city? Because you find a cell distasteful?"

"You'd study her," Yara said. "You'd cut her open to see how the miracle works."

"If it came to that, yes." He did not look away. He never looked away; looking away was how inquisitors lost arguments and then lost cities. "Better one child opened than ten thousand swallowed. That arithmetic is ugly. I have carried uglier. But it is *true* arithmetic, and someone in this conversation has to be doing it, because you are doing something else entirely. You are doing feeling."

Yara's hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to rest on the hilt of her sword. Marrec noted it and did not comment. The blade, his informants said, hummed near the child. He wondered if it hummed now.

"You think I'm cruel," he went on, quieter. "Every smuggler thinks so. Every thief, every dealer in stolen hours. They think the law is cruelty wearing a uniform. But I have stood in the ashes of what happens when the law arrives too late — I served on the Salt Coast, Mistress Thornbury, before I wore this coat. I saw what a town looks like after the Hollow comes up through it. I counted the shoes. Four hundred pairs of shoes in a square, and no feet in any of them, and the well water tasting of iron and honey for a generation. So yes — I will burn a shop as a warning. I will offer you a bargain that costs a child her freedom, because her freedom is already spent; it was spent four hundred years ago by your guild's founders when they stole her from her oath. All that remains to be decided is whether she spends what's left in a warm room with scholars, or in a thornwood with things that wear the faces of the dead." He extended the file toward her, arm straight, rain sliding down his sleeve. "Take it. Burn it yourself, if you like. That way you'll know I kept my word — and you'll know what keeping your word to me buys."

For a long moment she did not move. The rain fell between them, silvering the lamplight from the corner of the row, and Marrec watched her face with the attention he gave to difficult texts — reading not what she said but what she was, the architecture of her. A woman built entirely of rules, he thought. Rules and loneliness. He had read such women before. They always believed their rigidity was armor. It was not. It was a wall with no one behind it, and walls like that could be walked through by anyone who offered, sincerely enough, to stand guard.

That was the pity of it. That was the tragedy he had seen a hundred times and would see a hundred more: that people like Yara Thornbury could not be threatened into righteousness, but they could be *argued* into it, if the argument were cruel enough and true enough. Lawful cruelty was seductive precisely because it worked. Because every step of it made sense. Because at the end of it stood a warm room and a saved city and a clean conscience, and the only price was one small mute girl who had already been stolen once and would never know the difference.

*You are saving her,* he told himself, holding the file out into the rain. *Whatever she chooses, you are saving her. Remember that.*

Yara reached out.

His heart lifted — and then her hand closed not on the file but on his wrist, firm and cold with rainwater, and she leaned close, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, meant for him alone beneath the drumming eaves.

"Four hundred years ago," she said, "twelve swordspeakers swore a covenant, and a thirteenth was stolen. Do you know what happened to the twelve, Inquisitor? I've been reading your forbidden texts — yes, we have our spies too, and ours are better. One became coral rather than break her word. One walks a riverbed still. One went mad keeping hers too well. An oath to the Hollow isn't a duty, Marrec. It's a debt, and debts of that size don't get paid by scholars in warm rooms. They get paid in everything you are." Her grip tightened. "So here is my counter-offer, and I'll make it plain, since you respect plainness. Come to the drowned chapel at Greyhaven on the night of the next dark moon. Come alone. And bring the fragment — the whole of it, everything your office keeps. Because I think you've been reading that vellum wrong for four hundred years. I think the Crown didn't find a warning in your archives." Her grey eyes held his, and there was no hatred in them at all, which was somehow worse. "I think it found instructions."

She released his wrist, stepped back into the rain, and was turning away when Marrec — who had cornered kings' bastards and broken archheretics and never once been left without the last word — found, to his genuine surprise, that he had none.

"One more thing," Yara said over her shoulder, unhurried, almost gentle. "Your informants are good. Mine are better. Ask yours who signed the guild's founding charter, and where that signature came from, and why the wax on it was black." A pause, and the ghost of something that was not quite a smile. "Then ask yourself, Inquisitor, why an office devoted to hunting the Hollow has spent four centuries keeping the Hollow's own paperwork."

She walked away down Candlewick Row, hood still down, rain streaming off her, sword humming — he could hear it now, faintly, a note like a struck glass — and did not look back.

Marrec stood a long time in the wet dark with the file in his hand, and above the rooftops, through a tear in the clouds, the moon hung with its second shadow, thin as a knife's edge, counting down.

Thirteen turns. Eleven remaining.

And in his breast pocket, against his heart, the old black-wax seal seemed, for the first time in four hundred years, to be warm.
