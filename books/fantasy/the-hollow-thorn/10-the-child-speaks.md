## 10. The Child Speaks

The third oath had come to them wrapped in a fisherman's net.

Yara had found it three days ago in the drowned shallows off Greywater Point, where an old swordspeaker named Ilset had walked into the sea rather than outlive her promise — and the sea, being honest in the way that only large and patient things are honest, had kept her word for her. The blade had been crusted white with salt-bloom, its edge soft as old cheese, but when Yara's own sword touched it beneath the water, the two of them had *sung* — one long note that traveled up her arm and lodged somewhere behind her sternum, where it still hummed now, days later, like a struck bell refusing to finish its business.

She carried the salt-oath wrapped in oilcloth against her back. It was heavier than any blade had a right to be. Kept promises, she was learning, weighed more than broken ones. Broken ones were light as ash; you could scatter them anywhere.

Now the moon rose over the ruined mill above Greywater, and Yara sat on a fallen beam with Vess curled against her side, watching that pale coin climb through the ragged teeth of clouds. The child had not spoken since they'd fled the burning vault nine days ago. She made sounds — small, urgent, musical sounds in the old tongue that made Yara's blade tremble in its wrapping — but no words that meant anything Yara could hold.

Except tonight, under this particular moon, with the third oath's song still living in Yara's chest, the air felt different. Thinner. As if the world were holding a page open.

"Vess," Yara said — the name she'd given the child herself, borrowed from nothing, because a person without a name was cargo and this child was not cargo. "Can you understand me?"

The child looked up. Her eyes caught the moonlight and did something eyes should not do: they held it, kept it, let it go slowly, like water through cupped hands.

Then, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge, she said:

"I understand *you*. I have always understood you. You are the one who writes everything down."

Yara's whole body went still. Not just her limbs — something deeper, the way a forest goes still before a storm decides which way to fall. The child's voice was small and clear and wrong in one impossible particular: there were echoes in it. Not the echo of an empty room. The echo of many rooms. Many throats. As if twelve voices stood behind that single small sound, all speaking at once, all agreeing to speak as one.

"You can talk." Yara's voice cracked on the second word. She laughed, or something near laughing, and pressed her hand flat against the beam to steady herself. "Nine days. Nine days I've talked at you like a madwoman, narrating every river crossing, and you could talk."

"I could not." Vess frowned, testing her own mouth the way one tests a limb after sleeping wrong on it. "Words need... a door. Your sword opened a door when it sang. The other two helped. They were waiting to be asked." She touched Yara's arm, gravely, formally, the way one touches a stranger one has decided to trust anyway. "You have been asking without knowing it. That is what oaths do. They ask."

*Thirteenth turn.* The words her blade had spoken twenty years ago and then again in the vault. Yara's hand drifted to the hilt at her hip, and the sword hummed, low and content, like a dog recognizing its master's footsteps.

"What's your name?" Yara asked. "Your true one. Not the one I gave you."

Vess considered this for a long time. Moonlight moved across her face. When she answered, the twelvefold echo was stronger, and each voice seemed to carry a different weight — some old, some young, one of them unmistakably a woman who had loved the sea enough to die in it.

"I was called Vess last. Before that, I was called Maren-of-the-Ninth-Oath. Before that, Odile. Before that—" The child stopped. Her small brow folded. "I don't know how to say 'I' correctly. I was twelve people once. Twelve swordspeakers who sealed the covenant between the human realms and the Hollow, on the night of the first full thirteenth turn, four hundred years ago. We swore ourselves into the pact itself. We became its living hinge." She said these enormous things plainly, the way children say enormous things, having not yet learned to apologize for the size of what they know. "And then your guild stole us."

The words landed like cold water down the spine.

"The guild didn't steal a *child*," Yara said slowly. "The guild stole—"

"Us. All of us. Folded together. Compressed into something small enough to carry in a cart, small enough to sell, small enough to forget." Vess's voice did not rise. That was the worst part. There was no anger in it, only a terrible bookkeeping clarity, and Yara thought absurdly: *she would have made a fine ledger-keeper.* "Twelve oaths, pulled out of the covenant and bound into one vessel. The founders of your Guild of Impossible Things needed a miracle big enough to establish themselves, and there is no bigger miracle than a peace between worlds. So they took the peace. And they sold pieces of it for two hundred years."

Yara remembered the ledger. She could see it with terrible vividness: the great vellum book in the vault, the founding entries in ink gone brown as dried blood. *Item: one covenant, gently used.* She had read those entries a hundred times and never once understood what she was reading. She had catalogued miracles the way a customs officer catalogues cloth — by weight, by origin, by price — never asking what it cost the thing itself to be a thing.

"I'm sorry," she said, and heard how small the words were, how insufficient, how much like a merchant's apology for a delayed shipment. "I've been keeping their books. I've been writing down what they stole, neatly, in columns—"

"Yes," Vess said. "But you also hid me in the vault. You broke your law before you knew why it mattered." The child tilted her head, studying Yara with those moon-holding eyes. "That is why the doors opened for you. Oaths recognize debt. You owe the covenant something, and you began paying before anyone told you the amount."

* * *

They did not move on that night. The moon was too full and the road too dark, and besides — Yara told herself — the child deserved one night of talking, after nine days of silence. But the truth, which Yara permitted herself only in the privacy of her own skull, was simpler: she could not stop listening.

They sat by a small fire in the mill's shell while the wind worried the broken roof tiles, and Vess talked the way a dammed river talks once the stone cracks — haltingly at first, then all at once.

She remembered being Maren best. Maren had been a fisherwoman's daughter from the Ninth Reach who had taken up the sword at fourteen to keep raiders off her village, and who had sworn her oath standing knee-deep in the tide. She remembered Odile, who laughed too loudly and cheated at dice. She remembered Brannoc, who was afraid of horses his entire life and rode them anyway, because the covenant required travel and courage, he said, was just fear that had learned arithmetic. She remembered a woman whose name came to Vess only as *the one who kept bees*, and whose oath had been about sweetness — about the promise that the two realms would trade not only safety but beauty, songs across the border, honey-colored light.

"And me?" Yara asked quietly, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the child's voice had grown hoarse. "Do you remember me?"

Vess went very still. It was the stillness Yara had learned to dread — the stillness of someone deciding how much truth a moment can hold.

"I remember your kind," she said at last. "Swordspeakers after the breaking. The ones who inherited the blades without inheriting the meaning. You were given a humming sword and a guild rulebook and told the humming was tradition." She reached out and laid her palm flat on the oilcloth bundle that held the salt-oath. Under her touch, Yara felt the blade shiver like a living thing remembering it had once been free. "Ilset walked into the sea because she could feel us, Yara. Faintly, all her life, the way you feel weather coming. She felt twelve people screaming inside a locked box, and no one would tell her what the box was, and her oath had no door left to walk through. So she chose the sea. The sea keeps promises. It's the only thing that does, reliably."

Yara looked at her hands. They were competent hands, ledger-keeper's hands, swordspeaker's hands, and she thought of every entry she had ever written — the widow buying back her husband's voice, the thief selling his name, the thousand small trades recorded in fair and careful script — and she wondered how many of those items had been pieces of Vess. How many times had she weighed and priced and shelved a fragment of a person who was currently sitting beside her fire, warming her feet, learning to use the word *I*?

"Widow Ashen," Yara said abruptly. "Three years ago. She bought back one hour of her dead husband's voice. I logged it myself. Was that—"

"That was Brannoc's laughter," Vess said. "One hour of it. It was the kindest thing anyone ever bought from the guild, and it was still theft."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called its wooden call, twice, and fell silent. Yara found that her eyes were wet and did not entirely care.

"They treated you as freight," she said. "Cargo. An item in inventory. And I—" Her throat closed. She tried again. "I want you to know that I see you. Not the vessel. Not the thirteenth fragment of the covenant. *You*. Whatever you are apart from all that — the girl who likes honey, who hums when she thinks no one's listening, who steals bread crusts off my pack when she thinks I'm asleep—"

"I don't steal them," Vess said with immense dignity. "You leave them."

"—that person deserves a name that belongs to her. If you want one. If Vess isn't—"

"Vess is mine." The child said it fiercely, suddenly, with such force that both swords hummed in answer. Then, softer, wondering: "You gave it to me. Given names are strange. They're the only kind of name that starts as love instead of duty." She looked into the fire. "Keep calling me Vess. Let the other eleven sleep until we can wake them properly."

*The other eleven.* The words turned over in Yara's mind and showed her their underside: *split herself back into twelve souls*, if the Hollow Court demanded it, if the reforging required it. A singular self, given up like a coat in winter. She pushed the thought away. There would be time for costs later. There was always time for costs later, and never enough time for anything else.

* * *

Near dawn, when the fire was ash and the moon had gone west to attend to other business, Vess woke screaming.

Yara was up with a blade in her hand before her mind finished arriving — old training, good instincts — scanning the ruin for inquisitors, for wolves, for the thorned shadows that had begun haunting the edges of her sleep. Nothing. Only the grey light, the ruined roofline, the child sitting bolt upright in her blanket with her fists pressed to her mouth.

"Vess." Yara knelt, slow, hands open. "It's me. You're safe. What did you see?"

The child lowered her fists. Her eyes were wide and wet and ancient, and when she spoke, the twelvefold echo was back — but fractured now, the voices disagreeing, some of them frightened.

"A dream. Or a memory. Or a message." She swallowed. "The Hollow is preparing for us. The court has heard the oaths waking — Ilset's salt-song travels under the ground, they can taste it. They are gathering to judge whoever comes." She grabbed Yara's wrist, hard, harder than a child should grip. "Yara. Listen. One of the judges will be wearing my face. The Hollow kept impressions of all twelve of us, the way wax keeps a seal. And there is worse." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "There is a man in iron who serves the Crown, who carries our twelfth oath inside him without knowing it. He is coming for us, and he is closer than you think, because he does not need to search. He only needs to follow the singing."

Yara's blood went cold and then colder. She thought of Marrec — the courteous voice, the patient eyes, the wax seal pressed onto the memory-dealer's ashes. *A vessel of the old tongue,* he had asked for. She had thought herself hunted. She had not understood she was a lantern running through dry grass.

"How close?" she managed.

Vess tilted her head, listening to something below hearing — the hum of three oaths, perhaps, the pulse of a covenant waking bone by bone.

"He slept last night in Greywater town," she said. "He rides at first light."

Above the eastern hills, as if summoned by the sentence, the sky had begun, traitorously, to brighten.
