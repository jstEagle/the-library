## 12. Flight Through the Thornwood

The vault was still burning behind them when Yara took Vess's hand and did not let go.

They ran through the guild's undercrofts, past shelves of bottled names that had begun to burst in the heat — each one popping like a struck bell, releasing little ghosts of other people's lives into the smoke. A woman's wedding day. A fisherman's last morning. Somewhere above them Sef's voice was shouting something to someone, and Yara did not stop to learn what. She had seen his face in the firelight through the grate: not triumphant, not even afraid. Just tired. The tiredness of a man who had decided the world was a ledger too, and that some entries had to be crossed out for the rest to balance.

She hated him for it. She would hate him later, when there was time. For now there was only Vess's small cold fingers in hers and the sound, far behind, of inquisitor boots finding the guild's back stairs.

"The thornwood," she breathed, more prayer than plan.

There was one door out of the undercroft that no ledger recorded. Not because it was secret — because writing down what lay beyond it would have been an entry the Guild of Impossible Things could never honor, and so the founders had left it blank on purpose. Yara had read about it in the forbidden ledgers, the ones now curling into ash: *the unrecorded door, which opens onto the border between what is kept and what was given away.*

It opened onto the Hollow Thorn.

* * *

The door was iron, older than the guildhouse around it, and it opened at Vess's touch without a sound.

Beyond it there was no night and no day, only a grey-gold light like the hour before dawn stretched forever. Thorn trees stood in ranks as far as the eye could follow — black-limbed, silver-barked where the light caught them, their branches braided together overhead into a ceiling that might have been sky or might have been roof. The air smelled of rain on old stone and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun.

Vess stepped through first. Barefoot still; the child had lost her shoes somewhere in the vault fire and had not asked for new ones. Her feet found the thornwood floor — soft loam, scattered with pale petals that were either flowers or fallen stars — and the whole forest seemed to lean toward her, every branch bending a degree, the way wheat bends after wind.

"They know you," Yara whispered.

Vess looked up at her. In this light the child's eyes were not grey but green, deep green, the green of things growing in places no sun reaches. She did not answer. She never answered. But her hand tightened around Yara's, and Yara understood that too as speech.

Behind them, the iron door hung open on the guildhouse cellar — and through it came torchlight, and voices, and the particular unhurried tread of men who believed the world belonged to them.

"Down," Yara said, though there was no down here. "Away."

They went.

* * *

The thorns tested them before they had gone a hundred paces.

Yara heard her mother's voice first.

"Petra—" No. *Yara.* "Yara. Come inside, love, the stew's going cold." It came from off among the trees, warm and ordinary and impossible, because her mother had been dead eleven years and her mother's kitchen had been three kingdoms from any place like this. Yara stopped walking. Her body stopped as if her bones had been hooked.

"Don't," she said aloud, to the wood, to herself. "I know what you are."

But the voice did what voices do. It grew closer. And then there was a shape moving between the trunks — a woman in a brown apron, flour to the wrists, smiling with that half-smile that always meant *I knew you'd come back eventually.*

"You're so thin," her mother said. "Look at you. Running messages for thieves and calling it a life. Come home, Yara. I made enough for two."

"It isn't real." Yara's throat was tight. "You died in the winter. I sold my grief to the guild myself, third shelf, blue jar, because I couldn't carry it anymore. That's how this works, isn't it? You take what people give up and you wear it."

Her mother's smile flickered — and beneath it, for just a moment, Yara saw the thornwood looking back at her through her mother's face. Patient. Curious. Almost gentle.

"I'm sorry," the shape said, in her mother's voice, with something that sounded horribly like sincerity. "We only wanted to see what you loved. It helps us know where to put the path."

Then the apron unraveled into briars and the woman was gone, and where she had stood the brambles parted, opening a corridor of clear ground winding deeper into the grey-gold light.

*A path,* Yara thought. *It made me a path out of my own grief.* She wiped her face with the back of her hand, angry at the wetness there, and realized Vess was watching her with an expression no seven-year-old should have been able to make: solemn, and knowing, and unbearably kind.

"I'm all right," Yara lied, and took her hand again.

* * *

The second test was worse, because it was not aimed at Yara at all.

They had walked — hours? minutes? the light never changed — when Vess stopped so suddenly Yara nearly pulled her over. Ahead, in a clearing ringed by white-thorn, stood twelve figures.

They were swordspeakers. Yara knew it the way she knew her own blade's hum: by the way they held themselves, weight forward, hands loose and ready, oath-sworn and oath-shaped. Twelve of them, arranged in a broken circle with one gap. And they were singing — a low wordless chord in the old tongue, the language that lived in Vess's mouth and no one else's.

Vess made a sound Yara had never heard from her. Small. Wounded. She stepped forward, and the figures turned, and every one of them had the child's face. Twelve children, all Vess, all looking at her with the same terrible tenderness.

"We remember being whole," they said, in twelve voices that were one voice. "Do you?"

And Yara saw — she could not help seeing — what the child had been before the guild's founders had done their work. Not a girl. A covenant. Twelve souls braided into one living knot, the thirteenth turn of the pact made flesh, and then cut apart, and folded, and folded again, until what remained was small enough to smuggle and mute enough not to testify.

"No," Yara said, kneeling, taking the real Vess — the one whose hand she held, the one who trembled — by both shoulders. "Listen to me. Whatever it shows you, whoever it shows you — I am here. Do you understand? Here." She pressed the child's palm flat against her own sternum, over her heart. "This is real. This is yours. Nothing in this wood can take it."

The twelve shapes sang on. Vess closed her eyes. And slowly, one by one, they dimmed — not banished, Yara noticed, but *soothed*, like dogs called off — until only the clearing remained, empty and white-thorned and quiet.

When Vess opened her eyes, the green in them had gone softer. She leaned into Yara, forehead against her collarbone, and stayed there a long time.

That was when Yara understood that whatever she had been building between them since the vault — the hiding, the running, the fierce unnamed thing in her chest — it had a name now, whether she said it aloud or not.

Not ward. Not charge. Not cargo, not vessel, not asset of the guild.

Family. Chosen, in fire, with no paperwork and no witnesses. The one kind the ledgers had no column for.

* * *

They slept in the roots of a tree the size of a cathedral, curled in a hollow that the thornwood seemed to have grown for exactly that purpose. Yara woke once in the timeless light to find Vess awake, watching her.

"What is it?" Yara murmured.

Vess reached out and touched Yara's temple, very lightly, where the first memory-payment had left its hollow feeling — the missing years, the mother's face gone smooth as an unwritten page. Then the child drew a shape in the air between them: a circle, broken, with one piece set slightly apart.

"Yes," Yara said quietly. "That's me. Missing pieces."

Vess shook her head. Drew it again — but this time she moved the loose piece inward, fitting it against the others, and pointed at herself, and then at Yara, and then made a bridge of her two small hands.

Yara laughed, wet-eyed and surprised. "You think you can fill in what I've lost?"

Vess shrugged, in the manner of children who have just explained something obvious.

"You strange, impossible creature," Yara said, and pulled her close, and for a while the grey-gold light was almost kind.

* * *

The inquisitors found the border two days later. Or perhaps two hours. Time in the thornwood was a rumor.

Yara heard them before she saw them: the wrongness of bootfalls on loam that should have swallowed sound, and then Marrec's voice, carrying through the trees with terrible mildness.

"Mistress Thornbury. We know you are tired. We know the wood has been showing you things. None of that needs to matter. Walk out with the child and this ends cleanly."

She had Vess up on her back, arms locked under the girl's knees, the oath-blade drawn and humming against her hip. The hum was louder here than it had ever been in the human world — a low sustained note, like a finger on a wineglass, and the nearer the pursuit came, the higher it climbed.

"There are six of us," Marrec called. "You have a child on your back and a sword that sings. Be reasonable."

"Reasonable," Yara said to Vess, softly, "is what he calls cruelty that has learned good manners." Then, louder: "Inquisitor! Ask your masters what happened to the twelfth sword!"

Silence. Then, carefully: "I don't know what you mean."

"I know you don't!" She was laughing, breathless, half-mad with exhaustion, backing deeper into the briars as the blade's note climbed and climbed. "That's the tragedy of you, Marrec — you guard a door you can't remember exists! But your law remembers. Your wax remembers. Everything the Crown built itself on top of remembers, and it is *tired*, and so am I, and we are leaving."

The thornwood chose that moment to agree with her.

The briars ahead of them — impassable a heartbeat before — folded open like hands. Behind, they knitted shut with a sound like portcullises dropping, and Yara heard one of the six inquisitors cry out as a branch took his torch, and another swear as the path simply ceased beneath his boots. Marrec's voice came one last time through the closing green, and it was not angry. That was the worst of it. It was patient, and it was certain, and it said:

"The moon turns regardless, Mistress Thornbury. Thirteenth turn comes to everything."

Then the wood closed over them like water, and the pursuit was gone.

* * *

Yara ran anyway, because terror does not believe in doors that close behind you. She ran until Vess's grip on her shoulders tightened — a signal, deliberate, practiced — and then she stopped, chest heaving, in another of those endless gold-grey glades.

The blade's note settled. Lowered. Became, unmistakably, a purr.

"Well," Yara managed. "You're enjoying yourself, at least."

She slid Vess down and knelt to look at her — really look. Smudged. Barefoot. Alive. And when the child met her eyes, Yara saw that the fear in them had changed. It had not lessened. It had *converted*, the way ore converts in fire, into something harder and brighter.

Resolve. The child had resolve now. Yara recognized it because she felt it kindling in her own chest, matching it ember for ember.

"All right," Yara said. She sheathed the humming blade and took Vess's hands. "Here's what I know. Eleven oaths are scattered somewhere in this world or under it. The moon is turning whether we sleep or not. The Crown wants you in a wax-sealed box, and my guild — my *guild*, Vess, twenty years of my life — sold you to them for the price of surviving another season." Her voice cracked and she let it. "So we are past asking whether this is fair. We are past asking whether I am strong enough. There is only forward, and the next oath, and the next after that, until the circle closes or I drop."

Vess squeezed her hands. Once. Twice. Deliberate. Counting.

"Eleven," Yara agreed. "Eleven oaths. And then the twelfth, and then—" She stopped, because she remembered what the forbidden ledgers had said the final payment must be, and she was not yet ready to say that part aloud to anyone, least of all the small person holding onto her as if she were the last solid thing in two worlds.

Instead she lifted the child onto her hip — she was heavier than she'd been at the guild door, Yara noticed; fear burned calories even in miracles — and squared herself toward the deepening wood.

Somewhere ahead, faint as a bell underwater, the oath-blade began to hum a new phrase. Not *thirteenth turn* this time. Something older. A direction. A name.

Yara listened, and smiled, and it was not entirely a frightened smile.

"That way, then," she said. "Lead on."

And the thornwood, which tests all comers and keeps few, opened before them like a book deciding, at last, to be read.
