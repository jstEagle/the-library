## 13. The Hollow Court

The descent did not feel like falling.

It felt like being *remembered* — pulled upward through layers of soil and root and older, stranger strata, the way a word rises to the tongue after years of silence. Vess felt each layer as it passed: loam that tasted of rain, clay that tasted of grief, stone that tasted of names. Yara's hand never left hers. Through the thorns' closing grip, through the tearing light above, through everything, that callused palm held on, and Vess understood — in the wordless way she understood the old language humming in her own blood — that this was the one thing in either world she could not lose.

Then roots parted like curtains, and they stood in the Hollow.

Vess had expected darkness. What she found was dusk — an eternal, honeyed gloaming beneath a sky of woven branches, where no sun had ever risen and none was needed. Light came from the thorns themselves, from pale blossoms that opened along every black stem like eyes remembering how to see. The air smelled of wet iron and flowering rot. Rivers ran here without water, channels of silver mist winding between trees whose trunks were carved — no, *grown* — with faces.

She knew those faces.

*Twelve,* something in her whispered. *We are twelve. We were twelve.*

And yet when Vess counted them — the sleeping visages pressed into the bark, mouths half-open around words they'd sworn centuries ago — she counted only eleven. The twelfth face was smooth wood, unfinished, waiting. She reached toward it without meaning to, and Yara's grip tightened.

"Don't," Yara breathed. "Not yet. Not until we know the rules."

There were no rules. There was only the court.

It assembled itself out of the gloaming the way frost assembles on glass — gradually, then all at once. Figures stepped from the thornwood, or perhaps the thornwood stepped out of them; it was impossible to say where the exiled oaths ended and the forest began. They wore shapes that had almost been human once, and their almost-human-ness was the most terrible thing about them. A woman whose body was a lattice of brambles filled with singing. An old man grown into his own walking staff, moss silvering his shoulders. Twins joined at the shadow, sharing one heartbeat between them. Twelve figures — no, Vess counted again, her heart stuttering — *twelve*, though the tree-faces had shown her eleven. One of them wore its shape loosely, like a coat borrowed for the occasion, and its outline kept trying to return to something else.

At their center, on a throne that was less furniture than wound — a great knot of thornwood grown around an absence — sat the eldest of them. Its face had been beautiful once. Now beauty and ruin shared it equally, like two sisters refusing to divide an inheritance.

"You come bearing our stolen sister," it said. Its voice was the sound a promise makes when it breaks. "Or you come wearing her."

Vess felt Yara step forward — felt the swordspeaker's body angle itself, instinctively, between Vess and the throne, the way she'd angled it between Vess and Marrec's crossbows, between Vess and the burning vault, between Vess and every sharp thing the upper world had thrown at them. Eleven oaths hung at Yara's back in a satchel of grey leather, and even wrapped and warded, they sang. The Hollow heard them. Every blossom along every thorn turned toward the sound like flowers finding a sun.

"Her name is Vess," Yara said. Her voice carried in the vast gloaming, steady in the way that cost her something; Vess had learned to hear the price in it. "I am Yara Thornbury, last oath-bound of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I have brought her home."

"*Home.*" The word moved through the court like a stone dropped into still water. The bramble-woman laughed, and her laughter was birdsong played backward. "The thieves speak of home now. The guild that cut her from us speaks of *home*."

"I didn't—"

"No." The eldest rose from its throne of absence, and the whole forest leaned with it. "You did not. Your hands are clean. Your ledger is clean. You were not yet born when your founders came beneath the moon's grace and took what was freely given and made it *cargo*. But inheritance is not only coin and name, little speaker. It is debt. And you carry their debt in your blood the way she carries our oaths in hers."

The court closed around them by inches. Vess watched the thorns lean in, patient as tide. Beside her, Yara's hand found her oath-blade's hilt — not drawing it, just touching it, the way a person touches a doorframe in a shaking house.

"She isn't cargo," Yara said. "She's a child. Whatever the founders did, whatever sin I'm heir to — she is seven years old and her name is Vess, and I will not stand here while you weigh her like grain."

Something flickered across the eldest's ruined-beautiful face. Not softness. Recognition, maybe — the look of one who remembered, distantly, what it was to defend something small.

"The child is not on trial," it said. "You are."

* * *

They judged her in the old way, which was to say they did not ask questions at all.

Instead they *read* her. The court came close, one by one, and laid their hands — or the things they used instead of hands — upon Yara's head, her shoulders, the blade at her hip, and wherever they touched, Vess saw Yara's life spill out into the gloaming like ink into water. It was not gentle. It was not cruel. It was simply thorough, the way winter is thorough.

Vess saw what the court saw. She could not help it; the visions bloomed in the shared air between them, silver and merciless:

A girl of nine, standing before the great ledger for the first time, reciting the guild's three laws with her mother's ring already too big for her finger. *Record honestly. Trade fairly. Keep nothing that was given in trust.* She believed them the way children believe walls hold up houses.

A young woman counting out three years of a widow's grief in fair measure, and feeling, for one hot moment, pride — and beneath the pride, so faint she'd never named it, loneliness sitting in her chest like a stone she'd mistaken for her own heart.

Corvane's hand on her shoulder in the vault firelight. *"You're the only honest one left, Yara. That's why it has to be you."*

The child at the door, barefoot in the storm. The blade humming. The first law breaking in her hands like thin ice — *keep nothing given in trust* — and the strange fierce joy of breaking it.

Three years of her mother's face, gone. Six more memories spent on graves and rivers and a madwoman who wept when Yara gave the seventh back. The growing hollow where a self used to be. *What remains when the self is spent?*

Marrec in the burning shop, courteous as a knife. Corvane on his knees, choosing courage at the end. The vault burning. Sef's face in the smoke. The thornwood closing over them like a green mouth. And always, always, running through all of it like a thread through cloth: the small warm weight of a child's hand in hers.

When it ended, Yara was on her knees in the silver-mist riverbed, breathing hard, stripped in a way no thief or inquisitor had ever managed. Vess dropped beside her and held on, and Yara let herself be held, which the court marked, and weighed, and did not forgive.

"We have seen," said the bramble-woman, and her voice was almost tender now, which was worse. "She keeps faith. She spends herself for our stolen sister. And still — still — she stands heir to the breach. The covenant was broken by hands that taught her hands their trade. The ledger she loves was written in our blood."

"The debt does not care that she is kind," said the twins with one voice.

"The debt only cares that it is *owed*," said the man grown into his staff.

The eldest descended from its throne of absence and stood before Yara, and Vess felt the air go thin and old.

"Yara Thornbury. Last of the oath-bound. Heir of the Guild of Impossible Things." Each title landed like a verdict. "For the breach your founders made, the covenant demands a price beyond your gathering. Eleven oaths you carry — we feel them, we *ache* for them — but eleven cannot knit what thirteen sealed. The twelfth turn approaches, and the twelfth turn requires a speaker willing to give everything she remembers. Everything. Not three years. Not a mother's face. The whole ledger of herself, burned clean, so that a new page may be written."

Yara lifted her head. Vess saw it coming before anyone said it — saw it in the set of Yara's jaw, in the way her hand found Vess's hair, smoothing it back the way she had on the worst night of their flight.

"And if she refuses?" Vess asked.

Every blossom in the Hollow turned toward her. It was the first time she had spoken aloud since the third oath's moonlight, and the old language poured off her tongue like water finding its old bed.

"If she refuses," the eldest said, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow crueler than mercy would have been, "then the moon completes its turn unsealed, and the thornwall falls, and both worlds bleed into each other until neither remains. That is not a threat, child. It is arithmetic."

"But you would rather it didn't come to that," Vess said. "All of you. I can feel it. You've been alone down here for so long, and you don't want the worlds to end — you want to be *remembered* rightly. You want what was stolen to be given back." She stood, small and barefoot among giants, and the eleventh unfinished face in the thornwood seemed to lean closer. "So leave the door open. I can hear it. There's a door in your judgment, and you've left it open on purpose."

Silence, vast and rooted.

The eldest looked at her for a long moment — looked at her the way one looks at a lock that has finally found its key. Then it turned to Yara, still kneeling, still unbowed.

"There is a door," it admitted. "Because she asked for one, and because she is ours, and because even judgment grows tired of judging. Swear the oath, speaker. Bind your life to the reforging. Gather what remains. And when the thirteenth turn comes full — pay what the covenant asks, or fail, and let the blade take its due. Those are the terms. Judgment without mercy, and one door left open, because a child demanded it."

Yara rose. Slowly, joints aching, memory-hollowed, she rose, and drew the oath-blade at last, and its song filled the Hollow until the dead rivers shimmered.

"I swear it," she said.

And Vess, watching her — watching this woman who had already given away half of herself and stood ready to give the rest — felt for the first time the shape of the terrible arithmetic waiting at the top of the world, and began, quietly, desperately, to think of another way to pay.

Above them, far away, the moon began its twelfth turn.
