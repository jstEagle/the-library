## 14. An Oath Is a Debt

The judgment hall of the Hollow Court had no ceiling that Yara could find, only a slow spiraling dark where thorns grew upward instead of down, and somewhere in that dark hung a moon — not her moon, but a sister to it, silvered and scarred, turning with terrible patience toward its thirteenth face.

She had been condemned here. Heir to the thieves, the court had said, its hundred voices speaking through roots and riverwater and the dry rattle of seedpods. And yet a door had been left open, because Vess had stood in front of her with her small bare feet planted on the black soil and refused — *refused*, in the old tongue, in words like knuckles cracking — to let them close it.

Now they sat in an antechamber of living wood, Yara and the child, waiting for whatever came next. The walls breathed. The benches were grown, not built, their grain swirling like fingerprints. Somewhere far off, water moved with the rhythm of a sleeping heart.

Yara laid her sword across her knees.

It had never been so heavy. When she'd drawn it from the drowned chapel's coral altar, carrying the second oath drunk deep into its steel, she had thought she understood what weight meant. Then the third, pried from a riverbed where a swordspeaker had walked into the current still holding his word like a lantern. Then the fourth, fifth, sixth — each one a season of grief compressed into a single night of digging, diving, bargaining. The blade drank every oath and grew heavier, as if promises had mass, as if kept faith were a metal denser than lead.

*Thirteenth turn,* the sword had said, twenty years silent, and now it murmured almost constantly, a low vibration she felt in her wrist bones more than heard.

Vess was watching her. The child did that — watched without blinking, without the fidgets children were supposed to have, as though she had spent too long being cargo to remember how to be restless. But lately there were new things in her. A way of tilting her head when curious. A habit of counting things under her breath in the old tongue, doorways and stars and the buttons on Yara's coat. Small humanisms growing back like grass through cobblestones.

"You're going to do something stupid," Vess said. Her voice was still strange to hear — eleven years old and ancient, both at once.

"I'm going to do something necessary. There's a difference."

"Corvane said that too." Vess's chin lifted. "Before he gave himself to Marrec's chains."

Yara flinched. She hadn't told the child how Corvane died; she hadn't had to. Vess remembered being twelve people once, and one of those twelve had apparently known how news traveled in the dark places of the world. Grief for Corvane sat in Yara's chest like a swallowed stone — the guildmaster who had hidden the child behind his own cowardice, then died behind his own courage, decades too late and not a moment too soon. She hadn't finished mourning him. She suspected she wouldn't get the chance.

"The court will give us until the next turn of their moon," Yara said. "No more. After that, the covenant breaks past mending, and everything above — every name, every memory, every small miracle the guild ever traded — unravels into the Hollow. I need time. Time is the one thing I can't buy anymore." She turned her hand over, palm up, showing the faded scars of old trades: three years gone to the Market of Borrowed Years, her mother's face with them. "I've spent memory. I've spent years. There's only one coin left that the old law respects."

Vess went very still. Even for her, it was a remarkable stillness — the stillness of someone who understood exactly what was about to happen and could not decide whether to allow it.

"No," the child said.

"It's my choice."

"It's a *stupid* choice."

"It's the only one left." Yara stood, and the bench-wood creaked beneath the absence of her. She lifted the sword, and its hum deepened, recognizing the shape of her intention before she'd spoken it aloud. Of course it did. It had been listening to her heartbeat for twenty years. "A swordspeaker's oath isn't a promise, Vess. Promises are made of intention, and intentions bend. An oath is a debt written into the body. If I swear myself to the covenant's restoration — all twelve oaths, reforged, the circle closed before the thirteenth turn — then the blade holds me to it. Every hour I waste, it takes from me. Every failure—"

"—it takes *you*," Vess finished. "I know what oaths are. I *was* one."

The words landed harder than any blow the court had struck. Yara knelt so they were eye to eye. Up close, the child's eyes held colors that didn't belong in a human face — a green shot through with root-brown, like light through leaves — but they also held tears, and tears were entirely, stubbornly human.

"Then you know," Yara said quietly, "that I'm not choosing death. I'm choosing to be *bound*. To something worth being bound to." She reached out and touched the child's cheek, the way she had that first storm-night at the guild door, when her blade had hummed against her back like a struck bell and she had broken guild law for the first time without understanding why. She understood now. She had been bound to this child since the knock before dawn. Everything since had just been the paperwork.

"The ledger," Vess said suddenly.

"What?"

"When you swear. The guild always writes things down. You told me — nothing exists in trade unless it's recorded." The child's mouth curved, and it was such a fragile, fierce little smile that Yara's chest ached. "So write it. Write me into your book. Write the oath into your book. If you're going to spend yourself, spend yourself *properly*."

Yara laughed — a rough, startled sound that echoed strangely in the breathing walls — and dug the ledger from her pack. Not the great ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things; that had burned in Sef's treachery, along with the vault and twenty years of careful entries. This was a smaller book, bought from a market stall in borrowed years, its pages soft as moth wings. She had been keeping it out of habit. Now she saw it was something else: continuity. Proof that the covenant was not only a thing that had been broken, but a thing that could be *kept*.

She opened to a fresh page. The Hollow's dim light made the ink well glow like a bruise.

*Entry the first,* she wrote, because some rituals deserved to survive even when their institutions burned. *Item: one oath, swordspeaker's, sworn by Yara Thornbury, last of her order. Terms: the restoration of the covenant entire — twelve oaths gathered, twelve speakers honored, the circle closed before the moon completes its thirteenth turn. Collateral: the swearer's life, forfeit upon failure. Witness:* 

She paused, pen hovering.

"Witness," she said aloud, "is the party to whom the debt is owed. That's the covenant itself. But—" she looked up at Vess, "—a good ledger names everyone with a stake."

Vess crawled onto the bench beside her, solemn as a magistrate, and pressed one small finger below the ink. Where she touched, the page darkened, and a mark bloomed there — not a fingerprint but a sigil, thorned and circular, the seal of the old tongue rendered in Yara's own book.

"There," the child said. "Witnessed. Now you can't weasel out."

"I have never weaseled out of anything in my life."

"You forgot your mother's face. That's weaseling."

The barb went in clean, the way only truths spoken by children can. Yara set down the pen. Then she rose, carried the sword to the center of the antechamber where the black soil showed between the root-boards, and knelt.

The blade knew. Its hum climbed through registers she had no names for, and the steel began to warm in her grip, and along its fuller the old script surfaced like fish rising to feed — the same letters Vess spoke, the language older than kingdoms, older perhaps than the idea of kingdoms.

Yara bowed her head over the flat of the blade.

"Hollow beneath and crown above," she said, and her voice sounded different down here, layered, as though the court's hundred voices had come to listen through her throat. "I am Yara Thornbury, daughter of no house that matters, keeper of ledgers, last speaker of the swords. I take up the debt my founders scattered. Twelve oaths I will gather. Twelve keepers I will honor, whether they slept in salt or stone or madness. The circle I will close before the thirteenth turn, or let the blade collect what I owe."

The warmth in the steel became heat. Not pain — not yet — but a pressure, like a hand closing around her soul to test its weight.

"And if I falter," she continued, steadier now, because the words were doing something to her, straightening her spine from the inside, "if I bargain, or break, or turn aside — then take me. Not quickly. Take me the way debts are taken: honestly, completely, with interest. Let my name be the last entry in my own book."

The blade *answered*.

Light ran up it like dawn running up a mountain range, and the script blazed, and Yara felt the oath settle into her — not into her mind, where memories lived and could be traded away, but deeper, into the marrow, into the architecture of her. It felt like a bridge being built across a gorge inside her chest. It felt like a chain and a handrail, indistinguishable. It felt, absurdly, like the great ledger's leather cover under her fingers on ordinary nights, the smell of ink and tallow, the quiet competence of writing down what was true.

Something in her sang. Something else in her counted the cost and found the arithmetic brutal and correct.

When she could see again — she hadn't noticed the light taking her sight — Vess was standing in front of her, and the child was crying, silently, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand as though tears were an indignity she refused to permit.

"You absolute idiot," Vess whispered.

"Yes," Yara agreed. She sheathed the sword. It settled against her hip heavier than ever, and yet somehow easier to carry, the way a burden shared with your own future self becomes lighter. "But I'm an idiot with a deadline now. That improves matters considerably."

"How long?"

Yara did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. The Hollow's moon turned slow; the world's moon turned slower still, dragging its thirteenth face toward fullness like a wound closing in reverse. Eleven oaths remained unfound — no, ten, she corrected herself, the blade already held three and the fourth waited in a grave outside the fen-town of Mireholt, if the madwoman's riddles could be believed. Ten oaths. One season. A lifetime's work, budgeted into weeks.

"Long enough," she said, which was either faith or a lie, and she chose not to examine which.

They left the antechamber together. The court's door — the one left open — had changed while they waited. It was no longer a gap in the thorns but a proper doorway now, arched in pale wood, and beyond it Yara could see not the Hollow's dark but a gray northern sky, rain coming down in threads, the smell of peat and cold iron. The borderland. The thornwood. The way home, or at least the way *toward*.

As she stepped through, the blade spoke against her hip — not its old two-word murmur but a full sentence, clear as a bell over water:

*Four sleeps north, the keeper who would not forget.*

Yara stopped. Rain touched her face, real rain, blessedly ordinary rain.

"The madwoman," she said softly. "The one who kept her oath too well."

The blade hummed assent.

Behind her, Vess took her hand — small, warm, gripping hard enough to hurt, gripping like someone who had learned that hands could carry you away from everything — and together they walked out of the Hollow Court into the thorned borderland, debtor and witness, bound and binding, while overhead the true moon wheeled its patient, merciless arc toward the thirteenth night.

Ten oaths. One season. One life, posted as collateral.

Yara Thornbury had made worse trades. She just couldn't, at the moment, remember any of them.

* * *

They walked north through the thornwood for a day and a night, and the thorns let them pass — mostly. The illusions came anyway, as the plan promised they would, drawn up out of memory like water from a well.

Yara's came at dusk: her mother, standing in a kitchen Yara could no longer picture, saying Yara's name in a voice she could no longer hear. She stood in the rain and let the ghost speak to her, and grieved without knowing why, and when the thorn-shape finally dissolved she found Vess holding her hand tighter, having watched the whole thing without a word.

"What did you see?" Yara asked.

"Twelve people," Vess said. "All of them arguing about whether to save the world." A pause. "They decided yes. Eventually."

Ahead, through the thinning trees, a light burned in a fen-side cottage — one window, lamplit, steady as a held breath. Four sleeps north, the blade had said, and yet here it was after one. Either the Hollow's distances bent for the sworn, or the keeper who would not forget had seen them coming.

From inside the cottage, faint but unmistakable, a woman's voice was reciting — endlessly, precisely, in the old tongue — the terms of an oath.

And she was reciting Yara's name.
