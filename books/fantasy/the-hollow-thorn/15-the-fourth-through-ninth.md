## 15. The Fourth Through Ninth

The fourth oath lay beneath a hill that had no name on any map, because the woman who kept it had asked the cartographers to forget.

Yara climbed through the autumn fog with Vess on her back, the child's arms looped around her throat like a scarf, her breath warm against Yara's ear. The blade — heavier now, salt-tempered and strange — rode at her hip and pulled downward as though it knew where it was going. It had been pulling for three days. Swordspeakers' blades did not point. They *yearned*.

The grave was not marked by stone but by absence: a circle in the hillside where the heather refused to grow, a ring of bare earth exactly wide enough for a body laid standing up. The swordspeaker buried there — Yara had found her name scratched into the margin of a forbidden ledger, *Maren Ostwick, who would not kneel* — had asked to be interred upright so she could keep facing the direction of her promise even after death.

Yara knelt at the edge of the bare circle and did not dig. She had learned that much from the drowned chapel. Oaths were not taken from the dead. They were *received*, if the dead consented, and consent had to be asked for properly, in the old forms, with something offered in exchange.

She drew the blade and laid it flat across her palms.

"Maren Ostwick," she said to the empty earth. "I am Yara Thornbury, last of the bound. I come to carry what you carried. I have nothing to give you but this."

She opened her memory the way the chapel's coral woman had taught her — not with words, but with willingness. She reached for a memory and held it out like a coin on an open palm.

She chose her father's hands. Broad, cracked at the knuckles, teaching her to whittle a boat from scrap pine when she was seven. The smell of wood shavings. The way he'd said *steady now* when the knife slipped and she'd bled and cried and he'd laughed and wrapped her finger in his own handkerchief.

The earth drank it.

She felt it go — a smooth, painless extraction, like a tooth pulled under good anesthesia, and then the strange hollow afterward where the tooth had been. She knew she had owned a father once. She could see the shape of the fact, the way you can see the dent left in grass after something has lain there. But the hands were gone. The smell of shavings was gone. *Steady now* was gone.

In exchange, warmth rose through the soil, and the blade grew heavier by one more weight, and somewhere beneath the heather a voice that had been holding its breath for sixty years finally exhaled.

Vess slid off her back and stood looking down at the bare circle. Then the child did something Yara had never seen her do before: she curtsied. A small, grave, formal dip, the kind of gesture that belonged to courts older than any court Yara knew.

"She says thank you," Vess signed, and then, hesitantly, spoke aloud in her rusted bell of a voice: "Thank... you." Two words. Her vocabulary grew with every oath they recovered, as though the fragments of herself scattered through the world were also fragments of her speech.

Yara sheathed the sword and found she could not remember why her eyes were wet.

* * *

The fifth oath lived in a river.

They came to it in the first hard week of winter, following the blade's yearning north through country where the roads had been allowed to grow over. The river was called the Sennet, and it ran cold and green through a valley of slate, and halfway along its length there was a pool where the water moved in a slow, deliberate spiral, turning and turning without ever going anywhere.

The ledger fragment had been explicit: *Tomas Rell gave his oath to the water rather than let the Crown take it. The water has been keeping it ever since. Do not fish for it. Ask.*

Yara stripped to her shift on a bank rimmed with ice and waded in, and the cold was a living thing, a fist closing around her chest. Vess waited ashore, wrapped in both their cloaks, watching with those old, old eyes.

At the center of the spiral, Yara stopped and offered her payment.

This time she chose carefully. She was learning the arithmetic of herself — which memories were load-bearing and which were ornament. She had already lost her father's hands, her mother's face, the name of the village where she'd been born, the taste of the bread the guild cook made on festival mornings. She was becoming a map with districts burned away, still navigable, less and less herself.

She offered the memory of her first kill.

It seemed right. It seemed almost merciful. That night in the rain outside Corvane's predecessor's door, the man with the crossbow, the way the bolt had gone through his throat and the sound he had made — she had carried it twenty years like a stone in her boot. Let the river have it. Let the river grind it smooth.

The water took it, and the relief was immediate and total, and then it was horrifying, because she remembered *that* she had killed — the guild ledgers recorded it, her rank required it — but the killing itself had become a sentence in someone else's report. *Subject dispatched one assailant.* No rain. No sound. No blood on her hands that she could feel anymore.

And the river gave up Tomas Rell's oath, rising through the spiral in a column of green light that bent around her body without wetting her, and poured itself into the blade, and the blade sang — actually sang, one low note that iced over the surface of the pool in a widening ring.

When she waded out, shaking, blue-lipped, Vess met her at the bank and pressed their foreheads together, the child's version of an embrace, learned from watching Yara do it to her.

"Who taught you to swim?" Vess asked suddenly. Aloud. Four words strung together, a miracle.

Yara opened her mouth to answer and found nothing there. Not the memory gone — she had never offered that one — but nothing *to* remember. She searched for the pond, the summer, whoever had pushed her off the dock laughing, and found only smooth blank water.

"I don't know," she said slowly. "I don't think anyone did. Or I've forgotten them. Or—" She stopped. Counted. Tried to count what was left, and the counting frightened her, because the ledger of herself was getting thin, entries struck through on every page, and she could no longer be sure which blanks were losses and which had always been empty.

"Does it hurt?" Vess asked.

"No," Yara said honestly. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

The sixth oath belonged to a madwoman who was not mad.

Her name was Ilse Vane, and she lived in a tower of her own construction on the moors east of the Sennet — a tower built of stacked stones and ship timber and the ribs of wrecked boats, lashed together with rope and wire and, here and there, with what looked disturbingly like braided hair. She had been building it for forty years. She would tell anyone who asked, and many who didn't, that the tower was a promise, and promises must be built or they rot.

She was seventy years old and had been a swordspeaker at nineteen, sworn to guard a bridge that no longer existed over a river that had changed course, and when the bridge fell she had refused to release her oath, because releasing it felt like lying, and lying felt like dying. So she had held it. Alone. For fifty years. And the holding had worn grooves in her mind the way water wears grooves in stone, until everyone who saw her called her mad, and perhaps she was, but her madness was the cleanest thing Yara had ever encountered: a single promise, kept past all reason, polished smooth by half a century of keeping.

Ilse met them at the tower door with a crossbow, which she lowered the moment she saw the blade.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you're one of mine. You're one of ours." Her eyes went to Vess and widened, and her hand began to shake. "And you're the thirteenth. They told us about you. Before. They told us you'd come, someday, and we argued about whether we should hope for it or dread it. I hoped. I always hoped. Hoping is easier than dreading; it takes less upper-body strength."

She invited them in. The tower's single room was hung with hundreds of small objects, each labeled in a careful hand: *the day the bridge fell. the color of the water that morning. my sister's laugh. the toll I charged and the people who paid it.* Fifty years of a life, catalogued against loss.

"You're gathering them," Ilse said. It wasn't a question. "The oaths. To reforge it. To finish what we started." She sat down heavily on a stool, among her labelled relics, and looked at her own two hands. "I can't give you mine."

"Then I'll go," Yara said gently. "No one will force—"

"You misunderstand. I *can't*. Not won't. Can't." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, loving and broken. "An oath is a debt, girl. Mine isn't finished. The bridge fell, yes, but the crossing remains — people still need to get from one side of a life to the other, and someone has to stand in the middle and say *this far and no farther*. I swore to be that someone. So I built a bridge out of myself instead. It's not done." She gestured at the tower, the ropes, the hair. "It's never done. When it's done, the oath releases. Until then—"

"Until then it's yours," Yara said.

"Until then it's mine." Ilse looked at Vess for a long moment. "But I'll tell you what I can give. Knowledge. The seventh oath — the one in the crypt at Aldermoor — it's guarded. Not by soldiers. By grief. Old Wenna sealed herself in there with it, alive, and she's still alive, girl, she's been alive for a hundred and thirty years because her oath won't let her die while it's unkept, and she has gone *through* madness and out the other side into something worse, which is clarity. She will test you. She will ask you what you're willing to become. Answer honestly. She can smell a lie through stone."

Yara stayed three days, helping Ilse lash new timbers to the tower, learning the geometry of a promise made physical. On the third morning, Ilse pressed something into her hand: a small labelled object. *the face of the last person who believed me.*

"Give it to Wenna," she said. "She'll know what it means."

Yara tried to refuse. Ilse put the crossbow back in her hands' reach, smiling, and Yara accepted.

She did not realize until they were a mile out onto the moor that accepting had cost her something too — that some transaction had occurred beneath the level of her noticing, that Ilse's gift had been a trade all along, and the price had been drawn silently from Yara's account. She racked her mind trying to find what was missing and couldn't locate the gap. That was the worst part. The losses were becoming invisible. She was spending herself in denominations too small to count.

* * *

Wenna of Aldermoor was exactly as terrible as promised.

The crypt beneath the ruined abbey smelled of candle smoke and centuries, and in its deepest chamber, behind a door carved with twelve interlocking thorns, an ancient woman sat on a stone bench beside a sarcophagus, upright, alert, her eyes clear as a hawk's. She was small and withered and wrapped in robes that might once have been guild colors, and when Yara entered she did not rise.

"So," Wenna said. "The collector comes. Show me your ledger."

"My what?"

"Yourself. Open it. Every page." And Yara found, to her horror, that she could not refuse — the old woman's oath filled the room like pressure at depth, and within it, resistance was impossible. Wenna read her. It took a long time. It felt like being flayed by someone very gentle.

When it was over, Wenna was silent for a while, and then she said: "You've lost your mother's face. Your father's hands. Your birthplace. Your first kill. Whoever taught you to swim. Something else, recently — you don't know what. And you're not finished, are you? You mean to spend all the way down. You've already decided that."

"Yes," Yara said.

"Why?"

And Yara looked at Vess, waiting by the door with her solemn old eyes, and said: "Because she's worth more than I am."

Wenna laughed — a dry, rustling sound, like pages turning. "Wrong answer. Right feeling, wrong answer. If you'd said duty, I'd have turned you out. If you'd said justice, same. But love — love isn't a reason, child, love is a *weight*, and weights are what oaths are made of." She lifted the sarcophagus lid herself, impossibly strong, and inside lay not bones but a folded banner, brown with age, embroidered with the sigil of the twelfth speaker. "Take it. Take her oath. And take my advice, freely given: start writing down what you remember. Everything. Names, faces, smells. Write it all and hide copies. Because the self you're spending is the only currency you have, and a fool spends without keeping books."

So Yara took the sixth-and-seventh together — Wenna's oath passing with the banner, released at last after a century of waiting, the old woman slumping with a sigh of profound relief, aging forty years in forty seconds, dying with her hand in Yara's and a smile on her face, whispering, *"kept, kept, kept"* — and the blade grew heavy enough that Yara could barely lift it, and she understood that the weight was the point. An oath should weigh. That was how you knew it was real.

That night, by firelight, she began the book Wenna had told her to write. *My name is Yara Thornbury. I was born—* and stopped, because she no longer knew where. *I was born,* she wrote firmly, *and that is enough for now.*

* * *

The eighth oath was in a grave after all, a true grave, in a churchyard in the lowlands, and it cost her the memory of Corvane teaching her the guild's rules her first night, his voice patient and dry, and she grieved that one strangely, because whatever else Corvane was — coward, keeper of rotten secrets — he had been the closest thing to family the guild had ever given her, and now even her grievance against him was dissolving, leaving only the fact of him, untextured, like a figure seen through frosted glass.

The ninth was the worst.

It lay at the bottom of a peat bog, and the bog demanded not one memory but a *layer* — a whole season of her life, offered whole. Yara stood at the bog's black edge with Vess clinging to her sleeve and chose the season she could least afford, because the bog was wise enough to reject anything cheap: the spring two years ago, the whole of it, the false spring of ordinary happiness, the guild running smooth, the trades coming easy, evenings in the common room with Sef cheating at cards and Marta singing badly and Corvane pretending not to listen. All of it. The bog took it in one long swallow, and Yara surfaced from the offering gasping, knowing that she had been happy once, in a specific place with specific people, and unable to summon a single detail of it. Happiness without content. A receipt for joy, the purchase forgotten.

Vess held her hand the whole walk back to the road. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing useful to say, and they had both learned the economy of silence.

Nine oaths. Nine weights in the blade, nine holes in Yara Thornbury. She sat by their fire that night and wrote in her hidden book by feel, racing her own erosion: *Sef cheats at cards. Marta sings. Remember that someone sang.* Three oaths remained — the tenth across the sea, the eleventh somewhere in the Crown's keeping, and the twelfth—

The twelfth, the forbidden ledgers agreed, had never been lost at all. It had walked into the service of the Crown generations ago and been absorbed so completely that no record of it survived, not even for itself.

Which meant, Yara wrote slowly, the pen dragging, that the final piece of the covenant was wearing an inquisitor's seal and carrying the Crown's authority, and the only way to reach it was to break into the inquisition itself.

She looked at the entry for a long time. Then she closed the book, and fed the fire, and woke Vess softly, because they had a sea to cross, and the moon would not wait for them to be ready.
