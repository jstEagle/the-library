## 17. The Tenth and Eleventh

The sea took three days to give up its dead.

Yara stood on the cliff above Greyharbor with the wind pulling her coat sideways, watching the tide work at the drowned chapel's foundations far below. The tenth oath had been easy to find and hard to take — a swordspeaker named Ollivan Rask who had walked into the water two hundred years ago rather than let the Crown's men take his blade, and whose promise had soaked into the salt until the whole bay hummed with it when the moon was right. She had waded out at low tide with Vess's small hand gripping hers, and she had spoken the words of release into the green dark, and the sea had given her back a weight of light that sank into her blade like a stone sinking into deep water.

The cost had been her father's voice.

She knew this because she had written it down. That was the discipline now, the ritual that kept her from unraveling entirely: after each recovery she sat with paper and ink and recorded what was gone, in her own hand, before the forgetting finished its slow meal of her. *Paid: my father saying my name. Paid: the sound of rain on the roof of the house I grew up in. Paid: my ninth birthday.* The list ran eleven pages now. She did not reread it. Rereading felt too much like counting coins you'd already spent.

What frightened her was not the holes. It was how comfortable the holes were becoming. A memory gone did not ache; it simply left a smooth place, like river stone, and her mind slid over it without friction. She could no longer remember why she had once been afraid of the dark, only that she had been, and that the fear had been about something specific, something with a face.

"You're doing the staring-at-nothing again," Vess said beside her.

Vess spoke plainly now, in the common tongue, though sometimes at dusk her words came out doubled — one language laid over another like a print on wet glass. She was nine years old and carried twelve souls folded inside her like letters in a drawer, and she had decided, with the terrible pragmatism of children, that worrying was a task for adults and she would not be doing it.

"I'm deciding," Yara said.

"About the last one."

"About the last two."

Vess was quiet. Below them the sea boomed against the chapel stones, patient as an auditor.

The ledger of oaths — Yara's own ledger now, the old guild book abandoned in a burning vault half a kingdom away — said there were thirteen. Twelve swordspeakers had sworn the covenant between the human realms and the Hollow, and a thirteenth had been stolen before it could be sworn, and that thirteenth was the girl standing next to her, chewing a strand of dried seaweed like it was a personal enemy. Eleven oaths now rested in Yara's blade, and the blade had grown heavy past sense. She wore it slung across her back with the strap cutting into her collarbone, and some mornings she woke with her shoulders aching as if she'd spent the night carrying a sleeping child — which, in a way, she had.

Two oaths remained. The eleventh belonged to Serane Vos, who had sailed east across the Sea of Glass thirty years ago carrying her oath into exile, and whose descendants, if any lived, kept no address the Crown's records admitted to. Finding her meant a ship, a season, luck.

The twelfth —

The twelfth was the problem. Because the twelfth was not lost. The twelfth was not scattered across graves and rivers and madwomen. The twelfth oath had been recovered by the inquisition decades ago and filed, catalogued, and locked away like evidence of a crime, which was exactly what it was.

It was in Dain Marrec's vault.

* * *

They took a room above a chandler's shop in Greyharbor, and Yara spread her maps on the floor and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the drowned chapel.

"East is four months by ship, if we find one, if the weather holds, if the Crown doesn't put our descriptions on every pier from here to the Glass Coast." She tapped the map. "And then we come back, and then we do the impossible thing anyway, except by then the moon will have turned."

"The moon turns in nineteen days," Vess said. She said it the way other children reported the weather.

"Nineteen days." Yara sat back against the bed frame. The room smelled of tallow and rope. "So we can't do both. We choose."

"Choose which?"

"That's what I'm lying awake deciding."

But she already knew, and had known since Greyharbor's first bell, and the knowing sat in her chest like swallowed ice. There was no choosing. There was only order of operations. The eleventh oath across the sea might as well be on the moon; the twelfth was in a building she had walked past a hundred times in another life, back when she was the Guild of Impossible Things' most obedient servant and the law was something that happened to other people.

"We go to the capital," she said. "We break into the inquisition."

Vess stopped chewing. For a long moment the child studied her with eyes that were gray the way the sea is gray — one color made of many. Then she nodded, once, as if Yara had announced they'd be having soup for supper.

"You've done this before?" Vess asked.

"No."

"Broken into anything?"

"A wine cellar. I was sixteen. I got stuck."

Something happened to Vess's face that Yara slowly recognized as a smile — rare enough still to feel like a small miracle, one of the few the guild had never thought to trade in. "Good," Vess said. "I was worried you'd be sensible about it."

* * *

Audacity, Yara discovered, was not a quality. It was a condition, like fever, and it arrived precisely when everything else had been stripped away. She had no guild. Her guildmaster was in Marrec's custody or dead — she refused to decide which until she had proof. Her ledgers were ash. Every friend she had ever made through the covenant was either hiding from her or selling information about her, and possibly both. She had a heavy sword, a stolen child, eleven oaths, a list of things she no longer remembered, and nineteen days.

A woman with nothing to lose does not plan carefully. But she plans *freely*, and there is a freedom in it that careful people never taste. Yara sat up three nights drawing the inquisition's headquarters from memory — the Hall of Seals, the record annexes, the evidence vaults beneath, the old smuggler's drainage tunnel that the guild had used twice in living memory and that Marrec, being thorough, had almost certainly found and sealed. Almost certainly. Almost was a word she had learned to live inside.

The plan, such as it was, had three parts.

First: get caught. Not immediately, not stupidly, but inevitably. Marrec's inquisition ran on paperwork the way a mill runs on water, and paperwork required a prisoner. She would walk into the net wearing a face she had bought from a memory-dealer in Greyharbor — a minor functionary's identity, a customs clerk named Aldous Wren who had died owing debts and leaving no mourners. The dealer had sold her Wren's gait, his stooped shoulder, the habit he had of clearing his throat before speaking. Yara had paid for it with the smell of bread baking, which she suspected she would miss more than she could currently understand.

Second: while the clerks processed the unremarkable smuggler Aldous Wren, Vess would go in through the drainage tunnel — the *almost* — because the wards keyed to detect vessels of the old tongue would never think to look for a child coming up through the floor of the records annex, and because Marrec's own obsession was the flaw in his fortress. He collected evidence. He kept everything. And everything kept must be shelved, and shelved things could be reached.

Third: Yara, in custody, would be questioned. By Marrec himself, if his reputation held. And somewhere between his questions and her answers, she would learn whether the twelfth oath was where she believed it was, and how thick the door between it and her was.

"And me?" Vess asked, the fourth night, when the plan lay finished on the floor between them like a dissected animal.

"You stay out of sight until the tunnel. Then you find the vault and wait for me."

"What if you don't come?"

Yara looked at her. The question was not fearful; it was logistical, and somehow that was worse.

"Then you take the blade and you run east, and you find Serane Vos, and you finish it without me." She said it steadily, because Vess deserved steadiness even if Yara had to forge it. "You are the covenant, little one. I'm just the hand that carries it."

Vess considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "If you don't come," she said, "I'm going to be very annoyed with you."

"Noted," Yara said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, and Vess pretended not to hear, which was its own kindness.

* * *

They entered the capital separately, on the eighth day remaining, in a rain that had been falling so long the gutters had become streams and the city smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and fear. The Crown had posted new broadsheets: the thirteenth turn approaching, curfews extended, rewards offered for word of "a tall woman bearing an old sword, traveling with a mute child." The word *mute* pleased Yara grimly. Marrec's intelligence was good but not perfect, and every stale detail was a small gift of time.

The Hall of Seals rose over the Temple Quarter like a black tooth, its windows lit against the rain, its doors flanked by wardens in gray lacquered armor. Yara had passed it a hundred times in her old life with the particular contempt of a criminal for the law — contempt being safer than the fear underneath. Now she stood across the square under an awning, wearing Aldous Wren's stooped shoulder, and looked at it the way a diver looks at deep water.

Her blade was not with her. That was the hardest part. It lay wrapped in sailcloth in the tunnel mouth beneath the city, waiting with Vess, and Yara felt its absence the way an amputee feels weather. Eleven oaths, and she had left them in a hole under the street, because no clerk alive would process a smuggler who carried a swordspeaker's blade, and because — she admitted it only in the dark of her own skull — if things went wrong, the blade and the child needed to survive together, and she did not fully trust herself to protect either.

"Three bells," she murmured to no one. "Then I'm Aldous Wren, and Aldous Wren is tired, and Aldous Wren wants to go home."

At the third bell she crossed the square.

Getting arrested, it turned out, was easier than avoiding it. She let her purchased gait falter near the warden post; she fumbled papers she didn't have; she cleared her throat — Wren's habit, bought and paid for — and answered questions with the flat, resentful competence of a man whose only crime was existing near something interesting. The wardens searched her, found nothing but forged customs seals of exquisite mediocrity, and marched her inside through doors that closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The cells beneath the Hall of Seals were dry, warm, and better lit than any room Yara had ever rented. This offended her obscurely. She sat on the cot and waited, and the waiting had a texture she recognized: the inquisition was deciding what to do with her, and somewhere above, clerks were writing her name — Wren's name — into ledgers very much like the ones she used to keep, recording a small impossible thing in the margin of a large lawful world.

Near midnight, boots in the corridor. Unhurried. She knew the rhythm before the key turned — she had heard it once across a market crowd, courteous and patient as frost forming — and by the time the door opened she had arranged her face into Wren's baffled indignation.

Dain Marrec entered and set a lantern on the floor between them. Up close he was thinner than rumor painted him, gray at the temples, with ink stains on his fingers that no amount of scrubbing seemed to remove. He pulled a stool from the corner, sat, and studied her for a long moment without menace and without warmth, the way a man studies a document for forgery.

"Aldous Wren," he said. "Customs clerk, Saltgate district. Delinquent filings. Two counts of falsified manifests." He unfolded a paper. "Arrested loitering within forty paces of the Hall of Seals during a curfew you, as a resident clerk, would know intimately."

"The rain," Yara said, in Wren's flattened vowels. "I ducked under the wrong awning. You can check with my landlady."

"Oh, I intend to check with everyone." He folded the paper away. "Here is what interests me, Master Wren. In twenty-three years of service, I have learned that coincidence is a currency, and someone always spends it. A minor clerk with mediocre forgeries wanders toward my hall on the exact week my agents report movement in the smugglers' tunnels beneath this quarter." He tilted his head. "Tunnels your guild — pardon, the late Guild of Impossible Things — used twice that we know of."

Yara let Wren's confusion sit on her face and prayed it read as confusion and not as the sudden cold drop of hearing her worst assumption confirmed aloud.

"Tunnels," she repeated. "I file grain tariffs."

"Mm." Marrec rose, retrieved his lantern, and paused at the door. "You'll stay the night, Master Wren. The landlady can be reached in the morning. Sleep well — the beds here are better than you deserve." At the threshold he stopped, not turning. "One more thing. The woman we're hunting — Thornbury. Tall, carries an old sword. Do you know what strikes me about the reports? Everyone describes her differently. Tall, short. Dark hair, fair. As if the description keeps... changing hands." He glanced back, mild as milk. "As if she keeps paying for new faces. Which would be a very guild thing to do, wouldn't it? Spend memories like coin."

He left. The lock turned. Yara sat very still in the warm dry cell and listened to her own heartbeat count down the hours, and understood that she had not fooled him at all — that perhaps she never had — and that he had locked her in anyway, gently, the way you pen a letter you have already decided to answer.

Which meant, she realized as the lamp guttered, that the third part of her plan had inverted itself. She was not going to extract information from Dain Marrec.

He was going to offer it to her. And whatever he wanted in exchange, she had the sudden, sick certainty that she was going to want to give it.

Above her, faint through floors of stone, she heard — or imagined she heard — a sound like a child's footsteps in a tunnel, moving patiently downward, toward the vaults, toward the blade wrapped in sailcloth, toward the last oath of the twelve, filed and shelved and waiting in the dark.

Nineteen days had become eighteen. Somewhere below, the moon's shadow lengthened, and the first lock of the deepest vault, all unknowing, began to dream of opening.
