## 18. What Marrec Keeps

The vault beneath the Inquisition's Hall of Records had no lock.

This was not an oversight. Dain Marrec had designed it that way, forty years ago, when he was young enough to believe that fear was a better guardian than iron. A lock could be picked. A lock could be bribed out of a sleepy warden. But a door with no lock — a door that opened only for the man who carried the Crown's seal pressed into the flesh of his palm, a brand older than his career, older than his name in the ledgers — that door asked a question of everyone who approached it, and the question was simple: *are you certain?*

Most people, confronted by an unlocked door, became uncertain. They hesitated. They looked over their shoulders. And hesitation was all the door needed.

Marrec descended the spiral stair at midnight with a lantern in one hand and a cup of cold chicory in the other, because he had not slept properly in eleven days and had stopped pretending he would again. Above him, the Hall of Records settled into its nighttime sounds — the tick of cooling stone, the sigh of paper, the distant footfalls of the night clerks who copied confessions in a hand so uniform it might have been printed. He knew every sound the way a husband knows a wife's breathing. He had spent more of his life down here than he had spent in any house he'd ever owned.

He had never thought of that as sad before. It occurred to him now, briefly, the way a splinter occurs to a hand long after the wood is gone.

* * *

The trouble had begun, properly, with the girl.

No — Marrec corrected himself, setting the lantern on its iron hook and running his thumb along the edge of the first shelf. The trouble had begun twelve days ago, when a fishmonger in the Saltrow district reported a child who spoke in her sleep in a language that made his dogs howl. That was the report that mattered. Everything since — the burned memory-dealer's shop, the interrogations, the file on the Guild of Impossible Things spread open like a dissected bird — everything since had been consequence.

He pulled the file now, though he did not need to. He knew it by heart. He had written half of it himself across three decades, in his own careful clerk's hand: *Yara Thornbury. Oath-bound. Last of the swordspeakers. Keeper of ledgers.* Below that, in ink he had mixed himself from ash and tallow so that it could not be forged: *Do not underestimate. Do not threaten what she loves. She will trade anything for a principle and nothing for herself.*

It was rare, in his experience, for a person's file to be accurate. Files were portraits painted by gossip. But Thornbury's file had proven true in every particular, which meant either that he understood her completely or that she understood herself completely, and he suspected — with the small, unwelcome ache of a man recognizing something in a mirror — that it was the latter.

She had broken into the Hall tonight. Of course she had.

He had known since dusk. There was a way the building held its breath when someone moved through it who did not belong — a pressure behind the ears, like weather coming off the sea. Marrec had felt it at vespers, had finished his supper without hurrying, had wound the clock, had trimmed the lamps, and had come down here to wait, because there was only one thing in this entire city worth stealing, and he had always intended to be present when she found it.

He set the file down and touched the shelf where it had stood.

Behind it, in a recess no deeper than a prayer book, lay the thing he kept that no warrant described, no inventory listed, and no superior living or dead had ever been told about. A sword hilt, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle with age. No blade — or rather, a blade that had become something else, something like a seam of black glass grown through the shape of steel, humming so faintly that you felt it in your teeth before you heard it in your ears.

He did not touch it. He never touched it. In forty years he had dusted around it, inventoried around it, built the very architecture of his life around it, the way a tree builds around a fence wire swallowed in its heartwood.

He was aware, distantly, of footsteps above. Soft ones. Careful ones. She was good; he would grant her that. Better than the two agents he'd sent to the drowned chapel had been, and they had come back with salt-water lungs and stories they begged him not to log.

Marrec poured a second cup of chicory from the flask he'd brought, out of a habit he could not explain even to himself, and sat down on the stool by the desk to wait for her the way one waits for rain one has already decided not to curse.

* * *

She came down the stair at the turn of the hour, and she came armed, and she came afraid, and both of those things were correct of her.

Yara Thornbury stood at the bottom step with her oath-blade drawn and her boots silent on stone, and Marrec watched her take in the room: the shelves, the lantern, the file lying open on the desk, the second cup steaming faintly beside it.

"The door," she said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles were not white; she had trained that out of herself, or the blade had. "There was no lock."

"There never has been." He gestured at the stool opposite. "Sit. Drink. You've been in the sewers; I can smell the river on you, and the chicory is better than whatever you've been drinking out of guilt."

"You knew I was coming."

"I've known since the Saltrow report, Mistress Thornbury. Tonight I merely knew the hour." He folded his hands. Up close, in the honest light of a single lantern, he knew what she saw: a grey man, lean as a filing cabinet, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that clerks described in their own diaries as *patient*. He had cultivated patience the way other men cultivated vineyards. It was the only crop that grew in ground as stony as his.

"I'm not here to talk," she said.

"No one is ever here to talk. Talking is what happens while people decide whether to do the thing they came to do." He nudged the cup an inch closer to her side of the desk. "You're looking for the tenth oath. It isn't in the evidence vault upstairs — that's a decoy, a lead box full of river stones and a very convincing forgery of a swordspeaker's hilt. You would have wasted a night on it and possibly gotten Sef's contact killed, and I'd have had to write another file. This—" he nodded at the recess behind the shelf, not moving to point, "—is what you actually want."

For the first time, uncertainty crossed her face. It looked almost like pain. "Why would you tell me that?"

"Because," Marrec said, and found, to his genuine surprise, that the answer had been waiting in his mouth for longer than he'd known the question, "I'm tired."

* * *

Here is what Dain Marrec remembered, and what he did not:

He remembered being nine years old and swearing an oath on the Crown's law, because in the year of his childhood the law had been the only roof between him and the winter that had taken his family, and oaths sworn by children are the deepest kind, cut before the soul has calloused. He remembered rising through the Inquisition on a reputation for incorruptibility, which was true, and for ruthlessness, which was also true, and for never asking why, which was the foundation under both. He remembered forty years of files. He remembered the smell of tallow ink. He remembered a dream he'd had maybe once a decade, all his life, of a garden of black thorns and a voice like water saying his name — not *Dain*, but some older name, a name that fit his mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for — and waking each time with the sense of having missed an appointment of enormous importance.

What he did not remember was why the hilt in the recess hummed when he entered the room. He had told himself, decades ago, that it hummed for everyone. He had never tested this. Some part of him had understood, with the mute self-preservation of a body refusing to look at a wound, that testing it would cost him everything he was.

He was an unbroken oath. He simply did not know whose.

Twelve swordspeakers had sealed the covenant between the realms. Eleven had scattered their oaths into hiding when the guild broke the pact. One had not hidden. One had done something far cleverer and far crueler: he had buried his oath inside a duty so total that the duty became the burial mound, and the boy who swore it grew into the man who enforced it, and the man who enforced it forgot there had ever been anything underneath.

The Hollow did not forget. Somewhere beneath the world, in a court of exiled promises, a chair sat empty, and had sat empty for forty years, waiting for a grey man with patient eyes to remember his name.

* * *

Thornbury did not sit. But she lowered the blade — not sheathing it, lowering it, the way one lowers a voice — and crossed to the shelf, and drew out the oilcloth with hands that shook only a little.

"Where did you get this?" she asked.

"It was here when I took custody of the vault. My predecessor logged it as 'scrap, no value.'" He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "My predecessor was a fool in most respects. In that respect, I think now, he may have been wise."

She peeled back the cloth, and the black-glass seam of the blade caught the lantern light and threw it back wrong — threw it back the color of deep water, of thornshadow, of the space between two thoughts — and Yara Thornbury went very still, in the way of a woman listening to something speak.

Then she looked up at him, and her expression was the last one he expected, the one his whole apparatus of patience had been built to prevent:

Pity.

"Don't," Marrec said.

"You didn't ask what I saw."

"I don't need to. You looked at me the way people look at the condemned." He rose, slowly, because his knees had begun to ache in the cold, and found that his hands wanted to straighten his coat, and let them. "Whatever story you've assembled, Mistress Thornbury — whatever the blade told you, whatever the thornwood showed you, whatever theory the madwoman with the eleventh oath whispered into your ear — consider the alternative. Consider that I am exactly what I appear to be. A servant of the law. Forty years of files. Nothing underneath."

"That's precisely what frightens me," she said quietly. "A man is not nothing underneath. I've read your file too, Inquisitor. All of it. Three decades, and do you know what's missing from it? Not a single entry of cruelty that wasn't procedure. Not one indulgence. Not one day off. You built yourself into the law so completely that there's no Marrec left to find guilty." Her thumb brushed the black seam of the blade. "That's not devotion. That's a cage with the door welded shut from the inside. And I think—" her voice gentled, which was somehow worse than if she had accused him, "—I think something put you in it. Something old. Something that needed a jailer who would never wonder about his cell."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above them, a night clerk dropped a stack of papers, and the sound fell through the stone like a stone through water.

Marrec opened his mouth to deliver the rebuttal he had prepared — the one about sedition and sentimentality and the necessity of order — and discovered that the words would not come, because she had said *jailer*, and some door far back in him had heard the word and rattled on its hinges.

The dream surfaced. Black thorns. Water saying his name. The appointment missed.

"How long," he heard himself ask, and his voice sounded strange to him, younger, "have you known?"

"Since the drowned chapel. The second oath sang a list of the twelve, and eleven names were riddles. Yours was just a description." She met his eyes. "*The one who forgets on purpose.*"

"I haven't forgotten on purpose."

"I know," she said. "That's the tragedy of it. Someone arranged the forgetting so well that even the arrangement is hidden from you. You think you chose the Crown. Inquisitor — children don't choose roofs. Children are *put* under them." She lifted the wrapped hilt, cradling it against her chest like a child itself, and stepped back toward the stair. "I could fight you for this. You'd lose, and I'd regret it. Or you could arrest me, and the moon completes its turn in four nights, and everything burns — your Hall, my guild, the child, all of it, every ledger in every land. Or—"

"Or," Marrec said, because his mind, that tireless filing machine, had already run the columns, already weighed the precedents, already arrived at the conclusion that forty years of procedure had been quietly preparing him for and could not say aloud: *or the law itself was founded on a theft, and I am the receipt.*

"—or," she finished, "you let me walk out of here with the twelfth oath. And then, when you're ready — if you're ready — you come and find me, and we talk about giving it back."

"Giving what back?" The question escaped him raw. "Giving *what* back, Thornbury? Name it. Say it plainly."

She paused on the bottom stair, lantern-light climbing her face, and for a moment she looked at him with such sorrow that he understood, finally, the shape of the cage — not its bars, but its dimensions.

"Yourself," she said. "I'm asking you to let me give you back yourself. Think about whether you can survive that. Most men can't."

Then she was gone, up the stair, soft-footed as weather, and the Hall of Records closed its breath behind her.

* * *

Dain Marrec sat alone in the unlocked vault until the chicory went cold in both cups.

He should raise the alarm. Procedure required it within the quarter-hour; there were forms; there was a bell; there was a lifetime of habit reaching for his hand like a faithful dog. He did not move. The recess gaped empty behind the shelf, and the absence of the humming was louder than the humming had ever been, and in that silence he heard — very faintly, from very far beneath the world, or perhaps from beneath his own ribs — water saying a name.

Not *Dain*. Not *Marrec*. Something older, patient as stone, that fit his mouth like a key.

His hand, entirely without instruction from the rest of him, rose to his palm, where the Crown's seal was branded into the flesh — and he noticed, for the first time in forty years, that the brand was not the Crown's seal at all. It had merely been worn down, worn smooth, worn *interpreted*, the way a coin worn smooth becomes whatever the holder needs it to be. Underneath, in lines fine as roots:

A thorn.

Marrec began, very quietly, to laugh — or to weep; in the dark of the vault, with no witnesses to file the distinction, it was impossible to say which — and above him, over the sleeping city, the moon slid one night closer to its thirteenth turn.

Four nights, she had said.

He reached for the blank form on his desk, and the pen, and the tallow ink — and wrote, in a hand that shook for the first time since childhood, a single line that belonged in no file anywhere:

*I remember that I promised to remember.*
