## 20. The Thirteenth Night

The moon rose over the Hollow Thorn like a coin dropped into a well of ink.

Yara stood at the center of the drowned chapel's ruin — no, not the chapel; that had been the second oath, salt and coral and a woman who had loved her word more than her life. This place was older. The thorns had carried them here in the last hours, parting like a crowd making way for a funeral, and at their heart lay a circle of white stone worn smooth as an old coin: the Oathground, where twelve swordspeakers had once knelt and made the world hold together.

Eleven blades now hung around her neck on a cord that should have broken her spine with its weight. Eleven oaths, reforged, each one a voice, each one a debt paid by someone who had kept faith until keeping it killed them or worse. The eleventh she had taken from a river three nights ago, pulled from the mouth of a fisherman's ghost who had guarded it for two hundred years out of habit rather than hope.

One seat remained empty on the circle. Twelve stones, eleven filled with light the color of old honey. The twelfth waited for her.

*It will take everything,* Vess had said, in her new voice, her true voice, the one that remembered being twelve people at once. *Not your life. Your remembering.*

Yara touched the ledger at her hip. She had brought it, of course. She had brought it through graves and rivers and the burning of everything she had built her small rigid life upon, because a swordspeaker keeps accounts, and this was the last entry she would ever be able to read.

"Tell me again," she said. "All of it. I want to hear it while I still know what words mean."

Vess sat on the twelfth stone, barefoot as the night she'd arrived, though she was taller now — the covenant filling her back in, month by month, as the oaths returned to the circle. Her eyes were the gray of the space between storm and calm. "The covenant was spoken by thirteen," she said. "Twelve speakers and one vessel to carry the oath between realms when the speakers slept. When the guild stole me, the circle broke, and the moon began counting. Thirteen turns of grace. Tonight is the last."

"And the price."

"The circle must be whole. Twelve voices bound into the blade, and the blade bound to a living speaker." Vess looked down at her hands. "But I cannot be both vessel and voice. I am the thirteenth. The covenant needs a twelfth speaker who is willing."

"Willing," Yara repeated. "That's the word you keep using."

"Because it matters." Vess's chin came up, and for a moment Yara saw all twelve of the old dead in her face at once — the coral woman's stubbornness, the madwoman's terrible clarity, the fisherman's patience. "The first covenant failed because it was taken. Stolen. A vessel seized before she could choose. If we rebuild it the same way, it will break the same way. It has to be given."

The wind moved through the thornwood, and the thorns sang. Not menacingly tonight. Like a congregation waiting for the sermon to end so they could go home.

Yara walked the circle slowly, touching each stone. She had done this every night since the ninth oath, a ritual she hadn't planned, the way she used to straighten the ledger's corners before closing the vault. Each stone hummed under her palm with a different note. The second oath, salt. The fourth, iron and rain. The seventh, which she had pried from the grip of a skeleton in a barrow, smelled faintly of bread baking, and she had cried for an hour afterward without knowing why, because the memory of her mother's kitchen had been the price of taking it.

She had lost so much already. Three years at the market of borrowed years — her mother's face, gone, replaced by a shapeless warm grief that visited her at dusk. The name of the street she grew up on. The taste of the first meal she ever cooked alone. The sound of her own laughter at twenty-two, which she suspected had been a good laugh, because something in her chest always leaned toward it like a plant toward sun.

And now the rest. All of it.

"I want to be clear about the terms," she said, in her ledger-voice, the one that had priced widows' grief and thieves' names without flinching. "Everything means everything. My name. My guild. Corvane." Her throat tightened. "Him too?"

"Him too," Vess said gently. "You'll know he mattered. You won't know why."

Yara nodded. That was fair. That was honest accounting. She had spent her whole life believing that debts should be exact, that mercy lived in precision, and here at the end the covenant was precise with her. It did not pretend she would survive intact. It only promised the world would.

"And Vess survives. Whole. Both halves of her — the vessel and the—" She stopped. Because there was a thing she had not said aloud, the thing she had been carrying since the Thornwood, since the illusions, since the night the thorns had shown her a memory she didn't have: a small room, a fire, a child laughing, a future she had never lived but had somehow, impossibly, grieved.

Since the Thornwood, Yara had known she loved this child. Not as a charge. Not as a duty. As family. Chosen, sworn, absolute.

"You'll live," Vess said. "You'll run your little shop and write your little ledger and some mornings you'll look at a girl with gray eyes and feel something you can't name, and it will ache, and it will also be the best thing in your day." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I've seen it. I remember forward, sometimes. It's the curse of being thirteen people."

"Then it's a good trade," Yara said.

"It isn't." Vess stood. "It's a terrible trade. And I'm asking you anyway, because you're the only one left who would say yes, and that's the whole rotten truth of it."

Yara smiled. It surprised her how easy it was. "Good. Then we understand each other. In the guild we called that an honest contract."

* * *

They prepared as the moon climbed.

There was no grand ritual. The covenant, it turned out, was mostly bookkeeping — which Yara found, absurdly, comforting. The eleven blades had to be laid on the eleven stones, point inward. The speaker had to kneel on the twelfth stone and speak the words of binding, which Vess taught her syllable by syllable in the old tongue, the language the child had arrived speaking, the language Yara's bones apparently knew, because her mouth shaped sounds she had no translation for and they felt like coming home.

Her oath-blade — her own, the one that had hummed at a mute child in a storm, the one that had spoken *thirteenth turn* after twenty years of silence — went last, on the twelfth stone, in front of her knees.

"When you finish the words," Vess said, "the circle closes. The moon finishes turning. And then it takes payment." She hesitated. "I can do it instead. I've been thinking about it. If I split myself—"

"No." Yara said it fast, harder than she meant to. "Whatever you're hiding behind those gray eyes, whatever you think you can give up to spare me — don't. You are the only piece of the old world worth saving whole. That's not sentiment. That's arithmetic. Twelve souls in one body is a miracle. One soul emptied out to save them is just another theft." She met the child's gaze. "We are not going to found the new covenant on a theft. We are not going to be Corvane's founders. Do you hear me?"

Vess's jaw worked. Something moved behind her eyes — twelve expressions at once, grief and fear and a stubbornness Yara recognized as her own, reflected back from somewhere impossible.

"I hear you," Vess said finally.

"Then stand there and watch," Yara said, "and remember it. Remember me. All of it. Every stupid detail. Because if I'm going to forget, somebody damn well better keep the record."

She drew the ledger from her hip and opened it on her knee, right there on the stone, and took out her pen, and by the light of the honey-colored blades she wrote the final entry of the Guild of Impossible Things:

*Received: one covenant, broken. Paid: one speaker, entire. Terms: freely given. Witnessed by the moon, the thorn, and the thirteenth.*

*Balance owed: nothing.*

*Account closed.*

Her hand shook only a little. She closed the book and set it in Vess's arms.

"Keep it open," she said. "New page. New guild. Write down what I forget, so I can buy it back someday if the trade ever turns honest again."

"I will," Vess whispered.

* * *

Yara knelt.

The stone was cold through her trousers. Above her, the moon stood at the top of the sky, swollen and strange, ringed in a thin halo like breath on glass. The thorns at the edge of the clearing had gone utterly still, ten thousand black spears holding their breath.

She began the words.

The old tongue poured out of her like water finding a channel it had always known. She understood none of it and all of it — each syllable landed in her chest with a weight she could feel, and with each one, one of the eleven blades lifted from its stone. They rose into the air around her, slow as dust in light, and began to turn, a slow wheel of honey-colored fire, and the wind died, and the singing thorns fell silent, and the whole world leaned in to listen.

*Thirteenth turn,* her own blade had said, twenty years silent, on the night a barefoot child knocked. Now she spoke the words herself, and the blade blazed brightest of all.

The last syllable left her mouth.

The circle closed.

Light went up from the stones like a fountain, silent, total, and the eleven blades rang — one clear note, then twelve, then a chord that was not a sound at all but a feeling, the feeling of a door that had hung broken for centuries swinging shut on oiled hinges, the feeling of a wound knitting, the feeling of *enough*. The moon's halo flared white. Somewhere beneath her feet, deeper than roots, deeper than stone, Yara felt the Hollow exhale — a vast dark breath of relief, of hunger briefly stilled, of a pact remembered.

Then the payment came due.

It did not hurt. That was the first thing she noticed, and the last thought she would ever have as wholly herself: *it doesn't even hurt.* It was more like a tide going out. Memories loosened from her like barnacles from a hull, gently, one by one, each one lifting away with a small bright pop she felt behind her eyes.

Her mother's kitchen — already half-gone — finished dissolving, and she felt the grief that had replaced it dissolve too, and was grateful, and forgot she had been grateful.

Corvane. His office smelling of ink and cowardice. His hand on her shoulder the night he told her to turn the child away. His death, weeks ago, refusing to give up her route, his decades of rot ending in one clean act — she held onto that one, clawed at it, *let me keep the courage even if I lose the man*, but the tide did not bargain. He went. She felt his importance drain out of her heart like warmth from a cooling cup, leaving only a vague sense of having lost something large, a ship seen sinking far offshore.

The guild. The widow who bought her husband's voice. The thief who sold his name. The great ledger's leather smell. The rules she had served like scripture. Gone, gone, gone — and underneath them, astonishingly, remaining: the *why*. She could not remember a single trade, a single rule, a single night of quiet competence masking loneliness, but she remembered that she had believed in exactness, that mercy lived in precision, that debts should be honest. The tide took the furniture and left the floor.

Sef's betrayal. The burning vault. Marrec's face when she had offered him his lost oath instead of a fight, and the terrible cracking of the law around him as he let her take it. Gone. She would wake tomorrow not knowing why she trusted no one in a uniform.

The Thornwood. The illusions. The night the thorns showed her the small room, the fire, the laughing child, the future never lived. She gripped that one with both hands as it slid loose, and for one blazing moment she saw it whole — saw the life she might have had, the daughter she might have raised, the ordinary happiness she had traded without ever possessing it — and then it went, and what remained was not the memory but the love itself, stripped of every image, pure and imageless, like a flame after the candle has burned away.

The tide reached the shore of her.

And there, standing at the waterline of her emptying self, was Vess.

The girl had not stayed on her stone. She was kneeling in front of Yara now, inside the wheel of turning blades, holding the ledger against her chest, and tears were running down her face, and her mouth was moving — saying something, saying it over and over — and Yara realized with the last flicker of her remembering mind that she could no longer understand the words.

No. Not quite the last flicker.

Because Vess stopped crying. Vess went very still. And Yara saw — with the final coin of awareness she possessed, spending it the way she had spent everything else, deliberately, on purpose — she saw the girl look down at the ledger in her arms, and then up at the wheel of blades, and then at the moon, and Yara saw in those gray eyes a decision being made, a decision with teeth in it, a decision that was going to cost.

"*Don't,*" Yara tried to say.

But the old tongue had already left her, along with everything else, and the word came out as nothing at all, and the moon completed its turn, and the light took her —

— and Vess opened the ledger to a fresh page, and stepped into the circle's heart, and began to speak.
