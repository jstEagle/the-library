## 22. The Ledger Left Open

The shop smelled of cedar oil and rain.

Yara Thornbury had chosen the street for its ordinariness. Lamplighters' Row, they called it, though no one had lit a lamp on it with any ceremony in forty years; the name was a leftover, like so many things, kept because no one could be bothered to scrape it off. The building had been a cobbler's once, then a chandler's, and now it was hers: a narrow front room with a counter of scarred oak, shelves that bowed under the weight of honest goods — needles, salt, candlewax, thread, the small inventory of small lives — and, behind a curtain of faded blue, a back room where she kept a single ledger bound in green leather.

She did not trade in memories anymore. She did not trade in names, or hours, or borrowed years, or any of the impossible things that had once filled her days like water filling a cracked cup. What she traded in now was simpler. A widow came for tallow candles and stayed to talk about her son in the river trade. A boy bought his first straight razor on credit and Yara wrote the debt in the ledger with the same care she had once given to entries worth kingdoms. When people paid, she crossed the line through. When they couldn't, she waited. It was, she had decided, the only kind of bookkeeping that had ever made sense to her, and it had taken her the better part of two lives to learn it.

Two lives. That was how she thought of it now, though the phrase was not quite right. Not two lives. One life, with a door knocked out of the middle of it, and light coming through the gap from a room she could no longer enter.

The gaps were the strangest part, months on. She had expected them to ache constantly, the way a missing tooth aches, the tongue returning again and again to the wound. And for a while they had. But grief, she had discovered, requires a shape to hold, and hers had none. She knew she had loved someone once — a mother, she thought, though she could not have said how she knew — and the knowing sat in her chest like a stone sits in a riverbed, smooth and permanent and unremarkable. The water of her days went over it. Sometimes she woke weeping and could not say why, and she would lie in the grey light and let it pass through her like weather, and by breakfast it was gone, and she did not mourn it. She had stopped mourning it. That was the phrase she used, when she thought of it at all: *stopped mourning*, as if mourning were a coat she had hung up by the door and simply never taken down again.

There were other absences, smaller ones. The taste of a particular honey-cake. A song whose melody lived in her hands but not her ears, so that sometimes she caught herself drumming it on the counter and could not finish it. A face — one face — that surfaced in dreams more often than any other: young, sharp-eyed, laughing at something Yara had said, though the dream never showed her what. She woke from those dreams with her cheeks wet and an absurd, groundless happiness in her ribs, and she would make tea and watch the steam climb and think: *someone. Someone I chose.*

She had written it all down, early on, before the last of it went. She remembered doing that much. A letter to herself, sealed and hidden, to be opened only if she ever needed to know. It was in the vault below the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth beside the blade.

The blade. Oath-Weaver hung on the wall of the back room now, plain and quiet, its humming gone to silence. Eleven notches of light slept in its steel like fish at the bottom of a pond, visible only when the sun struck it at certain angles. It did not speak anymore. It did not need to. Its work was done, and done things rest.

The bell above the shop door rang — a real bell, brass, bought secondhand — and Yara looked up from the ledger with her pen still wet.

* * *

Autumn had come to the city in earnest. Through the window she could see the gutters running amber with fallen leaves, and the lamplighter making his rounds in the failing afternoon, touching flame to wick after wick until the whole street glowed like a string of held breaths. Business was slow. Slow was good. Slow meant the world was behaving itself.

Her first customer of the hour was old Mistress Aldery with her weekly half-pound of salt, and her gossip with it: the miller's daughter wed at last, and wasn't it a scandal how quick, and didn't the bride look pale. Yara weighed the salt, wrapped it, took the coin, laughed in the right places. She was good at this. She had been good at listening for longer than she could account for — it was one of the skills that had survived the door knocked out of her, along with her penmanship and her instinct for when a lie was being told to her face. Some things go deeper than memory. Some things are carved into the hand.

After Aldery left, a tinker spread his sad assortment on her counter and haggled her into buying a dented kettle she did not need, and she let him win, because his hands shook and winter was coming. Then a long quiet, in which she inked the day's entries and listened to the leaves tick against the window glass, and the light moved across the floorboards the way it always moved, patient as water.

The bell rang again near dusk.

"Closing soon," she called, without looking up. "If it's thread you're after, I've black and brown and nothing else till the caravan comes."

No answer. Only the sound of the door easing shut again, softly, politely, and small feet on her boards — bare feet, by the sound of them, on a night already cold enough to frost the pump handles.

Yara set down her pen.

The girl stood just inside the door, half in shadow, hooded against the wind. Small. Ten years old, perhaps eleven, thin in the way of children who have walked a long way on short rations. Her cloak was travel-stained and her hem dark with road mud, and she stood the way no beggar child stood: without apology, without the practiced cringe of the streets. She stood like someone arriving exactly where she meant to arrive.

And her eyes —

Yara's hand went to the counter edge and gripped it.

The girl's eyes were brown shot through with gold, flecked like a hawk's feather, and Yara knew them. Not remembered them — *knew* them, the way the hands know a song. They were eyes she had seen across a ledger counter and in a storm-lit doorway and in the green depths of a thornwood where illusions wore your own face, and beneath the world, in a court of exiled oaths, when a stolen fragment of twelve souls had lifted her chin and refused, absolutely refused, to be a thing instead of a person.

But Vess had been six years old. This girl was older. And Vess had split herself into twelve — Yara knew that the way she knew the stone in the riverbed of her chest, without source, without story. Twelve souls scattered back into the world to knit the covenant closed. Twelve girls or women or perhaps not-girls-at-all walking somewhere under the same sky, each carrying a piece of what had been whole, none of them whole themselves.

Neither of them whole. That had been the price. Both saved, neither entire.

"Shop's closing," Yara said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "But I've bread, if you've walked far. And tea."

The girl considered her. Then she reached into her cloak and brought out a book.

It was small, palm-sized, bound in leather gone soft and dark with handling, its corners worn round as river stones. Old. Older than the guild, Yara thought; older than the Crown; older than anything on Lamplighters' Row except perhaps the row itself. There was no lock on it, no clasp, no sigil. Just a cover and, when the girl laid it on the counter between them and opened it, pages of dense, flowing script in a language that predated every kingdom whose coins lay in Yara's till.

The old tongue. The language of the Hollow. The language the child had spoken barefoot in a storm while a blade hummed in answer, twenty years and one knocked-out lifetime ago.

"I can't read it," Yara said quietly.

"I know." The girl spoke for the first time. Her voice was clear and low and unhurried, and it landed in Yara's chest like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there. "That's why I brought it. You're supposed to read it out loud. The words remember themselves when they're spoken. That's how oaths work."

"And who told you that?"

The girl almost smiled. "Someone who used to be me."

The fire popped in the little stove. Outside, the lamplighter finished his round and the street lay full of soft gold, and somewhere far off a cart rattled home before the true cold came down. Yara looked at the book, and at the girl, and felt the stone in her riverbed shift — not painfully, not like grief. Like a bed being turned toward the morning.

"There's tea," she said. "Sit. Tell me your name."

"You won't know it," the girl said, pulling out the stool and settling onto it with the gravity of someone much older wearing a much younger body. "I only got it last spring. But—" and here she paused, and the hawk-gold eyes fixed on Yara with an expression that was achingly, impossibly familiar, the expression of someone deciding to trust, "—the person who gave it to me said you'd want to hear it anyway. She said you'd understand why. Even with the holes."

"Did she," Yara managed.

"She said to tell you: some debts are paid in joy. And this one's yours to collect." The girl slid the book across the counter until it touched Yara's ink-stained fingers. "Start anywhere. It doesn't matter where. It all comes around."

Yara opened the cover. The pages breathed out a smell of thorn and salt and old rain, and the script seemed to move the way water moves, and her oath-blade — silent on its wall for a year and more — gave one soft note, deep in the back room, like a sleeper turning over, like a heart remembering its rhythm.

She began, not knowing the words, to read aloud.

And the words, faithful as oaths, began to remember themselves.
