## 2. What the Veil Remembers

The charm had been made of nothing.

That was what haunted Elodie most, walking home through the gray hours before dawn. He had sold charms for eleven years, and every one of them was a small architecture of lies: river-stones polished to look like luck, candle stubs blessed with words that meant nothing, knots tied in red thread while he murmured nonsense syllables he'd invented himself at nineteen, bored and half-drunk in a rented room above a tannery. The lies were the point. People bought the ritual of hope, and hope did the rest, and no one had ever been healed by anything Elodie Sandoval had ever sold.

Except Marta Voss had died holding his knot-work in her fist, and her granddaughter's fever had broken before sunrise.

He climbed the stairs to his rooms above the chandler's shop with his coat still smelling of the night market—tallow smoke, fried onions, the wet-wool scent of the poor—and sat down at his table without lighting a lamp. The ledger sat where it always sat, beneath a loose floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and discovery. Eleven years of names. Every person the Temple of Records had struck from the rolls of Vael: the magic-touched, the sympathizers, the ones whose crime was a cousin or a grandmother or a single ill-advised prayer spoken aloud in a year when prayers had teeth.

Two thousand one hundred and six names. He copied them from whatever sources debt allowed him access to—the Temple's own discard piles, misfiled censuses, the marginalia of archivists who grew careless after their third cup of wine. Corven thought him merely meticulous. Meticulous was a virtue in a copyist. Obsession with the erased was heresy.

Elodie unwrapped the ledger now because he could not stop thinking about Marta Voss's hands, and about the word she'd whispered before she pressed her coin into his palm. *Answer me.* Not *help me*. Not *save her*. She had asked him for a prayer answered, and he had given her thread and lies, and something on the far side of the world—or the far side of the veil—had apparently been listening.

He set the book on the table and opened it to the first page.

The ink was faintly warm.

He told himself it was the room, the residual heat of his hands, the oilcloth trapping warmth like a blanket. But when he turned to the letter M, running his finger down past merchants and midwives and men struck out for singing the old harvest songs at a funeral, the page seemed to hold light the way water holds moonlight—not glowing, exactly, but *remembering* glow, as though brightness had recently passed through it and left an impression behind.

His finger stopped on a name. **Marta Voss.** Struck from the rolls nine years ago, along with her husband and two sons, for the crime of keeping a shrine to Lumen-the-Dawn-Mother in a root cellar.

"She asked me," Elodie said aloud, to the empty room, and felt foolish, and said it again anyway. "She asked me to answer."

The lamplight in the street below guttered. Or it didn't. He couldn't be sure anymore, and that was the worst part—that certainty itself had begun to feel like a luxury from another life.

* * *

He spent three days doing what he did best: copying, cross-referencing, lying by omission. By day he transcribed tithe records for Archivist Corven, who watched him over the top of his spectacles with the patient hunger of a man waiting for a debt to ripen. By night, when he should have been selling charms, Elodie walked.

Vael City at night was a city pretending to sleep. Watchmen patrolled the Processional with lanterns painted with the Temple's eye-and-quill sigil; curfew bells rang at the ninth hour and were ignored everywhere south of the Tanner's Canal. Elodie moved through the ignored places, and he carried with him a list he'd compiled from the ledger's margins—every miracle rumor he could extract from the night market's gossip without seeming to ask.

A fishmonger on Salt Row whose drowned son had walked out of the harbor and into her kitchen, dripping, smiling, gone again by morning. A lamplighter near the Old Exchange who swore his dead wife's voice came up through the sewer grates every dusk, singing a lullaby in a dialect officially extinct. A child in the Weaver's Quarter who drew spirals on every wall—spirals that appeared, faintly, on the walls themselves, as though the plaster were learning them.

Seven rumors in three nights. And when Elodie plotted them, as he plotted everything, against the mental map of the city he knew better than its own cartographers, the pattern emerged so cleanly that he stopped in the middle of Ropemaker's Walk and stood very still until a drunk stumbled into him and swore at him and moved on.

Every miracle sat within thirty paces of a place where names had been erased.

Not near temples. Not near the seats of power, where the Inquisition's wards hung thick as cobwebs. Near the *erasures*. The tenement on Salt Row where a family of hedge-witches had been taken twenty years ago, their apartment re-let, their names scraped from the lease-book. The alley off the Old Exchange where a priestess of the Dawn Court had been executed, her name later removed even from the execution rolls—as if killing her once hadn't been enough. The Weaver's Quarter schoolhouse built on the footprint of a burned chapel.

The veil, Elodie thought, standing in the dark with his breath fogging, doesn't cover everything evenly. It's thin where they cut people out of the world. It thins over every wound.

And I have spent eleven years writing down exactly where all the wounds are.

The realization arrived not as thought but as temperature—a cold that started in his chest and spread outward, down his arms, into his fingers. Two thousand one hundred and six names. He had thought of the ledger as an act of mourning, a private rebellion, a way of refusing the Temple's great amnesia. He had never imagined it might be a map. A key. That his careful, clandestine, *criminal* devotion to the forgotten might make him not a mourner but a hinge.

Somewhere across the city, something was pressing against the veil from the other side. And it was pressing hardest where his ink was darkest.

* * *

On the fourth night he went back to Salt Row, because the fishmonger's story was the freshest and the drowned son had *walked*, which was more than voices through grates.

Brin Ocas was a broad woman with forearms like mooring rope and the flattened affect of someone who had wept herself empty years ago. She let Elodie in only because he bought smoked eel from her stall every market day and had once, quietly, undercharged her for a fertility charm she hadn't needed but had wanted anyway.

"He stood in the doorway," she said, staring at her stove rather than at him. "Water coming off him like he'd just come up from the deep nets. He said—" Her jaw worked. "He said, *Ma, you kept my plate.*"

"Did you? Keep his plate?"

"I keep everyone's plates." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were terrifyingly level. "You think I'm mad. Everyone thinks I'm mad. But you sell charms, Sandoval, and your charms don't work, I've known that for years, and I bought them anyway because they're *kind*. So tell me kindly: is my son alive?"

Elodie wanted to lie. It was, professionally speaking, his greatest skill. Instead he heard himself say: "I think something is coming back. Through the places this city tried to forget. Your son was struck from the census, wasn't he? After the drowning. The Temple ruled his death a judgment and erased the record."

Something flickered in Brin Ocas's face—hope and horror wearing the same expression. "Why would erasing him let him *walk*?"

Because, Elodie thought, the veil is woven out of records. Because the Temple didn't just outlaw magic, it unmade the evidence of it, name by name, roll by roll, and a seal made of forgetting has holes wherever the forgetting failed. Because somewhere, a god who was supposed to be dead found a gap the exact shape of a drowned boy whose mother kept his plate.

He said none of this. He said, "I'm going to find out," and gave her a knot of red thread for free, and hated himself for the habit of it even as her shoulders eased.

Outside, snow had begun to fall—the first of the season, fat and silent, softening the rooftops of Salt Row. Elodie turned toward home, and had gone perhaps forty paces when he felt it.

Warmth. At his breast.

He stopped beneath a leaning awning and pulled open his coat. He had taken to carrying the ledger with him—he couldn't articulate why, only that leaving it behind felt like leaving a limb—and now, through the oilcloth, through his shirt, he could see it: a faint luminescence, gold-white, pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse. As he watched, it brightened. Dimmed. Brightened.

Rhythmic. Patient. Like something knocking.

Down the length of Salt Row, in the window of the fishmonger's house, a candle flame stood perfectly still in a drafty room. On the alley wall beside him, frost was forming in patterns—spirals, fine as lacework, climbing the bricks toward his hand.

Elodie ran. He was thirty years old and he ran through falling snow like a boy pursued, all the way home, up the stairs, into his rooms, where he slammed the door and put his back against it and breathed.

The ledger lay on his table, unwrapped, glowing steadily now, its light spilling across the floorboards like spilled honey. Two thousand one hundred and six names, and every one of them awake.

"No," he said to it. "No, I kept you hidden. I kept you *safe*. You're a record. That's all you are. You're grief written down so it can't be denied."

The light did not answer in words. It answered in memory: his mother's voice singing a harvest song the Temple had outlawed, the melody surfacing from eleven years of deliberate forgetting, note-perfect, as though some vast attention had reached into him and turned over the stones of his mind to show him what still lived underneath.

They had erased her too. His mother. Third page of the ledger, first column. He had been fourteen.

Elodie slid down the door until he was sitting on the floor, the way he had sat the night the Inquisitors came, the night Dain Vasq had looked at a terrified boy surrounded by contraband and said, in front of witnesses, *"This one is nothing. Move on"*—a mercy that had never once, in five years, stopped feeling like a debt of its own.

The ledger pulsed. Warm. Insistent. *We are here,* it seemed to say. *We were always here. Read us.*

And underneath the wonder—underneath the terrible, intoxicating sense that the world was wider than the Temple had permitted—it coiled the fear, cold and specific: miracles clustered around erasures. Erasures recorded in one place, and one place only. If the gods could find the wounds, then so could anyone who followed the same map. And there were people in Vael whose entire profession was following maps to forbidden things.

There was a knock at his door.

Three measured raps, unhurried, official. Through the wood, a voice he knew from five years of nightmares, courteous as a drawn blade:

"Master Sandoval. Inquisitor Vasq. Might I trouble you for a moment of your time?"
