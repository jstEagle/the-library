## 3. The Inquisitor's Mercy

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a copy of the *Codex of Tithes* that Elodie had been sent to rebind—a book no one had requested in eleven years. He knew before he broke the wax seal what it would say. The seal itself told him: not the Temple's sun-and-quill, but a single vertical line bisected by a horizontal stroke. The mark of the Inquisition.

*You are invited to attend upon Inquisitor Dain Vasq at the House of Questions, tomorrow, the ninth hour. Come alone. Come unburdened.*

*Unburdened.* Elodie read the word four times by candlelight in his rented room above the charm-shop, and each time it meant something worse. It could mean: come without weapons. It could mean: come without your lawyer of friends and excuses. Or it could mean, in the Inquisition's particular dialect of courtesy: we know what you carry, and we want to see how you behave when asked to set it down.

He thought about running. He had thought about it often over five years—the southern ferries, the salt roads, a new name in a city where nobody sang hymns. But running required money, and money required the night market, and the night market required him to be exactly who he was: Elodie Sandoval, debt-bound copyist, seller of harmless charms, keeper of a ledger that had begun—two nights ago, in the dark after Maren Ashka's prayer was answered—to glow.

It glowed now, faintly, through the floorboard where it was hidden. A pale gold seep like light under a door. He pressed his palm against the wood as if he could hold the light in by pressure alone.

"They know," he said to the empty room, because saying it aloud made it survivable. "They have to know."

The ninth hour came gray and cold. The House of Questions did not look like a place where questions lived. It looked like a rich man's townhouse gone to seed—shuttered windows, a garden strangled by ivy, a door answered not by a guard but by a servant girl who took his coat and asked, mildly, whether he'd eaten. That frightened him more than chains would have.

Dain Vasq received him in a study lined with books whose spines had all been inked over. Black rectangles where titles used to be. Elodie recognized the work—it was Temple erasure, the same brushstroke pattern he'd spent years reconstructing from fragments—but he had never seen so much of it in one room. Hundreds of books. A library of names that no longer existed.

"Sit," said the Inquisitor.

Dain Vasq was not what rumor made him. Rumor said tall; he was merely broad, thick through the shoulders like a man who had once done labor and never quite lost the habit of bracing himself. Rumor said cruel eyes; his eyes were tired, brown, almost kind, which was its own kind of terrible. Kindness in an Inquisitor was a lure, and every fish in Vael knew the shape of hooks.

"You've been copying for the Temple six years," Dain said, pouring tea without asking. "Debt-bound since the incident. Do you know what the incident cost? Not the fine. I mean truly."

"No one ever told me the number."

"Four hundred crowns. You've paid back perhaps sixty, at the rate Archivist Corven allows." He handed over the cup. Their fingers did not touch. "At that rate you will die in the Temple's service. Corven has arranged the arithmetic that way. Did you know?"

Elodie's throat tightened. "Why do you care about my arithmetic?"

"I don't." Dain sat behind his desk, and the lamplight caught the silver chain at his collar—the inverted veil sigil, the eye closed by a bar. "I care about what you do when you're not doing arithmetic. Tell me about the night market."

"The night market sells soup and stolen buttons."

"It also sells charms. Small mercies, the sellers call them. Charms against headache, against unfaithful lovers, against bad milk." Dain's voice stayed level, conversational, the voice of a man discussing weather. "Most of those charms are theater. Dried herbs and confident lies. And then there is one seller whose charms—" he paused, tasting the next word, "—work."

The tea went cold in Elodie's mouth.

"Inquisitor, if someone's selling you working magic, I'd be careful. That's a serious accusation in this city."

"It is. So let me be precise, and you listen, and we'll both be careful." Dain opened a drawer and laid three objects on the desk between them like a dealer laying cards. A child's wooden bird, painted blue. A scrap of cloth embroidered with a name half-unpicked. A small tin locket, dented. "Three families. Three prayers. All answered within the last fortnight. The bird flew. The name on the cloth returned to the cloth, letter by letter, while the mother watched. The locket grew warm enough to scald, and inside it appeared a lock of hair belonging to a grandmother dead nine years. Hair that matched what remained in her grave."

Elodie said nothing. His heart was a drum being played badly.

"All three miracles," Dain continued, "occurred within fifty paces of places where records were destroyed during the Purification. A birth registry burned. A chapel's roll of names scraped blank. A census page cut out with a knife, leaving a scar of stubble-edged paper." He leaned forward. "And in each case, before the miracle, witnesses remember seeing a thin man with ink-stained fingers standing very still, holding something under his coat that gave off light."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the patience of an institution.

"You spared me," Elodie said finally. "Five years ago. Everyone said I'd burn. Even my advocate said it. And you walked into the chamber and pardoned me and never said why, and I have spent five years waiting for this conversation."

"And here it is," Dain said. "Show me the ledger."

* * *

*Dain watched the boy's face do the arithmetic of survival.*

That was what he privately called them, the ones he summoned: boys and girls, though most were older than him now, worn down by the state's slow grinding. People who had committed small crimes of memory in a country where memory itself had been criminalized. He watched their faces calculate—what does he know, what can I deny, what will denial cost—and Elodie Sandoval's face went through all of it faster than most. Clever. Frightened. Honest underneath, which was rare and dangerous.

Five years ago, Dain had stood in a judgment chamber with a file full of evidence—receipts for charm components, witness statements, a search order already drawn—and he had torn the order in half in front of the tribunal. His colleagues had assumed corruption. His superiors had assumed leverage. Only Dain knew the truth: he had pulled the file expecting a smuggler and found instead a list. Forty-one names, copied in a young clerk's meticulous hand, each one cross-referenced against the destruction records of the Purification. Names that existed nowhere else in the world except in that hidden ledger and in the memory of people dying of old age.

One of those names was *Vasq, Ilse*. His sister. Erased at nineteen for healing a fever with bare hands. Officially she had never been born, and their mother had been ordered to agree, and their mother had agreed, and something in Dain had broken along a seam he didn't know he had.

He had not known, five years ago, why the ledger mattered beyond grief. Now he did. The gods were answering prayers again, and they were answering along the scars of erasure, and the boy who kept the list of erased names was either the key to the whole catastrophe or the only person alive who might survive it.

"Show me the ledger," he repeated.

"Why?" Elodie set down the untouched tea. "So you can confiscate it? So the Temple can finish what the Purification started? You've got a room full of inked-out books behind me, Inquisitor. I counted them coming in. Two hundred and thirty. I know what those brushstrokes mean better than your own clerics do—I've spent six years reconstructing what they erased. So don't ask me to hand over the only true record left, and pretend there's a version of this where I benefit."

There it was. Not defiance exactly—something more exhausting than defiance. The flat clarity of a man who had already priced out every outcome.

"You're right," Dain said. "If I take the ledger, the Temple wins. Corven has been petitioning my office for two years for authority to conduct 'archival integrity reviews' in the night market. He wants your ledger, Sandoval. He wants it more than he wants you, and he wants you in irons as a bonus." He turned one of the objects on the desk—the child's wooden bird—so its painted eye faced the boy. "But the Temple isn't the only power in Vael anymore. Something is answering prayers. Something old. And when old things wake up, countries burn. I have read the histories that survived. I know what happened last time."

"So do I. I've read further than you have." Elodie's voice dropped. "I know what was erased and *why*. Some of those names belong to people who did monstrous things in the god-war. And some belong to healers. My ledger holds both. That's the trouble with truth, Inquisitor—it doesn't sort itself for your convenience."

"No," Dain agreed quietly. "It never does." He stood, crossed to the shuttered window, and opened it a hand's width. Cold air moved through the room, carrying the smell of the river and, beneath it, faintly, something sweeter—incense, maybe, or lightning. Across the city, a bell began to ring in a tower that had held no bell for twenty years. He heard the boy's breath catch.

"That started Tuesday," Dain said. "Bells ringing in towers that don't exist. Statues sweating. Prayers answered at the sites of erasure, always at the sites, as if something is feeling along the wounds of this country trying to find where it was cut." He turned. "I am offering you protection. Real protection—a writ under my seal, immunity for the charms, an end to Corven's arithmetic. Your debt, bought and burned. In exchange, you help me identify the miracle-sites. All of them. Before the Temple seals them, or before whatever waits behind the veil decides it's done knocking."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out of here free, and I give you until sundown before Corven receives an anonymous letter. I won't lie to you about what happens after." Dain held his gaze. "I spared you once for reasons I couldn't explain. This is the explanation. I need you, and I hate needing anyone, and you should weigh that hatred carefully because it means I'll keep my word—men like me protect the things we hate to need."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. The bell across the city finished ringing, but the sweet smell lingered, strengthening, impossible.

"The sites," Elodie said at last. "I can find them. Better than your surveyors. Because the ledger doesn't just hold names—it holds *where* they were taken from. Every entry has a place." He swallowed. "But I want one thing in return that you haven't offered."

"Name it."

"When this ends—whatever way it ends—I want the ledger read. Publicly. Every name spoken aloud where the whole city can hear it." His ink-stained fingers curled against his knees. "You erased a country, Inquisitor. I want it given back."

Outside, far off, another bell rang—one that shouldn't exist, in a district that had been renamed twice. Dain felt the sound pass through the glass like a hand testing a latch.

"Agreed," he said, and hated how easily it came, and hated more the flicker of something in the boy's expression—not relief but recognition, as if Elodie had just understood something about Dain that Dain hadn't yet admitted to himself.

They shook hands. The boy's grip was cold.

"One more thing," Elodie said at the door, not turning around. "The third miracle-site. The locket with the grandmother's hair. You said the grave matched." He paused. "Inquisitor—whose grave were you able to check? Whose family keeps records that old?"

Dain did not answer. The boy nodded slowly, as if the silence were itself an answer, pulled his coat from the servant girl's arms, and walked out into the gray morning.

Alone in his study of inked-out titles, Dain poured the untouched tea back into the pot, an old habit of poverty, and stood a long time looking at the window. Beneath the floorboards of this house, wrapped in oilcloth in a place no warrant would ever reach, lay a single sheet of paper in a dead woman's handwriting. *Ilse Vasq*, it began, and then the record of her nineteen years.

The bells were ringing again. More of them now. Closer.

And underneath their ringing, so faint he might have invented it, something that was not a bell at all—something rhythmic and vast, like breathing, like a tide drawing back from a shore it intended to take.
