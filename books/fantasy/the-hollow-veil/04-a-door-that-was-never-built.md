## 4. A Door That Was Never Built

Sefa Ilyen had spent eleven years learning to think of the veil as weather.

That was the trick of it, the thing they taught you in the Engineering Collegium before they ever let you near the anchor-works: do not call it a machine, do not call it a wall, do not call it a sin. Call it climate. A pressure gradient over the whole country, holding back something that wanted in. You did not feel guilt about weather. You maintained it. You calibrated it. You went home at the end of your shift and ate dinner and slept like anyone.

She had been very good at sleeping like anyone, for eleven years.

Then the prayers started getting answers, and Sefa stopped sleeping entirely.

She stood now on the roof of her own tenement in the Saltrow district, three stories above the night market's guttering lanterns, watching for Elodie. She had watched for him four nights running. She knew his habits better than she knew her own—knew he worked the far end of the market where the lamplight failed, knew he never sold to children, knew he kept his left hand closed when he lied so the charm-seller's gesture wouldn't betray him. She had known him since they were nine years old and stealing plums off the Temple terraces, back when neither of them had debts or secrets worth the name.

Tonight he came early. She saw him below, hood up, moving through the crowd with the particular carefulness of a man who has recently learned he is being watched. He stopped at a stall, spoke to no one, bought nothing. Then, without looking up—without needing to—he said, quietly, in the old cadence they'd used as children to pass messages across a schoolroom:

"You're leaking grief, Sefa. It carries."

Her chest tightened. Of course he'd felt her. Whatever was happening to the city, it had happened to him first, and more completely, and she had known that since the ledger began to glow in her instruments' shadow like a coal refusing to die.

She descended.

* * *

They met in the stairwell between floors, because she could not bear to bring him inside where the anchor-diagrams were pinned to her walls, where the truth of her life hung in inked concentric rings. In the stairwell there was only a lamp and two people who had once been children together.

"You've been following me," Elodie said. Not an accusation. Worse—an observation, gentle as a physician's touch.

"I've been trying not to." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You have no idea how hard I've been trying not to."

"Then stop trying." He pulled back his hood. In the lamplight his face was thinner than it had been a month ago, shadows carved under his eyes, and there was something new in him—a stillness, a listening quality, like a man standing in a doorway hearing voices on the other side. "Tell me what you know about the veil, Sefa. The real veil. Not the temple catechism. Not 'the gods abandoned us for our sins.' I stood in a dying woman's room last week and asked the old way for mercy, and mercy came. Something answered me. And every miracle since has bloomed around places where names were cut out of the record." His eyes held hers, steady and terrible. "You're a veil-engineer. You keep the seal. So tell me: what is it sealing?"

The question she had dreaded for eleven years, asked in the voice of the boy who used to split stolen plums with her down the middle.

"We should go up," she said. "You need to see it drawn."

* * *

Her rooms smelled of lamp oil and cold tea. She had cleared the table before he arrived—she had planned this conversation a hundred times in a hundred sleepless hours—and now she unrolled the diagram across it with hands that would not quite stay steady.

Elodie looked down at it for a long moment. "This is a machine," he said.

"It is not a machine," Sefa said automatically, the catechism rising in her throat, and then she made herself say the true thing, the first time in eleven years she had said it aloud to anyone outside the Collegium's sealed halls. "It is a machine. That's the first lie. Everyone in Vael thinks the gods left, or died, or were never there—that the silence is natural, like an empty nest. It isn't. The gods are *there*, Elodie. They have always been there. We built a lid over them and called it heaven's absence."

He didn't step back. She had expected him to step back.

"Show me," he said.

So she did. Her finger traced the outer ring. "The Veilwork. Eleven years ago, at the end of the Godswar—you won't find the Godswar in any record, that's part of the design—the armies of the old orders were calling down acts that were leveling cities. Ashfall over Vael-Sar. The drowning of the Lowmarch. Children born with their names already screaming. Both sides claimed the gods sanctioned them, and maybe both sides were right, and that was the horror of it. The war wasn't ending. It was *escalating*. Every prayer was a weapon and every believer was ammunition." Her finger moved inward. "A coalition of engineers from all three faiths designed the Veilwork. Not to kill the gods—they may not be killable—but to deafen the connection. To make prayer a one-way letter that never gets delivered. No answers, no miracles, no power. And without power, the war just... ran out of breath. Men put down swords they could no longer swing by divine mandate."

"And the cost?"

There it was. He always found the cost; he had been finding costs since he was nine years old and counting the plums they owed the orchard-keeper against the ones they'd eaten.

"The erased," Sefa said quietly. "The veil needs anchors. Living anchors, at first—people whose names were bound into the working. And after..." She swallowed. "After, the anchors needed to be forgotten to hold. A name remembered is a thread; a name recorded is a rope. So the state struck them from every register. Birth rolls, death rolls, parish books, family Bibles rewritten. Whole lineages unwritten. The people in your ledger, Elodie—" her voice caught on it, "—aren't just victims of a bureaucratic purge. They are the load-bearing structure of the peace. Their erasure is what holds the lid on."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Neither of them moved to right it.

"My sister's name is in one of those registers," Elodie said at last, very softly. "Mara Sandoval. Died in the Lowmarch flooding, age six. Except there was no flooding, was there. There was a war, and she was an anchor, or collateral of the anchoring, and then she was *unwritten*."

Sefa closed her eyes. "I'm sorry."

"You helped build it." Not cruel. Just placed, like a stone set down on a table.

"Yes."

"Why?" And this was the blade under the gentleness, because Elodie had never been able to hate anyone properly, he only ever wanted to understand them, and being understood by him was worse than being hated. "You were twenty-two. You were the kindest person I knew. You cried over dead birds. How did you help build a machine that unmakes people?"

"Because I had watched a city drown," Sefa said, and her voice finally broke loose from everything she'd held it with for eleven years. "Because I stood in the water at Lowmarch and pulled bodies out for six days and on the seventh day I stopped finding bodies and started finding *pieces*, and the priests on both banks were singing hymns over the wreckage, each side certain their god willed it. I was twenty-two and I wanted the screaming to stop more than I wanted anything in the world, and they offered me a way to make it stop, and I said yes. I drafted the resonance harmonics for the third anchor-ring. My handwriting is in the founding documents. I have spent eleven years telling myself it was weather." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you know what the Collegium calls the veil's maintenance schedule? *Climate stewardship.* We gave it a boring name so we could live with it. That's the second lie. There is no name boring enough to live with this."

Elodie reached across the table—not for the diagram, but for her hand, the way he had when they were children and one of them had fallen out of a tree. His palm was warm and ink-stained and shaking slightly.

"Sefa. The prayers are being answered. Your lid is failing."

"I know." She turned her hand over and gripped his, hard, because if she didn't hold onto something she was going to come apart. "I know. Three weeks ago the pressure gradients inverted along the whole eastern anchor-line. The veil isn't tearing from outside—it's being *called* from inside. Something on the other side heard a prayer get through, one crack, one answered mercy, and now every erased soul pressed against that membrane is pushing toward the sound." She met his eyes. "And the resonance signature of the breach, Elodie—I've run it forty times. It matches a document. A handwritten ledger of names, copied in a clerk's hand, hidden somewhere in Saltrow. Your ledger. Every name you wrote down in secret, every memory you kept alive, has been a hairline fracture in the seal. You didn't mean to. I know you didn't mean to. But you have been keeping the dead remembered, and the remembered dead are the one thing the veil cannot hold."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst part. He absorbed it the way stone absorbs rain, and what surfaced in his face wasn't fear.

"Good," he said.

"*Good?*"

"They were real, Sefa. Mara was real. The Lowmarch dead were real. If remembering them cracks the lid, then the lid was built on a crime, and the crack is the most honest thing in Vael." His grip tightened on her hand. "Come with me. Help me open it properly—controlled, witnessed, not a rupture. You know the harmonics. You could—"

"No." She pulled her hand free, and the word came out harder than any she'd ever aimed at him. "No. Elodie, listen to me. You think the door opens onto justice. I have *seen* what's behind it. I built the lock and I have read the founding reports and I am telling you that the last time that door stood open, cities burned and children drowned and the gods themselves took sides in a slaughter that lasted nine years. The peace is false, yes. The history is a lie, yes. But the *quiet* is real. Mothers in Vael have raised eleven years of children who were not conscripted, not burned, not drowned for a god's amusement. If you tear the veil, you don't free the country. You restart the war—with interest, with eleven years of rage banked on both sides of the membrane."

"And if I burn the ledger instead?"

"Then the fractures close. The veil reseals. Nothing changes for anyone except you, and me, and the dead, who will go on being patient." Her throat closed around the next words. "Burn it, Elodie. Please. I am begging you as the person who has loved you longest in this world. Burn the ledger, and I will spend the rest of my life finding another way—slowly, safely, a way that doesn't drown anyone. But if you carry that book one step further toward the anchor-works, I will stop you. Do you understand me? I helped build the door. I know exactly where its hinges are. And I will use everything I know."

The lamp flame steadied. For a long moment the only sound in the room was the city outside—the night market winding down, a drunk singing, the ordinary blessed noise of a country at false peace.

Elodie straightened. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and final, and it sounded like a man stepping off a ledge.

"I believe you'll try, Sefa. That's what breaks my heart." He pulled his hood up. "But Mara's name is in my book. And I have decided that no peace built on forgetting her is a peace I'm willing to keep."

He was gone down the stairs before she could answer, and Sefa stood alone over the diagram of the door she had helped build, her hands flat on the table, trembling.

Then she turned to the shelf, and took down the sealed message-tube reserved for Collegium emergencies, and broke its wax.

By dawn, Archivist Corven would know everything.
