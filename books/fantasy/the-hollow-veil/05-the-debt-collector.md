## 5. The Debt Collector

The summons came the way debts always came: quietly, on good paper, with someone else's seal.

Elodie found it waiting on his desk when he returned from the night market, folded once and weighted beneath the inkwell he was not permitted to own. The wax bore the Temple of Records' sigil—a closed eye above an open book—and the hand that had addressed it was Corven's, precise as a scalpel, each letter standing at attention.

*Master Sandoval. Your presence is requested at the ninth hour. Come alone. Bring nothing.*

Bring nothing. As if Elodie could bring anything else. Everything he owned fit in two satchels and one ledger bound in calfskin, hidden behind a loose brick in his chimney where it sat now, faintly warm against his back when he stood too close to the fire. It had been warm for three days. He tried not to think about what that meant.

He did not sleep. At the eighth hour he dressed in his temple grays, the uniform of a man who belonged to an institution rather than to himself, and walked through streets still slick with morning fog. The city felt different now. Since the woman Maren had died holding his hand while her impossible prayer settled over her like snowfall—since her grandson's fever broke in a house three districts away from any temple—Vael had developed a listening quality. People paused at shrines they'd stopped seeing years ago. Old women mouthed names under their breath at doorways. And everywhere, in the corners of things, Elodie saw the places where records had been scraped clean: the pale rectangles on memorial walls, the gaps in census rolls, the way certain streets curved around lots no one would build on, as if the ground itself remembered something the paperwork forbade.

The Temple of Records rose out of the fog like a jaw. He climbed its steps past clerks who did not meet his eyes, and into the archive levels where the air tasted of dust and cold iron, down corridors lined with ledgers that held every debt in Vael—every coin owed, every favor outstanding, every life mortgaged against some future the state had already spent.

Corven's office was at the center of the labyrinth, naturally. The spider sits where all threads converge.

Archivist Corven was not what people expected. They imagined a monster; they got a small, neat man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles he polished when he wanted time to think. He looked up as Elodie entered, and smiled the smile of a man greeting a favorite nephew.

"Elodie. Sit. You look terrible."

"I've been working."

"You've been *working*." Corven set down his pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the desk's edge. "Yes. I imagine you have. Tell me about the Vasseroy district."

Elodie kept his face still. Five years of copying records for this man had taught him that Corven never asked a question he couldn't already answer. "It's a poor district. Textile work. High child mortality."

"It is," Corven agreed pleasantly. "And three nights ago, a boy named Tomas Vasseroy recovered from a fever that had already reached his lungs. The attending physician wrote a report—the man is old-fashioned, keeps private notes the censors haven't gotten to yet. He describes the recovery as, and I quote, 'inexplicable by any known mechanism.'" Corven removed his spectacles and polished them. "Do you know what I do with inexplicable events, Elodie?"

"Record them."

"I *contain* them." The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water. "That has been my function for twenty-two years. Every miracle, every haunting, every prayer that got answered by something other than the sanctioned liturgy—I find it, I file it, and it stops being true. Not erased. Erasure leaves edges, and edges invite questions. Containment is cleaner. The event simply becomes unremarkable. A boy got better. Mothers are grateful. Move along."

He put his spectacles back on and regarded Elodie over their rims.

"The problem," Corven said, "is that lately the containment is failing. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Liar." No heat in it. Almost fond. "You know because you're the reason. Or half of it. There's something wrong with the veil itself—it's thinning, fraying, whatever verb you prefer—and prayers are getting through the holes. But holes need edges, Elodie. Something has to define where the fabric tore. And every single breach site in this city—" he drew a folded paper from his desk drawer and laid it flat between them, "—corresponds to a name on this list."

Elodie looked down. His own handwriting looked back at him. Forty-one names, copied from memory over five years of secret nights, the record of everyone the state had struck from history. He had written them small, to save space. Now they seemed to shout.

"That's a copy," Corven said. "You keep the original somewhere in your rooms. Behind the chimney brick, third from the hearth floor. You really should find better hiding places; the man who installed your stove owes me money."

The room tilted slightly. Elodie gripped the arms of his chair. "How long have you known?"

"About the ledger? Four years." Corven smiled again, and this time there was nothing familial in it. "Why do you think you were never audited? Never reassigned? Why your debt terms were so generous, your hours so flexible, your little night market so very profitable? I don't crush useful anomalies, Elodie. I cultivate them. You are the most interesting thing in my collection."

*Collection.* The word landed like a blow. Five years. Five years of careful, invisible living, and he had been a specimen in a jar the entire time, watched through glass by a patient man with clean fingernails.

"What do you want?" Elodie asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that.

"Your ledger."

"And if I refuse?"

Corven spread his hands, the picture of reasonableness. "Then I invoke the early-call clause. Your contract permits it—you signed it, you can read it, section eleven, subsection four. The full balance of your debt comes due immediately. You cannot pay it; no one could; that was the design. So you go to debtor's prison, which in the Temple's case means the Silent Cells below this building, and your assets are seized to offset what you owe. All your assets." He tapped the paper with one finger. "Including the one behind your chimney. My men will be at your rooms within the hour regardless, Elodie. The only question is whether you walk out of here having chosen, or whether you're carried."

*Every path runs through him.* Dain Vasq wanted the ledger to find miracle-sites, to hunt what leaked through. Sefa wanted it burned, to seal the wound she'd helped make. And Corven wanted it contained, filed, made untrue—the names of the dead locked in a drawer forever, doing exactly what the state had done to them in life.

Three hands reaching for the same book. None of them reaching for the people in it.

"They're not yours," Elodie said quietly. "The names. They belong to the people who carried them. To whoever loved them enough to remember."

"Sentiment." Corven said it the way another man might say *contamination*. "You misunderstand what's happening, and your misunderstanding will get people killed. The veil isn't just fraying—it's *straining*. Whatever is on the other side has been pushing for thirty years, and now it's found purchase, and every answered prayer widens the tear. When it goes, it won't be gentle. I have read the sealed accounts of what came before, Elodie. The war your friend Sefa helped end. Cities that no longer exist because we agreed to forget them. I am trying to prevent that. Whatever else I am, I am the only person in this building whose motives you can actually verify: I want things quiet. That's all. Quiet."

"And the names stay buried."

"The names stay *safe*." Corven leaned forward. "There's a difference, and your life may depend on understanding it."

For a long moment neither spoke. Somewhere far above, bells marked the quarter hour, and Elodie thought of Maren dying with her son's name finally spoken aloud after nine years, thought of how her face had eased—not healed, nothing was healed—but eased, the way a fist eases when someone finally takes its weight.

If he gave up the ledger, that weight went into a drawer. If he refused, it went into a drawer anyway, and he went into the dark beside it.

Unless.

"There's a third option," Elodie said.

Corven's eyebrows rose a fraction. "I'm listening."

"You get the ledger. I deliver it myself, today, freely—no seizure, no prison, no clause invoked. In exchange, I keep my position and my freedom, and I assist your containment efforts. You said yourself I'm the most interesting thing in your collection." He held the Archivist's gaze. "Use me properly, then. I can identify breach sites faster than your surveyors. I knew these people, Corven. I know where they lived, where they prayed, what they lost. Your men take months to map what I can walk to in an afternoon."

It was a lie wrapped in a truth wrapped in desperation, and he watched Corven weigh it, watching for the flaw.

"Why?" Corven said at last. "Why would you surrender it willingly?"

Because Dain expects me to run to him. Because Sefa expects me to burn it. Because the only move none of them have planned for is the one where I hand it to the spider myself—and a book in a spider's web can still be stolen back. Because every path forward runs through someone who wants to use me, so I'll choose the user whose grip I understand best.

"Because," Elodie said, "prison doesn't help anyone. And because I'd rather be the hand that gives than the throat that's cut."

Something flickered behind Corven's spectacles—approval, or its counterfeit. He extended his hand across the desk, ink-stained fingers open.

"Done. Deliver it by sundown. We'll begin tomorrow."

They shook. Corven's palm was dry and cool, like paper.

* * *

Elodie walked home through streets that had begun to fill with ordinary noise—vendors, cart wheels, a child chasing a hoop—and felt the ledger's warmth through his coat before he even opened his door. Inside, he knelt at the hearth and drew it out from behind the brick. The calfskin cover was hot as a sleeping animal. When he opened it, the forty-one names shimmered faintly, ink catching light that wasn't there, and beneath the last entry, in a space that had been blank that morning, new writing was surfacing.

Not his handwriting. Older. Deeper, as though pressed up from underneath the page.

Two words, in a script the Temple had outlawed and Elodie had only ever seen scratched into the underside of a shrine:

*WE HEAR.*

He stared until his eyes burned. Then, hands steadier than he had any right to expect, he closed the book, wrapped it in oilcloth, and began to plan how a man steals back his own soul from the center of a spider's web—because sundown was coming, and with it Corven's outstretched hand, and Elodie intended to put something in it.

Just not everything.
