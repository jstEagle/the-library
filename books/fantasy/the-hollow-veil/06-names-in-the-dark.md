## 6. Names in the Dark

Dain Vasq had broken into the Temple of Records three times in his life. The first two times, he'd been wearing its colors.

Now he wore a porter's coat two sizes too large and carried a crate of lamp oil on his shoulder like any other night laborer, and Elodie Sandoval walked three paces behind him with his collar up and his ledger strapped flat against his ribs beneath a scribe's smock. The boy—man, Dain corrected himself; five years was long enough to stop calling him a boy—moved through the service yard the way water moves through cracks: without hurry, without resistance, without ever quite being where you looked.

*You spared him once,* Dain thought, adjusting the crate's weight against his spine. *And now you're teaching him to break the law you enforce.*

The thought should have felt like treason. It felt instead like taking off boots after a very long march.

"Gate watch changes at the bell," Elodie murmured, not looking at him. "Corven keeps the third shift thin because he trusts the locks more than the men."

"You've studied this place like an enemy."

"I've worked here like a prisoner. It's the same study."

The bell rang—dull bronze, half-swallowed by fog rolling off the canal—and the gate guards drifted toward the brazier at the corner of the yard, hands out for warmth. Dain crossed the shadow between wall and door without breaking stride, set down the oil crate beside six identical crates that belonged there, and let Elodie's pick find the lock before his own hand could. The scribe's fingers were quicker. Of course they were. A man who sold charms out of a night market learned doors the way priests learned psalms.

Inside, the Temple smelled as it always had: dust, tallow, ink gone sour in its pots, and beneath it all something older—the dry mineral breath of paper that no one would ever read again. Dain had spent eleven years breathing that smell. He had told himself it was the smell of order. Standing in it now with a criminal at his back, he understood it was the smell of a held breath.

"The sealed archive is below the Scriptorium," Elodie whispered. "I've copied records above it for four years. I used to feel the cold coming up through the floor and tell myself it was just cellars."

"It isn't cellars." Dain led him left, past the stair, into the corridor where the lamplight gave way to darkness and the darkness gave way to a second corridor that appeared on no floor plan Dain had ever signed. "It's a throat."

Elodie made a sound that might have been a laugh, strangled. "That's what she called it too."

"Sefa."

"Don't say her name like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you're already writing her confession."

Dain stopped walking. In the dark he could hear the younger man breathing, quick and controlled, the breathing of someone who had decided to be afraid later. He remembered that breathing. He had worn it himself, once, standing over a sister's bed while the fever took her voice and left everything else.

"There are only two kinds of people who come down here," Dain said quietly. "Those who think they'll find proof the state lied, and those who already know. Which are you tonight?"

"Neither." A pause. "I'm the kind who's tired of carrying names nobody will say out loud."

*Good answer,* Dain thought, and hated how much it sounded like his own.

---

The lock on the archive door was not a lock. Dain had known this for years and had never been able to prove it, which was its own kind of cruelty—it looked like iron and bolts, but when he laid his palm flat against it, the metal was warm, faintly, the way skin is warm, and it pulsed if you waited long enough. Veil-work. Sefa's guild's masterpiece, threaded through the Temple's foundations like roots through a grave.

"It knows us," Elodie breathed. His hand hovered near the door without touching. From inside his smock, a light was growing—a soft gold bleeding through the fabric, and Dain realized with a lurch that it came from the ledger, from the book itself, glowing against the man's ribs like a coal wrapped in linen.

"Hold still," Dain said. "Let it read you."

"That's not comforting."

"No. But do it anyway."

The pulse under the metal quickened, slowed, matched itself—to Elodie's heartbeat, Dain realized. To the rhythm of the names he carried. Then the bolts drew back one by one, unhurried, almost courteous, and the door swung inward on air that had not moved in a generation.

They went down together.

The sealed archive was smaller than Dain had imagined all these years, and vaster. One chamber, circular, shelved floor to unseen ceiling—but the shelves did not hold books. They held *names*. Tens of thousands of them, each inscribed on a slip of vellum no larger than a thumb, each slip pierced through with an iron pin, each pin driven into the shelf wood. A wall of pinned butterflies. A wall of pulled teeth. Dain had signed the orders that filled perhaps a tenth of these shelves, and seeing them all at once, physical, countable, *finite*, did something to his chest that he would spend the rest of his life declining to name.

"They kept them," Elodie whispered. He had gone gray. "All this time. They didn't burn anything. They just—" His hand rose toward the nearest shelf and stopped short, trembling. "They erased the people and filed the erasure."

"Records are the Temple's god," Dain said. "Even the ones that damn it."

At the chamber's center stood a lectern, and on the lectern lay a single bound text, chained at the spine, its cover stamped with the sigil of the founding synod. Dain lifted the chain aside—it fell away easier than it should have, as if the room wanted them to read—and opened the ritual to its first page.

He had expected scripture. What he found was engineering.

*The Veil is not a wall,* the founding hand had written, in ink still dark as wounds. *A wall can be climbed. The Veil is a forgetting, and forgetting must be fed. Every name struck from record loosens the gods' grip on the world; every memory unwitnessed thins their presence; in time they will be unable to gather enough of themselves to knock.*

Below, in a different hand—later, hurried, the letters leaning as if written by someone running:

*Correction, eleventh year: the Veil holds, but it is not one-way. What is forgotten here does not die there. The erased persist beyond it, whole, waiting. And the door answers to one key alone: true record. Whoever holds the genuine names of the erased—not copies, not summaries, the true tally, witnessed and kept—holds the hinge. Let no such ledger be permitted to exist.*

Dain read the last line twice. Then a third time, aloud, because Elodie had gone so still beside him that silence had begun to feel like a symptom.

"*Let no such ledger be permitted to exist.*"

"I know," Elodie said. His voice was strange, flattened. "I'm holding it."

"And the cost." Dain turned the page. The final section was written in red that had browned with age, and it was not metaphor, though the founding hand had tried to make it sound like one. *The hinge opens by sacrifice of the opener's own naming—that is, of his blood, freely given, line by line, until the reading is done. There is no partial opening. There is no closing afterward. Write this plainly for whoever comes after: the door takes the reader's lifeblood as toll, and the reader must know this and open it anyway, or it will not open at all.*

The lamp-oil crate upstairs could have fallen and Dain would not have heard it. The world had narrowed to the page, to the red ink, to the sound of Elodie Sandoval reading over his shoulder and going quieter with every word.

"So that's the price," Elodie said at last. Flat. Almost relieved, which frightened Dain more than despair would have. "Blood. Fine. I've bled for less honest reasons."

"No." Dain closed the book. The sound of it rang through the chamber like a struck bell, and ten thousand pinned names seemed to shiver. "You don't understand what 'freely given' means in founding-era script. It doesn't mean willing. It means *knowing*. You have to understand exactly what you're losing and choose it with your eyes open—and then keep choosing it, line by line, for however many names are in your book. How many, Sandoval?"

A long silence.

"Four thousand, two hundred and six," Elodie said.

Dain turned to face him. In the gold light leaking from the ledger, the young man's face looked carved, already memorialized, and Dain felt something crack along a seam he had kept soldered for five years.

"Then you'd die a thousand times over," he said, "or bleed out a tenth of the way through, and the door would stay shut, and the Temple would have your ledger and your corpse and everything they wanted." He heard his own voice go rough. "There has to be another way. There's always—"

"Why did you spare me?"

The question landed like a dropped blade. Dain had prepared for it for five years. He had rehearsed answers in the bath, on horseback, at his desk while signing warrants: *you were useful, you were young, the paperwork was inconvenient.* All of them lies, all of them ready.

He found he could not say a single one.

"Because I read your file," he said instead. "Before the arrest. That's procedure—I read everything. And your file listed your known associates, and among them was a woman arrested in the first purges, nineteen years ago. A hedge-charmer from the river districts. Ilse Vasq." His mouth had gone dry. He made himself finish. "My sister. She was erased when I was fourteen. I joined the Inquisition because they told me the purges were justice, and I believed it right up until the night I stood in your shop with irons in my hands and saw, in your effects inventory, a scrap of paper in your handwriting. A practice page. You were learning to write her name. My sister's name. Beautifully. Over and over. Like a prayer."

Elodie had not moved. The glow from the ledger lit the space between them like a small held dawn.

"I didn't spare you out of mercy," Dain said. "Mercy would have been arresting you. Sparing you meant I knew what the Temple was, and stayed inside it anyway, for five years, waiting to see if you'd find what we just found. Because if anyone could open that door—" his voice failed, steadied, "—it was the man who taught himself my sister's name for no reason except that names shouldn't be lost."

"Dain—"

"She's in your book." It wasn't a question. He had checked, years ago, through channels he still ashamed of. "Page forty-one. Ilse Vasq, taken in the second purge, no trial. You wrote *beloved by her brother* in the margin. I've read that margin a hundred times."

"Yes," Elodie said softly. "She's in my book. All of them are."

Above them, muffled through a generation of stone, the Temple bell began to ring—and kept ringing, wrong, urgent, the alarm cadence. Somewhere overhead, doors were being locked, lanterns lit, orders shouted. Dain's hand went to the ritual text, torn between seizing it and burning it.

Elodie was already moving toward the stairs, ledger glowing brighter now, much too bright, and when he spoke his voice carried a resonance that did not entirely belong to him—as if four thousand voices were learning to use his throat.

"They know we're here," he said. "And Dain—they're not afraid. They're *glad*."

The pinned names on the walls had begun, very softly, to hum.
