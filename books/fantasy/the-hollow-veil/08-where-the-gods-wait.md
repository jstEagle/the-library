## 8. Where the Gods Wait

They came for him at the hour when the prison lamps burned lowest, and they came with fire.

Elodie had learned the prison's sounds the way he'd once learned the Temple's: the creak of the gate chain, the boots of the night wardens, the particular cough of the man in the cell across from his who had been arrested, so far as Elodie could tell, for selling cheese. So when the cough stopped mid-rhythm, he woke before the shouting started.

Then came light—orange, wrong, spilling up the stairwell like water running backward—and a voice he knew.

"Third cell. Left side. Move."

Dain Vasq stood in the doorway of smoke with a crowbar in one hand and an expression that suggested he was doing arithmetic he disliked. His Inquisitor's coat was gone; beneath it he wore the plain wool of a dockworker, and there was blood on his sleeve that did not appear to be his own.

"You're burning down a state prison," Elodie said, throat raw from days of dust and silence.

"I'm burning down *Corven's* prison. There's a difference, and it's mostly arson." Dain wrenched the lock. "Up. Questions on the move."

"The ledger—"

"Was moved at dawn to the Temple's inner archive. Which is why we have until dawn *tomorrow*." He hauled Elodie upright by the arm, and his grip was gentler than his voice. "Half of it, Sefa's people got out first. Don't ask me how I know. Walk."

* * *

The city at night was not the city Elodie knew.

He had lived in Vael-Capital his whole life, had sold charms in its markets and copied lies in its archives, and he had never once seen it like this: lit from below. A faint gold glow rose out of the cobblestones themselves, pooling in the gutters, clinging to doorframes where once—before the Veil, before the Erasure—small gods' marks had been chiseled and then scraped away. The scraped places glowed brightest. The city remembered the shape of what had been removed from it.

And everywhere, prayers. He could feel them now, had been able to feel them since the night the dying woman's charm had answered—a low murmur under the world, like rain on a roof. But tonight the murmur was a chorus. Every whispered bargain, every frightened plea, every grandmother's grumble addressed to saints who officially did not exist—it all pressed against him, and at the center of it, warm as a coal in his breast pocket, beat the surviving half of his ledger.

"They can feel you," Dain said quietly, steering him through an alley where the glow ran ankle-deep. "The pages Corven didn't burn. Half a book of true names, loose in a city full of prayers. You're not carrying a ledger anymore, Elodie. You're carrying a tuning fork."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be."

They crossed the Weavers' District, past the fountain where three weeks ago a blind beggar had opened his eyes. Past the tenement stairwell where a child lost in fever had woken whole. Miracle-site after miracle-site, each one marked in gold, each one a place where a name had been struck from record—and Elodie felt each one hum as he passed, felt the half-ledger lean toward them like iron filings toward a lodestone.

The first site—the dying woman's site—was a shuttered dyehouse by the canal, and it glowed like a hearth.

"She prayed here," Elodie said. "Maren Ostreva. That was her name. It's in my book. She asked for one more winter, and something gave it to her."

"And now we ask what gave it," Dain said, "and what it wants in return."

He set his lantern on the dye-stained floorboards and stepped back. There was nothing else to do. No ritual in the stolen pages, no words of power. Only a man, a book, and a room full of answered prayer.

Elodie took out the ledger.

The surviving pages were singed brown at their edges, and they were moving—not fluttering, but *breathing*, rising and falling like a sleeping animal's flank. When he opened the cover, every name written there lifted off the paper in threads of gold light, hundreds of them, drifting upward through the ceiling of the dyehouse and out into the night over the canal, and Elodie felt each one go like a tooth pulled.

Then the dead spoke.

* * *

It did not feel like speaking. It felt like remembering things that had never happened to him.

He was standing in a wheat field outside Vael-Capital, except the capital was smaller, ringed by walls that no longer existed, and the sky was full of gods.

Not the sanitized figures in the Temple's permitted iconography—robed, mild, safely allegorical. These were vast and strange and *near*: a woman whose hair was rivers, walking through the wheat without bending it; a fat, laughing god of ovens and small debts; something antlered and patient that lived in the space between fenceposts. They walked among people the way neighbors walk among neighbors. They argued about weather. They sat at funerals and wept.

And then the war.

He saw it in flashes that hurt to hold: the god-touched armies, miracles weaponized, a river goddess drowned under her own flood while soldiers screamed her name as a battle cry. He saw cities burn because two nations each claimed the same god's favor. He saw a plague of prayers—millions of voices screaming to heaven at once until heaven itself began to crack, until the sky above the battlefield split like old varnish and something looked through that even the gods feared.

He saw a council of survivors—gods and mortals together, faces he somehow knew: young versions of faces carved into the Temple's oldest stones. And he heard the argument that ended everything.

*"Seal it," said the woman with river-hair, and her voice was exhausted beyond grief. "Cut us off. Cut ourselves off. Let them forget us, and let us forget them, or the thing behind the sky will come through next time, and there will be no one left to bury anyone."*

*"And the names?"* someone asked—someone with Sefa's voice, younger, shaking. *"You want us to erase them? People will die rather than be forgotten. Their children—"*

*"Their children will live," she said. "That is the trade. Peace for memory. Silence for survival. Build the veil, engineer. Build it well."*

And Sefa—young Sefa, twenty years younger, tears cutting tracks through soot—had built it well.

Elodie came back to himself on the floor of the dyehouse, gasping, Dain's hand hard on his shoulder. The gold light still hung in the air, waiting.

"You saw it," Dain said. Not a question. His face was wet; he either didn't know or didn't care. "My sister. Did you see my sister?"

Elodie searched the fading images. "There were thousands. I couldn't—"

"She sang at the sealing." Dain's voice cracked clean through. "She was one of the choir that anchored the ritual. Her name went into the veil itself—that's why it's in your ledger, why it called to me from the first day I read over your shoulder five years ago. She's not dead, Elodie. She's *woven in*. Part of the wall. Every day for twenty years the veil has sung with her voice inside it."

The horror of it settled over both of them, and neither spoke for a long moment. Beyond the shuttered windows, the city's glow pulsed like a heartbeat, and the chorus of prayers rose and fell.

"So that's the peace it stole," Elodie said finally. "And that's the war it ended." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Both sides are true. That's the filthiest part. Corven's right that opening the door could bring the thing behind the sky back. Sefa's right that she saved millions. And none of it matters, because none of it justifies *this*—" he held up the half-burned ledger with a shaking hand— "*erasing people. Maren Ostreva died unnamed. Your sister has been a brick in a wall for twenty years. You don't get to buy peace with forgetting and call it mercy.*"

"No," Dain agreed quietly. "You don't."

"Then help me finish it. Tonight, before Corven burns the rest. The anchor is beneath the Temple—I saw it in the ritual pages. If the ledger reaches it whole—"

The dyehouse doors exploded inward.

Sefa stood in the doorway, backlit by torchlight and the gold glow of a dozen Temple lanterns, and behind her came Corven's grey-cloaked archivists with iron cages for books and shackles for people. She was crying. She had clearly been crying for some time.

"I'm sorry, Elodie," she said. "I bought you a day. I tried to give you a day. But the ledger's already ash, and the anchor is sealed, and if you make me, I will stop you the way I stopped a war."

Behind her, at the edge of the torchlight, Archivist Corven smiled, and in his hands he carried the other half of Elodie's book—and a flask of holy oil, already uncorked, for burning.
