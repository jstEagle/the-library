## 10. Everything Carried

The first thing to go was the calendar.

Elodie stood in the Temple's great atrium, the ledger still warm against his chest, and watched the frieze above the entrance doors soften like wax held near a flame. The triumph of Vael Unbroken—generals raising swords over kneeling heretics, the First Archivist receiving law from a godless sky—ran like watercolor in the rain. The faces of the kneeling blurred first. Then the generals lost their eyes. By the time he had counted ten breaths, there was no frieze at all, only pale stone, smooth as an eggshell, waiting for whatever history would be carved into it next.

Around him, the city was doing the same thing.

Through the shattered doors he could see the Avenue of Records unmaking itself—not burning, not crumbling, but *forgetting*. Cobblestones that had been laid by masons whose names Corven's predecessors had struck from every ledger rose up and drifted apart like ash deciding it would rather be smoke. The Temple spire leaned, hesitated, and quietly became a field of wildflowers. Somewhere a bell tower rang once with no one to ring it, and did not ring again.

"It doesn't hurt," said a voice beside him.

Sefa. She was sitting on the bottom step of what had been the altar, her veil-engineer's gloves still on, though the instruments they'd served were already gone. Her face was streaked with soot and something else. She wasn't looking at the dissolving city. She was looking at her own hands.

"I built it to hold," she said. "I tested every seam. And now I can feel all of it letting go, and it's—" She laughed, wet and broken. "It's like being forgiven."

Elodie crouched beside her. He wanted to say *you betrayed me*, and he wanted to say *you saved me from Corven's fire*, and both were true, and neither mattered anymore, because the truth was walking through the streets of Vael wearing the bodies of everyone who had ever been erased.

A woman made of lamplight passed through the doorway where a door had been. She paused. She looked at Elodie with eyes like the night market at closing hour, and she smiled, and she said, in a voice like his mother's reading aloud:

*You kept our names.*

Then she was gone, and the doorway remembered itself, and Elodie understood that this was how the gods came home—not with trumpets, but with gratitude, door to door, street to street, until the whole country was lit from inside.

* * *

They found Dain where the anchor had been.

The chamber beneath the Temple was half-gone—the ritual circle still glowing its last gold, the Veil-works Sefa had spent nine years perfecting now nothing but a memory of machinery, which is to say, honest at last. In the center, where the door stood open onto a country that had never existed and therefore could never fall, Dain Vasq knelt with his hands pressed flat to the threshold, holding it wide.

He was translucent at the edges. Light moved through him the way it moves through early morning fog.

"Inquisitor," Elodie said, because titles were all that was left of either of them.

"Elodie." Dain's voice was calm. It was the same silk-wrapped-blade voice from five years ago, except now the blade was gone and only the silk remained. "Your debt is paid."

"What?"

"Corven." Dain tilted his head toward a heap of gray robes near the wall—a heap that was becoming less than a heap with every heartbeat, unraveling into names it had spent a lifetime refusing to speak. "The Temple's ledgers burned with everything else. Every debt recorded in ink that lied. Including yours." He smiled faintly. "You're free, charm-seller. Free of the Temple, free of the archive, free of me. You could stay. They'll want you to stay. The man who read three thousand names aloud while the world ended—they'll want to make you a monument."

"I don't want to be a monument."

"No," Dain agreed. "You want to carry them." He lifted one fading hand and pointed through the open door.

Beyond the threshold there was no landscape, exactly. There was *elsewhere*—a country of soft light and standing stones and rivers that ran with the color of answered prayers, and along its far edge, figures. Hundreds of figures. Thousands. The erased dead of Vael, waiting at the border of a nation that had denied them, patient as only the forgotten can be patient.

"My sister is out there," Dain said. "Merin Vasq. Struck from record when she was nineteen years old for lighting a candle to the Weaver. I pardoned you because your ledger had her name in it, and I couldn't bear to let it burn twice." His outline flickered. "She wrote to me last night. Did you know they can write letters here? She wrote: *Tell my brother the door was worth holding.*"

"Dain—"

"Don't waste a eulogy on me, Elodie. I was an instrument of the lie for twenty years. This is the only true thing I ever did." He pressed his hands harder against the threshold, and the light through him flared. "Go. Take the book. Someone has to remember them on the other side too, or forgetting starts all over again. That's the nature of the world. Veils grow back."

Sefa stood. She wiped her face with the back of her glove, and when she spoke, her voice was the steady voice of a woman who had built things and therefore knew exactly how they fell.

"The borders are dissolving outward," she said. "Vael won't vanish—it'll *unwrite*. In an hour there will be no Vael. Just land, and people, and every map in the world suddenly wrong. Anyone still inside when it finishes..." She looked at Dain, then at the door, then at Elodie. "Anyone who wants to exist outside history walks through. Anyone who wants to build something new stays."

"And you?" Elodie asked.

"I'm staying." She almost smiled. "Somebody has to design a country that doesn't need veils. I owe the profession that much." She reached out and touched his cheek, briefly, the way friends do when there isn't time for anything longer. "Burn nothing, Elodie. Not even the parts about me. Especially not those."

Then she turned and walked up out of the chamber, into the unwriting light, to argue with the future.

* * *

The crossing took no time at all, or all of it.

One moment Elodie was stepping over a threshold held open by a dying man; the next he was standing on grass that hummed underfoot, the ledger clutched to his chest, the door behind him narrowing to a slit of gold. Through the slit he saw Dain rise at last from his knees—whole, unbent, younger than Elodie had ever seen him—and walk away into the light without looking back, the way a man does when the watching has finally stopped mattering.

The door closed. Where it had been there was a standing stone, old beyond reckoning, carved with no name at all.

Elodie sat down against it and opened the ledger.

The pages were scorched at the corners where Corven's fire had taken its tithe—forty-one names gone to ash before Sefa turned on him, forty-one souls who had arrived in the new country ahead of the book, waiting anyway. But the rest were there. Three thousand and sixty-two names, in his cramped, careful, contraband hand. Merin Vasq. The dying woman from the night market whose prayer had started everything. Children. Weavers. A cobbler who had once sold shoes to an archivist and been erased for mentioning it.

As he read, they came to him. Not ghosts—*citizens*. They gathered in the long golden evening of the place that had no false history to defend, and they looked at the book, and some wept, and some laughed, and one old man took Elodie's hand and said, "You gave us back our weight," and Elodie discovered that he was crying too, and that it didn't feel like grief.

It felt like an accounting, finally balanced.

* * *

They offered him everything. Of course they did.

Word traveled fast in a country made entirely of memory. Within a season—he learned later it was called a season, though the sun here kept gentler time—the elders of the standing stones came to the small house he'd built near the river, and offered him a seat among them, and lands, and a title older than Vael had ever been: *Keeper of Names*. There would have been a house in the capital. Students. Statues, probably. He'd seen enough statues lately to know how they ended.

"You could stay forever," said the eldest, a woman whose face shifted gently between a dozen faces, all of them hers. "No one here erases anything. No one here will ever call you criminal, debtor, heretic. Your book could rest."

Elodie looked down at the ledger, lying open on his table in the last light.

"No," he said. "It can't."

Because that was the thing no one wanted to say aloud, the lesson written in the ashes of a country-sized lie: veils grow back. Forgetting is not an event; it is a weather. Somewhere beyond these hills there were other Vaels—other temples, other archives, other comfortable nations resting on the silence of the disappeared. And somewhere in those places, someone was selling harmless charms by night and copying lies by day, and did not yet know why their hands shook.

"I'll walk," Elodie said. "I'll carry the book. When people forget—and they will—I'll be the one who remembers out loud. That's the only restoration I want."

The elders did not argue. In a country of the erased, they understood exile better than anyone. The eldest simply touched the ledger's cover, once, the way you touch a wound that is also a door, and said: "Then carry us well."

* * *

He left at dawn, if dawn it was.

The road out of the god-country wound down through hills where the grass whispered in three thousand voices, and at the border stone—the plain one, the unnamed one—he stopped and did what he had done every morning since the world unwrote itself. He opened the ledger and read one page aloud. Names into the wind. Merin Vasq. The cobbler. The children. Forty-one blanks where Corven's fire had fed, which he read as silences, honored the way you honor empty chairs.

Then he closed the book, shouldered his pack, and walked down into the lowlands, where the first smoke of ordinary hearths rose from ordinary houses full of people who believed their history was safe.

Behind him, the hills dimmed like a lamp carried into another room.

Ahead of him, the world lay wide and forgetful, and heavy with names.

And Elodie Sandoval—free, unforgiven by no one, owed by everyone, owner of nothing but a book and a road—began, at last, to walk.
