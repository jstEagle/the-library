## 2. A Language Older Than Kings

Morning came to Vell the way it always did: slowly, and without ceremony. The light slid down the eastern wall of the valley like water finding a channel, pooling first in the birches above the mill, then spilling across the rooftops of Thatchrow, then finally reaching Felix's forge, where it found him already awake, sitting at his worktable with a cup of barley tea gone cold between his hands.

He had not slept. The child had.

She—Wren, she had signed, when he asked her name in the clumsy hand-talk travelers sometimes used; she had no words he could understand yet, or so he'd believed—lay curled on the pallet by the hearth where he'd put her sometime past midnight, wrapped in the good wool blanket he kept for the worst of winter and almost never needed. Her breathing was even now. The shivering had stopped an hour before dawn. In sleep, her face lost its terrible gravity and became simply what it was: a child's face, perhaps eight years old, sharp-chinned and hollow-cheeked, with a smudge of soot on one cheekbone that Felix had been unable to persuade her to let him wash.

He had tried everything last night. Bread soaked in honey. Questions in the trade-tongue, in lowland Vellish, in the clipped border-speech of the passes where he'd soldiered. She had looked at each attempt with the same expression—a kind of patient sorrow, as if he were a large and well-meaning animal making sounds at her—and had answered only once.

The answering was what kept him from sleep.

Because he *had* understood it. One sentence, spoken in a voice too old for her throat, in a language he had not heard in twenty years and had told himself, over twenty years of telling, that he had forgotten.

*Veth-kara ilun, veth-kara sel.*

*You came to me, and I was waiting.*

He set down the cold tea with a hand that was not entirely steady, and looked at the child, and felt the floor of the world tilt beneath the worktable's solid oak.

---

There had been a season—it was the season, though he never called it that, even in the privacy of his own skull—when the company had gone up into the headwaters country, north and east, beyond the last of the lowland kings' roads, into mountains where the maps went blank and honest about it. They'd been hired to guard a surveyor who died of fever in the second week, and after that they'd simply stayed, because the passes were snowed shut behind them and because Serah, their captain, said there was nothing for them below but creditors.

The people of the high valleys spoke the First Tongue.

Felix had learned it the way soldiers learn anything—badly at first, then suddenly all at once, out of hunger for warmth and bread and the names of things. It was not a language built for buying and selling. It had eleven words for snow and none for debt. Its verbs changed depending on whether you spoke to something living, something dead, or something that had never lived at all, and the children corrected your grammar with a solemnity that made the whole company laugh, except Aldous, who said it wasn't a language at all but a prayer that had gotten loose, and everyone else had laughed harder, and Aldous had not.

Aldous, who had been right.

Felix rose quietly, banked the fire to a low orange breath, and crossed the room to look down at the sleeping child. Up close, in honest light, she looked worse than she had by firelight—thin in the way that wasn't poverty exactly but absence, as if she'd been somewhere that fed on more than flesh. There were shadows under her eyes like thumbprints. Her lips were cracked. And when the blanket slipped as she turned, he saw again the thing he had seen by candlelight and refused, very deliberately, to think about: the mark at the base of her spine, a sigil like a coiled knot of lines, dark against the pale skin, precise as engraving.

Not a birthmark. Not a brand. He knew brands; he'd carried one himself once, on the shoulder, in another life. This was something else. This was *written*, in the way that contracts are written.

He tucked the blanket back up over it and stepped away as if the sight had burned him.

*Veth-kara ilun, veth-kara sel.*

He made himself tea he didn't drink. He split kindling he didn't need. He did all the small mechanical devotions of a man trying to outrun his own thoughts, and they caught him anyway, halfway through stacking wood, when he realized he was humming—not a tune, but the cadence of it, the falling rhythm of that single sentence, over and over, the way a man hums a hymn he swore off decades ago.

The First Tongue was dead. That was the story. The high valleys had emptied two generations ago; the traders said the villages stood roofless, the shrines choked with lichen, the grave-cairns tumbled open by frost. Whatever the speakers had prayed to had stopped answering long enough ago that even prayer gave out. Felix had come down out of those mountains twenty years ago carrying three things: a scar over his heart shaped like nothing natural, a silence in his chest where something used to speak, and a fluency he had spent two decades carefully, methodically forgetting.

You can forget a language. Anyone who says otherwise has never tried, and anyone who has tried knows how it goes—you don't lose the words, you build walls around them. You stop translating. You let them go feral in the dark of you, and after enough years you can walk past a locked door in your own mind and feel nothing but a faint chill, the way you might pass a house where someone once died.

Last night, a half-frozen child had knocked on that door from the inside.

---

Midmorning brought Marta up the lane with eggs and questions, in that order.

"Found her on your step, did you." Marta was seventy if she was a day, built like a doorframe, with eyes that had watched this valley for longer than most of its fences had stood. She set the basket on his table without being invited, which was Marta's way, and looked at the sleeping child with an expression Felix couldn't read—which, from Marta, was itself remarkable, because he had spent six years learning to read her and thought he'd mastered the alphabet.

"Before dawn," Felix said. "Half-frozen. No sign of anyone on the road."

"No one comes over the ridge this time of year," Marta said. "No one comes over the ridge any time of year. That's rather the point of us." She clicked her tongue. "Does she talk?"

"Not in anything I know."

"Mm." Marta's gaze stayed on the child a moment too long, and something moved behind her weathered face—recognition, maybe, or arithmetic. "Well. Fever's the danger, first days. Keep her warm, keep her drinking. I'll bring willow bark."

"She needs—" Felix started, and stopped, because what he'd nearly said was *she needs someone who speaks her tongue*, and there was no honest way to finish that sentence. "She needs a healer to look at her, eventually."

"Hollis is in Lowmere till the thaw." Marta gathered her basket, then paused at the door, and for a heartbeat the old woman looked older than the valley itself. "Felix. Whatever she says, however she says it—mind you listen careful."

The door closed. Felix stood looking at it for a long moment, hearing under Marta's plain Vellish accent something that might have been, in another woman, another place, a warning—or a blessing.

---

In the afternoon the fever came, as fevers do, riding in on the heels of warmth like a thief following a generous host.

The child's skin went hot and dry; her breathing quickened; she began to murmur. Felix did what the village did for fevers—cool cloths, broth when she could take it, willow bark steeped bitter—but there was a limit to what a farrier's craft could offer, and he felt that limit pressing on him with every hour, the old soldier's fury at wounds he couldn't bind.

It was the murmuring that undid him.

At first it was just sound—the shapeless vocalizing of the deeply ill, and he told himself it was only that. Then the sounds began to have edges. Then the edges began to have meanings, arriving in him unbidden, untranslatable into the trade-tongue and yet perfectly, horribly clear, like remembering how to swim while drowning.

*"Ilun-veth, ilun-kara, sel-venna tor..."*

*I waited. I waited so long. Where did you go?*

He sat on the stool beside the pallet with a cool cloth forgotten in his hands and listened to a feverish eight-year-old pray in the tongue of a dead people, and the vertigo took him fully then—not dizziness of the body but of the self, the ground-floor conviction of who he was tilting and cracking like river ice in spring. Because these weren't random phrases dredged up from a mercenary's winter. These were prayers. He knew the form. He knew the *cadence*. The high-valley folk had prayed like this at dusk, facing the mountain, in the rising-falling rhythm that Aldous had called a loose prayer and the children had corrected and the old woman of the valley—whoever she'd been, he'd never learned her name, only her voice, cracked as a bell—had sung every evening without fail:

*Sel-venna tor. Sel-venna kara.*

*Where did you go? Come back to us.*

They had prayed to a god. And Felix had knelt among them once, awkward and ironic and secretly hungry, and the god had *answered*—not thunder, not vision, just presence, a pressure like a hand laid flat against the inside of his chest, warm and vast and utterly certain. For one season of his life, Felix Halloran had been heard.

And then the passes. And the bargain. And the scar over his heart, and the silence that followed him down out of the mountains like a dog that had given up following but wouldn't leave.

The god hadn't gone silent everywhere, he understood suddenly, sitting in his small warm room with a stranger's fever burning inches from his hands. The god had gone silent *to him*. Perhaps silent to everyone—or perhaps the high valleys had emptied because the prayers stopped being answered, or perhaps the prayers stopped because—

*Because someone sold something,* whispered a voice in him that sounded like Aldous. *Someone made a trade, and gods keep accounts, and the ledger closed.*

"That's madness," Felix said aloud, to the empty room, in Vellish, in his own flat farrier's voice, and the child stirred and answered him.

Not in fever-murmur. Clearly. Looking at him—with eyes half-open, glassy with fever and yet fixed on him with an attention that had nothing feverish in it at all—she spoke in the First Tongue, slow and deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

*"Tor veth-kara, Ilun-sel. Tor veth-kara."*

*You know my name, wolf-of-the-long-waiting. You know my name.*

Then her eyes rolled back and the fever took her under again, and Felix sat alone in the ringing quiet of his forge, hearing the word echo in the chamber of his skull where he had walled up a language twenty years ago.

*Wolf.* The old name. The company's name for him, and after the company died, the passes' name for him, the name the hedge-shrines still whispered if you listened at the wrong hour—the Wolf Who Broke His Pack, they called him in places he'd sworn never to go again. A child who had never seen him before last night, who could not say bread or water or *help me* in any tongue he offered, had reached into the sealed vault of his buried life and pulled out the one word he'd left there on purpose.

Outside, the afternoon light was failing early behind cloud. Somewhere down-valley a dog barked twice and stopped, as if hushed. The fire ticked. The child breathed, fast and shallow, praying in her sleep to a god Felix had sold his portion of silence to, and every word landed in him like a knock at a door he had boarded over so carefully, so long ago, that he had almost convinced himself there was no room behind it.

Almost.

He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, hands hanging empty between them, and listened—for the first time in twenty years, deliberately, terribly, the way a man listens at a grave—to the fever-prayers of a child who should not exist, speaking a language that should be dead, in a valley that magic cannot enter.

And beneath her thin voice, beneath the wind testing the eaves, beneath even the drum of his own guilty heart, he heard—or dreamed he heard, and would spend the rest of his life unsure which—the faintest pressure against the inside of his chest.

Like a hand laid flat.

Like something waiting on the other side of the boards, patient as winter, beginning, very softly, to knock.
