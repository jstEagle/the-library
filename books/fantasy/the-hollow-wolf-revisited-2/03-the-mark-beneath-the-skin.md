## 3. The Mark Beneath the Skin

The fever came with the second night.

Felix had told himself it was only exhaustion—the child had walked into Vell through the high passes in the dead of autumn, after all, and no one crossed the Shearwind in that season without paying something for it. He had fed Wren broth, wrapped the small body in his own spare blanket, built the fire up until the one-room cottage glowed like a coal. He had done all the sensible things a farrier does when a stray turns up half-dead on his step.

But sensible things did not stop the shivering. And they did not stop the words.

Wren spoke in sleep now more than waking—long, low strings of that other language, the First Tongue, rising and falling like water finding its way downhill. Felix had not heard it spoken aloud in twenty years, and hearing it now was like pressing an old scar: the ache was familiar, and the familiarity was the worst part. He knew the cadence. He knew which syllables fell at the end of a plea and which rose at the end of a praise. He had knelt in a circle of speakers once, a lifetime ago, and listened to them pray to a god who still answered then.

*Still answers*, some treacherous part of him whispered. *Listens.*

He put another log on the fire and refused to think about it.

By midnight the fever had teeth. Wren's skin burned through the blanket, and the child had begun to thrash, small fists striking at things that were not in the room—or perhaps were; Felix had stopped trusting his own certainty about such things the moment he'd opened his door. The words coming out of Wren had changed too. They were no longer prayers. They had the shape of warnings.

Felix did what his mother had done for him, in another valley, before everything. He filled the copper basin from the well, tested the water until it was warm but not hot, and began, gently, to undress the child.

Wren wore a shift of undyed wool, coarsely woven, travel-stained at the hem. It came away damp with sweat. Felix worked by firelight, moving with the deliberate slowness he'd learned from years of handling horses in pain—no sudden motions, no surprises, hands always visible. The child whimpered but did not wake. Steam curled off the basin. Outside, the wind moved through the bare rowans along the lane with a sound like someone turning pages.

It was when he lifted Wren to ease the shift over the small shoulders that he saw it.

At first he thought it was a birthmark—a splash of darkness across the child's back, between the shoulder blades where the skin is thinnest and palest. Then the firelight shifted as the flames settled, and the mark shifted with it, and Felix understood that it was not dark pigment at all. It was a scar. Old, healed, precise. Burned into the living skin the way a smith burns a maker's mark into iron: deliberately, expertly, once.

A sigil.

Felix went very still. The basin water lapped against his wrists. The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse resumed its small industry, indifferent to the fact that the world had just tilted beneath a farrier's knees.

He knew the mark.

He knew it the way a man knows his own heartbeat—not because he studied it, but because he carried it. Slowly, with hands that no longer felt entirely like his own, Felix set the child down among the blankets, drew the blanket to Wren's chin, and pulled open the collar of his own shirt. He reached back over his shoulder, fingers finding the place over his left breast where the skin had never quite healed right, and pressed.

The scar there was ridged, raised, twenty years old. But he had seen it in a polished shield-boss once, in a camp mirror, in the grey light of dawn after the passes—and he had traced its lines a thousand times since without ever letting himself understand what he was tracing. A circle broken at the top. Three descending strokes within it, like rain falling inside a ring. A root-shape at the base, or a hand, or a hook.

The same. Line for line, curve for curve, exactly the same.

The feverish child on his hearth bore his scar on their back.

"No," Felix said aloud, to the room, to the fire, to whatever patient thing might be listening. His voice sounded strange in the small cottage—rough, unused to argument. "No, that's not—that isn't how it works."

But even as he said it, the arithmetic was already running, cold and automatic, the way it ran when he balanced a ledger or priced a shoeing. Twenty years ago, in the passes, he had gone down on his knees in the snow and made a bargain with something that wore a courtier's courtesy like a mask over nothing at all. He had sold something. That was the word the masked thing had used—*sold*—and it had asked him to sign the bargain in the only ink that could not be forged. It had pressed its thumb, cold as a grave-iron, against his chest, and the burning had gone all the way through him and out the other side, and when it was done there had been a mark on his heart and a hole in him shaped like whatever it had taken.

He had never asked what became of it.

That was the thought that surfaced then, breaking the surface of twenty drowned years like a body finally giving itself up to the river's pull. He had signed, and he had bled, and he had crawled back up the pass to his dying company with the bargain sealed, and he had *never asked*. Not what the Court would do with the thing he sold. Not where it would be kept. Not whether it could be grown, or spent, or—gods forgive him—*planted*.

The fire cracked. Wren moaned in sleep and turned, and the sigil caught the light again, and Felix felt his stomach drop through the floorboards of the world.

Because he knew what the Court did with souls. Everyone in the old companies knew, the way everyone knows the sun rises: the Hollow Court bought and held and hoarded, kept its vaults beneath the mountain full of flickering lantern-things, each one a person's worth of light locked in bone-white glass. You did not sell your soul to the Court and expect it to sit idle in a drawer. Souls were stock. Souls were seed. Souls were currency that appreciated, if you had the patience of the dead.

And the Court had patience the way mountains have weight.

*Twenty years*, Felix thought. The number arrived with the gentleness of a knife. *The god went silent twenty years ago. I made my bargain twenty years ago. This child is—*

He counted backward from Wren's face, the fine bones of it, the agelessness of children between six and eight. Seven years old, perhaps. Six.

Not twenty.

Relief started to rise in him, foolish and brief, and then he looked at the sigil again—at the perfection of it, the healed antiquity of it, a burn that should have been fresh on a newborn and instead looked like it had been part of the child since before the child was anything at all—and the relief curdled. Marks like this were not made in haste. Whatever had been written into Wren's back had been written long before this body existed to carry it. Planned. Grown toward. The way a farmer plans a field two seasons ahead of the planting.

Or the way a creditor plans a collection.

Felix sat back on his heels. The basin water had gone cool around his wrists. He became aware, distantly, that he was shaking—not the shaking of fear exactly, though there was plenty of that, but the shaking of a man whose body has understood something his mind is still refusing to sign for.

He had assumed, all these years, that he had paid with a piece of himself. That was how he'd carried it: a splinter removed, a coin spent, a wound that ached in winter. He had built his whole quiet life in Vell on that assumption. He had come to this valley because magic could not follow him here, because the valley's silence was total and democratic and kept out gods and Courts alike, and he had told himself the price was paid, the debt closed, the account settled in full. One piece of a soul, traded for the lives of eleven comrades. Bitter arithmetic, but honest arithmetic. He could live with honest arithmetic.

Except the comrades had died anyway. All but him. And now a child stood—or lay—in his house wearing his scar, speaking his god's language, arriving at his door on the exact night the wind changed, and every honest column in every ledger Felix had ever kept was suddenly, catastrophically unbalanced.

*What did I sell?*

The question had teeth. He had spent twenty years not asking it, and now it had walked up to him wearing a child's face and asked itself.

* * *

He finished the bathing because the work was in his hands and his hands needed work. Warm cloth along fevered skin, careful circles behind the ears, water combed through the tangles of Wren's hair. The child quieted under his touch the way animals did, which was its own small grief—trust given so easily to a stranger, as if trust were the only coin Wren had left.

The sigil he did not touch again. He could not have said why. It felt less like modesty than like the rule about sleeping swords: you do not handle an edge you do not understand.

While Wren slept, Felix did the thing he had sworn he would never do again. He went to the chest at the foot of his bed—the one he kept locked, the one Marta had once joked held his first wife's linens, and he had let her believe it because the truth was worse—and took out the oilcloth bundle at the bottom.

Inside was a soldier's kit, twenty years out of service. A whetstone worn to a smile. A knife with a wolf's head pommel, the garnet eyes plucked out and sold in a bad winter. And a scrap of vellum, soft as cloth now with handling, covered edge to edge in notes in his own younger handwriting: vocabulary, declensions, fragments of prayer. His season among the First Tongue speakers, preserved like a moth in amber. He had copied these pages out obsessively in the months after the passes, telling himself it was to keep the language alive, knowing even then that what he was really doing was interrogating his memory for the meaning of the words Wren—no. Not Wren. There had been no Wren then. For the meaning of the words *the child on his doorstep* had spoken.

He found the page he wanted near the bottom. He had transcribed it from a speaker named Ossa, an old woman with river-grey braids who had liked him for reasons she never explained, and who had once drawn a symbol in the ash of her cookfire while explaining the theology of debts.

The circle broken at the top. The three falling strokes. The root, or the hand, or the hook.

Ossa had called it *vess-halat*. Felix turned the vellum toward the firelight and read his own younger translation beneath it, the letters cramped and urgent: *the vessel-mark. the sign of a thing carried for another. not owned — owed. the bearer holds what is not theirs until the true claimant comes.*

Until the true claimant comes.

Felix set the vellum down very carefully, as if it might go off.

Outside, the wind had died completely. That was wrong for Vell in late autumn; the wind here was a fixture, a neighbor, something you planned your day around. Its absence pressed against the cottage like a held breath. In the new silence Felix could hear everything: the fire settling, the mouse, the small wet whistle of Wren's breathing, and—far off, at the very edge of hearing, so faint he might have made it out of his own dread—a sound like many riders keeping a slow, patient pace along a ridge.

They could not enter the valley. Whatever the valley was, whatever bargain had made it, the Court's creatures could not cross its boundary. He had staked twenty years of his life on that fact.

But waiting, he understood now, was not entering. Waiting cost the Court nothing. The Court had mountains of time.

Wren stirred and murmured, and the murmur resolved, in the First Tongue, into words Felix's traitor memory translated before he could stop it:

*He is close. The one who carries my other half. He is close, and he does not know yet what he is.*

The child's eyes did not open. The voice was Wren's voice, thin and young, but the syntax was not a child's—it was formal, archaic, the diction of the prayer-circles, of Ossa and her river-grey braids, of a tongue that predated kings and possibly predated the mountains.

Felix knelt beside the pallet. His heart was doing something he refused to name, hammering against the scar over it as if trying to break through the wall of his ribs and show him what was written on the other side.

"Wren," he said softly. The child did not wake. "Who is your other half?"

No answer. Only the fever-breath, and the fire, and the enormous listening silence of the windless night.

He stayed there a long time, kneeling on cold stone, watching the sigil rise and fall with each small breath. Guilt had come up in him the way drowned bodies come up—slowly at first, a shadow in deep water, then all at once, bloated and undeniable, breaking the surface where everyone could see. Because there was a version of the truth assembling itself in him now, and he could not look away from it: that the thing he'd sold in the snow had not been a splinter of himself at all. That the Court did not deal in fractions. That when a desperate man signs away *a portion of his soul*, the Court takes the portion and grows it, tends it, invests it the way it invests everything—

—and that somewhere in those twenty years, in the dark beneath the mountain, what he had sold had been made into a child.

His scar, on Wren's skin. His god's language, in Wren's mouth. His door, chosen out of all the doors in Vell.

*The true claimant comes.*

Felix rose on legs that felt borrowed, banked the fire, and stood a moment in the dark with his hand pressed flat over his own heart, over the matching mark, feeling it ache in time with the child's breathing like two halves of something trying to remember how to be whole.

Tomorrow, he would need answers. Tomorrow he would walk down to Marta's kitchen and ask her, plainly, what the founders of this valley had paid for its silence—and why, in twenty years, no one had ever once told him.

Because he was beginning to suspect that Vell's peace had a price too. And that prices like that were never paid only once.

On the pallet, Wren sighed, and turned, and in sleep reached one small hand out from under the blanket—reaching, blindly, toward the place where Felix stood, as if even dreaming, the child knew exactly where the other half of the mark was keeping itself.
