## 4. What the Oath Bought

The forge kept honest hours. That was the thing Felix loved most about it—the way iron refused to lie. Heat it and it softened; quench it and it hardened; strike it wrong and it cracked along lines no smith could charm away. There was no arithmetic in the anvil's song except the true one: what you gave was what you got.

He had spent twenty years teaching his hands that truth.

The morning after the child came to his door, Felix stood at the forge with a ploughshare cooling in its slack tub and watched steam rise off the water in slow grey ghosts. Wren slept upstairs still, in the loft bed he'd made for himself when he first came to Vell—narrow as a coffin, built for a man who expected to leave before he settled into it. The child had not woken once through the night, only breathed, shallow and quick, like a bird cupped in two hands.

*Prayers,* he had thought, over and over, while the snow ticked against the window. *Those were prayers.*

He knew them because he had knelt among people who spoke them. One season, long ago—a season of frozen mud and borrowed tents, of a war so far from any border anyone could name that the men stopped calling it a war and called it *the weather*. The First Tongue speakers had taken him in because he could shoe horses and hold his temper, and they had taught him their prayers the way you teach a child to whistle: casually, without imagining the habit would outlast the teaching.

He had not thought of those words in twenty years. He had made very sure not to.

Now they were sleeping in his loft, wearing a child's ribs like a stolen coat.

Felix pulled the ploughshare from the water and set it on the bench. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the other thing about ironwork, the way it demanded steadiness until steadiness stopped being a performance and became simply what you were. But beneath the steadiness, down where the scar sat over his heart—a thin white line, half a finger long, that he told the valley folk was from a horseshoe nail—he felt the old cold waking up.

It had been waiting for him to remember. It was patient that way. Everything from that part of his life was patient.

*You sold something,* said the voice he did not allow himself to hear on ordinary days. *You never asked what became of it.*

He banked the fire. He hung the sign. He went inside, climbed the ladder, and sat on the edge of the loft bed with his elbows on his knees, watching a feverish child dream in a language older than kings.

And because memory is a door that opens outward whether or not you mean to push it, he went back.

* * *

The pass at Sorrel Notch, twenty years ago. Late autumn, the year the war ate itself.

There were eleven of them left out of forty. They had names then, every one: Brennick with his laugh like a cart going downhill. The twins, Ash and Aldous, who finished each other's sentences and each other's kills. Old Sefa Marrow, who claimed she'd been a soldier since before Felix was born and dared anyone to do the math. Tammas, who was seventeen and lied about being nineteen. Corvin Halloran, Felix's brother, who carried their mother's prayer-beads wrapped around his sword-hilt and believed, with the simple ferocity of the young, that the god they'd sworn to was listening.

They had all sworn together, that company. The Oath of the Grey Wolf, taken kneeling in a shrine so small the priest had to stand outside. *Our feet for the road, our hands for the work, our souls for the watch.* A mercenary's oath, mostly theater—a way of making hired killing feel like vocation. But the god had been real enough. Felix remembered the warmth of it, the sense of something vast turning over in its sleep and noticing them, the way a mountain might notice ants. For six years the warmth held. Arrows bent. Rivers parted. Once, Felix swore afterward, a whole hillside had declined to avalanche.

Then, somewhere between one campaign and the next, the warmth went out.

No thunderclap. No omen. Just silence—one morning the prayers landed on nothing, like stones thrown down a well that had grown bottomless overnight. The priests argued. The auguries came up blank, which was worse than bad news; blank meant the god wasn't angry, wasn't testing, wasn't there. The companies that had sworn to it began to die at ordinary rates again. Arrows flew straight. Felix's brother said the silence was a trial. Sefa Marrow said the silence was an answer, and refused to say the answer to what.

Then came Sorrel Notch, and nobody had breath left for theology.

The enemy had herded them into the high pass on purpose—Felix understood this later, when understanding was useless—and closed the trap behind them with fire. Below, the drop was four hundred feet of scree and ice. Above, the ridgeline crawled with crossbows. Ahead, the pass narrowed to a throat barely wide enough for two men abreast, and behind that throat, somewhere in the gathering dark, the rest of the enemy host waited with all the patience of winter.

Eleven against a thousand. Even the god's silence seemed, in retrospect, less like abandonment than like simple mathematical agreement.

They made camp in the throat of the pass because there was nowhere else. They burned their spare bow-staves for heat. Tammas, seventeen and lying about it, asked Felix if he thought they'd see spring, and Felix—who had never learned to lie well—said *yes*, and hated himself for the shape of the word in his mouth.

Corvin sat sharpening his sword by firelight, prayer-beads clicking against the hilt, and said, quietly, "Someone should go talk to them."

"Talk to who?" Ash asked.

"The ones in the mountain." Corvin nodded toward the rock walls around them, toward whatever lived in the deep places under the world. "Every story agrees. The Hollow Court buys things. Souls, years, names, firstborn daughters, last words. They've been buying since before the kingdoms. And every story agrees they pay."

"With what?" Aldous said. "What does a dead mountain want with coin?"

"Not coin," Corvin said. "Soul-price. A portion. That's the old rate—you can sell a piece of yourself without dying. A tenth. A hand's worth." He looked at Felix across the fire, and his face in the flames was very young and very certain, the way it had been the day they took the oath. "You're the one who talks best. You're the one who read the contracts for the company."

"I read *supply ledgers*, Corvin."

"Same skill. Ink is ink." His brother smiled, and it was the smile that would follow Felix for the rest of his life, the one that said *I believe in you* as though belief were a gift rather than a debt. "Go ask them what a tenth of a soul buys these days."

Felix went.

Not because he believed. Because there was nothing else to do, and because motion was better than sitting, and because—though he would not admit this for twenty years—some starving corner of him wanted to shout into the general silence of heaven and have *anything* answer, even a thing that lived under a mountain and bought souls like a merchant buying wool.

He walked up the throat of the pass alone, past the picket fires of men who watched him go with eyes that already looked like widows'. The dark between the rock walls thickened. The cold changed—not colder, but *older*, a cold with intention in it, like a hand laid flat against the back of his neck.

At the narrowest point, where the stone leaned together overhead like conspirators, the mountain opened.

There was no door. There was simply a place where the darkness stopped being empty. And in it, something regarded him. He never managed, afterward, to describe what the Court's presence was like—except that it reminded him of a countinghouse at night: orderly, patient, lit by nothing, aware of every ledger ever opened within its walls.

*State your business,* it did not say, but he heard it anyway, the way you hear pressure change before a storm.

"My company is dying," Felix said. His voice sounded very small and very human, and he found he didn't mind. Smallness was honest. "Forty became eleven. By morning it'll be none. I'm asking what it costs to change that."

The silence considered him. Somewhere far above, a crossbow bolt struck the rocks where he'd been standing ten minutes ago, and neither he nor the dark paid it any attention.

*A tenth,* the Court said at last, in a voice like dry pages turning. *The old rate stands. A portion of the soul, freely given, freely signed. In exchange: the lives of those you name. All of them. Through this night, through this war, through all the deaths that seek them.*

"And the price is fair? No trick? No hidden clause?"

Something that was almost amusement moved through the dark, the way wind moves through a crypt.

*We are not tricksters, oathbreaker-to-be. We are accountants. Our bargains are exact. What we promise, we deliver. What we take, we keep.* A pause, precise as a knife coming down on a mark. *Do you understand what a soul-portion is, Felix Halloran?*

His name in its mouth felt like a hook going through him.

"It's a piece of what I am."

*It is a piece of what you will be.* The pages turned again. *Souls are not stones, to be cut and leave the remainder unchanged. Souls are seeds. Take a portion from a seed and you take a portion of everything it would have grown into. Every love. Every memory. Every self you have not yet met.* The dark drew closer, courteous as a clerk. *We will choose the portion. You will not know what you have lost, because losing it will be part of the loss. You will walk out of here whole-feeling and light-handed, and you will never be able to miss what is missing, because the shape of the missing will be gone too.*

Felix stood in the cold with his brother's faith burning behind him and a thousand men waiting beyond the pass, and he did the arithmetic soldiers do, the bitter kind, the kind that has no column for anything but survival.

"That's acceptable," he said.

*One condition of our own,* the Court said. *The portion must be given freely—not traded under duress, not bartered like a horse. Say: I give this willingly, knowing the cost.*

He thought of Tammas, seventeen and lying about it. Of Sefa Marrow's terrible arithmetic. Of Corvin's beads clicking like a heartbeat.

"I give this willingly," Felix said, "knowing the cost."

The mountain exhaled. Something passed through him—not pain, worse than pain, a *subtraction*—and he felt a line draw itself over his heart, cool and fine as a stylus, and then he was standing alone in an ordinary dark pass with snow beginning to fall, and he felt wonderful. Light. Unburdened. Certain, with a certainty he would mistake for grace for twenty years, that he had done the right thing.

He walked back to the fire and told them it was done.

* * *

The enemy broke at dawn.

Felix never fully learned why. The histories said plague swept their host in the night; the survivors' tales said a landslide; one mad sergeant swore the sky itself had turned against them. Eleven men walked out of Sorrel Notch past a thousand corpses that had not one wound among them, and Corvin laughed and shook Felix's shoulder and said, "You see? Even silent, He listens," and Felix let him believe it, because the truth—that he had gone shopping in the dark and come home with eleven lives bought at a tenth of a soul—was not a truth that fit inside his brother's faith.

For three days, it worked. Three days of marching down out of the mountains, three days of Tammas humming and the twins arguing about whose turn it was to carry the cookpot, three days of Sefa Marrow squinting at Felix and saying nothing, which from Sefa was louder than shouting.

On the fourth day, at a river crossing called the Grey Ford, the enemy's second host found them.

There was no bargain for that one. There was no time to climb back into any mountain. There was only a ford, and arrows, and water running red, and Felix doing the only arithmetic left: how many could he pull out of the current before the current took him too.

He pulled out three. Himself and no others.

Brennick died laughing, which was somehow worse. The twins died together, mid-sentence, each finishing the other's. Tammas died with his lie finally true—nineteen, or near enough that it counted. And Corvin died last, or nearly last, pinned under his horse in the shallows, prayer-beads snapped and scattered downstream, looking up at Felix with the water taking him inch by inch.

He didn't curse Felix. That was the unbearable part. Twenty years later it remained the unbearable part. Corvin smiled—the same smile, the believing smile—and gripped Felix's wrist with a drowning man's strength and said:

"You tried. That's more than most. Go live, little brother. Live enough for—" and the river finished the sentence.

*Live enough for what?*

Felix had spent twenty years not finishing it.

* * *

He came back to himself in the loft with the candle burned down to a thumb's width and Wren's fever breaking at last, sweat damp on the child's brow. Outside, the snow had stopped. The valley lay silent in starlight, magic-proof, godless, safe—the sanctuary he had chosen precisely because nothing holy or unholy could reach him here.

Except it had. It had knocked on his door.

Felix pressed his palm flat against his chest, against the thin white scar, and felt beneath it the old absence, smooth-edged and total, the hole shaped exactly like whatever he'd been before he signed his name in the dark. A tenth. A seed's portion. Every love, every memory, every self he had not yet met.

He had assumed, all these years, that the Court had taken from *him*.

But the Court was an accountant. Its bargains were exact. And a soul-portion was not a stone to be cut—it was a seed—and the Court grew things, everyone knew that, the Court grew things in the dark the way farmers grow winter wheat, patiently, for harvest.

Wren stirred in sleep and murmured a word in the First Tongue, and Felix recognized it now, cold settling through him like floodwater.

It was a word for *belonging*. As in: *this belongs to us*.

Down in the village, faint on the night air, a dog began to bark at something on the ridgeline.
