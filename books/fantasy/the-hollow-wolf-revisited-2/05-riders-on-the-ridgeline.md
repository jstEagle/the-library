## 5. Riders on the Ridgeline

Felix saw them first as a smudge of grey against the morning's grey, and for one merciful heartbeat he mistook them for weather.

He was at his forge before dawn, as always, working a plow-share for the Aldous farm while the valley still held its blue half-dark. That was the discipline of Vell: you rose with the hammers, you worked with the smoke, you were nothing more interesting than a man who bent iron. Twenty years he had practiced this smallness, worn it like a coat so well-fitted that sometimes he forgot there had ever been another shape beneath it.

Then Wren screamed from the loft above the stable where the child slept, and Felix dropped the tongs into the quench-trough with a hiss like a dying snake, and ran.

The boy was sitting bolt upright in the straw, eyes wide and fixed on something far past the walls of the world, and when Felix gathered him up the words came pouring out in that language older than kings — *Vael ith morath, vael ith morath* — *they are at the edge, they are at the edge* — and Felix's blood went cold in a way no winter of Vell had ever managed.

He carried the child to the window of the loft, the little north-facing window he had chosen years ago for reasons he'd told himself were only about light.

Beyond the valley's rim, where the land climbed toward the pass that everyone in Vell pretended did not exist because no one ever used it, seven riders sat their horses in a line along the ridgeline. Grey cloaks. Grey horses. They did not move. They did not need to move. They were positioned with the spacing of men who understood that watching was its own kind of weapon, and even at that distance Felix felt their attention settle over the valley like frost forming on glass.

"Hollow Court," Wren said — and the terrible thing, the thing that made Felix's hands shake on the child's shoulders, was that Wren said it in Common, plain and small, the way a child says *rain* or *supper*. "They've come to collect."

"I know," Felix said. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. "But they can't come in."

Wren turned and looked at him then, and there was something in the look that belonged to no child — an old patience, a ledger-keeper's calm. "No," the boy agreed. "They can wait."

* * *

By midmorning the whole village knew.

That was the nature of Vell. Nothing happened here; therefore everything that happened was everyone's business within the hour. Felix heard it first from Tam Broadhurst's youngest, pelting down the lane with the breathless joy of a boy delivering catastrophe: riders on the ridge, seven of them, maybe nine, they don't come down, they just sit there, Old Hewitt swears one of them hasn't blinked since sunup.

Felix kept his hands moving through the motions of his work. He banked the forge. He fed the chickens, because the chickens didn't care about bailiffs. He split wood until his shoulders burned, and all the while the village's talk moved through him like water through a net, carrying pieces he needed.

They weren't soldiers. Soldiers rode down and demanded bread and beds and answers. These sat above the tree line and let the valley do their work for them, which is to say: they let fear walk downhill on its own legs. By noon three separate people had asked Felix, casually, the way neighbors ask neighbors, whether he'd seen the riders, and whether he knew anything about them, and wasn't it strange, a stranger arriving in autumn and now this.

Felix smiled his farrier's smile — modest, dull, slightly deaf — and said he wouldn't know a bailiff if one bit him.

Marta said nothing at all.

She lived in the house adjoining his, close enough that her kitchen smoke mixed with his, and she had been his neighbor for eleven of his twenty years in Vell. She was a widow with a bad hip and a sharp tongue and a ledger-keeping mind, and she had never once asked Felix about his past, which in a village like Vell was not ignorance but courtesy, or perhaps strategy. Now she stood at the fence between their properties with her arms folded, watching the ridgeline, watching him, saying nothing, and her silence had the weight of a drawn bow.

"Marta," he said finally.

"You've got ash on your face," she said. "And that boy of yours has been asking after you in a language I don't know, and I've lived sixty-one years in this valley and I've never once heard a language I don't know." She turned her head slowly. Her eyes were pale river-stone eyes, patient as erosion. "Come inside, Felix. The wind's coming up."

There was no wind. He went inside anyway.

* * *

Her kitchen smelled of dried thyme and tallow and forty years of bread. She set a cup of cider in front of him without asking whether he wanted it, and put Wren by the fire with a heel of honey-cake, and the boy ate with the solemn concentration of someone who remembered famine, though Felix could not have said how he knew that. Then Marta sat down across from Felix, folded her hands, and looked at him the way she looked at accounts that refused to balance.

"Twenty years ago," she said, "before my time here, before most anyone's time here, the founders of Vell made a bargain."

Felix kept his face still. It was the oldest skill he had.

"This valley has no ghosts," Marta went on. "You've noticed? No hauntings, no hedge-witches, no charms that take. A woman can hang iron over her door and it does nothing but rust. Children born here have no second sight, no dreams worth telling. We're the dullest valley in the world, Felix Halloran, and we are dull on purpose."

The use of his name landed like a hammer-blow. He did not flinch. He was proud of not flinching, later, when he thought of it.

"The bargain was paid for," Marta said. "Everything true costs. And the ones we bought our dullness from—" she tipped her chin toward the ridgeline, toward the seven grey smudges beyond the glass— "they keep the books."

"How long," Felix said quietly, "have you known what I am?"

"That's the wrong question." She sipped her own cider. "I don't know what you are. I know what you aren't. You aren't a man who came to Vell for cheap land. Nobody comes to Vell for anything except to stop being somewhere else. And I know that when you arrived, twenty years back, half-dead and lying about it, old Mother Anselm — rest her — took one look at you and told me: *that one's running from something with teeth.*" Marta's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "I said, then why did you sell him the cottage? And she said, because things with teeth can't get in, girl. Because that's the whole point of the place."

Felix's throat was dry. The cider tasted of apples and of the particular sweetness of things said too late.

"And now there's a child in your house," Marta said, "who speaks the language of whoever built this valley's walls, and riders on the ridge who won't come down but won't leave either, and every soul in this village is going to start doing arithmetic. Strangers add up, Felix. That's all suspicion is — arithmetic done by frightened people."

"What are you telling me?" he asked. "That I should run?"

"I'm telling you," Marta said, with the flat clarity of a woman setting out weights on a scale, "that when the reeve comes knocking — and he will, probably tomorrow, probably with that soft apologetic face of his — you'll be needing a story. And I've decided, God help me and I don't know why, that the story is: the boy is your sister's orphan, sent to you because family is family, and the riders are toll-collectors from over the pass who've no business in Vell and know it, and the village should mind its own fields." She leaned forward. "It's a thin story. But it's mine to give, and I've got thirty years of being believed behind it, and that counts for something."

"Why?" Felix asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Why would you do that? You don't know me. Not truly. Whatever I am—"

"I know exactly whatever you are." For the first time her voice cracked, just slightly, around the edges. "Twenty years you've shod my horses and never once charged me full price and never once made me feel small about it. When my Tomas died you sat up three nights building his coffin and you wouldn't take payment and you cried at the graveside like it was your own brother in the box. Men show you who they are by what they do with their hands, Felix. Everything else — names, oaths, whatever's chasing those riders — that's paper." She stood, joints cracking, and began, decisively, to knead dough that did not need kneading. "Paper burns. Hands hold."

Felix sat in her warm kitchen with terror and gratitude tangled so tightly in his chest he couldn't tell which was which, and across the room Wren watched Marta work the dough with an expression Felix would remember for the rest of his life: the expression of someone seeing kindness performed who had been raised, somehow, somewhere, in a place where kindness was a currency too rare to waste.

* * *

The reeve came the next afternoon, exactly as predicted.

Aldous Reeve — no relation to any actual office, the title was self-appointed and the village indulged him — was a round, mild man whose talent was listening to both sides and agreeing with neither. He stood in Felix's doorway with his hat in his hands and delivered the news with genuine apology: the village council had met, such as it was, and questions had been raised, and it would ease everyone's minds if Felix could speak to the matter of the riders, and — he winced — the boy.

Felix gave him Marta's story, word for word, having rehearsed it against the anvil all morning. Sister's orphan. Toll-collectors. Mind your fields.

The reeve nodded along, relieved, nearly finished — and then paused in the doorway and said, gently, terribly:

"It's only — Widow Marta vouches for you, and that carries weight, no argument. But young Tam says the boy called out in his sleep last night, and his mother says it was the same tongue the riders' leader used when Hal Joss went up under a flag of parley yesterday. Hal says the grey fellow spoke back to him polite as you please, but the words were wrong in the mouth, like hearing a dead man talk. So the question isn't really about tolls, is it, Felix." The reeve's mild eyes were anything but mild. "The question is what the boy is, and what you're hiding, and whether whatever's sitting on our ridgeline is sitting there *for* us or *for* you."

Felix held the man's gaze. Behind the reeve, at the far end of the lane, Marta stood in her garden pretending to pull winter kale, and she gave him the smallest nod, once, like a hand on a shoulder.

"They're for me," Felix said.

It cost him everything to say it. He heard the words leave his mouth and felt twenty years of careful invisibility tear straight down the middle like wet cloth.

"But they can't come in," he went on, and here he chose truth deliberately, because lies had layers and each layer was a rope to hang from later. "This valley is closed to them — to everything like them. Your grandmothers' bargain holds. They'll sit up there and starve their horses before they cross your boundary, because they can't. All they can do is wait, and make you afraid, and hope you'll hand me over to make the waiting stop." He let that stand in the cold air between them. "So the question for your council is simple. What kind of village hands over a neighbor?"

The reeve was quiet a long moment. Somewhere downslope a dog barked at nothing; in Vell, dogs barked at nothing, because there was very little something to bark at.

"Well," he said at last, putting his hat back on. "That's more honesty than I expected, and I thank you for it. Council meets again Thursday. I'd not pack my bags yet, if I were you. But I'd not unpack, neither." He hesitated, then added, softer, almost kindly: "For what it's worth, Felix — nobody in this village believes you'd hurt that boy. Whatever else folk are wondering. That much shows."

He trundled off down the lane. Marta abandoned her pretense of gardening and walked up to stand beside Felix in the doorway. Together they watched the reeve go, and beyond him the ridgeline, where seven grey shapes had not moved since morning and would not move tonight.

"He's right, you know," Marta said. "About the showing. Anyone with eyes can see how you are with that child."

"Can they see how I am *because* of that child?" Felix asked. It slipped out before he could stop it, and he heard what it confessed: that the tenderness was new, that it terrified him, that he had spent two decades learning to love nothing that could be taken.

Marta looked up at him, and her river-stone eyes held something he hadn't seen in them before — not suspicion, not even pity, but a kind of fierce, unsentimental resolve, the look of a woman deciding which side of an account she stood on.

"Felix," she said, "those riders haven't blinked since sunup three days ago. I've been watching them through my spyglass. And this morning, one of them raised his hand — just lifted it, slow, like a man greeting someone he knows — and pointed it at *your chimney smoke.*"

The forge-fire behind Felix crackled. In the loft above, Wren began, very softly, to sing — a thin thread of the First Tongue winding down through the floorboards, a prayer or a warning, and Felix could not tell which, and the grey riders on the ridgeline, all seven at once, turned their heads toward the sound like compass needles finding north.
