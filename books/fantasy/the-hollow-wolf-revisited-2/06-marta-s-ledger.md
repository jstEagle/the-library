## 6. Marta's Ledger

The ledger lived in a box under Marta's bed, and the box was iron, and the iron had gone cold in a way that iron should not.

She had known this for forty years, since the night her mother pressed it into her hands with fingers that shook like winter branches and said, *Now it is yours, and I am sorry, and do not open it more than once a year.* Her mother had died before the first snow of that season, quietly, in her sleep, with her face turned toward the window as if she expected someone to come knocking. No one came. That was the whole point of Vell. No one came.

Marta kept the box under the bed because under the bed was where her mother had kept it, and her grandmother before her, and so on back through women whose names had worn smooth as river stones from being spoken only in whispers. The box did not rust. She had checked, once, out of some stubborn housewifely instinct—run her thumb along its seam expecting orange flake and grit—and found instead a surface like frozen water, cold enough to ache, unmarked by any year. Whatever had been done to that iron, it had not been done by human hands.

Tonight she would take it out. Tonight she had to.

* * *

It began, as these things always began, with bread.

Marta baked on Thursdays, had baked on Thursdays since she was nine years old and tall enough to work the peel, and the whole lane knew the smell of her loaves the way they knew the smell of rain. But this Thursday the dough had refused her. She had set it to rise by the hearth the way her mother taught her, wrapped in linen, tucked against the warm stone, and when she came back an hour later it sat in its bowl pale and sullen and flat as a coin left in a dead man's pocket. She remade it. It refused again. On the third attempt she caught herself whispering to it—*come on, love, rise, there's a good thing*—and stopped, because talking to bread was something her mother had done only in the last winter of her life, when the fear had gotten into her the way damp gets into a wall.

The third batch rose. Of course it rose. But it rose wrong—too fast, too eager, splitting its cloth like something hatching—and when Marta pulled the loaves from the oven their crusts had blistered into patterns, and the patterns were not random. She stood over them with flour to her elbows and understood, with the particular dread of a woman who has spent her whole life pretending not to understand things, that her bread had just told her something.

The marks looked like eyes. Or doors. Or both—eyes that were doors, doors that were watching.

She cut one loaf open and the crumb inside was streaked grey, like ash worked through it, though her flour was white as ever flour was. She fed that loaf to the pigs. The pigs ate it. Then they stood at the fence line all evening facing north, toward the mountain, silent, in a row, like mourners.

That was three days ago. Since then: the well water tasting faintly of iron no matter how long she let the bucket stand; her hens laying eggs with shells thin as parchment, veined blue underneath like bruises; and last night—the worst of it—a sound from the direction of the ridge, very far off, very faint. Not a horn. Not a wolf. Something between: a long sustained note like a wet finger run round the rim of a glass, except the glass was the sky itself, and it went on until her teeth ached, and then it stopped, and every dog in the village began to howl at once and then, all together, mid-howl, shut up.

Marta had sat in the dark after that with her hands folded in her lap and thought: *it is time to read the ledger.*

* * *

She waited until full dark, because the ledger was not to be read in daylight—that much instruction had come down with it, along with the once-a-year rule and the warning about iron and the final, strangest instruction of all, which was that the reader must be a woman who had buried someone she loved. Her mother had never explained that one. Marta had qualified at twenty-six, when she buried Tomas, her husband of four years, taken by the fever that came down off the mountain passes one wet spring, and she remembered that in the weeks after his funeral the box under her bed had grown warmer. Not hot. Warmer. Like a body lying near another body. As if the ledger were interested in grief, the way flies are interested in sugar.

She knelt on the floorboards beside the bed, dragged the box out into the light of a single tallow candle, and broke the seal of wax her own thumbprint had made a year ago.

Inside, on a bed of dried yarrow gone brown as old blood, lay the ledger.

It was not paper. Paper would have been a mercy. It was hide of some kind—not cow, not goat, nothing she could name—thin and supple and faintly luminous, the color of moonlight through milk, and it was stitched at the spine with wire that was not wire, and the pages bore writing that was not ink. The script moved. Slowly, the way frost moves across a window, but it moved, rearranging itself each year into new columns, new sums, new entries in a hand that was elegant and patient and utterly without warmth. Forty years of reading it had taught Marta the truth she least wanted: the ledger wrote itself. The women of her line were not its keepers. They were its readers. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

She opened to the first page, as she did every year, because the first page was the foundation, and a woman who forgets her foundations builds her house over a hollow.

*In the two-hundredth year of the Long Silence,* the opening entry ran, in that moving silver script, *the valley of Vell was purchased.*

Purchased. Not founded. Not settled. Purchased, the way one purchases a bolt of cloth or a cask of wine or, the ledger did not say but Marta's spine said for it, a soul.

*Let it be recorded,* the entry continued, *that the founders of Vell, being folk who had seen what magic makes of the world—scoured fields, salted wells, lords who burned with cold fire and armies that walked out of dreams—did gather their goods and their courage and go seeking the one power that trades in protection rather than ruin. They found it beneath the mountain, in the Court that has no reflection. And the Court heard their plea, and considered, and named its price.*

Marta knew the next lines by heart. She had read them forty times, kneeling on these same boards, by this same kind of candle, and every year they landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well.

*The price was souls. Not one soul, nor ten, but a tithe: one soul in every generation, freely given, born within the valley's bounds, marked at conception and delivered at maturity. In exchange, the Court drew its circle. Within it, magic cannot enter. Prayer goes unanswered. Blessing and curse alike break upon the valley's edge like weather upon stone. The gods themselves, great and small, turn their faces away. Vell shall be quiet. Vell shall be forgotten. Vell shall be safe.*

*Safe*, Marta thought, staring at the word until it lost its shape. Yes. That was the bargain her whole life had been built inside, the way a fish's life is built inside water. Why there were no shrines in Vell, not even little ones, not even the chipped roadside kind every other village in the world seemed to sprout like mushrooms. Why no midwife in living memory had ever whispered a charm over a difficult birth, though every midwife beyond the hills did it without thinking. Why the valley's children grew up thinking the rest of the world's stories about wizards and wonders were just stories, the way children everywhere think the things they lack are simply the shape of the world.

And why, when strangers came over the pass, they always paused at the valley mouth with a puzzled look, like men who have walked into a room and forgotten why. The valley forgot them back. That was part of the purchase too. *Vell shall be forgotten.* The Court had bought them their invisibility with the same coin it took their children.

Except the coin was never paid all at once. That was the horror of it, the thing Marta had understood slowly across forty years of annual readings, the understanding arriving the way rot arrives in a beam—from the inside, invisible, until one day the floor gives way. The tithe was not due every generation. It was due when the Court called it due. Some generations passed untouched, sixty years, eighty, the ledger's pages filling with entries marked *DEFERRED* in that patient silver hand, and the people of Vell forgot there was anything to defer, and the valley's quiet felt like peace instead of payment held in escrow. And then, without warning, an entry would appear in fresh script, still glistening somehow, still wet:

*CALLED.*

* * *

Marta turned the pages with hands that had begun, somewhere in the last minute, to tremble.

The historical entries were the ones she knew. 200th year: the founding tithe, a girl-child named Ilse, *delivered*. 263rd: a boy, name struck through, *delivered*. 341st: twins, which the ledger noted with something almost like relish, *both delivered, surplus credited*. She had wept the year she first truly understood those entries—she'd been young, newly widowed, raw enough to feel every death in that silver script—and she had never fully stopped weeping since, only learned to do it silently, efficiently, the way Vell did everything.

But it was the recent pages that mattered tonight. She turned past her mother's era, past her grandmother's, past the long stretch of DEFERRED that had lulled three generations into forgetting, and found the entry that had appeared eleven months ago, the one she had read by candlelight with her heart hammering like a fist on a door:

*A vessel has been conceived within the bounds. The debt of the Long Silence falls due. Let the vessel be nourished and let it be delivered at the turning of the year, when the sun stands lowest and the bell rings beneath the mountain. Upon delivery, the account of Vell is settled for another age.*

Eleven months ago. Marta had read it and told herself, desperately, that it might be anyone, any child conceived anywhere in the valley that season, and that perhaps the Court's arithmetic was generous, and that perhaps—

And then, six days ago, Halloran's boy—Halloran the farrier, the quiet one, the one who mended her plow-chain and never asked questions and whose silences had always struck her as too practiced, too maintained, like a man holding a door shut from the inside—had come to her at dusk with a half-frozen child wrapped in his coat, and asked her, in a voice carefully stripped of everything, whether she knew anything about foundlings.

She had said no. She had lied to him with the ease of forty years' practice, and he had believed her, or pretended to, and taken the child home, and Marta had lain awake all night listening to the wind come off the ridge and knowing.

The child was the tithe. The child was the payment. The child was *Wren*, though Marta hadn't known the name yet then—she knew it now, because names traveled through a village faster than fire—and the ledger had known it first. She turned to the newest page now, the page that had written itself sometime in the last three days, while her bread refused to rise and her pigs faced north and the sky rang like a struck glass.

The entry was short. The shortest in the book.

*The vessel has reached the boundary. The mark is manifest. Collection approaches. Let none of Vell interfere. Interference voids the sanctuary.*

Marta read it twice. Three times. *Interference voids the sanctuary.* Two hundred years of safety, hers and her neighbors', her hens and her bread and her neighbors' children asleep behind their doors—all of it hanging on the delivery of one child to a court beneath the mountain, and any hand raised to stop it would tear the whole bargain down like a thread pulled from a hem.

She closed the ledger. The script was already shifting on the cover, rearranging itself, patient as frost.

* * *

She sat on the floor for a long while afterward, the iron box cold against her knee, the candle burning down toward its puddle.

She thought about the mothers. That was where her mind always went, every year, the place the ledger could not touch: the mothers of Ilse and the nameless boy and the twins, the women of her line who had read these same pages and then walked out into their villages and said nothing, baked their bread, mended their fences, watched the chosen children grow up among them laughing and knowing. Had they told the fathers? Had they told anyone? Or had they carried it alone, generation after generation, the way she carried it, this lead weight sewn into the lining of an ordinary life?

Her mother had carried it alone. Marta understood that now in a way she hadn't at nineteen, when the box changed hands. Her mother had known, for years probably, that the debt was falling due again—had watched the young women of the village with that appraising sorrow Marta had mistaken for mere melancholy—and had said nothing to anyone, and died with her face to the window.

And now there was Halloran. Quiet Halloran, who had appeared in Vell fifteen years ago with a farrier's hammer and a soldier's shoulders and a story about wanting quiet work, and whom the valley had forgotten the way it forgot everyone, and whom Marta had privately, over the years, filed away as a man running from something. A man running from something does not settle in the one place in the world where nothing can follow. Unless, she thought now, with the cold sliding up her spine, unless he wasn't hiding from what followed him. Unless he was hiding from what he'd *done*.

He had a scar over his heart. She'd seen it once, the summer he'd torn his shirt on her fence mending it, a white ridged thing like a seal burned into the skin. She had thought: soldier. Now she thought: *mark*. And the ledger spoke of vessels bearing marks, and the child on his doorstep had arrived at *his* door, of all the doors in Vell, half-dead, in the exact season the collection fell due.

None of which was coincidence. Marta had lived too long in a purchased valley to believe in coincidence anymore. Coincidence was just pattern you hadn't paid for yet.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, the ledger's script gave off its faint light, silver as foxfire, seeping around the edges of the iron lid like breath around a clenched jaw. She slid the box back under the bed, and lay down on top of the blankets fully dressed, and stared at the ceiling beams, and did not sleep.

Toward dawn she made her decision, and it was not the decision her mother would have made, or her grandmother, or any of the smooth-named whispering women back down the line of her blood. They had read the ledger and kept it. She had read the ledger, and read the newest entry, and read the man and the child at the end of the lane, and she was eighty-one years old and had buried everyone she loved and owed the future exactly one act of courage.

*Interference voids the sanctuary*, the Court had written.

Well. Marta Halloran's neighbor had been a liar for forty years, and she was tired, and the morning was bright, and she had a fresh loaf—risen properly at last, plain-crusted, ordinary, safe to eat—and she was going to carry it down the lane to the farrier's forge, and knock on his door, and tell him everything.

She was halfway down the path, bread warm through the cloth, when she saw him standing at his gate, looking north toward the ridge, and saw what he was looking at: five riders in grey, motionless on the skyline, patient as posts, waiting at the exact line where the valley's quiet began.

They could not enter. The ledger had promised her that much, and the ledger did not lie; it only collected.

But they could wait. And as Marta stood clutching her bread on the frozen path, she saw Felix Halloran see them—and saw him go, all at once, absolutely still, the stillness of a man hearing a debt collector speak his name.

The child was awake, then. The mark was showing. And whatever Felix Halloran had sold beneath the mountain twenty years ago, it had finally, at the winter's turning, come to be counted.
