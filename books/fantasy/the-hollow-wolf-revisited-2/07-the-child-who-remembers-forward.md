## 7. The Child Who Remembers Forward

Wren knew the smell of the farrier's house before ever smelling it.

That was the trouble with remembering forward. Everything arrived twice—once as a knowing, soft and gray like ash settling on snow, and once as the thing itself, loud and warm and real. The bread Marta would bake three days from now already sat in Wren's chest, heavy and honeyed. The sound of Felix's hammer on iron had been ringing in Wren's ears since before the mountain, since before the cold road, since before—well. Since before was a country Wren could not find on any map, though Wren owned every map that mattered.

The maps were the problem. The maps were the gift. The maps were the debt.

Wren lay in the narrow bed beneath the eaves of the farrier's loft, watching dust turn in a blade of morning light, and did what Wren always did in the quiet hours: opened the doors inside and let the other life breathe through.

* * *

The vaults first. They always came first, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Bone-white corridors, tall as cathedrals and hushed as tombs. Rows upon rows of cases made of glass that was not glass—Wren knew its true name, a word in the First Tongue that meant *the skin of held things*—and inside each case, a light. Not firelight, not starlight. Soul-light, the color of whatever the soul had loved most. Some burned amber like harvest kitchens. Some burned blue as a river in winter. One, far down the eastern row, flickered the exact shade of a woman's laughter, though Wren had never heard the woman and knew her name anyway: Ilsabet, who sold her soul to buy her son a fever-free childhood, and whose light guttered now because the son had died anyway, fevers being poor respecters of bargains.

Wren knew all of them. That was the cruel architecture of it. The Court had grown Wren in the dark of the mountain for twenty years—not born so much as *assembled*, stitched from the portion of soul that once belonged to someone else, fed on the memories of the vaults until the vaults became more familiar than any mother's face. Wren was raised by lanterns. Wren's lullabies were the murmuring of ten thousand sold lives, each one whispering its story through the glass, and a small child-shaped vessel walking the rows on small bare feet, learning each light by heart.

That was the purpose. A vessel must know what it carries. A vessel must *long* for it, so that when the time came, the soul would not need to be forced home. It would come willingly, the way water comes to a low place.

And oh, Wren longed. Even here, even in the farrier's warm loft with woodsmoke in the rafters and a real bed under a real blanket, some hollow chamber behind Wren's sternum ached toward the mountain the way an empty socket aches toward a lost tooth. *Come home,* the ache said. *You are only half of yourself. The bright half is waiting under glass.*

Wren pressed a palm flat against the sternum, over the mark that burned there like a coal wrapped in silk, and whispered the word that meant *no* in the First Tongue.

The ache did not listen. It never listened. But whispering helped, a little, the way singing helps a child in the dark—not because the dark cares, but because the voice proves the singer still exists.

* * *

Downstairs, the hammer rang against iron. Ring, pause, ring. Felix.

Wren smiled without meaning to. There were things Wren knew about Felix Halloran that Felix Halloran did not know Wren knew, and the list was long enough to fill a book, and every page of it was a weight.

Wren knew his name before anyone spoke it—had known it in the vaults, where his file was kept in a drawer of black oak near the western stair, thinner than most, because he had paid in futures rather than pasts. Wren knew the taste of the oath he'd sworn to a god who no longer answered: iron and pine smoke and the specific salt of tears frozen on a beard in high passes. Wren knew that when Felix slept, he slept on his left side with his back to the door, which soldiers do, and that he woke at every sound, which broken men do, and that between sleeping and waking there was a moment—Wren had watched it from the stairs these past two nights—when his face went young and unguarded and he said a name into the pillow. *Bram.* Wren knew who Bram had been. Bram had died in the pass at Greywater with Felix's oath-ring clenched in his fist, and the ring had gone back to Felix anyway, because oaths are stubborn as burrs.

Wren knew, too, the thing Felix could not yet say aloud even to himself: that the bargain in the mountain pass had not bought what he thought it bought. That arithmetic was wrong. That the payment had been misdirected—or rather, *directed exactly as intended*, by parties who intended everything—and that the truth of it waited below the mountain like a trapdoor under a rug.

Knowing that felt like holding a hot stone. Wren kept having to set it down and pick it up again. If Wren told him, the trapdoor would open too soon and everyone would fall through at the wrong time, and time, Wren had learned, was the one material the Court respected. Things had to happen in their season. Debts had to ripen. A soul harvested early was thin wine; a soul harvested at solstice, at the sealing, at the turning of the year's great lock—that was the vintage the Queen wanted, and the Queen always got her vintage, except.

Except Wren remembered forward, and in the forward-remembering there was a night in a vault where something went wrong for the Queen. Wren had seen it the way one sees lightning before the thunder: a flash of a shattered case, of light escaping upward like embers off a bonfire, of old shades spending themselves like coins flung into a wishing well. Wren did not know if the vision was a promise or merely a possibility. Remembering forward was like that—every future arrived with its siblings, all of them claiming to be the true one, and choosing among them was like choosing which echo is the voice.

But in nearly every version, Felix stood in the vault. And in nearly every version, Felix wept. And in one version—the one Wren loved best and trusted least—Felix walked out of the mountain lighter than he had entered, hollowed like a gourd, humming a tune he no longer knew the words to.

Wren wanted that version. Wren was afraid of that version. Both feelings lived in the same small chest, side by side like mice in a wall.

* * *

Marta came up the ladder at midmorning with porridge and that look she wore, the one folded shut like a letter. She set the bowl down and studied Wren with eyes that had counted a great many things in this valley and forgotten none of them.

"You slept," she said. "Truly slept?"

"Truly." This was not entirely false. Wren slept; the other life simply came along, the way a dog follows a child everywhere, even to bed.

"No dreams?"

Marta asked it carefully, the way you ask a wound if it hurts. Wren understood why. Marta's ledger—Wren had seen it in the forward-memory, the fat book bound in grey leather hidden under her floorboard third from the hearth, the one with names going back two hundred years, each generation's payment written in ink that changed color as the debt came due—Marta's ledger had a page reserved for this winter, and the page was blank, and blank pages frightened keepers of ledgers more than full ones.

"I dreamed of lights," Wren said, because it was true and also safe. "Rows and rows of them."

Something crossed Marta's face like a cloud crossing a field. She smoothed it away, but Wren saw the shape underneath: *she knows*. Of course she knew. Every keeper of Vell had known, in their season, what the valley rested on. That was the price of keeping. You inherited the knowledge along with the ledger, and you carried it the way farmers carry weather-knowledge, silently, in the bones.

"Eat," Marta said gruffly. "Growing children need feeding."

"I am not growing," Wren said, and then, because the other life slipped its leash sometimes, added: "I am being finished."

Marta went very still. The porridge steamed between them. Somewhere below, the hammer rang—ring, pause, ring—and the sound seemed to reach up through the floorboards and hold the room together.

"That's a strange thing to say."

"It's a strange thing to be." Wren picked up the spoon and ate, obediently, because obedience was easier than explanation, and explanation was impossible anyway. How to tell a woman who kept a ledger of souls that the bowl in front of her held the current entry? How to say: *I am the interest on your valley's loan. I am the coupon torn off at the end of two hundred years. I am what the sanctuary costs, and I have known my own price since before I had a name to sign it with.*

Wren ate. Marta watched. Outside, the light lay white and thin on the frost, and far off—at the very edge of hearing, or perhaps only at the edge of the forward-memory—a bell tolled once, deep under the earth, counting down days neither of them mentioned.

* * *

Afternoon. Felix let Wren come down to the forge.

This was new. The first two days he had kept Wren upstairs like contraband, but something had shifted in him overnight—Wren had seen it happen in the dark, had watched from the stair as Felix sat alone at his table turning a horseshoe nail over and over, and had heard him say, to no one, in the First Tongue no less, a single word: *why?* The god had not answered. The god had not answered for twenty years. But asking had loosened something, and by morning Felix had decided—Wren could see the decision sitting on him like a coat—that hiding the child in a loft was no kind of keeping, and that if the world was coming for them both anyway, they might as well stand in the same room.

So Wren sat on a stool by the quenching trough and watched Felix work, and it was the best afternoon Wren had ever had in either life.

There was a rightness to it that Wren could not explain and did not try to. The heat of the coals on one cheek, the cold breath of the trough on the other. The ring of iron, which was not unlike the ringing of the vault-bells, except that forge-sounds made things and vault-sounds unmade them. Felix's hands, scarred and sure, moving with the particular grace of men who have made peace with their tools if not with themselves. Once, a spark leapt free and landed on Wren's wrist, and Felix swore and lunged across the shop to brush it away, cradling the tiny burn like a wound of war, and Wren looked up at his stricken face and thought: *in eleven of the possible futures, you die for me. In four of them, I die for you. In one, we both walk out of the mountain hollowed and free, and in that one, this is how it starts—you burning your hands on small salvations until you learn to make the large one.*

"What?" Felix said, because Wren was staring.

"Nothing," Wren said. "Your fire is good today."

Felix snorted, but Wren saw the corner of his mouth move. He went back to the shoe he was shaping, and after a while, quietly, almost to himself, he began to hum. An old marching song. A company-song. Wren knew every verse, including the ones Felix had forgotten, including the last verse, which was not a verse at all but a list of the dead, sung soft, and Wren's chest ached with the knowing of it.

"Felix," Wren said.

He glanced up, hammer paused. It was the first time Wren had used his name in his hearing. Wren watched it land—watched the small confusion, then the larger one, the dawning arithmetic of *I never told you my name*—and Wren hurried to spend the moment before it curdled into fear.

"Marta said it," Wren lied smoothly. Lying, Wren had discovered, was a skill the vessel had been built with, the way ducks are built with oil. "She talks about you."

"She complains about me," Felix said, but the tension went out of his shoulders, and he returned to his work, and Wren exhaled.

It was getting harder to hide the knowing. That was the truth of it, the weight of it, the unbearable part: the other life pressed outward constantly now, like spring sap under bark, like water seeking cracks. Each day the memories of the vaults grew louder, and the forward-rememberings came faster, and the First Tongue rose in Wren's throat at odd moments—in sleep, mostly, waking Felix twice now with prayers in a dead language pouring out of a child's mouth—and Wren could feel the seams of the disguise splitting. Soon the valley would hear. Soon the riders on the ridgeline would hear. Soon the mountain itself would hear, and stir, and send its collectors down the white corridors to fetch what was owed.

Wren did not want to go back. That was the simplest sentence in Wren's possession and the hardest to hold. Everyone assumed the vessel would want to return—had been *built* to want to return, and the wanting was there, honest and awful, the socket-ache, the homesickness for a self never lived—but underneath the building, underneath the making, there was a small stubborn remainder that had woken on the mountain road in the snow and chosen the lit window instead of the dark corridor. Chosen the forge-smell over the glass-smell. Chosen the man with the hammer.

The Court had made Wren to be a door. Wren intended, with all the small ferocity available to a seven-year-old body carrying twenty years of someone else's soul, to be a wall.

* * *

Evening. Felix banked the forge and carried Wren up to the loft, because the child had fallen asleep on the stool with a half-eaten apple in hand, and he set Wren down with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him.

In the borderland between sleep and waking, the doors inside opened all the way, and Wren fell forward into tomorrow.

The riders first—grey cloaks at the valley's edge, patient as frost, nine of them though the village would count eight and argue about the ninth. Then Marta's ledger open on her table, her finger on the blank page, her mouth a hard line. Then the boundary stones, and beyond them the world where magic ran like blood in veins, and Felix stepping across that line with his oath flaring visible around him like a wound made of light, and every hungry thing in the dark country turning its head.

Then the ford, and the hounds, and Wren's own mouth opening to speak words that would freeze leaping beasts mid-air, and the cost of it, the terrible cost, the light going out of Wren by whole rooms—

Then the tower, and the ghosts, and Felix on his knees before the angry dead saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry* in the First Tongue, which was, Wren realized with a jolt, the only language in which his apology had ever been true—

Then the gates, three of them, each one a mouth—

Then the vault, and the case, and the light that was Wren's own bright half burning amber-gold behind the skin-of-held-things, and Felix's hands, scarred and sure, coming down upon it like a hammer upon a shackle—

Then the bell. The solstice bell. Ringing and ringing and ringing, and in the ringing, a silence deeper than the Court had ever contained, and in the silence, a voice—not thunder, never thunder, just one word, small as a seed, spoken by a god who had been silent for twenty years, and the word was—

Wren woke.

The loft was dark. Snow ticked softly against the shutter. Below, Felix slept on his left side with his back to the door, and somewhere in his dreams he was saying *Bram*, and somewhere above the world the silent god was keeping its own counsel, and somewhere beneath the mountain a case of not-glass held half of Wren burning patiently, the way lamps burn for sailors, the way windows burn for the lost.

One word. The god's word. Wren had heard it in the forward-memory and already it was slipping, the way dreams slip, the way names slip. Wren reached for it in the dark and caught only its shape: short, and old, and gentle the way a knife is gentle when it cuts a knot rather than the throat.

Wren lay still, heart drumming, and did the arithmetic of the days remaining. Seventeen. Eighteen, if the snow delayed the riders, but the snow would not delay them; they were patient as frost, and frost is never late.

Seventeen days to learn how to tell a man the truth that would break him open: that the bargain he had knelt in the snow to make had never been his to make, that the soul he thought he'd spent was standing right beside him eating apples, that everything he'd believed about his own sin was a story the Court had written and let him carry like a stone in his belly for twenty years.

Seventeen days. And the first step was tomorrow, and the first step was the hardest, and Wren practiced it now in the dark, in a whisper, in the First Tongue because the truth sounded kinder there:

*I know your name, Felix Halloran. I have always known your name. I was made from the price of it.*

Below, the hammer hung silent on its peg. Above, the snow went on ticking at the shutter like a slow clock. And far away, under the roots of the mountain, in a cathedral of bone-white glass, a light that was half of a child leaned toward the surface of its case and began, for the first time in twenty years, to flicker—because somewhere in the world above, its other half had just made a decision.

Wren felt it happen. Felt the pull reverse. Felt the bright half stop calling *come home* and start, very faintly, very impossibly, listening.

The vessel sat up in the dark, eyes wide, and whispered the only prayer it had ever made that was wholly its own:

*Wait for me. I'm coming to break you out.*
