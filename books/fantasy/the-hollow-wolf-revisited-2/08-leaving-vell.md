## 8. Leaving Vell

Felix packed the way he had once packed for retreats: fast, silent, and with the particular grief of a man choosing what to abandon.

The farrier's tools stayed. That hurt more than he expected it to. Twenty years of calluses lived in those handles—the hoof knife worn to the shape of his thumb, the rasp with Marta's initials burned into the grip the winter she'd decided he was staying whether he liked it or not. He wrapped Wren in his thickest cloak instead, and in the space where the tools would have gone, he laid a hammer, a whetstone, and the short sword he'd buried under the hearthstone the first night he'd arrived in Vell and told himself *never again*.

The sword came out of its oil-cloth wrapping looking like something that had been sleeping. Which, Felix supposed, they both had.

Wren stood in the doorway watching him, small hands buried in the cloak's folds, breath fogging in the pre-dawn cold. The fever had broken two days ago, but the child still had the translucent look of someone who had been somewhere else recently and hadn't entirely come back.

"You're frightened," Wren said, in the First Tongue, the words falling into the room like stones into a well.

"I'm being careful," Felix said in the same tongue. It felt obscene, using it for small talk. The language was built for prayer and oath-making; every sentence came out sounding like a vow.

"Those are different words for the same thing." Wren tilted their head. "In the old speech, anyway. *Frightened* is just *careful* pointed at yourself."

Felix stopped packing. He looked at the child—this strange, borrowed vessel with another soul folded inside it like a letter—and felt the vertigo again, the one that had been living in his chest since the night of the knocking. "Who taught you that?"

"The mountain," Wren said simply, as if that were an answer, and maybe in the First Tongue it was.

Outside, Vell lay grey and quiet under the last of the dark. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, straight and slow in the windless cold. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. The valley looked exactly as it had looked every morning for twenty years, and Felix understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade between the ribs, that he had never once believed it would last. He had only gotten very good at not checking.

He checked now. He lifted the hem of his shirt and looked at the scar over his heart—the pale, branching mark like frost on a windowpane, the twin of the sigil on Wren's back. In Vell it was just a scar. Had always been just a scar. He had spent two decades telling himself that was all it had ever been.

Tonight, for the first time since he'd crossed the boundary stone, the scar itched.

"It knows we're leaving," Wren said quietly. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And when we cross over—"

"Then everything I buried comes back up out of the ground," Felix said. He buckled the sword belt on, and his hands did not shake, because twenty years ago they had learned not to, and some lessons outlive the men who paid for them. "And everything hunting us can see me."

Wren considered this with the terrible calm of children discussing weather. "Good," the child said. "I want them to see you. I want them to see you coming."

Felix almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was something rawer. "Wren. When we leave this valley, I stop being a farrier. Do you understand? Whatever I was before—I'll be it again. And the things that were waiting for me to be it again have had twenty years to get hungry."

The child reached up and took his hand. Small fingers, chapped from the cold, gripping with surprising strength.

"You held my hand through the fever," Wren said. "You didn't let go even when I bit you. So." A shrug, vast as a mountain range. "I'm holding yours now. Same rule."

*Same rule.* Felix swallowed around the knot in his throat and led the child out into the dark.

* * *

They went on foot, down the lane past Marta's house, where a single candle burned in the front window though the hour was long before dawn. Felix had meant to pass without stopping. He failed.

Marta opened the door before he knocked. She was dressed, her grey hair braided tight, a bundle in her arms that she pushed against his chest without ceremony: bread, hard cheese, a flask that sloshed with something stronger than water, and a pair of good boots that were, unmistakably, not his size but close enough.

"You knew," Felix said.

"I've known for twenty years, you great fool." Her voice was low and fierce. Behind her, in the dark of the house, her grandchildren slept. "Nobody hides this well unless they're hiding from something with patience. I baked the bread yesterday because I figured today or tomorrow. The boots are my late husband's. He'd have wanted them running toward something."

"Marta—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't thank me, don't explain, don't make it a scene. You take the child"—and here her voice caught, just slightly, on the word *child*, because Marta had held Wren too, had fed Wren stew and taught Wren which fence posts the cats slept on—"and you go do whatever it is people like you have to do. And then—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "And then either come back, or don't, but don't come back halfway."

Felix took her hand. It was work-rough and warm. "If I could explain what you've been to me—"

"You'd do it badly and cry, and neither of us has time." She squeezed his fingers once, hard, and let go. Then she crouched to Wren's level, and all the iron went out of her face, and what was left underneath was so tender it hurt to look at. "You eat the bread slowly. You keep your hood up. And you bring him home, do you hear me? Him too. He pretends he doesn't need anyone, but he's the worst liar in this valley, and I have met every liar in this valley."

"I hear you," Wren said solemnly, in accented but comprehensible Velltongue, and Marta laughed wetly and kissed the top of the child's head, and then stood, and became iron again, and shut the door before anyone could say anything unforgivable.

The candle in her window stayed lit until they were out of sight of it.

* * *

The boundary of Vell was not marked by anything so honest as a wall. It was marked by a stone—an old boundary-marker, moss-eaten, leaning at the angle of a tired man—and by the way the birds sounded different on one side of it than the other, though no one in the village had ever remarked on this, because noticing things like that was how you ended up leaving.

Felix stopped ten paces short of the stone.

The riders were visible on the ridgeline above the valley's eastern rim: six grey shapes, motionless as standing stones, exactly where they had been for three days. Bailiffs of the Hollow Court. They could not enter. The valley's founding bargain kept them out the way a locked door keeps out rain. But they could wait, and they were clearly built for it, and as Felix watched, one of them turned its head—not quickly, not with any human urgency, but with the slow, absolute attention of a thing that has never once needed to hurry.

"They see us," Wren whispered.

"They've always seen us. They just couldn't touch." Felix rolled his shoulders. His heart was going hard—not fear, or not only fear. Underneath the fear was something he hadn't felt in twenty years, and recognizing it was like finding an old wound had started itching before a storm. Anticipation. His body remembered what his mind had tried to forget: that he had once been very good at exactly this, the moment before the door breaks.

"Wren. When we cross, hold onto me and don't let go. No matter what you feel. No matter what *I* look like."

"What will you look like?"

Felix looked at the scar over his heart. "Like something that made a promise," he said. "A long time ago. To something enormous."

He took the child's hand, and walked forward, and stepped over the line the moss-eaten stone had guarded for longer than anyone in Vell could remember.

Magic hit him like cold water.

That was the only way to describe it, and Felix had spent twenty years failing to describe it even to himself. It didn't pour in so much as *remember* him—a flood of everything the valley had walled out, rushing into every dry channel of him at once. The world snapped into focus the way a landscape snaps into focus when clouds leave the sun. He could suddenly hear the river thinking. He could smell the age of the mountains. Every tree on the slope turned slightly, in some sense that had nothing to do with branches, to look at him.

And the scar woke.

Heat bloomed over his heart—not pain, worse than pain, *recognition*. The pale branching mark filled with light the color of old ice, and Felix felt his oath reassert itself through every inch of him, the ancient words he'd spoken kneeling in the snow of another lifetime rising up through his bones and writing themselves across him in letters anything supernatural could read. He felt himself become *visible*—not to eyes, but to whatever passed for eyes among the things of the world's deep places. A beacon. A signature. A debt walking upright.

He staggered. Wren's grip held him up.

"Felix," the child said, and it wasn't worry in the small voice. It was awe. "You're *bright*."

"That," Felix managed, through teeth clenched against the flood, "is the problem."

Because he could feel it now—feel the oath the way he hadn't since the day he'd traded pieces of himself away in a mountain pass. It was whole again, or whole enough, and it was *loud*, and it was pointing. Like a compass needle made of guilt. Every power in a hundred miles could follow it. Everything that had ever wanted the Wolf Who Broke His Pack now knew precisely which shadow on which road was his.

On the ridgeline, the six grey riders moved.

There was no sound. That was the worst part. Six figures that had stood motionless for three days simply began to descend the slope in perfect unison, cloaks stirring in a wind that didn't reach the valley floor, and the distance between them and the boundary stone began, patiently, terribly, to shrink.

"Run," Felix said, and picked Wren up, and ran.

* * *

He ran the way he had run twenty years ago, and hated how well his body still knew it. The road south out of the high valley dropped through pine and scree, switchbacking along the river gorge, and Felix took it at a pace that would have killed a normal man's lungs within the mile, because he was not, whatever else was true, a normal man anymore—he was an oath given legs, and oaths do not tire the way flesh does.

Wren rode his back with cheek pressed between his shoulder blades, and after the first mile the child began, very softly, to sing.

It was the prayer again—the one from the first night, the one no living tongue should know. But sung now, and changed. Slowed. Shaped. And Felix, listening as his boots hammered the frozen ground, realized with a lurch that Wren was singing it *to him*. Not to the silent god. To the man carrying the child. A blessing for road-weariness, one of the oldest forms in the First Tongue, older than kings, older than the Court, from the days when travelers sang each other safe because there was nothing else to be done.

The song settled into him like snowfall into a furrow. His breathing eased. His stride lengthened. The terror didn't leave—if anything it sharpened, because he could now *hear*, with his newly reopened senses, the riders behind them: not footsteps, but a kind of pressure, a displacement of the world's attention, six silences sliding down the mountain at the speed of consequence.

But under the terror, undeniable, disgraceful, alive: exhilaration.

God help him, he felt *awake*. Twenty years of careful invisibility peeling off him like bandages, and underneath, the man he'd been—scarred, damned, sworn, blazing. He could feel the small gods of the countryside ahead already turning toward him like flowers toward a dangerous sun. He could feel his name coming back, syllable by syllable, the way blood comes back to a limb that's been asleep.

"They're not gaining," Wren said against his shoulder. "Why aren't they gaining?"

"Because they don't have to." Felix vaulted a fallen pine without breaking stride. "Bailiffs don't chase. They *escort*. They know where we're going, Wren. We're going to the Court. They're going to the Court. Everyone's going to the same funeral." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and didn't try to sand it off. "We're just early."

Below them, the gorge opened onto the foothills, and the foothills onto the long grey country beyond Vell—the world he'd renounced, spread out under a sky just beginning to bruise with dawn. Somewhere out there was a hedge-priestess he didn't yet know he needed. Somewhere out there were three gates and a queen and a vault full of sold souls. Somewhere out there, faint as a star behind cloud, something enormous that had been silent for twenty years was—

Was that—

Felix stopped so abruptly that Wren's chin bumped his shoulder.

For half a heartbeat—one breath, drawn and released—amid all the roaring noise of his reawakened senses, amid the pressure of the riders and the itch of the scar and the child's song still hanging in the air, he had felt something turn toward him from very far away. Not answering. Not speaking. Just... turning. The way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice.

Then it was gone, and the silence closed back over the place where it had been, smooth as water.

"What?" Wren whispered. "What is it?"

"Nothing," Felix said. "Old habit. Listening for someone who doesn't pick up."

He shifted the child higher on his back, looked once more at the ridgeline—where the six grey riders had paused, arranged in a line against the brightening sky, patient as winter—and ran on, down out of the mountains, into the wide world where everyone could finally see him.

Behind them, at the head of the descending riders, the lead bailiff raised one grey hand, and in a voice like a ledger closing, spoke Felix Halloran's true name aloud for the first time in twenty years.

The mountains repeated it. The river repeated it. And somewhere beneath the world, in a cathedral of bone-white vaults, something that had been sleeping for two decades opened its eyes, and smiled, and said:

*"There you are."*
