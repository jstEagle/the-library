## 9. The Road of Small Gods

The valley let them go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a schoolhouse door—reluctantly, and only because it must.

Felix felt Vell's silence fall away behind him in layers, like bandages peeled from a wound he'd forgotten he had. First went the muffled quiet, that cotton-soft hush where prayer died before it finished leaving the mouth. Then came the smell: cut hay and cold stone giving way to wet earth, pine resin, the iron tang of a world that remembered what it was made of. And last—when they had walked a full morning and the valley's rim was a blue line at their backs—came the rest of him.

It poured back into Felix like cold water into a dry boot.

He staggered on the rutted road and put a hand against a fencepost to keep from kneeling. Wren, walking beside him with a bundle of Marta's bread under one arm, looked up sharply.

"You're *glowing*," Wren said—not in the First Tongue this time but in plain trade-speech, learned fast as children learn everything, and said with the flat alarm of a child stating an inconvenient fact.

"I know." His voice came out rough. Twenty years of being no one, of being a farrier whose hands smelled of hoof and charcoal, of passing through villages without so much as a dog lifting its head—and now every ward, every oath, every scrap of the old bargain woke at once and hung off him like lit lanterns. He could feel it himself, a low hum under his skin, a heat along the scar over his heart. To anything with eyes for such things, he would be visible for miles. A bonfire in a country of candles.

"Is it bad?" Wren asked.

"It's honest," Felix said, which was not an answer, and Wren knew it wasn't, and let it go the way children do when they've already decided the grown-up will lie.

They walked on.

* * *

The road south from Vell's borderlands ran through country Felix did not know, which was itself a strange mercy. He had spent his mercenary years everywhere else—the passes, the river wars, the long bitter campaigns along the mountain spine—but never here, in these soft green folds between the valley and the high country, where the land rolled gentle as a sleeping animal. Farmers were cutting late hay. Crows argued over the stubble fields. It should have been peaceful.

It was not peaceful, because of the shrines.

There was one at nearly every crossroads, and more besides: little stone houses no bigger than a birdcove, some older than the roads themselves, their niches holding a candle stub, a cup of milk going skin-firm in the cold, a knot of red thread. Small gods. Not the great silent one Felix had sworn himself to—nothing so vast and absent—but the little local powers, the god of this ford and that orchard, the grandmother-goddess of a particular bend in the river who liked cream and hated lies. In the valley, such things could not exist; magic could not cross the boundary, and neither could divinity of any size. Out here they were thick as sparrows.

And out here, they all knew his name.

He felt the first one before he understood what he was feeling—a prickle at the back of his neck as they passed a shrine set into an oak's roots, a fat-bellied stone figure with a worn-smooth face. As they drew level, the air around the little god *stirred*, the way water stirs when a fish turns beneath it.

*Wolf,* something whispered, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. *The Wolf is on the road.*

Felix kept walking. He did not look at the shrine. He did not look at Wren, whose head had turned toward it anyway, quick as a wren's.

A mile on, a roadside marker to some forgotten surveyor grew warm as they passed. *Wolf. Wolf Who Broke His Pack.* Two miles after that, a cup of milk left out for a hearth-god rippled in its saucer though there was no wind, and the whisper came again, and this time it was joined by another voice, and then another, small gods calling to small gods down the length of the road like farmwives calling news across fences:

*He's moving south. The Wolf is moving south.*

"Who are they talking about?" Wren asked, after the fourth or fifth shrine. There was milk left out at that one too, and the child had watched it tremble with wide, unblinking eyes.

"Me."

"Why do they call you wolf?"

Because I ran with a pack, Felix thought. Because I led them into the mountains. Because I came down alone. Because a wolf that loses its pack is either dead or something worse than dead, and I am the thing worse than dead.

"Old story," he said.

"Marta says you don't tell stories."

"Marta says a lot of things."

Wren considered this with the grave patience of the very young, then reached up and took Felix's hand—not for comfort, Felix suspected, but the way a person takes hold of a rope in weather. The child's fingers were cold. They were always cold now, since the fever, since the mark, since everything.

"They're scared of you," Wren said. "But not the way the riders are scary. More like... the way dogs are scared of thunder."

That was close enough to the truth that Felix didn't trust his voice, so he said nothing, and they walked, and the small gods whispered, and the road unspooled south through the green country like thread off a spool.

* * *

By late afternoon the whispers had changed key.

At first they had been only startled recognition, gossip, the shock of small holy things encountering a legend made flesh. But as the day wore on and the road bent toward the foothills, the tone shifted. Fear curdled into warning. At a shrine where three lanes met—an ugly little cairn of stacked stones with a horse-skull set on top—the whisper came sharp enough that even Wren flinched:

*South, Wolf? South is the Court's country. The bailiffs have your scent. Turn east. Turn anywhere. You cannot outrun what you owe.*

"They can smell me," Felix said quietly. Not to Wren. To himself. "Twenty years inside that valley and the first day out, they can smell me."

"What can?" Wren asked.

"The things I used to work for." He crouched at the cairn, which was absurd—he hadn't knelt at a shrine in twenty years, hadn't dared, his god being silent and the lesser gods being gossips—but he was tired, and the light was failing, and the horse-skull's empty sockets seemed to be watching him with something that was almost pity. "Is there shelter ahead?" he asked it. "Somewhere the riders won't think to look?"

The skull said nothing, obviously. Skulls say nothing. But the air around the cairn went still and attentive, the way a room goes still when someone is deciding whether to help you, and then—so faint he might have imagined it—a pressure at the edge of his mind, a direction, a pull, like a finger pointing down the westernmost lane.

*Toward the river,* the whisper came at last, grudging as a coin pried from a fist. *The priestess. If she'll have you. She owes nothing to anyone, which is why she can afford mercy.*

"Thank you," Felix said.

*Don't thank me,* snapped the little god, offended. *I'm only doing it because she'll know if I don't.*

* * *

They found the priestess's house an hour past dusk, exactly where the pull had pointed: a crooked cottage tucked into the elbow where a stream met the river, its walls living with ivy, its chimney smoking a thin grey ribbon into the violet sky. Shelves of jars glowed faintly in the windows—not magic, Felix thought, or not much of it, just herbs and tinctures and the ordinary alchemy of preserving. A garden gone wild and deliberate at once. Wind chimes made of spoons and bits of broken glass, turning without wind.

Wren stopped at the gate. "She knows we're coming," the child said.

"She'd better," Felix said, "after I asked her neighbor for directions."

The door opened before he knocked. The woman standing in it was perhaps sixty, built like a fence post, with iron-grey hair chopped short and eyes the pale yellow-green of birch leaves in spring. She wore an apron stained with something purple and held a wooden spoon like a scepter. She looked at Felix for a long moment—at the glow she could surely see, the hum of the reawakened oath, the scar over his heart—and then at Wren, and her expression did something complicated.

"Well," she said. "The Wolf. On my doorstep. With the Court's own child on his hand like a tame starling." She stepped back and jerked her head. "In, before the dark sees you. And wipe your boots. I mean it."

Inside was warm and crowded and smelled of woodsmoke, rosemary, and lamp oil. Bundles hung from every beam. A cat regarded them from a chair with the entitlement of minor royalty. The priestess—Sef, she said, offering no other name and no hand to shake—took their wet cloaks, fed them barley soup with dumplings heavy as fists, and asked no questions through the whole meal, which Felix recognized as its own kind of interrogation. She was letting him decide how much to volunteer. It was, he had to admit, exactly what he would have done.

It was Wren who broke first, because Wren always broke first; the child had no talent whatsoever for the silences adults hide inside.

"We're going to the mountain," Wren announced, mouth half-full of dumpling. "To get my soul back. From the vault. Before the solstice bell."

Sef set down her spoon.

Felix closed his eyes. "Wren."

"No, no." Sef raised a hand. "Let the child talk. Children and small gods are the only ones who tell the truth anymore." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, studying Wren with those birch-leaf eyes. "You know about the vault, do you?"

"I remember it." Wren's voice changed when the child said things like that—went older, went distant, went somewhere else entirely. "White cases, tall as trees. Everything humming. My soul is in the third row. It sounds like a struck bell that never finishes ringing." The child blinked, and was six years old again, and shoveled in another dumpling.

Sef sat back slowly. She looked at Felix the way one looks at a man who has brought a lit fuse into a powder store.

"And you intend to take it out of there."

"That's the plan."

"Do you know what stands between here and there?"

"Some," Felix said carefully. "Riders. Hounds. The gates—I've heard there are gates."

"*Three* gates." Sef stood, went to a shelf, and took down a bottle of something amber and a single clay cup, pouring for herself alone. "Sit properly, Wolf, and listen, because I'm only saying this once and I'm only saying it free because someone once did my grandmother a kindness I won't name. After tonight, everything costs."

Felix sat properly.

"First gate is the Gate of Names." She held up one finger. "You speak your true name at it, whole and entire, and it owns you forever—your labor, your luck, your bones when you die. Most people don't even know their true names anymore, which is the Court's favorite kind of joke. Those who do, and give it, become the Court's tools. Some of the bailiffs hunting you were men once. Good men. They gave their names to buy something they couldn't live without, and now they spend eternity collecting from others."

The fire popped. The cat yawned.

"Second gate is the Gate of Debts." Second finger. "It weighs you. Every promise unkept, every debt unpaid, every wrong you did and didn't mend—it shows you the ledger, and it demands payment on the spot. Coin means nothing there. It takes payment in what you *are*. Years. Talents. Loves. Men have walked through that gate having paid their sight, their courage, their memory of their mother's face. What you lose depends on what you owe." Her eyes flicked to the scar over his heart, and away. "You, I suspect, owe a great deal."

"And the third?"

Sef drank. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone careful, the way voices go near graves.

"The Gate of Silence. The innermost door, before the Court itself. No god can follow past it—not the small ones, not the great ones, not the fallen and not the forgotten. Whatever power you carry, whatever oaths bind you, whatever prayers you've hoarded—all of it stops at that threshold. You go through alone, stripped, with nothing but what's in your own two hands and your own two choices." She set down the cup. "People assume that's the easiest gate, because nothing attacks you there. It isn't. It's the worst one. Because at the Gate of Silence, the only thing standing between you and what you want is yourself, and most of us know how that fight goes."

Silence filled the kitchen, the ordinary human kind, which suddenly seemed precious.

"There's a fourth thing," Felix said. "The solstice bell."

"The solstice bell." Sef nodded slowly. "When it rings, the Court seals its vaults until spring. Anything inside stays inside. Anything owed becomes owed *forever*—written into the world's bones, no appeal, no undoing. Nine days from now, by my reckoning." She looked at Wren, asleep now—or pretending to sleep—curled in the chair with the cat, small hands folded under one cheek. "Nine days to walk five days' hard road, break into a fortress that has never been broken, steal from a Queen who has never lost, and come home again. Carrying a child."

"Yes," Felix said.

"You're mad."

"Yes."

Sef studied him for a long time. Outside, the wind chimes stirred, though there was still no wind, and beneath the rattle of glass and spoon-metal Felix heard—or thought he heard—the small gods of the garden whispering to each other, low and urgent: *the Wolf, the Wolf, the Wolf is here.*

"Here's what I know about you, Wolf," Sef said at last. "Not the songs. The songs are filth and flattery mixed. I know that twenty years ago a company of mercenaries went into the Grey Teeth passes and did not come down, and that the Hollow Court's ledgers recorded a payment made in that season unlike any payment before or since. I know that the man who made it walked out of those mountains alive and alone, and that every god from here to the sea has called him breaker and coward and thief ever since." She leaned forward. "And I know that none of them—not one—has ever asked what he bought with it. Or what it cost him to carry the buying."

Felix's throat had gone tight. "What's your point, priestess?"

"My point is that mercy is expensive and I'm about to spend some." She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "You can't take the child to the mountain on rumor and stubbornness. You need a guide who knows the old paths, the shrine-roads, the ways the Court's hounds hate to travel. I haven't left this riverbend in thirty years, and I'll curse myself daily for agreeing, but—" she jabbed the spoon at him— "*but*, I'll take you as far as the foothills. For a price."

"Name it."

"One true memory." Sef's eyes were steady and not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Chosen by you. Given freely. I keep it—in a vial, on my shelf, where I can visit it. That's my rate. Everyone pays me in memories; it's the only currency I trust. Take a night to choose. Choose well, Wolf, because whichever one you give me, you'll wake tomorrow and it will be a hole in you shaped like something you loved."

She gathered the soup bowls and carried them to the washbasin, and the conversation was plainly over.

Felix sat by the fire a long while, listening to the child breathe, listening to the wind chimes whisper, listening to the small gods murmur his infamous name out in the dark like a tide going in and out. Somewhere south, under the mountain, a bell waited to ring. Somewhere behind, grey riders moved through the night with his scent in their nostrils.

And somewhere in his own head, twenty years of memories sat waiting to be counted, priced, and sold—one of them, soon, gone forever.

He began, with dread, to make a list.

* * *

He did not sleep. Toward the small hours, Wren woke crying—not loudly, the way children cry from nightmares, but silently, tears running down into the pillow while the child stayed perfectly still, which was somehow far worse.

"I dreamed about the vault," Wren whispered, when Felix came to kneel beside the chair. "But it wasn't a dream. It was remembering. And Felix—" the child's cold fingers found his wrist and gripped— "there was a case in the third row that wasn't mine. It was older than mine. And the name on it was yours."

The fire had burned to embers. In the next room, he heard Sef stop moving, stop breathing, listening.

"The name on it," Felix said carefully, "was mine?"

"Not your name." Wren's eyes were huge and dark in the ember-light. "Your *true* name. The one the first gate wants." The child swallowed. "Felix. Someone already paid it. Twenty years ago. It's been sitting in the third row ever since, and it's been *waiting* for you."

Out in the garden, every wind chime rang at once, though there was no wind at all.
