## 10. The Hedge-Priestess's Price

The priestess's house stood where the road bent away from the river, a crooked thing of wattle and turf that seemed less built than grown, as if the land had decided one morning to raise a shelter and had done so in a hurry. Smoke rose from its chimney in a thin, obedient line. Chickens muttered in a run of grey mud. A row of glass bottles caught the low sun on the windowsill, each one holding something that glowed faintly and did not flicker the way flame flickers.

Felix stopped at the gate with Wren's small hand in his and felt the hair on his arms rise.

"You feel it too," he said.

Wren nodded. The child had spoken little since the ford, since the hounds, since the words that had come out of that small mouth in a voice that was not entirely a child's. Wren's eyes had a bruised look, the look of someone who had carried something heavy a very long way and set it down only to find their arms still shaped around it.

The door opened before Felix knocked.

The woman in it was old in the way of trees—gnarled, weathered, still unmistakably alive at the root. Her hair was iron-grey and bound with red thread, and she wore a hedge-priest's patched cassock under a shepherd's cloak, and around her neck hung a dozen small charms that should have jangled when she moved and did not. Her eyes were the pale gold of winter honey, and they went to Felix first, then to Wren, then back to Felix with an expression he had seen only twice before in his life: once on a woman reading a death warrant, and once on himself, in a mirror, the night he signed the contract beneath the mountain.

"The Wolf of Vell," she said. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "And the payment. Come in, before the road sees you."

*The payment.* Felix's jaw tightened, but Wren was already stepping past him, drawn by some current Felix could not feel, and there was nothing to do but follow.

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of rosemary, woodsmoke, and something older underneath—cold stone, the way caves smell. Herbs hung from every beam in bundles gone brown and dusty. A fire burned in a hearth built into the wall's curve, and before it stood a table set with three cups, already poured, already steaming, though the chimney smoke outside had told no one they were coming.

"Sit," the priestess said. "I am Sef. I keep the shrines between here and the mountain's shadow, such as they are, such as the small gods let me. You ate at my ford shrine last night. You left an offering. A copper and your blood on the stone, though I don't suppose you knew the blood was an offering. Old habit, that. Mercenaries bleed on everything."

"You know what I am," Felix said.

"I know what you *were*." Sef lowered herself into a chair of woven hazel and pushed a cup toward him. "Drink. It's yarrow and honey and a little something to keep your dreams from walking. The child drinks too. The child especially."

Wren took the cup in both hands and drank, and some of the bruised look eased from those eyes, and Felix drank too because his throat was raw from three days of walking with his oath-mark burning under his collar like a coal that would not cool.

Outside the valley, everything had come back. That was the thing he had not been prepared for, though he should have been, though he had known it in the abstract the way a man knows the abstract fact of drowning. In Vell, his oath had been a scar: closed, quiet, a white line over his heart he touched on bad nights and otherwise forgot. Here, past the boundary, it was a wound again—open, singing, visible. He had seen his own reflection in the river that first morning and had looked away from it quickly. The mark showed now. Through his shirt, through his coat, through anything: a pale sigil over his heart, glowing like a lantern in a dark room, announcing him to everything in the world that had eyes for such things.

The riders had turned to follow within the hour. He and Wren had cut east through the hills, off the roads, sleeping in ditches and shrines, and the riders had gained anyway, unhurried, inevitable, the way winter gains.

"You're being hunted," Sef said. It was not a question.

"Grey cloaks. Nine of them, maybe ten. They don't ride fast. They don't need to."

"No. They don't." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "The Hollow Court's bailiffs. Collectors. They cannot touch you while you walk the shrine roads—old law, older than the Court pretends, and the small gods still hold to it out of spite if nothing else. But the shrine roads end at the mountain's shadow. And past that shadow there are three gates, and past the three gates there is a vault, and in that vault is the thing you're walking toward, and the thing you're running from, and I'd wager my roof those are the same thing."

Felix set down his cup. "The hedge-priestess at the last shrine said you might guide us. She said you knew the ways under the mountain."

"I knew them when they were ways. Now they're prices." Sef looked at him, and her honey-pale eyes were very still. "Nothing under the mountain opens for free. Not for you. Not for anyone. The Gate of Names wants a name. The Gate of Debts wants payment. The Gate of Silence—" she paused, and something crossed her face, some old grief worn smooth as a river stone, "—the Gate of Silence wants what it always wants, which is everything you have left after the first two gates are done with you. But none of that is my price. My price is for me. For the guiding. For what it will cost me to walk a marked man to the Court's door and live with what comes after."

"Name it," Felix said. "Silver. I have some. Not much, but—"

"Silver." Sef laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry and short, like a branch breaking. "Child, I have buried four husbands and outlived every god I ever served, and I keep the memories of the dead in bottles on my windowsill. What would I want with silver?"

* * *

She showed him the shelf that evening, when Wren slept by the fire under a quilt that smelled of lavender and decades.

The bottles were small, no bigger than a fist, each one stoppered with wax and labeled in a spidery hand. And in each one, something moved. Not light exactly. Not smoke. It was like looking at the moment just before remembering—like the shape a memory makes in the half-second before it becomes words. One bottle held a summer. He could tell; it was golden and slow and full of the sound of insects. Another held something dark and quick that pressed against the glass when Felix came near, and Sef moved it gently out of sight with one hand, the way you'd move a candle from a draft.

"What are they?" he asked, though he already knew. He had known since the doorway. The knowing had been sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone all day.

"Memories," Sef said. "True ones. Taken whole, with the feeling still in them. I have the last summer of a man who died at the ford in the year of the red snow. I have a woman's memory of her mother singing, and her mother has been dead two hundred years, and when I unstopper that bottle in the dark, the singing is still perfect." She touched one bottle, tenderly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head. "The small gods eat memory, did you know that? It's the only worship they have left. Every prayer that fades, every name that's forgotten, every face that dissolves—it all trickles down to them, and it keeps them alive, barely, the way ash keeps a bed of coals. I am a priestess of small gods. I feed them. And they let me keep a little for myself."

"And my price," Felix said, "is one of mine."

Sef looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the hedges, and somewhere down the dark road a nightjar called its one dry note.

"You understand what I'm offering," she said. "Not a thing. Not coin. A true memory, taken whole. You'll choose it, because the taking must be willing or it curdles into something else, something I won't have in my house. But once it's in the glass, it's gone from you. Not hidden. *Gone.* You'll know you had it the way you know a room had a window that's been bricked over. You'll feel the shape of the absence for the rest of your life. And the shape of an absence, mercenary, is its own kind of haunting. Some of my bottles were paid for by people who came back a year later begging me to give them back. I never do. That's the whole of the bargain. That's the whole of every bargain, in the end. You pay, and you live in the space where the payment used to be."

Felix looked at the fire. Wren slept, breath slow and even, one hand curled near the coals' warmth. On the child's back, under the borrowed shirt, the soul-sigil burned like a brand that matched the one over Felix's own heart, and twenty years of not-asking pressed against the inside of his ribs.

"I sold something once," he said. He had not meant to say it aloud. It came out flat, the way you recite a thing you've recited to yourself so many times the words have worn smooth. "To save my company. I signed, and I bled on the paper, and I never asked what it was I gave. I told myself it was a piece of my soul, some small dark corner, nothing I'd miss. And everyone died anyway, except me, and I came here, to a valley where nothing could collect, and I spent twenty years not asking." He turned to look at her. "I'm not losing another memory without choosing it. So I'll choose. But I want you to know what you're buying from. A man who has already sold without looking."

Sef's expression did not soften, exactly. Something behind it moved, the way water moves under ice.

"Good," she said. "Then you'll choose with your eyes open. That's more than most manage."

* * *

He walked that night, alone, up the low hill behind her house, because he could not choose sitting down.

The moon was a fingernail paring in the west. Below him the shrine road unspooled silver-grey toward the distant smudge of the mountains, and somewhere along it, patient as frost, nine or ten riders were also walking toward the mountain, and they would arrive when they arrived, and no speed of his would change that. He had stopped running from that truth somewhere around the second day. You could not outrun a debt. You could only arrive first, or arrive armed, or arrive willing to pay something the creditor had not thought to ask for.

He took inventory, because inventory was what he knew. Twenty years in Vell had given him a farrier's patience and a farrier's hands, and a farrier knows that you do not cut a shoe from a foundered hoof in one pull; you ease it, you respect the living tissue, you take only what the hoof can spare.

What could he spare?

His mother's face—he had already half-lost that to time, and losing the rest felt less like payment than like finishing a job the years had started. Too easy. Sef would know it was too easy, and worse, it would be a lie of a different kind: paying with a coin already spent.

The passes. The snow. The company dying in the white silence, Brand's hand going slack in his, Ilsa's last breath fogging the air between them—those he needed. Those were the wound itself. Cut them out and he would lose the reason he was walking toward the mountain at all. A man who forgets why he bleeds stops bleeding on purpose.

Wren. Every hour of Wren—the fevered first night, the first words in the First Tongue, the small weight of the child asleep against his back on the road. No. He would sooner pay with his hands.

And then, sitting in the cold grass with his arms around his knees, he found it. It rose in him quietly, the way water rises in a well when you've finally stopped bailing.

The day before the contract. The last good day.

They had camped below the pass in a fold of the mountain where the wind couldn't reach, and the company had been alive, all of them, every one—Brand telling a story so full of lies that Ilsa had laughed until she cried, and Tam had been whittling a wolf from a piece of pine for no reason anyone asked about, and the fire had been good, and the wine had been bad, and Felix had sat among them thinking, *this is the shape of my life, and it is enough, and I will have a hundred days like this.* He had thought that. He could still feel himself thinking it, warm as a stone held against the chest. And the next day the snows had come down like a judgment, and the day after that he had knelt in a vault of bone-white stone and signed his name in his own blood, and the last good day had become the last day, full stop, the horizon of everything he had been before he became the man who survived.

That was what he could spare. That was the payment.

Because he understood, sitting in the dark, what he had never let himself understand: he had been carrying that day like a splinter. Every year in Vell, every quiet morning at the forge, every time Marta had brought him bread and he had flinched from the kindness of it—he had been measuring the present against that day and finding it wanting, and the wanting had kept the wound open, and the open wound was what the Court's mark fed on. Take the day away and there would be nothing to measure against. Nothing to grieve forward from. Only the forge, and the child, and the road, and whatever came after.

It was the truest coin he had. That was what made it a real price.

* * *

In the morning he told Sef, and she did not ask him twice, which he was grateful for, because a second asking would have broken something.

They did it at her table, in full daylight, because she said shadows made the taking slippery. She set out a bottle—new, unlabeled, its glass so clean it was barely there at all—and a small knife of grey metal, and a bowl of water in which a single sprig of rosemary floated.

"Hold the memory," she said. "All of it. Every sense. Don't tell it to me—memories told are memories edited. Just hold it, whole, and when it's at its brightest, I'll draw. It will feel like a thread being pulled. Let it come. If you fight it, it tears, and a torn memory hurts worse than a lost one. Trust me on that. I've seen the torn ones."

Felix closed his eyes.

The fold in the mountain. The windless cold. Brand's voice doing the voices of three different lords, all of them fools, all of them dead in the story and laughing about it. Ilsa laughing until she had to put her cup down. Tam's knife whispering through pine. The fire. The bad wine. The warmth of being among them, the certainty—God, the *certainty*—that there would be a hundred more days.

It had never been brighter. Twenty years of keeping it in the dark had made it burn like a coal finally given air.

He felt the thread.

It did not hurt, which was the horror of it. It was like the moment of falling asleep, the gentle unclasping, the hand letting go of the day's last thought. He felt the fire go. He felt the laughter go, and the wine, and Brand's voice, and the whittling, and then the warmth itself, the certainty, the *enough*—and he reached for it, he couldn't help reaching, and Sef said, very quietly, "Let it come," and he let it come, and then it was in the glass.

The bottle on the table held a small gold light. It moved slowly, the way firelight moves on a wall. It was beautiful. It was the last good day, and it was beautiful, and it was not his anymore.

He did not remember it.

That was the thing no one could have told him properly. He sat at the table looking at the bottle and he knew—knew with total certainty—that he was looking at the day before the contract, the day with the fire and the company and the bad wine. He knew it the way you know the shape of a bricked-over window. But when he reached for it, there was nothing. No faces. No fire. Just the shape of the absence, smooth and cold, exactly where something warm had been, and the grief arrived all at once, whole, with nothing left to grieve *about*—grief like a bell with the bell gone, ringing anyway.

He was crying. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain on someone else's roof.

"There," Sef said softly. She was stoppering the bottle with wax, sealing it, and her old hands were steady and her eyes were not quite dry either, because she had done this a hundred times and it never got easier, and that, Felix understood dimly through the ringing emptiness, was the only honest priesthood left in the world. "There it is. Paid in full."

"It's so quiet," Felix said. His voice sounded strange to him. "Inside. Where it was."

"I know." She set the bottle on the shelf, between the summer and the dark quick thing, and wrote a label in her spidery hand, and turned the label to the wall so that even she would not read it idly. "It stays quiet for a while. Then other things grow into the quiet. That's not a promise. It's just what happens, with gardens and with people."

Wren was awake. Felix didn't know how long the child had been watching from the hearth—long enough. Wren crossed the room without a word and climbed into his lap, which the child had never done, not once in all the weeks since the doorstep, and put one small hand flat against Felix's chest, over the burning mark, over the new absence.

"It's still you," Wren said. In the First Tongue, the old prayer-language, the words falling soft and certain as snow. "The day was never the shape of you. The shape of you is what stayed."

Felix held the child and said nothing, because there was nothing in him to say with, and because the words had gone in past the absence, past the ringing, all the way down to whatever it was that had stayed.

Sef was already packing. Rope, bread, a hooded lantern, a coil of red thread, and—wrapped in oilcloth, handled like something sleeping—the bottle. She tucked it into the bottom of her pack, and Felix understood that she was coming, that the price had never been only the memory, that she had bought herself a place on the road to the mountain because some debts of her own ran that direction.

"Three days to the mountain's shadow," she said, shouldering her pack. "We walk the shrine roads while the light lasts. And Felix—" she paused at the door, and the wind came in around her, smelling of coming winter, "—eat well this morning. Sleep tonight if you can. Because tomorrow, or the day after, we cross the ford at the river's knee, and the ford is the last place the old law holds, and things are waiting there that don't care about old law. I heard them in the water last night. They know your name."

"They knew my name twenty years ago," Felix said. He rose, and settled Wren against his hip, and looked east toward the mountains where the sky had begun to bruise. "It's the knowing what I've become that they're coming to collect."

Sef smiled without any joy in it at all, and opened the door onto the cold.
