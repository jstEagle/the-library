## 12. The Company of Ghosts

The tower announced itself an hour before they saw it.

Wren felt it first—went rigid in the cart bed and said, in the First Tongue, a word Felix had not heard spoken aloud in twenty years. *Veth.* Home. Or the older meaning, the one that made his skin crawl: *the place where the dead keep your things.*

Felix reined the mule to a stop and looked up through the bare birches at the hill ahead. It was exactly as he remembered it, which was the first wrong thing. Hills eroded. Trees fell or grew. But this rise sat beneath its crown of ruined stone with the patient stillness of something posed, arranged, waiting to be found by precisely the right traveler.

The watchtower had been old when his company garrisoned it—a border post from some kingdom that no longer existed, squatting on the ridge between two valleys like a tooth left in a jaw. He had wintered there once, the worst and best winter of his life, twenty-two people crammed into a roundhouse built for eight, burning green wood and telling lies about how rich they'd all be by spring.

He had never come back. He had told himself it was because the road didn't go that way.

The road went that way.

"You knew," he said quietly. Not to Wren. To the memory that had been speaking inside the child for three days now, rising through Wren's hollowed places like silt disturbed in a pond. "You've been steering us."

Wren looked up at him with those too-old eyes and did not deny it. The child's voice, when it came, was half their own and half something borrowed: "They're cold, Felix. They've been cold for twenty years, and they can't die, and they can't leave, and they don't know why." A pause. "You know why."

He did. He had always known, in the place where knowledge lives that you refuse to visit—the cellar room of the self, door barred, floor swept, nothing stored there officially. He had traded a portion of his soul to the Hollow Court to buy his comrades out of the pass. That was the story. That was the arithmetic he had lived by: *I paid; I lost; they lived.*

Except they hadn't lived. Except the Court took payment and gave him back a company of ghosts instead of men, echoes sustained by the coin he'd spent, wandering the ruin where they died—or where they should have died—and he had walked away and let himself believe the lie because the alternative was a grief with no bottom and no floor.

"They're angry," Wren said. Not unkindly. Children are cruel without meaning to be, but Wren delivered this like a physician delivering a prognosis. "Not all of them. Some of them just miss you. One of them—" the child tilted their head, listening to something Felix could not hear, "—one of them has been keeping count of the days."

* * *

He left the mule tethered at the treeline with Sef, who refused on principle to approach anything that lit itself. "Dead things that remember are worse than dead things that forget," she said, wrapping her grey shawl tighter. "Forgetful dead want rest. Remembering dead want *something else*." She pressed a vial of salt into his hand and would not meet his eyes. "If they ask you to stay, Felix Halloran—and they will—remember that you have somewhere to be before solstice."

Wren insisted on coming. There was no arguing with the child; there never had been, not really, not since the night on the doorstep when a frozen scrap of a person had spoken the god's own tongue through blue lips. So they climbed together through the birches as the light went long and amber, and the cold came down off the ridge like a hand laid gently on the back of the neck—not threatening, just *present*, the way a creditor is present.

The tower's door had rotted away years ago. Beyond it, the roundhouse opened to the sky, walls standing to twice a man's height, the old arrow slits framing strips of darkening sky like slits cut in cloth. Snow lay in the corners where the roof had failed. And in the center of the floor, in the ring of stones that had held a fire twenty winters ago—

There was a fire.

It burned low and steady and orange, giving off heat Felix could feel on his face from the doorway, and it gave off no smoke, and it cast shadows that moved wrong—shadows that sat around it in a circle, seven of them, talking.

"—all I'm saying is if we rotated watch proper, the way Doss wanted, we wouldn't be short every third night—"

"We're not *short*, Tam, we're *dead*, it's a different roster entirely—"

"Then whose turn is it? Because someone has to take first watch and it isn't going to be me again—"

The voices stopped. All of them, mid-word, the way birdsong stops. Seven shadows turned their heads toward the door with a synchrony that no living group of soldiers ever managed, and Felix's hand found the wall beside him because his knees had opinions of their own.

He knew them. Of course he knew them. Twenty years had worn the faces smooth in his memory, and here they were, worn smooth the same way—as if the ghosts were copies made from his remembering rather than from the men themselves, and the thought arrived with its teeth already sunk in.

Tamris Vael, who'd been first spear, broad and laughing, his shadow flickering where the firelight couldn't decide whether he deserved edges. Doss the quartermaster, thin as a ledger line. Old Maren, who'd taught Felix to hold a blade so it was an extension of the arm and not a stick attached to it. The twins, Ashen and Ashe, indistinguishable even in death, sitting shoulder to shoulder. Little Piet, youngest of them all, seventeen forever. And at the edge of the circle, apart from the others, a seventh shade whose face Felix's memory had mercifully blurred and which the firelight now, cruelly, restored.

Captain Yeva Sorrel. Who had ordered the retreat from the pass. Who had died last of all, holding the rearguard, and whose last words to Felix had been *get them home*.

"Felix." Tamris said it first, and his voice was warm, and that was somehow the worst thing yet. "Felix, you absolute bastard. Look at this. Look what the wind blew in."

"Twenty years," Doss said, and his voice was dry as the paper he'd kept his accounts on. "Two hundred days short of twenty years, by my count, though the counting's been hard, since none of us sleep."

"You came back." Maren stood. Her shade wavered like heat-haze over coals. "We said you wouldn't. We had a bet going. Piet bet you would."

"I bet you would," Piet said softly, and smiled, and Felix's chest did something structural.

* * *

They circled him, the way wolves do, the way soldiers do, touching him with hands that passed through his coat and left cold behind like the ghost of a touch, which he supposed was exactly what it was. They asked questions in a tumble—was the war over, had the kingdom fallen, did anyone still sing the marching song, was his beard grey, it was grey, gods, look at him, grey as ash—and Felix answered what he could and lied about the rest, and the whole time Wren stood in the doorway watching with the expression of someone reading a book whose ending they already knew.

It was Yeva who stopped it. She raised one hand—not commanding, never commanding, she'd led by suggestion the way water leads a river—and the others settled.

"Why now?" she asked. Her voice was quiet, and underneath the quiet ran something that had been quiet for twenty years and had finally finished sharpening itself. "Why are you here now, Felix?"

"The Court," he said. "The Hollow Court. They've come to collect a soul they say I sold. It belongs to this child." He put his hand on Wren's shoulder. "I'm going to get it back."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Seven shades looked at each other in a language of glances perfected across decades of shared tents and shared watches, and Felix watched them do it and understood, suddenly, completely, that they knew something he did not.

"What did you sell us for, Felix?" Yeva asked.

"You know what I—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "The pass. You were dying. All of you. The Company was down to eleven walking wounded and the Court's bailiff offered terms and I signed. My soul against your lives. That was the trade. That was always the trade."

"That was what you *told yourself*," Doss said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat precision of a man reading figures aloud. "We've had twenty years to read the contract, Felix. It's carved on the lintel upstairs. We can't leave, but we can climb stairs."

"Show him," Ashen said—or Ashe; even now Felix couldn't tell.

* * *

The lintel stone above the inner stair had been hidden under plaster when the company garrisoned the tower. Sometime in the intervening years the plaster had fallen—or been shed—and the stone beneath bore letters cut deep and old, in the formal script of the Court, which does not fade because it is not written in ink but in agreement.

Felix read it three times. The words rearranged themselves each time, refusing to settle into the shape he wanted.

*RECEIVED OF FELIX HALLORAN: ONE SOUL, INTACT, TOGETHER WITH ITS FUTURE FRUIT.*

*IN EXCHANGE: THE LIVES OF THE COMPANY OF THE GREY WOLF, ELEVEN SOULS, THEIR TERMS EXTENDED UNTIL SUCH TIME AS THE FIRST INSTALLMENT RIPENS.*

His mouth was dry. "Extended," he said. "Their terms extended. You didn't—you weren't saved. You were *deferred*. You died anyway, and the Court just—" He looked at them, at the flickering, fragmentary, furious patience of them, and the whole architecture of his penance shifted and groaned like ice on a river. "You've been dead for twenty years. You died in the pass. This—" he gestured at the fire, at the circle, at the argument about watches that never ended because there was nothing left to guard, "—this is what my soul bought. An echo. A *receipt*."

"And the fruit," Yeva said quietly. "Read it again, Felix. The future fruit. What do you think ripens?"

He thought of the mark on Wren's back. The sigil matching his scar. *The perfect vessel*, grown for twenty years in some crèche beneath the mountain, seeded from his own sold soul and planted, at the appointed hour, on the doorstep of the one man in the world who might love it enough to come and die for it.

"No," he said. "No. The child isn't—I didn't sell *them*. I sold *mine*. Mine—"

"Yours," Doss agreed. "And everything yours would ever become. Every choice. Every year. Every word you'd ever speak and everyone who'd ever speak back to you." The quartermaster's shade flickered, and for a moment Felix saw through him to the fire, to the wall, to the snow beyond the broken roof. "The Court doesn't buy coins, Felix. It buys orchards. And it planted yours in a valley where magic can't follow, and waited for it to walk out."

Piet drifted close. Up close his edges were worse—fraying, translucent, a boy worn thin by two decades of being remembered wrongly. "Does it help," the boy asked, "if we tell you we're not angry? Mostly. Doss is angry. Doss is always angry. But the rest of us—" He shrugged, and the shrill gesture cost him; he thinned almost to nothing and gathered himself back. "The rest of us are glad you came. Even knowing what it cost you. Even knowing what you cost."

"That's not loyalty, Piet," Felix said hoarsely. "That's the lie still working. On all of us. I built my whole life on the idea that you lived—that somewhere you were farmers, smiths, fathers—"

"Some of us are farmers," Tamris said, grinning his old grin, and for a moment it was unbearable how alive he seemed. "Ghost-farmers. We grow memories. Terrible yield. Bad soil."

But Yeva wasn't smiling. She stood apart, arms folded, looking at Felix the way she used to look at maps before a bad crossing. "You need the way into the mountain," she said. Not a question. "Through the three gates. Past the Queen. To the vault."

"Yes."

"Then hear the truth all the way through, Captain's orders, and then take it." She stepped closer, and her shade threw no heat, only a cold that smelled faintly of the pass—of iron and snow and the specific copper smell of a battlefield at dusk. "The Gate of Names owns whoever speaks true. The Gate of Debts takes collateral. The Gate of Silence is where even gods go deaf. But there is a fourth road, an old one, a smuggler's cut through the roots of the mountain, and it opens for the dead and for those the dead vouch for." She looked back at her company—at her ragged, fraying, faithful dead. "Eleven of us were written into that contract. Six of us are left with enough substance to matter. And we can spend what's left."

"Spend it on what?" Felix asked, though he already knew, and the knowing was a knife turning slowly.

"On you," Maren said simply. "On the child. On finishing the job, since apparently nobody else is going to do it properly."

"No." He backed up a step. "No, you've spent twenty years—"

"Piet bet you'd argue," Ashen-or-Ashe murmured.

"Twenty years of *what*, Felix?" Yeva's voice rose, and the fire flared with it, shadows leaping up the curved walls like thrown spears. "Of watching a door we can't open? Of arguing about watches? You think this is survival? We are what's left after the receipt is cashed. We are the *change* the Court gave you and never expected you to spend. Well." She spread her hands, and they were shaking, and Felix saw that under her fury was something older and more tired: relief. "Spend us."

Behind him, Wren spoke in the First Tongue—one soft word. *Veth.* Home. And the fire in the ring of stones burned suddenly brighter, steadier, as if the word itself were fuel, and every shade in the roundhouse turned toward the child with expressions Felix had spent twenty years believing he'd never see again.

Hope.

"Tell us the plan, Wolf," Tamris said. "We'll want first watch anyway. Someone has to keep the ghosts honest."

Outside, far off across the darkening country, a bell began to toll—deep, slow, patient, counting down the days to solstice. And beneath the ringing, so faint it might have been the wind in the birches, Felix heard the mountain answer: a sound like a vault door testing its hinges, like ten thousand sold souls turning over in their sleep, like the Court noticing, at last and with interest, that the wolf had found its pack.
