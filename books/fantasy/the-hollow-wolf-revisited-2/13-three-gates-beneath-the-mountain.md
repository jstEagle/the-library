## 13. Three Gates Beneath the Mountain

The mountain had been growing in the window for three days, and by the fourth it had stopped being scenery and become a fact. It rose out of the winter plain the way a verdict rises out of a long trial—inevitable, patient, indifferent to argument. The locals called it the Greywife. The maps called it nothing at all, as if the cartographers had agreed among themselves to leave the space blank and let the ink dry around it.

Felix stood at the narrow window of the waystation with his palms flat against the cold stone and watched the last light go out of its peak. Behind him, Wren slept in a nest of blankets by the hearth, breathing in the small, whistling rhythm that Felix had learned to read the way other men read weather. The soul-mark on the child's back glowed faintly through the linen when the fever came, a blue-white shimmer like moonlight trapped under skin. It glowed now.

*Soon,* it seemed to say. *Soon, soon.*

"You're brooding," Sef said from the corner where she sat cross-legged with her boots off and her vials arranged on a cloth before her like a merchant laying out wares. "I can hear it. Brooding has a sound. Like a wet log trying to burn."

"I'm planning."

"You're brooding *with* a plan-shaped object in your hands." She nodded at his fists. He looked down and found, indeed, that he was holding nothing, and holding it hard. He opened his fingers. The knuckles ached.

He crossed the room and sat down across from her, and the firelight moved over the hedge-priestess's face—brown and seamed as old boot leather, one eye the grey of river ice and the other, the one she'd traded something for once, he suspected, a milky blue that saw, he had begun to think, rather more than the other. Around her neck, on a cord strung with dozens of tiny glass vials, one vial hung apart from the rest, stoppered with wax, holding a curl of light that was not light. His memory of the sea at Carrowmere. He had sold it to her for her guidance, and he missed it the way a man misses a tooth—not constantly, but at every meal.

"Wren's mark is brighter," he said.

"I know."

"The riders stopped following two days ago, and I liked it better when they followed. Things that stop following you are things that no longer need to. The mountain already knows we're coming."

"Very good." Sef said it without praise, the way a fencing master says *very good* before knocking your blade into the snow. "Then we're past the part where I pretend you have time to rest. Come here. Both of you."

"Wren's sleeping."

"Wren's listening. They're always listening. Half of what's in that child isn't a child." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. Felix went to the hearth and touched Wren's shoulder, and the boy—Wren was a boy today; the child's gender shifted like weather, and Felix had stopped asking—opened one eye, dark and old and calm.

"I'm listening," Wren agreed, and sat up, and the mark on their back pulsed once, gently, like something turning over in its sleep.

Sef pulled a stick of charcoal from her coat and drew on the hearthstones, where the soot was thick enough to take a line. She drew a mountain first, quick and sure, a triangle with its peak sheared flat. Then, beneath it, she drew three lines, one under the other, like a ladder going down into the dark.

"The Hollow Court isn't a court the way your king's court is a court," she said. "No throne room. No banquets. It's a vault with pretensions. Everything in it is stored, catalogued, owed, or waiting. Souls, names, years, promises, the last breaths of hanged men, the first words of dead children. The Court buys what people are desperate enough to sell, and it keeps what people are stupid enough to owe." She tapped the first line. "Three gates between the world and the vault. You go through all three, or you go through none."

"Or we go around," Felix said. "There's always a way around. There was a way around every siege I ever—"

"There is no way around." She said it flatly, without drama, and that flatness was what made his stomach drop. "The Court is not a building, Felix. It's a bargain. The mountain is just where the bargain keeps its furniture. You can walk through solid rock for a hundred years and you'd still be inside it, because the Court's walls are the terms of the contracts it holds. You signed one, twenty years ago. You're already inside the walls. You've been inside them since the night you made your trade. You just haven't walked up to the door yet."

Wren made a small sound. Felix looked over. The child had pulled their knees to their chest and was watching the charcoal lines with an expression Felix had no name for—recognition, he thought. The look of someone seeing a house they'd lived in before they could walk.

"The first gate," Sef said, and drew a circle around the topmost line, "is the Gate of Names."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the winter dark, a fox screamed once and went quiet, as if it too were listening.

"Every soul has a true name," Sef went on. "Not the name your mother gave you—that's a handle, a thing for calling you to dinner. The true name is the shape of you underneath. What you are when everything you've borrowed has been taken back. And the Gate of Names is a door that opens only one way: you speak your true name to it, and it owns you. Completely. Not a servant—the Court doesn't want servants, servants have wills, wills are messy. It wants *property*. You speak, and you become a thing in a case, like the souls in the vault. You will stand in the gatehouse of the Court forever, aware, and unable to move, and unable to want anything, because wanting is not a feature of property."

"And everyone who goes in," Felix said slowly, "has to speak it."

"Everyone who goes in *honestly*." Sef's grey eye fixed on him. "But the Gate of Names is old, and old things are literal, and literal things can be lied to by the letter. It asks for a true name. It doesn't ask whose."

Felix felt the cold come up through the hearthstones and into his knees. "No."

"You have a false name. The one you've lived under in Vell. The one the valley gave you, that the valley believes. It's true in the way that matters—it's *true of someone*. It's been spoken ten thousand times over a farrier's forge, with love, with annoyance, with bread in hand. It has weight. It might be enough to open the door."

"And then?"

"And then the Gate owns that name. And the Gate is thorough, and the Gate is patient, and one day—maybe a year, maybe ten—it will come looking for the person that name belongs to. It will send its bailiffs to every village called Vell, to every forge, and it will ask after a farrier who never existed, and when it finds the man who *borrowed* the name—" She shrugged, and the vials clicked against each other at her throat. "The Court does not forgive bookkeeping errors. It collects them."

"Then it can't be my name either. Not Wren's real—" He stopped, because he didn't know Wren's real name. Nobody did. The child had arrived speaking a dead language and bearing another soul's mark, and the name Wren was the name Felix had given them on the first frozen morning, kneeling on his own doorstep. *Wren. Small brown bird. Nothing to do with anything.* He had named the child the way you name a stray, and it was the only honest thing he'd done in twenty years.

"Then whose?" Sef asked softly.

And Felix looked at Wren, and Wren looked back, and the child said, in the First Tongue, a single word, and then said it again in the common speech so that Sef could hear it too:

"Mine. Give them mine. The false one. I made it up myself, before you found me—I had it in my mouth when the snow was eating my hands. It's the only thing I brought with me that's really mine." The child's chin came up, stubborn, and for a moment they looked exactly like the mercenaries Felix had once commanded, the ones who'd volunteer for the worst watch with a grin because the alternative was admitting fear. "It's a good name. I practiced it."

"You are not spending your name to open my door," Felix said.

"You spent a *soul* to open everyone else's." Wren said it simply, without accusation, which was the worst way to hear it. "I remember the arithmetic. I remember it better than you do."

The fire crackled. Sef said nothing, and her silence had the quality of a held door—she was letting the argument stand because it was an argument they would need to have again, higher up the mountain, with less time.

"We'll come back to it," Felix said at last, and hated how much it sounded like surrender. "The second gate."

* * *

Sef drew the second line and circled it, and this time her charcoal hesitated, and broke, and she had to take up a fresh stick, and Felix understood that the hesitation was the lesson.

"The Gate of Debts," she said. "The Court's favorite. The other two gates are walls. This one is a mirror." She drew two facing arcs, like parentheses, like a mouth about to speak. "It opens onto a long gallery, and at the end of the gallery is a scale, and when you step into the gallery the scale weighs everything you owe. Every debt. Not just coin. The soldier whose death you ordered to save three others. The promise you made and broke to a woman in Carrowmere—"

"Don't," Felix said.

"—the oath you swore to a god who has been silent for twenty years, which is either paid or unpaid, and the Court knows which, and you don't." She said it gently, but gently, from Sef, was only a way of not slowing down. "The gate offers you terms. Pay what you owe, and pass clean. Or fail to pay, and become *collateral*—which means the Court holds you against the debt, the way a lender holds a field. You don't die. You're held. And the interest compounds."

"What does held look like?"

"You've seen it. On the ridgeline. The grey-cloaked riders." She met his eyes. "Every bailiff of the Hollow Court was once a debtor who stood in the gallery and couldn't pay. That's what the grey is. It's the color of a person the Court owns until further notice. They ride and collect and wait and ride, because the Court gives its collateral something to do, and the something is always *more collecting*. The debt never shrinks, Felix. It breeds."

Felix thought of the riders at the valley's edge, the way they'd sat their horses without eating, without sleeping, without *breathing* that he'd ever seen, patient as frost. He had assumed they were soldiers. Soldiers were the easy thing to assume. Everything terrible in the world was easier to face if you called it an army.

"Twenty years ago," he said, "I paid the Court. I paid for my company's lives. That debt is settled."

"Is it?" Sef asked, and the question had no teeth in it at all, which meant it had teeth everywhere else. "You paid *something*. Into the vault. For *something*. But you've told me yourself you never saw the contract. Never read the terms. You were dying in the snow, and something spoke to you in the dark, and you said yes. And now a child walks out of that same dark wearing a mark that matches your scar." She let that sit. "What if the debt isn't settled, Felix? What if what you paid wasn't the price—what if it was the *down payment*, and the Court has been growing the balance ever since, the way a lender grows a field of collateral? What if the Gate of Debts is going to open its mouth and show you the full ledger for the first time?"

"Then I'll pay it."

"With what?"

He didn't answer. The fire answered for him, settling into coals with a sound like a long exhale. With what. With the forge, with the farrier's hands, with the twenty years of small honest mornings that were the only wealth he had left. With Wren, the ledger would say, the ledger had always said. And he would stand in that gallery and refuse, and refuse, and refuse, until refusing was all that was left of him, and the Court would take the husk and put it on a horse in a grey cloak and point it at the next valley.

Wren's small cold hand found his. He hadn't heard the child cross the room.

"Third gate," Wren said, to Sef, in the tone of a soldier asking for the rest of the bad news. Felix was beginning to understand that this was how Wren survived what was in them: they walked into bad news the way other children walked into the sea, all at once, eyes open.

* * *

Sef looked at Wren for a long moment. Then she drew the third line at the bottom of the mountain, deep, deeper than the others, almost off the hearthstone, and circled it, and her hand was very steady now, steadier than it had been for the second gate, and Felix understood that this was the part she had rehearsed.

"The Gate of Silence," she said. "And here I have to tell you the truth, and the truth is that I don't understand it, and I have walked pilgrims to the foot of this mountain for forty years and I have never once gone past the second gate, because I always found a reason, and the reason was always that I was afraid." She wiped her hands on her coat, leaving grey smears. "The Gate of Silence is the last door before the vault. And it is called that because past it, the gods cannot follow. Not the little gods on the roadside. Not the river mothers, the hearth-fathers, the nameless ones in the wells. Not the Court's own masters, whatever they are. *Nothing* that speaks can cross. The gate eats voice. All voice. Prayer, curse, command, song. A man goes through it and comes out the other side unable to say his own name even if he wants to—and remember what the first gate does to names. Past the Gate of Silence, you are alone in a way that nothing in the world is alone. Whatever you find there, you find without help. Whatever you do there, you do without witness. Even mine." She touched the vial at her throat, the one with his sea in it. "Even the gods'."

"The god I swore to," Felix said. The words came out of him slowly, like stones hauled up from a well. "The one that's been silent for twenty years. The one whose silence I built my whole life on top of, the way you build a house on a grave. If I go past that gate—"

"He can't follow you."

"And if I'm standing in the vault, and the Court is taking Wren's soul out of its case, and the Queen is offering me whatever it is she's going to offer me—there will be no voice in the dark. No sign. No forgiveness. Nothing but me and what I choose."

"Yes."

"And if I die there, I die unheard."

"Yes."

Felix sat with that. Outside, the wind had come around to the north and was worrying the waystation's shutters, and the Greywife stood against the last of the light like a closed fist, and Wren's hand was still in his, small and cold and gripping hard, and Felix realized that the child was trembling—not with fear of the gates, he thought, but with something else. Recognition again. Homesickness, the terrible kind, for a place that had never been home.

"Good," Felix said.

Sef blinked. "Good?"

"Good," Felix said. "Because every other door in my life, something else was speaking. The god. The Court. The snow. The arithmetic. Twenty years ago something spoke in the dark and I said yes before I understood the terms, and I've been paying for a voice that wasn't mine ever since." He looked at the charcoal mountain, the three gates, the long ladder down into the dark. "The last door is silent. Good. Then whatever I say in there will be the first thing in twenty years that's only mine."

Sef looked at him for a long time, the grey eye and the milky one, and then she did something he had not seen her do: she took off one of the vials at her throat—an empty one, small, plain—and pressed it into his hand.

"For the road past the last gate," she said. "Pilgrim's custom. You carry an empty vessel into the silence, and you fill it with whatever you find there. A word. A breath. A name. Most come back with it empty." Her voice roughened. "Some come back with it full and don't know what they're carrying until the day they need it."

Felix closed his hand around the glass. It was warm from her skin, and it caught the firelight, and it was nothing, and it was everything, and he put it on its cord around his neck beside the vial that held his sea.

Wren was already asleep again, or pretending to be, curled by the hearth with the mark glowing faintly through their shirt like a lamp behind a door. Felix lay down beside the child, as he had every night since the doorstep, and listened to the wind, and watched the coals, and did not sleep.

Because in the silence between the wind's breaths, deep under the sound of the mountain, he heard it—the thing he had been hearing for three days and refusing to name. A ringing. Very far away. Very slow. A bell being tested, the way a man taps a hammer against an anvil before the work begins.

The solstice bell, practicing.

Five days, he thought. Five days, and three gates, and one empty vial.

The bell rang once more, far under the stone, and stopped—and in the silence it left behind, Wren spoke without waking, in the First Tongue, in a voice that was not entirely the child's own:

*"He is coming down to us. The Wolf. Open the second gate. He owes."*
