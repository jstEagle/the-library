## 14. The Gate of Names

The mountain did not look like a place where names mattered. It looked like a wound in the world, a black shoulder of stone shouldering up through the cloud line, its lower slopes furred with pine that grew crooked and pale, as if the trees themselves were uncertain whether they had permission to live here. The wind came down off it smelling of iron and old snow, and beneath that, faint as a memory of a memory, something sweeter and worse: incense, or rot, or both.

Felix stood at the edge of the tree line with Wren on his hip and Sef a pace behind him, and he made himself look at it for a long time before he let himself walk toward it.

"You'll want to decide now," Sef said, "what you're willing to lose."

"I've decided everything twice already," Felix said. "Ask me again at the door."

She almost smiled. Her face was a landscape of small weather—lines like streambeds, eyes the grey of river stones—and when she smiled it was always sideways, as if humor were something she kept in her pocket rather than on her face. She carried her vials in a bandolier across her chest, each one stoppered with wax and holding a curl of light that might have been smoke or might have been soul-stuff; one of them, Felix knew, held a morning from his own past, though he could no longer say which morning, because that was the price and the point. He felt the absence the way you feel a tooth pulled: not pain exactly, but a strange new geography in the mouth, a hollow where something used to fit.

Wren's head lay against his collarbone. The child was warm through his shirt, warmer than a child should be, and had been since the ford. Since the hounds. Since Wren had opened their mouth and spoken words in the First Tongue that froze three Court beasts mid-leap, and then collapsed against Felix's chest like a puppet with cut strings, lighter than before, as if some part of them had been spent in the speaking.

"Are you awake?" Felix murmured.

"Mm." A small hand found the collar of his coat and gripped it. "The mountain is loud."

"It hasn't said anything yet."

"It's thinking about us." Wren's voice was thin and clear, a bell rung in an empty room. "It thinks in doors."

Felix looked at Sef. "Doors?"

"The Court has three gates," she said. "You know this. I told you at the fire, and you didn't sleep after, so don't pretend you've forgotten."

He hadn't forgotten. He'd been turning the knowledge over all night like a stone in the hand, looking for the side of it that wasn't sharp.

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon. The pines thinned. The light went from gold to pewter to nothing much at all, and the cold came up out of the ground itself, rising through boot leather, through flesh, settling into the bones like a tenant who intended to stay. Felix's breath hung around him in clouds that did not disperse but drifted behind him, trailing, as if even his exhalations were reluctant to leave him alone in this place.

Twice they passed shrines cut into the rock—niches no bigger than cupped hands, holding offerings gone to frost: a clay bird, a knot of red thread, a child's shoe worn soft as cloth. Small gods' country. At the second shrine, the little god inside it stirred when Felix passed, and he felt it the way you feel someone watching from a window—a pressure between the shoulder blades, a whisper too quiet to be words.

*Wolf,* it seemed to say. *Broken Wolf. We know what walks up our mountain.*

"Let it watch," Felix said, to no one, to Wren's hair, to himself.

Sef heard anyway. "Reputation is a kind of name," she said. "Remember that, when we reach the first gate. The Gate of Names doesn't only want what your mother called you. It wants everything the world calls you. Every handle by which you can be gripped."

"And if I give it none?"

"Then it takes you apart looking for one." She walked on, staff clicking on stone. "I've seen what comes back out of the Gate of Names when it finds nothing. Not much comes back. And what does is mostly hands."

Wren stirred against Felix's neck. "What's a gatekeeper?"

"A servant of the Court," Felix said. "Older than most gods, probably. Made of rules instead of flesh."

"Do I have to be polite?"

Felix laughed despite himself—a short, startled sound, swallowed quickly by the wind. "Where did you learn about politeness?"

"Marta says you catch more with honey than with knives." Wren yawned. "But she also says sometimes you need the knife."

"Marta," Felix said, "is wiser than anyone in Vell has any right to be."

The path bent around a shoulder of rock, and there it was.

* * *

The Gate of Names stood in a cleft of the mountain like a tooth in a jaw. It was not built so much as grown—or perhaps carved by something that thought in anatomy rather than architecture. Two pillars of bone-white stone, veined with grey like old ivory, rose thirty feet and curved toward each other without meeting, leaving a gap barely wide enough for two people abreast. Between them hung a curtain of darkness that was not shadow. Shadow has depth. This had none. It was simply absence, flat as painted night, and it moved—not rippling, but *turning*, slowly, like a page being considered.

Before it, on a plinth of the same ivory stone, sat the Gatekeeper.

Felix had expected a monster. What he saw was worse in every particular: a figure of human proportions, robed in grey so fine it seemed woven from fog, its face smooth and featureless as an egg—no, not featureless. Featureless the way a mirror is featureless. As Felix watched, the blank face shifted, and for one vertiginous moment he saw his own features there, faintly, like a reflection in still water. Then Marta's face. Then Sef's. Then a face he did not know—young, laughing, half-remembered—and then blankness again.

"It wears whatever looks at it," Sef murmured. "Don't stare. Staring is an introduction."

The Gatekeeper's head turned toward them. There were no eyes, but Felix felt seen nonetheless—felt catalogued, weighed, entered into some ledger whose pages he could hear turning in the silence.

"Three travelers," the Gatekeeper said. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone and the dark and the space behind Felix's teeth. It was a pleasant voice. That was the worst thing about it. "One guide, contracted and paid. One child, marked. One man, marked deeper."

"We seek passage to the Court," Sef said, stepping forward. She had told Felix on the climb that she would do the talking, that the Gate respected procedure the way a blade respects a whetstone, and that procedure was the only weapon they would be permitted to carry through. "As is lawful under the old compacts. Supplicants have right of approach."

"Supplicants have right of approach," the Gatekeeper agreed, "upon payment of the toll. The Gate of Names requires a name. Speak your true name, traveler, and pass owned but whole. Refuse, and be searched."

Felix stepped forward.

The Gatekeeper's face became his own face—fully, precisely, the scar over his heart rendered in ivory, the grey threading his beard, the crow's feet earned in Vell's forge-light. Looking at it was like looking into a pond and seeing not a reflection but a rival.

"My name," Felix said carefully, "is not for sale."

"Then you will be searched." The dark between the pillars turned another page. "It is not cruel. Most survive it. Fewer remain themselves."

Behind him, he felt Sef go very still. She had warned him of this. She had laid out the options on the night of the fire like a peddler laying out knives: give the name and be owned; refuse and be unmade searching for it; or find a third road, if he was clever enough and lucky enough and willing to pay a different price entirely.

Cleverness. His whole life, cleverness had been the tool he reached for first—the mercenary's virtue, the survivor's sin. Cleverness had gotten his company into the passes twenty years ago, taking the contract no sane company would take, because the pay was triple and Felix had been young and certain that skill was a substitute for wisdom. Cleverness had gotten him out alive when everyone else died. Cleverness had built him a quiet life in a valley that forgot, and cleverness had kept him invisible for twenty years, and every single piece of it had been purchased with something he could never get back.

And now cleverness offered him one last trick, and it was a filthy one, and he had known it was coming since Sef first described the gate, and he had been rehearsing it in the back of his skull like a man rehearsing his own betrayal.

Because the Gate wanted a name. Any true name. It did not specify whose.

"Wren," Felix said, and his voice did not shake, which he would hate himself for later. "Step forward, little one."

The child slid off his hip and stood beside him in the frost-bitten grass, small and grave, looking up at the mirrored face with the unblinking calm that had frightened Felix since the first night on his doorstep—that calm that was not a child's calm at all but something older wearing a child like a coat.

"What are you called?" the Gatekeeper asked Wren, and its face softened into something childlike, something gentle, and Felix understood that it was doing what it always did: becoming whatever would make the answer come easiest.

Wren looked at Felix. Waiting. Trusting. That was the unbearable part. Not the lie itself—he had lied to better judges than this—but the trust, offered up like a small warm hand.

"Tell it your name," Felix said. "The one I gave you. Tell it true, because it will be true while you speak it."

Wren nodded, solemn as a communicant. "My name is Ash," the child said clearly. "Ash of Vell. Marta gave it to me when I came to the valley, because I was found in the ash of the hearth-fire, and Marta says a foundling needs a name the way a seed needs soil."

Every word of it true. Every word of it a lie. That was the beauty and the filth of it: the false name was real, given in good faith by a woman who loved the child, worn for weeks, answered to, slept in, dreamed in. It was a true name of a false person, and the Gate, which dealt in truths the way a miser deals in coins, could not tell the difference—did not want to, perhaps, because the Gate was not a judge but a scale, and scales do not care what weighs upon them.

The dark between the pillars shivered. The Gatekeeper's borrowed face flickered—Ash's face, briefly, a child's round cheeks rendered in ivory—and then settled into blankness again.

"Ash of Vell," it repeated. "Accepted. Entered. Held."

And then, in the pleasant voice, almost kindly, it added the part Felix had known was coming and had hoped, in the coward's way, not to hear:

"This name is now collateral of the Hollow Court. Should the bearer fail to satisfy the debts carried through this gate, the Court will claim the name entire. It will be sought across the world. Every record that bears it will burn. Every tongue that speaks it will forget. The child called Ash of Vell will become a rumor of a child, and then not even that."

Felix's stomach dropped through the frozen ground.

He had known. He had *known*, standing at the fire while Sef talked, that this was the shape of the trick. Give the Gate a name and the Gate holds it. Give it a false name and the Gate holds a false name—but a held name is a hunted name, and the hunt would not distinguish between the name and the child who wore it. Somewhere behind them, in the world they had left, the machinery of the Court was already beginning to turn, clerks of bone and ink opening ledgers, setting quills to parchment, writing *Ash of Vell* into the great book of things owed. If they failed—if they died in the vaults, if the solstice sealed before they climbed free—then the child would not merely die. The child would be *unwritten*. Marta would stand at her window some evening trying to remember why she kept a third bowl, and never know.

"That's the toll," Sef said quietly, to Felix, not to the Gate. "There is no clean way through. I told you. Every door costs. You chose which cost, and who pays it. That's all choosing ever is."

Felix knelt in the frost and took Wren's small shoulders in his hands. Up close, the child smelled of woodsmoke and fever and, underneath, faintly, of the vaults—of cold stone and lamp oil and the particular stillness of things kept.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm going to teach you a word, and I want you to keep it somewhere deep, deeper than dreams. Can you do that?"

Wren nodded.

"The word is *Vell*. When we're through—all three of us, on the other side—you say it to me, and I'll say it back, and then you can say it aloud whenever you like. But until then, you're Ash, and only Ash, and you don't tell anyone else, not even the nice ones. Especially not the nice ones."

"Why?"

"Because names are handles," Felix said. "And I've spent my whole life making sure nothing had a grip on me. I won't start letting go now, least of all you."

Wren pressed one small palm flat against Felix's cheek. The hand was hot with fever. "You had a grip on you already," the child said. "When I met you. Something had hold of your heart and wouldn't let go. I could feel it through the door."

For a moment Felix could not breathe. Then he gathered the child up, and turned to the gate, and walked into the dark.

* * *

Passing through the Gate of Names was like passing through the moment of falling asleep—the instant when the body tips over some interior edge and the mind, for one terrible half-second, is not sure it will come back. The darkness closed over them without temperature, without texture, without time. Felix held Wren tighter. Somewhere very far away, or very close, a quill scratched across parchment, and he understood with absolute clarity that the sound was the sound of his cleverness being recorded, filed, and set aside for collection.

Then daylight—grey, high-mountain, ordinary—and they stood on the far side of the cleft, and the gate was behind them, and nothing had happened to them at all except that they had paid.

Sef came through last. She paused at the threshold, took a small vial from her bandolier, and poured a single drop onto the ivory plinth—an offering, or a fee, or a habit; she did not explain and Felix did not ask. Then she joined them, and the three of them stood in the wind, alive, unnamed, owed.

Below them, the mountain opened downward into gloom: a stair cut into the living rock, descending past the reach of any lantern, toward the second gate and the third and the cathedral of bone-white vaults where sold souls burned like lamps.

Felix looked back once at the Gate of Names. The Gatekeeper had not moved. Its blank face was turned toward the world beyond the mountain—toward Vell, toward the lowlands, toward the wide green world where a child named Ash of Vell now existed as a debt written in a book.

*Hunting my lie,* Felix thought. *Chasing a name I invented around the world like a dog chasing a stick I threw.*

It should have felt like victory. It felt like the ford, like the hounds, like every clever thing he had ever done: survival with a bill attached, interest accruing in the dark.

"Come on," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he meant it to. "Two gates left. Let's see what else I can spend that isn't mine."

Wren, on his hip, said nothing. But the child's hand crept up under his collar, over his heart, and rested there—light as a bird, hot as a coal—directly above the scar, directly above the mark, and did not move for a long, long time.

And far below, at the edge of hearing, the mountain began, very softly, to ring.
