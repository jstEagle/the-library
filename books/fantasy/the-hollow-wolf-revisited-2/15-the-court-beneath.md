## 15. The Court Beneath

Wren had known the mountain before they ever reached it.

Not the way Felix knew it—Felix knew things in the shape of fear, all edges and old blood, knowledge worn like a stone in a boot. Wren knew it the way you know a song you have never heard sung aloud, the way your mouth knows the shape of a word that has never been spoken to you. The mountain was in Wren's chest before Wren's feet ever touched its slopes. It had always been there. That was the whole trouble.

*You are coming home,* said the not-voice, the one that lived behind Wren's eyes and was not Wren, except on certain nights when the boundary between them went soft as breath on glass.

*I'm coming to steal something,* Wren answered, because Felix had said *steal*, crouched at the tree line with his hand warm on Wren's shoulder, and Wren had decided to believe him. Believing Felix felt like holding something sharp by the blade. You could do it. You just had to be careful.

*Yes,* the not-voice agreed, gentle as snowfall. *And what do thieves take, little lantern?*

*Things that aren't theirs.*

*Hmm.* A pause like the space between two heartbeats. *We shall see.*

The stair down began where the last gate's light died behind them—a seam in the rock so narrow Felix had to turn sideways, dragging Wren after him by the wrist. Sef had gone no farther than the Gate of Debts; she had pressed her glass vial of Felix's memory against her lips like a kiss and said, *No one who carries my payment goes past the second gate. I'd lose my trade.* Then she had looked at Wren for a long moment with her milky eye and said something in the First Tongue, very quietly, and Wren had understood it without trying, which was itself a kind of terrible: *When you see it, don't touch it until you know whose reaching it is.*

Wren had not told Felix what she'd said. There were too many things Wren did not tell Felix, and every one of them sat inside Wren like swallowed stones—smooth, cold, increasing in number.

The seam widened. The walls changed.

That was the first thing: the walls stopped being walls. Rock gave way to something pale and grained, and Wren put a palm flat against it as they descended, and it was warm. Bone. Not bone either—the Court was older than any body large enough—but bone the way ivory is bone, the way teeth are, the way a pearl is an oyster's grief made smooth. The passage had been *grown*. Ribs of it arched overhead, and between the ribs hung lamps with no flame in them, only a slow pulsing glow, amber and dim, like embers seen through closed eyelids.

They flickered as Wren passed beneath them.

All of them. In sequence. Like faces turning.

"Did you see—" Felix started, and stopped, because he had seen. His voice in the dark sounded scraped thin. "The lights."

"They're saying hello," Wren said, before thinking better of it.

Felix's grip on Wren's wrist tightened, then deliberately loosened, finger by finger, the way he did when he was frightened and didn't want Wren to know. Wren knew all of Felix's ways by now. That was another stone inside.

"How do you know that?"

"I don't," Wren said honestly. "I just know it's true."

The stair ended, and the Court opened.

* * *

It was a cathedral. It was a granary. It was a hive and a harvest and a library, and none of those words were right, and Wren stood at the top of the final steps with Felix's hand now clamped over Wren's mouth—not silencing; Wren hadn't been going to scream—and understood that human language simply ended here, the way a map ends at the sea.

The vaults rose in tiers, hundreds of them, thousands, spiraling down into a depth that had no bottom, only a deepening amber haze like light drowned in honey. Each tier was lined with cases. Each case held a light.

Some burned steady and white, taut as a held note. Some guttered. Some pulsed slow and sleepy, dream-lanterns, souls so old they had forgotten what they'd been and now only glowed the way coals remember fire. One, far below, flickered in a rhythm that was unmistakably laughter. Another wept—there was no other word for it—its light running down the inside of its case in bright slow drops that vanished before they pooled.

"There are so many," Felix whispered. He had taken his hand from Wren's mouth. His face in the lamp-glow was gray. "Gods below. There are *so many*."

*Every bargain ever struck,* said the not-voice, fond as a grandmother showing off a pantry. *Every debt paid. Every soul sold, pledged, wagered, or left in pawn by someone who meant to come back and never did. Look how well we keep them. Look how none of them go out.*

Wren looked. And looking, felt the worst thing yet—not horror, horror was for Felix, horror was a living man's luxury—but *recognition*. The way the lights turned as Wren passed was not menace. It was greeting. They knew Wren. Somewhere under their long burning, in whatever remained of the people they had been, each of them remembered a piece of the same thing Wren carried: fragments of this place, corridors of it, the smell of warm wax and cold stone, the sound the great doors made when they sealed at solstice. Twenty years of someone else's memories had been poured into Wren like water into a vessel, and the vessel had been *made here*, shaped on this wheel, fired in this warmth—

"You've been here before," Felix said. It wasn't a question. He was watching Wren's face instead of the vaults, and his eyes were doing the thing they did when he was doing arithmetic he hated.

"No," Wren said. "Yes. I don't know." The stones inside shifted. "I remember walking down these stairs. But I never walked down these stairs. I remember the doors closing. I remember—" Wren's hand rose without permission, pointing down, down, into the honey-dark. "*Her*. Sitting on a throne that isn't a throne. I remember her voice saying my name before I had one."

Felix went very still. In the Court's amber light his scarred face looked carved, temporary, like something that might be taken back if the carver changed his mind.

"Which way?" he asked finally, gruffly, as if asking directions to a well.

But Wren already knew. Wren had always known. The knowing pulled, gentle and absolute, like a hook set behind the breastbone, and Wren followed it down the spiral stairs with Felix's boots sounding behind him—*clack, clack, clack*, the loudest thing in the world, because everything else here was quiet in the way sleeping things are quiet—and came at last to the third tier from the bottom, to a case set apart from the others on a pillar of its own, the way you set apart the thing you are proudest of.

* * *

It was small.

That was the first wrongness, the first lurch of the homesick feeling: it was *small*. Wren had imagined—without ever having imagined it—a blaze, a bonfire, something worthy of twenty years of growing, and instead there was this: a light no bigger than a cupped hand, curled on itself in its bone-white case like a child asleep in the cold. Its color was hard to hold. Gold, then grey, then a green like the first day of spring seen through water. It breathed. Slowly. Patiently. With the patience of something that has waited so long it has forgotten waiting is a thing that hurts.

It was Wren's soul.

Wren knew it the way you know your own reflection even distorted—even aged, even altered—because there is always one feature that betrays it. The light had a rhythm, and the rhythm matched Wren's heartbeat exactly. Not similar. *Exactly.* When Wren's heart beat, the light flared. When Wren's heart rested between beats, the light dimmed. Two things beating as one across twenty years and a pane of bone-white glass.

"Oh," Wren said. Just that. A single syllable, falling out of Wren like a tooth.

*There you are,* murmured the not-voice, warm with satisfaction. *Do you see how well we kept it? Fed it on time and silence. Polished it with patience. It has grown so much, little lantern. It has become exactly what we needed it to be. Do you want to know what that is?*

Behind Wren, Felix had gone rigid. Wren could hear it without seeing—heard the change in his breathing, heard the leather of his glove creak as his fist closed. He was reading the case the way he read contracts, hunting for hinges, for seams, for weakness. Later Wren would understand that Felix had recognized the light too, though not by its rhythm. By its color. By the green of the first day of spring. But that understanding belonged to a later hour, and this hour belonged only to the glass and the small breathing light and the pull behind Wren's breastbone.

Sef's warning rose up in Wren's memory: *don't touch it until you know whose reaching it is.*

Wren's hand was rising.

Wren watched it rise the way you watch weather happen—aware, distant, unable to vote. The fingers uncurled. The palm turned toward the case. And the pull behind Wren's breastbone became a *song*, low and wordless, the First Tongue before the First Tongue had words, and every lamp in the tier above them flared at once, and the weeping soul's tears ran faster, and the laughing one went suddenly, terribly silent.

*Whose reaching is it?* asked Sef, in Wren's head, in her cracked hedge-priestess voice.

And Wren—standing before the small gold-green light that beat in time with Wren's heart—did not know.

That was the answer. That was the whole horrible answer, laid out plain as a ledger: Wren did not know whether the hand rising toward the glass belonged to Wren or to the soul, whether Wren was reaching for home or home was reaching for Wren, whether the vessel yearned toward its contents or the contents strained toward their vessel. Twenty years of growing together in separate rooms, and neither of them knew anymore which one was the ghost.

Wren's fingertips touched the glass.

Everything stopped.

The lamps. The pulse of the light. Felix's breath, caught audibly in his throat. Even the not-voice fell silent, and the silence it left was enormous and attentive, the silence of a held instrument an instant before the note.

Through the pane, through the small curled light, Wren felt—it did not speak, it had never learned to speak, speaking was what happened after—it *felt*: a recognition so vast and so simple that it broke something loose in Wren's chest, some bolt or seal that had been set there at the making. It felt like the moment at the ford when Wren had spoken the hounds still, but inward, inverted, aimed not out at the world but back along twenty years into Wren's own marrow. *You came. You came. You came.*

"I came," Wren whispered, and fogged the glass with breath, and in the fog, for half a heartbeat, Wren saw a reflection that was not quite Wren's—older, taller, wearing a crown of woven dark, standing in a hall of bone-white light—and then the fog faded, and the reflection was only a child again, hollow-eyed, hand pressed flat to a case.

"Wren." Felix's voice, rough, close. His hand settled over Wren's shoulder—warm, callused, real in a way nothing else in this cathedral was real. "Step back from it. *Now.*"

"It's mine," Wren said. The stones inside Wren were all singing at once. "It's mine, and it knows me, and I don't know if I'm allowed to want it."

"You're allowed." No hesitation. None at all. Felix knelt so their faces were level, and his eyes were wet and furious and certain. "Whatever else happens down here, whatever they tell you—you're allowed to want yourself. That's the one debt in this place nobody gets to collect."

And Wren believed him. That was the terrible, wonderful thing about Felix: believing him cost nothing, even when everything else cost everything.

Then the light in the case flared—not gently, not in rhythm, but *aloud*, a shout of gold-green—and every soul in the cathedral answered it, thousands of lamps kindling in a wave that rolled upward through the tiers like a wind through wheat, and the Court woke around them with a sound like a vast throat clearing, and from the bottomless dark beneath the lowest tier, unhurried as winter, came footsteps.

*Click.* Bone heels on bone floors. *Click. Click.*

The not-voice returned at last, and it was smiling; Wren could hear the smile in it the way you hear rain in a roof.

*She knows you've arrived,* it said. *She has been setting out the cups.*

Felix drew Wren behind him, one arm barring the child from the stair, and faced the darkness where the footsteps grew, and said, in a voice that shook only slightly, only at the very bottom:

"Stay behind me. Whatever comes up those stairs—stay behind me."

But Wren, peering past Felix's elbow into the rising dark, already knew what would come up them, had known since the doors of memory opened years before Wren was born, and whispered the truth like a prayer in the oldest tongue there was:

"She doesn't want the soul, Felix. She wants to talk to *you*."

The footsteps paused—one step below the lamplight—and smiled.
