## 16. Audience with the Hollow Queen

They came for him in the hour before dawn, when the mountain's heart beat slowest.

Felix had expected chains, or at least hands. Instead there was only a door—bone-white, seamless as an egg—and a voice like frost forming on glass: *The Queen will see the wolf now.*

Sef had told him about the three gates. She had not told him what waited past them. He had imagined a throne room out of his old nightmares, all iron and screaming, but the corridor that unspooled before him was quiet as a chapel. The walls were pale stone veined with something that might have been silver or might have been scar tissue. Lanterns hung at intervals—not flame, not light exactly, but *souls*, each one a soft gold flicker behind glass, and as Felix passed they turned toward him the way flowers turn toward a sun they distrust.

Wren walked beside him. They had tried to take the child away—a bailiff with grey gloves and a face like a closed book had reached for Wren's wrist—but Wren had spoken a single word in the First Tongue, and the bailiff had withdrawn his hand as though from a stove. So they walked together, down and down, past vault after vault, and Felix kept his eyes forward because he could feel the souls watching, could feel them *knowing* him.

*Wolf,* one of them whispered, without sound. *Wolf who broke his pack.*

He had been hearing it since the ford. He was beginning to suspect he would hear it forever.

At the end of the corridor stood doors twice a man's height, carved with a scene Felix refused to study too closely: figures kneeling, figures rising, a mountain opening its mouth. The doors opened on their own, the way mouths do.

* * *

The throne room was a hollow.

That was the only word for it. The chamber was vast and round and empty, its ceiling lost in darkness overhead, its floor polished to a black mirror that showed Felix his own reflection walking on air. And at the center, where a throne should have been, there was nothing at all—only a ring of standing stones, and seated among them, small as a grandmother shelling peas, the Hollow Queen.

She did not look like a queen. She looked like a woman who had once been someone's daughter, worn down by centuries of arithmetic. Her hair was white as the vaults. Her dress was grey. Her hands were folded in her lap, and her eyes—

Her eyes were the worst part. They were patient. Nothing else in this place was patient; everything here hungered or flickered or burned. But her eyes had the stillness of a thing that has already won and is merely waiting for the loser to understand the terms.

"Felix Halloran," she said. Her voice was dry and kind, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "Farrier of Vell. Late of the company of the Grey Wolf. Late of everything, really." She gestured to the stones across from her. "Sit. This will take longer than you'd like."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "You always did. That's how they knew you'd come. Pride is such a reliable compass needle."

Wren pressed close against Felix's leg. The Queen's gaze moved to the child, and for one unguarded moment her patience cracked, and Felix saw underneath it: hunger, vast and old and *tired*. Then it sealed over again, smooth as the vault-glass.

"You have questions," she said. "Everyone who comes this far has questions. Ask them. I am generous with truth—it costs me nothing, and it buys so much."

"The child," Felix said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "What is Wren? What did you do?"

"What did *I* do?" The Queen tilted her head. "Interesting. Most men in your position ask what *they* did. What they signed. What they owed." She unfolded her hands at last, and laid them palm-up on her knees, an old woman offering bread. "But you've spent twenty years asking what you did, haven't you? Every night. I've heard it. Prayers travel strangely down here—they pool, like water in a cellar. Yours pooled deepest of all."

"Answer the question."

"I will answer every question you carry," she said, "even the ones you haven't found words for yet. That is my gift to you tonight, wolf. Clarity. It is the cruelest thing I own."

She rose. Up close she was taller than she'd seemed, and thinner, as if the centuries had been drawn out of her thread by thread. She circled him slowly, and where she passed, the lantern-souls dimmed, leaning away.

"Twenty years ago," she said, "you were dying in the Greymaw passes. All of you. The company of the Grey Wolf—eleven spears and a farrier who'd learned to hold one. Snow to the waist. Wounds going bad. And a god who would not answer." She let that last word hang. Felix's jaw tightened; he said nothing, and she nodded as if he had. "Yes. You prayed. You prayed until your voice broke, and the silence answered instead. And in the silence, something else spoke. Me."

"I remember."

"Do you?" She stopped before him. "Do you remember the *terms*? Truly? Or do you remember the version you've retold yourself ten thousand times, the version that lets you sleep two hours a night instead of none?" Her voice softened into something almost gentle. "Say it aloud. The bargain as you understand it. I want to hear it in your mouth."

Felix's hands curled at his sides. He knew the words the way he knew the scars on his knuckles.

"My soul," he said. "A portion of it. In exchange for the lives of my company. Eleven men and women who were dying in the snow. I paid, and they lived long enough to—" His throat closed. "Most of them lived."

"Mm." The Queen walked on, circling. "And when they died anyway—one of fever, two of old grudges, the rest of time itself—you called it bitter arithmetic. Survival's tax. You told yourself the Court had cheated you, or fate had, or the god's silence had. You never once asked whether the coin you handed over was the coin you thought it was." She completed her circle and faced him again. "Shall I tell you what you actually sold?"

The floor seemed to tilt. Felix braced himself against nothing.

"A portion of a soul," the Queen said, "is not a portion of *you*. Souls are not loaves of bread, wolf. They are seeds. Cut a seed and you do not get half a plant—you get a whole new growing, in different soil, in different season. When you pressed your hand to my altar and gave me 'a portion,' you gave me a cutting of everything you are. Your courage. Your stubbornness. Your terrible, bottomless capacity for love." Her eyes went, deliberately, to Wren. "And twenty years later, that cutting put down roots."

"No." The word came out of Felix flat and cold, a stone dropped into a well. "No. Wren is a valley child. Wren has a mother somewhere, a—"

"Wren has a *vessel*," the Queen said, "grown in the dark beneath Vell for twenty years, tended by midwives who believed they served the valley's founding bargain, fed on prayers the child didn't know were prayers. Wren is the harvest of what you sold me. The soul I took from you that night did not sit in a vault gathering dust. It *grew*. It grew the way all sold things grow down here—toward the shape of their longing. And what does a soldier's severed love long to be, Felix Halloran?" She spread her hands. "It longs to be held. To be kept safe. To be *found by the one who cut it loose*. So we wrapped it in flesh and set it on your doorstep in the first snow, and you opened the door, because of course you did. You have been opening that door for twenty years."

The silence afterward was total. Even the lantern-souls seemed to stop flickering.

Felix became aware, distantly, that he was on his knees. He did not remember falling. The black mirror of the floor showed him a man with his head bowed, and beside that reflection, a small one—Wren, gripping his shoulder with both hands, saying something in the First Tongue over and over, a word Felix suddenly understood with a clarity that felt like a blade: *stay. stay. stay.*

"That's not true," he said to the floor. "You're lying. The Court lies. That's what courts do."

"I have never once lied," the Queen said, and there was something almost wounded in it. "Lying is a tool of desperate powers. I have never needed it. Truth, freely given, breaks men far more efficiently. Ask your god—oh. Forgive me." A pause, delicate as a knife between ribs. "You can't. He doesn't answer."

Felix lifted his head. His eyes were wet and he did not care. "Why tell me this? Why now? You could have taken the child at solstice and let me live out my life believing I was a good man who'd made a hard bargain."

"Because I want something from you," the Queen said simply. "And I have learned that men give anything to protect the story they believe about themselves. So I am taking the story away. And then—" she returned to her stones and sat, folding her hands again, the picture of a woman settling in for tea, "—I will make you an offer."

* * *

"Here is the state of things," the Queen said. "In six days, the winter solstice seals the vaults. Whatever is inside them at the ringing of the bell belongs to the Court absolutely—no trade, no appeal, no god's intervention, for the contracts predate gods' interest in mortal affairs. The child's soul—the cutting, the harvest, call it what you like—is in my keeping. At solstice it will be decanted fully into the vessel, and the vessel will be mine: a creature grown from a mercenary's love, raised on First Tongue prayers, able to walk where magic cannot and speak what the dead obey. Do you understand what I am building, wolf? Your valley bought itself a sanctuary with souls. I have spent those years building the key that opens any sanctuary ever purchased." She smiled, and it was the smile of a ledger balancing. "Every bargain like Vell's, everywhere in the world, ends the same way. With collection. I am merely patient about the paperwork."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you may leave. Both of you. Tonight." She said it lightly, watching him the way a fisherman watches still water. "Walk out through the Gate of Silence, cross the passes, go home to Vell. Raise the child. Teach it horseshoes and honesty. Live your little lives." She leaned forward. "Keep your memories, Felix. Every one. The snow in the pass. Marta's bread. The weight of the child against your chest on the road. Twenty years of penance, intact. All I ask is that you leave what's in the vault where it lies."

Felix stared at her. "You're letting me go."

"I'm *offering*," she corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference is the point. If I take the vessel by force, I gain a shell with a soul rattling inside it, hating me, fighting me, fraying at the seams. But if you *choose*—" her voice dropped, went almost tender, "—if you choose to walk away, knowing what the child is, knowing what waits below, then the vessel comes to me whole. Willing, even. Grown from your love, Felix. Shaped by your abandonment. There is no forge like a father's leaving."

"I'm not its father."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You're its *maker*. Which is worse."

Wren's grip on his shoulder tightened. Felix reached up and covered the small hand with his own, and the touch grounded him the way hammer-strikes used to, the way the rhythm of iron on iron had always ordered a world gone to noise. Think, he told himself. Think like a man planning a fight, not a man drowning.

Because there was a flaw. There was always a flaw; Sef had said so, and Sef dealt in gates the way he dealt in hooves. The Queen wanted his choice. Wanted it witnessed, wanted it *freely given*—which meant the choice had to be real, which meant the door out had to be real, which meant—

Which meant she was afraid of something. Queens of infinite patience did not offer trades unless the alternative frightened them.

His eyes drifted, deliberately, to the walls—to the vaults beyond, the thousands of lantern-souls he'd passed in the corridor, flickering in their cases. And he saw, now, what he had been too broken to see on the way down: that some of the flames leaned toward Wren. That the nearer they had drawn to the child, the brighter they had burned. Sold things, straining toward the one soul among them that was made of the same fire as hope.

"You're afraid of them," Felix said quietly.

The Queen's stillness changed by a fraction. A hairline crack across a frozen lake.

"The dead of your company linger in my halls," she said, "sustained by what you sold. Echoes, angry and fragmentary. They are nothing. They—"

"They know the way in," Felix said. "To the vault. Chapter and ward. Because they've had twenty years down here to learn it, and because they were soldiers, and soldiers case the ground." He rose to his feet, slowly, joints cracking, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he'd once used to rally eleven spears in a snow-choked pass—the low, even tone that made frightened people stand up. "You didn't bring me here to gloat. You brought me here because the bell rings in six days and the wards are thinnest when the debtor stands in the room. You need me to choose. So here is my choice, Majesty."

He stepped forward, and the lantern-souls along the walls flared like a struck match.

"I don't accept."

For the first time in what might have been a thousand years, the Hollow Queen's face moved. Not anger—something older and more startled, the expression of a woman who has just watched a debtors' ledger catch fire.

"Wolf," she said softly, "you cannot steal from the vault. The shades will spend themselves against the wards and be nothing, and you will be ash before the second gate, and the child will be mine either way. You are trading everything for nothing."

"I've done the arithmetic," Felix said. "I'm better at it than I used to be." He put his hand on Wren's head, between the small horns of bone-white hair. "Come on, little one. We're going to go look at some locks."

Behind them, as the great doors swung open, the Queen's voice followed them down the corridor—not angry, not threatening, just terribly, patiently certain:

"The bell rings in six days, Felix Halloran. And every soul in my keeping knows your name."

All along the walls, in their cases, the sold things began to whisper it.
