## 17. The Vault of Sold Things

The corridor behind the Queen's audience hall was not built for walking. It was built for *descending*—a throat of black stone that swallowed the light of their lanterns a step ahead of them and gave it back grudgingly, as if the mountain itself were reluctant to let them see what it held.

Felix went first. Wren's small hand was buried in his, cold as riverbed stone, and he did not let go. Behind them, faint but never fading, came the whisper of his name traveling along the walls like water finding cracks in masonry. *Felix Halloran. Felix Halloran.* A thousand sold things repeating him to one another in the dark, passing his name down the corridor the way soldiers once passed a watchword.

"Are you afraid?" Wren asked. The child's voice echoed strangely here, doubled, as though something beneath the floor were answering in unison.

"Yes," Felix said. "But I've been afraid for twenty years. It gets to be an old coat. You stop noticing the weight."

"That's sad."

"It is." He squeezed the small hand. "Don't grow up to be me."

They walked down.

* * *

The vault announced itself before they saw it: a warmth in the cold, a glow through the final archway like sunset trapped underground. Then the passage opened, and Felix stopped breathing.

It was a cathedral. There was no other word for it. The ceiling arched up into darkness beyond the reach of light, ribbed with bone-white pillars, and between those pillars, tier upon tier upon tier, rising higher than any church he had ever looted or defended, stood the cases.

Each case was glass grown from the mountain itself—smooth, seamless, faintly luminous—and inside each one burned a soul.

They did not look like what Felix had imagined in his worst nightmares. They looked like *lanterns*. Little flames of every color a fire could be and some it shouldn't—amber and green and a blue so deep it hurt, rose-gold, the grey-white of moonlight on snow. Some burned steady. Some guttered. One near the entrance flickered in a rhythm Felix recognized without wanting to: the rhythm of a heartbeat, quick and frightened, beating inside its glass forever.

"How many?" Wren whispered.

Felix counted the nearest rows only, because counting all of them would have broken something in him that he needed whole. "More than there are stars over Vell," he said. "Maybe more than there are people left alive who remember them."

He found himself searching the tiers anyway, helplessly, for faces. But souls don't have faces. That was the horror of it. They were just *light*, nameless and burning, and somewhere in this forest of glass was the piece of himself he'd cut away in a freezing pass twenty years ago, and somewhere else—the case that mattered—was Wren.

"Which one are you?" he asked softly.

Wren didn't answer with words. The child simply turned, slow, the way a compass needle turns, and looked up—not across the vault but *up*, to a tier so high Felix hadn't bothered marking it, where a single flame burned whiter than all the others, edged in gold, pulsing gently like something sleeping and dreaming.

And even from this distance, even across all that dark air, Felix felt the pull. A homesickness that wasn't his. Wren's hand trembled in his, and the child took one involuntary step toward the stair, eyes wide and wet, mouth open on a word that wasn't in any living tongue.

"Wren." He knelt, caught the small shoulders. "Look at me. Look at *me*."

The child's eyes focused slowly, like someone surfacing from deep water. "It's warm up there," Wren said, bewildered. "Why does it feel like home? I've never been there. I've never been *anywhere*."

"I know." His voice cracked on it. "That's why we're going to break it open."

"You can't." The voice came from behind them—from everywhere. Not the Queen. Something older and more mechanical, the vault speaking through the mouths of its own wards. Along the base of every pillar, sigils flared awake, silver-grey, patient. "The cases are warded by contract. The contracts are older than your god. Older than your grief. Turn back, thief, and be weighed."

"No," Felix said.

He drew the farrier's hammer from his belt—the one tool he'd carried out of Vell, iron-headed, honest, made for shaping horseshoes and nothing holy—and the whispering along the walls rose to a hiss.

Then the dead arrived.

* * *

They came up through the floor the way frost comes through soil, silently and all at once. Seven shapes of grey-green light, ragged at the edges, wearing the tattered suggestion of armor Felix had last seen rusting in a mountain pass two decades gone. Brenna first, tall and hollow-eyed, her shade missing the arm she'd lost years before her death. Then Coric and the twins, Ash and Ellery, blurred together as they always had been. Tammas, young Tammas, who'd died at nineteen and still looked it. Old Hesk, captain's knots still ghosted into his faded hands. And last, limping, grinning, impossible—Doss.

"You absolute bastard," Doss said, and his voice was wind through a dead man's helmet, and Felix's knees nearly went. "You *came*. Twenty years we've been stuck to you like burrs, and you finally walk into the one place in the world we can get our teeth into."

"You're—" Felix's throat closed. He had imagined this meeting a hundred times, in guilt and in drink, and in none of those imaginings had the dead looked *glad* to see him. "I thought you'd hate me."

"Oh, we did," Brenna said. Her shade drifted closer, and where she passed, the ward-sigils on the nearest pillar dimmed, flinching. "For about a decade. Then we got bored of hating you. Hatred takes energy, and we're running low." She looked past him, up at the white flame on its high tier, and something moved through her translucent face that might have been tenderness. "Is that the payment? Is that what you bought us with?"

"A piece of my soul," Felix said. "Or so I believed. The Queen says otherwise. She says—" He couldn't finish it.

"We heard," Tammas said quietly. "We hear everything you tell yourself, Felix. We've been living in it." The young shade smiled, sad as a winter morning. "It's a cramped house."

"The wards," Hesk interrupted, captain to the end, his voice cutting through grief like a blade through canvas. "Seven of them. We counted on the way in—we've been circling this mountain since you crossed the valley line, waiting for you to stop being a coward." The old man's ghost fixed Felix with eyes like banked coals. "One ward per soul. That's the arithmetic, Halloran. You always were better at sums than you let on."

Felix understood then, and the understanding went through him like cold iron.

"No," he said. "No. I didn't bring you here to spend you. I brought you here to *free* you—"

"This is how you free us, you great soft fool." Doss laughed, and the laugh was bright and terrible. "We're echoes. We're the shape a soul leaves in a coat. Every year we're less. Another decade and we'd be nothing but a draft under a door. But *here*—" he spread his insubstantial arms at the blazing cathedral of glass, "—here we're worth something. Here, a soul's echo can crack a contract, if it gives everything it has left. Let us do the one thing soldiers ever do that matters."

"Die well," Ash said—or Ellery; the twins' voices had always been interchangeable.

"For each other," the other twin finished.

"Brenna." Felix turned to her, begging now, hammer hanging forgotten at his side. "Brenna, please. I sold *something* to save you. If it wasn't enough—if it was never enough—let me at least carry you out of here whole—"

She reached out and touched his cheek with a hand that had no weight and all the weight in the world.

"Felix," she said. "Sweet, stubborn, guilty Felix. You've been carrying seven ghosts for twenty years. Put us down."

* * *

The first ward broke over Brenna like a wave over a rock.

She walked to the pillar at the vault's heart, laid both ghost-hands against the burning sigil, and pushed. The contract fought her—he saw it, a writhing of silver script, clauses and conditions snapping at her like dogs—but she was a soul-echo standing inside a cathedral built of soul-stuff, and she gave herself to the breaking of it the way she'd once given herself to holding a bridge at Cael Ford: completely, laughing, swearing at the enemy in three languages.

The sigil cracked. Brenna blazed—one instant of unbearable brightness, her true face surfacing through the echo, young and whole-armed and *relieved*—and then she came apart into motes of light that rose, unhurried, toward a ceiling no one had ever built for them to reach.

"Tell my sister," her voice said from everywhere and nowhere, growing fainter, warmer, "the ford held. Tell her it *held*."

Felix stood with tears running freely down his face and did not wipe them away. Wren pressed against his side, watching the lights rise with an expression far too old for the small face.

"They aren't sad," the child said wonderingly. "Why aren't they sad?"

"Because they finally get to put the pack down," Felix managed.

Coric went next, and then the twins together, hand in ghostly hand, spending themselves on a single ward so that neither would have to go alone. Their light mingled as it rose. Tammas broke his ward shouting with joy—a boy's shout, a battle-cry, a whoop of pure release—and Hesk went last of the six with military precision, saluting Felix crisply before he pressed his fading hands to the final outer sigil and dissolved mid-salute, which was, Felix thought through the blur, exactly how Hesk would have wanted it.

Which left Doss.

Doss had saved the seventh ward for himself—the innermost one, the master-ward, the lock at the center of the lock, wrapped around the stair that led up to Wren's high-burning case. He stood before it now, rocking on his heelless ghost-feet, grinning up at Felix with that same crooked grin he'd worn the day they'd met, two starving boys lying about their ages to a recruiting sergeant.

"Well," Doss said. "This is the part where you say something noble and I say something crude, and then I do it anyway."

"Doss—"

"You want to know the worst part?" The grin faltered, just slightly. "The worst part is that I remember dying. Not the pain—I remember being *glad*. Because you were alive, and the deal had worked, and whatever you'd paid, it bought the thing it promised. For one second, bleeding out in the snow, I thought: *it worked, Felix got us out, Felix always gets us out.*" He shook his head slowly. "Twenty years I've wondered if that second was worth what it cost you. So go on. Break the case. Let the kid live. Give that second back its meaning."

Felix crossed the space between them and gripped the ghost's shoulder, knowing his hand would find nothing, gripping anyway.

"When the god answers me," he said, "if it ever answers me—I'll ask it to save you a place by the fire."

"Ask it for ale too," Doss said. "Good ale. Not that Vell swill." And he turned, and pressed himself against the master-ward, and burned.

The explosion of light lifted Felix off his feet. For a heartbeat the entire vault was noon—every case, every flame, every sold and shelved soul flaring in sympathy—and through that blaze Felix heard, layered over Doss's last laugh, a sound he had not heard since before the pass: the whole company, all seven of them, singing the old marching song, badly, joyfully, off-key.

Then silence. Real silence. The kind the valley of Vell had taught him to mistake for peace.

Every ward was dark. The stair stood open.

* * *

The case at the top was smaller than the others. Child-sized. The white flame within it burned with a rhythm Felix knew better than his own heartbeat now, because he'd listened to it for weeks—quick, warm, stubborn, alive.

His hammer had never been meant for this. Iron shaped shoes; it did not shape fate. But the wards were dead, and the glass was only glass now, and when Felix swung, the case split with a sound like the first ice breaking on a spring river—a sound, he thought wildly, like the valley waking.

The flame did not escape. It *poured*. White-gold light flooded out of the broken case and across the vault floor and up, and Wren—standing below on the stair, arms raised without being told—caught it the way dry earth catches rain. The light sank into the child's skin, into the bone-white hair, into the soul-mark on the small back, filling hollow places Felix hadn't known were there until he watched them fill. Wren gasped, staggered, glowed—and laughed, a real child's laugh, ringing off the pillars, and every soul-case in the cathedral flickered in answer, ten thousand little flames leaning toward the sound like flowers toward sun.

"It's me," Wren breathed, hands pressed to their own chest. "Felix, it's *me*, I'm all here, I—"

The bell rang.

Not the solstice bell—not yet, not for five more days. This was the vault's own bell, deep beneath the floor, a single stroke that rolled up through the mountain's bones and out into the dark, and Felix understood it instantly, the way a soldier understands a signal horn: *breach. Intrusion. Alarm.*

Above them, miles of stone upward, he felt the Queen turn her attention like a lighthouse turning its beam.

And all around them, in ten thousand cases, the sold souls began to burn brighter—not in fear, Felix realized, but in *hope*, straining against their glass, and the whisper that ran around the walls now was not his name anymore. It was a new word, passed from flame to flame, gathering strength:

*Free. Free. Free.*

Wren's hand found his again, strong now, warm now, whole.

"Run?" the child asked.

"No," Felix said, picking up the hammer, turning toward the stair that led to the Gate of Silence, feeling the mountain begin to shake. "Walk fast. And if anything gets in our way—"

The second bell stroke fell, and the vault doors at the top of the corridor began, very slowly, to close.

"—we go through it."
