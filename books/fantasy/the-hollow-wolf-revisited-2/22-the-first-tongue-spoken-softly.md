## 22. The First Tongue, Spoken Softly

The first frost of the second winter came on a morning so still that Wren could hear the apples falling in Marta's orchard, each one landing with a soft brown thud like a heartbeat slowing toward sleep.

Wren stood at the forge door with a cup of cider going cold between small hands and watched the breath of the valley rise. Vell had learned to breathe again. That was how the children said it, anyway—the ones born after the solstice, who would never know the old silence that had lain over the valley like a lid on a pot. To them, magic was simply weather. It came up through the roots of the rowan trees in autumn and pooled in the low fields where the mist gathered, and if you were patient and quiet and a little bit lucky, you could catch it in your cupped palms the way you caught snowflakes.

The older folk were slower to trust it. Wren understood that. The older folk had spent their whole lives in a valley that forgot, and forgetting had been a kind of safety, and now remembering crept back into the world like water under a door, unstoppable and a little frightening, carrying things with it from far away.

But Wren remembered forward as well as back—that was the gift and wound of what Wren was—and so Wren knew something the older folk did not: that the valley's new magic was not the Court's old magic come creeping home. It was smaller than that. Kinder. It smelled of bread and hot iron and wet wool. It did not demand. It only offered, the way spring offers, without asking anything back except to be noticed.

"Felix says you're to stop standing in the doorway letting the heat out," Marta called from her kitchen across the lane, "or he says it in so many words, which is to say he's said nothing at all and is waiting for you to notice him noticing."

Wren grinned and drank the cider and went inside.

* * *

The forge smelled the way it always smelled—charcoal and quenched steel and the ghost of horses—and Felix stood at the anvil with his sleeves rolled, working a plowshare that belonged to the Hollis farm, and when Wren came in he looked up the way he always looked up: quickly, completely, the whole of his attention arriving at once like a bird coming to land.

He did not know Wren. That was the shape of their life now, and both of them had learned to live inside its shape the way you learn to live inside a house with one wall missing—carefully, tenderly, keeping the fire away from the gap.

Felix Halloran had come home hollowed. He remembered how to shoe a horse and how to mend a hinge and how to hold a frightened child through a fever-night, because those things lived deeper than memory, in the hands and the spine. But he did not remember the mountain passes. He did not remember the company of soldiers whose shades had spent themselves like coins to open a vault. He did not remember the silent god, or the oath, or the twenty years of penance that had worn him smooth as river stone before Wren ever knocked on his door.

Marta had told him everything, in the end—all of it, gently, over three evenings by her fire—and Felix had listened the way he listened to everything, wholly and without flinching. Then he had sat for a long while looking at his own hands.

"I'm sorry I don't remember him," he'd finally said—not to Marta, not to Wren, but to the man he had been. "Whoever he was. I hope I'd have liked him."

And Wren, who remembered forward, had known even then what Wren knows now: that Felix had gotten the better bargain twice. Once at the Gate of Silence, when the god's single word had unbound the old contract. And once more, quietly, every day since—because the man who remained was not the man who had been, minus his memories. He was a man made almost entirely out of kindness, with the cruelty and the shame and the bitter arithmetic all burned away like slag skimmed off good iron.

"Sit," Felix said now, nodding at the stool by the window. "I saved you the good part."

The good part was this: the first hour of the morning, when the forge was warming and the light came slantwise through the east window full of floating dust, and they read together. Not books—Vell had few enough of those—but the prayers. The First Tongue.

Wren had begun teaching him in the spring. It had happened by accident, the way most true things happen. Felix had been mending the gate-hinge at the shrine by the well—the little stone niche where the village had always left offerings, though for twenty years no one had been sure anyone was listening—and Wren had watched him work and felt the words rise up unbidden, the way they always rose, the old syllables that were less language than music, and Wren had spoken them aloud without thinking.

Felix had gone still. Utterly still, hammer in hand, in a way that made Wren afraid for a moment that the hollow places in him had cracked.

Then he'd said, very quietly: "Say it again. Slower."

So Wren had said it again, slower, and Felix had repeated it in his rough farrier's voice, mangling the vowels, and the strange thing—the thing that made the hair stand up on Wren's arms even now—was that the prayer had worked anyway. Better, maybe, than it ever had in Wren's careful mouth. Because Felix had spoken it the way it was meant to be spoken: not as knowledge but as longing.

That was the secret of the First Tongue, Wren had realized. It wasn't a tongue at all. It was what the tongue pointed at. The Court had used it as a key for locks; the old speakers had used it as a hand reaching into the dark. And the dark—the honest silence below the mountain, where the Queen had crumbled into quiet—reached back.

Now, a year on, Felix could stumble through six prayers. His accent was terrible. His heart was perfect.

"From the top," Wren said, settling onto the stool, and Felix set down his tongs and wiped his hands and folded them the way Wren had shown him, and together they began.

* * *

*Ashen-vel, ashen-vel, keleth im mara*—small god, small god, warm your hands at our fire—

The words went up through the rafters and out through the chimney smoke, and Wren felt them go, felt them the way you feel a stone leave your hand when you skip it across still water. Out over the lane. Out over the orchard where the last apples hung like lanterns. Out past the ridgeline, up the mountain's shoulder, down into the deep and honest dark.

And then—smaller than a sparrow's heartbeat, softer than frost forming—they felt it come back.

Not thunder. Never thunder. The god did not answer in thunder; Wren suspected it never truly had, and that the old stories about thunder had been told by people too frightened to hear what was actually being said. What came back was warmth in the forge that needed no coal. A smell like rain on hot stone. A feeling, brief and unmistakable, of being *known*—the way the valley knew Felix's hands, the way the apple tree knew when to let go.

Felix closed his eyes. He did it every time, and every time his face did the same thing: a slow unbuckling, grief and gratitude arriving together and finding they could share a chair.

"She answered," he murmured, when it passed. He always said *she*. Wren had never corrected him. Perhaps the god was a she; perhaps she was whatever the speaker needed. "After twenty years of nothing, and me not even remembering what I was waiting for. She waited anyway."

"You didn't wait," Wren said. It was an old argument between them now, a comfortable one, worn smooth as a stair-stone. "You built things. You fixed hinges. You took in a frozen child off your step. She heard all of it. Prayers aren't only words."

Felix laughed—a sound Wren had spent the year collecting, hoarding, like the soul-light once hoarded in the vaults below the mountain, except this treasure no one could steal. "Look at you. A year old in this valley and already wiser than me."

"Everyone's wiser than you. Marta says so."

"Marta says a great many things." But he was smiling as he banked the forge.

* * *

In the afternoon, the children came.

They came every third day now, when Felix taught letters at the meeting-house and Wren taught what the meeting-house had no name for. There were nine of them this season—the oldest nearly twelve, the youngest still trailing her mother's apron strings—and they arrived in a clatter of boots and scarves and one runaway goat that Tam Olliver swore was following him on purpose out of spite.

They sat in a half-circle on the warm stones outside the forge, and Wren stood before them, and the lesson began the way it always began.

"Who can tell me what a prayer is?"

"A wish," said Tam.

"A askin'-for," said little Sef-named-after-the-priestess, whom everyone called Sprig because there had to be only one Sef in any room.

"Both right," Wren said. "But here's the truer thing. A prayer is a door you build with your mouth. And the First Tongue—" Wren held up both hands, palms open, the way the hedge-priestess Sef had once held hers, a lifetime ago and only a year ago both at once, "—the First Tongue builds doors that have always been there. You're not making anything. You're just knocking."

"Is the god behind the door?" asked Mara Hollis, who was seven and missed nothing.

Wren considered. This was the question, the one the children circled every time, the one the adults never dared ask aloud. Behind the door. In the walls. Under the mountain where the Queen had crumbled.

"No," Wren said at last. "That's the best part. There's no behind. The god is the door. And the hallway. And the cold air on the other side that smells like morning." Wren crouched down to their level, all of them, the whole bright flock of them with their chapped cheeks and their curious eyes. "When you pray, you're not talking *to* someone far away. You're letting someone far away talk *through* you. Like singing into a well and hearing the well sing back."

"Did it hurt," asked Sprig, "when you were in the vault? Marta says you were in the vault."

The children always asked. They were too young for tact and exactly the right age for truth, and Wren loved them for it.

"Yes," Wren said. "It hurt like homesickness hurts. Like missing a home you've never seen. But here's what I learned down there, and you should keep it forever: things that are taken can be given back. Things that are broken can be sung back together. Nothing the Hollow Court owned stayed owned. Not the souls in the lantern-cases. Not the valley. Not even the Queen herself—she just forgot, is all. Forgot so long she turned into the forgetting." Wren touched the place at the center of their chest, where the soul-sigil had burned and now, faint as an old scar, still shimmered when the light was right. "So we don't forget. That's the whole lesson. We remember out loud, together, and remembering out loud is the strongest magic there is."

Tam raised his hand, which he did even outdoors, even for goats. "Can we try the knocking one? The short one?"

"The short one," Wren agreed, and the children scrambled into their circle, knees touching, palms open, and nine small voices began the shortest prayer in the First Tongue—the one that meant, as near as translation could reach, *here, here, here, we are here*.

They spoke it badly. Their vowels scattered like startled birds. Sprig sneezed halfway through and Tam giggled and Mara elbowed them both.

And the god answered anyway. Of course the god answered. It came as a shiver of warmth through the circle, as the smell of bread from Marta's kitchen though Marta hadn't baked today, as a single rowan leaf spiraling down out of the clear sky to land precisely in the middle of them, red as an ember.

The children gasped. Then they screamed with joy. Then the goat screamed too, for reasons known only to goats.

From the forge door, Felix watched with his arms crossed and his heart—Wren could see it, could always see it, plain as a lit window—wide open.

* * *

That evening Marta came to the forge carrying a pie the size of a shield, and the three of them ate at the long table while the frost silvered the windows, and afterward Marta brought out the ledger.

It was the same ledger she had shown them a year ago, in the worst season, when the truth of the valley's founding had lain between them like a drawn knife. The founding bargain. The souls paid, generation after generation, to keep Vell magic-proof and safe. Wren's name written at the bottom of the newest page in ink that had dried the color of old blood.

Only now Marta had turned to a fresh page. At the top, in her strong crooked hand, she had written:

*Debts of the Valley of Vell.*

And beneath it: *Paid in full. Witnessed by the god, the wolf, and the child. Let this book hold gifts from now on, and only gifts.*

"What'll we put in it first, then?" she said. "A clean ledger wants filling. That's just sense."

Wren thought about it. Thought about the hounds frozen mid-leap at the ford. About Sef the hedge-priestess, somewhere on her endless roads, one glass vial lighter of Felix's memories and richer, Wren suspected, than she'd ever admit. About the shades of the old company, dissolving gratefully into peace, their loyalty finally set down like packs carried too long. About the ember-souls rising from the Court's ruin like sparks from a bonfire, going home to bodies and bellies and births all across the world.

About Felix, at this table, passing the salt to Marta without being asked, laughing at a joke he couldn't possibly know the origin of because the joke had died with the man who made it—and laughing anyway, because laughter lived in the hands and the spine, deeper than memory.

"Write down the leaf," Wren said.

Marta frowned. "What leaf?"

"The one that fell into the circle today. Red as an ember, out of a clear sky, into the middle of nine praying children." Wren smiled. "First gift received. Put it on record. Let the ledger start the way it means to go on."

Marta wrote it. Her pen scratched in the lamplight, and outside, the frost ticked against the shutters, and somewhere beneath the mountain the honest silence held—all the way down, all the way through, a silence with a god inside it instead of a hunger.

* * *

Later, when Marta had gone home and the forge-fire was banked to a red breathing glow, Wren climbed the loft stairs to the room above the smithy, where the window faced the mountain.

Felix came up a while after, as he always did, to say goodnight. He stood in the doorway a moment, filling it, smelling of charcoal and cold air, and Wren pretended to be asleep, and Felix pretended to believe it, and this too was a prayer, though neither of them named it—one said silently every night: *you are safe, I am here, the door will hold.*

Then footsteps on the stairs, descending. The click of the banked forge. The small sounds of a man keeping watch over a valley that no longer needed watching, because some habits are love wearing work clothes.

Wren lay in the dark and listened forward.

That was the thing no one else could do, the thing that made Wren strange even now, even beloved: Wren could hear tomorrow coming the way you hear rain approaching over fields—a softness, a gathering. And what Wren heard, lying in the loft above the forge with frost on the glass and a god humming in the stones, was this:

Spring. A green so loud it was nearly a sound. Children running down the lane with new prayers in their mouths, bad vowels and all. A wedding, maybe—Marta would deny everything, but Wren had heard forward, and Marta laughed more these days, and there was a widower in the next valley with kind eyes and a good orchard. Travelers on the road, coming to Vell not to hide but to learn, because word had spread on the hedge-priestess's roads that there was a valley where the god answered children, and people were hungry for that the way the hungry are hungry.

And further—years out, soft as a rumor—another knock on another door in another town, a child speaking words no living tongue should know. But this time, when the villagers gathered around the frightened foundling, someone would say: *take them to Vell. There are people there who understand.*

Wren smiled in the dark and whispered the short prayer into the pillow, the smallest voice in the smallest hours:

*Here. Here. Here. We are here.*

And from everywhere—from the rowan trees and the frost and the deep kind dark beneath the sleeping mountain—came the answer, warm as a hand on the head, familiar as a heartbeat:

*I know. I have always known. Welcome home.*
