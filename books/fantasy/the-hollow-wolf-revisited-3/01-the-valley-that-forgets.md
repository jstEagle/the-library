## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The mortar knew the sound of her hand before she did.

Silas Sandoval had ground ink in the Temple of Quiet Hours for eleven years, and in all that time the pestle had never once skipped, stuttered, or betrayed her. It was the most honest relationship in her life. She pressed it into the dried gall-and-soot cake with both palms and turned it clockwise — always clockwise, because Brother Anselm insisted counterclockwise grinding made scripture melancholy, and Silas had long ago stopped arguing that ink could not read its own direction.

The scriptorium smelled of tallow, cold stone, and the particular dusty sweetness of vellum that had been waiting longer than most kingdoms. Forty desks in forty rows, each with its sloped board and its little clay pot and its window slit just wide enough to admit a knife-blade of light. In summer the light was gold. In winter it was a pale gray apology.

This was a winter morning.

"Mind your margins," said Brother Anselm, drifting past her desk the way he drifted past everything: like a man perpetually late for an appointment he had made with himself. He was ninety if he was a day, and his hands shook so badly he hadn't been permitted to touch a quill in twenty years, which everyone agreed was a mercy, since his last attempt had produced a psalm in which the word "beloved" was rendered as "beetroot."

"They're straight," Silas said.

"They were crooked yesterday."

"Yesterday you'd had the fever."

"Fever sharpens the eye." He moved on, trailing the smell of camphor and grievance, and paused at young Novice Tam's desk to confiscate something. From the angle of Tam's shoulders — hunched, guilty, ears going red as sealing wax — Silas guessed it was the drawing he'd been hiding inside his copy of the Litanies. Tam drew dragons. Everyone under fifteen drew dragons; it was practically a symptom. The temple's official position on dragons was that they had never existed, and the unofficial position, held by every child who had ever heard the old shepherd songs, was that they had absolutely existed and probably still did, somewhere over the mountains where the air got strange.

Over the mountains. The words sat in Silas's chest like a swallowed stone.

She looked at them anyway, through her window-slit. You could see the pass from the scriptorium if you stood and craned — a notch in the eastern ridge where the sky came down almost to touch the rock, and beyond it, on certain clear days, a shimmer that the elders called *heat-haze* and never explained further. Nobody from the valley went past it. Not because of guards, exactly. There were no guards. There didn't need to be.

Magic couldn't follow you into the valley, the priests taught, because the valley had been blessed hollow — emptied of enchantment so that faith alone might fill it. What they meant, though everyone was too polite to say it, was that magic couldn't follow you *in*, but nothing prevented you from walking out into it. Except that no one ever did. The road down from the pass simply wasn't taken. Sheep refused to graze near the border stones. Travelers who came through — there were perhaps three a year, peddlers with sunburned faces and coins nobody recognized — always seemed relieved rather than disappointed to leave again, and none of them could say why.

Silas had asked once. She had been nine years old, newly arrived after her mother's death, standing in the great cold nave with a ledger-clerk's hand heavy on her shoulder, and she had asked why the valley forgot.

Brother Corvane — not High Priest then, only a deacon with beautiful handwriting and a smile that arrived slightly before he did — had crouched to her level. "Because forgetting is a kind of mercy," he'd said. "A wound that remembers itself never heals. This whole valley is one long exhale, little scribe. Wouldn't you rather rest than remember?"

She had said yes. She had been nine, and tired, and her mother's debts had followed her into the temple like a second shadow. Of course she'd said yes.

Eleven years later she was still saying yes, six hours a day, in a hand so disciplined it could have been set by a printer.

* * *

At midday the bell rang for bread and broth, and the scriptorium emptied with the shuffling eagerness of men who had been sitting still too long. Silas stayed. She had learned early that the hour when everyone else left was the only hour that belonged to her.

Not that she did anything scandalous with it. She finished her page. But she finished it slowly, letting the last line take as long as it liked, and while the ink dried she allowed herself the small treason of looking out the window at the mountains instead of down at the vellum.

Today the pass was clear. The shimmer beyond it was doing its heat-haze trick in the dead middle of winter, which meant either the elders' explanation needed work or the world outside the valley needed explaining, and Silas had given up expecting anyone official to volunteer for the job.

"You're doing the thing," said Novice Tam.

She started so badly she nearly blotted the page. Tam was seventeen, all elbows and bad posture, hovering beside her desk with the confiscated drawing somehow already back in his fist. "You're supposed to be at bread."

"So are you."

"I'm conducting an act of quiet rebellion." He unfolded the paper on her desk with the reverence of a man revealing a relic. It was a dragon — of course it was — drawn in charcoal, wings spread, rendered with the clumsy grandeur of someone who had clearly never seen one but believed in them completely. "I found it in the *Annals*. Underneath, I mean. Someone scraped off the writing and drew this underneath, ages ago, and the scraping didn't quite take."

Silas looked. And looked again.

Under Tam's charcoal dragon, ghosted through the scraped parchment like a bruise, there was another shape. Not a drawing. A sigil — a coiled mark, angular, deliberate, the kind of thing a scribe makes once and means forever. It was nothing like any glyph in the temple canon. She would have sworn she'd never seen it before, except some deep cellar of her mind insisted otherwise, insisted with the ugly certainty of a name remembered a half-second too late.

"It's nothing," she said. "Some novice's mischief. Old novices are the worst."

"You went white."

"The light's poor."

"The light's fine." Tam squinted at her with the merciless honesty of the very young. "You know what it is."

What Silas knew was that she had eleven more pages to copy before Vespers, that her mother's debt stood at nineteen years and four months as of last week's audit, and that curiosity was a luxury item, priced out of her reach like foreign sugar and freedom. "I know that if Brother Anselm catches you with that, he'll burn it and assign you the Penitential Psalms until spring. Put it away. Copy your lines. Forget things. It's what we're for."

Tam went, unconvinced, folding the paper into his sleeve where confiscated things apparently lived now. Silas sat a moment longer with the sigil burning behind her eyes, then dipped her pen and wrote *Blessed are the quiet hands* in letters too perfect to have been made by anyone with a heartbeat.

* * *

Afternoon passed the way afternoons always did in the Temple of Quiet Hours: like honey off a cold spoon. She copied the Litanies of Provision, then three leaves of the Ordinal, then the marginalia corrections Brother Anselm wanted entered into the master codex — including one correction that struck her as odd even through the numb routine. A passage in the oldest annals, a history of the founding wars, had been struck through and replaced with a bland genealogy. *Here the first temples were raised upon the bones of the—* became *here the first temples were raised upon the firm foundation of holy ground.* The original word was gone, scraped away, same as under Tam's dragon. Whatever it had named, somebody had decided, centuries ago, that the valley should forget it.

Silas copied the correction without comment. That was the job. That had always been the job: to be the instrument through which the past was tidied. Her mother had died owing the temple two hundred crowns for medicines and burial, and Silas had been handed a quill instead of a bill, and told that six hours of copying equaled one day of interest, and that in roughly thirty years the ledger would show zero and she would be free to go wherever freed scribes go. Thirty years. She'd do the arithmetic sometimes, in idle moments, the way other people counted sheep. Nineteen years and four months. If her eyes lasted. If her back lasted. If the wanting didn't rot something in her first.

Wanting, Brother Anselm liked to say, was the tax the faithful paid on having eyes.

She wondered, not for the first time, whether he actually believed it or merely found it convenient, and then put the question away with all the others, in the drawer where she kept the shimmer over the pass and the scraped-out word and the sigil that wouldn't stop glowing faintly behind her eyelids.

The bell rang for Vespers.

* * *

The nave at evening was Silas's favorite lie. By day the Temple of Quiet Hours was administrative — ledgers, audits, the dry machinery of debt — but at Vespers the hundred candles came up and the stone drank the light and turned warm as bread crust, and for one hour the building pretended to be what it claimed. The brothers and sisters filed in. The choir began the opening versicle, voices braiding up into the dark rafters:

*O Hollow Valley, keep us from remembering.
O Quiet Hours, hold us in your keeping.
What burns beyond the border, let it burn alone.*

Silas sang the responses on autopilot, her body kneeling, her voice obedient, her mind doing what it always did at Vespers — floating up along the vaults, out through the clerestory, east toward the pass, toward the impossible winter shimmer. Someday, she thought, in the vague way you think about things that will never happen. Someday when the ledger reads zero.

That was when the knocking started.

It came from the western door — the pilgrim's door, disused so long that ivy had welded it half-shut — three blows, uneven, small. The kind of knocking a person makes when their knuckles barely have strength left to make it.

The choir faltered. Brother Anselm, presiding tonight, frowned his way down the steps, and two of the stronger brothers went with him, torches lifted. The bar groaned. Ivy tore. Cold air poured into the nave like water into a hull, carrying the smell of snow and pine and — faintly, absurdly, unmistakably — woodsmoke and scorched feathers.

On the threshold, mud to the waist, hair in gray-black snarls, barefoot despite the frost, stood a girl of perhaps eight years.

And she was talking. Not crying — talking, urgently, pleadingly, in a language Silas had never heard and yet, in the marrow-deep way that has nothing to do with learning, almost understood. The syllables rose and fell like weather. One phrase repeated, over and over, the girl's cracked voice pushing it out like a hand pushing open a door:

Something like — *the fire is not mine to keep. Something like — please, before they come.*

High above the altar, one of the hundred candles guttered sideways, all at once, as though a breath had crossed the entire nave — though every door was shut.

Beside Silas, old Sister Betha whispered the only sensible sentence spoken that night: "*That's no tongue of any kingdom I know.*"

No, Silas thought, heart slamming against her ribs like a trapped bird. No kingdom at all. Older. The words were older than kingdoms, older than the temple, older than the tidy corrected histories on her desk — and she could nearly understand them, which was impossible, which was forbidden, which felt, for one vertiginous instant, like remembering.

Then High Priest Corvane rose from his chair beneath the eastern window, and his smile arrived, as it always did, slightly before he did.
