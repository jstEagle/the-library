## 2. A Knock at Vespers

The bell for Vespers rang the way it always did — three slow strokes, then a pause, as if the bell itself were gathering its courage before committing to the rest.

Silas Sandoval had heard that bell eleven thousand times, give or take. She had done the arithmetic once, late at night, and then felt foolish about it. Eleven thousand bells, and every one of them had meant the same thing: put down your pen, wash the ink from your fingers, go and kneel with the others and ask the Quiet Hours to pass over this house.

She put down her pen. She washed the ink from her fingers. She went and knelt.

The chapel of the Temple of Quiet Hours was a beautiful place in the way that a well-made lock is beautiful — precise, cold, and built entirely around the idea of keeping something out. Candles burned along the walls in glass cases, so that even the flames could not touch the air. The floor was pale stone swept twice daily. The windows were narrow and set high, and through them you could see only sky, never the valley below, never the terraced fields or the river or the road that led out of the valley and into the world where, everyone agreed, magic still lived.

Here it did not. Here it could not. That was the whole point of the valley, though no one explained the point anymore; they simply repeated the doctrine, which said: *What is dead cannot be tempted. What cannot be tempted cannot fall.* The founders had chosen this hollow precisely because whatever force moved through the wider world had never taken root here, and the temple had been raised on that emptiness like a house on bedrock.

Silas knelt on the bedrock with forty other souls and recited the Vespers litany from memory, her voice joining theirs in the low communal drone that the acoustics turned into something almost like warmth.

*Blessed are the hours that pass without wonder.*

* Blessed are the hands that copy and do not create.*

* Blessed are we who keep nothing, so that nothing may be taken from us.*

She had copied that last line four hundred times. She knew exactly how many strokes of the pen each letter required. She had never once thought about what it meant until tonight, when a strange restlessness had been sitting under her ribs all day like a swallowed stone.

It had started that morning with the birds. The sparrows that nested in the eaves of the scriptorium had all flown up at dawn in one motion, wheeling east, and had not come back. Old Brother Fenwick had grumbled that they'd gone to raid somebody's grain, but Silas had watched the empty eaves all afternoon and felt the swallowed stone shift.

Animals left the valley sometimes. But they didn't usually leave all at once, in one direction, like something had asked them a question they needed to answer far away.

*Focus,* she told herself, and focused. This was the discipline her mother had drilled into her before dying and leaving Silas nothing but debts: *A scribe's mind is a clean page, Sila. Nothing smudges what isn't there.* Her mother had been a lay sister of modest gifts and enormous certainty, and when the fever took her, she had left behind eleven years of unpaid tithes and a daughter who now worked them off at the rate of one copied codex per season.

Eleven years of debt, six years served. Five to go.

*Five more years of ink and stone and sky-through-narrow-windows,* she thought, and then immediately chastised herself, because longing was a smudge, and smudges got you assigned extra penance copying, which was ironic in a way she refused to find funny.

The litany ended. High Priest Corvane rose to give the evening blessing, and the chapel's attention gathered around him the way water gathers toward a drain.

Corvane was not an old man yet, but he carried himself like one — unhurried, deliberate, his gray-streaked hair cut close and his vestments always falling in perfect lines, as if cloth itself respected him. He had a voice made for blessings, low and resonant, and eyes that never quite participated in the serenity of his face. When he looked at you, you had the sensation of being read. Not judged — Silas had decided long ago that Corvane didn't judge, judgment implied the possibility of finding something worthless — but *read*, the way a scribe reads a manuscript, checking for errors.

"Blessed are we who keep nothing," he said, and forty voices answered, "so that nothing may be taken from us."

"Go in quietness," said Corvane. "The Hours will pass over—"

He stopped.

Silas would remember afterward that he stopped mid-word, and that the silence that followed was the strangest silence she had ever stood inside — not the valley's usual hush, which was comfortable and worn like an old coat, but a silence with a hole torn in it. A silence that was being *listened to*.

Then came the knock.

Three strokes against the great doors of the chapel. Slow. Deliberate. Then a pause — the same pause the bell used, as if gathering courage — and three more.

Every head turned. Forty faces, forty expressions of identical scandal, because nothing knocked at the Temple of Quiet Hours during Vespers. Nothing knocked at all, really. Pilgrims had stopped coming two generations ago; the valley road saw a merchant caravan perhaps twice a year; and the doors of the chapel were opened at dawn and dusk by the door-warden according to a schedule that did not admit surprises.

Brother Aldous, the door-warden, looked to Corvane. Corvane, after a moment's consideration — Silas watched him weigh the interruption against the doctrine of hospitality and find, visibly, hospitality wanting — nodded.

Aldous unbarred the doors.

Cold air came in first. It smelled wrong, or rather it smelled like *somewhere else* — rain, woodsmoke, wet earth, and underneath it something faintly mineral and hot, like a forge banked for the night. Silas felt the swallowed stone in her ribs turn over.

And then the child came in.

She was small — seven, maybe eight years old, though it was hard to tell, because she was caked head to foot in dried mud, as though she had been dipped in the valley's clay and worn it home. Her hair was dark and matted. Her feet were bare and bleeding at the heels. She stood in the doorway swaying slightly, the way a candle flame sways, and she looked out at the assembled congregation with an expression of such flat, exhausted defiance that several of the brothers took an involuntary step backward.

On her chest, drawn in ash or charcoal or something darker, was a mark. A spiral, coiled tight, with three lines crossing it like claws.

Silas's breath caught.

Not because the mark was frightening. Because the mark was *familiar*, and familiarity in the Temple of Quiet Hours was a category error. Nothing here was familiar. Everything was either doctrine or debt.

"Where is the child's keeper?" Corvane asked, in the tone he used for rhetorical questions, which was also the tone he used for actual questions.

The child answered him.

It was not screaming, though it had the volume of screaming. It was language — syllables rising and falling with unmistakable intent, a question, maybe, or an accusation — delivered in a tongue that sounded like river stones being argued over by fire. It hit the chapel like weather. Two brothers flinched. Sister Odile pressed a hand to her mouth.

"That is no tongue of men," whispered Brother Fenwick, who claimed to have traveled in his youth, which everyone doubted.

"It is no tongue at all," said Sister Odile. "It is a fit. The child is fevered."

The child fixed her eyes on Odile and said something shorter, sharper, and somehow, impossibly, contemptuous. Then she coughed, swayed harder, and kept talking, quieter now, as if repeating herself for the hard of hearing, as if the fault were surely theirs.

And Silas —

Silas stood very still, because something was happening inside her skull that she had no doctrine for.

She could *almost understand it.*

Not the way one understands a foreign tongue learned from books. It was deeper and stranger than that — the words arrived already half-translated, the way a melody arrives with its mood attached. She didn't know the word for *door*, but she knew the child was saying something about a door, or a threshold, or a thing crossed that could not be uncrossed. She didn't know the word for *fire*, but she knew — with absolute, sick certainty — that somewhere in that stream of syllables was the word for fire, and that it was old. Older than any word she had ever copied. Older than the temple. Older, some animal part of her insisted, than the kingdom whose coins paid her debts.

*That's impossible,* said the disciplined part of her mind, the part shaped by her mother and five years of clean pages. *You have never left the valley. You have copied nothing but scripture. There is no mechanism by which you know this.*

But knowing things without mechanisms was, she understood dimly, rather the definition of magic, and magic did not exist here, and so the alternative explanation was that she was going mad, which was at least doctrinally permissible.

The child's gaze swept the room and snagged — on Silas.

Everything about the girl changed. The sway steadied. The exhaustion sharpened into focus. And those eyes, mud-framed and fever-bright, widened with something that looked horribly like recognition, followed by something that looked horribly like hope, and both of them together were worse than any accusation.

The child spoke again. Directly. To her.

And Silas, standing among forty kneeling colleagues in the most silent building in the most silent valley in the world, understood — almost, nearly, at the edge of comprehension — that the child had just said:

*I found you. I crossed everything, and I found you.*

"No," said Silas, out loud, before she could stop herself.

The word dropped into the chapel like a coin into a well. Forty heads turned back toward her. Corvane's gaze settled on her with the gentle weight of a full inkstone.

"Scribe Sandoval," he said pleasantly. "Something to add?"

"I—" Heat crawled up her neck. "No, Your Holiness. Forgive me."

"You spoke." He tilted his head, reading her the way he read manuscripts. "In response to the child. Interesting. Do you know her tongue?"

"No." Too fast. "I mean — I don't know it. It's only... she seemed to be asking for help. Perhaps."

"Perhaps," Corvane echoed, tasting the word. He smiled, and the smile did not reach the reading part of his eyes at all. "Sister Odile, the child is clearly fevered and clearly lost. Take her to the reliquary — the antechamber, not the vault — and see that she has water and blankets. We will send word to the valley villages tomorrow and discover where she belongs."

"The villages won't know her tongue," said Silas.

The room went quiet again. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words had simply come out, the way ink comes off a pen that's been dipped too deep.

Corvane regarded her for a long moment. Around them, the congregation began, awkwardly, to file out, their footsteps loud on the pale stone, their glances flicking between the High Priest and the scribe who had spoken out of turn twice in one Vespers.

"No," Corvane agreed at last, mildly. "They will not. But *you* may have a talent worth cultivating, Scribe Sandoval. The Hours provide in unexpected ways." He turned toward the doors, then paused. "Come to my study after compline. Bring nothing. We'll discuss your... ear."

He walked out. Silas stood alone in the chapel with the smell of somewhere else fading from the air and the swallowed stone beneath her ribs now beating like a second heart.

* * *

The reliquary antechamber sat behind the sanctuary, a round stone room where the temple's few holy objects were displayed under glass: a saint's fingerbone, a chalice of doubtful provenance, a scrap of banner from a war nobody remembered starting. The wardens locked the child in there for the night "for her own protection," which was the phrase temples used for locking children in rooms, and posted Brother Aldous outside with instructions to admit no one.

Silas came anyway, after compline, carrying a cup of honeyed milk she had absolutely no authorization to bring.

Aldous looked at the cup. He looked at her. He was sixty and kind and had known her mother, and he sighed the sigh of a man watching history repeat itself in an inconvenient generation.

"Ten minutes," he said. "If anyone asks, I was praying."

"You're a terrible liar, Brother Aldous."

"I'm an excellent liar. I'm just bad at sinning." He unlocked the door.

Inside, the child sat cross-legged on the stone floor, wrapped in a blanket, the mud on her face now streaked where tears had cut channels through it. She had eaten nothing. At the sight of Silas she surged to her feet and began speaking — fast, urgent, both hands out, the words tumbling over each other like water over rocks.

"I don't understand you," Silas said softly, setting down the cup. "I want you to know that. Whatever you think I am, I'm not — I'm a scribe. I copy prayers. I don't even feel them. I owe this place five more years of my life, and I've never once broken a rule, and I don't know why you looked at me like that."

The child stopped talking.

She cocked her head. And slowly, deliberately, she raised one muddy finger and pointed at Silas's chest — at the exact spot beneath her ribs where the swallowed stone had been sitting all day.

Then she said one word. Just one. Quietly, the way you'd tell someone their own name.

And Silas didn't know the word, but she knew — the way she had known all evening, the way the sparrows had known at dawn — that it meant *fire*.

Behind her, in the corridor, very softly, a door latch clicked.

Someone had been listening.
