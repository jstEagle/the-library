## 3. Words Older Than Kings

Sleep had never been Silas Sandoval's particular talent, but that night it abandoned her entirely.

She lay in her narrow cell above the scriptorium and listened to the valley breathe. The Temple of Quiet Hours did not creak the way other buildings did. It *hummed* — a low, patient note in the stones, like a monk who had taken a vow of soundlessness and forgotten how to stop. The novices said it was the architecture. Silas had copied enough of the temple's oldest hymns to suspect it was something older than architecture, though she'd never dared wonder too loudly what.

Tonight the hum felt different. Tonight it felt like it was listening.

*The child is still here,* she thought, for the hundredth time since vespers. *Locked in the reliquary like a jar of spoiled honey.*

She turned over. The straw tick rustled its disapproval. She turned back.

It was no use. Her mother's voice — dead eleven years now, but never more than a corridor away — spoke up from wherever mothers kept their opinions after death: *You'll lie there all night chewing on it, Wren-bird, or you'll get up and go look. You always were a girl who had to read the last page first.*

Her mother had called her Wren-bird because, as a child, Silas had been small and quick and prone to darting at things much larger than herself. Nothing about that had changed, apparently, except the size of the things.

Silas got up, dressed in the dark by touch, and took the one thing in her cell she was not supposed to have after hours: the little brass lamp with the shuttered flame, permitted only to scribes working late on commission. She told herself she was going down to check the drying ink on the evening's pages. She told herself this with the same serene conviction with which the temple told beggars that charity built character.

The corridors at midnight were rivers of shadow. Silas moved through them the way she did everything — quietly, correctly, and with her heart hammering out a rhythm entirely improper for a woman of the Quiet Hours. Past the dormitories, where Brother Anselm snored in three keys. Past the refectory, where the great copper pots sat gleaming in the moonlight like a congregation of bald monks. Down the stair that smelled of tallow and cold stone, to the lowest level of the temple, where the reliquary stood behind a door of black iron banded with silver.

Two wardens should have been posted there. She found them asleep — actually *asleep*, slumped against opposite doorposts like a pair of bookends someone had knocked over. That was wrong. Wardens of the Quiet Hours did not sleep at their posts; they were selected specifically for the ability to stay awake through sermons, which was widely considered a sign of divine favor.

Silas crouched beside the nearer one. His face was slack, peaceful, his breathing slow as tide. There was no smell of wine. There was nothing at all — just sleep, deep and sudden, as if the whole temple had exhaled and taken them with it.

*The hum,* she thought again. *Something in these stones wants me to open this door.*

Or wanted the child to be found. Or wanted — and here her mind shied away like a horse at a snake — wanted *itself* remembered.

Silas straightened up, adjusted her lamp shutter so only a blade of light escaped, and let herself into the reliquary.

* * *

The room beyond was long and low, lined with niches, and every niche held a relic under glass: fingerbones of saints who had probably never existed, scraps of robe, a vial of dust labeled *Ashes of the Serpent of the North* in handwriting Silas recognized as her own master's — he'd relabeled half the collection decades ago when the old tags faded, and had cheerfully admitted he was guessing.

In the very center of the floor, where the lamplight pooled, sat the child.

She wasn't crying. Silas had half expected tears, or fury, or the feral wariness of an animal in a trap. Instead the girl sat cross-legged in the thin blanket they'd left her, drawing in the dust of the floor with one deliberate finger. She looked up when the door opened — not startled, not afraid. *Expecting.* As if she'd known exactly which scribe would come down the stairs tonight, and had simply been tidying up while she waited.

"You," Silas said softly, and then, feeling foolish, "hello."

The child tilted her head. Up close, in honest light, she was perhaps seven years old, mud-stained to the elbows, with a scrape healing on her chin and eyes the color of banked coals — not red, not gold, but that impossible in-between color that made Silas's own eyes ache to look at, the way staring at a hearth makes the rest of the room go gray.

The girl pointed at the floor.

Silas looked. And stopped breathing.

The child had drawn a circle. Within the circle, a shape like a wolf's skull stretched into a flame, or a flame folded into the shape of wings — it shifted depending on how you looked at it, which was impossible, because dust does not move. Around the circle ran lines of writing, small and sure and utterly unlike any alphabet Silas knew.

Except she *did* know it. Not with her mind. With some older room in her, some cellar of the self where her mother's lullabies and the smell of ink and the sound of rain on the scriptorium roof were all stored together. The words meant — she was nearly certain, the way you're nearly certain of your own name — *that-which-gives-and-is-not-diminished*. Or possibly *the gift unspent*. Or both. Both at once, the way a single word can hold two meanings when the language is old enough and honest enough to allow it.

"Where did you learn that?" Silas whispered.

The child said something in reply — a short phrase, liquid and strange, consonants falling like water over stones. And here was the thing that made the hair rise along Silas's arms: she understood maybe one word in five, and yet she understood the *shape* of it, the intent, the way you understand a stranger's grief across a market square without hearing a word.

*I know this,* she thought. *I don't know how I know this.*

Because she did, didn't she. She knew it from the codex.

There was exactly one book in the Temple of Quiet Hours written in anything resembling those letters. It lived in the restricted archive, chained to its lectern, and Silas had catalogued it once, years ago, as a junior scribe with more curiosity than sense: *Codex Primordial, provenance unknown, script unidentified, contents forbidden to all ranks below High Priest by decree of the Founding Synod.* She had touched it exactly once — a single page, before Brother Cassian had cleared his throat in a manner that could curdle milk at forty paces.

The sigil on that page had been identical to the one the child was now drawing in the dust of the reliquary floor. Identical down to the smallest stroke. A symbol that had supposedly died out before kingdoms had names, drawn by a mud-caked child who had wandered out of the hills during vespers.

Silas knelt. Slowly, the way you kneel to a deer or a burning coal.

"That mark," she said, pointing. "Can you tell me about it?"

The child studied her for a long moment. Then she reached out, took Silas's hand — her grip was surprisingly strong, her fingers warm, warmer than a child's fingers had any right to be, warm as a stone left too long in summer sun — and pressed Silas's palm flat against the dust, inside the circle.

The warmth went through Silas like a bell struck inside her ribs.

For one heartbeat — one — she saw it. Not imagined; *saw*. Vast shapes crossing a sky that had never been this valley's gray ceiling, wings that threw shadows over whole counties, fire that was not destruction but *speech*, a language spoken in light, and beneath it all a feeling like standing in a doorway with the whole of winter outside and someone you love keeping the fire —

Then it was gone. The lamp guttered. The child let go of her hand.

Silas stayed kneeling, trembling slightly, her palm still tingling. Every discipline she had — every prayer, every hour of copying, every careful small resignation she had built her life out of like a wall of borrowed bricks — told her to stand up, walk out, lock the door, and report a disturbance to the morning chapter.

Instead she heard herself say, in the tone she used for especially difficult passages of scripture, "Right. Then we're going to need more light."

She opened the lamp's shutter fully. In the widened glow, she shrugged off her outer robe, spread it beside the child's dust-circle, and sat down on the cold stone like a girl at a lesson. The child watched her do all of this with an expression that, on anyone else, Silas would have called *satisfaction*.

"I'm Silas," she said, tapping her own chest. "Si-las."

The child considered this solemn negotiation. Then she tapped her own chest and produced a word. It sounded like a crackle and a fall — like the last syllable of a bonfire collapsing into embers. Silas tried it twice.

"Ember?"

A nod. Decisive. Apparently that would do.

"All right, Ember." Silas dipped her finger in the dust. "Let's see if we can talk properly."

What followed was the strangest hour of Silas Sandoval's carefully ordered life. They built a vocabulary out of drawings and patience. Ember drew a cup: Silas mimed drinking; Ember nodded and said a word, and Silas repeated it until her accent stopped making the child wince. Ember drew a stick figure with a frown: Silas offered *sad? angry? lost?* — the third one landed, and Ember's face did something complicated and young. Ember drew a house, then crossed it out. Silas did not ask. Some translations can wait.

But when Silas drew the temple — the simple scribe's shorthand for it, a tower and a bell — Ember went very still. Then she drew the sigil again, the wolf-skull flame, and set her finger upon it, and looked at Silas with those coal-colored eyes, and shook her head slowly.

No. This place and that mark did not belong together. Had *never* belonged together.

And Silas, who had spent twenty years copying prayers about the righteous founding of the Quiet Hours, about the blessed synod that had cleansed the world of serpent-fire and given the valleys peace — Silas felt the first hairline crack run through the wall of her certainty, and heard it, faintly, the way you hear ice shift on a pond.

"The founding," she said aloud, to nobody, to the dust, to the child who couldn't understand the words but somehow understood everything else. "What happened at the founding?"

Ember answered in her water-over-stones language, a full flowing sentence, and gestured — wings, falling, a circle broken. Broken by hands. She drew hands. Many small hands, joined together into one large grasping hand, closing over the flame-sigil and squeezing.

Stealing it.

Silas sat back on her heels. The reliquary hummed around them, patient as ever, and for the first time in her life she wondered whether that hum was comfort — or waiting.

* * *

"There is a perfectly reasonable explanation," said a warm voice from the shadows of the doorway, "for why my two best wardens are sleeping on duty, and I confess I'm looking forward to hearing yours, Sister Sandoval."

Silas came up off the floor so fast she cracked her knee on the stone and barely noticed. The lamp-light couldn't reach the far corner by the door, but it didn't need to. She knew that voice the way the valley knew rain: High Priest Corvane, First Voice of the Quiet Hours, keeper of the restricted archive, owner of the softest boots in three provinces.

He stepped forward into the light, and even at midnight, even roused from his bed, he looked composed — silver at the temples arranged as though it had applied for the position, robes immaculate, smile arranged just so, the smile of a man who found the world consistently amusing and had never once been surprised by it.

"Your Grace." Silas bowed, heart slamming. "I — the child was distressed, I thought —"

"At midnight. Alone. Unchaperoned." He said it pleasantly, the way a man reads charges aloud at a trial he has already decided to enjoy. He walked past her, unhurried, and looked down at the dust. At the circle. At the sigil.

Silas watched his face with the attention of a lifetime spent studying men's expressions for the parts they didn't say. And she saw it — brief as a fish turning under water. Not surprise. Not confusion.

*Recognition.*

Corvane crouched, examined the drawing without touching it, and murmured, almost to himself, in a cadence that was not quite prayer: "So it wasn't a forgery after all. Brother Aldous, you tedious old pedant." Then he rose, brushed the dust from his fingers as though the gesture could also brush away having said it, and smiled down at Silas with renewed warmth.

"She drew this?" he asked.

"Yes, Your Grace."

"And you understand it."

It was not a question, but Silas treated it as one, because lying outright to the First Voice seemed inadvisable and admitting everything seemed fatal. "I recognize it, Your Grace. From the restricted codex. I catalogued it years ago. One page. Before Brother Cassian stopped me."

"Mm. Cassian always did have excellent timing." Corvane walked a slow half-circle around the child, who watched him with an expression of profound, unchildlike dislike. "Do you know, Sister, most people who sneak into my reliquary at midnight come looking for treasure." He glanced at the saintly fingerbones in their cases. "They're always disappointed. But you—" the smile deepened, "—you came looking for a *word*. That's rarer. That's worth something."

"Your Grace, I only —"

"Spare me the apology, it's late and we're both wearing it already." He waved it away with a flick of two fingers. "Here is what will happen instead. Tomorrow, you will be assigned to the child. Feeding her, bathing her, sitting with her — a woman's work, gentle work, well suited to a scribe of your… temperament. You will talk with her. Draw with her. And you will write down everything she says, everything you understand of it, in a report to me alone. No copies. No confiding in Brother Cassian, whom you evidently find intimidating, and rightly so."

Silas's mouth had gone dry. "And in return, Your Grace?"

Corvane paused. Turned. And gave her the full force of that charm, undiluted, the charm that had gotten three abbots to fund the new bell tower and one visiting duke to donate a reliquary he'd sworn he'd never part with.

"In return," he said gently, "we might discuss your ledger. Twenty-two years of debt, isn't it? Your mother's debt, God keep her, transferred to a daughter who never signed for it. Eleven years served. Eleven remaining, at current rates." He clicked his tongue softly. "Debts are such heavy things, Sister Sandoval. I hate to see them carried unnecessarily."

Behind him, Ember had gone very still, watching Corvane the way a rabbit watches a hawk-shaped cloud — not because it understands death, but because something older than understanding has advised caution.

"What would you have me find out?" Silas asked quietly.

Corvane's smile did not move, but something behind it did — something that settled into place like a lock turning. "Only the truth," he said. "Whatever it is. Whatever it costs. Truth is the one relic we never keep under glass, Sister. Come to my study tomorrow evening, after the child sleeps. We'll discuss terms."

He paused at the door, silhouetted against the corridor's dark, and added, almost as an afterthought:

"Oh — and Sister? The wardens. Say nothing of them. Sleep comes to us all in the Quiet Hours; it would be uncharitable to make a fuss." The smile, in the gloom, was mostly teeth. "Good night."

The iron door closed behind him with a sound like a full stop.

Silas stood alone in the humming reliquary, her knee throbbing, her palm still faintly warm, her mind split cleanly down the middle — one half whispering *eleven years, gone, just like that, freedom*, and the other half replaying that tiny underwater flicker of recognition on his face, and asking the question she was beginning to suspect would follow her to her grave:

*If the sigil in the codex was real — then what else in our oldest books was true?*

And what, exactly, had the founding synod done with all that stolen fire?

Beside her, in the dust, Ember quietly rubbed out the drawing with the flat of her hand — all except the sigil itself, which she traced once more, deeper, before covering it too. As if to say: *remembered, not erased.*

Silas blew out the lamp. Somewhere above them, the temple hummed on, and for the first time it sounded less like a hymn and more like a held breath.
