## 4. The Ledger of Debts

High Priest Corvane had a theory about ink.

Most men thought of it as a tool. A few — poets, mostly, and other useless creatures — thought of it as blood. But Corvane had spent thirty years in the Temple of Quiet Hours, and he had come to understand that ink was memory made obedient. You could pour anything into it: prayers, lies, the names of the dead. It held whatever you asked of it and never once complained.

He was thinking about this as he watched Silas Sandoval through the lattice screen of the scriptorium gallery, her head bent over her copying desk like a question mark that had given up on ever being answered.

She ground her ink the way she did everything — precisely, quietly, with the grim economy of a woman who had learned early that the world charged interest on every wasted motion. Her mortar moved in small circles. Her shoulders were two knots of discipline. Even from this distance he could see the smudge of black on her left knuckle, the permanent stain of a scribe who had been writing since she was nine years old and would be writing, if the ledgers had their way, until her hands gave out around sixty.

*Forty-one years remaining on the debt,* Corvane thought, consulting the number the way another man might consult his own pulse. *Give or take the years the temple deducts for feast days.*

He descended the gallery stairs without hurry. He had never once hurried in his life; it was one of the many things people mistook for holiness.

* * *

The scriptorium smelled of tallow and cold stone and, beneath it, the faint mineral tang of the valley itself — that curious deadness in the air that visitors always noticed and natives always forgot. Magic did not grow here. It did not even visit. The Temple of Quiet Hours had been built in this valley for exactly that reason, though the reason had been written out of the founding documents some eight centuries ago and replaced with something about humility.

Corvane had read the original documents. That was rather the point of him.

"Silas Sandoval," he said pleasantly.

She came to her feet so fast her stool rocked. Ink-stained fingers tucked behind her back — an old habit, hiding the mess, as though the High Priest of the entire temple had never seen a dirty hand before.

"Your Holiness." She bowed. "I didn't hear you."

"No one ever does. It's my single greatest talent." He smiled at her, the smile he had practiced in front of polished bronze for twenty years until it looked less like a weapon and more like a lamp being lit. "You're working late."

"The Vespers copies are behind. Brother Anselm's hand cramps in the cold, so I took his quires."

"And no one asked you to."

"No one had to, Your Holiness."

"Mm." He let the silence sit between them for a moment, because silence was a tool too, and he was very good with tools. "Walk with me."

Her eyes flicked — just once, quickly — toward the half-finished page. He watched her weigh the unfinished line against a direct summons from the High Priest, watched duty crush diligence the way it always did, and felt the small warm satisfaction of a man confirming that a lock still works.

They walked. The cloister corridor was long and grey and lit by lamps that burned without flickering, because there was nothing in the air to make them dance.

"You've been in the reliquary," Corvane said.

It was not a question, and he said it lightly, the way one remarks on weather. He felt her go still beside him — not dramatically, not the way an amateur guilt-stumbles, but with the controlled stillness of a woman recalculating everything at speed. She was clever. He had always known she was clever. It was one of the reasons she was dangerous, and one of the reasons she was useful, and the difference between those two things was going to be decided in the next ten minutes.

"I brought the girl water," Silas said. "She wouldn't drink from the brother who delivered supper. She seemed frightened of him."

"Frightened of Brother Odo? He's the gentlest man in this temple. He weeps at lambing season." Corvane turned his head and studied her profile. "Did she speak to you?"

Silence. Good. Lies required preparation, and he had caught her before she'd had time to brew one.

"Silas." He stopped walking. In the lamplight his shadow fell across her, long and priest-shaped. "I'm going to tell you a thing now, and I want you to listen to all of it before you decide how much of yourself to spend defending it. May I?"

"...You may, Your Holiness."

"The child speaks a language older than any kingdom in the realm. Older than the realm itself. There are perhaps four living scholars who can parse its grammar, three of whom are liars, and I am the fourth." He smiled again, softer this time. "And you, Silas Sandoval, who has never left this valley, who has copied eleven thousand pages of scripture and not one word of anything else — you stood in front of that child last night and your face said *I almost understand her.*"

The color drained from her face in a most satisfying gradient.

"I don't—"

"You do. Don't insult us both." His voice stayed gentle. Gentleness was the blade he preferred; it went in deeper and bled less on the floor. "I have watched you for nine years. Do you know why? Not because you're the best scribe — though you are. Because you copy prayers you don't believe, and you do it perfectly, and only a person who *wants* to believe does that. Hunger, Silas. You are the hungriest soul in this building. And hungry people find doors."

Somewhere down the corridor, a door actually did close — one of the novices shuffling off to Compline — and Corvane permitted himself a private flicker of amusement at the universe's occasional sense of theater.

"The girl," Silas said carefully, "is a child. Lost, filthy, half-starved—"

"She is a vessel," Corvane said, "carrying the last dragon-fire in the world."

The words dropped into the corridor and lay there.

He watched Silas absorb them. Watched the scribe's mind do what scribes' minds do — reach for reference, for citation, for the marginalia of the restricted codices she was absolutely not supposed to have opened. He knew she'd opened them. The dust told him. Dust was another kind of ledger, and Corvane read all the ledgers.

"That's a story," she whispered. "A nursery story. Fire-drakes and the First Temples—"

"A story we tell children so they won't ask what the reliquary vaults are *for*." He resumed walking, and after a moment her footsteps followed, because he had known they would. "Dragons were real, Silas. Their fire was real. And when the last of them died — or was helped along, depending on which account you prefer — the fire did not die with them. Fire, properly tended, passes on. It sleeps in bloodlines, thins across generations, waits. And now it has finished waiting, and it has walked up to our door in the body of a mud-caked child who drinks water only from the hands of Silas Sandoval."

"She drank from my hands because I was the only one who knelt down," Silas said, with a flash of something that was almost anger. Almost. He catalogued it. Anger was fuel; he'd simply need to be sure it burned where he wanted.

"Perhaps," Corvane agreed amiably. "Or perhaps the fire chooses, and it has chosen the one scribe in this temple who can almost hear its language. Either way, here we are."

They reached the end of the cloister, where a narrow window looked out over the inner court. Below, the reliquary wing sat squat and blind under the moon, its single lit window glowing amber. The girl's window. He wondered what she saw when she looked out of it. Valleys, probably. Valleys and walls. Everyone in this temple spent their lives looking at valleys and walls.

"Here is what happens next," he said. "Tomorrow morning, the girl will be brought to the translation chamber. There will be questions — gentle ones, I promise you, I am not my predecessors — about where she came from, what she carries, what the fire wants. And she will answer none of them, because she answers none of *us*. Except—" he turned, and let the lamplight catch his face at the angle he knew was most trustworthy, "—she spoke to you. So you will sit across from her, and you will translate, and you will help me understand what has come into our keeping."

"And if I refuse?"

There it was. He'd been waiting for it, and he savored it the way he savored the first sip of winter wine.

"If you refuse," Corvane said, "nothing. Truly. You'll return to your desk, and your debt, and your forty-one years of copying scripture by lamplight until your eyes fail, and the temple will assign the translation to someone less gifted, and the girl will be handled by methods I would very much prefer not to describe to a sensitive soul such as yourself." He paused. "Brother Odo weeps at lambing season, but he does not weep at everything."

Silas's jaw tightened. He watched her do the arithmetic — the girl's safety weighed against her own conscience, conscience against obedience, obedience against the simple animal fact that a child locked in a stone room needed an ally, and Silas Sandoval had never in her life been able to walk past a creature that needed something.

People thought Corvane's power lay in threats. It did not. Threats were crude; any warden with a cudgel could threaten. No — his power lay in knowing exactly which kindness a person could not refuse, and then offering it first.

"But," he continued, before she could speak, "if you *accept* — if you lend your gift to this work, freely and gladly — then I will do something for you in turn."

"My debt," Silas said flatly. "That's what this is. You want to buy me with my mother's ledger."

"Buy you?" He pressed a hand to his chest, wounded. "Silas. I want to *free* you. Your mother borrowed eighty talents from this temple to bury your father and feed you through the famine year, and the interest has compounded for nineteen years, and you have paid and paid and paid, and the sum outstanding is forty-one more years of your life. Forty-one years, Silas. You are thirty-one years old. Do you know what the world outside this valley looks like? Have you ever seen the sea? The dragon-road? A city where the lamps burn colors instead of white?" He shook his head slowly, sorrowfully, a shepherd counting his lost sheep. "You have given this temple your whole life already. I am offering to give you the rest of it back."

The silence stretched. Down in the court, the reliquary window glowed on, patient as a coal.

Corvane watched her — truly watched, because this was the moment, the hinge, the little creak of iron before the gate swings — and saw the war move behind her eyes. Duty on one side. The girl on the other. And underneath both, faint as a watermark, that hunger he had named: the wanting-to-believe, the wanting-out, the wanting-*more* that she had starved neatly for nineteen years and that he was now setting a bowl of, steaming, in front of her.

Take it, he thought benevolently. Hungry people always take it. That is what makes them mine.

"If I translate," Silas said at last, slowly, "the debt is erased. All of it. In writing. Tonight."

"In writing tonight," he agreed warmly. "I'll draft the remission myself. My hand is nearly as fine as yours."

"And the girl comes to no harm. Whatever your questions are, they stay questions."

"The girl comes to no harm," Corvane said, and meant it, in the way that mattered to him, which was the way that kept the vessel intact. Every word true. None of them the whole truth. It was, he felt, the closest thing to prayer his profession allowed.

Silas looked down at her ink-stained hands. When she raised her face again, something had settled in it — not trust, he noted with approval; he'd have been disappointed by trust, trust was brittle — but resolve. A direction chosen. A door opened.

"Then I'll translate," she said.

"Wonderful." He beamed at her, and for one unguarded instant the beam was genuine, because Corvane genuinely loved this part — the part where the ledger balanced, where a soul signed itself over with a nod and called it mercy. "Come to the translation chamber after Prime. Bring your patience and your ear. Oh—" he added, turning back as if struck by an afterthought, and watching her flinch at the afterthought, "—and Silas? The codex you consulted last night. *Foundations of the First Age*, third shelf, the one with the broken clasp. Return it to its place, and we'll say no more about it. A translator should keep her sources tidy."

He left her standing in the lamplight with her guilt and her bargain, and climbed the night stair toward his study with the light, springy step of a man whose investments had just matured.

* * *

In his study, Corvane poured himself a measure of plum wine and unlocked the drawer beneath the false bottom of his desk.

Inside lay a scrap of vellum, eight hundred years old, recovered at considerable cost from a ruin in the eastern wastes. On it, drawn in a pigment that still gleamed wetly after eight centuries, was a sigil: a coiled flame within a circle of teeth.

The same sigil, he was quite certain, that the feral child had scratched into the reliquary dust with one grimy finger while Silas Sandoval watched with her heart in her throat.

He turned the vellum in the lamplight. Around the sigil ran a line of the old tongue, and he mouthed it now as he had mouthed it a hundred times, translating not with scholarship but with appetite:

*What is freely given burns brightest. What is taken burns longest.*

Corvane smiled, set down the wine, and drew a fresh sheet of paper toward him. There was a remission of debt to draft, after all. Forty-one years, erased in a few elegant lines of ink.

Memory made obedient.

He dipped his pen, and began to write.
