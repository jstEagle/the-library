## 5. What the Fire Remembers

The reliquary smelled of dust and old silver and, faintly, of something I could not name — a warmth like bread left too long near a hearth, or the ghost of a struck match. I had been coming to it every evening for six days now, after the last office was sung, when the corridors emptied and even Brother Anselm's perpetual coughing subsided into the rhythm of his sleep.

I told myself I came to translate.

I told myself a great many things that week.

* * *

Ember sat cross-legged on the floor between two reliquary cabinets, because they would not give her a bed and she refused the cot they'd dragged in anyway, as if sleeping on temple furniture were some kind of surrender. She had arranged her few possessions around her with great ceremony: three smooth stones, a bird's nest (empty), and a chicken bone polished to a shine that suggested months of devoted handling.

"You brought the book," she said, before I had even closed the door behind me. She said it in her own tongue, that language older than any kingdom, and then — because we were practicing — she repeated it in mine, each word placed carefully like a stone in a wall. "You. Brought. The. Book."

"I brought the book." I set my satchel down. "How did you know? You can't see through doors."

"You walk different when you carry books." She tapped her chest. "Heavy here. Happy here." She moved her hand from her sternum to somewhere vaguely near her ribs. "Brother Anselm walks sad-heavy. No happy."

"Brother Anselm carries ledgers."

"Ledgers are heavy-sad," Ember said, with the certainty of someone stating that water is wet, and I found I had no argument to make against this.

I knelt across from her and unwrapped the codex. Not the restricted one — I wasn't ready for that again, not yet, not since I'd seen Corvane watching me from the shadows with those patient, weighing eyes. This was a copy of a copy of a copy, a catechism primer so old its margins had been annotated by four generations of scribes who all apparently hated each other. But it had the sigils. And Ember had the words.

This was our ritual now. She would speak; I would listen. Somewhere in the space between her language and mine there was a door, and every night it opened a little wider. It made no sense. I had never studied Old Tongue — no one had, not really, not since the Concordance standardized scripture eight hundred years ago. And yet when Ember spoke, words surfaced in me like fish rising to feed, half-formed, flickering, almost graspable.

"It remembers you," Ember had said on the third night, watching my face with unnerving directness. "The words. They remember you."

I had chosen not to think about what that meant.

Tonight she leaned over the catechism and pressed one finger beneath the first sigil — the coiled serpent-with-wings that appeared on page one of every holy text in the valley, the emblem of the Temptation Overcome, the Beast Subdued by the First Priests. Every child learned it before they learned their letters. I had copied it perhaps forty thousand times.

"Say what your book says," Ember commanded.

"'And the dragon was cast down, and its fire was quenched, and the people rejoiced, for the priests had delivered them from terror.'" The words came out flat and automatic, worn smooth by repetition, a pebble in my mouth.

Ember's face did something complicated. Pity, I thought, and contempt, and a kind of weary recognition, the way an adult looks at a child insisting the moon follows them home.

"Not true," she said.

"It's scripture—"

"Not true." She said it kindly, which somehow made it worse. Then she began to speak, low and flowing, her hands moving — her language was spoken with the whole body, I was learning, hands shaping meaning in the air like a potter at a wheel — and I closed my eyes and listened for the door to open.

And it opened.

* * *

What came through was not translation exactly. It was more like remembering something I had never known. Fragments, images, impressions carried on words whose roots went down deeper than the words themselves:

*A valley green. Wings overhead like storm-clouds given purpose. And the small ones — the soft ones, the quick clever soft ones — bringing gifts to the fire-givers. Grain. Songs. Their children, laughing under dragon-shadow, unafraid.*

"The doctrine says they burned the villages," I whispered. "That's why the First Priests rose up. To protect—"

*No.*

One word, and it landed like a stone dropped down a well. Ember watched me, waiting, her hands still moving, patient as tide.

*I gave,* she said next, and the shape of the giving unfolded: *Fire freely offered, to light the cold seasons, to heal the sick fields, to bless the forging of plows and bells. Given, not taken. That was the covenant. The fire must be asked for. The fire must be thanked. The fire must be returned.*

"Returned how?"

She showed me. Her cupped hands lifted, turned, poured nothing into the air — an empty gesture that nonetheless made the hair on my arms rise, because I felt it, felt the shape of a rite older than the temple, older than the valley itself. *Fire goes back to the sky through gratitude. Through the open hand. Never through the closed fist.*

"And then?"

Her hands stilled.

I had noticed, over our nights together, that Ember had a way of going quiet before difficult things, the way a swimmer goes still before diving. She looked at me now with an expression far too old for her face.

*Then came the men who wanted the fire but not the asking,* she said. *Who wanted power without thanks. Who built a house of quiet hours and wrote new words in old books.* Her finger came down on my catechism, on the beautiful black sigil of the Beast Subdued. *They wrote the ending first. Then they made it true.*

"What do you mean, made it true? The dragons—"

*Were invited.* Her voice cracked on it, and for the first time I heard the child underneath the ancient tongue — heard the hurt. *A feast. A consecration. Come, fire-fathers, come bless our new temple, come sleep in our deep vaults where the winter stores are kept. And when the wings were folded and the sleep was on them—*

She stopped. Her hands made a motion I didn't understand, sharp and final, a gesture like a door slamming, like a neck snapping, and then she touched the sigil again.

*Quenched is the wrong word,* she said softly. *Stolen is the right one. They cut out the fire while the dragons slept and trusted them. They put it in vessels. They fed it, drop by drop, into the priesthood, generation by generation, and called the warmth holiness. Your prayers feel warm, scribe? That is not grace. That is what is left of my family, burning slowly in a lamp.*

The silence afterward was very long.

Somewhere above us, the temple settled and ticked, stone cooling in the night air, the thousand small sounds of a building at rest. Sounds I had found comforting my entire life. Tonight they sounded like the creaking of a ship, and I was suddenly, terribly aware of being far from shore.

"That can't be true," I said. My voice came out strange. "The founding texts have been verified. Cross-checked against the archives at—"

"Against the archives your own temple keeps?" Ember tilted her head. "Scribe. If you steal a thing, do you leave the receipt lying where everyone can read it?"

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to argue. I wanted to march up to the scriptorium and pull apart the Concordance line by line and prove her wrong, prove that my life's work rested on something solid, that the prayers I copied meant what they said, that the warmth I'd spent thirty years failing to feel was real grace and anyone could find it if they were only good enough, only faithful enough, only—

Only I had never felt it. In thirty years of copying holy words by the ten-thousand, I had never once felt anything but ink under my fingernails and the ache of my mother's debt sitting on my shoulders like a yoke. I had assumed the failure was mine.

What if it hadn't been?

"Scribe," Ember said gently, using the word she'd coined for me and refused to replace, though I'd taught her my name days ago. "Sil-as. Your face is doing the rain thing."

"The rain thing?"

"When the outside is dry but inside is weather." She reached over and patted my knee with a sticky hand. "Is okay. When I learned my family was stolen, I broke seven plates. Maren—" she stopped, caught herself, frowned. "A woman. Fed me soup. I broke her plates."

"Maren," I said. "The hedge-witch?"

Something flickered across Ember's face — quickly hidden, but not quickly enough. "A story. For later."

"Ember—"

"Later," she said firmly, with the iron authority of a nine-year-old, and I let it go, filing it away with the growing pile of things I was choosing not to think about. It was becoming quite a large pile. I suspected it would eventually fall on me.

Instead I looked down at the catechism, at the coiled beast stamped in faded ink, and asked the question I had been circling for six nights.

"Why me, Ember? Why can I almost understand you? No one else in this temple can. High Priest Corvane has scholars from three kingdoms trying, and they get nothing."

Ember studied me for a long moment. Outside, a bell tolled the hour — the ninth hour of night, the Hour of Kept Silence, when the whole valley was supposed to be asleep. When I was supposed to be asleep.

"The words remember you," she said again. "I told you. The old tongue isn't learned, Silas. It's owed. It lives in families that once served the fire." She hesitated, and I saw her choose her next words with care. "Your mothers' mothers' mothers. Someone in your blood stood close to a dragon once. Close enough to catch the words like sparks off a forge."

"That's impossible. My family are valley folk. We've been here forever. There's been no magic in the valley for—"

"A thousand years," Ember finished. "Yes. Because your temple took the last of it and locked the vaults and called the emptiness peace." She smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. It had teeth in it, small white teeth, and behind them something vast. "But valleys remember what temples forget, scribe. Seeds remember. Springs remember. Blood remembers."

The candle guttered. I would swear afterward — I do swear, now, to anyone who asks — that the flame bent toward Ember, just slightly, the way flowers bend toward sun.

"And what about you?" I asked quietly. "Why are you really here? Not how you got past the wardens. Why. What are you carrying that makes Corvane clear his calendar and smile at a mud-child?"

For a moment I thought she wouldn't answer. Then she held out her hand, palm up, and I leaned close, expecting to see one of her treasures — a stone, maybe, or the sacred chicken bone.

Her palm was empty. But it glowed.

Faintly — so faintly I might have imagined it, might have blamed the candle, my tired eyes, my own wanting — but glowing nonetheless, a warmth beneath the skin like sunlight through a closed eyelid. And in that glow I glimpsed something moving, something coiled and patient and immense, something looking back at me.

I jerked away. Ember closed her fingers.

"They don't know yet," she said. "The priests. They think I am a lost child who speaks strangely. They think the vaults below are empty." Her eyes lifted to mine, and in the dark they seemed lit from behind. "But the fire is waking, scribe. After a thousand years, it is waking. And when it wakes fully, everyone will know. So you should decide soon whose side you stand on."

"My side?" I laughed, and it came out brittle. "Ember, I'm a debtor. I don't have sides. I have obligations."

"Yes," she agreed solemnly. "Obligations." And then, with the terrifying precision of children and cats, she added: "High Priest watches you at night. From the shadow by the anointing-oil shelf. He thinks you don't see. He counts your visits."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the reliquary's chill.

"How do you—"

"He smells of ink and ambition," Ember said simply, gathering her stones and her nest and her bone into her lap as if preparing for bed. "Same smell as the men in the oldest memory. The ones who built the house of quiet hours." She lay down on the hard floor, curled around her treasures, and closed her eyes. "Goodnight, Silas. Tomorrow we learn the word for *betrayal*. It is a very useful word. You will need it soon."

I sat there a while longer, listening to her breathing slow into sleep, watching the candle flame lean toward her like a flower toward sun.

Then I gathered my forbidden thoughts and my heretical questions and walked back through the silent corridors of the Temple of Quiet Hours, past the anointing-oil shelf —

— where, in the deep shadow beside it, something shifted. A fold of darkness that breathed. A patience that watched me pass.

Corvane knew I came. Corvane had always known.

And come morning, he intended to tell me why.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat at my desk in the scriptorium with a blank sheet before me and an inkwell I kept forgetting to dip my pen into, turning over fragments — *given, not taken; the fire must be thanked; the closed fist* — until the words lost their shapes and became only sounds.

Near dawn, a novice came through with the morning's assignments, and I took the slip from her hand without looking, the way I had ten thousand mornings before.

Then I looked.

My assignment for the day, in Corvane's own precise hand, was not copying at all.

It was preparation of rite-scrolls. For a ceremony to be held at month's end, before the assembled priesthood, in honor of — the slip said — *the Consecration of a Vessel.*

Below the title, in smaller script, a single line of instruction:

*Scribe Sandoval will render the vessel's speech for the rite. Fluency confirmed. Do not discuss the matter elsewhere.*

I read it three times. *Vessel.* The word sat on the page, innocent as any other, and I thought of a small body curled around three stones and a bird's nest, of a palm that glowed, of a flame leaning like a flower toward warmth.

They were not translating her.

They were preparing her.

And I had just agreed — six nights of agreement, documented, initialed, filed — to be the voice that talked her gently into it.
