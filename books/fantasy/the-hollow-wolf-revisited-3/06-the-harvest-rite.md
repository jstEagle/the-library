## 6. The Harvest Rite

The scrolls arrived on a Tuesday.

Silas knew it was Tuesday because Tuesdays were ink-grinding days, and Brother Anselm had developed the unfortunate habit of delivering urgent work precisely when her mortar was wettest, so that she would have to choose between ruining a week's pigment and letting him stand there breathing through his nose at her until she surrendered. He was doing the nose-breathing now, a stack of fresh vellum balanced against his chest like a sleeping infant.

"From His Reverence," he said. "Translation work. The girl's language."

"Corvane has translators."

"His Reverence says you have an *ear* for it." Anselm said *ear* the way other men said *heresy* — reluctantly, and with a glance over his shoulder. "He wants drafts by week's end. There are... diagrams."

Diagrams. Silas wiped her hands on her apron and took the stack. It was heavier than translation work had any right to be — forty sheets at least, most of them covered not in Ember's looping script but in Corvane's own small, precise hand, interleaved with sketches.

"Anselm," she said, before he could escape. "Why does the High Priest write in cipher?"

"He doesn't."

"He does. Look." She turned a sheet toward him. The columns of glyphs were neat as stitching, but they were none of them letters from any alphabet she'd copied in fifteen years at the Temple of Quiet Hours, and she had copied *everything*. "This is a substitution scheme. I've seen novices do it to pass notes about supper."

Anselm looked. Anselm went pale in his particular way, which was to say all at once, like a candle pinched out. "Then I expect His Reverence will want someone clever enough to read it," he said, and fled.

* * *

She worked on it that night because she could not help herself. That was the trouble with Silas Sandoval, she had long ago concluded: give her a locked box and she became a woman possessed. Fifteen years of copying prayers she couldn't feel had taught her every discipline except the one that mattered, which was leaving things alone.

The cipher took her four hours. Corvane was good, but ciphers were made by scribes and broken by scribes, and Silas had spent more hours than she cared to count inventing games for herself inside the tedium — counting letter-frequencies in psalms, mapping which brothers favored which stock phrases, building little architectures of repetition in her head to keep the ache of the valley at bay. Corvane's cipher cracked like a walnut under a cartwheel once you noticed he never used the glyph for *th* twice in a row.

And then she sat very still for a long time, because what she had cracked open was not a translation.

It was a liturgy.

The word *harvest* appeared eleven times in the first three pages. She counted. Counting was what her hands did when her mind wanted to scream. Eleven times, always in the same construction: *the harvest of the gift*, *the harvest vessel*, *the harvest rite, being the restoration of right order*. Around it clustered words she knew too well from the oldest codices — *vessel*, *emptying*, *return to source* — and diagrams of a circle of standing stones she recognized with a lurch as the reliquary itself, its floor redrawn with channels running outward from the center like roots pulled inside-out.

Channels. Drains.

In the margins, in Corvane's hand, small annotations in plain script where even he apparently forgot to encrypt: *flow rate insufficient — widen second channel.* And beneath that, almost tender: *she will not feel it after the third hour.*

*She will not feel it after the third hour.*

Silas set the page down. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere far away she understood that her heart was going very fast, but her hands had been trained by fifteen years of exacting work, and they refused to shake during business hours.

They were going to drain the child.

Not imprison her. Not ransom her. Not study her, whatever Corvane's honeyed voice had implied when he'd offered to erase her mother's debt. They were going to lay her in the reliquary, open channels in the floor, and take the last living dragon-fire out of her body the way you tapped a keg — and when the keg was empty, the annotations made clear what happened to vessels. *Vessel integrity fails at exhaustion point,* one note read, followed by a small, tidy calculation of how much fire remained versus how much the rite required, followed by the number that was the difference between those two things, which was nothing. Which was less than nothing. Which was a dead eight-year-old girl on a temple floor with the priesthood's future poured out of her.

*The restoration of right order.* Right order. Silas thought of Ember's face when she'd first seen the sigil in the old codex, the way the child had touched the page as if greeting a relative. She thought of the girl's laughter yesterday, sudden and startling as a bird taking wing, when Silas had mispronounced a dragon-word so badly that Ember had fallen off her stool.

*She will not feel it after the third hour.* As if that were mercy. As if anesthesia were the moral summit of the undertaking.

Silas got up, walked to the window, opened it, and breathed the cold valley air until she stopped feeling sick. The valley that forgot. Magic could not follow you here; everyone said it like a blessing. *Safe. Quiet. Nothing here can hurt you.* She had believed it was a shield her whole life. Standing at the window with Corvane's cipher cooling under her fingers, she understood for the first time that it might also be a pen. A place where power could not follow was also a place where power could not leave — and if you caught something with power in it, something small and tired and far from home—

Well. What a convenient valley in which to keep a harvest.

* * *

She did not sleep. She considered, briefly and seriously, praying about it, and found that the prayers came out wrong — came out as questions, which the Temple of Quiet Hours held to be a form of insolence. *What is right order? Who ordered it? Ordered by whom?* By morning she had worn a groove in the floorboards between her cot and the window, and had arrived at the only answer that survived the night:

She had to see the rest of it. All of it. Before she decided anything, she needed to know exactly how far down the rot went.

The full file was in Corvane's private archive, and no one entered Corvane's private archive. But Silas had been grinding this temple's ink for half her life, and there was no lock in the building whose key she had not either held, copied, or watched dangle from a distracted brother's belt while he complained about his knees. Corvane's archive lock was new and fine, but its keeper was old and vain, and vanity kept a schedule. Each dawn, Corvane processed to the reliquary to observe Ember through the grate — *observing her wellbeing,* the record stated, though Silas had watched him watch her, and it was the watching of a man counting coins. Each dawn he passed the archive door. Each dawn he paused there, checked the seal, and then stood a moment in the corridor admiring, Silas now realized, himself: straightening his robes in the polished bronze plate mounted beside the frame.

The bronze plate was screwed into the wall directly above the lintel.

Silas waited for dawn the next morning with her heart behaving disgracefully, pressed into the alcove of the vestry stair with an armload of linens she genuinely did need to deliver, eventually, to somewhere. Corvane came down the corridor on schedule, radiant with purpose, checked his seal, and turned to the bronze to admire the man who was about to save the world. For nine seconds, the High Priest of the Temple of Quiet Hours gazed lovingly at his own reflection, and for nine seconds, a woman with an armload of altar linens stood on the second step of the vestry stair and reached up and over the door, and lifted the wax impression kit she had prepared from her sleeve, and pressed it to the seal hanging loose from its hasp, and got a clean cast, and stepped back down, and was walking serenely toward the laundry when he finally finished loving himself.

Brother Anselm nodded at her in passing. "Linens?"

"Linens," said Silas, whose voice did not shake, because her hands had trained the rest of her well.

* * *

The archive smelled of Corvane: cedar oil and cold iron and something underneath both, something like the air before lightning. She unlocked it with her cast at midnight, when the corridors belonged to the deaf old brother who patrolled with a lantern he was too thrifty to refill, and slipped inside, and lit no lamp, because her eyes had learned dark rooms the way her hands had learned steady ones.

The file was exactly where logic said it would be — central shelf, eye height, because Corvane believed in hierarchy even in filing. She took it back to her cell and read everything, and the reading took until the bell for Lauds, and by the end of it Silas Sandoval, who had spent fifteen years believing herself a woman without convictions, discovered that she had one, and that it was burning her up from the inside.

It wasn't just Ember. Of course it wasn't just Ember. The harvest rite was the opening movement of a symphony. Corvane's papers laid it out in the patient language of engineering: the fire, once taken, could be divided — *portioned* — among the high clergy, each carrying a fragment of living flame in a warded vial, each fragment enough to work true miracles in a world where miracles had gone quiet. Kingdoms that had forgotten magic would remember it suddenly, terribly. And whoever held the fire would hold the fear, and whoever held the fear would hold the crowns, and the crowns would come crawling to the Temple of Quiet Hours to beg for protection from the very thing the Temple had loosed.

There was a page near the end, a projection table, kingdoms listed down one side and projected casualties down the other, and Corvane had annotated it in his tidy hand: *Acceptable, given eternal stakes.* He had underlined *eternal* twice.

Silas closed the file. In the silence of her cell she heard Ember, somewhere below in the stone reliquary, turning over in her sleep. Or perhaps she imagined it. It didn't matter. The sound arranged itself in her chest alongside everything else and became, irreversibly, a decision.

* * *

Burning documents in a temple is a delicate art, because temples are full of people whose entire spiritual vocation is noticing things. Silas chose her method with a craftsman's care. The temple kitchen kept a great copper cauldron for rendering tallow, and twice a week it was fired hot enough to boil fat, and on those mornings the whole east wing stank so thoroughly of smoke and grease that Brother Odilo — the infirmarian, who had opinions about ventilation and shared them freely — burned aromatic brushwood in the hearths to fight the smell. Smoke in the east wing on rendering days was not merely unremarkable. It was traditional.

She waited for rendering day. She cut Corvane's precious file into pieces small enough to feed to a hearth, wrapped them in oilcloth against her stomach, and went about her morning like any other morning: ink ground, linens delivered, psalms copied in her flawless, dead, obedient hand. At the midday bell she carried her basket of waste parchment — genuine waste, she'd checked, practice alphabets and spoiled initials — down to the east wing, and fed the kitchen fire honestly for a quarter hour, establishing herself in the sight of God and Brother Odilo as a woman disposing of rubbish.

Then Odilo went to argue with the cook about the ventilation, as he did every rendering day, God bless him and his righteous lungs, and Silas knelt at the hearth with her basket, and began feeding it Corvane's symphony.

The diagrams went first. The standing stones, the channels, the careful measurements of a child's capacity. The paper caught quickly; the ink flared greenish, alchemical, and for one heartbeat the flames in the hearth stood up taller than the chimney should allow, and the heat on her face felt less like fire and more like attention — like something enormous turning over in its sleep and looking at her.

Silas froze, a scroll-half in her hand.

*I see you,* said the heat, or she imagined it said, which amounts to the same thing in a temple. *I know whose hands these are.*

"I'm sorry," Silas whispered, absurdly, to a hearth. "It's not me it wants. I know that."

The flames settled. She kept working.

Page by page. The liturgy. The portioning scheme. The projection table with its twice-underlined *eternal* — she held that one longest, watching the kingdoms' names curl, and felt something in her chest that was terror and exhilaration fused so completely she couldn't tell which was which, the way you can't tell, in a struck match, where the sulfur ends and the wood begins. Fifteen years of copying. Fifteen years of *right order*. And here was the order, revealed, and it fit in a hearth basket, and it burned like anything else ever made by human hands.

The last thing to go was the margin note. *She will not feel it after the third hour.* Silas read it one final time, aloud, very quietly, so that the words would at least have been spoken honestly in this building once.

"She'll feel everything," she told the ashes. "That's rather the point of her."

The bell rang None. She dusted her knees, reloaded her basket with legitimate rubbish, and walked out past the cook and Odilo, still arguing, and down the corridor, where she passed Corvane coming the other way, and bowed her head with fifteen years of perfect practice.

"Scribe Sandoval." He smiled his honeyed smile. "How go the translations? I hoped for drafts by week's end."

"They're going slowly, Your Reverence," Silas said truthfully. "The material is dense. Some passages seem almost to resist meaning."

Something flickered behind his eyes — brief, professional, the flicker of a man checking a seal. "Dense material rewards persistence," he said warmly. "Do persist."

"Oh," said Silas Sandoval, with the gentle smile of a woman who had spent the morning committing arson, "I intend to."

He walked on. She counted her own heartbeats until he rounded the corner — seven, eight, nine — and then she turned, not toward the scriptorium, but toward the reliquary stairs, because the translations were due in five days, and Corvane would notice their absence in six, and whatever Silas was going to do about a stolen child in a magic-dead valley, she had approximately one week in which to do it.

Below, behind the grate, she could hear Ember humming a song in a language older than kings.

It sounded, Silas thought with a wild, giddy lightness she hadn't felt since childhood, like a warning.
