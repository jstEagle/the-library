## 7. Flight Across the Border

The scrolls were still smoking when Silas climbed through the reliquary window.

She had imagined this moment a hundred times in the gray hours between prayers — imagined it noble and deliberate, herself standing tall before the high altar declaring some great truth. Instead she had burned the preparation scrolls in a candle flame, panicked when Corvane's voice echoed down the cloister, shoved Ember out the window first, and followed with considerably less grace than she'd have liked, catching her hem on the latch and landing in the herb garden face-first among the feverfew.

"Are you hurt?" Ember whispered — not in her old tongue, but in the valley speech, halting and strange, words she had only begun to learn in the last three days. It was still astonishing every time. A week ago the girl had spoken only that language older than any kingdom, the one that made Silas's teeth ache and her heart lean toward something vast. Now she spoke like a child taught by a hurried tutor, which, Silas supposed, was exactly what she was.

"Only my dignity," Silas said, spitting out a leaf. "And I hadn't much use for it."

Ember considered this gravely, decided it was a joke, and smiled — a small, crooked smile that Silas had already learned to treasure the way misers treasure coins.

They went out through the herb garden's back wall, where the stones had crumbled years ago and no one had bothered to mend them because nothing in the Temple of Quiet Hours was ever allowed to crumble visibly. Silas had copied an entire codex on the theology of visible perfection. She thought of it now with something that felt almost like laughter, or maybe grief wearing laughter's coat.

Behind them, the temple bells began to ring the alarm. Not vespers, not matins — the *third* bell, the one rung only twice in Silas's lifetime, both times for floods.

"They know," Ember said.

"They know." Silas took the girl's hand. It was warm. Of course it was warm. The child carried the last living dragon-fire in her small body, and her hand was warm as a stone left in summer sun, and Silas held onto it like a rope over a cliff, which was not far from the truth.

The valley lay silver under a waning moon — wheat fields, orchards, the tidy lanes of a town that had never once in Silas's twenty-six years seen anything worth alarming anyone. The road east ran straight and honest to the ferry at the river, and so they did not take it. They took the drainage ditch along the mill-race instead, wading in cold water to their knees, because Silas had spent her whole life reading about pursuit and evasion in adventure romances she copied for wealthy patrons, and in those books one never took the honest road.

She had never expected the books to be useful. She had certainly never expected to be grateful for the tedium of copying *The Ballad of the Ninefold Quest* four separate times.

"The wardens will check the ferry," she murmured, more to herself than to Ember. "Then the north ford. Then—" She stopped. Then what? She had never actually planned beyond *away*. Away was not a place. Away was a direction with no map, and Silas had lived her entire life inside a debt ledger with the borders drawn for her.

"Then they will ask the river," Ember said.

Silas glanced down at her. "What?"

"The river tells things." Ember said it simply, the way one says *the sky is blue*. "Water remembers being rained. It remembers everything it touches." She frowned, working at the valley speech like someone prying open a stubborn lock. "It is... hard to say small."

"It is hard to say small," Silas agreed faintly. Three days ago this child had been a locked mystery; now she was a half-open door, and through the door Silas could see a world where rivers gossiped, and she did not know whether to weep or laugh or both.

They came up out of the ditch at the old shepherd's path below the eastern hills, and there Silas stopped, because ahead of her — closer than she had ever been, close enough to touch if she dared, and she did not dare — stood the valley's edge.

Everyone knew about the edge. Everyone in the valley knew it the way fish know water: the invisible boundary where magic could not follow. The founders' doctrine said the gods had swept the valley clean of sorcery to make it a sanctuary of pure faith. Silas had copied that doctrine eleven times. *Here the soul stands naked before heaven, unshielded by any art.*

What the doctrine did not mention — what nobody mentioned, because nobody left — was what it felt like to stand near the boundary. But Silas could feel it now, even from thirty paces: a wrongness in the air, like a held breath. The moonlight looked slightly too sharp. The nightingale song sounded slightly too clear. Everything in the valley was *slightly too*, all the time, and she had never known until this moment, standing at the rim of it, that she had lived her whole life inside a silence so complete it had masqueraded as peace.

"I can't feel anything here," she said. "I've never felt anything anywhere. I didn't know there was anything to feel."

Ember squeezed her hand. "Come and feel."

* * *

Crossing the valley's edge was like walking into weather.

One step, Silas was in the dead quiet she had always called normal. The next step, the world *hummed*. There was no gentler word for it. Magic came off the outside land like heat off a forge, like sound off a struck bell, and it went through Silas not around her, and she staggered and would have fallen except Ember caught her — small arms, impossible warmth, holding her up as easily as a fence post holds a leaning gate.

"Oh," Silas said. It was not a dignified utterance. It was the entire contents of her vocabulary compressed into one syllable.

The grass was *greener* here. That was the absurd part. She had expected the outside world to look corrupted, dangerous, the way the sermons painted it — thorned and shadowed, a wilderness of temptation. Instead the meadow grass glowed faintly silver-green under the moon as though each blade were lit from within by some tiny private dawn. A fox watched them from a hillock with eyes like amber lamps, utterly unafraid, and when Ember looked at it, the fox *bowed*, actually bowed, its head dipping once, before trotting away.

"Did you see—"

"Yes," Ember said, and giggled.

Silas had never heard her giggle. In the temple the girl had been all wariness and watchfulness, a wild thing in a cage of candlelight. Here, two hundred paces past the boundary, she was laughing at bowing foxes, and Silas found her own mouth doing something it rarely did — smiling without deciding to.

The vertigo came later, walking. It came in waves. Every hour or so the sheer *presence* of magic would crest over Silas again and the ground would feel unreliable, the sky too near, her own skin too full of whatever-it-was that filled everything. She kept talking through those waves, because talking anchored her, and Ember kept answering, and somewhere in the fourth hour of stumbling east through the hills Silas realized they were having the most ordinary conversation in the world — about food, mostly, about how Ember had never had honey cake and Silas had eaten exactly four in her life, one per year on the festival of Quiet Hours, and how this was a tragedy requiring immediate correction.

"You count your cakes," Ember said, horrified.

"One doesn't *count* them, one *rations*—"

"You count them." Ember looked at her with such pity that Silas laughed until she had to sit down on a rock, and the rock hummed gently beneath her, and even her laughter had a faint harmonic to it out here, and none of it — none of it — felt real, and all of it felt more real than twenty-six years of ink and prayer.

That was when the dogs bayed, far behind them, on the western hills.

The laughter died in Silas's throat like a snuffed candle.

"Wardens," she said. "That's a warden pack. Six hounds minimum, handlers behind. They'll have our scent from the reliquary." Her mind, trained for years on scripture and romance alike, laid out the tactical situation with horrible clarity. "We're on open hillside. Moon's nearly full. We can't outrun hounds, and I don't—" She stopped herself from saying *I don't know how to fight*, because obviously she didn't, obviously, she was a scribe whose greatest physical accomplishment was carrying a stack of codices up forty stairs.

Ember had gone very still. The warmth coming off her had changed quality — not hearth-warmth anymore but something with an edge to it, and when she turned to look westward, her eyes caught the moonlight and threw it back orange.

"Don't," Silas said quickly, kneeling to take the girl's shoulders. "Whatever you're thinking of doing — the fire — don't. Not yet. Not until we understand it. Promise me."

"They want to cut it out of me," Ember said. Flat. Old. For a moment she sounded less like a child learning valley-speech and more like the thing that had carried that first ancient language across a thousand years of hiding. "I heard the priests. Through the reliquary door. They used your words for it. *Harvest.*"

"I know." Silas's voice cracked on it. "I know what they meant. That's why we're running. But fire that answers fear is fire you can't take back, and I've read enough to know how that story goes. Please. Promise me."

A long moment. Down in the valley, the dog-song rose and fell.

"Promise," Ember said at last, reluctantly, and the orange faded from her eyes, and Silas exhaled.

"Right. Then we do this the scribe's way." She scanned the darkening hills. "Hounds follow scent. Water breaks scent. Is there—"

"There's a stream," Ember said instantly, tilting her head as though listening to something Silas couldn't hear — which, Silas was beginning to understand, she literally could. "Down there. And past it, a house. A crooked house. Someone lives there who..." Ember's brow furrowed. "Who smells like the valley but isn't of it. Like us."

"Like us how?"

"Half-and-half," Ember said. "Belonging nowhere. On purpose."

Which was, Silas reflected as they slid down the hillside toward the sound of running water, either the best news she'd had all night or the prelude to a fresh catastrophe. With her luck recently, probably both.

* * *

The stream saved them. The hounds lost the thread at the water, circling and complaining on the far bank while Silas and Ember scrambled upstream through shallows cold enough to make her gasp, and then cut away through bracken toward the crooked house Ember had smelled.

Crooked was accurate. The cottage leaned eastward at an angle that suggested it had started drinking sometime in the previous century and never stopped. Its thatch sprouted weeds and at least one ambitious elder sapling. A fence listed around a yard full of goats, chickens, beehives, and — inexplicably — a bathtub full of rainwater in which floated dozens of candles.

Silas knocked, because her mother had raised her to knock even at the end of the world.

The door opened on a chain, revealing one eye, gray and shrewd, above a knife that appeared with the smoothness of long practice.

"We're not buying anything," the eye said. Voice female, sixty-ish, worn smooth like a river stone.

"We're not selling anything either," Silas said. "We're running. From the Temple of Quiet Hours. And the child with me is—"

"I can smell what she is from here," the eye said. "Smelled her coming since moonrise. Whole hill's been buzzing like a kicked hive." The chain rattled; the door opened on a broad woman in a patched apron, iron-gray hair skewered up with what appeared to be a knitting needle, the knife now tucked away but her stance suggesting it was never truly *away*. She looked at Ember a long moment. Something in her weathered face went complicated — grief and awe arm-wrestling behind her eyes.

"Well," she said finally. "Come in before the hillside files a complaint. And wipe your feet. I've just scoured that floor with charms and I'll not have temple mud on it."

Inside, the cottage was warm, cluttered, and alive. Dried herbs hung from every beam. A kettle put itself on the hook without anyone touching it, which made Silas jump so badly she nearly sat in the cat. Shelves sagged with jars labeled in a cramped hand: *Beech Ash — for sorrow. Adder's Tongue — DO NOT. Grandmother's Good Luck (weak batch).*

"You're a witch," Silas said.

"I'm a hedge-wife," the woman said with dignity, pouring tea into mismatched cups. "Witch is what the temple called me when they came through twelve years back and burned my mother's books and read my name off their lists of *abominations practicing within sight of holy ground*. Maren Thistlewood. Formerly of the valley, formerly licensed, formerly respectable." She handed Silas a cup. "Now I'm formerly everything, and I find it very restful."

"Maren," Ember repeated, tasting the name, and Maren Thistlewood looked at the child again and had to turn and fuss violently with the kettle to hide whatever crossed her face.

"How did you know we were coming?" Silas asked.

"Bees told me. Bees always tell me things, useless gossip mostly, but tonight they were beside themselves." Maren sat down across from them, and the humor drained out of her like tea from a tipped cup. "Third bell rang in the valley. Wardens on the east road with fire-pikes. And a bird — a wren, if you'll believe it, sent by an old friend in the temple laundry — says the High Priest has offered a pardon and fifty crowns for the return of 'a stolen relic and the apostate scribe who took it.'" Her gray eyes fixed on Silas. "That'll be you two, then."

"Apostate scribe," Silas repeated. It should have frightened her. It did frighten her. But underneath the fear, some stubborn buried thing turned over in its sleep and warmed itself at the word. *Apostate.* Twelve years of copying prayers she couldn't feel, erased in a single line on a warden's list. It was the freest she had ever been described.

"They'll come at dawn," Maren said. "Dogs lost you at the stream, but dogs aren't the only thing Corvane keeps. He keeps *papers*. Names, debts, informants. He'll know every door you might knock on within a day." She drummed her fingers on the table, and the candles in the bathtub outside flickered in unison, and she swore softly at them and they stopped. "You can't stay. You can't go back. Which leaves forward, and forward means the border cities, and the border cities mean the dragon-blooded exiles, and *they*—" she pointed her spoon at Ember, and her hand was not quite steady, "*they* will smell her the moment she crosses the city gate. Half the exiled houses have spent two centuries dreaming of dragon-fire to burn the temples down. They won't ask her permission any more than Corvane would."

Silas looked at Ember, who was wrapped in a blanket three sizes too big, cradling a cup of honey-heavy tea, watching the fire in the hearth with an expression of deep kinship. The most wanted child in the world, and she was trying to figure out how to toast bread on the hearth-flames without asking.

"So everywhere we run," Silas said slowly, "someone wants to own her."

"Now you're learning," Maren said. "Welcome to the outside, scribe. It's the valley's problem too, mind — everyone's. Magic's dying everywhere else, thinning out like ale at a wedding. Whatever she carries may be the last of it." She leaned forward. "So here's my offer. Rest tonight. I'll ward the house till it squeaks. At first light I walk you to the crossroads myself, because you two wouldn't survive a market square, let alone a court. And after that—" she spread her hands, calloused and scarred and steady, "—we improvise."

"Why?" Silas asked quietly. "Why help us? You said yourself the temples burned your mother's books. You owe them nothing. Helping us makes you a target again."

Maren was quiet a moment. Outside, a goat complained philosophically.

"When I was a girl," she said at last, "my mother told me the dragons weren't monsters. That the old stories were written by the winners, same as always. Everyone in the valley says that kind of talk is madness." Her jaw set. "Well. I've been mad for forty years, and tonight madness walked up my path and knocked politely and wiped her feet. Seems the least I can do is pour it some tea."

Ember looked up from the hearth. "Thank you," she said, carefully, in valley-speech, and then added something in the old tongue — soft, melodic, a phrase that made the hearth-fire stand up straighter and burn brighter, and made Maren's eyes go wide, and made Silas's heart ache in a way she couldn't name.

"What did you say?" Silas whispered.

Ember smiled the small crooked smile. "I said: *the fire remembers kindness longest.*"

Far to the west, faint but unmistakable, a horn blew once from the valley's edge — a warden's signal, answered by another, nearer than Silas liked. Dawn was hours off yet, and the hunt had not waited for it.

Maren was already on her feet, blowing out candles. "Change of plan," she said briskly. "We leave tonight."
