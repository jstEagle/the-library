## 8. The Court of Ash and Salt

Maren Thistlewick had been thrown out of finer cities than Ashfall, and she'd been thrown out of worse ones too, which she considered a respectable average for a woman of her talents and temperament.

"Keep your hood up," she murmured to Silas, who walked beside her with the stiff-backed gait of someone who had never in her life been asked to look inconspicuous. "And you—" she glanced down at Ember, who was doing an admirable impression of a mud puddle that had learned to walk, "—try to look like a child. A boring child. A child nobody would want."

Ember looked up at her with those banked-coal eyes and said something long and liquid and utterly untranslatable.

"Aye, well. Same to you, little dragon."

The city rose out of the salt marshes like a bad memory: Ashfall, last stop before the borderlands proper, built on the bones of a harbor the sea had abandoned two hundred years ago. The streets were packed chalk-white earth, and the buildings were the color of old bone, and everything smelled faintly of brine and woodsmoke and, underneath it all, something Maren had hoped never to smell again—the ozone tang of working magic. Real magic. The kind that made your teeth ache.

Three days past the valley's edge, Silas had stopped flinching at it. Mostly.

"It's louder here," Silas said quietly. It was the first thing she'd said since dawn. She'd said very little since the ford—since the wardens, since Maren had watched her own basket of charms scatter into the current while she ran instead of fought, and hated herself for it in the thorough, methodical way she hated herself for most things.

"Louder," Maren agreed. "That's what I said. You weren't listening. You were busy looking tragic."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You've got a look. Like a woman who's read her own funeral sermon and found the punctuation wanting." Maren adjusted the strap of her pack and steered them left, away from the harbor road, where a knot of soldiers in ash-grey livery were checking papers. "We want the Saltmarket. There's a house there. Old friends."

"Your friends tried to sell me a cursed comb once," Silas said. "You told me about them. On the road. At length."

"The comb was *fairly* priced considering the curse was mild. And that was different people. Mostly." Maren caught Silas's expression. "Look, scribe. You want sanctuary? Sanctuary costs. In the valley it cost you copying prayers till your eyes bled. Out here it costs favors, and favors cost knowing people, and knowing people means knowing people you'd rather not. That's the whole arithmetic of being alive outside your pretty temple walls."

Silas said nothing, but she shifted Ember closer to her side, and Maren noted it with a professional's eye: the scribe had gone from cargo-carrier to mother-hen somewhere on the road, and hadn't noticed the change herself. Interesting. Dangerous, possibly. But interesting.

* * *

The house in the Saltmarket looked like nothing—a narrow-fronted building squeezed between a chandler's shop and a shrine to some drowned god, its windows fogged with salt-spray, its door painted the grey-green of old copper. Maren knocked twice, paused, then knocked three more times in a pattern that meant, in the old hedge-cant, *it's only me and I'm probably not followed.*

The door opened on a chain. One dark eye appeared in the gap.

"You're supposed to be dead," said the eye.

"Reports of my death were greatly exaggerated by me, actually. I started the rumor myself. Saved on funeral expenses."

The eye blinked. The door shut. A long moment passed—long enough for Maren to say "patience" through her teeth—and then the chain rattled off and the door swung wide on Lady Sefira Veyra's youngest cousin, or niece, or some relation Maren had never bothered to keep straight, wrapped in a dressing gown and holding a candle like she intended to stab someone with it.

"Maren Thistlewick," said the woman. "In my doorway. With *children*."

"One child. One adult who behaves like one. Can we come in before the whole street decides to remember us?"

They came in. The house inside was larger than its front suggested—it went back and back, rooms opening into rooms, hung with salt-stained tapestries and lit by lamps that burned without oil. Maren saw Silas notice the lamps. Saw her hand twitch toward where her ink-stained fingers remembered pens instead of charms.

The cousin-niece—Ottavia, that was her name, Ottavia Veyra—set the candle down and looked them over the way a fishwife looks over a doubtful catch.

"You're running from the Temple of Quiet Hours," she said. Not a question.

"We're running *past* it. Different thing entirely."

"There are temple wardens in Ashfall. Six arrived yesterday. Grey cloaks, silver cords." Ottavia's gaze slid to Ember, who had gone very still by the cold hearth, and lingered there. "And this morning, every noble house in exile received a letter describing a girl. Mud-colored hair. Eyes like coals. Carrying a sigil that hasn't been seen in living memory." She turned back to Maren, and her voice dropped to something soft and terrible. "What in the deep salt have you brought into my house?"

* * *

It was Ember herself who ended the argument—or began it; with Ember it was hard to tell the difference.

Ottavia wanted them out before dark. Maren wanted wine and a locked room. Silas wanted, visibly, to be anywhere else but was too proud to say so. And then Ember walked to the hearth, knelt, and pressed her palm flat against the blackened stone.

The fire that answered her wasn't fire. It was a sigil, drawn in light on the soot: a coiled shape, winged, ancient, burning white-gold at the edges. Maren had seen dragon-marks before—in smuggled rubbings, in the margins of forbidden bestiaries—but never *alive*, never moving, never humming at a frequency that made the lamp-flames lean toward it like courtiers toward a king.

Ottavia made a sound like she'd been struck.

"That's the Mark of the First Clutch," she whispered. "That's—that's the seal of the Old Courts. That's—" She rounded on Maren. "Do you know what you're carrying? Do you have any idea?"

"Some idea," Maren said. "Increasingly detailed ideas daily."

"This child could raise the exiled houses. This child could put Lord Veyra back in his seat at Court. This child could burn the Temple of Quiet Hours to its foundation stones and salt the ashes." Ottavia's face had transformed; the suspicion had burned away and left something younger and hungrier underneath. "Where did you find her?"

"She found us," Silas said.

Everyone turned. The scribe had risen from her chair, and there was something new in her voice—not the flat temple cadence, not the frightened whisper of the road, but something between. Steel that hadn't decided yet whether it was a sword or a lock.

"She came to us. She was locked in a reliquary by the high priests of my order, who intend to cut the fire out of her and use it to crown themselves over every kingdom on the coast. We took her because leaving her was unthinkable. We keep her because she is a child, not a weapon. If your houses mean to make her a banner, you should know now: I have burned scrolls for less."

A silence followed. Maren, who had spent thirty years reading rooms, read this one carefully. Ottavia's hunger didn't fade—hunger like that doesn't fade—but it put on manners, the way hungry things do when they've spotted a bigger animal.

"You speak boldly for a debt-bound scribe," Ottavia said.

"I speak accurately. It's a habit they never managed to beat out of me."

"Well," said Maren brightly, into the charged air, "now that everyone's threatened everyone, shall we eat? Because if the wardens are going to kick this door in tonight, I refuse to meet my end hungry."

* * *

They ate salt cod and pickled fennel and bread that was mostly ash-adjacent, and afterward Ottavia sent word up the hill, and by midnight the house was full of exiles.

Maren had forgotten what the dragon-blooded looked like when gathered. They were beautiful the way storms are beautiful—all of them tall, all of them carrying some trace of the old inheritance: eyes like furnace doors, hair that moved against the wind, hands too warm for comfort. They wore their poverty like costume jewelry, threadbare velvet and paste jewels, and they spoke of vengeance in the tones other people used for weather.

And through the middle of them walked Lord Cassian Veyra himself, who had crossed half the marshes when the message came, and who knelt in a borrowed sitting room before a mud-streaked eight-year-old with the reverence of a man before an altar.

"Little flame," he said. His voice was honey poured slow. "Do you know what you are?"

Ember looked at him for a long moment. Then she said something in the old tongue—three words, low and clear—and every dragon-blooded exile in the room went pale as bone.

"What did she say?" Maren asked Silas under the noise.

Silas's face was strange. "She said... 'I know what I am. Do you?'"

Veyra laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm as brandy, and Maren thought: *there's the danger.* Not cruelty. Charm. He rose and turned to Silas with his hand extended, as though they were equals meeting at a garden party.

"And you must be the scribe. Ottavia says you burned scrolls for this child. I confess I'm intrigued. A woman of the Quiet Hours with a spine—it's like finding a pearl in a codfish."

"I get that a lot lately," Silas said, not taking the hand.

"No?" He withdrew it gracefully. "Forgive me. Exile makes one formal. We lost our courts, our seats, our names in the ledgers of the realm. All we kept was each other and the old dream. And now—" he gestured at Ember, and his eyes went soft and shining and utterly false, "—the old dream has walked through your door wearing muddy boots."

"And what exactly," Maren asked, "is the old dream when it's at home?"

"Justice," said Veyra simply. "The temples stole the fire. Stole our birthright, slaughtered our kin, wrote lies in their holy books and called it scripture. For a thousand years we've been scattered and diminished, hiding in border cities, marrying out, fading." He spread his hands. "And now the last true flame arrives, carried by a child, guarded by an honest scribe and a disreputable hedge-witch. Tell me that isn't providence."

"It isn't providence," Maren said. "I don't believe in it. I believe in luck, and luck is just providence that hasn't introduced itself yet."

He smiled at her. It didn't reach anywhere important. "Then call it luck."

Later—much later, when the exiles had gone up the hill to report to the others and the house was quiet—Maren sat on the doorstep with a cup of something medicinal and watched the lamps of Ashfall gutter in the salt wind.

Silas joined her. Ember was asleep inside, curled in a real bed for the first time since the reliquary, and even asleep her fingers moved in little patterns, drawing sigils on the blanket.

"They'll help us," Silas said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Oh, they'll help. That's not in doubt." Maren drank. "Question is what the helping costs, and who does the paying. Did you see Veyra's face when the girl marked the hearth? That wasn't awe, scribe. Well—it was awe. But awe was only the first course. Underneath it he was measuring her. Like a man measuring a hearthstone for how much heat it'll give his hall."

"He saved us from the wardens' letters. Ottavia will hide us."

"For now. Until hiding us stops being useful and pointing us becomes more so." Maren turned the cup in her hands. "I've known his family since before you were born, girl. The Veyras aren't evil. That's the trouble. Evil you can guard against. But they're hungry, and they've been starving a long time, and starving folk will tell themselves any story that puts bread in their mouths. Right now the story is: the child is our salvation. Give it time. The story becomes: the child is *ours*. And after that: the child is a weapon we aim at the temple. And after that—"

"After that she's in a reliquary again," Silas said quietly. "Just one with better tapestries."

Maren looked at her sideways. "You learn fast for a woman raised copying prayers."

"I had a good teacher of suspicion. She talks too much, but her instincts are sound."

"*Rude.* Accurate, but rude." Maren drained the cup and stood, joints crackling like green kindling. Inside, the lamp that burned without oil flickered—just once, just slightly, in a rhythm that might have been wind and might have been a message, and both of them felt the magic in the walls shift like a sleeper turning over.

Because that was when the knock came at the front door. Three sharp raps, then two, then one—the pattern Ottavia's watchers used to signal a warden patrol passing. Except the pattern was wrong. Reversed. And reversed, in the old hedge-cant, it meant something else entirely.

It meant: *they're not passing. They've stopped. And they're asking for you by name.*

Maren set down the cup. Down the street, six grey cloaks stood in the moonlight at the mouth of the alley, and the one at their center held up a letter sealed with the crest of the Temple of Quiet Hours, and called out in a voice like a closing ledger:

"Maren Thistlewick. Lady Ottavia Veyra. We've come to discuss terms regarding the child."

Behind her, she heard Silas breathe one word—"Corvane"—and heard Ember wake upstairs with a small, terrible sound, like a match being struck in a dark room.

"Well," Maren said, to no one in particular, as she reached for the door. "Terms. My favorite subject. Let's see what the temple's paying these days."
