## 9. Lord Veyra's Bargain

Lord Cassian Veyra had learned long ago that the secret to power was patience, and the secret to patience was good wine. He poured himself a third measure of the amber spirit distilled in the salt-caves beneath his estate — a drink that tasted of smoke and old grievances, which he considered appropriate — and watched his guests through the window of his study.

They were crossing the courtyard below. The scribe woman walked like someone counting her steps against a debt only she could see. The hedge-witch walked like someone who had already stolen the silverware and was looking for the exit. And between them, small hand tucked into the scribe's, walked the child.

The child who carried fire.

"Three guests," said Serah, his captain, from the doorway. "One of them bites. The witch tried to pick my pocket on the stairs."

"And did she succeed?"

"She got my knife."

Veyra smiled. "Then she has taste. Send up bread and the good honey. If we are going to make monsters of these people, we should at least feed them first."

* * *

The study smelled of cedar and candle smoke and, faintly, of salt from the caves below — the whole house was built over them, as if Lord Veyra wished to be reminded, even asleep, of what had been taken. The walls were lined not with portraits but with maps, and not one map showed the borders the kingdoms agreed upon. Every border here was drawn in ash.

Silas Sandoval stood in the center of the room holding her cap in both hands like a student called before a headmaster. Maren lounged against a bookshelf with the practiced ease of a cat pretending it hadn't knocked anything over. Ember sat on the floor by the fire because chairs were, apparently, for people with less important things to do than stare into flames.

"You honor us with your hospitality," Silas said, in the careful cadence of someone reciting courtesy she did not feel.

"I honor you with nothing yet," Veyra said pleasantly, gesturing them toward seats. "Hospitality is what I'm deciding whether to offer. Sit. Eat. The bread is better than anything your temple ever served you — I know, I grew up on their charity. Dry as penance."

Maren took a roll immediately. Silas hesitated, then nudged Ember toward the table, and the girl ate with the solemn concentration of someone who remembered hunger as a permanent condition rather than an event.

Veyra let them eat. This too was craft: never negotiate with empty mouths, they bite; never negotiate with full ones, they sleep. Somewhere in the middle was honesty.

"You crossed four hundred miles of warden-patrolled country," he said at last, "with a dragon-child, a runaway scribe, and—" he nodded at Maren, "—whatever that is."

"Hedge-witch," Maren said around bread. "It's on my card."

"—and you came to my city, to my court of ash and salt, because every other door closed behind you. So let us skip the part where we pretend this is a social call." He leaned forward. "You need protection. I can give it. Walls no warden breaches, gold enough to buy silence in three capitals, and a name that still makes temple priests check over their shoulders."

Silas set down her cup. "And in exchange?"

There it was. She didn't flinch from it, didn't dress it up. He found himself, unexpectedly, liking her for it. It made what he intended to do more difficult, which annoyed him, so he stopped liking her.

"The child carries the last dragon-fire," Veyra said. "I want it."

Ember looked up from her bread. Her eyes caught the lamplight and held it a moment too long, the way embers hold heat after the log's gone dark.

"Not *her*," Silas said quietly. Not a question. A wall going up brick by brick.

"No one wants the *child*, Lady Scribe. Children grow, forget, marry, die of ordinary fevers. What I want is what she carries. My family was dragon-blooded when your temple was teaching peasants that dragons were demons. We kept the old pacts. We sheltered the last clutches when the temples came with their forged doctrines and their holy knives." His voice stayed level, but he could hear the salt in it himself. "Nine hundred years of exile. Nine hundred years watching lesser men wear crowns bought with our cousins' bones. And now, at last, the means of reckoning walks through my gate covered in mud and eating my honey."

"So it's vengeance," Silas said.

"It's *justice*."

"They look alike from inside," said Maren, mildly, examining her nails. "I've been both. They're twins, and one of them lies."

Veyra turned on her. "You were banished for selling love-charms to married lords."

"I was banished for *accurately pricing* love-charms to married lords. There's a difference, and it's the difference between us, my lord. Everything has a price. The question is who pays." She tipped her head toward Ember. "Because it won't be you."

The room went very quiet. Ember had stopped eating. She was watching Veyra with that unnerving stillness children have when they understand far more than their years allow, or perhaps when their years are not what anyone believes.

"I'm not proposing harm," Veyra said, gentler now, because he knew how to modulate a room the way other men modulated their voices. "A rite of transfer. Freely consented, when she's older, when she understands—"

"She's six," Silas said.

"She's the last vessel of a fire that burned before your kingdom had a name. Age is a matter of perspective."

"Age is a matter of *teeth*," snapped Maren, "and hers haven't finished coming in. Listen to yourself. You're doing sums on a child's life and calling it arithmetic."

Veyra rose and walked to the window. Below, his household moved through the evening routines — guards changing, lamps lit, the ordinary machinery of an extraordinary exile. He had built all this on grievance, stone by stone, and grievance was load-bearing. He knew that. He'd known it for thirty years and built anyway.

"Lady Scribe," he said, without turning. "Tell me what you know of the betrayal."

He heard her shift. "That the first temples forged doctrine to justify slaughtering the dragons. That their fire was harvested to fuel the priesthood's power. That my order's oldest codex contains the sigil she draws in her sleep."

"And does your scripture tell you what the fire was *for*? Before it was stolen?" Now he turned. "It was a covenant. The dragons gave it freely — warmth for the valley, light for the long winters, magic seeded into the bloodlines of those who kept faith. It was never meant to be owned. It was meant to be *tended*. And every generation since, some clever man has decided he alone could tend it better. Your High Priest Corvane. The crown princes. Me, if I'm honest on my worse days." He smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "I am offering you protection, Lady Scribe, because I believe I would be a better keeper of that flame than Corvane, who will burn the child alive within the month. You may weigh me against him. But do not pretend the scale has a third option marked 'nobody.'"

Silas was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the steadiness of someone who had spent years copying words and had finally learned to mean one.

"There is a third option. You just haven't considered it, because none of us have. Nobody holds the fire. It goes back to being given freely — to whoever needs warmth, whoever asks. Not a weapon for Corvane. Not fuel for your war. Given. Like it was at the beginning."

"That's not how power works."

"No," Silas agreed. "It's how *gifts* work. Power is what happens when people stop being able to give things."

Veyra stared at her. Somewhere in the walls, the sea-salt caves ticked and settled, the way old wounds do at night.

"You sound like a sermon," he said finally.

"I should. I copied ten thousand of them. It's the first one I've written myself."

Maren snorted into her wine. Ember tugged Silas's sleeve and whispered something too low to catch, and whatever it was, it made the scribe's mouth twitch despite everything.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the guests had been shown to rooms they probably wouldn't sleep in — Veyra stood again at his window, and Serah joined him with two glasses and no commentary, which was why she was captain.

"They'll refuse," she said.

"They've refused."

"You'll let them go?"

"I'll let them go." He swirled the amber spirit. "Corvane's wardens will do the work either way, and I won't have it said that House Veyra murders children under its own roof. My grandfather drew a line there. It's the only line any of us ever managed to keep."

Serah drank. "But."

"But." He sighed. "You don't survive nine centuries of exile by letting valuable things walk out your gate unmarked, Captain. If the fire falls into Corvane's hands, I need to know where it went. If it goes free—" he almost laughed at the absurdity of it, "—if it goes *free*, then the world changes shape, and I intend to at least have a map of the new one."

"What kind of mark?"

"The old kind." He set down the glass. "Fetch the signet. The one we don't show guests."

She left. Veyra looked out at the dark harbor, at the ships riding anchor with lanterns dimmed, at the city of exiles and smugglers and dragon-blooded remnants that had grown around his grievance like coral around a wreck. Beautiful, in its way. Built entirely of what remained after something larger died.

He wondered, briefly, what Silas Sandoval would build instead. Then he put the thought away, the way he put away all thoughts that cost more than they returned.

When Serah returned with the signet ring — black iron, older than the kingdoms, shaped like a coiled wing — he pressed it into warm wax and sealed the mark onto a scrap of parchment, then onto the doorpost of the room where Ember slept, invisible unless you knew to look, felt unless you were looking for it.

Not a curse. He wasn't Corvane, whatever the witch thought. Just a thread. A way of finding them across any distance, through any storm. A lord's claim, quiet and patient, on a thing that had refused to be claimed.

"They'll feel it eventually," Serah said.

"By then I'll have decided whether I'm their enemy or their ally. Possibly both. Possibly neither." He smiled at his own reflection in the dark glass. "That's diplomacy."

Down the hall, in the guest room, he heard the scribe's voice, low and steady, telling the child something — a story, maybe, or a prayer, though he doubted Silas believed in prayers anymore. And beneath her voice, faint as a pulse, he heard the other sound. The one that had woken him at night since they arrived. The sound of something ancient turning over in its sleep, dreaming of open skies.

"Freely given," he murmured, tasting the words like the salt-wine. "We'll see, little fire. We'll see who gives you, and who takes, and what's left of us all when the bill comes due."

Far off across the plains, beyond his harbor, beyond his maps, temple bells began to ring — wrong hours, urgent hours, the tolling that meant wardens mobilized and roads watched. Veyra listened, and counted, and understood that whatever choice the scribe made next, she would make it running.

He poured one last glass, and waited for morning.
