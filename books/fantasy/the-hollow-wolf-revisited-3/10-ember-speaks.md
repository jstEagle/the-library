## 10. Ember Speaks

Ember had three names before she had one.

The first was a sound like wind through a burning forest — too large for a girl's mouth, too old for any living tongue. The second was the word the dragon-memory used when it spoke to itself in the dark: *little hearth*, *small flame*, *the last warm thing*. The third was "Ember," given by Silas Sandoval on their third night together, when the girl had pointed at herself and made the shape of the question with her eyebrows, because even people who cannot speak need names.

Silas had been feeding her barley soup. She'd blown on the spoon, tested it against her own lip the way mothers do without knowing they do it, and said, "You're small. You're bright. You keep things alive." A pause. A crooked almost-smile. "Ember."

The dragon-memory had stirred at that, deep beneath Ember's ribs, and turned the name over like a jeweler examining an unfamiliar stone. Then, grudgingly, the way an ancient creature admits anything: *It will do.*

It would do. It had done. And now, on the fourth night since they'd fled Lord Veyra's hall of ash and salt, sitting in Maren's cottage while the hedge-witch snored like a landslide and rain ticked against the shutters, Ember sat across the table from Silas and decided — with the full weight of a thousand years of memory pressing down behind her eyes — that tonight she would try to give the scribe the truth.

Because the truth had teeth. And because Silas deserved to be bitten before anyone else got the chance.

* * *

Here is what it is like inside Ember's head.

Imagine a library where every book is on fire but none of them burn. Imagine standing in that library forever, unable to read most of the shelves, hearing the books whisper to each other in a language of heat and hunger. That was the dragon-memory. It did not speak in words, mostly. It spoke in *knowings* — sudden, total understandings that arrived the way a headache arrives, except instead of pain there was history.

Tonight the memory had been restless. It had paced the inside of her skull like a wolf in a pen too small, and every time Ember looked at Silas — bent over Maren's table, mending a torn sleeve by candlelight, tongue caught between her teeth in concentration — the pacing stopped. The wolf lay down. The books went quiet.

And then one book opened all by itself.

*Show her,* said the memory, in the voice that was not a voice. *Or I will.*

That was the trouble with carrying something ancient. Ancient things had no patience. They had watched mountains rise and wear down to gravel; they could wait out a kingdom, a war, a whole age of the world — but they could not wait for a nervous woman to finish darning a sleeve.

Ember reached across the table and took Silas's hand.

Silas froze. Her needle hung mid-stitch. "Ember?"

The girl didn't answer with her mouth. Words were hard for her still — they came out crooked and small, like birds learning to fly in a barn. Instead she closed her eyes, and opened hers.

* * *

Fire, first. Always fire first.

Ember let Silas see the way the memory began: a world stitched with warmth, dragons coiled in the high places like great patient cats, their breath sinking into the roots of things so that wheat grew gold and rivers ran sweet and magic moved through the world the way blood moves through a body — everywhere, unnoticed, essential.

Then the temples came. Not with armies at first. With *gratitude*.

*This is the part they will not have told you,* the memory murmured, and Ember felt Silas's hand tighten in hers. *This is the part we showed only to the ones who asked nicely.*

She saw priests kneeling before dragons — human figures, small and earnest, offering gifts of song and incense and written praise. The dragons, vain in the gentle way of very old creatures, preened. They taught the priests the grammar of fire. They gave freely, as they had always given freely, because giving was what fire *did*. You did not hoard warmth. You shared it or you smothered.

And the priests learned. Oh, how they learned. Ember felt Silas's breath go shallow as the knowing bloomed: the first rites were real. The first blessings were true. For a hundred years, temple and dragon lived in something like love.

*And then,* said the memory, and its voice went cold the way coals go cold, from the outside in, *they wrote it down.*

Silas flinched. Ember understood why. Of course she did. Silas was a scribe; ink was her sacrament. To learn that writing itself had been the weapon —

The knowing unspooled: the doctrine drafted in secret, the careful rewording of the oldest prayers until *freely given* became *rightfully owed*, became *justly taken*, became, at last, *duty of the faithful to harvest.* The council of high priests who signed the order with quills dipped not in ink but in stolen flame, diluted a thousand times, enough to bless a sword, not enough to save a dragon.

The war that followed was short. Dragons are strong, but strength means nothing against betrayal wearing your own gift. One by one, the high places went dark. One by one, the fires were carried away in iron vessels and poured into reliquaries and vaults and the foundations of temples, and the world outside the temples forgot what warmth had ever felt like on its own terms.

Except the valley. The valley they emptied completely. Stripped it bare, ward by ward, prayer by prayer, until no magic could live there at all — a vault disguised as a holy place, a prison shaped like paradise, where nothing that remembered could ever grow.

*They built your home as a lockbox,* the memory said, gently, terribly. *And they put you in it, little scribe, and called it service.*

* * *

When Ember opened her eyes, Silas was crying.

Not loudly. Silas never did anything loudly. Tears simply stood in her eyes the way rain stands in clouds before it decides, and her face had gone very still, the stillness of someone holding a door shut against a flood.

"The codex," Silas whispered. "In the restricted archive. The sigil you drew — the dragon — it was in the margins of the oldest text, annotated out, argued against. Someone tried to erase it and couldn't." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I copied that page forty times. I thought the margin note was heresy."

"It was," Ember said.

Both of them blinked. It was the longest sentence Ember had ever managed aloud, and it had come out clean, unbroken, the words flying straight instead of tumbling. The dragon-memory hummed with satisfaction, insufferably pleased with itself, and Ember shot it an internal glare. *Yes, fine, well done, don't gloat.*

Silas laughed — a wet, startled sound. "You spoke."

"Words are... doors," Ember said, and frowned at her own clumsiness, and tried again. "Before, the doors were heavy. Now—" She pressed a hand to her sternum, where the fire lived. "It opens them for me. When it wants to be known." She hesitated. "It has wanted to be known by you for a long time."

Something moved through Silas's expression then, quick and complicated — fear wearing hope's clothing, or maybe the reverse. "Why me? I'm nobody. I'm a debt with a pen attached."

"You're not," Ember said fiercely, and surprised herself with the fierceness. "You're the one who blew on the soup."

Silas stared at her. "The— what?"

"You tested it. Before me. Every time." Ember struggled, reaching for the knowing underneath the feeling. "The priests who took the fire, they measured everything first. Worth. Yield. Cost. They held the cup up to the light to count what was in it before they drank. You—" She shook her head, frustrated with language, with its smallness. "You put the spoon against your own lip. First. Every time. You check if it will hurt before you give it."

The candle between them guttered, steadied, and burned a shade brighter. Neither of them remarked on it. Both of them noticed.

* * *

Maren snored on, gloriously oblivious, one boot off and one boot on, having fallen asleep mid-argument with herself about whether the wards on the cottage needed renewing before dawn. (They did. They always did. Maren's wards were less a wall than a polite suggestion, and the local spirits treated them accordingly.)

Ember looked at Silas across the candlelight and knew the hardest part was still ahead. The memory pressed at her, insistent: *Tell her. Tell her all of it.*

"I have to tell you something else," Ember said quietly. "And you won't like it. And I need you to hear all of it before you decide anything."

"That's an ominous sentence," Silas said. Her hands had found each other in her lap, fingers laced tight, the scribe's habit of holding herself together. "Go on."

"The fire isn't just in me." Ember searched for the shape of it. "It's not a thing I *carry*, like a stone in a pocket. It's more like... a river. I'm the riverbed. The water moves through me. But water doesn't belong to its bed." She paused. "And rivers choose their courses."

Silas went very still.

"When Veyra wanted my fire, I could feel it pull toward him — the way it pulls toward anyone who wants it *for themselves*. Wanting is a kind of handle. The fire can be gripped." Ember's voice dropped. "But gripping isn't keeping. The dragons learned that at the end. Everything taken from them curdled. Every stolen flame rots the thief, slow, from the inside. Corvane's reliquaries are full of rot. That's what's wrong with him. That's why his kindness looks like kindness but feels like a trap. He's mostly rot now, with a man-shaped shell around it."

"And me?" Silas asked. Her voice was barely there. "What does the fire feel when it looks at me?"

Ember took a breath. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the shutters like impatient fingers.

"It doesn't look at you," she said. "It *leans*. Like a plant toward a window." She reached across the table again, palm up this time, open. "It has been leaning toward you since the reliquary. Since before. It knows something about you that you don't know yet, and it's terribly smug about it, and I'm sorry, because what it knows is going to frighten you."

"Ember." Silas's hands had gone white in her lap. "Tell me."

*Now,* said the dragon-memory, soft as ash falling.

"The fire chooses its bearer," Ember said. "It always has. In the old days, the dragons didn't pick their heirs by blood. The fire picked. It went where it was loved freely — not worshipped, not feared, not *wanted*. Just loved. And it's been alone for a thousand years, locked in a child who can't hold all of it, passed down through girls who broke under the weight, and it is so tired, Silas, it is so tired of being treasure—"

Her voice caught. Inside her, the vast old grief of the last dragon rolled over like a whale surfacing, and for a moment she wasn't a girl at a table at all but something enormous and wounded remembering the sky.

"—and it has chosen," she finished, barely above the rain. "It chose you. It's choosing you right now. It's asking."

The candleflame leaned toward Silas. Actually leaned, bending sideways against the draftless air, straining toward her like a dog toward a whistle only it could hear.

Silas looked at it. Then at Ember. Then at her own hands, as if expecting them to have changed, to be glowing or clawed or somehow officially different — but they were just a scribe's hands, ink-stained and trembling.

"I copy prayers," she said numbly. "I've never once felt one. I told you that. The valley eats it all. There's nothing in me for a fire to want."

"There's everything in you," Ember said. "That's exactly the problem. Don't you see? It's not asking for worship. It's asking for *you*. Your years. Your warmth. Freely given — the way the first gift was given, before it was ever written down wrong. And freely given can't be taken back, and freely given will change you, and—" Her throat tightened. "And I don't want it to take you. I've lost everyone. Everyone who ever carried me ended up a story someone else tells. I don't want you to become a story. I want you to stay at the table, blowing on the soup."

Silence. Rain. Maren snored, turned over, muttered something about insolent spirits, slept on.

Silas unclasped her hands. Looked at the leaning flame for a long, long moment. And then — being Silas, being the woman who checked the spoon against her own lip first — she did the most frightening thing Ember had ever seen anyone do.

She smiled. Small and crooked and terrified.

"Well," she said, "at least somebody finally wants me around. Even if it's several centuries old and on fire."

"Silas—"

"Don't ask me to answer tonight." She held up a hand, and her voice wobbled but held. "A person should get to sleep once after learning her entire religion was built on a lie before she decides whether to become a lamp. That's just reasonable."

Ember laughed despite everything — a real laugh, surprising them both, bright and cracked and young.

And that was when the wards went off.

Not Maren's polite suggestion-wards. Something else — a sharp, ugly shriek of disturbed magic rolling up from the tree line, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the rhythmic clink of chain mail and the disciplined tread of many boots moving through wet grass.

Maren sat bolt upright, both boots somehow still half-on, hair standing like startled grass. "Wardens," she said flatly, all sleep gone. "Lots of them. Coming up the ford road." She looked at Silas, at Ember, at the candle still bending toward the scribe like a compass finding north, and her weathered face went grim.

"They didn't follow you here, girls," she said, reaching for her staff. "They were *sent*. Somebody told them exactly where you'd be."

Ember's hand found Silas's in the dark, and the fire inside her banked low, ready, listening — and far away in the night, at the edge of hearing, the river began to run loud.
