## 11. Wardens at the Ford

The river announced itself a full hour before we saw it, first as a change in the birdsong — more of it, brighter, the way sound behaves near open water — and then as a smell that made Ember stop dead in the middle of the road and inhale like a woman greeting a lover.

"Fish," she said, in her half-language of old words and gestures. She patted her own stomach twice. "Fish. Fish."

"You had bread this morning," I said.

She gave me the look she'd perfected over two weeks of travel: the look of someone who found my objections charming but irrelevant. It was remarkable how much like Maren she'd become. Remarkable, and slightly alarming, because Maren had taught her that look deliberately, as one teaches a child to hold a knife.

Maren herself was a hundred paces ahead, walking with her staff planted at each step like a surveyor measuring the world for sale. "There'll be a ford," she called back without turning around. "There's always a ford where the willows go down to the water. My grandmother used to say the rivers are honest — they show you exactly where they mean to let you cross."

"Your grandmother sounds like she talked a great deal."

"She never stopped talking. Died mid-sentence, which everyone agreed was fitting." Maren paused at the crest of a low hill and squinted. "Ah. There it is. And there's our problem."

I came up beside her. The valley behind us was already a memory — I hadn't looked back in three days, partly from discipline and partly because every time I did, my chest did something architectural and unsound. Ahead, the river ran broad and silver-brown through a shallow bowl of meadow, and the ford was exactly where Maren had promised: a wide gravel shoal where the water thinned to ankle-depth and the willows leaned over it like gossips.

The problem was the four horses tethered among those willows.

"Wardens," I said. My voice came out flat, which surprised me. Somewhere between burning the preparation scrolls and crossing the border, I had apparently acquired a voice for bad news. It sounded nothing like mine.

"Temple grey and temple cut," Maren confirmed. She counted on her fingers, an old hedge-witch habit that always looked faintly comic against the gravity of what she counted. "Four riders. Could be tax men. Tax men don't wear boots you could boil soup in, though. Those are warden boots."

"How do you know so much about warden boots?"

"I sold them liniment for thirty years. You learn a lot about a man from his feet." She turned to look at us both, and her humor folded itself away somewhere, tucked into a pocket like spare coins. "They're not here by accident, Silas. Corvane knows which way we went. Which means he knows where the crossings are. Which means—"

"Which means this is the easy part," I finished. "And there's a harder part coming."

"There's always a harder part coming. That's not prophecy, dear, that's just Tuesdays." She rapped her staff on the ground. "Here's how it is. They want the girl. They may want you — Corvane strikes me as a man who ties off loose threads, and you, love, are unraveling at speed. But mostly they want Ember. So Ember goes into the trees, quiet as anything, and waits until either you come get her or the crows tell her it's safe."

"And you?" I asked, though I already knew. I think I'd known since the moment I saw the horses. There is a particular dread that arrives ahead of its own explanation, like thunder before lightning.

"Me, I'm going to walk down there and be a nuisance," Maren said cheerfully. "An old woman alone on the road is the most invisible creature in creation. Nobody looks at us. We're furniture. I'm going to be very confused, very deaf, and very slow, and by the time they've finished being annoyed at me, you two will be a mile downstream."

"Maren—"

"Don't." She held up one finger, and for a moment the humor left entirely and I saw the woman underneath — the one who'd been disgraced from some court or other for reasons she told differently every time, the one who'd taken in two fugitives out of what she claimed was boredom and I knew was something older and kinder. "Don't argue with me at the top of a hill, Silas. It's undignified. Argue with me later, when we've all got wine."

Ember tugged my sleeve. She looked from Maren to me and back, and whatever she read in our faces, she understood it perfectly — children always understand abandonment first, in their bones, before any language catches up. Her chin set in that stubborn line.

"No," she said. Old word, simple word. Then a flood of the older tongue, rapid and rising, her hands cutting shapes in the air: stay, together, no leaving, no—

"It's three hours, little ember," Maren said gently. "Maybe less. Then wine and arguing, both."

But Ember wasn't listening to Maren. She was looking at me, and her hand crept up and pressed flat against my sternum, right over the heart, the way she did when she wanted to say something too large for her broken vocabulary. Through the cloth of my shirt I felt warmth — not body-warmth, not quite. Something with an opinion.

*It has chosen you.* That was what she'd tried to tell me once, weeks ago, gesturing at my chest with an expression of terrible apology. I had chosen not to understand. I was becoming expert at choosing not to understand things.

"Go," I told her. "Trees. Downstream. If we're not there by dusk—" I couldn't finish it. She didn't make me. She kissed Maren's cheek, then mine, fierce and quick, and slipped into the willow-shadow like water finding its level, and then she was gone, and the morning felt emptier for it.

Maren straightened her shawl, checked that her knife was loose in its sheath, and became — visibly, astonishingly — a dotty old woman. Shoulders rounded. Eyes gone vague. Mouth slightly open in the manner of the perpetually bewildered. "Right," she said in her normal voice. "Wine and arguing. Don't be late." And she started down the hill toward the ford, humming.

* * *

What follows, I must set down honestly, because honesty is the only thing I have left that the Temple didn't give me.

I watched from the treeline, forty yards off, with a view of everything, and I did nothing. That is the shape of my cowardice: precise, well-positioned, with excellent sightlines.

Maren reached the ford at the same unhurried pace she'd have used to visit a neighbor. One of the wardens rose from his saddle to meet her — a big man, sun-reddened, with the patient look of someone who'd stood guard in a great many uninteresting places. She greeted him warmly. He shook his head. She pointed east, then west, then produced from her basket — she had brought her basket, gods preserve her, she had stopped to pack her basket — a jar of something, offering it around. The wardens clustered. For one shining minute it worked; it actually worked; they were laughing, or at least smirking, and Maren was edging them away from the willows, away from the downstream path, a shepherd of fools.

Then the tall one — the one who'd hung back, the one with a scribe's ink stains on his fingers even in temple grey — said something short and sharp, and everything changed.

He'd seen her eyes flick downstream. Or he'd simply grown bored of the game. Warden-Captain Vess, I learned his name later, and I hate that I know it, hate that it lives in my head now alongside scripture and my mother's face. He stepped past Maren's basket and put two fingers to his mouth and whistled, and the fourth rider — the one I hadn't even spotted, posted downstream among the reeds like a chess piece I'd failed to count — spurred toward the trees.

Toward Ember.

I heard myself say Maren's name. I don't remember deciding to say it. And Maren, bless her crooked heart, took the opening I'd handed her and ran with it — literally, gloriously, swinging her basket into the tall warden's face so that pickled something-or-other exploded across his cuirass, and jabbing her staff into the knee of the second, and shouting in three languages, two of which I'm fairly sure she invented on the spot, words that would have gotten her arrested in any port city I've ever heard described.

They took her. Of course they took her. Four trained wardens versus a seventy-year-old woman with a staff and a vocabulary of filth — it was never a fight, only a delay. But oh, what a delay. She went down under three of them still shouting directions at me — *downstream, you idiot, DOWNSTREAM* — and the last thing I saw before I turned and ran was Vess hauling her upright by the arm, and Maren spitting blood and grinning at him, actually grinning, because even captured she refused to give anyone the satisfaction of a frightened face.

The fourth rider crashed into the willows ahead of me.

I ran. I am not a runner — scribes develop strong wrists and weak everything else — but terror lends a certain athleticism. Branches whipped my face. The river roared somewhere to my left. Behind me I could hear the horse blundering through the undergrowth, the rider cursing, and ahead, small and fast and golden in the green light, Ember flew through the trees like she'd been born to forests instead of reliquaries.

He cut us off at the bank.

One moment the trees, the next a wall of horseflesh and temple grey, and the warden swung down with a net — a *net*, as if Ember were a fish, as if the last dragon-fire in the world were something you scooped — and I felt something in me snap, clean, like a bone.

"No," I said.

He looked at me with the flat professional patience of a man dealing with furniture. "Stand aside, madam. This doesn't concern—"

The warmth in my chest opened its eye.

I want to be precise about this, because everything afterward depends on precision. I did not decide to use it. I did not know how. What happened was simpler and far worse: I was afraid, and grieving, and furious, and the scrap of fire Ember had left in me — the fragment that had settled into my bones during some long night I couldn't name — *answered*. It answered the way a hound answers its master's whistle. Eagerly. Lovingly. Without any consultation whatsoever.

Light went out of my hands.

Not flame — I would understand flame later, flame has manners. This was light, white-gold, silent, and it struck the warden's net and unmade it. Not burned it. *Unmade* it, the ropes forgetting themselves into ash that drifted down like gray snow. The horse screamed and bolted. The warden fell back, and where the light had passed close to him his sword-hilt glowed dull red and he dropped it with a yelp, and he looked at me — at *me*, Silas Sandoval, debt-scribe, ink-stained, five-foot-nothing — with an expression I had never once received in twenty years of copying prayers.

Reverence. And underneath the reverence, fear.

"Stay back," I said, and my voice came out doubled, layered, as if something spoke through me and merely borrowed my throat. "Stay back or I swear by every god your Temple forgot, I will do worse than your net."

He stayed back. Wardens are brave men in aggregate and sensible men individually, and he had just watched a middle-aged woman delete a weapon with her bare hands. He backed his horse away, made a sign against evil that was older than his order, and rode hard for the ford, presumably to report that the fugitives were accompanied by something out of the oldest, most forbidden pages of the codex.

The silence after he left was enormous.

I stood on the riverbank with my hands still raised, shaking, smoking faintly at the fingertips, and the warmth inside me curled up like a cat that had done something delightful and expected praise.

"Well," said Ember, at my elbow, having materialized from nowhere with the uncanny timing of cats and children. She looked at the drifting ash of the net. She looked at my hands. And then she smiled — wide, blazing, proud as a parent at a recital — and clapped.

"*Yes,*" she said. Old word, first word she ever taught me. Yes. Fire. Heart. Home. All the same word, in her language, which should have warned me about everything.

"Yes," I repeated, hollow. "No. Ember, I didn't — I didn't choose that. I didn't decide anything. It just *did* it." I wrapped my arms around myself. The river ran on, indifferent and beautiful, and I thought: this is what the dragons felt, isn't it. This eagerness. This thing in you that wants to help whether you ask or not, that loves you so much it burns through your hesitation. No wonder the temples feared them. No wonder the temples *wanted* them.

Ember took my smoking hands in her cool ones and held them, and looked up at me with an expression far too grave for her size.

"Choose," she said slowly, feeling her way through the old tongue toward my language, assembling meaning like a child stacking stones. "Fire... listens. Always listens. To wanting. Even..." she tapped my chest, "...quiet wanting. Even wanting you hide." A pause. Her dark eyes held mine, and there was no comfort in them at all, only truth, which is sometimes the same thing. "You wanted. Today. First time true."

First time true. As if all my years of careful, disciplined, prayerful restraint had been one long lie the fire had politely ignored, and today I had finally been honest, and honesty had consequences.

Downstream, the ford was chaos shrinking into the distance — wardens regrouping, Maren's staff lying abandoned in the gravel, a basket floating serenely away toward the sea, pickles and all. Every step we fled took her further from us and closer to whatever Corvane would make of her. And I had done it. My shout, my stupid grieving shout, had cost her the distraction she needed. The fire in my chest purred. The rest of me felt like ash already.

"We have to go back," I whispered. "We have to—"

A hand on my arm. Ember shook her head once, slow and absolute.

"Can't," she said. Then, with terrible care, choosing each word like a coin from a nearly empty purse: "Back is... her gift. Forward is... her wish." She pointed downstream, toward the border cities, the exiled courts, the whole loud dangerous world Maren had spent two weeks telling us about. "Old woman," Ember added, and her voice cracked, and for a moment she was only a child again, "old woman says... live loudly. Is insult... to die quietly."

Live loudly. Maren's catechism, taught over cookfires and stolen apples, usually while she was doing something that would horrify both our mothers.

So we went downstream. I made myself walk and not run, made myself leave the staff in the gravel because taking it would have meant going back for it, made myself carry the grief the way I'd carried my mother's debt — silently, constantly, folded against the spine. The fire in my chest kept pace, warm and pleased with itself, and I caught myself, twice, almost fond of it, and both times the fondness frightened me more than the wardens had.

By dusk we reached the ridge above the border road, and saw it: lights, many lights, spread along the river plain below. A city, or a camp, or a court — and rising above it all, faint in the last light, a banner I recognized from the restricted folios I'd once been punished for reading.

A dragon sigil. Wings unfurled. Waiting.

"They'll know what you are here," I murmured. "Both what you are, and what I've become."

Ember laced her small hot fingers through mine.

"Good," she said. And below us, the fires of the exile camp burned like a scatter of fallen stars, and somewhere behind us Maren sat in a warden's cart with blood on her teeth and a plan already growing, I'd have bet anything, behind her mild old eyes.
