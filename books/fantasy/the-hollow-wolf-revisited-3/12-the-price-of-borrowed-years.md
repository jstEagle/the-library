## 12. The Price of Borrowed Years

The cell had one window, and it was the kind of window that existed mostly to apologize. A slit of grey stone, high in the wall, through which Maren could see a sliver of sky and, if she stood on the bench and craned her neck, the top of a cypress tree that swayed like it was trying to get her attention.

She'd named the tree Gerald.

"Gerald," she said, from the floor, where she lay with her hands laced behind her head, "I want you to know that if I die in here, I'm leaving you my good cloak. The blue one. Don't let the wardens have it, they'll only ruin it. They have no sense of drape."

Gerald swayed. Maren chose to interpret this as gratitude.

Three days. Three days in this cell beneath the Temple of Quiet Hours — which, she had decided, was a name that deserved to be mocked even more than she'd originally thought, because there was nothing quiet about the hours down here. There were the footsteps of the brothers overhead, soft as snowfall. There was the drip of water somewhere behind the eastern wall, patient as a debt collector. And there was the sound of Maren herself, humming the old hedge-songs her grandmother had taught her, the ones with no magic left in them but plenty of spite.

They'd taken her staff. Her knife. Her boots, which struck her as genuinely cruel, because whatever else the temple was, it was not warm. Her toes curled against cold flagstones as she considered, for perhaps the hundredth time, whether the girl and Silas had gotten across the river. Whether Ember was still breathing. Whether Silas —

Whether Silas had done the sensible thing and run, or the Maren thing and turned back.

"Knowing her," Maren told Gerald, "she turned back. That woman has a spine like a lute string. You can bend it all day and it just wants to sing something inconvenient."

The bolt in the door slid back.

Maren didn't get up. She'd learned by the second visit that getting up suggested eagerness, and eagerness, in an interrogation, was the first domino. Instead she rolled onto her side and watched as two wardens entered — the tall one who never spoke, and the short one who never stopped — followed by a third figure whose robes whispered along the stones like he resented having to touch them.

Brother Halvard. The interrogator. He had the face of a man who had been told all his life that he looked trustworthy, and had spent decades making sure the compliment went to waste.

"Sister Maren," Halvard said, folding himself onto the stool they'd brought him. It was the only furniture besides the bench, and she suspected they'd brought it specifically so that he could sit while she couldn't. "You're looking well."

"I'm looking frostbitten," Maren said. "There's a difference. Though I admire your commitment to flattery. Most men lead with threats. You lead with lies about my complexion. It's almost tender."

"You've been our guest for three days now—"

"*Guest.* Oh, that's lovely. Is there a bell I ring for the bath? Because I'd ring it. I'd ring it until your ears bled."

Halvard smiled the way winter smiles at a late harvest. "And still joking. Good. It means your spirit is intact, and we want it intact." He produced a small book from his sleeve — a slim thing, bound in green leather gone soft with handling. "We also want your cooperation. These are not, despite what you may have been told, incompatible goals."

"The last man who said that to me sold me a horse with three legs and a grudge."

"And how did that work out?"

"He won races out of pure anger. Which is more than I can say for your theology."

The silent warden shifted. Halvard raised two fingers without looking at him, and the man stilled. *Trained,* Maren thought. *Like dogs. Like everything in this place.*

"Sister Maren," Halvard said, and now his voice changed — dropped its polish, found something underneath it, worn and almost human. "I am going to speak plainly, because plainly is faster, and I have forty-one other prisoners and a headache. You sheltered a fugitive scribe and a child carrying contraband fire across half the borderlands. By every law of the Concordat, you should already be ash. You are not ash. Do you wonder why?"

"A little," Maren admitted. "I assumed bureaucratic backlog."

"The High Priest Corvane believes you may be useful." Halvard opened the little book. "He is wrong, in my estimation, but I am paid to be wrong in his direction. So: useful how? Let us find out together, and then you may go home to your hovel and your herbs and whatever it is hedge-witches do when nobody's watching."

"We nap aggressively," Maren said. "Go on."

"What do you know of the courts?"

It was such a sideways question that for a moment Maren forgot to be clever. "The courts?"

"The royal court at Vell. The exiles under Lord Veyra. The dragon-blooded houses in the salt city. You traveled among them. You saw their musters." He turned a page. "Tell me what you saw."

Maren sat up slowly, and not for performance this time. Because underneath the question, she could feel the shape of the thing he wasn't asking, the way you feel weather before it breaks.

"Why does a temple care about army movements?"

"Answer the question."

"I'm a hedge-witch, Brother. I cure warts and find lost goats. Army movements are above my station."

"Your station," Halvard said, "is wherever Corvane decides it is. And right now he has decided it is here, answering me, while your joints thaw." He closed the book. "But since you insist on ignorance, allow me to educate you. Consider it payment for the boots."

He rose, walked to the slit of window — Gerald's window — and stood with his hands clasped behind him, a man delivering a sermon to a tree.

"For six hundred years," he said, "the temples have held the valley and the marches. We hold them because the kingdoms are afraid. Fear of the wilds. Fear of magic. Fear of each other. Fear is our foundation, Sister Maren, and foundations must be maintained. But fear erodes. The new generation asks questions. Why can't magic cross our borders? Who really killed the dragons? Questions are rust. Rust spreads." He turned. "Corvane intends to stop the rust."

"How?"

"By giving the kingdoms something new to fear. Something so large that every question dies of embarrassment."

The drip behind the eastern wall counted out four seconds. Five. Six.

"You're going to start a war," Maren said.

"I'm going to *describe* one," Halvard corrected gently. "There is a difference. Veyra's exiles will make a move — they believe themselves provoked, and they will be right, because we shall provoke them. The crown at Vell will answer — they believe themselves threatened, and they will be right, because we shall threaten them. Two armies on the plains. Two courts screaming betrayal. And in the middle of the screaming, the temples will offer sanctuary, order, protection. Every frightened farmer, every merchant with warehouses full of grain he cannot guard, every nobleman who suddenly remembers that his grandfather worshipped quietly and lived long — they will all come running to the one roof that never burns." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Within five years, the crowns will kneel voluntarily. Within ten, they will beg us to take the weight. Absolute authority, freely given. No conquest required. Only a match, and dry tinder."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, echoing off the stone.

"That's it?" she said. "That's the grand design? Burn the world so everyone runs into your church?" She shook her head. "Brother, I've known village bullies with better plans. At least they got to enjoy the beating."

"You think it monstrous."

"I think it *small*. All that power, all that fire — and the best use you can imagine is making people afraid enough to obey you? That's not ambition. That's a man building a bigger cage because he's scared of the bird." She stood, finally, barefoot and defiant on the cold stone. "And here's what your precious Corvane doesn't understand, because he's spent too many years talking to men who nod: fear doesn't hold forever. Fear is a loan. And the interest comes due all at once, in the night, with knives."

"Poetic," Halvard said. "Also irrelevant. The plan proceeds whether you approve or not. Which brings us to your usefulness." He returned to the stool and sat, and the worn humanity came back into his voice like a mask going on. "The scribe. Silas Sandoval. Where would she run?"

"No idea."

"Sister."

"Truly none. She never tells me anything. Woman's a locked book with the pages glued. I once asked her favorite color and she gave me a citation from scripture."

"She was seen at the ford. With the child. Heading east toward the ruined archive at Callowmere."

"Was she? Sounds like a busy woman."

"Maren." His voice hardened, just slightly, the first crack in the varnish. "There is a warden company riding east as we speak. Forty men. If they find her, she will be brought back in chains, and the child—" he paused, delicately, "—the child will be brought back in a box. Not a coffin. A *box*. There is equipment involved. You don't want to know, and frankly neither do I, and I have seen most things."

The cold in Maren's feet climbed all the way up to her heart and settled there.

"So cooperate," Halvard continued, softer now. "Tell me where she'll go next. What she'll seek. Who she'll trust. And when she's recovered — safely, gently, I promise you the box remains empty — you walk out of here. Your boots. Your staff. Even the cloak. Home by spring planting."

Maren looked at him. Really looked — past the tired eyes, past the borrowed weariness, down to the place where a man keeps the account of his own soul and has learned not to open it after dark.

"You're offering me her life to save mine," she said. "That's not a bargain, Brother. That's a test. And you've already failed it, because you think anyone could pass."

"It isn't her life against yours. It's her life against hers *and* yours. Simple arithmetic."

"I failed arithmetic," Maren said, "on purpose, at age nine, to spite a tutor. It's the only exam I ever cheated proudly." She folded her arms. "Here's what I'll give you instead, free of charge. A prophecy. Hedge-witch quality, so mind the craftsmanship. The war you're planning? It happens. I can't stop that, and maybe nobody can. But it doesn't end the way he thinks. Fire doesn't belong to the man who strikes the match, Brother Halvard. Fire belongs to itself. And there's a child out there carrying the oldest flame in the world, and she has chosen her side, and her side is not yours."

"The child is eleven years old and cannot speak."

"Oh," Maren said, and grinned, and it felt so good to grin in that grey place that she did it wider. "You sweet, doomed man. She speaks fine. You just haven't been listening in the right language."

* * *

They left her alone after that. No supper — punishment, or theater, hard to say. Maren paced the cell until her feet went numb, then sat on the bench and pulled her knees up and let herself, for exactly one hundred heartbeats, be afraid.

She counted them. It was a discipline her grandmother had taught her. *Fear is a guest,* the old woman used to say, *and guests behave better when you watch the clock.*

At heartbeat sixty she thought about Silas at the ford, turning to look back the way she always did, as though the past might follow politely if you kept checking. At heartbeat eighty she thought about Ember's hand in hers on the road, small and hot as a coal wrapped in cloth. At ninety-nine she thought about Maren herself, old Maren, hedge-Maren, who had spent forty years perfecting the art of not being important, and who had somehow stumbled into being the most important coward in the borderlands.

At one hundred, she stopped being afraid and started being furious, which was much warmer.

"Right," she said to Gerald. "Options."

Option one: tell Halvard something false. Send the wardens chasing west while Silas ran east. Risky — Halvard would verify, and verification meant pain, and pain meant Maren discovering precisely how much of her grandmother's stubbornness was inherited versus earned.

Option two: say nothing. Endure. Hope the arithmetic worked out. But hope, her grandmother had also said, was what you called patience when you were too polite to admit you were scared, and Maren had never been polite a day in her life.

Option three—

She was still working on option three when the door bolt slid back, earlier than any visit had a right to be, and it was not Halvard who came in.

It was a brother. Young. Novice-thin, with ink stains on his fingers that no warden would ever have, and eyes that went everywhere except at her face.

He set down a bowl of broth — actual supper, unpunished supper, which meant something had changed upstairs — and as he straightened, he did a strange thing. He tapped the rim of the bowl twice with his fingernail. Then, glancing at the silent warden in the corridor, he mouthed words at her, quick and clumsy:

*The archive. She's going to the archive. I heard the dispatch.*

Then he was gone, door bolting behind him, footsteps retreating up the stair, and Maren sat very still with her heart doing something undignified in her chest.

A brother of the temple. Feeding information to a prisoner. Which meant either a trap, or a crack — one small hairline fracture in the great grey wall, some young man somewhere in this machine who had read too much scripture about mercy and believed it.

Maren picked up the bowl. The broth was thin and over-salted and absolutely the finest thing she had ever tasted.

"Well, Gerald," she murmured, drinking it down. "Callowmere archive. Forty wardens. One old woman with no boots." She wiped her mouth and felt the fury settle into something harder and quieter and far more durable — the thing her grandmother had called *rope*, the slow twist of purpose that holds when everything else burns. "They think they've caught the distraction."

She set down the bowl and smiled at the ceiling, at the temple, at Corvane and his beautiful terrible plan.

"They haven't realized I *am* the plan. Or at least," she amended, honest to the end, "I'm about to become somebody's."

Somewhere above her, bells began to ring vespers — and beneath the bells, faint but unmistakable, the whole valley shivered, as if something old had turned over in its sleep and begun, very softly, to glow.
