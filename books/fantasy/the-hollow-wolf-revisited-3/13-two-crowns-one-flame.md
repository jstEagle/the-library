## 13. Two Crowns, One Flame

The summons came twice in one morning, which Silas would later decide was the exact moment she stopped believing anyone in power wanted anything other than the child sleeping against her shoulder.

The first arrived on horseback: a herald in royal blue and silver, sweating through his finery despite the autumn chill, unrolling a scroll stamped with the seal of the Crown of Aldermere. The second arrived by crow — or rather, by a woman with a crow perched on her shoulder and the ash-grey sash of House Veyra knotted at her waist. She simply handed over a letter sealed in black wax, inclined her head to Ember with something dangerously close to reverence, and rode off without waiting for an answer.

Silas stood in the doorway of Maren's cottage holding both messages, feeling rather like a woman who had caught two falling knives and was now expected to juggle.

"Well," said Maren, from her chair by the fire, where she was grinding something that smelled like burnt rosemary and spite. "Don't keep us in suspense. Which one wants to use the girl as a weapon, and which one wants to use her as a *symbol* of a weapon?"

"The crown requests our presence at court." Silas read aloud, flatly. "*Under guarantee of safe conduct and the protection of the realm.*"

"Protection from what?"

"It doesn't say."

"Course it doesn't." Maren blew a strand of grey hair out of her eyes. "And the exiles?"

Silas unfolded Lord Veyra's letter. It was shorter, and more honest about it. *"Lady Sandoval. You have seen my hospitality once and fled it before dawn, which I am told is the custom of your people. I ask only that you hear me again. The fire belongs with its own blood. Bring the child to the Plains of Cinders, and no hand but yours will ever touch her. I swear it by the last name I have left."*

"By his last name," Silas repeated. "That's very moving. He had three houses burned for that last name, I imagine."

"He had four," said Maren. "I was there for one of them. It was very dramatic. There were swans."

Ember stirred against Silas's shoulder and made a small sound — not the old language anymore, not for some weeks now, but plain speech, low and careful, like someone learning to walk on words. "Are they fighting over me again?"

"No one is fighting over you," Silas said automatically, and then heard herself, because it was a lie of exactly the kind she used to copy out in doctrine: technically true, entirely false.

"They're fighting over the *fire*," Ember said, with the terrible precision of children. "It's the same thing right now. It's living in the same place I am."

Silas looked down at her. Six months ago this had been a feral thing at a temple door, mud-caked, speaking a tongue older than any kingdom, and Silas had understood it the way you understand a song from childhood — not the words, but the shape of them. Now the girl spoke plainly, ate with a spoon most days, and occasionally said things like *it's living in the same place I am* while picking at a scab, and every single time it landed in Silas's chest like a dropped anvil.

"Here's what we know," Maren announced, in the tone of a woman taking charge because someone had to and everyone else was busy having feelings. "The royal army is massing at Westford. Veyra's host is gathering on the Cinders. That puts us" — she jabbed her pestle at the window, beyond which lay hills, a river, and a great deal of impending history — "directly between two armies, one dead temple, one live witch, and absolutely nowhere sensible to be."

"You could stay here," Silas offered.

"I could also set myself on fire, but I'm told that's redundant lately." Maren waved at the hearth, where the flames did seem to lean toward Ember when she passed, like dogs toward their favorite. "No. Wherever you go, trouble goes, and I've decided I'd rather watch trouble up close than hear about it secondhand. Besides, someone has to teach the girl which mushrooms kill you. Her education has been shockingly neglected."

Ember, who had eaten a mushroom two days ago that Maren still hadn't identified, looked pleased with herself.

* * *

They traveled north by back roads, which Maren insisted on calling "the scenic route" whenever Ember complained, and which Silas privately understood to mean *the route least likely to get us hanged*. The land itself had changed since they'd crossed out of the valley all those months ago. Magic hummed everywhere now — in hedgerows that flowered out of season, in wells that sang if you leaned close, in the way birds sometimes flew in shapes that were almost letters. Since the night at the ford, since Silas had held a scrap of Ember's fire and it had answered her *too willingly*, the whole world seemed louder.

Or perhaps it was just Silas who was louder now. Hard to say. She tried not to think about it. She thought about it constantly.

On the third day they crested a ridge and saw the plains.

Both armies. At once. The royal banners of Aldermere — blue and silver, a stag crowned in light — spread across the western fields like spilled ink. And eastward, on the scorched ground that gave the region its name, the dragon-blooded exiles flew their grey-and-ember standard, their tents black, their cookfires burning strange colors. Between them stretched six miles of empty grassland, and Silas felt her stomach drop through the earth and keep going.

"That's a lot of people," Ember said quietly.

"That's a lot of people who each believe they're the hero of this story," Maren said. "In my experience that's how the worst ones start."

Two roads led down into the valley of the plains. Two invitations waited in Silas's satchel. She stood at the crossroads — literally, there was even a signpost, rotted and pointing in contradictory directions, which struck her as the most honest piece of civic infrastructure she'd ever encountered — and understood, with the clarity of a woman reading her own death warrant, that there was no road here that didn't end in someone's hands around Ember's fire.

The crown would make the girl a relic. A national treasure, kept in velvet, displayed at coronations, bled gently and politely forever.

Veyra would make her a sword. Honored, venerated, wrapped in grief and vengeance, swung until nothing was left to swing.

And Corvane —

Corvane wanted her emptied out entirely, and Silas had watched him smile while explaining why that was mercy.

Every faction. Every banner. The same hunger wearing different clothes. *Controlled*, that was the word under all of it. Not freed. Not asked. Controlled.

"Silas?" Ember tugged her sleeve. "Your face is doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you copy scripture with your eyebrows."

Silas laughed despite everything — a short, cracked sound — and crouched to the girl's level. "Listen to me. Whatever happens down there, whatever anyone offers or promises or demands — you are not a weapon. You are not a relic. You are a person who happens to be carrying something enormous, and enormous does not mean owned. Do you understand?"

Ember considered this with grave brown eyes. "You're going to give me away anyway," she said. "To whichever one scares you less."

The words went in like a needle finding a vein.

"I don't want to scare you less," Silas said. "I want to keep you."

"But you can't." Ember shrugged, small and devastating. "That's why your face does the thing."

* * *

Maren, being Maren, solved the problem of two armies by refusing both roads and walking straight down the middle, along the streambed, waving cheerfully at pickets on either side like a madwoman visiting relatives. It worked, in the way that audacity often works on soldiers who have drilled for weeks and are bored past reason.

They made camp that night in the ruins of an old waystation, half-claimed by ivy, and Silas sat up keeping watch while the others slept. The fires of both armies glimmered on the horizon, west and east, like two hostile suns refusing to rise.

She took out the two letters again. Read them again. The language of protection. The language of belonging. Underneath both, in ink no one had used, the same sentence: *give her to us.*

A sound behind her. Ember, awake, wrapped in Maren's spare cloak, settling beside her without asking.

"Can't sleep?"

"The fire keeps me up," Ember said. "It's excited. It doesn't like armies."

"Doesn't it?"

"It likes *one* person in each army. Isn't that funny? It's like — it's like it remembers them." Ember frowned at her own hands. "It says things. Not words. More like weather. Right now it's saying *storm coming* and also *the man with the cold hands is afraid of you*."

Silas went still. "Which man with cold hands?"

"The one who smells like the reliquary." Ember pulled her knees up. "He's coming tomorrow. Both of them sent word today. The crown wants you at dawn, and the grey lord wants you at dusk, and the cold-hands man wants you whenever he can get you, and he doesn't care whose tent he gets you in."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I can feel him now." Ember looked up, and for a moment her eyes caught the firelight wrong, the way metal catches it. "Ever since the ford. He's been getting closer, and the closer he gets, the more the fire wants to — " She stopped. Pressed her hands flat together, hard, the way she did when she was holding something in. "I don't want it to hurt anybody, Silas."

"I know."

"And I don't want to be given away."

Silas put her arm around the girl and drew her close, and over the small warm weight of her looked out at the twin fires on the horizon, and felt the trap closing with a softness that was worse than teeth. There was no tent to choose. There was no road that wasn't owned. Dawn belonged to the crown; dusk to the exiles; and somewhere between them moved a man with cold hands who would burn the whole plain for a fragment of stolen flame.

"Tomorrow," Silas said slowly, "when the king's men come — I want you to stay behind me. Whatever happens. Whatever I say. Can you do that?"

"Why?" Ember asked. "What are you going to say?"

And Silas, who had spent her life copying other people's true words and never once spoken her own, found that she didn't have an answer yet. Only the beginning of one, forming somewhere beneath the fear, in the place where the fire had touched her at the ford and called her *willing*.

"I'm going to say something new," she said. "For the first time in my life, I think."

Out on the dark plain, a horn sounded from the west — the crown's camp, waking early. And far to the east, another answered it, lower, stranger, like something old clearing its throat.

Dawn was coming from both directions at once.
