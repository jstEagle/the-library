## 14. The Confession Codex

The archive had been a cathedral once, before the river changed its mind and took the city's heart with it.

Silas stood in the doorway of what remained — a great ribcage of stone with the sky for a roof, its shattered windows admitting more birds than light. Rain had come through that roof for two hundred years. Moss grew in the shapes of former congregations, green ghosts kneeling down the nave. Somewhere overhead, a pigeon cooed with the smugness of a creature that had never owed anyone anything.

"Charming," Maren would have said. *Fixable,* she'd have said, already prying up flagstones to see what was underneath.

Maren was not here. That was the whole point of being here.

Silas adjusted the strap of her satchel and stepped inside, her boots crunching on glass worn smooth as sea pebbles. Ember padded behind her, quiet as falling ash, though Silas could feel the girl's warmth at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades. The fire did not like this place. She'd known that since they crossed the threshold; it sat in her own chest too, a low wrongness, like a hymn sung slightly flat.

"Stay close," Silas murmured, in the old tongue now without thinking about it. Three weeks of travel and the language had settled into her mouth like a stone into a streambed. She no longer noticed the weight of it.

*Always close,* Ember answered, which was either reassurance or a complaint. With Ember it was often both.

They had come to Vell's Landing because there was nowhere else. The royal summons lay folded in Silas's satchel beside the exile lord's seal, two pieces of paper that might as well have been two jaws closing. Both courts wanted Ember. Both courts wanted the fire. And somewhere behind them, patient as rot, Corvane's wardens were following the same road.

But Veyra had let one thing slip, in his gilded tent full of vengeance: that if Silas wanted proof — real proof, the kind that could not be burned or argued with — she should look in the drowned archive at Vell's Landing, where the first temples had kept their records before the Quiet Hours were built on quieter lies.

*If you're going to betray everyone,* he'd said, smiling his terrible courteous smile, *at least be certain of what you're betraying them for.*

She had hated him for saying it. She had also, that night, lying awake under a hedge while Ember slept against her side like a banked coal, admitted to herself that he was right.

* * *

The scriptorium was beneath the nave, down stairs that had become less staircase and more suggestion. Water still pooled in the lowest corners, black and mirror-still, reflecting nothing but darkness above. But the upper shelves — the ones built high to escape the flood — had survived. Two hundred years of damp had turned most of it to pulp, but not all.

Silas ran her fingers along a spine and felt the particular give of vellum under cracked leather, and something in her chest clenched with an ache she refused to name. She had spent her whole life among books. Books had been the walls of her prison and the furniture of her faith. To stand in their graveyard felt like walking through her own future.

*What are we looking for?* Ember asked. She had climbed onto a reading desk that some long-dead monk had abandoned mid-sentence, ink pot fossilized beside a quill whose last word trailed off into nothing. Ember traced the unfinished word with one finger, frowning, as if the monk might yet return and finish it.

"The founding years," Silas said. "Before the Doctrine of Stillness. Before the valley." She pulled a volume free and opened it; it exhaled a smell like dust and old rain and something faintly sweet, the smell of knowledge going gently to sleep. "Corvane's power rests on a story, Ember. Dragons as monsters. Fire as sin. The temples as healers who tamed a burning world. Every prayer I ever copied stands on that story." She turned a page. A water stain had bloomed across the text like a brown flower. "I want to know if the story is true."

*And if it isn't?*

"Then I've spent my life copying lies," Silas said, "and my mother died paying off a debt to liars, and everything I am is—" She stopped. Breathed. "Then I need to know anyway."

Ember considered this with the grave seriousness she brought to all human foolishness. *You already know,* she said. *You knew when you saw my sigil. You knew when you burned the scrolls. This place will only tell you things your hands already understood.*

"That," said Silas, "is exactly what frightens me."

* * *

Three hours later, her hands were gray with mold and her hope was gray with disappointment. The surviving volumes were inventories, correspondence, liturgical calendars. One entire shelf was devoted to disputes over candle-wax suppliers, which even Silas — who had once read a forty-page treatise on the theological implications of marginalia — found difficult to care about.

She was reaching for what looked like a ledger when Ember made a sound.

Not a word. A note. Low, resonant, the sound the fire made when it recognized something, and Silas turned so fast she cracked her elbow on the shelf.

Ember stood at the far end of the scriptorium, before a section of wall where the shelving had collapsed outward from the flood line. Behind it, half-hidden, was a door. Small, unremarkable, iron-banded — the kind of door built to be forgotten. Carved into its lintel, worn nearly smooth by time, was a sigil.

A dragon, curled around a flame. The same sigil Ember had drawn in the reliquary dust a lifetime ago. The same sigil hidden in the temple's oldest restricted codex, the one Silas had been forbidden to touch since her first year of service.

*It's warm,* Ember said, wonderingly. She pressed her palm flat to the wood. *Underneath. Something underneath is warm.*

Silas joined her. The door was locked, of course — a lock older than any kingdom, iron gone orange with rust — but locks were made by people, and people, whatever else they were, were consistent. She knelt, examined the mechanism, and set to work with a hairpin and the edge of her belt knife, the way Maren had taught her during a bored evening on the road, saying, *You never know when you'll need to get into somewhere you shouldn't, love, and it's better to learn from me than from a warden.*

The lock fought her. Then, with a crunch and a shower of rust, it surrendered.

Inside was a room barely larger than a confessional, dry as a held breath, sealed so well that the air within still smelled of the century that had made it. And on a lectern in the center, alone, positioned with the care of something meant to be found only by someone who deserved to find it —

— lay a book bound in white leather, its cover stamped with the sigil of the Temple of Quiet Hours.

Silas's hands shook. She noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to another scribe entirely.

The title, in gold leaf gone dull, read: *Confessions of the Founding Scribes.*

*Open it,* Ember whispered. Her voice in the old tongue trembled. *Or don't. But choose quickly. The fire doesn't like waiting in small rooms.*

Silas opened it.

* * *

The first pages were ordinary enough — devotional notes, drafts of prayers she recognized, some of them verbatim from the canon she had copied ten thousand times. It was the marginalia that stopped her heart. Because the marginalia argued. The founding scribes had written their prayers in the margins of their doubts, and the doubts had won.

*We told them the dragons came to burn us,* wrote a hand labeled only *Scribe Ondrel, Third Year of the Founding.* *This is not true. We went to them first. We asked for fire, and they gave it — freely, as they give all things, asking only that we not take more than we need. We took more than we needed. We are taking everything.*

Silas turned the page. The handwriting had deteriorated, or perhaps the honesty had simply outpaced the penmanship.

*Sister Hale says the people cannot govern themselves without fear to bind them, and perhaps she is right, but I keep thinking of the dragon's face when we drove the first iron into the binding-circle. It did not roar. It looked at us the way a mother looks at a child who has broken something precious, and it asked — it actually asked — whether we understood what we were doing.*

*We did not understand. We understand now. The fire does not belong to whoever holds it. It belongs to whoever gives it. That is the whole of the mystery, and we have spent three years building a church on its opposite.*

*Doctrine of Stillness, final draft, approved by council:* — and here the handwriting changed, became formal, became the very letterforms Silas had spent her life reproducing — *Let it be written that the dragons were beasts of ruin, that their fire was theft from heaven, that the temples alone stand between mankind and burning. Let it be written until it is believed. What is written long enough becomes true. What is believed deeply enough becomes holy.*

*Forgive us. We have made a machine for manufacturing truth, and it works, and we are proud of it, and we are damned.*

Silas closed the book.

She stood in the tiny sealed room, in the smell of a dead century, and felt the floor tilt beneath her — except it wasn't the floor. It was everything. Every verse she had ground ink for. Every dawn bell. Every night she had pressed her palms together and felt nothing, and blamed herself for the nothing, because the doctrine said the faithful felt the Quiet, and if she felt nothing then the failure was hers.

There had never been anything to feel. The silence at the center of her faith wasn't holiness. It was a hole, cut deliberately, where something living used to be.

*Silas.* Ember's hand on her arm. Warm. Steadying. *Breathe.*

"I'm breathing."

*You are standing very still and your eyes are wet and you are breathing like someone trying not to make noise.* Ember tilted her head, studying her with those unsettling amber eyes. *In the memory-fire, when the betrayal happened, the dragons' keeper made the same face.*

"What happened to her?"

*She carried the truth out of the burning,* Ember said. *It cost her everything. It saved almost nothing. But the fire remembered her kindly, and the fire has a long memory, and I think — * the girl hesitated, choosing words with unusual care — *I think the fire keeps its promises to people like her. Even late. Even across a thousand years.*

Silas laughed. It came out cracked and wet and surprised her more than the book had. "You're telling me I'm a thousand-year-old promise?"

*I'm telling you the fire chose you before you were born,* Ember said, matter-of-fact as a weather report. *You can decide what to do about it whenever you finish deciding what to do about the book.*

* * *

She should burn it.

The thought arrived fully formed, wearing the robes of duty, speaking in Corvane's reasonable voice. Burn it, because it was dangerous. Burn it, because kingdoms had gone to war over less. Burn it, because the Doctrine of Stillness held the valley together — held *her* valley together, her mud and her bells and her grumbling candle-suppliers — and a lie that fed ten thousand people was surely worth more than a truth that would starve them.

Burn it, because loyalty was the only currency she had ever been allowed to hold.

She stood there a long time. The pigeon upstairs cooed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of all water everywhere.

"No," Silas said aloud, to the room, to the dead scribes, to herself.

Because here was the thing the doctrine had never accounted for, the flaw in the machine for manufacturing truth: she had spent her life copying. She knew, better than any priest alive, what writing could do. Writing was how lies became holy. Which meant writing was also how holy things could be *unmade* — carefully, line by line, witnessed and copied and scattered so widely that no bonfire could ever gather them all in again.

She was a scribe. Scribes did not burn books.

Scribes *copied* them.

She sat down right there on the cold floor of the confessional-room, spread her satchel open, took out ink and the good pens she'd been hoarding since Veyra's camp, and began to write. Not a copy — copies could be seized. She wrote it out three times in three different hands, one plain, one disguised as mercantile correspondence, one in the old tongue that no temple warden could read. She sewed each copy into a different hiding place: the lining of her cloak, the false bottom of her ink box, and — after a moment's thought, and a small fierce smile — inside the cover of her personal prayer book, where no priest in history had ever thought to look for heresy.

Ember watched her work, chin on her knees, radiating the particular satisfaction of a cat observing a mouse trap being properly baited.

*You kept the original too,* the girl observed, when Silas finally wrapped the white-leather book in oilcloth and tucked it against her ribs.

"Of course I kept the original."

*Veyra will want it.*

"Veyra wants a lot of things." Silas tied the oilcloth closed. Her hands had stopped shaking. Interesting, she thought distantly, how steady they became the moment she stopped asking permission. "So will Corvane. So will the crown. Everyone will want this book, because everyone understands what it is: the end of every argument, the end of every throne built on the story. It's the most dangerous object in the world."

*And you're carrying it through a country full of people hunting us.*

"Yes," said Silas. "I rather am."

*Good,* said Ember, with a flash of heat behind her eyes that was not entirely the girl's own. *I was worried, for a moment, that you'd become sensible.*

They climbed back up into the ruined nave, into the bird-light and the green ghosts of kneeling congregations. Silas paused at the doorway and looked back once — at the shelves, the drowned dark, the small iron door standing open behind its fallen shelves. She left it open. Let the rain in. Let the truth get damp and difficult and inconvenient. Secrets kept in dry little rooms were how the world had gotten into this state.

They were halfway across the nave when Ember stopped.

Her head came up sharply, nostrils flaring, and the warmth at her back suddenly became a furnace. *Smoke,* she said. *Horses. Many. Coming along the river road.*

Silas's stomach dropped through the mossy floor, through the drowned crypt, through two hundred years of flooded graves. Wardens. Or the crown's riders. Or Veyra's — and she honestly could not say which prospect frightened her least.

Then she heard the voice carrying ahead of the hoofbeats, thin and ragged and unmistakably, impossibly familiar, calling her name across the ruins:

"—Silas! *Silas!* They're coming, don't let them—"

Maren.

Alive. Captive. Being used as bait.

Silas's hand went to the oilcloth bundle against her ribs, and she understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what she was about to be asked to trade.
