## 15. Maren's Rope

The rope Maren had given Silas back at the ford — *for luck, and because you'll drop something, you always drop something* — was fraying at the third knot from the end.

Silas turned it over in her hands as she waited beneath the old bell tower of Saint Verity's, the ruined chapel where Corvane had agreed to meet. It was a good rope. Woven by hand, waxed against rain, knotted in a style she didn't recognize until Ember had pointed at it and said, in her plain new voice that still startled Silas every time, *"That's a debt-knot. You tie it when someone owes you."*

Of course it was. Everything about Maren was a debt-knot. The woman had tied herself to them at the ford, cut herself loose so they could run, and now hung in a temple cell waiting to see whether anyone remembered the other end of the string.

Silas remembered. Silas remembered everything. It was, after all, her profession.

"You're doing the thing," Ember said beside her. "Where you rub your thumb on the rope like you're trying to wear a hole in it."

"I'm thinking."

"You're worrying. Your thinking face has eyebrows. Your worrying face has eyebrows and rope."

Silas almost smiled. A month ago the girl hadn't spoken a word any living tongue could parse; now she'd developed the particular cruelty of children everywhere, which is to say, precision. The dragon-fire had chosen Silas — that was what Ember had told her in the dark of the ruined archive, in the voice that wasn't entirely Ember's — but the child herself had chosen Silas long before that, and there were days when that frightened Silas more.

"Corvane will come," she said. "He wants this book too badly to play games."

"Everyone wants things too badly," Ember said, with the weary tone of someone who carried the memory of a thousand years of people wanting things too badly. Then, gentler: "He'll come. And then what?"

And then. Silas looked down at the satchel at her feet. Inside it lay the Confession Codex — the true one, the original, pulled from beneath three false floors and a lie four centuries deep in the ruined archive at Greywater. In Corvane's own predecessors' hands, set down in ink that had not faded: the first temples had not defended the realm against dragons. They had invited the dragons in, sworn guest-right, sworn peace — and stolen their fire while the great beasts slept trusting. Every doctrine Silas had spent her life copying was a bandage over a wound the church itself had made.

She had read it twice through by candlelight, weeping both times, which she supposed made it the only scripture she'd ever truly felt.

"He'll come," Silas said, "and I'll give him what he wants, and he'll give me Maren, and everyone will walk away unhappy, which is how you know a bargain is honest."

Ember considered this with grave amber eyes. "That's not how bargains work."

"It's how *good* ones work."

* * *

Corvane came alone, or nearly — two wardens at the treeline, close enough to matter, far enough to pretend otherwise. He wore grey traveling wool instead of his high priest's vestments, which was its own kind of message: *today I am a reasonable man.* His hair was silvered at the temples now in a way Silas didn't remember from the valley, though perhaps that was only distance teaching her his face properly.

"Sandoval." He said her name the way he'd always said it in the scriptorium — with warmth laid over assessment, like gilt over iron. "You look well. Exile agrees with you."

"I'm not exiled. I fled."

"A distinction without a difference that I'm prepared to be generous about." His gaze dropped to the satchel, lingered, came back up. He did not reach for it. That was the discipline in him; Silas had copied enough of his marginalia to know the man could out-wait a glacier. "And the girl?"

"Not part of this."

"She is the whole of everything, Sandoval. She's simply not part of *this transaction*." He folded his hands. "Maren of the Ford Road. Hedge-witch, unlicensed, twice censured, once imprisoned. Currently enjoying our hospitality in the Undercroft of Quiet Hours, cell nine. The damp one. She complains about the damp."

"I know. She complained about the damp in every house we ever shared."

Something flickered across his face — genuine amusement, quickly filed away. "Then you understand the urgency. The book."

"The prisoner first."

"Sandoval—"

"Prisoner. First." Silas lifted the satchel strap an inch, let it fall. "I've been a debtor my whole life, High Priest. I know how creditors cheat. You don't get to inspect the goods before you've paid."

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the broken belfry above them, stirring the dead leaves of a bird's nest wedged in the beam.

"Maren of the Ford Road," Corvane said at last, "will be released at the border shrine of Ashen Well within the hour. Unharmed. Fed, even — the kitchens have been warned. She will be escorted to the boundary stone and no further, and she will not be followed." He paused. "This is not because you frighten me. It's because I find I respect craftsmanship, and what you've done — fleeing, surviving, digging up what you dug up — is craftsmanship."

"And what happens after?"

"After?" He smiled, and for a moment Silas saw the young man who must once have knelt in this same order believing in service, before service calcified into appetite. "After, Sandoval, depends entirely on what's in that bag."

She opened the satchel and drew out the codex.

It was an ugly thing, honestly. Leather cracked as old riverbed, boards warped, one clasp missing. Four hundred years of being hidden had done what four hundred years of being venerated never does to holy books: it had left marks. Silas held it a moment longer than she needed to, feeling its weight — not the physical weight, though that was real, but the weight of every prayer she'd ever copied that had been a lie with beautiful margins.

"You've read it," Corvane said. Not a question.

"Twice."

"And?"

Silas looked up at him. "You already knew."

"I suspected." For the first time, something tired moved behind his composure. "I found a reference in Abbot Cael's private letters eleven years ago. A hint that the founding doctrine rested on a document that contradicted it. I sent three archivists to look. None of them found it. Two of them stopped reporting afterward." He extended his hand. "You found it in a season. I told the council you would. I told them the quiet ones always do."

"Why do you want it destroyed? If the truth comes out—"

"If the truth comes out," Corvane said softly, "the temples burn. All of them. Every valley, every spire, every copy-house full of scribes grinding ink for debts their grandmothers incurred. Do you imagine the kingdoms will thank us for the confession? They will burn the church, and then they will burn each other over what fills the space where the church used to be, and the girl's fire will be fought over by men with armies instead of men with ledgers." His hand stayed open, patient. "I am offering to prevent a war, Sandoval."

"You're offering to prevent a *reckoning*. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," he agreed. "They rarely are. But reckoning kills more people."

Silas set the codex in his hands.

She watched his fingers close around it — carefully, reverently, the way he'd touch anything he intended to destroy — and felt nothing like triumph. She'd imagined this moment for six days, rehearsed it on the road, imagined feeling light. Instead she felt like a scribe handing over a finished page: relief and emptiness in equal measure, and ink stains that wouldn't wash off.

"Maren," she said.

"At Ashen Well within the hour. You have my oath."

"Your oath is worth a burned page."

"My oath," Corvane said, tucking the codex inside his coat with surprising tenderness, "is the only currency I've never devalued. Ask anyone I've ever bought." He turned to go, then stopped. "Sandoval. The girl. When this is over — and it will be over soon, one way or another — bring her somewhere green. Somewhere far from all of us. She deserves a life that isn't a weapon."

For one absurd heartbeat Silas believed him meant it.

Which is why what she said next hurt to say, and why she said it anyway:

"High Priest. Before you go. There's something you should know about the book you're holding."

He turned. Wariness, finally — the glacier cracking. "Speak."

Silas reached into her coat and drew out a sheaf of pages — forty of them, closely written in a small, disciplined hand. Her hand. The same hand that had copied ten thousand prayers in the Temple of Quiet Hours, the hand that could reproduce any script it had seen twice.

"I'm a copyist," she said. "It's the only thing I've ever been allowed to be. Did you really think I'd hand over the only copy of the most dangerous book in the world?"

The silence stretched. Above them, in the belfry, wind worried the dead nest.

"Where are the others?" Corvane asked quietly.

"Three copies. One went to Lord Veyra's chancery a week ago, under seal, addressed to every dragon-blooded house in exile. One went to the royal court at Carrowmere, to the Queen's Own Archivist, who owes me a favor and hates your order professionally." She let him absorb that. "And the third is with a woman named Odell, a laundress in the town of Marrow Bridge, who cannot read a word of Old Script and doesn't need to. She knows only that if anything happens to me or the child, that packet goes to the town crier, who also cannot read Old Script — but the *first page*, High Priest, I wrote in plain trade-tongue. Large letters. Short words."

"You wouldn't." But his voice had changed, and they both knew it.

"I burned the preparation scrolls rather than let you kill a child," Silas said. "Burning is not the thing I'm afraid of anymore. It's the thing I'm best at."

Corvane looked down at the codex in his hands — the original, worthless now as leverage, priceless still as proof — and Silas watched something happen behind his eyes that she would spend the rest of her short life trying to describe. Not rage. Rage would have been simpler. It was recalculation, vast and cold, a ledger rebalancing itself in a single terrible instant: every plan, every timeline, every careful decade of patience, rendered suddenly perishable.

"You've made war inevitable," he said.

"No. I've made *lying* inevitable to end. Those aren't the same thing either. You taught me the distinction, actually — it's in your commentary on the Ledger of Debts. Chapter four. 'A debt concealed compounds; a debt confessed can be discharged.'" She shrugged. "I checked the citation twice. I'm thorough."

Something almost like admiration crossed his face, and that was the worst part.

"Within the hour," Corvane said, "the wardens mobilize. By nightfall, Veyra's host will know we're coming — if they don't know already, since you've clearly been busy — and the plains between here and Carrowmere will be an army's width wide with blood." He straightened his coat over the codex as if settling a vestment. "I want you to understand, Sandoval, that I take no pleasure in this. When the histories are written — the true ones, the ones you've made unavoidable — I intend to be the man who tried to keep the fire contained. You'll be the woman who lit the plains."

"No," Silas said. "I'll be the woman who refused to hold the lid down while you built the pyre. The difference matters. It's going to matter very much, quite soon, to a great many people."

He studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he bowed — a small, precise bow, the kind exchanged between equals, and it landed on her like a blow.

"Ashen Well," he said. "Your hedge-witch will be there within the hour. I keep my oaths, Sandoval. It's the last decent thing about me." He turned, and the wardens fell in around him, and the three of them went down the hill through the bare trees like ink bleeding into cloth.

* * *

Ember was quiet the whole ride back to the safehouse — an abandoned drover's inn where Maren had once, by her own account, cheated a toll-keeper, gotten drunk, and fallen in the horse trough, in that order. Silas talked anyway, because silence with Ember had a way of filling up with dragon-memory, and Silas wasn't strong enough tonight for a thousand years of grief that wasn't hers.

"He bowed to me," Silas said. "Did you see that? He actually—" She dismounted badly, as she dismounted everything, and stood clutching the saddle. "I had a speech prepared. About truth. About justice. I got through maybe half of it and mostly it was citations. I cited his *own commentary* at him, Ember. Who does that?"

"A scribe," Ember said, sliding down from her pony with the easy grace of someone raised by wolves and recently civilized by stubbornness. "The right scribe." She looked toward the plains, where the evening light was going the color of old brass. "He's moving already. I can feel it. Like a hot wire under the ground."

"So can I." And it was true — the fire in her, the scrap of dragon-flame that had answered her at the ford, was restless tonight, prickling along her arms like a fever deciding whether to break. "Veyra will have gotten the copy by now. He'll muster. Corvane will muster. And the middle of it will be—"

"Us," Ember said. "It's always going to be us, isn't it? Until it isn't."

Silas didn't answer, because there was no answer that wasn't a lie, and she'd had her fill of those.

They reached the inn door as the first stars came out. Inside, Silas lit a candle with fingers that shook only a little, and sat down at the scarred table, and took out fresh paper.

"What are you doing?" Ember asked.

"Writing to Veyra. Warning him that Corvane moves within the hour, so his people aren't caught on open ground." She dipped the pen. "And then I'm writing to the Queen's archivist again, asking — begging — her to publish the codex before the armies meet, so that every soldier on those plains knows what they're actually fighting about. Maybe truth arriving in time counts for something. It's never been tested at scale."

"And after that?"

"After that," Silas said, "we go to the plains ourselves. Because whatever happens out there, it happens around you, and I am not letting you stand near it without me."

Ember put her hand over Silas's ink-stained one, steadying the pen. Her skin was warm — always warm, since the Guttering had begun, as though the fire inside her were burning its own walls for fuel.

"There's something you should know," Ember said quietly. "About what it costs to stop the fire from guttering out. What the dragons used to do, before the theft. What it requires." Her amber eyes caught the candlelight and held it, the way a wick holds flame. "Because I think you're going to offer. You offer everything. It's your worst habit, worse than dropping things. And I need you to hear the price *before* the moment comes, not during."

Outside, far off across the darkening plains, a horn sounded — once, then again, thin and clear as a crack in glass. Then a dozen more, answering from every direction at once.

The war had begun early.

"Tell me," Silas said, and set down the pen.
