## 16. Plains of Cinders

The grass on the plains had gone brown three days before the armies arrived, and Silas could not stop thinking about that.

It was the small things she noticed now — the way fear worked its way into her attention like water into a wall's cracks. The grass had died early. The birds had left. The horses wouldn't graze facing east, toward the temple host, as though they could smell what was coming off ten thousand warden spears: oiled steel and old certainty.

"They're waiting for dawn," Maren said beside her. She sat on an overturned supply cart, working a whetstone along her short sword with the unhurried rhythm of a woman who had done this before too many times. "Corvane likes his battles at first light. All that symbolism. *The light of truth drives out the dark.*" She snorted. "Man's never fought anyone in the rain in his life."

"You've fought Corvane before?"

"I've fought his kind." Maren tested the edge against her thumb, winced, went back to the stone. "Priests with armies. They always believe harder than you do. That's the trouble."

Silas looked down the slope to where Veyra's dragon-blooded host made camp in the old way — no neat rows, but rings and spirals, tents hung with sigils Silas now recognized from the codex pages she'd memorized and then burned and then regretted burning and then copied again from memory, because that was what scribes did, apparently, even traitor ones. It was in the bones now.

Behind her, Ember sat cross-legged in the grass, braiding daisies that had no business still blooming here, their stems faintly warm where her fingers touched them. The girl had not spoken plainly since the ford. She spoke in that older tongue when she spoke at all, and lately she had barely spoken at all — just watched the eastern horizon with eyes that held more years than her small body could account for.

"Ember," Silas said gently. "You should sleep."

"The fire doesn't sleep," Ember said, in the old language, and then — a gift, rare as rain in the valley — repeated it in words Silas knew. "But I will. A little." She laid her head against Silas's hip without ceremony, the way children do when the world has gotten too large, and within moments she was breathing slow.

Maren watched them both over the whetstone. "She trusts you," she said quietly. "That's either going to save everyone or get us all killed."

"Which is it?"

"Yes," said Maren, and went back to her sword.

* * *

Veyra stood at the lip of the ravine and did not like what he saw.

He was a man built for courts, not camps — tall and narrow as a drawn blade, silver-streaked hair bound back, hands that had never once been calledoused by honest work. His ancestors had worn scales. He wore a breastplate polished to a mirror, because if you were going to die for a stolen inheritance you might as well look magnificent doing it.

Below, spread across the cinder-grey plain like spilled ink, the temple wardens formed their squares. Ten thousand. Perhaps twelve. Corvane had emptied every garrison in the valley for this, and the discipline showed: ranks straight enough to draw blood from the eye, banners of the Quiet Hours hanging motionless in air that should have been moving.

Against him: four thousand exiles, hedge-knights, smugglers, and one disgraced witch who owed him nothing and kept reminding him of it.

"My lord." His captain, Brakka, came up the ravine path with mud to her knees and no reverence anywhere about her person. "Their left wing is thinning. They're shifting weight toward our center."

"Of course they are. They don't want my army. They want the girl." Veyra turned the thought over and found, to his mild surprise, that it tasted bitter. "And we are using her as bait."

"We are using her as *leverage*," Brakka corrected. "There's a difference."

"There is. Bait gets thrown away when the fish takes hold." He looked at his own reflection in his breastplate, distorted, unrecognizable. Somewhere below, Corvane was doing the same arithmetic he was: one child, weighed against a realm. "Send word to Marchetti. When the lines meet, she runs east through the marsh cut with the girl and the witch. No heroics."

"She'll ignore that order."

"She'll ignore that order," Veyra agreed, "which is why I'm sending it. Paper trail, Captain. If this goes wrong, I intend to be very clear about whose fault it was."

Brakka grinned, all teeth. "That's why the men like you, my lord. You're honest about being rotten."

He almost smiled. Then the temple horns began, low and long, rolling across the plain like the voice of something enormous waking, and there was no room in him for anything but the old cold anger, the inheritance he'd never asked for: *they took your fire, they took your name, they wrote you out of the world.* Four thousand voices rose to answer the horns — wordless, ragged, alive — and Lord Veyra drew his sword and led his people down into the light of dawn.

* * *

The battle, from the middle of it, was not a picture. It was a sound.

Silas would remember it afterward as sound: the horns and the drums and then the terrible ordinary noise of bodies colliding, which was less like thunder and more like a market crowd, if a market crowd were screaming. She remembered the smell — iron and horse and voided bowels — and she remembered Maren's hand hard around her wrist, hauling her through the churned grass while the world came apart on either side.

"East! Keep east!" Maren shouted. "Don't look, girl, looking costs time—"

But Silas looked. She couldn't help it; she was a scribe, and her whole life had trained her to record. So she recorded: a warden boy, younger than she was, taking an exile's axe and folding up like wet paper. An old woman in exile colors standing over a fallen standard-bearer and swinging a maul with the patience of someone kneading bread. Fire, somewhere — real fire, dragon-fire, loosed from some relic or bloodline — turning three ranks of pikes into torches, and the screaming that followed, and the smell that followed the screaming.

"This is what the codex promised us," Silas heard herself say, absurdly, to no one. "Peace. Order. The Quiet Hours."

"The Quiet Hours were always just the hours before the shouting started," Maren said grimly. "Move!"

They reached the marsh cut — a seam of black water and reeds running northeast between two low ridges — and Silas dared, for half a breath, to think they might make it.

Then the wardens came down into the cut ahead of them.

Six of them, then eight, white-cloaked, faces hidden behind visors shaped like closed books. Corvane's elite. They had flanked wide in the night; of course they had. Corvane had never needed to read Silas's heart to predict her, because she was predictable, because duty ran in straight lines even when it betrayed itself.

"Go back," Maren said. Not loudly. "Through the reeds, east, there's a rise past the water. You put that child behind the rise and you keep her there."

"Maren—"

"Marchetti." Maren turned, and for once there was no humor in her face at all, only a kind of settled calm that frightened Silas worse than any scream. "I have been running from that temple for thirty years. Thirty years of hedges and hollows and sleeping in ditches, telling myself I was free." She rolled her shoulders, spat into the black water. "Turns out freedom wasn't a place. It was a fight. And I'm overdue."

"You can't hold six of them."

"No," Maren agreed cheerfully, drawing her short sword and a long knife both. "I can't hold six of them. But I can be *extremely inconvenient* for a quarter hour, and a quarter hour is all you need, because I taught you the reed paths myself and they haven't learned them yet." She glanced back once, and the grin returned, crooked and warm and unbearable. "Look after the little one. And Marchetti — the copies you kept? Make sure they see daylight. That's how you kill men like Corvane. Not with swords. With *receipts*."

The first warden reached her.

What happened next happened fast, and Silas made herself watch every moment of it, because Maren deserved a witness. The hedge-witch fought like a woman who had learned brawling in taverns and refined it in alleys — no form, no honor, dirt in the eyes and boots to the knees and the knife coming in under the sword arm. She dropped the first warden. She dropped the second. The third opened her side, and she laughed at him — actually *laughed*, a wild bright sound that carried over the battle-noise — and broke his jaw with the pommel.

Silas grabbed Ember's hand and pulled her into the reeds.

From the water-path, bent double, she saw the last of it in flashes between the stalks: Maren backing toward the ridge, bleeding freely, still grinning, giving ground inch by measured inch so that the wardens' backs stayed to the marsh and their eyes stayed on her and never once turned east. Buying the quarter hour with her body, spending it like a woman who had finally found a debt worth paying.

The last thing Silas saw of her was the last thing Maren wanted seen: the old woman going down under white cloaks with her knives still moving, laughing, laughing—

—and then the reeds closed, and the water swallowed the sound, and Silas ran.

* * *

They made the rise as the sun cleared the ridgeline, and Ember collapsed against a dead oak, shaking.

Not with fear. Silas understood that now, kneeling beside her, gathering the small trembling body in. The girl shook the way a kettle shakes — pressure, heat, too much wanting to come out. The dragon-fire under her skin had felt the battle the way a tuning fork feels a struck bell. Every death on that plain rang through her.

"It hurts," Ember whispered, in the old tongue, then in Silas's own. "All of it. They're all so loud when they go."

"I know." Silas held her tighter. "I know, little flame."

"Is the witch woman dead?"

Silas could not lie to her. That was the one rule she'd set herself on this whole ruinous road. "Yes."

Ember was quiet a long moment. Her small fists closed in Silas's cloak. "Then the fire should remember her," she said at last, "even if the temples won't." And she pressed her palm flat against the earth of the ridge, and where she touched it, the dead grass greened — one small circle, one blade of living proof, absurd and holy amid all that ash.

Silas looked at it and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the worst moments, that they could not run much longer. Not because of the armies. Because of what Ember carried, and what carrying it was doing to a child who grieved for every stranger who died within a mile of her.

Below and beyond, the battle roared on. And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, Silas caught a new sound: horns again — but deeper, wrong-angled, coming from the south.

Not the temple's horns.

Something else had come to take the fire.

* * *

By noon, the battle had stopped being a battle and become a negotiation conducted with corpses.

Veyra saw it clearly from the shattered center of his line, where he stood ankle-deep in the mud of his ancestors' shame. The temple host had broken his left wing and then, inexplicably, stopped pressing — because Corvane's own right flank was dissolving, torn open by riders in colors Veyra did not recognize until he did, and then his stomach turned to lead.

The southern kingdoms. The crown's own lances. Drawn by rumor of dragon-fire the way carrion draws crows, arriving late and fresh to harvest what the temple and the exiles had spent the morning buying with blood.

Three powers on one plain. One child on a ridge. Veyra laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it whatsoever.

"My lord?" Brakka, grey-faced, her shield split down the middle.

"We were fools, all three of us," he said. "Corvane wants to *own* the fire. The crown wants to *weaponize* it. And I—" He looked down at his beautiful mirrored breastplate, spattered now with other people's blood, and finished the sentence honestly, because Brakka had earned honesty. "I wanted to *avenge* it. None of us ever once thought to ask what the fire wanted."

"And what does it want?"

Veyra looked east, toward the dead oak on the ridge, where a scribe and a child stood silhouetted against the smoke — and where, even at this distance, he could see the grass growing green beneath a small bare foot.

"I suspect," he said slowly, "it wants exactly what it has always wanted. To be given. Not taken." He sheathed his sword, and the gesture felt like closing a book he'd been reading his whole life. "Sound the withdrawal, Captain. Full retreat south. We are done fighting over that child."

"And Corvane?"

Veyra watched the white cloaks regrouping on the western slope, watched the tall figure at their center raise one arm toward the ridge — and saw, with terrible clarity, that Corvane had no intention of stopping. That he would walk through the crown's lances, through the exiles, through hell itself, smiling his gentle smile, because in his heart he had already crowned himself and the rest was paperwork.

"He keeps coming," Veyra said. "God help her. He keeps coming."

On the ridge, the smoke parted, and Silas Sandoval turned to face the west — and in her arms, Ember lifted her head, and her eyes had gone the color of coals about to gutter.

"I'm tired," the child said plainly. "The fire is going out."
