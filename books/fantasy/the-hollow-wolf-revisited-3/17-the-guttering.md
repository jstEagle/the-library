## 17. The Guttering

Ember had learned, in her years of being a creature rather than a person, that pain came in two kinds.

There was the kind that shouted — teeth and claws and the bright red flash of a warden's blade. That kind was honest. It wanted something specific from you: run, fight, hide. She could do those things. She had done them since before she had words.

And then there was the other kind. The quiet kind. The kind that lived in the chest like a stone dropped into a well and never heard hitting bottom.

The fire was dying.

She had known it for three days now, since the ford, since Maren's rope went slack and the wardens dragged Maren away into the dark and Silas burned a hole in the world with a scrap of borrowed flame to get them out. Ember had felt it then — not as a wound but as a *withdrawing*, the way a tide pulls back before anyone thinks to call it a wave. The dragon-fire inside her, which had roared through her whole small body for as long as she could remember like a hearth that would never go cold, had begun to gutter.

Now they crouched in the ruin of an old shepherd's hut on the shoulder of the Grayfell mountains, and Ember sat very still with her hands in her lap, because if she moved too fast she could feel the fire sloshing thin inside her like the last water in a skin.

Silas didn't know yet.

That was the problem with learning words late. Ember had spent eleven years saying nothing because there was nothing worth saying, and now that she had a language — Silas's language, plain and warm and full of ridiculous little words like *suppertime* and *blister* — she kept discovering that some things were still nearly impossible to say. Words were doors, Silas had told her once, laughing, and you have to walk through a hundred of them to get anywhere. But nobody had told her what to do when the door you needed was made of glass.

"Here." Silas pressed a tin cup into her hands. Steam rose off it — nettle tea again, because nettle tea was what grew on mountainsides and Silas said beggars couldn't be connoisseurs. "Drink. All of it. You've been gray as dishwater since dawn."

"I am always gray," Ember said. "I am a very gray person."

"You are *pale*," Silas corrected, "which is different, and worse." She sat back on her heels and studied Ember with those scribe's eyes, the ones that caught everything and wrote it down somewhere behind them. "Your hands are shaking."

"The cup is shaking," Ember said. "The cup is very nervous."

"Ember."

"It's the mountain air. It's rude. I don't like it either."

Silas did the thing she did when she wasn't fooled, which was say nothing at all and simply wait, patient as a page drying. Ember hated that thing. She loved that thing. She had decided, somewhere over the last weeks, that this was probably what having a mother felt like, though she'd never had one long enough to compare, and the thought made her chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with dying fire.

She drank the tea. It tasted like boiled grass and forgiveness.

Outside, the wind worried at the broken roof. Somewhere below, faint as a memory, the plains of Cinders still smoked where two armies had torn each other open looking for her. Looking for the fire. And here she was, holding all of it in her two gray hands, letting it leak away like a cracked pot.

*Tell her,* said the dragon-memory, in its old slow voice, like a river moving under ice.

*I'm getting there.*

*You have been 'getting there' since the ford.*

*Some doors are made of glass.*

The dragon-memory did not sigh, exactly. But Ember felt something move through her that was the dragon equivalent of a sigh, vast and weary and fond, the way a mountain might be fond of a stubborn goat.

"Silas," she said.

"Mm?"

"If I tell you something, will you promise not to do your face?"

"My face?"

"Your *face*. The face where you decide everything already and you're just waiting for me to finish so you can be right at me."

Silas's mouth twitched. "I don't have a face like that."

"You absolutely have a face like that. Veyra has one too. Everyone who reads books gets the face eventually. I think it's in the binding."

For a moment — one golden, ordinary moment — Silas laughed. Really laughed, the quiet snorting laugh she tried to hide behind her hand, the laugh that made her look young instead of tired, and Ember thought: *I want to keep this. Whatever happens, I want to carry this sound down with me.*

Then she set down the cup, and stopped hiding.

"It's going out," she said. "The fire."

*

The silence afterward lasted four heartbeats. Ember counted them, because counting was calming, because numbers were the first thing Silas had ever taught her and numbers never lied.

On the fifth heartbeat, Silas said, "No."

"Yes."

"No, it isn't. You're exhausted. You've been shot at, marched over half a kingdom, you haven't eaten anything today but—"

"A nettle. You gave me a nettle."

"—and you're frightened, and frightened people borrow trouble—"

"*Silas.*" Ember's voice came out strange — deeper than her own, threaded with the old resonance, the way it always did when the fire spoke through her. She watched Silas flinch and made herself keep going. "Look at my eyes."

Silas looked. And Ember let her see.

She opened herself the way the dragons used to open themselves to each other, the sharing that needed no words — except that now she had words, and found they helped after all. She showed Silas the inside of her, or the shape of it: the great dimming hearth at her center, banked low, its coals going gray at the edges. A thousand years ago it had been a bonfire. Even at the ford it had been a campfire. Now—

"Oh," Silas whispered. Her face did something terrible and careful. "Oh, Ember."

"It started dying at the ford. Maybe before. Maybe it's been dying since the temples—" She stopped. Since the betrayal, she almost said. Since the first theft. But Silas knew that history now; she'd wept over it in the ruined archive with the confession codex open in her lap like a wound. "Fire needs feeding. That's the whole secret. Everyone thinks fire is greed, burning taking taking taking, but fire is the most generous thing there is. It only wants to be given to. The dragons fed each other. That's what the giving was. Not sacrifice — *sharing*. One dragon giving its years to another when the other's ran short, freely, gladly, the way you'd share bread."

"And no one's given to yours."

"No one's left who can." Ember looked down at her hands. Small hands. Scribe's hands, almost, ink-stained these days from copying letters Silas set for her practice. "Veyra wanted to take. Corvane wants to take. Taking is why there's hardly any fire left in the whole world. Every stolen flame burns wrong. It burns out faster every generation. I'm the last living coal, and I'm in a body that's eleven years old and eats nettles, and the war spent most of what I had, and—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "When mine goes out, that's all of it. No more magic anywhere. Not fading. Gone. The springs stop. The hedge-crafts stop. Everything the world has left of wonder just... ends, quietly, in a shepherd's hut, with a girl drinking bad tea."

Silas put her arms around her.

It was not a dramatic gesture. That was the thing about Silas — everything real about her was undramatic. She just gathered Ember in, the way she did every night at bedtime, one hand cradling the back of her head, and held on, and Ember, who had survived alone in wild country for years by being nothing but sharp edges and silence, fell apart against her shoulder like a wet page.

They stayed that way while the wind worked at the roof and the light went amber and then blue.

Finally Silas said, into her hair, "You said *when someone gives*. At the ford. In the fire-speech, before you passed out. You said the fire could be saved if someone gave their remaining years. I've been turning it over ever since. I thought I'd misunderstood."

"You didn't misunderstand."

"Whose years, Ember? Yours are the ones that are running out, and you're eleven, you barely have any to give, and anyway—" She pulled back and held Ember at arm's length, and her eyes had gone bright and hard and terrified, and Ember knew, with the awful clarity of the dying, exactly what Silas was about to say next. "Anyway, it wouldn't work even if you did. Would it?"

Ember said nothing.

"Would it?" Silas repeated. "Because if the answer were *the bearer gives their own years*, you'd have done it already. You'd have done it at the ford and spared us all this conversation about nettles. So it has to be someone else. Someone whose years are theirs to give. Freely." The word came out of her like a tooth. "*Freely*, as the dragons once did. That's what you said."

"You promised not to do the face," Ember whispered.

"I'm not doing the face."

"You're doing the face. The face where you decide everything already."

"Ember." Silas's hands were shaking now, actually shaking, and she pressed them flat against her knees to stop them, a scribe's habit, steadying paper. "No."

"You haven't even heard—"

"There's nothing to hear. There is no version of this where I sit here and watch you die so I can—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "I copied prayers for fifteen years, Ember. Fifteen years of words about love and mercy and giving, and I never believed one of them, not once, not in my bones. Do you know what changed that? You did. Mud on your feet, screaming at my door in a dead language. You taught me that the words were true. And now you want to stand there and tell me the price of keeping them true is—"

"It isn't a price." Ember took her hands. They were cold, Silas's hands. When had Silas's hands gotten so cold? "Listen to me properly, because I'm going to say it the way the dragons meant it, and then I'm going to say it the way a person means it, and you're going to listen to both."

Silas listened. Of course she listened. Listening was her whole soul.

"The dragons didn't grieve the giving," Ember said. "Understand that. We — *they* — they were long-lived, longer than kingdoms, and near the end of a life, when the wings grew heavy and the fire wanted new ground, a dragon would find a younger one and pour itself out, all its hoarded years at once, and it was the happiest moment of its existence. Not because it was dying. Because it was *finishing*. Because everything it had been became fuel for everything someone else would be. There's a word in the old tongue for it. It doesn't mean sacrifice. Sacrifice implies loss. It means..." She searched. "It means handing the lamp forward. Lighting the next lamp from yours, when yours is nearly burnt anyway, so that the light never has to start over in the dark."

"That's beautiful," Silas said, unsteady, "and it changes nothing, because I am not a dragon, and my years are not nearly burnt, and I am *not finished*—"

"Now the person version," Ember said, and squeezed her hands, and leaned close, and said it plainly, the way Silas had taught her to say plain things. "You owe this valley fifteen more years of debt-slavery. You were going to spend them copying prayers you don't believe, in a building that was built on a lie, for priests who would have cut a child open on an altar. Those aren't your years, Silas. They never were. Your mother's debt was a fiction written by thieves, and every year you paid on it was a year stolen from you the same way the fire was stolen from us." She swallowed. "So don't tell me you'd be losing anything. Tell me you'd be *spending* it. On the first free choice you've ever been allowed to make. On the only person in the world who will ever know exactly what your laughter sounds like from the inside."

"Don't," Silas whispered.

"On the person who taught me words," Ember said, "when I had none. Who named me. Who chose *Ember* — do you understand? Not flame, not blaze, not anything grand. An ember. The part of the fire that survives the night. You named me for the thing that waits." Her own tears came now, hot and finally unhidden. "Let me wait for you. Let me be the coal that carries it. Give me the years, Silas. *Freely.* Or the fire dies hating the world, and I die with it, and everything everyone bled for on those plains — Maren, all of it — becomes nothing but ash that the wind gets to keep."

*

Silas got up.

She walked to the broken window and stood there with her back turned, and Ember let her, because grief needs room and Silas had spent fifteen years in rooms too small for hers.

The moon came up over the Grayfells, huge and white, and its light lay across the floor between them like a path neither of them had chosen.

"I prayed for a miracle once," Silas said at last, without turning around. "Did I ever tell you? First year in the temple. I was maybe your age. I knelt in front of the reliquary — the same one they locked you in, isn't that a joke — and I prayed for my mother's debt to be forgiven. Just wiped clean. One line in one ledger. I figured the gods could manage it." A breath that was almost a laugh. "Nothing happened. Obviously. And I decided right then that prayer was a coin that bought nothing, and I never knelt again."

"Silas—"

"But here's what I've been thinking, sitting in ditches and ruins with you for a season." She turned. Her face was wrecked and calm at once, the way it had been the day she burned the harvest scrolls, terror and exhilaration wearing the same expression. "Maybe nothing happened because the gods weren't listening. Maybe what listens is older than gods. Maybe it was listening the whole time and waiting for somebody to ask *correctly*. Not *take this burden from me*. Not *give me what I'm owed*." She crossed the room and knelt in front of Ember, eye to eye, and took both her hands, and her grip was steady now. "Maybe the correct asking is what you said. Handing the lamp forward."

"No," Ember said, and hated how small it came out. "No, you said no, you don't get to change your answer, that's not how answers work—"

"I'm a scribe, love. Changing the text is the entire job." Her thumb brushed the tear off Ember's cheek, matter-of-fact, the way she corrected a smudged letter. "But not tonight. Tonight you'll eat something that isn't a nettle, and sleep, and I'll hold the door of you shut with everything I've got, and tomorrow — tomorrow we climb. There's an old shrine past the ridge, Maren spoke of it once, a place where the first giving happened. If the fire must be rekindled, it should happen there, on the dragons' own ground. And if I change my mind in the night—" her voice caught, only once, and she mastered it, "-then you'll wake up and we'll think of something else. Scribes always think of something else. That's the other half of the job."

Ember stared at her. "You're lying. About the changing-your-mind part."

"Terribly," Silas agreed. "But kindly. Lie with me a few hours. Kindly lies deserve that much."

And because the fire was guttering, and because she was eleven, and because Silas's arms were the one warm thing in a world gone gray at the edges, Ember let herself be lied to. She slept in the circle of those cold hands and dreamed of dragons passing lamps down a chain of darkness, farther and farther, until at the very end of the chain stood a small figure in a scribe's robe, holding her little flame out into the black, whispering the oldest word in any language —

and far away, on a temple spire above the smoking plains, a man in white lifted a stolen fragment of fire toward the same moon, and smiled at how easy it had all been.
