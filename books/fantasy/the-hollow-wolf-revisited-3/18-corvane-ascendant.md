## 18. Corvane Ascendant

The wind at the top of the spire smelled of iron and old stone, and High Priest Corvane had always found it honest.

Down in the valley, the air was thick with incense and the sweet rot of candlewax, with the murmured prayers of a thousand throats saying words they did not understand. Down there, everything was softened, padded, made palatable. But up here, six hundred steps above the Temple of Quiet Hours, the wind told you exactly what it was. Corvane had climbed these stairs every morning for thirty years, and every morning he had thought: *this is the only true prayer left in the valley.* The wind asking nothing. The wind giving nothing. Just the world, raw and unadorned.

He stood now at the parapet with a brazier before him and a fragment of fire cupped in a reliquary of black glass, and he tried to feel like a thief.

He could not manage it.

* * *

He had been a young man once — this was the thing no one remembered about him, the thing he himself could barely hold onto anymore. He had been a novitiate of nineteen with ink-stained fingers and a voice that cracked during the litany, and he had believed. Oh, how he had believed. He had believed so hard it had hurt, the way a fever hurts, the way love hurts.

His mother had brought him to the temple gates when he was seven, because she could not feed him through the winter, and the temple took children of debt the way other institutions took coin. He remembered crying at first, and then remembering that crying was not permitted during the Hours, and then — this was the strange part — discovering that he did not need to cry anymore, because the temple had given him something better than his mother's kitchen ever had. It had given him *certainty*. Every hour had its purpose. Every prayer had its place. Every suffering had been accounted for, entered into the great ledger, and would be balanced in the end.

A boy who has known hunger will defend the ledger with his life. Corvane had learned that early. Order was not cruelty. Order was the wall between the poor and the dark.

And the Quiet — the blessed Quiet of the valley, where magic could not follow — he had understood even as a child that it was mercy. Magic was the old chaos, the age of dragons and burning skies and kings who claimed godhood by right of flame. The first temples had ended all that. The first temples had built the valley where power could not take root, and in doing so they had saved the small people, the hungry people, the people like his mother who had nothing to offer the world but their labor and their prayers.

That was the doctrine. That was what he had copied out ten thousand times in his own careful hand.

It had taken him forty years to learn the truth underneath it, and when he had learned it — crouched over the restricted codices in the deep archive, alone, at night — he had sat back on his heels and felt something crack quietly inside his chest, like ice on a river in spring.

The temples had not ended the age of fire. They had *stolen* it. They had betrayed the dragons, slaughtered them at the truce-fires, and taken their flame into vaults, and built their authority upon the theft, and written doctrines for a thousand years to make the theft holy.

Corvane had wept that night. He wanted that recorded somewhere, if any record of him survived: *he wept.*

And then, being the man he was, he had dried his eyes, returned the codex to its shelf, and begun to plan.

Because here was the conclusion he had reached, alone in the dark with the truth laid out before him like an opened body: if the foundation was theft, then the structure must either fall or be made worthy of it. He could tear down the church — and plunge the realm into the old chaos, the warring courts, the dragon-kings, the burning skies. Or he could complete it. Take the last fire, the fire that had somehow lived on in a feral child's blood, and make it serve the purpose the thieves had only pretended to serve. Not power for its own sake. Power as *stewardship*. A god-king who actually governed, who actually protected, who actually kept the ledger balanced — instead of a priesthood that lied.

They would call it theft. He had already heard them say it, in the whispers of the lower cloisters, in the frightened sidelong looks of the scribes. *Corvane means to steal the child's fire.*

But Silas Sandoval had burned the preparation scrolls, hadn't she? Had fled with the girl across the border into the loud, chaotic, magic-drunk world beyond the valley. And the exiled dragon-blooded lord Veyra wanted the same fire to fuel his vengeance. And the royal court wanted it to preserve their throne. Everyone wanted the flame. Everyone would use it.

The only difference — the difference Corvane clung to like a drowning man clings to a spar — was that *he* knew what it cost. He knew the history. He knew the sin. And a man who knows the sin is the only one fit to carry the burden of it.

*This is not ambition,* he told the wind. *This is penance.*

The wind, honestly, said nothing at all.

* * *

The fragment sat in its black-glass reliquary, no larger than a sparrow's egg, and it moved.

That was the thing the old texts had failed to convey, or perhaps the old texts had never held one this close. Fire was supposed to be still — a candleflame, a hearthfire, obedient. This was not still. It turned slowly inside the glass like a creature dreaming, and its color shifted through shades that had no names in any language Corvane spoke: gold going to green going to a white so deep it looked like a hole cut in the world. When he leaned close, he felt warmth on his face, and beneath the warmth, faintly, something else. A pressure. An attention.

As though it were looking back.

"Your kind wrote the first ledgers," he murmured to it. "Did you know that? Before the temples, before script. The dragons kept accounts of everything — debts, gifts, betrayals. The codices say so." He smiled thinly. "I have read the codices they didn't burn."

The fire turned. The pressure against his awareness deepened, patient and vast, and Corvane found himself thinking — absurdly, horribly — of his mother's kitchen, of the winter she had sent him away, of the way she had knelt and taken his face in her chapped hands and said, *be good where I cannot see you.* He had spent fifty years trying to be good where no one could see him. Perhaps that was all ambition ever was: goodness performed for an audience of none, curdling into something else when the audience never came.

"You will be better off with me," he told the fragment. "You have been passed hand to hand like a stolen coin your whole existence. Stolen from the dragons. Hidden in a child. Hunted by courts. At least I know what I am taking. At least I will *use* you."

The fire flickered — once, sharply, like a snort of contempt — and Corvane laughed despite himself, a dry sound the wind tore away.

There. That was the pity of it, wasn't it? He could feel it looking at him and he could feel what it saw: an old man in vestments too heavy for the season, standing at the top of a tower he had climbed out of duty until the climbing became the whole of him, preparing to do the very thing his entire faith was founded upon, and calling it correction. The fire pitied him. He was almost sure of it. Somewhere behind that white depth was a memory of the men who had come to the truce-fires with knives and scripture, and they too had probably spoken softly to the flames. They too had probably believed.

*I am not them,* he thought, and then, immediately, with the terrible honesty the spire-wind demanded: *I am exactly them. I have simply read more.*

* * *

Below, the plains were moving.

He could see them from here — two armies converging on the river country like slow-spilled ink, Veyra's host with their ash-grey banners and the royal column with its gilded eagles, and between them and behind them, his own wardens flowing south in disciplined black lines. Three powers, three hungers, all pointed at one small wounded girl and the fire she carried.

He had declared war within the hour of Silas Sandoval's little trick — the traded codex, the hidden copies, the smug look on her plain face as she'd walked out of his study. He had admired it, actually. He had admired her from the beginning, from the moment he'd watched her kneel beside the feral child in the reliquary and seen her translate not just the words but the *meaning*, her whole rigid faith bending toward wonder. She was the best of them. That was why she had to be wrong. If the best of the temple could walk away, then the temple deserved to fall, and he was not prepared to let it deserve anything.

The war would be brief. That was the tragedy he had accepted: wars were always brief for the ones who planned them and endless for everyone else. The courts would exhaust each other. His wardens would hold the center. And when the smoke cleared, the kingdoms — terrified, bleeding, leaderless — would come to the one institution that had never broken, the one spire still standing, and they would beg him to take the crown. He would refuse, publicly, twice. On the third asking, he would accept, with visible reluctance, for the sake of the realm.

He had rehearsed the reluctance in the mirror. It needed work.

And the fire — the fire would sit beneath the crown, not above it. He had designed the rite himself, correcting the errors of the original thieves. No draining, no harvesting, no death. A *covenant*, he called it. The fragment would anchor itself to the valley's heart and spread outward, a controlled return of magic to the world, distributed through temple channels, rationed, registered, taxed. Miracles on a schedule. Wonder with a ledger.

No one would ever have to send their child to a gate again because they could not feed it through the winter. That was the promise. That was the whole of it, if anyone had cared to ask, which no one ever did, because no one believed anymore that a man like him could want anything but the crown itself.

"They're wrong about me," he said aloud, to the fire, to the wind, to the plains below. "Silas is wrong. Veyra is wrong. Even the girl—" he stopped.

Even the girl. The feral child who spoke the old tongue, who had looked at him across the reliquary bars with those ember-colored eyes, ancient and unimpressed, and said — in the language older than kingdoms, which he had not understood and had understood completely — something he had carried for weeks now like a splinter:

*The fire does not go to the one who takes it.*

He had told himself it was superstition. Dragon-mythology. The romantic nonsense of a dying species. And yet.

And yet the fragment in its glass had never once warmed to him. Thirty days he had kept it, studied it, spoken to it, prayed over it in the old forms, and it remained exactly as distant as the moon. Whereas the texts all agreed — the true texts, the ones under the floor — that the fire had once *leapt* to its bearers. Had chosen them. Had gone gladly.

He looked down at the reliquary, and the fire looked back, and for one naked moment Corvane allowed himself to see the arrangement clearly: an old man holding a stolen thing that did not want him, atop a tower built on a lie he had inherited, about to perform a rite of his own invention, and calling all of it service.

"Pity me, then," he said quietly. "But pity is not a plan."

He set the reliquary into the cradle of the rite-apparatus — brass and silver and black iron, beautiful, precise, his own design — and began the preparations. The alignments. The inscriptions. Each step performed with the same care he had once given to copying scripture, because craft was the one honesty left to him.

Far below, a bell began to toll the Hour of Ashes, and the sound rose up the spire like water rising in a well.

And that was when he saw them — far off, at the edge of the plains where the light was failing: a small party moving fast along the river road. Four figures, maybe five. One limping. One small enough to be carried.

One walking with the particular stubborn stride he recognized anywhere, having watched it for years across a scriptorium — the stride of Silas Sandoval, coming home.

Corvane closed his eyes and felt something in his chest that was not quite fear and not quite hope.

"So," he said to the fire. "She's bringing you back to me after all."

The fragment flared — bright, brief, furious — and for the first time in thirty days, it moved *toward* the window. Toward the road. Toward her.

Corvane watched it, and understood, and smiled anyway, because a man at the top of a spire has nowhere left to look but down.

"Then we shall see," he murmured, "whose faith holds."

He turned to the stairs and rang for his wardens.
