## 19. The Giving

The battlefield had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Silas stood at the edge of the Plains of Cinders with Ember's hand small and burning-hot in hers, and she thought — absurdly, in the last moments she might have to think anything at all — of the ink mortar back in the Temple of Quiet Hours. Of how she used to grind the pigment in slow circles and pretend the rhythm was prayer. It had never been prayer. It had been waiting. She understood that now. Twenty-two years of copying other people's holy words, and every stroke of the stylus had been a question she hadn't known how to ask.

*Is this all there is?*

The fire in her palm answered: *No.*

"Tell me again," Silas said. "Slowly. And don't skip the part where I die, because I've noticed you have a talent for skipping the parts where people die."

Ember looked up at her. The girl was nine years old and smeared with ash and someone else's blood — Maren's blood, though Silas refused to let herself finish that sentence yet — and her eyes held the amber light of something far older than nine. When she spoke plainly now, her voice was still small, still child-shaped, but underneath it ran a current like a river under ice.

"The dragons did not hoard their fire," Ember said. "They *gave* it. That was the first covenant. Fire freely given grows. Fire taken only burns what holds it. The first temples took." Her small fingers tightened around Silas's. "You can give it back."

"And the price is everything I have left."

"Yes."

"All my borrowed years."

"They were never borrowed," Ember said, with the gentle ferocity of a child correcting an adult's arithmetic. "They were always yours. That's what makes them worth giving."

* * *

Let me tell you how we got here, Silas thought, because if these were her final thoughts she wanted them to be honest ones, and honesty required a ledger. She was, after all, a scribe. She thought in ledgers.

Item: one valley where magic could not follow, and one scribe who grew up inside its silence without knowing silence was unusual.

Item: one feral child who arrived speaking the language of dragons, locked in a reliquary by men who smelled power the way sharks smell blood.

Item: High Priest Corvane, who had once — she truly believed this — wanted to serve, and who had spent so many years serving himself instead that he could no longer tell the difference. She had seen him on the spire that morning, crowned in stolen flame, arms raised over his assembled wardens, and she had felt not hatred but a terrible pity, the way you pity a man who mistakes a mirror for a window.

Item: Maren, taken at the ford, traded back, fallen on the cinders covering their retreat. *Fallen*, Silas wrote firmly in the mental ledger, not *dead*. Scribes chose their words carefully. Words made things true.

Item: two armies — Corvane's white-robed wardens and Veyra's dragon-blooded exiles — grinding each other into the ash of the plain, both convinced the fire belonged to them, neither understanding that fire had never once in the history of the world belonged to anyone.

And the final item, the one that unbalanced all the others: a choice, offered not by priests or lords but by a nine-year-old girl with ash in her hair.

"You could refuse," Ember said now, as if reading the ledger over her shoulder. "The fire would still choose someone else. Someday. Maybe in a hundred years. Maybe too late."

"That's a terrible way to persuade someone."

"I'm not persuading. I'm just telling you the truth. Persuading is when you leave things out." A pause, and then, with a flicker of the ordinary child beneath the ancient current: "Maren taught me that. She said it's what lawyers do, and also high priests, and also me when I say the porridge is nearly ready when actually the porridge doesn't exist."

Silas laughed. It startled her — a real laugh, cracked and wet, standing on a battlefield at the end of everything. But that was the thing no one told you about dying, she supposed. Or about living right up to the edge of it. Everything ordinary became unbearably precious. Porridge. Ink mortars. Maren's terrible rope knots and worse singing. The way Ember had once eaten an entire honeycomb in the hedge-witch's cottage with her whole face.

"I want you to know," Silas said, kneeling so they were eye to eye, "that I am not doing this out of duty. I want to be very clear about that. Duty is what Corvane calls it when he wants something. This is—" She searched twenty-two years of copied scripture for the word and found none of them adequate, so she made one up, the way the girl had once made up languages. "This is *wanting-with-your-whole-chest*."

Ember nodded solemnly, as though this were a term of art from an ancient codex. Perhaps, Silas thought, it was.

* * *

The ritual, if you could call it that, required no circle drawn in chalk, no scrolls, no incantation. That was the joke of it — the great cosmic joke that Corvane had built a spire and an army and a theology upon, and the truth underneath was as simple as opening your hand.

"You hold the fire," Ember said. "You've been holding it since the ford. It came to you because you asked nothing of it. Now you give it back through yourself — all of yourself. Your years are the door. You open the door, and the fire goes home, and coming home it grows, and growing it sweeps clean."

"Sweeps clean," Silas repeated. "That sounds ominous."

"It means it will burn away everything false. Stolen fire. Stolen crowns. Stolen—"

"Ledgers?"

Ember considered. "Especially ledgers."

Of course, Silas thought. Of course the fire hated ledgers. She had spent her life in service to a bookkeeping God, a deity of columns and balances, of debt recorded and debt discharged, and the actual divine truth of the world turned out to be a gift economy run by enormous flying lizards who gave away their own hearts.

She stood. Across the plain, the battle was resuming in ragged pulses — horns, screams, the wet percussion of bodies. On the temple spire, distant and glittering, Corvane raised his stolen fragment higher, and the sky above him curdled an ugly orange, the color of a lie told loudly enough.

"Before I do this," Silas said, "I need you to promise me something. Not fire-promise. Girl-promise."

"Anything."

"Live boringly. For at least a decade. Plant the spring. Argue about porridge. Learn to whistle badly. Be nine for as long as nine lasts, and then be ten, which I'm told is insufferable, and enjoy every minute of it. Promise me you won't turn all this—" she gestured at the burning horizon, the ancient current in the child's eyes, "—into a burden before you're tall enough to carry it."

Ember's chin trembled. For a moment the dragon-light receded and there was only a small girl, orphaned a hundred times over, holding the last heart-fire of a murdered race in her chest.

"I promise," she whispered. "Girl-promise. The strongest kind."

"Well then." Silas rolled up her sleeves — a habit from the scriptorium, though there would be no more copying, ever, and wasn't that a strange grief, to lose the work you'd resented — and opened her hands. "Let us pray," she said, "for the first time in my life, in a language I actually speak."

* * *

It did not hurt.

That was the second cosmic joke, and Silas laughed aloud at it even as the light began to pour out of her. Everyone feared the giving. The temples taught that sacrifice was agony, that holiness was purchased with suffering, that the gods kept meticulous accounts of pain rendered unto them. But the fire came through her like spring water through a thawed stream, warm and unhurried, and every year of her life passed through her hands on its way out and she *felt* them, all of them, and they were —

they were good.

Her mother's hands over hers teaching the first letters. Rain on the reliquary roof the night she read the forbidden codex by candle-stub. Maren's rope landing in her palms at the ford. Ember asleep against her shoulder on the road, breathing like a bellows, smelling of woodsmoke and honey.

Not one year of it had been wasted. Not even the gray ones. Especially not the gray ones — because the gray ones had taught her to see light.

The fire went home through her, and coming home it remembered itself.

* * *

What happened next, no single witness saw entire, and later accounts would disagree on everything except its effect. But Silas saw it, in her last expanding moment, from everywhere at once, the way fire sees:

She saw the stolen fragment on Corvane's spire gutter, sputter, and go out like a candle pinched between wise fingers — and saw the high priest stagger, suddenly an old man in heavy robes, reaching for a crown made of nothing.

She saw the wardens' weapons grow too hot to hold, not scalding but *persuasive*, each sword gently suggesting it would rather be a plowshare.

She saw Veyra's exiles fall to their knees across the plain, weeping, as the sigils on their skin lit up for the first time in a thousand years — recognized, claimed, forgiven.

She saw Maren — *fallen, not dead, words make things true* — stir in the field hospital tents as warmth flooded wounds that had been cold for hours.

She saw the valley, far behind the mountains, shiver in its sleep as the dead air twitched, stirred, and drew one long, astonished breath.

And she saw Ember — small, upright, blazing gently like a hearth rather than a pyre — standing alone in the middle of the plains while armies stopped mid-slaughter to stare at a child wreathed in the oldest light in the world, unowned, ungoverned, given.

The rekindled flame passed over the battlefield like a tide, and where it touched, it did not destroy. It *corrected*. False oaths crumbled. Forged doctrine curled to ash in the archives of three kingdoms simultaneously. Somewhere in the Temple of Quiet Hours, every ledger of debt burst quietly into flame, every one, down to the oldest codex, until the temple smelled of smoke and freedom and the scribes ran into the courtyard shouting, unsure whether the world was ending or beginning.

Both, Silas thought, from everywhere. Both, always. That's the only prayer there ever was.

The light crested, hung above the world like a held note —

and settled, softly, into Ember's cupped hands, where it stayed, the size of a sparrow's egg, patient as a seed.

* * *

Silas came back to herself lying in the ash, and knew immediately what she had lost, because she could feel its absence the way an amputee feels weather. The years were gone. All the years after this one. She was, in the precise accounting she had spent her life perfecting, balanced to zero.

But oh — the *lightness*.

"Silas." Ember's face swam above her, tear-streaked and furious and radiant. "You didn't tell me it would feel like that. You didn't tell me it would feel like *losing*."

"Had you asked, I'd have mentioned it," Silas managed. Her voice sounded thin as old vellum. "But you were busy being cryptic. Family trait, apparently. You've been my family approximately four months and already you're cryptic at me."

"Don't joke."

"Joking is the entire estate I have left. Let me spend it."

Ember laughed and sobbed at once and gathered Silas's head into her lap, and around them the battlefield was transforming — soldiers lowering weapons, staring at their own empty hands; wardens removing their white hoods as though waking from a long obedience; and far off, on its spire, the temple going dark, dark, dark.

High above, the sky itself seemed lighter. Cleaned. As though the world had been wearing smoke for a thousand years and had finally been permitted to wash its face.

"What happens now?" Ember whispered.

Silas watched the armies stand down in slow, bewildered ripples, watched enemies help each other up off the cinders, watched the first green shoots — impossibly, gloriously — push up through the ash wherever the fire had passed, and she smiled with everything she had left, which was more than enough.

"Now?" she said. "Now we go home. I want to see the valley breathe. And you, First Mage of the Hollow—" she reached up and tapped the small blazing sigil over Ember's heart, "—you're going to need someone to teach you to write. Properly. With margins."

Ember bent close, and her whisper carried the warmth of everything given and nothing taken:

"Then we'd better hurry. We haven't got forever."

"No," Silas agreed, watching the sun set gold and clean over the plains of cinders, feeling the road home pull at her like a thread. "We haven't. Which is exactly enough."
