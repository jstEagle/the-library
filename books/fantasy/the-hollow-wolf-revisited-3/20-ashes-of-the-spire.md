## 20. Ashes of the Spire

The spire had stood for nine hundred years. Corvane had climbed it every morning of his adult life, and he had always believed — had built his whole self upon the belief — that the stone would outlast him.

Now he sat in the rubble of what had been the highest chamber in the Temple of Quiet Hours, and the stone lay around him like the bones of a great animal that had finally been permitted to die.

He did not know how long he had been sitting there. The fire had passed over the battlefield at dawn, and it was past noon now, and between those two moments there was a gap in Corvane's memory shaped exactly like his life.

His hands were burned. He looked at them with a kind of distant curiosity, the way one might regard the hands of a stranger. The stolen fragment of dragon-fire was gone from the reliquary box he had carried up the spire steps — gone, and not merely extinguished. *Unmade.* He had felt it leave the world, felt the flame pass overhead like a judgment that did not even bother to look down at him, and in that moment he had understood something so simple and so terrible that his mind kept sliding away from it the way a boot slides off ice:

It had never been his. None of it. Not the fire, not the authority, not the nine hundred years of doctrine he had spent his life defending and then, somewhere in the last dark year, quietly begun to bend toward his own purposes. The fire had chosen a scribe who ground her own ink. The fire had chosen a child who spoke in the old tongue. It had never once, in all its long history, chosen a High Priest.

He laughed. It came out as a cough, and then as something worse than a cough, and he pressed his burned hands against his face and let the sound become whatever it needed to be.

Below him, through a gap where the eastern wall had been, he could see the temple courtyard. Scribes were emerging from the undercrofts blinking, ash-sifted, alive. One of them — young Brother Aldous, who inked his margins with little birds when he thought no one was looking — was helping an elderly sister over a fallen beam, and the two of them were arguing about something, gesturing, exasperated, *alive*, and Corvane watched them with the peculiar hunger of a man watching a country he has been exiled from.

They would need a reckoning, of course. There would be questions. *Where is the High Priest? What was the rite? Why did wardens march on a child?* And Corvane found, sitting in the ashes of everything he had built, that he was tired of answers. Tired of shaping truth into useful forms. Tired of the great labor of his life, which had never actually been service, whatever he told himself in the small honest hours — the labor of holding power together with both hands while telling everyone, including himself, that his hands were empty.

He had once believed in service. That was the cruel joke of it. At nineteen, kneeling in this same courtyard, he had sworn the oaths with tears running freely down his face, and he had meant every word. Somewhere along the way — he could not find the moment; he had searched for it for years — meaning had become performance, and performance had become habit, and habit had become a man who locked a mud-caked child in a reliquary and called it protection.

A stone shifted behind him. He did not turn.

"You're alive, then," said Sister Ondine, who had run the temple kitchens for forty years and feared no one, including him. Especially, lately, him. She picked her way across the rubble with a basket on her arm, because Sister Ondine carried a basket the way other people carried weapons. "I checked the reliquary vault first. Figured if you'd survived, you'd be hiding somewhere with the valuable things."

"That's fair," Corvane said. His voice sounded strange to him. Rusty. Unrehearsed.

"It is." She set the basket down and looked at him — really looked, the way she used to when he was a young priest stealing honeycakes from her trays, before rank had built its wall between them. "You look terrible."

"I feel—" He considered. "Light. Which frightens me more than the alternative."

She nodded slowly, unsurprised, and handed him a heel of bread wrapped in cloth. "Eat. Then come down. There are people asking questions, and I've been putting them off by saying the High Priest was praying."

"I was," Corvane said. "For the first time in thirty years, I think it counted."

* * *

On the plains below the valley's rim, two armies stood in the cinders and did not fight.

It was the strangest morning any soldier among them had ever known. All night the lines had faced each other — Veyra's dragon-blooded host on the southern rise, their banners stitched with the old sigil, and the temple wardens drawn up on the north road in their grey lacquered armor — and the air had been thick with the particular silence before slaughter. Then the sky had caught fire.

Not burning. *Burning* they knew. This had been different: a river of gold passing overhead from east to west, slow as a procession, warm as a hearth seen through a window on a winter night. Every man and woman in both armies had felt it go over them, and every one of them had felt the same thing, though few would ever say it aloud: *it does not want us.*

Weapons do not stand down. Armies do not kneel. But that morning, on the plains of cinders, both happened anyway — not all at once, not in any order anyone commanded, but in ones and twos, like frost melting. A warden sergeant drove her sword into the earth and sat down beside it. A dragon-blooded captain with burn scars to his elbows took off his helmet and wept without shame. Men who had spent the night sharpening blades found themselves instead helping enemy soldiers dig enemy soldiers out of a collapsed trench, because after you have watched a god pass overhead and take no notice of your war, digging seems suddenly like the only honest work available.

By midmorning the truce was not agreed so much as discovered, already fully formed, the way you discover a bruise.

Veyra stood apart from it, on the southern rise, and did not kneel.

He was fifty-eight years old and had spent forty of those years preparing for a single day: the day the temples burned and the dragon-blooded took back what was theirs. He had drilled his host, courted his allies, buried two wives and a son in the cause, and worn his vengeance so long it had grown to fit him like skin. And now the thing he had wanted had happened — the spire was down, the wardens were standing down, the old order was cracking like river ice in spring — and Lord Veyra stood on his rise with his hand resting on the pommel of a sword he no longer knew how to use, feeling the terrible vertigo of a man whose ladder has been taken away.

"She refused me," he said.

Beside him, his marshal — a broad, patient woman named Ket — said nothing, because she had heard him say it four times already and understood it was not addressed to her.

"The scribe. Silas Sandoval. I offered her my protection, my armies, my name. A place at the right hand of the new age." He turned the words over like coins from a dead kingdom. "And she looked at me the way you look at a beggar who is robbing you politely, and she said, *the fire isn't yours either, my lord.*"

"Was she wrong?"

Veyra was quiet for a long time. Below them, a warden and a dragon-blooded soldier were arguing cheerfully about whose turn it was to carry water from the stream, and neither seemed inclined to concede.

"No," he said at last, and it cost him visibly, the way a tooth costs. "That's what galls. Forty years, Ket. I told myself I was the dragons' answer. Their justice. And the fire went over our heads this morning and didn't so much as pause." His jaw worked. "It chose a debt-bound scribe and a feral child. It chose *giving*. Everything I built was taking, dressed in my grandfather's colors."

Ket considered this. She was not a subtle woman, which he had always valued in her. "What will you do?"

"Do?" Veyra looked out over the plain, at the armies dissolving into crowds, at the smoke still rising off the spire, at the small figures moving through the wreckage with stretchers and water buckets. Something in his chest ached in a way he suspected would take years to name. "I suppose I'll learn to give something back. Gods help me. I haven't the faintest idea how."

"Start small," Ket said. "There's a warden over there who's about to steal our water cart."

Veyra watched the warden — a boy, really, sixteen at most, ash-faced and limping — drag the cart away with the transparent guilt of youth. And Lord Veyra, last hope of the dragon-blooded, terror of three kingdoms, sighed from the bottom of his soul and started down the hill to yell at a child about theft.

It was, he reflected, as good a beginning as any.

* * *

Maren woke to the smell of woodsmoke and someone humming very badly.

Her first coherent thought was that she was dead, and that the afterlife had made an error, because the humming was *awful* — tuneless, wandering, occasionally stopping to reconsider its choices — and surely the gods could have done better. Her second thought was that her ribs hurt in a way that meant she was emphatically not dead, because the dead, in her considerable professional experience with the recently deceased, complained far less.

She opened her eyes. Tent ceiling. Familiar tent ceiling. The field hospital the exiles had pitched beyond the plain, all canvas and rope and the smell of poultices.

"You snore," said Ember, sitting cross-legged beside her cot, humming again. The girl looked different. Maren groped for the difference and found it: she was sitting *up straight*, and her eyes were clear, and when she saw Maren watching her she smiled — a real smile, unguarded, the smile of a child who has recently learned she is allowed to have one. "Silas says you broke four ribs and a shoulder and that you're too stubborn to die, which she says is the only reason you're alive."

"Mm." Maren tried to prop herself up, thought better of it. "The temple cell. Corvane—"

"Gone. The fire took his stolen piece and most of his pride with it. He walked down out of the ruins yesterday and gave himself up to his own scribes. They don't know what to do with him." Ember's smile flickered into something older and sadder. "Silas says nobody ever knows what to do with a penitent. It's easier to forgive a wolf than a shepherd who says sorry."

"And Silas? Where's—"

The tent flap moved. Silas came in carrying a basin of water, moving carefully, and Maren felt the smile drain out of her face before she understood why.

Because Silas looked *old*.

Not tired-old, not grief-old. Old the way trees are old, the way stone is old — as if years had been poured out of her like water from a jug. Her hair, which had been brown shot with grey, was now white at the temples and thinning at the crown. Her hands, setting down the basin, trembled slightly, and there were new lines around her eyes that had not been carved by worry alone.

"Oh," Maren said. Stupidly. "Oh, Silas."

"Don't," Silas said, with a ghost of her old dry humor. "Everyone keeps doing the face. Ember does the face. The healers do the face. I'm considering charging for it."

"How much—"

"I don't know exactly. Enough." Silas settled onto the stool beside the cot, and her hand found Maren's uninjured one and held it, and her grip was still strong, which was somehow worse. "The fire needed years to rekindle. Freely given. So I gave mine." She shrugged, as if reporting on the weather. "I have some left. Months, perhaps. A season, if I'm disagreeable, which I intend to be."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Forty years of hedge-craft, of reading people the way she read weather, and she could find nothing in Silas's face but peace — a deep, settled, infuriating peace, the peace of a woman whose ledger had finally balanced.

"You could have let the girl die," Maren said quietly. "Or given half. Or found another way. There's always another way. I've sold enough false remedies to know people always believe there's another way."

"There was another way," Silas agreed. "Every other way ended with the fire owned by someone. Corvane, or Veyra, or the crown, or the temples reborn under a kinder name. The dragons gave their fire freely, and that's the only reason it was ever clean. Someone had to give it freely again." She squeezed Maren's hand. "Besides. You taught me haggling, Maren. I got a season out of it. In the market of eternity, I think I did rather well."

Ember made a noise that was half laugh and half sob, and pressed her face against the edge of the cot, and Maren put her good hand on the girl's head, and for a while none of them said anything, and the bad humming started up again, softer, almost a lullaby.

When Silas rose to go, Maren caught her wrist.

"Where now? Home?"

"Home," Silas said. "The valley. I want to see it breathe." She paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the afternoon light, and looked back with an expression Maren would keep for the rest of her long life. "And I want to be there when the ledgers burn. Every debt in that place, written in my mother's name and ten thousand names besides. I mean to watch every page."

* * *

Corvane knelt in the courtyard of the Temple of Quiet Hours, and the strangest part — the part he would try to explain, later, to whoever judged him, knowing full well no explanation would suffice — was how *small* it all was.

Nine hundred years of authority. Doctrines forged in secret, wars justified, a child caged, a scribe hunted, a god-king's crown half-built in his heart. And now he knelt on flagstones he had walked ten thousand times, before a row of scribes and kitchen sisters and two confused junior priests, and the whole vast architecture of his power fit inside a circle of ordinary people who mostly just wanted to know whether supper would still be served at the usual hour.

Sister Ondine stood at the front of the little tribunal, arms crossed, because apparently the temple's surviving hierarchy consisted of whoever had the strongest opinions, and Ondine's opinions had outlasted empires.

"You understand," she said, "that we can't absolve you. Whatever else happens, that's above our pay. There'll be inquiries. Kingdoms will send people. Some of them will want you hanged."

"Yes."

"And some of them will want you *pardoned*, which is worse, because they'll want to use you."

"Yes," Corvane said again. "I know. I invented that trick."

Ondine studied him for a long moment. Behind her, Brother Aldous whispered something to the elderly sister, who snorted. Even now, Corvane noted dimly, even here, the margins filled with small irreverent life. Perhaps that had been the temple's truest scripture all along, and he had simply never learned to read it.

"What do you want, Corvane?" Ondine asked, and it was the first time in twenty years anyone had asked him that question without wanting something back.

He looked at his burned hands. He looked at the gap in the eastern wall, through which the evening light was coming in gold and unhurried, touching the courtyard where a nineteen-year-old boy had once wept over holy oaths.

"I want to copy scriptures," he said. "The ordinary ones. The prayers at the front of the codex, the ones we made the debt-bonded copy until their eyes failed. I want to grind my own ink. And I want—" His voice caught, and he let it. "I want to write letters to every family the wardens touched. With my own hand. However many years that takes."

Someone in the crowd murmured. Ondine's expression did not change, but something behind it shifted, tectonic and slow.

"That's not penance," she said. "That's just work."

"Yes," said Corvane, and for the first time since the fire had passed over him, the lightness in his chest felt like something he could live inside. "Exactly that."

* * *

Far to the east, where the plains met the foothills, the fire was still traveling.

No one commanded it. No one followed it. It moved through the world like spring moving through a valley — arriving where it arrived, waking what it woke — and in the weeks that followed, hedge-witches in seven provinces reported their cold hearths warming on their own; a dried well near the border city ran sweet; and in the magic-dead valley itself, where no spell had lived for a thousand years, the first crocuses of the season came up through the snow a month early, glowing faintly gold at the roots, and no one could explain it, and no one tried very hard.

Unowned. Unasked. Given.

And on a road leading home, a white-templed scribe walked beside a child who talked constantly, joyfully, relentlessly, in plain and perfect Common, about everything — clouds, bread, the proper way to scold a mule — while behind them the mountains of the valley rose against the sunset, waiting to see what their first mage would make of them.

Silas stopped at the crest of the last hill, breathless sooner than she should have been, and looked down at the roofs of the town, the smoke of hearths, the distant grey bulk of the temple with its broken spire.

"You're crying," Ember observed.

"I'm copying a prayer," Silas said. "The oldest one I know. Finally true."
