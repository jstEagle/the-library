## 22. First Mage of the Valley

The spring came up through the old temple courtyard the way a secret does — quietly, and then all at once.

Ember found it on a morning in early thaw, when she had gone out to sweep ash from the flagstones because sweeping gave her hands something to do that wasn't grieving. The broom stopped mid-stroke. Between two cracked paving stones, where nothing had grown in a thousand years of forgetting, a thread of water rose silver and trembling, curled once in the cold air like it was checking whether anyone was watching, and spilled over into a pool no bigger than a washbasin.

She knelt. She put her fingers in it. It was warm.

Not fever-warm. Not fire-warm. Warm the way a hand is warm when someone takes it without being asked to.

"Oh," Ember said, out loud, to nobody, which was a habit Silas had given her along with everything else. "Oh, you *clever* old valley."

The water did not answer, but somewhere beneath her knees she felt the deep slow pulse she had come to know over the past year — the valley's heart remembering how to beat. Magic was returning here the way feeling returns to a limb that has been asleep too long: painfully, in pins and needles, one small spring at a time.

She sat back on her heels and let herself be happy about it for exactly as long as it took the sun to clear the eastern wall. Then she picked up the watering can, the shears, and the bundle of dried marigolds, and went to tend the grave.

* * *

Silas Sandoval's grave was not in the temple cemetery, because Ember had refused to allow it, and by now refusing things was something Ember did with a fluency that would have astonished everyone who had known the mud-caked child who arrived at Vespers four years ago. No. The grave stood on the hillside above the terraces, under the crooked rowan tree, facing west so that it caught every sunset the valley had to offer. Maren had said this was morbid. Maren said most things were morbid, in the tone of a woman who had spent enough years as a disgraced hedge-witch to consider morbidity a reasonable lifestyle choice.

"It's not morbid," Ember told her now, kneeling to pull the winter's dead grass from around the headstone. "It's practical. She hated mornings. This way she gets the good light."

"She's dead, love."

"That's when you'd want the good light *most*."

Maren snorted and settled onto the flat rock nearby, working at something with her hands — a knot, a charm, a half-finished mending of the world, it was always hard to tell which. Her hair had gone fully white over the winter, which she claimed was from the prison cell and Ember privately suspected was from teaching eleven village children basic herb-lore while pretending not to enjoy it. "Well. It's a fine spot. Better than the crypt Corvane offered, I'll grant you."

"Corvane can offer me the moon on a chain," Ember said, trimming the marigold stems, "and I'll still say no. Politely. I've gotten good at politely."

That much was true. A year ago, saying more than three words at a time felt like lifting stones; now she could argue, negotiate, joke, and — her newest and proudest skill — give speeches. Small ones. The village had asked her to speak at the spring festival, and she had managed a whole quarter hour without setting anything alight, which everyone agreed was growth.

She laid the marigolds against the stone. The letters there were ones she had cut herself, learning chisel-work badly and then adequately:

**SILAS SANDOVAL**
*Scribe. Sister. Giver.*

No dates. Ember had insisted on that too. "She wasn't a span of time," she'd explained to the stonemason, a patient man named Odo who charged her half price because he liked watching her talk. "She was a person. Put down what she was."

Below the words, small and almost hidden in the corner of the stone, someone — Maren, though she'd never admit it — had carved a tiny dragon sigil, wings folded, sleeping.

"Anything new?" Maren asked, nodding at the hillside below, where the terraces stepped down toward the valley floor.

"The third field woke up yesterday. Old Tam says his barley came in green overnight and he cried into his porridge." Ember grinned. "He also tried to pay me a chicken for it. I told him magic isn't for sale."

"What did you tell him it was for?"

"Nothing. That's the point." She said it lightly, but the truth underneath was heavy and warm as banked coals. That had been the whole lesson — the one paid for with borrowed years and burned ledgers and a war's worth of cinders on the plains. Fire freely given could not be owned. The moment anyone tried, it guttered. She had watched it happen. She had been the one it happened *to*, once, in a reliquary that smelled of dust and old prayers, before a tired scribe with ink-stained fingers knelt beside her cage and said *I think I almost understand you* — the first kind words anyone had spoken in Ember's language in a thousand years.

She touched the headstone, two fingers, the way Silas used to touch the covers of books she loved but couldn't afford to damage.

"First mage of the valley," Maren said, not quite teasing. "How does it sit?"

"Like a coat I'm still growing into." Ember considered. "Some days I want to hide under my bed. Some days I want to fix everything at once and I have to sit on my hands so I don't try. Mostly I just... listen. That's what she taught me. Before all the big stuff. Listen first."

"Mm." Maren tied off whatever she'd been knotting and tucked it away. "Your ten o'clock is coming up the path, by the way. Little Fen. He's been practicing."

Ember's heart did the thing it always did. "Is he ready?"

"He moved a leaf. On purpose. Mostly on purpose." Maren stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and paused at the top of the hill. Her voice went gentler than her face allowed. "You know she'd be insufferably proud of you. Quietly, in the margins of some ledger. But insufferable all the same."

"I know," Ember said. "I can hear it."

And she could — or something near enough. Not a ghost. The valley didn't do ghosts anymore; it did springs, and green fields, and children who could nudge leaves. But some mornings, when the light came through the rowan branches just so, Ember felt less like a girl visiting a grave and more like a student arriving at a lesson her teacher had left behind, written in a language older than kingdoms, that Ember alone could still read.

* * *

Fen arrived at a run, skidded on the wet grass, and saved himself only by grabbing the headstone.

"*Sorry!*" he yelped — to Ember, to Maren, and then, with the unerring instinct of an eight-year-old, to the headstone itself, bowing to it. "Sorry, Lady Sandoval."

"Scribe Sandoval," Ember corrected, hiding her smile. "She'd have corrected you herself, you know. She'd have said titles are how temples get ideas."

"What's a temple?"

Ember looked at Maren. Maren looked at the sky, wearing the expression of a woman declining to be responsible for history.

"A temple," Ember said, sitting down on the grass so they were eye to eye, "is a place where people kept important things locked up instead of sharing them. Come on. Show me your leaf."

Fen dug into his pocket and produced, carefully wrapped in a scrap of cloth, a single dry oak leaf. He set it on the flat rock with the reverence of a boy handling treasure, which, to him, it was.

"I did it twice yesterday," he whispered. "Once on purpose."

"On purpose is the part that counts." Ember cupped her hands loosely around the leaf but did not touch it. "Now. What did I tell you? Where does it start?"

"In the middle," Fen recited. "Not in the leaf. In the middle. You don't push it, you..." He frowned, hunting for the words. "You *ask* it?"

"You ask it. And then the important bit?"

"You wait."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"You wait anyway." He screwed up his face in concentration. Ember watched him reach — not with his hands, with whatever lived deeper than hands — and felt, faintly, like hearing a song hummed by someone who'd only heard it once, the small warmth of his gift flickering against hers. She added the tiniest breath of her own fire to the space between them, not pushing, just... keeping the cold off. Holding the door open.

The leaf trembled. Turned over. Rose a finger's width off the rock, spinning slowly, green-gold in the morning sun.

Fen made a sound like a kettle achieving its life's purpose.

"I DID IT — sorry — *sorry, Lady Sandoval* —"

"She doesn't mind," Ember said, laughing. "Catch it before it lands in the mud, though. Leaves hate that."

He caught it, clutched it to his chest, and looked up at her with the expression she had learned, over the past year, was the entire reason she existed now. Not power. Not destiny. Not the last living dragon-fire, bearer of the old war's memory, chosen one of prophecy and sigil. Just this: a child looking at her and seeing a door where before there had been a wall.

"Ember," he said, very seriously. "Can I do it again tomorrow?"

"You can do it every day for the rest of your life," she said. "That's rather the plan."

* * *

She stayed after Fen ran home to terrorize his family with levitating foliage. The afternoon softened. Clouds came up gold-edged over the western ridge, and the valley below began its evening ritual of chimney smoke and supper smells and someone, inevitably, playing a fiddle badly within earshot of everyone.

Ember talked to Silas, the way she did most days. Not prayers — Silas would have hated prayers, having copied ten thousand of them without ever feeling one land. Just talk.

"The archive is nearly catalogued," she reported. "We found another codex behind the false wall in the reliquary — yes, *that* reliquary, we turned it into a schoolroom, I thought you'd appreciate that. The codex has the original confession in it, the forgery, the whole rotten doctrine. Veyra wanted it. Wanted it *badly*. Said it would legitimize the exiles' claims forever." She pulled a weed. "I burned it."

She waited a beat, imagining the raised eyebrow.

"Not the information! Just the object. We copied it first, obviously, three copies, one goes to each court and one stays here where anyone can read it. Truth shouldn't be a relic, you taught me that. Or — well, you taught me it by dying for a book's worth of truth, which honestly was an extreme teaching method, and I've decided to learn it the efficient way instead."

The wind moved through the rowan. Somewhere downhill, the new spring chuckled over its stones.

"Veyra took it well, actually. Better than expected. I think watching the fire pass over the battlefield changed something in her — seeing it refuse both armies. She sends a letter every month now. Formal as a tax notice, but there's a postscript lately that's almost friendly. Last month it said, '*The plains are green again. I am told this is your doing. Do not let it go to your head.*' I've decided that means she misses us."

A cart rattled past on the road far below. Someone called a greeting to someone else. An ordinary evening, in a valley that had forgotten how to have them.

"I met a woman from the capital last week," Ember went on. "She'd heard rumors — a valley where magic grows like crops, a girl who teaches it free of charge. She asked me what I *am*. Mage? Priestess? Dragon-heir?" Ember shook her head at the memory. "I told her I'm a gardener. I don't think she believed me, but it's true. That's all any of this is. You plant. You wait. You keep the frost off. What comes up was never really yours."

The sun touched the ridge. Ember gathered her tools, then hesitated, as she did every evening, at the edge of the grave.

There was one thing she hadn't said yet today. One thing she saved, usually, for last, because it was the biggest and because saying it out loud had taken her nearly the full year to manage without her voice breaking.

"They're asking me to teach in the capital," she said. "A real school. For anyone. Every kingdom sending their children, even the courts that fought us — *especially* those, Veyra says, which is either wisdom or irony, I can't tell. And I keep thinking about a scribe in a quiet temple, copying prayers she couldn't feel, wondering if anything she wrote down mattered to anybody." Ember knelt and pressed her palm flat to the warm stone. "Everything you wrote mattered. Everything. I'm the proof. I'm the whole proof."

She stood. Shouldered her basket. Took one step toward the path, then turned back, because there was a last line, and it belonged here, at this hour, in this light.

Silas had copied it ten thousand times. The first prayer in the first ledger, in a young scribe's careful hand — the words she'd repeated until they wore grooves in her and never once came true, until the day she made them true with everything she had left.

Ember spoke it now in plain speech, the way Silas had finally earned the right to hear it:

"*May the light find a keeper who asks for nothing.*"

The spring laughed below. The rowan scattered gold across the grave. And the fire in Ember's chest — the last dragon-fire, freely given, freely kept — settled warm and quiet, like a hand taken without being asked to, and stayed.
