## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The waystation smelled of tallow, mouse, and other people's secrets, which was exactly what Xanthe had paid three copper bits for and exactly what they got.

It was a smuggler's cache, technically—a hollow scooped into the root-cellar of a ruined tollhouse where the old Thornfell road narrowed between two shoulders of stone. Smugglers called places like this *kneeholes*, because you had to stoop to enter and pray to leave. Xanthe had been living in one for eleven days, which was eleven days longer than any sensible person stayed anywhere, but invisibility was a discipline, and Xanthe had always been an overachiever.

They sat cross-legged on a pallet of moldy straw, eating cold turnip soup and rehearsing, for the hundredth time, the apology they would never deliver.

*I didn't mean to burn the ledgers. I didn't mean to read them, either. I especially didn't mean to understand them.*

Vessa would not care about the ledgers. Vessa would care that Xanthe had read the ledgers, and understood them, and asked questions with her eyebrows raised in that particular way that meant *mistress, your accounts do not balance, and I have noticed.* Eleven days ago Xanthe had packed a satchel, left the tower on the mountain's flank, and walked down into the valley with the specific intention of becoming no one.

It had been going wonderfully. Nobody had looked at them once.

Above the tollhouse's collapsed roof, the sky was doing its ordinary, dependable work. Stars in their usual constellations—the Shepherd, the Nine Sisters, the long pale river of the Wolf's Road that country folk said was the god's spilled milk. Xanthe knew them the way you know a ceiling: without looking, without thinking, without ever once being grateful.

The soup was bad. The straw itched. Xanthe was, for the first time in years, profoundly boring.

Then the Shepherd went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*—snuffed like a candle between wet fingers, and the Nine Sisters a breath later, and then the whole pale river of the Wolf's Road guttering away star by star, as though something enormous were walking the sky and drinking it.

Xanthe set down the soup bowl very carefully, the way you do when the alternative is dropping it.

"That's not weather," they told the mice. The mice, sensibly, had already left.

The darkness above was total in a way night had never managed. Night had always been a fabric with holes punched in it; this was the fabric itself, unbroken, pressing down. Xanthe's eyes watered trying to find a single point of light and found only their own reflection, faint and ghostly in the soup.

Then the mountain shuddered.

It was not an earthquake, though it moved like one. It was deeper, slower, wrong in the bones of it—an undulation that traveled *through* the stone rather than across it, the way a flank moves when a great body shifts in its sleep. Dust sifted from the tollhouse beams. Somewhere down the valley, dogs began to howl, dozens of them, all at the same pitch, all at the same moment, as if they'd agreed on it beforehand.

Xanthe knew—knew with the cold, specific certainty of someone who has read ledgers they shouldn't—that the border wards had just failed.

Vessa's wards. Vessa's *work*, the great lattice of keeping that had held Thornfell's borders for longer than Thornfell had held its name. Xanthe had carried kindling for those rites since they were nine years old. Had memorized the feeding-songs without ever being told what was being fed.

The mountain shuddered again, and this time it made a sound. Low, vast, and unmistakably a breath—drawn in through a throat the size of a valley, held, and let out slow.

"Oh," Xanthe said, to nobody, in a very small voice. "Oh, you're *asleep*. You're not a mountain range. You're asleep."

The realization did not bring terror, not at first. It brought something worse: the memory of every feeding-song, every rite, every ledger entry Xanthe had read with the balances gone wrong. *The keepers have stopped feeding it.* The thought arrived fully formed, wearing Vessa's handwriting.

And underneath it, arriving a half-second later like a debt collector: *if the wards are down, the wardens will be out, and the wardens will need someone to blame, and hedge-witches are right here.*

Xanthe had spent eleven days becoming no one. It had taken the sky about four minutes to make that impossible.

* * *

They heard the horses before the lantern-light found the road—shod hooves on old stone, too many, moving too fast for the hour. Xanthe snuffed their candle stub and pressed into the kneehole's deepest corner, behind a shelf of smugglers' false bricks, and did what they did best: made themselves small, quiet, and unremarkable.

The wardens came into the ruin above. Six of them, gray-cloaked, their lanterns hooded so the light fell only at their boots. Their voices carried down through the broken floor.

"—the tollhouse, then the mill. Warden-Superior says the hedge-folk knew it was coming. Says they *smelled* it coming."

"And if they didn't?"

"Does it matter? Somebody fed the dark, and it wasn't us." A dry laugh. "Witch-hunting's the only honest work left tonight."

Xanthe's heart performed a small, efficient act of treason against the rest of their body. *Hedge-folk.* Which meant anyone with a herb-dryer, a birthmark, or a suspicious quantity of chamomile. Which meant, in practical terms, Xanthe.

They were already cataloguing exits—the drain culvert, the badger run behind the east wall—when the screaming started.

It came from the road, high and short and cut off, followed by the ugly wet sounds of a fight going badly for one side. A warden shouted something about *hold her*, and then a lantern burst, and then a woman's voice—ragged, furious, and laughing, which Xanthe would think about later and find deeply alarming—said:

"Come on, then! I've got a *map* and a *grudge* and only the one knife!"

A body came through the floor.

Not gracefully. The rotted planking gave way all at once, and a stranger dropped into the kneehole in a shower of splinters and lantern-oil, landing badly, rolling, and fetching up against Xanthe's pallet with a grunt that suggested at least one rib had opinions now. She was slight, dark-haired, maybe thirty, with a gash along her forearm that was bleeding with real commitment, and she had a torn piece of leather clutched in her fist like a holy relic.

She looked at Xanthe. Xanthe looked at her. Above them, boots thundered across the ruined floor toward the hole.

"Are you a witch?" she demanded.

"I'm a *bookkeeper*," Xanthe said, which was true in the way that mattered least.

"Can you lie to wardens?"

"Extensively."

"Then you're hired." She shoved the leather fragment into Xanthe's hands. It was old, cracked, and inked with a line of mountains that were not mountains—a spine, a long sleeping curve of it—and at its end, a circle with a door drawn in the middle of it, and beneath the door, in a hand Xanthe knew better than their own: *the Hollow Door. Do not open it alone.*

Xanthe's stomach dropped through the floor the stranger had just come through.

"That's Vessa's writing," they said.

"Then you know her." The woman hauled herself up, hissing at her ribs. "Good. You can tell me why she's in my nightmares. Move—*move*—"

The first warden's boots came down through the broken planking, lantern-first, and the light swept the kneehole and found them both.

"Witch," the warden said, with the satisfaction of a man whose evening had just become simple.

"Actually," Xanthe said, rising with the leather map held up like a writ, "this is a keeper's warrant, and you're standing between me and my errand, which is a *hanging* offense, and I'd be happy to wait while you fetch someone who can read."

It was a magnificent lie. It was also, unfortunately, delivered while the stranger drove her knife into the warden's thigh, which rather undermined the legal tone of the proceedings.

What followed was less an escape than a series of increasingly undignified negotiations with geography: the drain culvert, which was narrower than advertised; the badger run, which was occupied and had *opinions*; and finally the goat track down into the black valley, where the two of them ran stumbling through a darkness without a single star, pursued by whistles and hooded lanterns, while beneath their feet—beneath everything—the great body of the world breathed in, and breathed out, and did not wake.

They ran until the whistles faded. They walked until the road became a smuggler's path and the path became a memory. And when they finally stopped in the lee of a fallen waymarker stone, the stranger slid down its face, pressed her bleeding arm against it, and laughed her alarming laugh at the starless sky.

"Odile," she said. "Since we're presumably going to die together. And you'd be the bookkeeper."

"Xanthe." They looked down at the leather in their hands, at Vessa's handwriting, at the door drawn in the belly of the sleeping curve. "And I don't think we're going to die. I think we're going to have to *answer* for something."

The waymarker stone was warm under Odile's blood. Warm, Xanthe noticed, the way a body is warm.

And carved into its face, so old the lichen had only just begun to eat it, was a line of script Xanthe had last seen in a ledger they were never supposed to read—a ledger whose accounts, eleven days ago, had stopped balancing.

The first entry, in Vessa's hand, dated forty years before Xanthe was born, read: *Road one, begun. She carries us; we must learn to walk softly.*

Xanthe looked up at the starless dark and understood, with terrible clarity, that their invisibility was over—that they had been known, and marked, and expected, for longer than they had been alive.

"We need to talk," they said to Odile, "about who your nightmares think I am."
