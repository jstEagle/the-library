## 2. Roads That Remember

The smuggler tunnels beneath Thornfell had a smell Xanthe associated with their entire childhood: wet stone, old tallow, and the faintly sweet rot of apples someone had hidden and forgotten. They had learned these roads at Vessa's knee, or so they'd believed. Now, walking them again at the head of eleven frightened refugees and one wounded stranger who bled on everything, Xanthe was beginning to suspect they'd only been shown the roads their mistress wanted them to know.

"Are we lost?" asked Brammel, the tanner's son, for the fourth time in an hour. He was nineteen, built like a beer barrel, and had cried exactly once since the stars went out—quietly, into his sleeve, where he clearly believed no one could see.

"We are not lost," Xanthe said. "We are taking the scenic route."

"There's nothing to see."

"Exactly. Very restful."

Odile limped along beside them, her bandaged arm cradled like a sleeping cat she wasn't sure she liked. She hadn't spoken much since the waystation. But she watched everything—the walls, the ceiling ribs of stone that did, if you let your eyes unfocus, look uncomfortably like the inside of something enormous—and she wrote. Whenever they stopped to rest, she sat apart with a little oilskin-wrapped journal and scratched away by candlelight as though her life depended on getting the facts down before the facts changed.

Xanthe tried not to read over her shoulder. Xanthe failed.

*Day three below,* Odile had written that morning. *The witch—Xanthe insists on no honorifics, which is either modesty or a legal precaution—walks these tunnels like other people walk their own kitchens. Either hedge-cunning runs deep here, or someone taught them. I intend to find out which.*

"Hedge-cunning," Xanthe muttered. "I'll have you know I trained under a mistress of the Marchetti line for nine years. I have *credentials*."

"You have a satchel of dried nettle and a strong opinion about mushrooms," Odile said, without looking up. "I stand by my notes."

"The mushrooms were *fine*."

"One of them screamed."

"It was a *puffball*. They do that."

It was the first joke either of them had made since the stars went out, and it landed so well that Brammel snorted tea through his nose, and for one warm moment the column of refugees behind them almost sounded like people instead of survivors. Then the tunnel turned, and the warmth died in Xanthe's chest, because the tunnel should not have turned there.

They had passed this marker an hour ago. Xanthe was nearly certain. And certainty about smuggler roads was the one professional vanity they had never surrendered.

The marker itself was a small brass disc nailed into the stone, green with age, stamped with a running-hare sigil that every cutpurse in Thornfell knew meant *safe passage, toll paid*. Except that under it, half-hidden by lichen, was a second mark: a thin line of script in that same old hand from the waystation stone. Vessa's hand. Forty years older than Xanthe.

*Road two, forked. She forgets herself when she sleeps; the paths must remember for her.*

"She," Xanthe whispered. "She who?"

"Your mistress," Odile said quietly. "Presumably. Unless you make a habit of carving scripture into mountains."

"I don't carve anything. I can't even keep a houseplant alive." Xanthe pressed their thumb against the script—an idle gesture, testing whether the letters were carved or painted—and the disc flared.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. Just a soft gold pulse, like a candle seen through skin, spreading out from under their thumb in a ring. And in the pulse's wake the tunnel *changed*: a hairline seam appeared in what had looked like solid wall, breathing open onto a stair that spiraled down into warm dark.

Brammel made a sound like a kettle giving up.

"That's blood-keyed," Xanthe said, staring at their own unbroken thumb. "That's old blood-work, keeper-grade, the kind they don't teach anymore because it eats the caster's—" They stopped. They counted backward. Nine years of apprenticeship, four years of errands into the hills, every drop scraped and stored and sworn away for "the work." *We must learn to walk softly.* "Oh," Xanthe said, very small. "She's been feeding me to the roads my whole life."

"Is that bad?" Brammel asked. "It sounds bad. It sounds like a bad thing."

"It's—" Xanthe began.

*Interlude found in Odile's journal, undated:* *The witch stood before the opened door for a long time. I have watched soldiers receive wounds with less expression. Whatever they loved is buried in these walls, and whatever they loved has been lying to them since before they could walk. I know something about loving liars. It does not get easier with practice.*

They took the stair. The stair led to a gallery, the gallery to a causeway that ran along the edge of a cavern so vast their candlelight simply gave up partway across, and somewhere far below, water moved with a rhythm too slow and too regular to be a river. Brammel said it sounded like breathing. Everyone politely declined to discuss it further.

On the third day, the wardens found them.

Xanthe smelled them first—or rather smelled the *absence* of them, a hole in the tunnel's living dark where six men with lanterns and iron-tipped staves should have announced themselves a mile off. Wardens wore hush-charms bought from the same hedge-folk they spent their careers hanging, which Xanthe had always considered the borderlands' most reliable joke. There was nothing funny about it now.

"Down," Xanthe hissed, and snuffed the candles, and eleven refugees folded themselves into the shadows of a wayshrine alcove while boots rang closer on the causeway.

The warden captain's voice carried beautifully in the cavern, the way confident cruelty always does. "Tracks end ahead. The witch is close. The Keeper's writ says bring the hedge-spawn alive—the god-starved take their power whole, and His Grace wants it *whole*, do you understand me? No cutting throats because they're inconvenient. We starved the beast forty years to fill our own cellars, and I will not have some village witch's scraps going to waste because Sergeant Hallow got enthusiastic."

A younger voice, uneasy: "Starved it, sir? The wards were to keep it sleeping safe—"

"The wards were a *manger*, boy. You think the tithe-grain goes to Thornfell's granaries? You think the mountain shakes because it's cold? Every harvest we've ever counted, we counted off its back, and every year we gave it back less, and now the keepers have stopped feeding it entirely because they mean to move on to richer country and leave the carcass to us. So yes. Alive. Move."

In the alcove, Xanthe felt Odile go rigid beside them—not with fear. With recognition. Xanthe looked at her, and she looked at the floor, and neither of them said a word, but Xanthe thought, with a dread that arrived fully grown like a tooth coming in sideways: *she knows that name. His Grace. She knows it the way you know your own father's name.*

Which was, presumably, because she did.

The wardens passed. The refugees breathed. And Xanthe sat in the dark doing arithmetic they did not want to do: forty years of roads, forty years of ledgers, forty years of a woman who had taught Xanthe that the wards were sacred, that the keepers were thieves, that the wolf-god was a story told to frighten children—all of it true, all of it lies, all of it braided together so tightly that pulling any single thread threatened to unravel the person Xanthe had been for twenty-six years.

Vessa had built these roads into the god's body. Vessa had keyed them to her apprentices' blood. Vessa had known the wards were a manger and had never, not once in nine years, said so.

Or—said so constantly, in the ledger Xanthe was never supposed to read, in a hand forty years steady, while telling everyone else the opposite.

*Which,* Xanthe thought, *is worse?*

*Interlude found in Odile's journal, water-damaged, edges burned:* *I came here to undo what my family did, and instead I am following a hedge-witch deeper into the corpse of the country my family helped kill. If the witch learns whose daughter I am before we reach the Door, they will leave me for the wardens, and I will deserve it. I have started leaving the journal where they might read it. That is either honesty or cowardice. I no longer know the difference. My father never taught me the difference. That is rather the whole problem, isn't it.*

On the fifth day, the road ended—not in wall, but in a door.

It stood free in the middle of the gallery, upright and impossible, a slab of black wood taller than two men, bound in hinges of pale bone-white metal that caught their candlelight and gave none of it back. No frame. No wall around it. Just a door standing in the dark like a sentence waiting for its verb, and carved across its lintel in Vessa's unmistakable hand:

*HOLLOW DOOR. KNOCK ONLY IF YOU MEAN IT.*

Beyond it, through seams that should have shown stone, showed instead a darkness that was not empty but *full*—a darkness with texture, with patience, with something enormous turning over in its sleep.

And leaning against the doorframe, arms crossed, exactly as though she'd been waiting five days and forty years simultaneously, stood a woman Xanthe would have known anywhere in the world.

"Mistress," Xanthe said. Their voice cracked on the second syllable.

"Hello, little moth," said Vessa. "You took the long road. Good. You needed to see what I built before I tell you what I broke."
