## 3. What Vessa Buried

The antechamber of the Hollow Door was not a room so much as a throat—a vast hollow of black stone that swallowed the light of their torches and gave back only a dim, greasy glow, as if the walls themselves were digesting it. Xanthe stood at the edge of it with Odile at their shoulder and forty-odd refugees huddled behind them in the last of the smuggler tunnel, breathing air that tasted of iron and old grief.

And there, at the center of the chamber, standing before a door that was not a door but a seam in the world—stood Vessa.

"Mistress," Xanthe said. The word came out cracked down the middle.

Vessa turned. She looked older than she had eleven days ago, when Xanthe had fled her tower with nothing but a satchel and a bruised sense of injustice. Her hair had gone grey at the temples. Her hands were stained to the wrist with something dark and shining—ward-ink, Xanthe realized. The same blood-keyed ink that marked every waymarker on the roads below.

"You brought them here," Vessa said. Not an accusation. Worse—an observation, calm as a physician's diagnosis. "All of them. I hoped you wouldn't."

"You *hoped*—" Xanthe's laugh came out wrong, too high. "You broke the wards. You let the stars go out. You let the wardens start burning hedge-circles from the Marches to the Salt Fen—and you *hoped* I wouldn't find you?"

"I hoped you'd be safe." Vessa's voice softened, and that was unbearable too. "You were always the best of my students, Xanthe. Too honest for your own good. That's why I never told you."

"Never told me *what?*"

Vessa lifted her stained hands, palms out, the gesture of a woman showing she held no weapon. "That this country is not a country. That Thornfell is not a mountain range. That everything you've ever loved is lying on its side in the dark, dreaming, and starving, and we are the reason."

* * *

Odile's journal — entry undated, ink smeared:

*Father always said the keepers fed the wolf out of mercy. That the tithe-towns existed so the beast would sleep easy. I believed him the way you believe the floor will hold: not thinking about it, just walking.*

*Then I found the ledgers. The feeding-songs have been getting shorter every century. Shorter, and thinner, and sweeter-worded. My grandmother's generation sang the god whole verses. My father sings it three lines and a blessing. The keepers aren't merciful. They're frugal. A sleeping god asks for less than a waking one, and a starving one asks for almost nothing at all.*

*I stole the map fragment because I wanted to see its face. I did not think about what it would want to do with its eyes once they opened.*

*Now the stars are gone and I am walking toward it with a witch who trusts me, and I have not yet found the courage to say whose daughter I am.*

*Perhaps tonight.*

*Perhaps not. Cowards live longer.*

* * *

"The land dies waking," Vessa said, later, by a fire that burned strangely blue and gave no heat. "I know. I've done the arithmetic a hundred ways. But hear the rest of it, Xanthe. Hear all of it before you hate me properly."

Xanthe sat across the fire with their arms around their knees, refusing to be comfortable, refusing to be comforted. "Go on, then."

"The keepers took the mountains from the wolf four hundred years ago. They didn't conquer it—they *anesthetized* it. Fed it just enough dream to stay under, and built their wards on its ribs like fences on a sleeping ox. Every border stone. Every ward-line. Every charm your mother hung over her door against ill-luck—they were all doses. Sedatives, stitched into the fabric of the land." Vessa poked the blue fire; it flinched. "And the keepers' rule has lasted four centuries because no one wakes up to object."

"So your answer was to withdraw the dose all at once."

"My answer was truth," Vessa said. "Truth is violent, yes. Truth kills. But a lie that lasts four hundred years kills slower, and it kills everyone, and it calls itself peace while it does it." She looked at Xanthen with those old steady eyes. "I thought you of all people would understand. You fled my tower because I lied to you about the ward-work. Small lie. Kind lie. And you could not bear it."

"This is different."

"It is the same thing, larger." Vessa spread her hands. "That's all scale is."

Behind them, in the mouth of the tunnel, one of the refugees began to scream.

* * *

They were going into the dark.

Not fleeing—*going*. Walking backward out of the firelight with expressions of perfect, terrible contentment, one after another, like sleepers turning over. Old Marta first, then the cooper's twin boys, then a young carter who had sung marching songs the whole road down. Xanthe grabbed the carter's sleeve and he simply smiled at them—the vacant, sweet smile of someone being called home—and stepped backward off the ledge into the black, and did not fall so much as *dissolve*, the darkness taking him the way water takes salt.

"They're being eaten," Odile whispered. "The god is eating them in its sleep."

"No." Vessa's voice was quiet and awful. "It's dreaming them. It dreams what it misses. Stars, mostly, now that the real ones are gone. And people—it has been alone a very long time." She did not look away from the dark. "By morning there will be fewer of us. By the night after, fewer still. Unless the Door opens."

"Then open it," Odile said, stepping forward, and her voice shook in a way Xanthe had never heard. "Open it and let them out. That's what it's for. That's why I brought the map."

Vessa turned and looked at Odile for a long moment, and Xanthe saw something pass between them—recognition, of a kind. "Keeper's blood," Vessa said softly. "I wondered. You smell of feeding-song."

Odile went white. Xanthe felt the ground tilt under them, though the ground hadn't moved.

"Your *journal*," Xanthe said slowly. "The entries you read aloud. 'My father says the keepers feed the wolf out of mercy.' I thought that was research. I thought you were a scholar."

"I am a keeper's daughter," Odile said. She stood very straight, the way people do when they've decided to stop running. "Serrine Odile Vasq, third daughter of First Warden Vasq. I stole the map because I needed to know if the ledgers lied. They didn't. Nothing lies like a family, and mine has been lying for four hundred years." Her chin trembled and she held it anyway. "I didn't tell you because you were kind to me, and I couldn't bear to watch the kindness die."

"Well," said Xanthe, and heard their own voice go strange and small. "It seems everyone in this room buried something."

"Then dig," said Vessa.

* * *

Odile's journal — final entry, written fast, by torchlight:

*Xanthe has gone to stand in front of the Door. Mistress Vessa says it opens only two ways: outward onto escape, which feeds the keepers' lie forever and leaves the god to starve behind us; or inward, onto sacrifice, which Vessa intends to make herself—her life, freely given, to wake the god clean.*

*But I watched Xanthe's face when the carter dissolved into the dark. That wasn't a face planning either option. That was a face looking for a third door.*

*There is no third door. There never is. Except—*

*I write this and my hand shakes. Except that everything Vessa has taught, everything the keepers have hoarded, comes back to one line: the god is not fed by blood or song or sacrifice. It is fed by truth freely given. We assumed that meant a death.*

*What if it meant a confession?*

*What if the Door doesn't want a body? What if it wants the thing none of us has been willing to say out loud for four hundred years?*

*Xanthe has picked up the hinge-stone shard. They're looking at their own palm.*

*Mistress, forgive me—I think I'm about to watch your student surpass you.*

* * *

Xanthe stood before the Hollow Door with blood already welling along their palm where the hinge-stone's edge had kissed it, and the seam in the world breathed against their skin—warm, vast, patient, hungry in the way of things that have waited too long to be told anything true.

"Vessa," they said, without turning. "If I do this—if I choose wrong—"

"Then we all dissolve into the dark dreaming of stars," Vessa said gently, "and it will have been my fault twice over. I can carry that. Can you?"

Xanthe pressed their bleeding palm flat against the hinge-stone.

The Door shuddered. And through the seam—not outward, not inward, but *through*, thin as dawn under a locked door—came the first pale suggestion of starlight any of them had seen in eleven days.

Behind Xanthe, the darkness of the antechamber went utterly, listeningly still.

"Oh," whispered Vessa. "Oh, you clever, terrible child. It isn't choosing. It's *asking.*"

The Door began, very softly, to open.
