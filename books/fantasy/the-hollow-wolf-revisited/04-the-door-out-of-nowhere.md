## 4. The Door Out of Nowhere

The antechamber smelled of cold iron and old prayers.

Xanthe stood at the threshold of the Hollow Door with their palm still stinging from where the waymarker blood had burned it on the road, and they looked—at last, truly looked—at what Vessa had built her whole life around. The Door was not a door. It was a hinge-stone the size of a chapel bell, set into the wolf-god's ribs like a splinter, its surface veined with channels that had once run silver and now ran dark as a dead man's veins.

Beyond it: nothing. The dark that had eaten the stars pressed against the stone like a tongue against a tooth.

"You can still walk away," Vessa said behind them. She sounded tired in a way Xanthe had never heard—not the tiredness of long roads, but of long arguments with oneself. "The Door opens onto starlight for anyone who gives it truth freely given. You could give it a small truth. That you were afraid. It would let you through."

"And everyone else?"

"Everyone else is the land's problem now." Vessa's voice hardened into the old lecture-tone, the one that had made Xanthe feel safe for eleven years and made them feel like a child for the first ten minutes of tonight. "I broke the wards to wake it. Let me finish what I started. My blood opened this place; my blood can feed it."

"That's not finishing," Xanthe said. "That's just another keeper. You've spent thirty years hating the keepers for deciding who gets fed and who gets eaten, and your plan is *you* deciding. On behalf of all of us. Again."

Vessa flinched. It was a small thing, a twitch at the corner of her mouth, but Xanthe had spent eleven years learning to read her face the way smugglers read weather, and they saw it land.

Behind them, Odile spoke up from where she sat binding the last of the refugees' wounds—her keeper's hands, gentle now, doing keeper's work in the wrong direction. "For what it's worth," she said, "my mother's order wrote down every law about feeding the wolf. There are nine hundred and forty pages. Not one of them asks the wolf anything."

"Odile," Vessa said warningly.

"No, I think she's right," said Bramble, the tanner's boy, who had been silent since the ambush except to ask twice whether wolves could be reasoned with and once whether they liked sausages. He was holding his sausage. Just in case, he'd explained.

Xanthe laughed. It came out ragged and surprised even them, but it loosened something in their chest, and they found they could breathe again.

* * *

The dark outside the antechamber chose that moment to try the walls.

It did not knock. It simply arrived at the edges of the room like water finding the low places, and three of the refugees nearest the far wall simply—stopped being there. No scream. No struggle. Just absence, and the terrible sound of Bramble's sausage dropping on the stones.

"The hinge-stone!" Vessa was already moving, knife out, toward the vein-channels. "Blood buys time. Mine will buy you all an hour—go through whatever way opens—"

"No," Xanthe said.

They didn't shout it. That was the strange part, the part they would remember afterward. They said it quietly, the way Vessa had taught them to say true things: plainly, without decoration, letting the words carry their own weight.

Vessa stopped. Knife poised over the channel that would take her blood.

"I know why you did it," Xanthe said. "I know you loved this land more than the keepers ever did, and I know you couldn't watch them starve a god for power one more winter. But you're doing it again, Vessa. The secret. The sacrifice nobody voted on. You broke the wards in the dark and you're going to bleed out in the dark and none of us get a word in any part of it." They held up their wounded palm. The cut from the waymarker had never quite closed; the blood-keyed paths recognized it, hummed faintly against their skin like a struck tuning fork. "You built these roads keyed to my blood. Did you ever wonder why? Because you knew, somewhere under everything, that someday someone would have to tell the truth out loud instead of burying it. And you hoped it wouldn't be you."

Vessa's knife hand trembled.

"So here's my word in it," Xanthe said, and walked to the hinge-stone.

* * *

Up close, the Door was warm. That undid them a little—that after everything, the god's body still ran warm as a sleeping dog, waiting to be woken gently rather than kicked.

The keepers' law, Odile had read aloud once from her mother's stolen papers, held that the Door answered only to blood and obedience: feed it a sacrifice, prove your dominion, pass through as master. Vessa's counter-law, scrawled in the margins of her own journals, held that it answered only to blood and surrender: feed it yourself, prove your devotion, be devoured gladly.

Both laws agreed on one thing. Someone pays alone.

Xanthe pressed their bleeding palm flat against the hinge-stone and thought: *what if no one pays alone?*

"Here's the truth," they said, to the stone, to the dark, to the wolf underneath everything, to Vessa standing frozen with her useless knife, to Bramble clutching nothing now where his sausage used to be. "We hid you. We built our little country on your back and called it ours and fed you scraps and lies and told ourselves the wards were kindness. That's the truth. Freely given. Take it—it's yours, it always was."

And then, because invisibility was over anyway, because terror had burned off in the walking and left something stubborn behind:

"We're sorry. And we'd like to stay. All of us. If you'll have us."

The hinge-stone drank the blood from their palm like rain into drought-cracked earth.

Then the channels lit—not silver, not the keepers' obedient glow, but a color Xanthe had no name for until they saw it reflected in Odile's tear-wet face and understood: starlight. The color of the sky before the night the stars went out. The Door split open along seams no one had known were there, and beyond it was not escape, not darkness, not the devouring void—

—it was sky. Actual sky, vast and salted with stars, hanging impossibly on the other side of a stone door inside a mountain inside a god. The wolf-god stirred beneath them, a shiver that traveled up through the floor and out through the whole Thornfell range, and above, in the impossible sky, the stars began coming back one by one, like lamps relit by a careful housekeeper working down a very long corridor.

The refugees wept. Bramble offered his second emergency sausage to the Door, which declined politely by remaining a door.

Only Vessa stood apart, and Xanthe turned to her with the words ready—the reconciliation, the *come with us*, the eleven years of love arranged into forgiveness—

But Vessa was looking past Xanthe, at the Door, at the light spilling around its frame, and her expression was the one Xanthe knew best of all: the look she wore when a spell worked exactly as intended and she hated herself for it.

"It was never going to open for me," she said softly. "I knew that when I built the roads. Blood keys the locks, but truth turns them. And everything I've done for thirty years has been true except the part that mattered." Her eyes found Xanthe's. "You were always the better witch, little shadow. I just never had the courage to say so while you were still mine to lose."

* * *

The passage stayed open three days.

Long enough for the refugees to cross into the starlit country beyond—Bramble first, saluting the Door solemnly; Odile last, pressing her mother's nine-hundred-and-forty-page book of laws into the hinge-stone's channel, where it dissolved into light like sugar into tea, which everyone agreed was deeply satisfying to watch.

Not long enough for Vessa. When Xanthe went to find her on the third morning, she was gone, leaving only her journal open on the antechamber floor to a page Xanthe had never seen, in handwriting gone soft with age:

*If the wolf wakes fed by truth, the Door will need a keeper who tells it. Not a feeder. A talker. Someone who sits with it in the dark and says honest things until the honesty becomes a habit.*

*I'm sorry it has to be you. I'm not sorry it's you.*

The Door closed at dawn on the fourth day, gently, the way a hand closes around something precious, and Xanthe Marchetti felt it seal—not shut, but *seal*, the difference between a cage and a vow—and knew they were on the wrong side of it forever.

Wrong side. They tested the phrase, turning it over like a coin. The refugees were out under returned stars. The keepers' law lay dissolved in light. Vessa's confession sat warm in their chest alongside the grief of her.

On the right side of the Door, then, if you asked the Door. Only the geography disagreed.

Xanthe sat down against the warm stone, unrolled Vessa's journal to a fresh page, and began, out loud, in the dark, to a god learning how to listen:

"Well. Where should we start?"

Far below, deep in the mountain's dreaming muscle, something enormous wagged—there was no other word for it—and the first star of the new night bloomed directly overhead, bright as an answer.
