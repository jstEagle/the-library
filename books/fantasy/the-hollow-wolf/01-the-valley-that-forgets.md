## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley had no name, which Juniper Sandoval considered one of its better qualities. Names invited questions, and questions invited travelers, and travelers were the one thing the valley could not abide.

Not that it said so out loud. The valley didn't say anything out loud. Nothing in the valley did — that was rather the point of it.

He rose before dawn the way every keeper before him had risen, in a cottage built by hands four generations dead, and he lit no lamp because there was nothing to light it for. The walk from the cottage to the gate took eleven minutes if he dawdled, seven if the wind was up and pushing at his back like an impatient grandmother. He always dawdled. It was the only conversation available on some days.

"Morning," he told the birches, who said nothing back. "Morning, frost." Same answer.

The gate stood at the valley's far end where two ridges met like cupped palms, and it was — there was no honest way around this — a door. Not a wall with a door in it. A door, standing alone, framed in black stone older than any kingdom Juniper could name, set into nothing at all. Behind it: solid mountain face, unbroken rock, as though the world had grown over whatever passage once existed and left the frame behind like a scar. Before it: the valley, green and quiet and stubbornly ordinary.

His grandfather's grandfather had carved the rules into the lintel, and time had worn them down to suggestions:

*Keep it shut.*
*Keep the rounds.*
*Tell no one what you tend.*

Three rules. Simple enough. Juniper had recited them since before he could read them, kneeling on this same cold stone while his mother pressed his palm against the door and said, *Feel that? Nothing. You'll feel nothing your whole life, and that nothing is your life's work.* She'd been right about the first part. He was still waiting on the second.

He put his hand flat against the wood.

Nothing. As always. The door was old oak gone silver-grey, iron-banded, and utterly, perfectly inert. No hum, no warmth, no whisper of anything on the other side. Most mornings that was a comfort. This morning, for reasons he couldn't have named if you'd held a knife to him, it felt like pressing his ear to a shell and hearing only his own blood.

"Still shut," he reported anyway. "You're welcome."

* * *

The valley forgot things. That was the other thing worth knowing about it, the thing the keepers' journal described in careful, apologetic handwriting: *the valley sheds what is not natural to it, gently, the way a tree sheds leaves.*

Magic slid off it like rain off waxed canvas. Juniper had tested this exactly once, at nineteen, when a peddler had wandered in selling charms — little knotted cords that glowed warm and promised luck, love, safe roads. In the lowlands they worked; the peddler swore it. Inside the valley they were just string. By the third day the man couldn't remember why he'd thought otherwise, and by the fifth he'd traded his entire stock to Juniper for bread and salt and walked out whistling, having forgotten he'd ever owned anything remarkable at all. Juniper still had the cords in a box under his bed. Dead string. He kept them anyway.

The forgetting didn't stop at magic. It reached further, softer. Travelers came through the high pass now and then — lost shepherds, a surveyor once, a pair of pilgrims hunting a shrine that had never existed — and they stayed a night or two, ate his stew, complimented his bread. And when they left, their eyes went soft around the edges. They remembered *a* valley. They never quite remembered *this* valley. One pilgrim had wept at the ridge without knowing why, and Juniper had stood beside her and felt, absurdly, that he ought to apologize.

Only he stayed sharp. Only the keeper. Whatever ran in the Sandoval blood — and the journal called it simply *the tending gift*, as if naming it might cost extra — it kept his memory iron-filing-to-a-magnet true while everything around him blurred. Which meant he alone remembered every traveler's face. He alone carried the valley's small history, and would carry it until he died, and then presumably hand it to someone, except there was no someone. There had been no someone for thirty-one years.

He did the rounds anyway. Morning round: check the hinges, sweep the threshold stone, log the date in the keeper's book in his own cramped hand — *Gate sealed. No change.* Evening round: the same, plus oil for the hinges he was not strictly permitted to use but used anyway, because a squeaking door offended him personally even if no one would ever hear it squeak.

"No change," he told the door that evening, closing the book. "Big news day. Try to contain yourself."

The door, being a door, contained itself.

* * *

Here is what the journal did not say, what his mother had not said, what Juniper had spent forty years refusing to think in plain words:

A cage implies a prisoner.

He knew the rule — keep it shut — and he knew the shape of the rule, the way it curled inward instead of outward. Doors keep things out. But this door faced the wrong way in his dreams. In them he stood not before it but beyond it, looking back through the frame at a country he had never seen, all towers and terraced hills and banners the color of embers, and something on the far side of the dream was knocking.

He woke from those dreams hungry in a way food didn't fix.

Tonight he sat on the threshold after the evening round, which broke roughly six lesser rules about posture and propriety, and watched the light die over the ridges. The valley lay gold-then-violet below him, his cottage a smudge of smoke, the garden rows straight as stitches. It was, by any fair measure, a good life. Warm bread. Dry roof. Work that mattered — he believed it mattered, he had to believe it mattered — and silence enough to hear himself think.

And underneath the contentment, patient as groundwater, an ache he had never once managed to name. It wasn't loneliness exactly, though loneliness lived in it. It was more like the feeling of a word sitting on the tip of the tongue for forty years. Like being the last speaker of a language and forgetting he'd ever known it.

"Forty years," he said to the door. "You could've hummed once. Just once. I'd have taken a hum."

Wind moved through the birches. Far down the slope, his cottage windows caught the last light.

And then the wind stopped — not faded, *stopped*, mid-breath, the way a clock stops — and the silence that followed had weight to it, texture, a held-inhale quality that Juniper had never heard in this valley because this valley did not hold its breath, this valley shed everything, gently, like a tree shedding leaves —

The door hummed.

Low. Faint. A single note under the iron bands, felt in his palm before his ears, rising like a struck bell deciding whether to ring.

Juniper did not move. His heart was moving enough for both of them.

Forty years of nothing, and now this. He pressed his hand harder against the silver-grey wood, half convinced his mind had finally gone soft from talking to doors, and the hum deepened — answered him? — and beneath it, so faint he'd deny it later, he could have sworn he heard something else.

Small footsteps. On the far side. Or the near side. Or somewhere between.

Down the slope, past the darkening garden, a light appeared in his cottage window that he had not lit — a small orange glow, ember-colored, flickering where no fire burned.

Juniper got to his feet slowly, the keeper's book forgotten on the stone, three rules dissolving into static behind his eyes.

Someone was in his house. And for the first time in living memory, the door at the end of the world had something to say.
