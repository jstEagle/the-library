## 3. The Knight Without a Country

The girl had been in his cottage for nine days, and the door had started humming at night.

Juniper noticed it first as a feeling rather than a sound — a low vibration in his back teeth when he lay awake listening to Ash's breathing from the loft. By the third night he could hear it plainly: the gate, half a mile down the valley path, singing to itself like a kettle deciding whether to boil.

He'd checked it twice. Walked down in the dark with his lamp and put his palm flat against the iron-banded oak, the way six generations of keepers had before him, and felt the hum climb up through his arm into his chest. Not angry. Not straining. *Listening.*

"You hear it too," he told the door, because talking to doors was apparently what he did now. "I know."

Ash heard everything, of course. She sat on his worktable swinging her feet while he mended the hinge of the smoke-shutter, watching him with those gray eyes that were old in a way eight-year-old eyes shouldn't be. She held her ember cupped in both hands like a bird she was keeping warm. It never cooled. He'd checked that too, once, with tongs and a bucket of creek water. The water had come away steaming; the ember hadn't changed color.

She said something in Vaelish — three soft syllables, rising at the end.

"I don't know what that means yet," Juniper said. They were building the language between them, a few words a day. *Ember* was *sul*. *Door* was *dor*, which made sense, which felt like a small kindness from the universe. Her name for herself was Ash only because he'd given it to her; her true name was longer and had a sound in it like wind moving through a hollow reed.

She pointed toward the gate. Then she pressed her hand flat over her own sternum, and said the word again.

"Someone's coming?" he guessed.

She nodded. Then, after a moment, she touched her collarbone where a chain would sit if she wore one, and made a shape with her hands — two hands closing around empty air, gripping something invisible.

*A sword,* he thought, and the cold went down his spine like well water.

* * *

Serah Vael-Ilyen had been walking for eleven days since the borderlands stopped being real.

That was the only way she could describe it. The country of Vael did not end; it *folded*. She would crest a hill expecting the plains of her childhood and find the same hill again, or a street she'd marched down as a cadet, or a field of ash where a wedding had been the day before — the guests still there, still laughing, turning slowly to look at her with eyes like smoked glass before forgetting her and returning to their feast.

The dead walked their errands. The living had mostly forgotten they were dead. And somewhere ahead of her, always one fold out of reach, an ember moved through the hollow like a second dawn.

She had sworn an oath on her sword-hilt the night the palace burned: *the child lives.* Not the queen — the queen was already gone. Not the country — the country was a wound pretending to be a place. Just the child. Serah had lost everything else to the Wardens' clean white fire and their tidy doctrine of mercy, and she had decided, with the particular stubbornness of the exiled, that she would not lose this.

On the twelfth day, the folds began to thin. The hills grew honest. Grass appeared that bent in wind that came from somewhere. And then, at the top of a ridge she did not recognize because it had never existed inside the hollow, she saw a valley so green it looked painted, and at its far end a door set into the mountainside, and past the door — impossibly, gloriously — trees without ash on them.

She also saw the four figures in gray following her trail along the fold-line below, moving with the patient economy of hounds who know the quarry is tired.

Serah ran.

* * *

Juniper heard the dog before he saw anything else — no, not a dog. A bell. A small cracked bell ringing somewhere up the valley path in the blue hour before full dark, and then Ash was off the loft ladder and across the room faster than he'd ever seen her move, and she wrenched open his front door and stood in the frame with the ember blazing in her fist like a held breath.

"Ash—" He caught her shoulder. "Inside. We don't—"

The woman came out of the tree line at a dead run.

Even at distance, even limping, Juniper could read her the way you read weather. Armor under a travel cloak, scorched at the hem. Sword drawn but low. Blood — hers, most likely — darkening the left side of her ribs. Short silver-gray hair plastered to her skull with sweat. And behind her, up the slope, four shapes in gray livery moving without hurry, which frightened him more than running would have.

She saw the cottage and corrected course toward it. She saw Ash and something broke open in her face — relief so violent it looked like grief — and then she saw Juniper standing in the doorway with his hand on a little girl's shoulder, and her expression shuttered so fast he actually felt the draught of it.

"Vaelish?" she said, hoarse, stopping ten paces out. Then, in trade-tongue, accented but precise: "Please. I need shelter. One night. Maybe less." Her knees were doing something unsteady that her pride was arguing with. "They won't cross your boundary line. They can't. But they'll wait."

"Who's *they*?"

"Wardens." She spat the word like a seed. "Gray cloaks. You've seen them? Followed me?"

"No one follows anyone in this valley," Juniper said. "Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out. That's—" He stopped. That was the whole of his life, actually, and he had never once said it aloud to another living person.

The knight's eyes narrowed. She looked at the valley walls, at the green, at the door far off humming audibly now in the dusk, and Juniper watched her arrive at some conclusion that made her go very still.

"You're the keeper," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the keeper."

"And she—" the knight's gaze cut to Ash, and all the steel went out of her voice at once, leaving something younger underneath. "Ash. *Sul-mira.* Look at you. Look at you, little one."

What happened next undid Juniper completely. Ash let go of his shoulder, crossed the ten paces at a toddling run despite the hour, and hit the wounded knight like a wave hitting a seawall. The knight dropped to one knee — a mistake, judging by the way her face went white — and wrapped the girl up with both arms and the sword-hand too, awkward, careful, burying her face in the small shoulder. She was saying things in Vaelish, rapid and broken, and Ash was answering, and neither of them seemed to remember Juniper existed.

He stood in his own doorway, on his own land, in the last light, feeling like a stranger at a funeral for a family he'd never had.

Then the knight looked up at him over Ash's head. Her eyes were pale gray-green, the exact color of the valley after rain, and there was blood on her mouth where she'd bitten her lip holding in a sound, and she said, in trade-tongue:

"She trusts you. That is the strangest thing I have seen in two years, keeper, and I have seen streets that repeat themselves." A pause. "Will you trust me?"

Every rule he had ever been taught said no. Six generations of keepers whispered it in his blood. *The door stays shut. The valley stays empty. You keep what you were given and you pass it on.*

Ash looked back at him over the knight's shoulder, and the ember in her fist lit them both gold, and she smiled.

"One night," Juniper said. "And you're bleeding on my floor, so we're going to deal with that first."

* * *

She introduced herself as Serah while he stitched her — sitting on his bench, shirt peeled to the waist with a pragmatism he found himself admiring slightly more than was professionally necessary, refusing the willow bark tea on the grounds that she needed a clear head.

"Exiled?" he asked, tying off. The wound was a slash, not a stab; someone had wanted her slowed, not dead.

"Condemned." She flexed her arm, testing. "Treason. I stole the queen's daughter out of a burning nursery instead of standing at my post."

"That doesn't sound like treason."

"It wasn't. That was the problem." She took the shirt back from him and didn't put it on immediately, and Juniper discovered a sudden fascination with repacking his needle case. "You stitched this well. For a man who talks to doors."

Heat climbed his neck. "You heard that?"

"The whole valley heard that, probably. You talk to the gate like it's an old horse." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. A place where a smile had been trained out and kept trying to grow back. "Does it answer?"

"It hums now," he admitted. "It hasn't hummed since before my grandfather. Since she came." He nodded at the loft, where Ash was asleep with the ember tucked against her like a coal in a banked fire, glowing faintly through the floorboards.

Serah went quiet. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone careful. "Keeper. There are things you don't know about that door. Things the keepers were never told, or weren't allowed to remember. About what side of it you're standing on."

"Tell me tomorrow," Juniper said. "You're swaying where you sit, and I don't argue with people until they've slept."

For a moment he thought she'd refuse — he could see the argument assembling behind her eyes, the exile's habit of trusting no roof, no host, no morning. Then Ash stirred upstairs and murmured a word in her sleep, and whatever armor Serah had rebuilt came down again all at once.

"Tomorrow," she agreed.

He gave her his bed and took the bench by the hearth, and lay there listening to two strangers breathing in his house for the first time in his life, and found that the ache he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton had gone quiet.

Near midnight, a sound woke him: the bell-crack of metal on stone, far off, down the valley.

He was at the window before he knew he'd moved. In the starlight, at the tree line, four gray shapes stood in a perfect line, facing the cottage. Not advancing. Waiting. And the tallest of them raised one arm, slow as a clock hand, and pointed — not at the cottage, not at the sleeping knight.

At the gate.

Behind him, Serah's voice came out of the dark, wide awake, flat and cold: "Keeper. Get your sword. They're done waiting."
