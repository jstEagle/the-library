## 4. What the Gate Keeps In

Serah Vael-Sarn had crossed the borderlands with a cracked rib and a fever, so she supposed she could be forgiven for sleeping through her first full day in the keeper's cottage. What she could not forgive was waking to find her sword leaning against a wall across the room, out of reach of the bed, and a stranger sitting in a chair beside it, watching her with the patient expression of a man who had been there for hours.

"You moved my blade," she said. Her voice came out as gravel.

"I moved it away from your hand." The keeper — Juniper, Ash had called him, in that old tongue Serah hadn't heard spoken aloud since she was a girl — did not apologize for it. "You were burning up. You kept reaching for it in your sleep. Twice you nearly cut yourself."

"Twice I nearly cut *you*, more likely."

"Once, maybe." He handed her a clay cup. Steam rose off it, carrying the smell of some bitter root tea. "Drink. You'll want your voice back. Something tells me you're going to use it."

She drank because her body overruled her pride, and the tea scalded down through her chest and settled warm where the fever had been rattling. Outside the single window, the valley lay green and utterly silent. No birdsong with any magic in it. No hum in the stones. She had felt it the moment she'd crossed the tree line — that flatness, that absence, like walking into a room where music had just stopped.

"This valley," she said. "It's dead ground. Null. How long has it been like this?"

"As long as there's been a gate." He said it easily, the way a man recites his own name. "Magic can't enter here. That's the whole point of the warding. My family kept it sealed so nothing from outside could cross in and corrupt what's inside."

*What's inside.* Serah set the cup down very carefully on the blanket, because if her hands were holding something when she started laughing, she might have crushed it.

"Keeper," she said. "Juniper. What exactly do you think is inside this valley?"

"The valley. Cottages, orchards, the river, my— " He stopped. Something flickered behind his eyes, the first crack in all that placid certainty. "The village is three miles east. There are perhaps two hundred souls."

"There are four hundred thousand," Serah said. "Give or take the ones who've already gone gray."

The silence that followed had weight. Juniper sat very still in his chair, and she watched him do the thing she'd seen done by soldiers receiving orders they couldn't survive: he held his face together while everything behind it came apart.

"That isn't possible," he said finally. "I've walked this valley my whole life. I know every farmstead. I know—"

"You know what the gate shows you." She pushed herself upright against the headboard, and her rib screamed, and she ignored it. "Ash. Come here, little ember."

Ash had been sitting on the floor by the hearth, drawing shapes in the ash — of course in the ash; the child had a sense of humor like a knife between ribs — and now she climbed onto the bed without being asked and curled against Serah's side. In her cupped palms she held the ember she carried everywhere, the one that had not cooled since before Serah met her, the one that burned without fuel, without smoke, without end.

Almost without end.

Because Serah had watched it for weeks now, and she could see what the child would not say aloud: it was smaller than it had been in the spring. Dimmer. A candle an hour from guttering.

"This," Serah said, "is the last dragon-fire in Vael. Maybe the last in existence. And Vael—" her voice caught, and she made herself push through it, because this man deserved the whole ugly truth and nothing softened around the edges, "—Vael is *here*. Under us. Around us. This whole valley is a fold, a pocket, a cell. Your ancestors didn't build that gate to keep magic out of paradise, Juniper. They built it to keep a country *in*."

He stared at her. Then at the window, at the green hills he'd walked every day of his life. Then at the door beyond the wall — the great sealed gate he tended like a shrine.

"No," he said. It wasn't argument. It was reflex. "No. I've been outside the valley. Once, when I was young, to the pass. There was nothing there but mountains and—"

"And what did you see when you looked back?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Serah watched him try to remember and watched him fail, watched the memory slide out from under him like a fish in dark water.

"You saw fog," she said gently. "You always see fog. The gate doesn't let its keeper look at the bars of his own cage."

* * *

He didn't speak for a long time after that. He went outside, and Serah let him go, because she knew — oh, she knew — what it was to have your country turn out to be a lie with walls around it.

Ash stayed pressed against her. Up close, in daylight, the girl looked older than eight. Not in her face — in her stillness. Children who had fled cities that no longer existed learned to sit quietly.

"You told him," Ash said. Not a question. She said it in Vaeldori, the dead tongue, and Serah answered in kind because some things should only exist in one language.

"He needed telling."

"He'll hate it."

"Yes."

"He'll hate *us*."

Serah looked down at the small face, at the soot-gray eyes that had seen the Wardens burn her grandmother's house and had stopped crying somewhere after that. "Maybe," she said honestly, because lying to this child was like lying to the fire she carried. "Or maybe he'll hate the ones who built the cage. Those aren't the same people, little ember. Whatever else they were, whoever wrote those oaths he keeps — they weren't him."

Ash considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "His bread is good. Better than ration bread. Even when the city still had bakeries."

It was such a small, ordinary grief that Serah laughed despite the rib, and Ash allowed herself half a smile, and for a moment the cottage was just a cottage and they were just people in it.

Then Juniper came back inside, and the moment folded itself away.

He stood in the doorway with dirt on his hands — he'd been digging, she realized, out past the garden, digging down along the old boundary stones — and his face was terrible and calm, the calm of a man standing on the edge of a drop.

"The boundary stones," he said. "There are ninety-one of them. I've counted them since I was six. I counted them again this morning." His jaw worked. "There are eighty-eight."

"The hollow is shrinking," Serah said. "Vael was folded into this valley during the war — folded small to fit, folded tight to hide. But a fold needs power to hold its shape. Dragon-fire. And dragon-fire burns down." She nodded at the ember in Ash's hands, glowing sullenly between the child's fingers. "When it goes out, the fold collapses. Everything inside it — every street, every ghost, every last soul of Vael — goes with it."

"How long?"

"Ash's fire bought it years once. Now?" She looked at the ember. She made herself say it plainly. "A season. Perhaps less. The Wardens know it too. They don't want to save Vael, Juniper. They want it to end *cleanly* — no survivors scattering into the world carrying the old blood, the old names. They're hunting the last-born so the flame dies with her instead of finding a new vessel."

Juniper's gaze went to Ash, and Serah watched something move through him that she recognized from her own mirror: the exact moment a stranger becomes someone you cannot put down.

"My whole life," he said quietly. "Every dawn ritual. Every oiling of the hinges. Every year I renewed the wards on that door, I was—" He laughed, short and awful. "I was oiling the locks on a prison. I thanked my father for teaching me. I *prayed* over it."

"You didn't know," Serah said. "They made sure no keeper ever knew. That's the cruelest engineering of the whole design — they gave the jailers a religion so they'd never ask about the crime."

"But you knew." His eyes came up to hers, and there it was — anger, looking for a place to land. "You knew what the gate was, and you came here anyway. Through Warden territory, wounded, with a child. Why come to the one place built to keep your country shut in? Why bring her to the lock?"

"Because the lock is also the key," Serah said. "Your gate isn't the true door, Juniper. It's the lid. Beneath the mountain at the valley's heart there's an original door — the first seam, the one your ancestors used to fold Vael in the first place. A true exit. If it can be found and opened, Vael doesn't have to die in the dark. It can *pour out*. Into your world. Alive."

"And the catch," Juniper said. He was learning fast; she'd expected no less from a keeper's blood. "There's always a catch."

"There's a catch," she admitted.

Outside, the light was beginning to fail, gold going to copper going to gray, and in the fading brightness Serah saw the ember in Ash's hands pulse — slow, arrhythmic, like a heart missing beats.

"So tell me," the keeper said, and his voice had changed, steadied, the vertigo behind his eyes hardening into something with edges. "Tell me what it costs."

Serah met his eyes and told him.

And far off, beyond the tree line, beyond the fog that wasn't fog, something began — very faintly, very patiently — to howl.
