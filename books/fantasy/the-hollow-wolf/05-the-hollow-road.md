## 5. The Hollow Road

The gate opened the way a wound opens: all at once, and with no going back.

Juniper had expected light, or thunder, or some grand mechanical groan of ancient hinges. Instead the door simply agreed to move. Ash's ember pressed against the iron, and the metal went soft as wax under her palm, and Serah said something in Vaeli that sounded like a prayer and a curse braided together, and then they were stepping through — Juniper last, because someone had to be last, because that was what keepers did.

Behind him the door sealed itself. He felt it in his teeth. A sound like a held breath.

Then he turned, and forgot how to breathe at all.

Vael was small.

That was the first thing. Not beautiful, not ruined, not terrible — *small*, in a way that made his eyes water trying to reconcile it. The country spread out before him: hills, a river, the distant towers of a city he recognized from Serah's stories as Caer Vaelith, her home. But it was folded. Folded like a letter crammed into an envelope too tight for it. The hills overlapped themselves. The river appeared on three sides of the same meadow, flowing different directions each time. And above it all hung a sky the color of old pewter, close enough that Juniper felt he could touch it if he stood on his toes.

"It's shrinking," he said. His voice came out flat, swallowed by the gray air.

"The fire is failing." Serah had gone still beside him, one hand resting on Ash's shoulder the way she always did when she needed to feel solid ground. "Every year there's less. Every year we lose a valley, a village, a road. We fold inward."

"Like a fist," Juniper said.

"Like a hand letting go."

Ash tugged his sleeve and pointed down the slope, where a road unspooled toward a cluster of stone houses — and kept unspooling, past them, around them, reappearing behind them as if the road were walking itself. She said something in Vaeli, quick and bright, and Serah translated with the careful voice she used for hard things:

"She says the road remembers where it's going even if the country doesn't remember where it is."

Juniper looked at the child. At the ember cupped against her chest, glowing through her shirt like a second heart. At the folded hills and the pewter sky and the road that walked itself.

"I'm supposed to be keeping this out," he said quietly.

"You're supposed to be keeping it *in*," Serah corrected. "There's a difference, keeper. You're standing inside your duty now. Look at it properly."

He looked.

* * *

They came to the village by late afternoon, though afternoon was a guess; the pewter sky didn't change color so much as it changed *mind*. The houses were stone, squat, chimneys cold. Doors stood open. Laundry hung on lines, stiff and unmoving, because there was no wind in Vael — or none that Juniper could find, anyway.

And the people were there.

His first instinct was to shout a warning. Then he saw how they moved: a woman sweeping a doorstep that was already clean, over and over, the broom whispering the same arc across the same stones. A man carrying a bucket from well to house, house to well, the water sloshing but never spilling. Two children chasing a hoop down the lane — the same length of lane, again, again, again — their laughter arriving slightly after their mouths moved, like sound traveling from very far away.

"They're dead," Juniper said. It wasn't a question.

"They're *echoes*," Serah said. "When the fire takes a place, it doesn't erase it. It repeats it. Like a song hummed by someone who's forgotten the words." She walked straight through the middle of the lane, and the sweepers and carriers and children parted around her without seeing her, without *being able* to see her. "Don't look for their eyes, Juniper. They haven't got any anymore. Just the shape of them, doing what they loved."

But Juniper did look for their eyes. That was his mistake, or his gift, or both.

Because when he looked — really looked, the way he'd spent thirty years looking at the gate, learning its grain, its seams, the places where the iron remembered being forged — he saw something else. Faint seams in the air itself. Threads of paler gray running through the world like cracks in glaze: along the edge of the lane, up the side of a house, across the sky. And where the seams crossed, the repetition stuttered. The woman with the broom flickered, mid-sweep, into a younger woman holding a baby. The man with the bucket flickered into a soldier, mud-caked, weeping, then back.

"Ash," he said slowly. "Can you see—"

She was already nodding, pointing at the seams, chattering in Vaeli.

"She says everyone can see them here," Serah translated. "It's just that no one who lives here looks anymore. Why would you? Looking hurts." Then Serah stopped. Turned. Stared at him. "Wait. You can see them?"

"Since we crossed."

"That's not possible. Only Vaeldori see the seams. Keeper blood. The old families built the doors, wove the wards—" She caught herself, and he watched the thought land on her face like weather. "Your family. Your family came from *here*."

"My family came from the valley," Juniper said. "As far back as anyone wrote it down."

"As far back as anyone *was allowed* to write it down." Serah's jaw tightened. "The keepers didn't come from nowhere, Juniper. Someone had to hold the door from the outside, and you don't give that duty to a stranger. You give it to your own blood and you make them forget why."

He wanted to argue. He found he couldn't. The seams were everywhere now that he knew to see them — in the lane, in the sky, faintly, terribly, in the faces of the echo-children chasing their hoop. His own reflection, when he passed a dark window, seemed to have seams too.

A homeland that was never his, and grief rising in him for it anyway. Grief with no proper name. He mourned a language he didn't speak, streets he'd never walked, a war he'd never been told about — mourned them the way you mourn a room in a dream, waking with tears on your face and no idea which part of you had been crying.

"Keep moving," Serah said softly. "Echoes are harmless. What follows the echoes isn't."

* * *

What followed the echoes came at dusk-that-wasn't-dusk, when the pewter sky dimmed to slate and the ember against Ash's chest burned brighter, as if answering some call Juniper couldn't hear.

They sheltered in a fold — a hollow between two overlapping versions of the same hill, where the seams ran thick and the repetitions blurred into static. Serah called it safe the way you call a roofless barn dry in a storm. Ash slept against Juniper's side, ember dimming with her breath, and Serah sat sharpening a knife that had seen better decades, and Juniper sat watching the seams pulse.

"They're stronger near the center," he said. "The seams. Brighter."

"Near the mountain, yes. Near the source."

"The source of what?"

Serah didn't answer right away. She tested the blade's edge with her thumb, and the firelight — the only warm light in this whole folded country — moved across her face, and for a moment she looked young and tired and very far from anywhere.

"There's a door under the mountain," she said finally. "Not your gate. The *first* door. The one your ancestors built all the others to hide. If any of us leave Vael properly — alive, whole, not as smoke through a keyhole — it has to be through that door. And it has to be soon."

"And the Gray Wardens?"

"Wardens want the fire to fail cleanly. No exodus, no survivors carrying Vael out into the world like an infection. They'll want Ash most of all." Her eyes went to the sleeping child, and something fierce moved through Serah's face, there and gone. "So we get to the mountain first."

Juniper lay back against the cold hill-that-was-two-hills and listened to the static-hum of a dying country. Somewhere in the dark, faintly, he could hear the echo-village repeating itself. The broom. The bucket. The children laughing half a heartbeat late.

He thought: *my great-great-grandmothers walked roads like these. Spoke that tongue. Built my gate to cage all this, and made their children forget.*

He thought: *and I can see every seam of it, and I cannot look away.*

He must have drifted, because the next thing he knew, Ash was shaking him awake — awake properly, urgently, her ember flaring white through her shirt — and Serah was already on her feet with the knife low, staring down the slope.

Below them, at the edge of the echo-lane, stood figures that did not repeat. Figures that cast no seams. Tall, gray, still as fence posts, and multiplying.

"They found the ember," Serah breathed. "Run for the mountain — *now*."
