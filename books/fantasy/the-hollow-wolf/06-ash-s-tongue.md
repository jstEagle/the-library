## 6. Ash's Tongue

Serah woke to the sound of Ash singing.

Not the wordless humming the girl did while Juniper cooked, not the counting songs she used to fall asleep. This was Vaelish — full-throated, ancient, the vowels rolling like water over stones that had been polished for a thousand years. Serah sat up in the dark of the keeper's cottage and listened, and her chest hurt in a way she hadn't let it hurt since the burning.

The fire in the hearth was low, banked for the night. But as Ash sang, it rose. It stood up out of the coals like something called by name, tall and gold and utterly silent, bending toward the child the way wheat bends toward sun.

*Vessel,* Serah thought, and the word arrived with the weight of a stone door dropping shut. *She's not carrying an ember. The ember is carrying her.*

"You're awake."

Juniper's voice from the doorway, soft. He'd learned to move quietly in his own house around them, which struck her as backwards — it was their presence that had made his silence necessary at all. He crossed the room and lowered himself beside her against the wall, close enough that his shoulder touched hers. A week ago he would have kept a careful hand-span of distance. A week ago they had both pretended that hand-span meant something.

"Listen to her," Serah murmured.

"I don't understand any of it." He said it without frustration now. He said everything about Vael without frustration lately, like a man learning to live inside a wound that had turned out to be someone else's. "But I know what it's doing to the fire."

"Yes." She watched the flame sway. "I should tell you what I've been avoiding telling you."

He didn't answer. He just waited, the way keepers waited, patient as stone, and she found she could speak because of it.

"The dragon-fire isn't a lamp," she said. "It doesn't sit in Vael and give light. It *is* Vael. Every street that still stands, every dead man still walking his errand through the market — all of it is held in the shape of memory by one flame. And flames need fuel. For three hundred years the fuel was the dragons themselves, dying one by one into the fire. The last dragon died forty years ago."

"What happens when—"

"When the fire gutters?" She made herself look at him. "Vael doesn't go dark, Juniper. It goes *out*. Not death. Death implies there was something left afterward. Everything that ever lived there simply stops having been."

In the hearth, Ash sang on. The fire leaned closer.

"And her?"

Serah closed her eyes. "The last-born. Born under the flame itself, in the sanctuary beneath the capital. Her mother was the fire-priestess; her father was no one anyone remembers. When the Wardens came for her, the priests sent her out through the folds of the country with an ember in her hands and a prayer in her mouth. We were told she was a refugee. Even most of us who fought for her were told she was a refugee." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "A lie of mercy. If we'd known what she carried, half of us would have been too frightened to fight, and the other half would have understood why the Gray Wardens would burn down every road between here and the borderlands to reach her."

"The ones hunting you. Hunting her." His jaw tightened. "Tell me plainly."

"The Gray Wardens aren't soldiers. They're accountants. They believe Vael is a debt that must be settled cleanly — that letting it linger, guttering, half-alive, is cruelty. They want Ash because if the fire passes into her and she dies outside the hollow, the country ends *with* her, tidy and final. No seams. No slow unraveling leaking into your valley. Just a closed book." Her hands curled. "Or so they say. I think some of them are afraid of what the fire would do if it ever got loose. I think fear wears accounting robes very well."

Juniper was quiet for a long time. In the dark she could feel him thinking, the way you can feel weather change.

"My whole life," he said finally, "I was told the gate kept magic out. That it kept people like you safe from people like—" He stopped. Started again. "And really it was a cage. Built by my own blood. And now there's a child in my kitchen who is a country, and men in gray coming to put that country out like a bad candle." He looked over at her. "What do you do when everything you were sworn to protect turns out to be a prison?"

"You pick a new thing to protect," Serah said. "That's what exile teaches you. Duty isn't the oath. It's the choosing."

Ash's song ended. The fire sank back into the coals, tame again, and the girl padded over in her nightshirt and wedged herself between them without asking, the way children do when they have decided a thing belongs to them. Juniper's arm came up around her shoulders automatically. Serah watched him do it and felt something in her chest come unmoored.

"She dreams in fire," Ash said suddenly, in accented but clear common tongue. Both adults went still. The girl rarely spoke of it. "I dream in fire too. But mine is going down. Like a bath draining." She looked up at Serah with eyes that held embers in them, always, even in the dark. "How long do I have, Sera? You can tell me. Grown-ups' faces tell me anyway."

Serah's throat worked. "Weeks," she said. "Maybe less."

Ash nodded, solemn as a magistrate, and laid her head against Juniper's ribs. "Then we should hurry," she said, and closed her eyes, and within four breaths was asleep, because children can do that, carry the end of the world all day and set it down at bedtime.

Juniper looked at Serah over the girl's head.

"We'll hurry," he said.

* * *

They left two days later, through the gate, down the Hollow Road into the folded remnant of Vael — Juniper walking the seams like a man born to it, which Serah supposed he was, his keeper's blood showing him the places where streets repeated and doors opened onto rooms they shouldn't. They made for the capital, where the original door slept beneath the mountain. Three days of walking a country that remembered being large.

It was on the third day, in a market square where ghost-vendors still sold ghost-bread to ghost-buyers, that Serah smelled ash where there was no fire.

She stopped. Juniper, two steps ahead, read her face instantly and put his body between her and the street without being asked. Ash's hand found hers and squeezed.

"How many?" Juniper asked quietly.

"At least six. They circled wide — they knew I'd smell the sulfur." She drew steel. "They've been herding us. This square has one road out."

"Then we hold the square until we find another."

"You've never fought."

"No," Juniper said, drawing the short blade Serah had spent a week teaching him to hold, "but I know this ground better than they do. It's remembering me."

They came out of the repeating streets in perfect silence, six figures in gray, faces like ledger pages, and the ambush broke over the square like weather.

Later, Serah could never assemble the fight into order — only into moments. The moment the first Warden's blade met hers and she discovered they fought like men who had never once been afraid of dying, which was worse than fighting men who wanted to kill you. The moment a second Warden flanked toward Ash and Juniper stepped into his path and shouted — shouted, in the old tongue of the keepers, a word he couldn't have known he knew — and the street itself shuddered, folding a half-step sideways, so the Warden's lunge took him into a wall that hadn't been there. The moment Ash screamed and the ghost-market ignited, harmless golden fire standing in every brazier and window at once, blinding the Wardens' gray sight.

And the moment Serah went down under two blades, arm screaming, certain — and Juniper was there. Not skilled. Not strong enough. Simply *there*, taking a cut along his forearm rather than let the killing stroke land, giving her the half-second she needed to roll, rise, and end it.

When it was done, four Wardens lay still among the ghost-stalls, and two had fled into the folding streets, and the square was quiet except for the sound of Juniper breathing hard and bleeding.

"You idiot," Serah said, and pressed her hands to his arm, and her own were shaking. "You absolute — you've never —"

"You were falling," he said, as if that explained anything, as if it explained everything.

"It's my job to fall. I'm the soldier. You're the —" She ran out of words. His blood was warm between her fingers. "Don't you ever do that again."

"All right," he said, clearly lying, and then, softer: "Serah. Look at me."

She looked. His eyes were the brown of the valley in autumn, and they were steady, and behind him Ash was watching them with her ember-eyes and trying very badly not to smile.

"I spent my whole life talking to a door," Juniper said, "because I thought nothing living would ever talk back. And then a child walked out of nowhere and a knight followed her through, and for the first time the world on both sides of that gate is worth more to me than the duty of it. If we die here tomorrow, or next week, or when the fire runs out — I need you to know it before anything else happens. Before the door. Before the end." His voice roughened. "You're what I chose. Not the oath. The choosing."

The square held its breath. Somewhere a ghost-bell rang for a market that closed three hundred years ago.

"I buried my country twice," Serah said. "Once when I was exiled, once when I learned the truth. I swore I wouldn't love anything that could be taken again." Her forehead came down against his. "And then you handed me bread in your kitchen and asked me if Vael had seasons, like it mattered, like *I* mattered. So yes. Yes, Juniper. Whatever's coming — we walk into it together."

Behind them, Ash clapped her hands once, delighted, entirely without shame.

"Told you," the girl said. "I told the fire weeks ago."

Juniper laughed — a real laugh, torn straight out of him — and kissed her, blood and dust and all, in a dead market in a dying country while a child cheered.

* * *

They bound his arm and moved on, because grief and joy both keep schedules. By late afternoon the mountain that held the capital rose ahead of them, black against a sky the color of cooling iron, and Serah felt the ember in Ash's small hand pulse like a heartbeat running low.

Ash had stopped walking.

"Ash?" Juniper crouched to her height. "What is it?"

The girl was staring at the mountain. The firelight in her eyes had gone strange — not guttering now, but *flickering*, quick and irregular, like a candle reading something in the wind.

"He's awake," she whispered.

"Who's awake?"

"The first one." She looked up at Juniper, and her small face was grave beyond its years. "The first keeper. He knows your blood is coming home, Juniper. And he wants to talk."

Far above, in the heart of the mountain, something ancient turned over in its sleep — and the whole hollow country shivered once, like a held breath finally moving.
