## 7. The City That Remembers Him

The capital of Vael had a name once. The streets still whispered it, worn smooth as a river stone — *Ael Vareth, Ael Vareth* — but the syllables came out hollow, like a bell with its clapper pulled.

Juniper walked those streets and felt them walk back.

It wasn't like the borderlands, where the country repeated itself in loops, the same crooked lane cycling past the same shuttered mill until Serah taught him to close his eyes and count his steps through the seam. Here the repetition had a purpose. The city was *remembering*, and it remembered him.

He knew it before he could say how. The cobbles warmed under his boots. Lamps that hadn't burned in two hundred years guttered awake as he passed, one by one, a trail of small orange witnesses. In a doorway, a woman made of dust and lamplight turned her head to follow him, her mouth moving around words that arrived a half-second too late to be heard.

"She's saying *welcome home*," Ash said quietly. She rode on Serah's shoulders now, too tired to walk, her ember cradled against the knight's neck where it pulsed like a second heartbeat. "The stones are saying it too. They're very loud."

"The stones can stop," Juniper said. His voice came out rougher than he intended.

The city did not stop.

* * *

They made camp in what had been a temple, or something like one — a hall of white pillars where the ceiling opened onto a sky that wasn't sky at all but a painted memory of one, clouds frozen mid-drift, a sun that gave light without warmth because warmth cost fire and fire was running out. Serah built no fire. There was no need; Ash's ember lit the whole hall in a soft amber wash, and the shadows leaned toward it like plants toward sun.

"Your blood," Serah said, without preamble. She'd been sharpening her sword for an hour, though there was nothing left in this country for the blade to bite. "I told you the keepers were Vaeldori. I didn't tell you which Vaeldori."

"You said a family. A line of doorwards."

"I said what my order was permitted to say." She set the whetstone down. In the ember-light her face was all planes and shadow, and she looked, for once, exactly as old as the things she carried. "The Galloways weren't doorwards, Juniper. They were the architects. Your ancestors drew the gate. They cut the valley out of the world and folded Vael into what's left, and they did it to end a war they couldn't win any other way."

Juniper heard the words. He felt them land somewhere distant, like stones dropped into a well he couldn't see the bottom of.

"They sealed their own country in," he said.

"With the dragons' consent. With the last dragon's consent — it gave its fire willingly, at the end, to keep the remnant breathing instead of letting the war burn everything to ash." Serah's jaw tightened. "That part might even have been mercy. But mercy doesn't need a keeper posted outside the door for three hundred years, telling every child born to the line *never open it*. Mercy would have left instructions for how to let people out."

The hall breathed around them. Dust drifted up from the floor in slow spirals, and Juniper realized the dust was listening.

"My whole life," he said. "Every ritual. Every dawn I swept the threshold. Every night I asked the door if it was cold." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I talked to a prison wall my family built. I loved it. I named the hinges."

Ash slid off Serah's shoulders and crossed the floor to him, small feet silent on stone. She put her hand flat against his chest, over his heart, the way she did when she wanted to know if a thing was true.

"You're angry," she said in Vaelish, and then, carefully, in his tongue: "Angry is allowed."

"Is it?" He looked down at her. Eight years old and carrying a dying nation in her ribcage. "Then why does it feel like treason?"

"Because you were raised by the lock," said a voice from the far end of the hall, "and locks teach obedience, not freedom."

* * *

The ghost stood between two pillars, and Juniper knew him the way you know your own reflection in dark water — distorted, delayed, but yours.

He wore the keeper's grey. Not Juniper's patched, road-worn version but the original: heavy wool, silver clasps shaped like interlocking doors, a key on a chain around his neck that hummed with a cold Juniper recognized from the gate itself. His face was Juniper's face aged forty more years — deep lines, a beard gone white, eyes the same restless grey that Juniper saw in his own reflection every morning and had always privately disliked.

"Aldous Galloway," Serah breathed. Her hand went to her sword out of pure reflex, absurd as it was. "First of the line."

"The first who stayed outside," the ghost corrected. His voice was dry as old paper. "My brother stayed inside. Someone had to hold each side of the door, and we drew lots, and I lost, and I have spent two centuries deciding whether losing was the luckiest thing that ever happened to me." He looked at Juniper, and looking at him was like looking into a mirror angled wrong. "You have his hands. Corren's. My brother's hands. He used to talk to the door too, from the inside. Did you know that? Did your fathers pass down that habit, or did it grow twice from the same root?"

"We weren't told anything," Juniper said. "We were given rules. Sweep the threshold. Oil the hinges. Never ask the door questions." He stepped forward, and the hall's dust rose around his ankles like a congregation rising for a hymn. "Never open it. Three hundred years of never. And all along—"

"All along, the door was a lid," Aldous said. "And you were the weight on top of it. Yes."

"Why show me this now? Why does the city remember me *now*?"

"Because the fire is guttering." The ghost's calm finally fractured, just at the edges, like ice under a boot. "Because the last dragon is dying, and when it dies, everything inside this fold dies with it — not quickly, not cleanly, but like a candle in a closed room, eating the last of the air. Because a child walked out through a crack in the seal carrying the dragon-heart in her chest, and the moment she did, the door stopped being a door and started being a wound." He raised one translucent hand, and the key on his chain lifted, turning slowly, catching the ember-light. "And because the key has been waiting three hundred years for a hand with the right blood, and yours just walked into my city."

Serah moved to Juniper's side. She didn't touch him, but her shoulder hovered a finger's width from his, and that nearness steadied him more than grip ever could. "What's he offering?"

"Two things." Aldous drifted closer, and where he passed, the pillars brightened briefly, remembering themselves. "Absolution. You can take my guilt into yourself — the architect's guilt, the whole weight of what we did — and carry it out of here, and live, and be free of it, because guilt shared across a bloodline grows lighter. Or you can take succession." The key turned again. "Become the last keeper, the true one, keeper of the *original* door beneath Mount Sorrow, and turn the key yourself, and decide with your own hands what happens to my country."

"And if I take neither?"

"Then the fire gutters, the Wardens catch the child, and everyone in this room dies inside a shrinking box while Gray men in gray coats file the paperwork." The ghost smiled, and it was Corren's smile, Juniper realized — the one in the old portrait in the cottage, the one he'd talked to as a boy. "I don't recommend it."

"It's not a real choice," Juniper said slowly. "Absolution is just a kinder cage. Take the guilt, keep the silence, go home, sweep the threshold another thirty years while a country suffocates behind me. You're offering me the same duty in different clothes."

"Yes," Aldous said simply. "I'm testing whether you'll notice."

Behind him, Ash made a sound — small, fierce, almost a growl — and the ember at her throat flared so bright that every ghost-shadow in the hall flinched back. "Take the door," she said in Vaelish, and then, stumbling, in his: "Not the sorry. The door. We don't need sorry. We need *open*."

Juniper looked at the key turning on its chain. He thought of the gate he'd tended his whole life, the door he'd loved like a companion, and understood at last that loving a thing and being its prisoner could happen in the same body at the same time, and often did.

"If I take succession," he said, "what does the original door cost to open?"

The hall went very quiet. Even the painted clouds seemed to lean in.

"Everything it has ever held," Aldous said. "Whoever turns the key gives up every memory of what lies behind it. Every face. Every street. Every story." His eyes went, deliberately, to Serah. Then to Ash. "The door takes payment in advance."

Juniper's heart did something complicated and painful. He didn't look at Serah. He was afraid of what his face would do if he did.

"Then we find the door first," he said. "And I'll decide what it costs when I'm standing in front of it — not in front of you."

For a long moment the ghost studied him, and Juniper had the strange sense of being weighed by every generation of his line at once.

"Good," Aldous said at last. "Corren argued with me too. It's the family talent." He extended the key on its chain, and it floated across the space between them, humming its cold note, and settled into Juniper's palm like it had always lived there. "Mount Sorrow is three days' walk, if the roads remember how to be roads. The Wardens entered the fold an hour after you did. And Juniper—" the ghost began to thin, dissolving into the amber light, his voice coming from everywhere now, from the pillars, from the stones, from the blood in Juniper's own veins, "—the Wardens aren't hunting the child anymore."

Serah went rigid. "Then what are they hunting?"

"The key," said the city, in ten thousand dust-voices, as every lamp in Ael Vareth went dark at once.
