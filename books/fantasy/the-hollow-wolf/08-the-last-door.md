## 8. The Last Door

The ember led them down.

Not metaphorically — Ash held it out in front of her like a lantern, and the little flame leaned forward into the dark of the mountain's throat, tugging at the air the way a dog pulls at a leash. It had been doing that for two days now, ever since they'd left the capital's ghost-streets behind and followed the old pilgrim road into the roots of Mount Vael itself.

Serah walked at the rear with her sword drawn. She'd stopped pretending she wasn't expecting the Gray Wardens an hour after they left the city, when she found the first sign: a smear of ash on a milestone that hadn't been there when the dead of Vael made their last procession along this road, centuries ago or yesterday — time didn't behave properly here anymore. Someone living had passed this way. Someone hunting.

"Tell me again," Juniper said. He was walking beside Ash with his hand hovering near her shoulder, not quite touching, the way he did when he wanted to offer comfort but wasn't sure it was welcome. "The door. What happens when we find it."

"You've asked four times," Serah said.

"And you've answered three times with different words."

"Because I don't know all of it." She kept her eyes on the dark ahead. "I know what the knight-orders taught us before the orders were dissolved. There was always a true door. The gate your family built above was a copy — a lock over a lock. The real one is under the mountain, older than the war, older than the keepers. It's how Vael came to be *folded* in the first place. Everything that happened to us went through that doorway."

"And to open it?"

Ash spoke without turning around. Her voice was small and level, the way children sound when they've rehearsed something until the fear is pressed flat out of it. "A keeper has to want it. Not just say yes. *Want* it, all the way down, so the door knows it isn't being tricked." She cupped the ember closer. "And then the door takes what the keeper remembers."

"Took me a while to understand what she meant by that," Serah said quietly.

Juniper stopped walking.

Serah heard him stop, and hated herself for the relief she felt that he finally had. She'd been circling it since the city, since the ghost of the first keeper had put its gray hand on his head and called him *heir*. She'd told herself he understood. She'd told herself the look on his face in the throne room meant understanding. But Juniper Sandoval was a man who talked to doors, who fed a fire no one sat beside, who had spent thirty years performing rituals whose meaning had been taken from him — and men like that learned to carry terrible knowledge without letting it change their step.

"The price," Juniper said. "Say it plainly."

"The door has held everything," Serah said. "Every memory of Vael that anyone ever carried through it. When a keeper turns the key, the door doesn't just open. It *empties*. Into him. And to make room—" She made herself meet his eyes. In the ember-light his face was hollowed and calm, which was worse than fear. "It takes everything the keeper holds that the door has ever touched. Every memory of Vael. Of the hollow. Of the road here. Of—"

"Of you," Juniper said.

The word fell between them and neither moved to pick it up.

Ash's ember flickered. Somewhere far above them, stone shifted — a settling groan, or something else.

"We can find another way," Serah said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"There is no other way." Ash said it without cruelty, the way she said most things. "The fire is almost out. You've seen it." She turned, and in the light of the thing burning in her hands, her eyes looked ancient. "I'm the last of it. When I go dark, everything inside the fold goes with me. The dead walking their errands. The streets that repeat. All of it. Unless we open the true door and let what's left of my country walk out into your valley."

"And the cost of that is him forgetting you exist," Serah said. "Forgetting any of this. Forgetting *me*."

"Yes," Ash said.

* * *

They camped in a side chamber where the mountain had once housed pilgrims — niches cut into the rock, still bearing the soot-smudge of lamps lit by people three hundred years dead. Serah took first watch because she couldn't have slept if you'd paid her.

She sat with her back to the wall and watched Juniper across the dying fire. He wasn't sleeping either. He lay on his side facing her, awake, and when their eyes met he didn't pretend otherwise.

"How long have you known?" he asked.

"That the price was memory? Since the city. Maybe before." She drew her knees up. "My order's founding text had a line in it nobody could translate. *The key turns only in a hand willing to be emptied.* We thought it was poetry."

"It's a very honest kind of poetry."

"Juniper." She waited until he looked at her properly. "You don't have to do this. We can go back. We can fight the Wardens at the gate above — I've held worse ground with fewer swords. We can buy years. Maybe the fire finds some way to last."

"It won't." He said it gently, the way you tell someone a patient is dying. "You know it won't. I've seen the seams, Serah. I'm the only one who can see them, and they're fraying everywhere. This morning the river ran backward for an hour and Ash pretended not to notice because she didn't want to frighten me. A country is dying around us, and the only exit costs a memory." He smiled, and it broke her heart, how easy the smile was. "Thirty years I kept a door I didn't understand. I think I can pay for one I do."

"Don't." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Don't make peace with it yet. Please."

He crossed the space between them. Sat beside her against the wall, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd stood on the wall of the gate the night the Wardens first probed the borderlands, back when trust between them was still a thing under construction.

"Tell me something," he said. "When this is done — when the door opens and I wake up tomorrow knowing nothing — what will I see?"

"A valley full of strangers," Serah said. "People who grieve you without being able to explain why. A woman with a scarred jaw who looks at you like—" She stopped.

"Like what?"

"Like a promise," she said. "That's what Ash calls it. She says I look at you like a promise."

Juniper was quiet a long moment. Then: "Will you tell me? After? Who I was?"

"I'll tell you everything. Every day if I have to."

"And if I don't believe you?"

"I'll tell you again."

He laughed softly, and leaned his head back against the stone, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the mountain breathed around them, and Serah memorized him — deliberately, greedily, the set of his shoulders, the crooked tooth when he smiled, the way his hand found hers in the dark without looking, as if his body already knew the shape of the loss coming for it.

Above them, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain groaned again.

This time it didn't stop.

* * *

They ran.

The passage beyond the pilgrim chamber climbed and split and climbed again, and behind them came a sound Serah knew from sieges and collapsing towers: the deep arrhythmic knock of boots on stone, many boots, moving in unison. The Gray Wardens had found the road. Or had been on it all along, letting the ember lead them, letting their quarry do the searching.

"They're herding us," Serah gasped, taking the steps two at a time with Ash slung against her back — the girl weighed nothing, another wrongness of the hollow, another debt the dying country owed its last-born. "The whole descent. They wanted us to find the door."

"Then let them watch us use it," Juniper said. He ran ahead, and Serah saw it again — the thing she'd seen in the capital — the way the mountain *recognized* him. Seams of pale light opened along the walls where he passed, hairline cracks showing something older and vaster behind the stone, closing again in his wake like water behind a keel.

The passage ended.

The chamber beyond was not carved; it was *found*, a cavern vast enough that the ember-light died before reaching its walls. And in its center stood the door.

It was nothing like the gate above. The gate was iron and oak and keeper's sigils, built by hands. This was a single slab of black stone standing upright in the cavern floor, taller than a siege tower, smooth as still water, and across its surface moved faint images — Serah caught a flash of orchards, a harbor, a woman laughing, ten thousand small memories sliding past like fish under ice. Everything the door had ever held. Everything it was waiting to give away.

Ash slid off Serah's back and walked toward it, and the ember in her hands rose, straining, and the images in the stone began to move faster, agitated, recognizing their own fire.

"No keyhole," Juniper said.

"You're the keyhole," Ash said. "Keeper. Put your hand where the fire points."

The ember drifted forward and settled against the black stone at the height of a man's heart, and beneath it, slowly, a shape surfaced in the rock — a depression in the exact outline of a spread hand.

Boots in the passage. Close now. Gray light bleeding up the stairwell, colorless as frost.

Serah drew her sword and put herself between the door and the dark. "How long?"

"I don't know," Juniper said. His hand was already rising. "I think it takes as long as it takes to mean it."

"Then mean it fast."

The first Warden came out of the stairwell like smoke given armor — tall, faceless beneath its hood, a blade of gray glass in each hand. Behind it, more. A dozen. Two dozen, filling the passage mouth, silent, patient as erosion.

Serah rolled her shoulders, settled her grip, and felt the dread in her chest sharpen, refine, harden into something with an edge. Fear was fuel. Her order had taught her that before the order burned.

"Juniper Sandoval," she said, without turning around, as the Wardens began to advance and the keeper of the gate laid his palm against the door of the world. "Whatever you forget — remember that you were loved. That part isn't the door's to take."

Behind her, she heard him breathe in.

The black stone began, very softly, to glow.
