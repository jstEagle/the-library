## 10. Stories Ash Will Tell

The valley learned to hold magic the way a cupped hand learns to hold water — carefully, at first, spilling a little, then steadying.

Ash felt it happen. She had been the ember for so long that she knew fire the way other children knew their own names, and on the first morning after the door, she woke in a strange soft bed and felt her flame *breathe*. Not gutter. Not strain against the shrinking walls of a folded country. Just breathe, wide and easy, like something finally given room.

Below her window, Vael was pouring itself into the valley.

That was how she thought of it, though it took three days for all of it to arrive. Streets unspooled down the slopes like ribbons shaken out of a drawer. The capital came last, settling into the low meadows with a sound like a long exhale, its pale towers leaning at angles no architect intended but no one minded. The dead walked their old errands right up to the edge of the new grass and stopped there, and looked around, and some of them wept, and some of them simply sat down, and by evening most of them were not dead anymore. The valley's clean air filled them like breath filling sails.

Ash watched from the window while Serah braided her hair, the knight's hands gentle in a way she saved only for Ash and never spoke about.

"Is it over?" Ash asked.

Serah's hands paused. "For you? Yes." Then, quieter: "For him—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Ash knew who *him* was. Everyone in the house knew who *him* was, because they all arranged themselves around him now, the way a family arranges itself around a hearth without ever quite saying so.

"Tell me again," Ash said. "What he did."

"Ash."

"You promised. You said if I kept walking when my feet hurt, you'd tell it as many times as I wanted."

Serah sighed, and Ash felt the sigh more than heard it, the small surrender of it moving through the braid. "He held the door," Serah said. "Against six Gray Wardens and the dark coming off them like cold water. And when I told him to run, he laughed. He actually laughed. And then he turned the key."

"And forgot."

"And forgot."

Ash looked out the window at the valley filling up with a country that had almost ended, and she touched the place behind her ribs where the dragon-fire lived, warm and patient, and she made a decision that morning the way children make decisions — completely, without any idea of how large it would grow.

She would remember for him.

* * *

Juniper Sandoval woke each morning in a house full of people and could not account for a single one of them.

Not with fear. That was the strange mercy of it. The forgetting had taken his memories whole, but it had left the shape of them behind, the way a removed tooth leaves a socket the tongue keeps returning to. He woke in a bed that smelled faintly of woodsmoke and someone else's soap, in a cottage larger than the one he'd tended — or thought he'd tended — at the gate, and he lay still and listened to the sounds of a life happening around him: boots on stairs, a kettle, two old men arguing in a language he didn't know but somehow understood the *feel* of, the way you understand rain.

There was a girl. She appeared at his bedside most mornings before he was fully awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor with her chin in her hands, watching him with an expression he couldn't read — too old for eight, too fierce for fondness, though he suspected it was fondness underneath.

"You're staring," he said, the fourth morning.

"I'm checking," she said. "You do this every day. Wake up confused. It passes in about a minute. Today it took fifty seconds. You're getting faster."

"Faster at what?"

"At coming back." She hopped up. "Come on. Serah made eggs. If you don't come soon, Bram eats yours, and then you sulk, and then I have to tell you a story to cheer you up, and I'm saving today's story because it's a good one."

He followed her down. He always followed her down. He did not examine why; examining anything too closely gave him a headache behind the eyes, a pressure like a door pressed shut from the other side.

The kitchen was full. It was always full. A knight named Serah — tall, gray-eyed, a scar through one brow, who moved like a drawn blade even carrying a skillet — put eggs in front of him without asking how he liked them, and they were exactly how he liked them, which unsettled him more than anything else in the strange arithmetic of his days. An old woman called Maren knitted by the fire. Two brothers from the capital argued over a map of the valley, deciding where the market should go. A boy with ash-gray eyes kept sneaking looks at Ash, and Ash kept pretending not to notice, and both of them were terrible at it.

And Serah looked at Juniper across the table the way — he searched for it, every morning, and found it every morning — the way you look at a promise. Like something had been sworn to him. Like she was keeping it.

"Do I know you?" he asked her once, early on, before he'd learned not to ask.

"Yes," she said.

"How?"

"Eat your eggs," she said, and her voice was steady but her hand wasn't, and after that he understood it was a wound he kept touching, and he tried, for her sake, to stop.

* * *

The stories started in the second week, when the confusion was worst.

He had gone out to the gate — because there was still a gate, standing open now, its great doors flung wide and tied back with iron chains, and everyone in the valley treated it like a monument, and Juniper alone went to it like a man visiting a grave he couldn't identify. He stood before it feeling the headache build, feeling the shape of the missing thing press against the inside of his skull, and he might have stood there until dark if a small hand hadn't slipped into his.

"You look like you need a story," Ash said.

"I don't—"

"There was a keeper," she said, ignoring him entirely, the way she did, "who lived alone in a valley where magic couldn't go. He talked to a door for thirty years. He named it. He called it *old grumble-face* when it stuck in winter."

"I would never name a door," Juniper said.

"He also sang to it," Ash said. "Badly. On festival days he sang the Lament of the Nine Rivers, and the door hummed back, and he pretended it was the wind."

Juniper opened his mouth. Closed it. There was something — a flicker, a warmth, gone. "That sounds like a foolish man," he managed.

"It sounds like a lonely one," Ash said. "But he didn't stay lonely. Because one day a child came to his gate speaking a dead tongue, carrying an ember that never cooled, and against every rule he'd ever been taught, he took her in. Fed her soup. Let her sleep by his fire. And when a wounded knight came hunting for her, bleeding half to death on his doorstep, he took her in too." She squeezed his hand. "He was good at taking people in. It was his whole gift. Doors, strangers, lost things. He just kept opening."

The headache behind his eyes flared and softened, flared and softened, like breathing.

"What happened to him?" Juniper asked. "The keeper."

Ash looked up at him, and for a moment she was not eight years old at all; she was the last-born of a dying country, the living vessel of its last flame, older than her bones. "He opened the last door," she said. "It cost him everything he remembered. But here's what nobody tells you about memory." She tapped his chest, right over the heart. "It leaks. It comes back in stories, in songs, in the way a person makes eggs exactly how you like them. He forgot his choice. He didn't forget himself. You can't forget yourself. It's the one door that locks from the inside."

They walked back to the cottage together as the light went gold, and Juniper found he was humming something — a lament, nine rivers' worth of grief — and couldn't say where he'd learned it, and found, strangely, that he didn't mind.

* * *

By midsummer, the valley held both magic and memory, and the two got along better than anyone expected.

Vael's survivors planted orchards that bloomed out of season. The dead-turned-living told their errands aloud like prayers, and the valley listened, and things grew. Children born in the hollow country learned to run under open sky. Maren's knitting needles moved on their own when she napped. And Ash's flame, no longer a nation's last candle, settled into being what it had always wanted to be: a hearth-fire, a campfire, a small bright thing in a kitchen full of people.

Serah stayed. Of course Serah stayed. She trained the valley's young ones in the yard behind the cottage, wooden swords clacking, and every evening she came in dusty and laughing, and every evening she looked at Juniper across the table like a promise being kept.

One night, after the others had gone up, she lingered. The fire was low. Ash was supposedly asleep and definitely listening from the stairs, a fact both adults pretended not to know.

"You hum that song sometimes," Serah said. "The one you can't place."

"Do I?"

"The Lament of the Nine Rivers." Her voice was careful, the way it was whenever she walked near the edge of his forgetting. "A keeper used to sing it. Badly."

"Was he bad at everything?" Juniper asked, smiling. "This keeper of yours. He sounds like a story someone invented to be kind."

"He was real." Serah looked into the fire. "He was stubborn, and dutiful, and funnier than he knew, and he chose us. Over everything. Over thirty years of duty. He chose a girl he'd known a week and a knight he'd known less, and he paid the price without hesitating, and if I spend the rest of my life paying that back—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I look at you every day like a promise, Juniper Sandoval. One day you'll ask me why. When you're ready. And when you do, I'll tell you everything, and it will either break your heart or mend it, and I've decided I can live with either."

Juniper sat with that. The headache stirred behind his eyes — the door pressing shut — and then, gently, it eased, as if something on the other side had decided to wait.

"Whatever you swore," he said finally, "I think I'd have sworn it too. Whoever I was."

Serah smiled, and it remade her whole face. "You would have," she said. "I know exactly who you are."

From the stairs, very quietly, Ash smiled too, and added another line to the story she was keeping, the one she told every morning and would tell for years: *and the keeper forgot, but the forgetting didn't take, because love is the one thing that remembers itself.*

* * *

On the first anniversary of the Opening — the valley had already made it a festival, because Vaeldori could turn anything into a festival — Ash climbed to the gate at dawn, alone, as she did every year, and pressed her palm to the ancient iron.

It hummed. It had hummed since the day she'd first touched it, and it hummed now, deep and glad, a door that had finally become what doors are for.

Behind her, footsteps on the path. Juniper, carrying two cups of cider, hair mussed, that familiar puzzled tenderness on his face. He handed her a cup and stood beside her, looking at the open doors, at the road beyond, at whatever lived in the space where his memories should be.

"I dream about this place," he said. "Every night. There's a door, and I'm holding it, and someone is shouting my name, and I'm laughing." He shook his head. "Isn't that strange? I wake up happy and I don't know why."

Ash took a sip of cider and looked up at the last keeper of the gate — the man who had chosen her, who had forgotten choosing her, who loved her anyway because love is the part that leaks through.

"Not strange," she said. "Sit down. I'll tell you a story."

And she began, as she always began, as she would begin every morning for the rest of their long lives:

*"There was a keeper who lived in a valley that forgot..."*
