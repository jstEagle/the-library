## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The word hung in the air between them like smoke that refused to rise.

*Tomas.*

Not spoken — not exactly. The child's mouth had shaped it wrong, or rather shaped it in a way Silas's ears had never been built to hear, so that the name arrived whole but strange, the way a face looks different reflected in still water. The vowels were too long. The consonants came from somewhere behind the teeth, down near the root of the tongue, as if the word had to be dug out of the body rather than released from it.

But it was his name. Silas would have known it in any mouth, in any language, in any world.

She sat very still. Outside the shop window, Vellum's Rest went about its evening — the lamplighter making his rounds along Cordwainer Street, a cart groaning under winter turnips, someone laughing in the Wayfarer's Rest two doors down. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary town. Ten years of ordinary town, and she had built her life on those ordinarinesses the way a mason builds on bedrock, stone by careful stone, and now one syllable from an eight-year-old's mouth had cracked the foundation straight through.

"No," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them less like hauntings. "No, you don't get to say that. You don't know what that is."

The child looked at her with eyes that were not frightened, not pleading, not anything a child's eyes should be. Patient. That was the word. The eyes of someone waiting for a slow student to catch up.

Silas pushed back from the table. Her chair scraped against the floorboards and the sound seemed too loud, as though the room itself had grown thinner since the child arrived. She crossed to the shelf above her drafting desk — the high shelf, the one she never touched except to dust it twice a year and hate herself both times — and took down the first of the lexicons.

Her mentor's name had been Odile Ferrant, and Odile had been many things: brilliant, difficult, secretive, dead eleven years now of a fever that should have killed a woman half her age. She had also been, in the way of great cartographers, a collector of languages. Not the court tongues, not the trade-pidgins every merchant's clerk could stumble through. Odile collected the *other* kind — the languages that existed before borders, the ones that named rivers by what they dreamed of becoming, the ones spoken only in places where the map itself grew uncertain. She kept them in seven volumes bound in green leather gone soft as cloth, and she had left them to Silas with a single instruction, written on a card tucked inside the first volume:

*If anyone ever speaks one of these to you, child, burn the shop and run.*

Silas had laughed when she'd read it. Odile's idea of humor was indistinguishable from prophecy, and after ten years Silas had decided the card was the former.

She opened Volume Three at the fire and began to turn pages.

* * *

It took until well past midnight.

The problem was that Hollowtongue — if it was Hollowtongue — did not behave like language was supposed to. Silas knew perhaps forty scripts and could read twenty of them fluently, and every one of them worked by agreement: this mark means this sound, this sound means this thing, and the agreement holds across kingdoms and generations. But the words the child spoke didn't seem to *mean* things so much as they seemed to *do* things. When the child said the word for water, the water in the washbasin trembled. When the child said the word for door, the latch clicked softly, once, like a dog turning over in its sleep.

Odile's notes were no comfort. In the margins beside her transcription of what she called *the Thornfell rite-tongue* — a fragmentary vocabulary assembled from three pilgrim accounts and one madman's confession, all over a century old — her mentor had written in her small furious hand:

*Not a language of description. A language of command. The speakers do not ask the world to change; they inform it that it already has. Extinct, thank every god. If it is not extinct, then nothing I have taught you will matter, because the maps will have begun to lie.*

*Extinct.* Silas remembered copying that word into her own commonplace book at nineteen, safe and smug, a fact about a dead thing in a dead place. Thornfell had swallowed its last choir the same season it swallowed Tomas. She had made her peace with that arithmetic. Two disappearances, one mountain range, zero survivors, case closed, grief filed away in the same high cabinet as the lexicons where it could gather dust instead of blood.

And now a mud-caked child sat at her table humming syllables that predated the kingdom, and every page Silas turned confirmed what her skin already knew.

Volume Four held the grammar. Or what passed for it. Hollowtongue declined its nouns not by number or case but by *distance* — a thing near the speaker, a thing far from the speaker, and a third form Odile had translated, with visible reluctance, as *a thing that is being taken*. The verb *to sing* had no past tense. It had a present, a future, and a form marked in Odile's glossary as *ongoing since before the present was invented*.

Silas set the book down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Behind her eyelids: the last morning. Tomas at the door with his pack already shouldered, twenty-three years old and lit up like a man who has finally been given permission. *"There's a valley north of the Thornfell massif that doesn't appear on any survey,"* he'd said. *"Not hidden, Nystrom — unmapped. There's a difference. Hidden places are hiding from people. Unmapped places are hiding from the world."* He'd kissed the top of her head, which he hadn't done since they were children, which she should have understood even then was a goodbye wearing a brother's face. *"Keep my ink wet. I'll bring you back something to draw."*

He had brought her back nothing. The search parties brought back less. And Silas, twenty-four years old and newly orphaned twice over, had done the only thing she knew how to do with an unbearable absence: she had charted around it. She drew maps of everything else with such obsessive precision that the hole in the middle of her life looked deliberate, planned, a cartographer's choice rather than a wound.

"You're going to have to do better than names," she told the child, without opening her eyes.

When she lowered her hands, the child was watching her with that terrible patience, and then — slowly, deliberately, the way one person offers bread to another — reached across the table and turned Volume Four toward herself.

* * *

The child read.

That was the only word for it. Silas watched small fingers move down Odile's transcriptions, hesitating here and there the way any reader hesitates at a bad handwriting or a corrupted source, correcting Odile's phonetics with small frowns, tapping a margin note twice as if to say *she got this part wrong, your teacher, she was guessing.* Of course she was guessing, Silas wanted to shout. She was working from pilgrims' letters and a madman's confession. She never heard it living. Nobody alive has heard it living —

Nobody except, apparently, the child at her table, whose lips moved through the dead language the way other children moved through a psalm learned at their mother's knee.

After a long time the child looked up and spoke again — several sentences this time, unhurried, each word placed like a stone in a wall. Silas caught fragments by cross-referencing the glossary as they came: *mountain* in its far-form. *King* in its third declension, the taken-form. *Winter*. *Door*. *Song* in the tense that had no beginning.

And one more word, repeated three times across the sentences, a short hard word that appeared nowhere in Odile's four surviving volumes.

"I don't know that one," Silas said. "Write it."

The child wrote it. The script was nothing like Common — it moved right to left in strokes like falling rain, and the moment Silas saw it her eye began to ache in a way she recognized from her worst working days, the days when a coastline refused to resolve, when the land itself resisted being drawn. Some places did not want mapping. She had always assumed that was a figure of speech.

She copied the word onto scrap paper, letter by letter, and carried it to the shelf, and pulled down Volume Six — Odile's index of scripts, the least-read and most dangerous of the seven, the one with the warning card in it — and ran her finger down the column of sample alphabets until it stopped.

The sample alphabet matched. Stroke for stroke, curve for curve, the falling-rain lines of it. Beside the alphabet, in Odile's hand, a single line of annotation:

*Script of the Hollow Choir. Found carved inside the sealed chapel at Greywater Ford, 40 years ago, walls and ceiling, thousands of repetitions of the same phrase. Translation contested. My translation: WE REMEMBER SO YOU DON'T HAVE TO. Burn this page if you are reading it after dark.*

It was, Silas noted distantly, after dark. It had been after dark for hours. She did not burn the page. She was beginning to suspect she had already failed every test Odile had ever set her, beginning with the one about running.

She returned to the table. The child had not waited. While Silas searched the shelves, the child had found a fresh sheet of her good linen-rag paper and her best iron-gall ink and the fine crow-quill pen Silas reserved for coastlines, and was drawing.

Silas stood in the doorway of her own workroom and felt the floor tilt gently beneath her, the way it does on a ship, or in a dream, or in the last moments before a truth you've outrun for ten years finally catches you by the collar.

Because the child could not draw.

This was obvious within seconds — the hand was untrained, the lines wobbled, the pen pressed too hard and spat blots. Any village schoolmaster would have despaired. And yet what emerged on the page was the most accurate piece of draftsmanship Silas Nystrom had ever seen, because the child was not deciding where to put the lines. The lines were being *remembered*. Ridges rose where ridges were. The pen found passes without searching for them. Rivers ran in channels that no survey had ever recorded, and they ran *correctly*, with the confidence of water that has cut its course over ten thousand years and sees no reason to lie about it now.

It was a map of the Thornfell massif. Not the published massif, the one on every merchant's road-map, the polite ring of peaks that guarded the kingdom's northern shoulder. This was the massif from the inside. The child drew valleys that existed between the mapped valleys, folds of terrain that no triangulation could reach — and Silas, who had spent a decade charting realms that lived in the creases of the world, recognized the technique instantly because it was *her* technique, the fold-logic, the between-space geometry she had believed was hers alone.

Except the child wasn't doing it as a craft. The child was doing it as memory.

And in the center of the map, drawn last, drawn with a care that made even the wobbling line go steady, the child rendered a structure. A hall. Long, low, sunk into a mountainside like a knuckle into a fist. Around it, in the falling-rain script, small careful annotations — dozens of them, labels for rooms Silas could not identify, and one label repeated at the heart of the structure, larger than the rest, written and rewritten as though the child needed to keep saying it to make it stay true.

Silas leaned over the page. Her shadow fell across the wet ink and the hall seemed to darken under it, and she had the irrational certainty that the building on the paper noticed.

"Translate it," she said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "The big one. Translate it for me."

The child pointed to the central label. Then pointed to Silas. Then, with excruciating gentleness, laid one finger against Silas's breastbone — over the heart, over the decade-old scar tissue where a grief had healed badly — and spoke the phrase aloud in Hollowtongue, and then again, struggling, in Common, word by word, like someone carrying water a very long distance in cupped hands:

"Where. The. King. Is. Kept."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the lamplighter finished his rounds, and Vellum's Rest settled into sleep, all its little honest lights going out one by one, none of them knowing what had just been said in the map-shop on Cordwainer Street.

Silas looked down at the map. At the hall in the heart of the mountain. At the label over its threshold, smaller, in the same falling-rain hand, and she didn't need it translated because she had already read it in Odile's Volume Six, the contested phrase, the one carved ten thousand times into the walls of a sealed chapel:

*We remember so you don't have to.*

"Tomas found this," she said. Not asking. Understanding arriving all at once, the way ice goes out of a river, all at once or never. "Ten years ago. He found the unmapped fold, and he found this place, and whatever is in it — whatever keeps kings — he walked in with his notebook and his ink and he thought he was bringing me back something to draw."

The child said nothing. The child simply turned the map a few degrees, so it faced Silas squarely, the way you turn a mirror toward someone who has been avoiding looking.

Silas thought: *I could burn it.* The thought was warm and cowardly and it fit her hand like an old tool. Burn the map, send the child to an orphanage in some larger town, nail the shutters closed, tell no one, grow old in Vellum's Rest with her moving river and her careful stones. Ten more years of ordinary. Twenty, if she was lucky.

Then she looked at the child — at Ash-something, at whoever-this-was, eight years old and carrying a dead language and a king's location in a body that should have been carrying marbles and a slingshot — and she understood that the warmth was not hers. It had never been hers. It belonged to the girl she'd been at twenty-four, standing at this same window, watching the search parties come home empty. That girl had chosen the burning. That girl had folded the grief away in the high cabinet and dusted it twice a year.

Silas Nystrom was thirty-four now, and tired, and owed the dead a debt she had been refusing to count for a decade.

"Right," she said, to the child, to the map, to the ghost in the margins of Volume Six. "Right."

She pulled her master atlas off the shelf — the working copy, the one with the frayed spine — and opened it flat on top of the child's drawing, to the map of the northern reaches, the honest, surveyed, kingdom-approved Thornfell. Then she took up a pencil and began, with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect, to draw the child's hidden valleys into the space between the printed ones. Fold-logic. Between-space geometry. The creases of the world.

The child watched her work for a while. Then, quietly, in that voice dug up from the root of the tongue, the child began to speak again — not words this time, but a low sustained tone, a single held note that made the fire gutter sideways and the ink in the well shiver into rings.

Silas's pencil stopped.

Because she knew the melody. Not the words — the melody. She had heard it once before, hummed off-key by a twenty-three-year-old packing his bag at dawn, laughing, saying *it's stuck in my head, Nystrom, been stuck in it since the pass, isn't that strange, isn't that funny* —

The child was singing her brother's song. The song he'd carried down out of Thornfell like a splinter. The song he'd been whistling when the mountain took him.

Outside, far off toward the northern hills, a bell that had not rung in eleven years began, faintly, to toll.
