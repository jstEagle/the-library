## 3. What the Crown Remembers

King Aldric Vane woke the way he woke every morning now: all at once, like a man surfacing from deep water with no memory of having gone under.

No dream. That was the first wrongness, though he had stopped counting it as such weeks ago. A king's sleep should be crowded — with battles old and new, with his father's disapproving voice, with the sea off the southern coast where he'd learned to swim, with small domestic terrors like forgetting a speech or arriving late to his own wedding. Dreams were the mind's ledger, his mother used to say, and every night it balanced itself whether you willed it or no.

His ledger had gone blank. Page after page after page.

He lay still beneath the furs and listened to the palace wake around him — boots in the corridor, the clatter of the kitchen yards, a cart groaning through the outer gate. Ordinary sounds. He clung to them the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, because they proved the world was still there even when he could not entirely prove that *he* was.

*You are Aldric Vane,* he told himself, as he did each morning, a litany begun out of habit and continued out of fear. *Son of Maren and Osric. Husband to Lira, dead these six years. Father to—*

Father to—

The name came. It always came. *Elowen.* He held it like a coin he was afraid would be called counterfeit. But the face —

He reached for the face the way one reaches for a word on the tip of the tongue, and found only a smooth place where something had been scooped away. He knew she was eight years old. He knew she favored her left side when she drew, tongue between her teeth. He knew she had a scar on her knee from falling out of the pear tree he had specifically forbidden her to climb. He knew all of this the way a scholar knows a country he has never visited — accurately, fondly, and at a distance.

But her face. Her actual face, the one he had kissed goodnight ten thousand times, the one he had watched scrunch up with fury and bloom open with laughter — gone. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a wall is gone where a door once stood, leaving plaster smooth and unbroken, as if there had never been a door at all.

Aldric sat up too fast and the room tilted. Grey light came through the shutters in slats. Somewhere below, Elowen would be waking in the nursery wing, and her nurse would be braiding her hair, and she would come bounding into his study before breakfast the way she did every morning, and he would look down at her and see —

See what? A child. A small girl with brown hair. His daughter, the records insisted, and the records were right, they had to be right, but the recognition — the warm, instant, bone-deep *there you are* — that was missing. In its place was a polite blankness, like greeting a stranger wearing his daughter's clothes.

*I am ill,* he thought. *This is an illness. Illnesses pass.*

He did not believe it. He was not sure how long he had not believed it.

* * *

The crown sat on its stand by the window, where the under-chamberlain left it each night for reasons of ceremony no one had ever adequately explained to him. Black iron, plain as a farmer's band, older than the dynasty — the coronation crown, they called it, dug up from beneath the chapel floor when the old gold one was lost. The smiths had offered to gild it. Aldric had refused without knowing why. Something about the black iron had seemed right, in those first strange weeks after the coronation, the way a fever seems right while it burns you.

It did not seem right anymore.

He looked at it across the length of the room and felt the look returned. That was madness, of course. Iron did not look. And yet there was a quality to the morning light around the crown that he could only describe as *attentive*, the way a cat is attentive to a bird it has decided not to pounce on yet.

"You're awake early, Majesty."

Wren Halloway stood in the doorway with the day's ledger against her chest. His chamberlain was a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and the unglamorous competence of someone who had spent twenty years making other people's chaos legible. She had served his father before him. Of everyone in the palace, she was the one whose presence he trusted most absolutely — which was fortunate, since lately he trusted very little else, including the evidence of his own mind.

"Chamberlain." He swung his legs out of bed. "What day is it?"

Her pause was brief. Too brief for anyone else to notice; he noticed. "The fourteenth of Frostmonth, Majesty."

"And the year?"

Another pause. This one longer. "Majesty?"

"Humor me."

"The three hundred and eleventh year of the Concord." She set the ledger on his desk and began, with practiced motions, to lay out his schedule. "Petitions at nine. The grain magistrates at eleven. Your daughter requests an audience regarding — I quote — 'a matter of extreme importance concerning the stables.'"

Something in his chest loosened at that. *Extreme importance concerning the stables.* That was Elowen entire, whoever Elowen was this morning. Some things survived even inside him, then. Small things. Things that lived in the hands rather than the heart.

"Wren," he said. "Do you ever wake and find things... missing?"

She looked up. Her eyes were quick and grey and missed nothing, which was usually a comfort and today felt like standing naked in a market square. "Missing, Majesty?"

"Not objects. Not stolen. Just — absent. As if they'd never been." He heard his own voice go quiet, conspiratorial, ashamed. "I dreamed nothing last night. I dream nothing most nights now. And this morning I could not—" He stopped. Started again. "There are gaps. In my memory. More than there should be at my age, and growing."

Wren Halloway was silent for a long moment. He watched her decide something — watch the decision move behind her eyes like weather. When she spoke, her voice was level and careful, the voice she used for delivering bad news to foreign envoys.

"What sort of gaps?"

"My mother's voice. I had it a month ago. I could hear it whenever I wished — her scolding, her singing. Now I know she sang, I know she scolded, but the sound itself is..." He made a gesture, helpless. "I have the shape of a thing and none of the substance. Like coins replaced with paper tracings of coins. And this morning—"

He could not say it. Saying it aloud would make it real in a way that frightened him more than the loss itself.

"This morning," Wren prompted gently.

"I looked at my daughter's portrait. The one in the west gallery. And I thought, *who painted that? Who chose that pose?* As if it were a picture of some nobleman's child sent for a marriage negotiation." His hands had closed into fists without his permission. "I know her name. I know her birthday. I cannot remember her *face*, Wren. My own daughter's face."

The fire popped. Down in the yard, someone shouted at a horse.

"Grief does strange things," Wren said at last, and it was the wrong answer and they both knew it, because grief had not taken his mother's voice, and grief did not grow, and grief did not feel like this — like being *edited*. "But I will send for the physicians—"

"No physicians." It came out harder than he intended. "No physicians, no priests, no one who reports to the council. If I am losing my mind, I will lose it privately, and if I am not—" He met her eyes. "Then something in this palace is taking pieces of me, and I would rather the thief not know I've noticed."

Something flickered across Wren Halloway's face then — something that was not surprise. Recognition, maybe. Or dread finding its confirmation. She bowed, precise as ever.

"As you wish, Majesty."

* * *

The day proceeded the way days proceeded now: smoothly, correctly, and with a strangeness underneath like water under ice.

At the petitions, a widow from the river districts wept over a flooded mill, and Aldric found himself giving judgment with perfect clarity and no feeling whatsoever. He knew the correct ruling — compensation, tax remission, royal engineers to assess the levee — and he delivered it, and the woman left weeping with gratitude, and he sat there afterward wondering why her tears had moved him the way rain moves a stone. He remembered being a man who wept at petitions. He had letters in his own hand, tender ones, written to grieving subjects in years past. Whoever had written those letters seemed to have been a different man entirely, one he knew only by reputation.

Between audiences, he stood at the window of his study and watched the city smoke rise, and performed his litany again. *Aldric Vane. Son of Maren and Osric. Husband to Lira. Father to Elowen.* Each repetition, he noticed, cost slightly more effort than the last, like walking upstream. How long before the current won? How long before he stood at this window knowing only that he was king, and then only that he was, and then—

"Majesty?" A clerk, with papers. He signed what needed signing. His signature had changed lately — smaller, more cramped, as if the hand doing it were trying to take up less space in the world.

It was late afternoon, the light going amber, when he saw the shadow.

He had stepped from the study into the long gallery to meet the grain magistrates, torches already lit against the early dark of Frostmonth, and his shadow ran out ahead of him along the floor — long and thin and wrong. He stopped. The shadow kept moving.

Only for a moment. Only half a heartbeat, perhaps less — the way a page turns, the way a blink misses things. Then it caught up to him and lay obediently at his feet as shadows do. He stood in the gallery with his heart hammering and told himself he had stumbled, that the torchlight had guttered, that eyes tired by a sleepless year invented tricks.

But he had seen it. He was almost certain he had seen it. His shadow had moved *without him*, the way a dog moves a half-step ahead of a master it no longer quite respects.

"Majesty?" The grain magistrates waited at the far end, bowing. Behind them, Wren Halloway stood by the wall with her ledger, and her face was pale, and her eyes were fixed not on him but on the floor beside him — on the place where his shadow lay.

Their gazes met across the length of the gallery. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing yet that could be said aloud in a corridor full of courtiers. But he saw in her face that she had seen it too, whatever it was, and that she had been seeing things for longer than she admitted, and that she was as afraid as he was.

That, strangely, was the greatest comfort the day gave him. Whatever was happening — illness, curse, or something with no name in any book he owned — he was not alone inside it. There was one person in this palace of polished smiles whose fear matched his own.

* * *

That night he dismissed his attendants early and barred the door himself, which caused a small scandal among the footmen and satisfied him completely. He built up the fire with his own hands. He took out paper and ink, and by candlelight he began to write everything he still possessed, the way a man facing a flood begins carrying things uphill.

*My name is Aldric Vane. My wife was Lira of the Marches; she smelled of rosemary and laughed before finishing her own jokes. My mother sang a lullaby about a boat on a grey sea; I no longer remember the tune. My daughter is Elowen. She is eight. She climbs trees she has been forbidden to climb. She favors her left hand when she draws.*

He wrote until the candle guttered low — names, dates, grievances, kindnesses, the layout of his childhood home, the taste of the honey-cakes sold outside the autumn fair. An inventory of a self. It filled four pages, and looking at the four pages he understood two things with terrible clarity.

First: that this was not sickness. Sickness scattered; this *selected*. Everything taken had been taken cleanly, along seams he could not find, leaving function intact and feeling hollowed. Someone — something — was curating him.

Second: the thefts were accelerating. A month ago it had been his mother's voice. A week ago, the smell of the sea. Today, his daughter's face. Whatever fed on him was growing bolder, or growing faster, or both.

And third — he set down the pen, and looked across the darkened chamber at the black iron crown on its stand, catching the firelight like a closed eye — third: the losses had begun the night he put it on.

He had told no one that. He had barely let himself think it, because the alternative was unthinkable: that the king of the realm had placed the instrument of his own unraveling upon his head with these two hands, smiling, before ten thousand cheering subjects.

The fire settled. The wind worried at the shutters. And from somewhere — deep in the walls, or deeper, in the bones of the mountain the palace was built on — Aldric fancied he heard a sound so faint he might have invented it: many voices, very far away, singing in a language he did not know, in perfect, patient, terrible unison.

He pulled his inventory of pages close to his chest, like armor, like a child's blanket, and did not sleep at all.

In the morning, Wren Halloway would request leave to inspect the archive vaults — and would find, three floors below the throne room, a record that would change everything: the register of royal decrees, and the fact that no decree bearing the true king's hand had been signed since the night of the black coronation.
