## 5. The Toll of Names

Brannoc had crossed the Vellwater border nine times in his life, and eight of those times the shrine at Marrow Ford had been nothing but a leaning stone arch furred with moss, the kind of landmark guides pointed at so their clients felt they were getting somewhere. The ninth time was now.

The shrine stood open like a mouth.

He reined the mule up short and stared. The moss was gone — stripped clean, every stone pale as bone, and where the archway had been dark before there was now a light that didn't come from anywhere. Not torchlight, not sun through cloud, though it was late afternoon and the sky over Thornfell's foothills had gone the color of dirty pewter. This light came from inside the stone itself, or from behind it, or from nowhere Brannoc had a word for. He'd led pilgrims and smugglers and one very nervous tax assessor through these hills for twenty years, and he had never once seen anything he couldn't put a name to. He hated that this thing already had one. He could feel it waiting for him to say it.

Behind him, the cart creaked. Silas pulled up beside him, Ash tucked against her on the bench, wrapped in two blankets despite the fact that the child never seemed cold.

"That's new," Silas said.

"That's wrong," Brannoc said.

"The distinction matters less than you'd like."

He grunted, because she was right, and because arguing with Silas Nystrom was like arguing with weather. She'd hired him with a debt he couldn't refuse — his own stupid fault, the winter he'd drunk away half the fee a client had paid her, and she'd covered him without a word until he sobered enough to be ashamed. Ten years of shame was a long leash. She'd barely tugged it when the mute child appeared at her door speaking a dead tongue, and here they were, three days out from Vellum's Rest, heading straight for the mountains he'd sworn off after the last time he'd led someone into them and come back alone.

Tomas Nystrom's name sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. It had been sitting there ten years. Some days he forgot it was a stone and thought it was just his heart.

They walked the mule forward. The light grew but did not warm. Twenty feet from the arch, Brannoc heard it: a sound like many voices breathing together, slow and even, the way a congregation breathes during the quiet part of a hymn.

He stopped the mule again. "We can go around. There's the old drover's path—"

"Adds four days," Silas said. "And if this is what I think it is, going around won't matter. It'll just mean we arrive later and it finds us anyway." She looked at him sideways. "You've heard of the Hollow Choir."

Everyone had heard whispers. Three years ago King Aldric had been crowned with some old iron circlet dug out of Thornfell, and since then the realm had been... comfortable. That was the only word Brannoc had ever been able to find for it. Comfortable the way a bed is comfortable when you're too sick to get out of it. People forgot things — small things, mostly, birthdays and debts and grudges — and nobody minded much, which was the part that had always made the hair on his arms stand up.

"I've heard," he said.

"Then you know why I need to see this." She swung down from the bench and lifted Ash after her. The child landed lightly, mud-colored eyes fixed on the archway, and did something Brannoc had learned to dread over three days of travel: she smiled.

Not a child's smile. An old one. A *knowing* one.

*What are you?* he thought, not for the first time. *And what did you do to make Silas Nystrom leave her shop?*

* *

*

The shrine's keeper — or what served as one — sat on a stool beneath the arch, and Brannoc's stomach turned over when he saw it, because it was almost a man. Almost. It wore brown robes and had hands and a face, but its face moved a half-beat behind its words, like an echo that had gotten ahead of its voice, and its eyes were the gray of river fog.

"Travelers," it said, and the breathing-voices underneath its voice breathed in. "Welcome to the toll."

"There's no toll at Marrow Ford," Brannoc said. His guide's voice, the flat professional one. "There's never been a toll. I've crossed here since before you were carved."

"There has always been a toll," the keeper said gently, "you have simply never had anything worth taking. Now." It tilted its head, and the movement was smooth and wrong, like water flowing uphill. "One memory, spoken aloud. A true one. Yours to choose. Give it freely, and pass. Refuse—" it spread its almost-hands toward the hills, where the light was going bad, "—and the road is long, and winter is coming, and something is walking in the folds of the world that eats the names of the lost."

Silas went very still beside him. Brannoc watched her watch the keeper, and he saw her arrive somewhere terrible, because her face — usually as readable as a locked strongbox — went briefly white around the mouth.

"It's leaking," she murmured. "It shouldn't reach this far south. Not yet. The solstice—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"You know what this is," Brannoc said quietly.

"I know what it resembles." She turned to him, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, pitched under the keeper's hearing — or maybe not; maybe the thing heard everything; maybe that was the whole horror of it. "Brannoc. We can turn back. I want you to know that's still true. Whatever happens past this arch, you chose in, and you can choose out."

He looked at her. Small woman, ink-stained fingers, eyes like her brother's — that same stubborn cartographer's certainty that the world could be measured, drawn, understood, *held*. He'd loved Tomas Nystrom like a brother himself, which was exactly how he'd failed him.

"And go back to what?" he said. "Vellum's Rest? Waiting for whatever this is to walk down out of the hills and knock on my door?" He snorted. "I'm a mountain guide, Silas. Mountains don't wait politely. You taught me that. Both of you did."

Something passed over her face — grief, quickly folded away — and she nodded once.

Ash tugged Silas's sleeve and signed something, quick fluid gestures. Silas signed back, lips moving faintly. Brannoc had picked up perhaps six of their signs in three days. This one he knew: *wait.*

The child stepped forward alone, right up to the keeper, and looked up into its fog-gray eyes.

"One memory," the keeper said, bending, and there was hunger in it now, real hunger, the kind Brannoc had heard in wolves' silence. "Speak, little one. What will you give?"

Ash opened her mouth — the mouth that had never once made a sound in Common or any living tongue — and breathed out.

Nothing came. No word, no whisper. Just a breath, visible in the cold, curling toward the keeper like smoke finding a draft. And the keeper *recoiled.* Actually rocked back on its stool, hands rising, the breathing-voices under its skin stuttering — a hitch, a stumble, a chorus missing its cue.

"There is... nothing to take," it said. Its echo-face flickered. "You are — you are not — "

Ash closed her mouth and smiled that old smile again, and walked through the arch without breaking stride.

The light didn't touch her. That was the thing Brannoc would remember afterward, telling it badly over drinks to people who wouldn't believe him: the light bent *around* her, the way water bends around a stone in a stream, and she came out the other side untouched, unexamined, un-*counted*, and stood waiting on the far side with her hands in her pockets.

"What in the frozen deep," Brannoc breathed, "is that child?"

"A better question," Silas said softly, staring, "than either of us has dared ask yet." But her voice had changed. There was fear in it, yes, but also something else — something almost like hope, and that frightened him worse.

* *

*

Silas went second. Of course she did — she'd never let him go first into anything dangerous, not since Tomas, not ever, as if she'd appointed herself the shield of everyone she traveled with and never asked anyone's permission.

She stood before the keeper for a long moment. Brannoc saw her lips move, shaping words too low for him to catch, and he realized with a lurch that she wasn't haggling. She was choosing. Deliberately, carefully, the way she chose which roads went on a map and which stayed blank.

Then she spoke aloud, one sentence, clear and steady:

"My mother's voice, singing the folding-song, the winter before she died."

The keeper breathed in. The air between them shimmered — Brannoc had no other word for it, the world going briefly thin as vellum — and then the sentence simply *left* her. He watched it happen. Watched her face change as something drained out of it, not pain exactly, more like the expression of a woman setting down a heavy bag she'd carried so long she'd forgotten its weight — except the relief lasted only a heartbeat, and then came the hollow where the weight had been, and he saw her understand, all at once, that she would spend the rest of her life reaching for her mother's voice and finding only the shape of the absence.

Her hand rose halfway to her mouth. Stopped. Came down.

"That's the trick of it," she said, and her voice was level but her knuckles were white on her map case. "You don't miss what's taken. You miss knowing you should miss it." She looked at the keeper with pure, cold hatred, the most emotion he'd seen on her in ten years. "Whose work is this? Whose song?"

"The Crown's," the keeper said pleasantly. "The Crown remembers everything now. Someone must hold what the realm forgets." Its head tilted further, further, further than a neck should go. "Pass, cartographer. Your memory is received with thanks."

She walked through. The light parted around her too — differently than around Ash, reluctantly, like a dog deciding not to bite — and she emerged on the far side and stood next to the child, and Ash took her hand without being asked, and Silas let her, and Brannoc felt something twist in his chest watching it.

Then it was him, and the mule, and the keeper turning its fog eyes on him with renewed appetite.

"And the big one," it said. "The guide. What does a guide carry, hmm? So many roads in your head. So many names of passes and fords and the graves of fools who didn't listen." The breathing-voices quickened, eager. "Choose well. Choose *richly.*"

"Don't tell it anything true about the route," Silas called sharply from beyond the arch. "Nothing about Thornfell. Nothing about—"

"I know," he said.

He stood there a while. The cold crept up through his boots. The mule shifted and blew warm air against his neck, and he rested a hand on its rough hide because it was the only honest company available.

Here was the truth of Brannoc Fenwick, disgraced guide, debtor, drinker, coward in one specific and unforgivable way: he did not carry many memories worth the keeping. His father had died when he was young and mean about it besides. His mother he remembered mostly as tiredness. He had no children, no trade but this one, no house, no land. What he had was a handful of bright things, hoarded like coins at the bottom of a dry well.

Mara's laugh, the first time he'd made her laugh properly, wine-drunk on a rooftop in Vellum's Rest, both of them nineteen and certain.

The morning he'd summited the Grey Tooth alone, younger and stupider, and cried like a baby at the view because no one had told him beauty could hurt.

His wedding day.

That one rose in him now, whole and warm, the way it always did when he let the well open: Mara in green because she refused white, said it looked like surrender; his hands shaking so hard she'd had to steady the ring herself; old Ferren bellowing the vows because the priest had drunk the ceremonial wine; rain starting halfway through and nobody moving, everybody dancing in it, Mara's green dress soaked dark and her laughing, laughing—

"Mara," he said aloud, before he could stop himself, before he could choose anything smaller. The keeper leaned in. "Our wedding day. All of it. The rain. Take the rain and the dancing and her laugh when the tent pole fell. Take it all, you gray-eyed thief, and be damned to you."

"Brannoc—" Silas started.

"Given freely," he said loudly, over her. "That's the price, isn't it? Freely given?"

The keeper smiled. Under its skin, the chorus sighed — a long, satisfied, communal breath, dozens of mouths inhaling in unison, and Brannoc thought, distantly, *that's what a choir sounds like from the inside, God help us all.*

"Freely given," the keeper echoed. "Received with thanks."

The world went thin. The air shimmered. And the day left him.

Not all at once — that was the cruel mercy of it. He felt it go the way you feel warmth leave a room, door by door. First the rain went, and he reached for it and found the memory said *it rained* but gave him no wetness, no smell, no drumming on canvas. Then the dancing went. Then the tent pole, the laughter, the green — *she wore something,* he thought, panicking, *she wore — it was — * — and then even the color was gone, and then her face, her dear ruined wonderful face, smoothing out of his mind like breath off glass, until what remained was a fact wearing the clothes of a memory: *I was married once. Her name was Mara. I loved her.*

*I loved her.*

Present tense. That survived. He clung to it as he walked through the arch, legs unsteady, the mule's lead rope biting his palm because he was gripping it too hard. The light passed around him and he barely noticed.

On the far side, Silas caught his elbow. Her grip was fierce.

"Why," she said. "Why that one? You could have given it a road. A river crossing. Anything."

"Because it was asking for one memory," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he meant, "and I'd rather it take the best of me honestly than the worst of me by accident. And because—" His throat worked. "Because if I'm going to lose things to whatever wears that crown, I want to choose the losing. That's all any of us get, Silas. The choosing."

She stared at him. Something shifted behind her eyes — respect, maybe, or recognition, the look of a woman revising a map she'd thought was finished.

Ash, meanwhile, had come to stand before him, small and grave, looking up with those mud-brown eyes. She raised one small hand and touched his wrist, right over the pulse, and held it there a moment. Then she signed, slowly, so he could follow: *hurt. stay.*

"Yeah," Brannoc said, his voice cracking clean in half. "Yeah, little ghost. I'll stay."

* *

*

They made camp that night in the lee of the shrine's far wall, close enough that the arch's sourceless light threw their shadows long across the frost. Nobody suggested pushing on. The road through the ford country was bad in the dark, and besides, none of them seemed able to leave yet — as if the toll had tied a thread to each of them and the threads needed a night to settle.

Silas built the fire high. Brannoc got out the salt pork and the black bread and the bottle of plum brandy he'd been saving for a disaster, and decided, looking at the pale arch glowing behind them, that a disaster was precisely what this was.

"To Mara," he said, and drank, and passed the bottle.

"To your mother's voice," Silas said, and drank.

Ash, offered the bottle, gravely mimed a sip and solemnly wiped her mouth, which set them both laughing — real laughter, cracked and sudden, the kind that comes out of grief like water out of a split rock. The child beamed at them, pleased with herself, and Brannoc thought: *three days ago I didn't know her name. Three days ago I didn't know hers, or yours, and now I'd walk into the frozen teeth of the north for either of you, and isn't that the oldest trick there is. Loss makes family faster than blood ever did.*

He said some of that out loud, or a mangled version of it, brandy-loosened, and Silas looked at him for a long moment across the fire.

"Tomas used to say something like that," she said quietly. "About maps. That the truest ones weren't of places." She turned the bottle in her ink-stained fingers. "I thought he was being poetic. He was always being poetic. I was the practical one." Her jaw tightened. "I'm beginning to suspect he was being precise."

The fire popped. Beyond the arch, far off in the dark foothills, something answered it — a sound like singing, very distant, very many, threading through the wind. Ash's head came up like a deer's. She stared north into the black, and her smile was gone, and for the first time since Vellum's Rest the child looked afraid.

She signed fast. Silas translated, her voice gone flat and careful, and Brannoc felt the night tilt under him:

*"They know we're coming,"* Silas said. *"And she says — she says the choir has started learning our names."*

Far to the north, faint as memory, something sang Brannoc Fenwick's wedding day back to him in a voice that was almost Mara's.

He did not sleep that night. None of them did.
