## 6. Wren's Heresy

The archives beneath the Hall of Records smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of paper that had been forgotten for a very long time. Wren Halloway had always found the smell comforting. Tonight it felt like a shroud.

She carried a single candle, though she did not strictly need it. The lamps along the archive walls were lit — they were always lit now, ever since the king's third year, ever since His Majesty decreed that no corner of his capital should know darkness. But she had learned to distrust light that burned without anyone tending it, the way one learns to distrust a dog that no longer barks at strangers. So she brought her own flame, small and honest, and left the great lamps guttering in their sconces like witnesses who had been paid not to speak.

Three years of ledgers. That was what she needed. Three years, from coronation to now, and every decree signed in the king's hand between them.

Wren Halloway had served the crown of Aldric Vane since she was nineteen years old, first as a scrivener with ink-stained knuckles and a head full of statute law, then as under-clerk, then as chamberlain — the youngest in the history of the realm, appointed because old Chamberlain Devries had died in his sleep and the king had looked at her across the council table with those grey eyes and said, *You count things properly, Wren Halloway. I trust people who count things properly.* She had counted things properly for eleven years. She had counted grain shipments and garrison wages, the candles burned at state funerals and the length of mourning veils. Counting was her devotion, her prayer, her way of loving a kingdom too large to hold in two arms.

And so it was counting that had finally betrayed her into heresy.

It began, as these things do, with something too small to notice. A discrepancy of a single day. The harvest remission for the Fenlow districts had been signed on the ninth of Reaping-month, but the courier logs showed the writ leaving the capital on the eighth. A clerical error, surely. She had corrected it in the margin and thought no more of it — except that she *had* thought more of it, because Wren Halloway did not make errors, and neither, in eleven years, had any clerk under her.

Then there had been the matter of the border tolls. Then the appointment of three new magistrates whose names appeared in the rolls before their letters of recommendation existed. Then the strange, slow thinning of the king's public appearances — from weekly audiences to monthly, from monthly to those terrible occasions when he stood on the balcony above the Coronation Square and waved with a hand that moved exactly as a hand should move and yet made Wren's skin crawl in some way she could not name and dared not examine.

*The shadow,* whispered a voice in her memory, her own voice, from three nights ago, standing at the window of her chambers as the king crossed the courtyard below in the light of the setting sun. *His shadow lagged.* Just half a heartbeat behind him, like a servant who had learned the master's steps but not quite mastered them. She had told herself it was the angle of the light. She had told herself many things lately, each one thinner than the last, until the whole fabric of her self-deception had worn through and she could see the shape beneath it, vast and patient and singing.

So now she counted. One last time, properly.

The ledger room for the second and third years of the black crown was at the far end of the archive, past the tax rolls and the census folios, past the shelf where the records of the old Thornfell expeditions gathered their grey fur of dust — she noted those in passing, without knowing why, the way one notes a door one will later need. She set her candle on the reading table, drew the coronation-year ledger toward her, and began.

Decree the First: proclamation of the coronation festival. Signed Aldric Vane, in that strong rightward-slanting hand she knew better than her own.

Decree the Second: appointment of the new Lord Marshal. Signed.

Decree the Third: the dissolution of the northern survey companies. Signed — and here Wren paused, because she remembered that decree, remembered the council chamber, remembered the king reading it aloud in a voice like a well-tuned instrument, and she remembered thinking at the time how odd it was that he never once glanced down at the page.

She turned forward through the months. Festival writs. Tax schedules. The strange quieting of the court, year by year, the ministers growing smooth-faced and agreeable, the debates shrinking to murmurs, the murmurs to silence. And through all of it, the signatures marching down the margins of history like soldiers on review:

*Aldric Vane. Aldric Vane. Aldric Vane.*

Perfect. Every one of them perfect. Identical in slant, identical in pressure, identical in the little flourish of the final *e*, which the real king — she was thinking of him now as the real king, and the thought made her hands go cold on the vellum — the real king had never once managed to reproduce. He had complained about it often enough, laughing, in the early years. *My own name defeats me, Wren. Every signature a small battle.* The flourishes varied. They had varied for thirty years of decrees, leases, pardons, love letters to his late queen. No man signs his name twice the same way; a signature is a heartbeat put to paper.

These signatures did not vary. These signatures were not heartbeats.

With fingers that trembled only a little, Wren laid two decrees side by side — one from the last month of the old reign, one from the first month of the new — and held her candle close. The old signature leaned and stumbled and bloomed. The new one sat on the page like something printed. Like something *stamped*. And once she saw it, she could not stop seeing it, page after page after page, three years of decrees, hundreds of them, and not one variation, not one human wobble, not one moment where the hand holding the pen had been tired or hurried or grieving or glad.

The real Aldric Vane had signed nothing. Not one thing. Not since the night the black crown came out of Thornfell and was set upon his head amid trumpets and incense and the weeping joy of ten thousand subjects.

Wren sat back in the dark and listened to her own breathing.

There was a version of her — the woman she had been a month ago — who would have found another explanation. Forgery, perhaps; a scheming regent; some ordinary treason that could be fought with ordinary law. She reached for that comfort now the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood, and found it would not bear her weight. Because forgery required a forger, and a forger required a motive and a face, and there was no face. There was only the king, unchanged, unaging-seeming, smiling his correct smile, and the guards in the corridors who no longer blinked — she had noticed that too, God help her, she had noticed it weeks ago and filed it away in the drawer marked *later* — and the servants who spoke less each season, and the city outside the windows going quiet in a way that cities should not go quiet, its bells still ringing but ringing *the same*, every morning the same peal, every evening the same peal, as if the whole realm were a music box winding down toward a single note.

And underneath everything, so faint she might have imagined it — she had *not* imagined it, she knew now she had not imagined it — a sound like singing. Heard in the walls at night. Heard in the spaces between the bells. Heard once, terribly, in the king's own voice, when he had laughed at some councillor's joke and the laugh had gone on a half-second too long and come apart, just at the edge of hearing, into many voices laughing together.

Wren closed the ledger.

"I count things properly," she said aloud, and her voice sounded very small and very brave in the dark, and she discovered that she was crying, silently, the way she did everything — efficiently, without spectacle — and that the crying changed nothing and helped anyway.

Because here was the heresy, the thing she could not unknow: the king was gone. Whatever wore his crown and signed his name and waved from the balcony, it was not Aldric Vane. It was wearing him the way she wore her chamberlain's chain — as an office, as an instrument, as a *costume*. And it was doing something to the kingdom. Eating it, maybe. Unremembering it. She had no word for the crime yet, but she had evidence, and evidence was the beginning of every law that had ever protected anyone from power.

Which meant she had a decision, and the decision had already made itself while she sat counting in the dark.

* * *

The royal signet lived in a lacquered box in the king's private study, in a locked drawer, in a room guarded by two men of the Crown Guard who had not blinked at Wren in — she tried to remember — she could not remember how long.

That was the horror of it, once you let yourself look straight at it. Guards were supposed to blink. Blinking was life; blinking was attention; blinking was the small involuntary proof that a man saw you and judged you and decided about you, a hundred times a minute. These guards watched her approach down the corridor with open, lidless eyes, faces smooth as eggs, and when she said, in her crisp chamberlain's voice, "I am required in His Majesty's study to prepare the morning papers," they stepped aside in perfect unison, like doors.

"Of course, Chamberlain," said the left one.

"The king is resting," said the right one.

They spoke in the same tone. They had spoken in the same tone for a long time. She wondered how many other things she had filed under *later*.

Inside the study, the candlelight found the room exactly as it always was: the great desk, the maps of the realm pinned to the wall — and Wren stopped, because the maps were wrong. Not torn, not faded. *Wrong*. The rivers ran where they had always run, the towns sat in their proper valleys, and yet the whole thing had a stillness to it, a painted quality, as if someone had copied the kingdom from memory and the memory had been going soft. The Thornfell mountains at the map's northern edge were rendered in careful detail, and around them, where the wild country should have broken into unmapped blankness, the blankness had been filled in with faint, repeating patterns — rows of tiny marks, like notes on a stave, marching off the parchment's edge.

She did not let herself look at them long. Looking too long at things felt dangerous now, felt like an invitation.

The signet box sat in the drawer where it had always sat. Heavy black lacquer, silver hinges, the crowned stag of House Vane embossed on the lid. Her hands hovered over it.

Stealing the royal signet was treason. There was no softer word. Eleven years of service, and it ended here, with her committing the one crime that every law she loved agreed was unforgivable — because the seal was the king's voice made metal, and to take it was to take the kingdom's word out of the kingdom's mouth. If she were caught, they would not even need a trial. There were cells beneath the Hall of Justice, and she had signed the warrants that sent men to them, and she knew precisely what happened in the dark down there, and none of it was quick.

But the alternative was the balcony, and the waving hand, and the laugh that came apart into many voices. The alternative was a kingdom signing itself away in a dead man's perfect hand until there was nothing left to sign.

*Treason against whom?* she asked the empty study, and the question rang like a bell, and there was her answer, and there was no answer after it.

She took the box. It was heavier than she expected, or perhaps her arms had grown weak. Inside, on its velvet bed, the signet gleamed dull gold, the crowned stag worn smooth by thirty years of a living man's hand. She pressed her thumb against the stag's flank, once, like touching a relic, like asking pardon.

"I'm sorry, Your Majesty," she whispered. "Wherever you are. I'm going to find you."

Behind her, in the corridor, the guards' boots did not shift. But somewhere deeper in the palace — below, in the foundations, in the stone itself — she heard it again. The singing. Faint and sweet and perfectly in tune, dozens of voices braided into one, rising like water in a sealed cellar.

And this time, the song paused. Just for a moment. The way a singer pauses when he realizes someone in the audience is not clapping.

* * *

She went out through the scullery, because the scullery was the one road from the private wing that passed no guards, and because Marta the cook owed her twenty years of small kindnesses and had a daughter in the fen-country Wren could pay handsomely to shelter a fugitive. Marta took one look at Wren's face — chamberlains are not supposed to have faces like that — and asked no questions, which was its own kind of courage, and stuffed a sack of bread and hard cheese into her arms and said only, "The east postern, m'lady. The latch sticks. Lift and push."

The east postern opened onto the tannery lane, and the tannery lane opened onto the city, and the city at midnight was the worst thing Wren had seen yet.

Not because anything threatened her. Because nothing did. The streets were full — fuller than they should have been at midnight — of people walking home from nowhere, moving with that same unhurried smoothness, faces calm, eyes fixed on the middling distance. A lamplighter stood at his ladder on Candlemaker's Row, but he was not lighting lamps; he was simply standing, looking up at the flame he'd already lit, and as Wren slipped past in her plain travelling cloak she heard him humming. A tune she didn't know. Simple, sweet, circular. Two streets later, a woman shaking out a carpet was humming it. By the river gate, a watchman hummed it, and the melody had spread through the midnight city like frost through a windowpane, everyone humming the same few bars, everyone in perfect unison, and no one — no one — appearing to notice they were doing it.

Wren walked fast and did not run. Running drew eyes, and she had begun to understand that eyes were what the thing in the palace used, the way a spider uses silk.

At the river gate the watchman raised his lantern. "Late night, Chamberlain."

"Business for the crown," she said, and showed him the lacquered box, and hated herself for how easily the lie came, and watched him nod and wave her through — and only when she was fifty yards down the towpath, breath burning, did she realize what had happened. She had shown him the box. He had not asked what was inside. No loyal watchman in eleven years of honest gates would have failed to ask what was inside the royal signet's case leaving the palace at midnight.

He hadn't asked because he hadn't wondered. And he hadn't wondered because wondering was memory, and memory was being taken, spoonful by spoonful, from the whole sleeping realm.

The ferryman was awake, mercifully, and mercifully ordinary — yawning, grumbling about the cold, demanding triple fare for a night crossing, blinking. Wren nearly wept at the blinking. She paid him double her purse's contents and sat in the stern with her arms around the signet box while the capital receded behind her, its thousand obedient lamps burning, its towers crowned with the palace where a hollow thing sang lullabies to a stolen throne.

Where would she go? The thought arrived only now, absurdly late, bobbing in the wake like a cork. She had evidence and a seal and no plan. The council was useless — she understood that with total clarity; the council had been useless for three years, nodding like reeds. The army answered to the Lord Marshal, who had been appointed by a dead man's stamped hand. The temples sang the same tune as the lamplighters. There was no one left to tell.

No one in the capital.

But the realm was wider than the capital. The realm had edges, and borders, and border-towns where the old roads crossed and the old tongues survived and the crown's long arm grew clumsy. Somewhere out there were people who kept records of their own — cartographers, archivists, hedge-witches, smugglers, all the stubborn folk who wrote things down that the center wanted unwritten. Her whole life she had trusted the center, the ledger, the proper channel. The proper channel flowed through a corpse's mouth now.

The ferryman's lantern swung. Ahead, the north bank rose dark and forested, and beyond it, days away, the grey shoulders of the Thornfell mountains stood up against the stars — the mountains from which the black crown had come, the mountains the old surveys had abandoned, the mountains every sensible soul avoided.

Which meant, Wren reasoned, watching them grow larger with every stroke of the oars, that they were exactly where the truth would be kept.

She opened the signet box one last time and looked at the crowned stag gleaming in the dark, and made it a promise, the first vow of her new heresy: *You'll sign true again, or I'll know why.*

Behind her, across the black water, the capital's bells began to ring the midnight peal — the same peal, note for note, as every night for three years.

Except tonight, for the first time, one bell was a half-beat late.

Someone, somewhere in that sleeping city, had missed the count.

Wren gripped the gunwale and smiled into the cold wind, and it was not a comfortable smile. It was the smile of a woman who has just realized the enemy is not omnipotent — only patient — and that patience can be beaten by people willing to be afraid and keep walking anyway.

"Hold on, Your Majesty," she said to the mountains ahead, to the king behind her, to whatever fragment of him might still be listening in the dark. "I'm coming. And I'm bringing the receipts."
