## 8. Choir of the Frozen Chapel

The storm found them an hour past dusk, and Brannoc had been grateful for it right up until the moment he understood what they'd have to shelter inside.

He smelled the chapel before he saw it — old stone and older incense, a sweetness gone stale, like flowers left in a sealed room for a hundred years. His nose had kept him alive on mountains that had killed better guides, and the nose said: *man-made, hollow, dry.* The nose did not say: *wrong.* But something behind the nose did, something older and less articulate, and Brannoc had learned over forty years to trust the part of him that spoke in shivers.

"There." He pointed through the whiteout to where a gable broke the ridgeline, black against black. "Chapel. Old road-shrine, maybe. Pre-kingdom."

Silas squinted through the snow. "There's no chapel on any map of this pass."

"There's no *pass* on any map of this pass, mistress. The mountain moves its furniture when it pleases." He said it lightly, because that was his way, but his hand had already gone to the axe at his hip, and Silas noticed. She noticed everything. It was, he was learning, both her gift and her affliction.

Ash pressed close to Brannoc's side — the child had taken to walking in his shadow on the steep stretches, small boots finding the holds he found, and he had stopped minding it around the second day. The girl looked up at the gable and made a sound. Not a word. A low, sustained note, almost a hum, and the snow between them seemed to lean toward it.

"What's she doing?" Brannoc asked.

"Listening," Silas said. "Or answering. With Ash it's hard to tell the difference."

They climbed the last stretch with the wind clawing at their backs like something trying to follow them in, and Brannoc got the door open — swollen oak, iron fittings gone to rust-bloom, hinges that screamed like a man being murdered — and shoved them all inside before the storm could follow.

Then he turned, and saw the singers.

There were eleven of them. Statues, he told himself. Carved figures, standing in two rows before a ruined altar, life-sized, robed in stone that had once perhaps been painted. Pilgrims, or monks, or a choir, frozen mid-procession by some sculptor long dead. He had seen a thousand such figures in a hundred chapels from here to the coast.

Except that every one of them had its mouth open.

Not slightly. Not in the stylized way of carved singers, lips parted to suggest song. Their mouths were open *wide* — jaws dropped, throats stretched, the full ugly mechanics of a human voice caught in stone. And their eyes — he moved along the row, counting, because counting was what Brannoc did when he was frightened, counting holds and rope-lengths and days of food — their eyes were not blank. They were *aimed.* Every statue's gaze was fixed on the same point in the empty air above the altar, as though something hung there. As though something still hung there.

"Silas," he said quietly. "Mistress Nystrom. I think we've found why there's no chapel on the map."

* * *

They made camp in the nave because the storm gave them no choice, and Brannoc told himself the arrangement of the statues was coincidence, and then he told himself he was too old to lie to himself, and then he stopped talking to himself at all.

The choir stood in a semicircle facing the altar. Which meant they faced the fire. Which meant that as the flames settled and warmed and threw their light, eleven stone faces flickered into expression above eleven stone throats, and Brannoc sat with his back to the wall and his axe across his knees and watched them out of the corner of his eye the way you watch a sleeping dog of unknown temper.

Silas moved through the chapel with her cartographer's hunger, all caution burned off by curiosity, sketching by firelight. She measured the nave in paces. She copied the worn inscriptions on the altar. She stood before the statues with her notebook and her charcoal and studied their open mouths with the same focus she gave to river-forks.

"They're not decorative," she said at last. "Look at the wear patterns. These weren't carved and left. People came here for generations and touched them — see, the hands, the hems, all worn smooth. They were a *congregation.*" She frowned. "A congregation of statues. That's backwards. Usually it's the people who leave and the stone that stays put."

"Everything about this place is backwards," Brannoc said. "The door opened outward. Did you notice? Every chapel door in the world opens inward. This one opens out. Like the building doesn't want to keep anything in." He paused. "Or wants to keep something *out.*"

Ash had not gone near the statues. The child sat as far from them as the nave allowed, curled against her pack, and she was doing the thing she did sometimes — humming, very low, a melody that wandered and returned on itself like a path through fog. The fire had gone blue at the edges again. Brannoc was getting used to that. He was not sure he would ever be *comfortable* with it, but he was getting used to it.

He was nearly asleep when the hymn started.

* * *

It did not come from outside. That was the first thing he understood, in the grey halfway place between waking and dreaming, with the storm hammering the roof and the fire burned to embers. The hymn came from inside the chapel. From inside the *stone.*

It was very faint. It was not music, exactly — it was the *shape* of music, the ghost of a melody, the way a footprint is the ghost of a foot. And it was coming from the statues. From their open mouths. From throats that had not been throats for centuries.

Brannoc sat up so fast his back cracked.

"Silas." His voice came out as a rasp. "Silas. Wake up. *Wake up.*"

She was already awake. She was sitting bolt upright with her notebook clutched to her chest like a shield, and her face in the ember-light was the color of old paper. "I hear it," she whispered. "I've been hearing it for an hour. I was trying to decide if I was dreaming."

"Are you?"

"I don't know. That's the problem."

The hymn swelled — or seemed to swell; it was impossible to say whether the sound was growing or whether their ears were opening to it, the way eyes adjust to darkness. It was a choir singing, unmistakably, many voices braided into one, and it had the terrible smoothness of a thing that had been sung so long it had worn all the humanity out of itself. No breath in it. No mistakes. Nothing alive.

And the statues' mouths were moving.

Not much. A tremor at the corners. The smallest possible motion, stone grinding against stone, a fraction of an inch of movement that no sculpture should be capable of and no sculpture should *need* — because if you were only stone, why would you bother to sing?

Ash stood up.

The child crossed the nave in the dark, small and certain, and planted herself in front of the semicircle of singers with her head tipped back and her fists at her sides, and she listened the way Brannoc had seen her listen before — with her whole body, eyes shut, swaying slightly. The blue light from the embers played over her face.

Then she began to speak. Not her wordless tongue this time. She spoke in that halting, careful way she had, picking through Common like someone crossing a river on stepping stones, and her voice in the dark was small and clear and worse than any scream Brannoc had ever heard on a mountain.

"They say," Ash said, "that they are not praying. They are *warning.*"

Silas was on her feet. "Warning about what?"

Ash's brow furrowed. She listened again, lips moving, translating. "The words are old. Older than the words I know. But it says — " She stopped. Started again. "It says: *it does not want your flesh. It never wanted your flesh. Flesh is heavy. Flesh forgets slowly. It wants what the flesh carries.*"

"Carries," Silas repeated.

Ash pointed, very simply, at her own throat. Then at her own head. "The voice. The names. The songs you learned at your mother's knee. The way your father said your name when you were small." She looked up at Silas, and her eyes were ancient in her child's face. "It collects voices. Not bodies. That is why the statues are empty. It took the singing and left the stone. The stone is what a person is *after.*"

The hymn swelled, and this time Brannoc heard it plainly, and what he heard underneath the melody — underneath the terrible perfect smoothness of it — was *people.* Voices. Hundreds of them, thousands, layered so fine they had melted into one sound, the way a rope is many threads. And every voice had once been somebody. Somebody's daughter. Somebody's guide. Somebody who had stood in this chapel on a stormy night and heard the singing start.

"How long," Silas said hoarsely, "has it been singing?"

Ash listened. "Since before the chapel. The chapel was built *around* the singing, to hold it. The singers were its first congregation. It sang to them for so long that they stayed. It sang them into stone." She tilted her head. "It is still singing to them. It does not know they are finished."

* * *

Brannoc did not sleep again. He sat with the axe and watched the mouths move, and he thought about the toll-shrine, and the memory he had paid there — his wedding day, Maren in the orchard with blossom in her dark hair, the whole of it lifted out of him like a splinter drawn by a careful hand — and he thought: *it started at the shrine. It started taking from me at the shrine, and I walked into its front hall and made a fire.*

He thought about the tune he'd caught himself humming on the third night, some melody he didn't know, that had felt familiar anyway.

He did not tell the others. Not yet. But he moved his bedroll so that his back was to the fire and his face was to the statues, because whatever came for them in this chapel, it would not come from behind.

Near dawn, the hymn changed.

It was subtle. A shift in the braid, one thread pulling loose — or rather, one thread turning, the way a head turns. Brannoc heard it before he understood it, and by the time he understood it his whole body had gone cold and still, the way it went cold and still on scree slopes when the mountain shifted above you and you had one breath to decide which way to run.

The hymn had stopped being a hymn.

It had become an address.

The eleven stone faces were still aimed at the empty air above the altar. But the singing — the singing had turned. It had come around, the way a searchlight comes around, and now it lay across the three of them like a hand. Across Brannoc most of all. He felt it pass over Silas like wind over grass; he felt it slide off Ash like rain off slate. But on him it *settled.* It pressed. It listened to him listening to it.

And the tenth statue from the left — the small one, the one he had privately thought of as the youngest, a figure no taller than a child — turned its head.

Stone screamed. That was the worst part, Brannoc thought later; not the movement, but the sound, the grinding shriek of a neck that had not moved in five hundred years moving anyway, dust sifting from its jaw, its open mouth yawning wider as if to drink more air, more song, more *voice* — and its eyes, its carved, aimless, ancient eyes, came around and found him.

Found him. Held him.

And in the ember-light, with the storm dying outside and the hymn pouring out of eleven stone throats in a language that predated every kingdom he had ever guided travelers through, Brannoc understood — with the flat, total certainty of a man reading his own death in a cornice of snow — that this was not a ruin, and not a warning, and not a myth.

It was a *choir.* It had always been a choir. It had been singing since before the mountains had names, patient as ice, and it was missing voices, and one of its voices had walked in out of the storm and sat down by its fire and paid a toll at its border and hummed its tune without knowing it.

The statue's mouth moved. No sound came out. But Brannoc heard the words anyway, inside his skull, in a voice made of every voice at once:

*You already know the melody. Sing it back to us.*

His hand found Ash's shoulder. The child was rigid, staring, her small hand rising — and Brannoc, who had spent his life running from nothing and had never once refused a mountain, did the only brave thing left to him.

He stayed silent.

The statue held him with its stone gaze a moment longer. Then the hymn turned slowly back toward the altar, the thread re-braiding itself, and the head ground back around to its original facing, and the chapel was only a chapel again — except that nothing, Brannoc knew, would ever be only anything again.

Dawn came grey through the broken windows. Silas was packing with shaking hands. Ash had not let go of his shoulder.

"It knows us now," the child said quietly. "It let us hear the warning so we would carry it. But it also let the warning hear *you.*" She looked up at him, and then east, toward Thornfell, where the peaks stood black against the paling sky. "We have to move. And Brannoc — " her voice dropped, gentle and terrible, " — you must not hum."

He looked at her. "Why?"

"Because," Ash said, "it already knows the song you would sing."
