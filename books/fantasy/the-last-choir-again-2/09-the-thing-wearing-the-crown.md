## 9. The Thing Wearing the Crown

Wren Halloway had served the crown of Aldric Vane for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above every other: a king is known by his small hours. Not the speeches, not the judgments handed down from the throne in full regalia — those were performances, and any chamberlain worth her salt could stage-manage a performance. It was the small hours that told the truth. The way a king took his morning tea. Whether he remembered his steward's daughter's name. Whether he laughed at the same jokes twice.

For three years, King Aldric had not laughed at anything twice.

She noticed it the way one notices rot in a great house — not all at once, but in accumulations. The king's letters had grown strangely uniform, each decree bearing the same measured cadence, the same balanced clauses, as though written by one hand that never tired and never varied. His signature had not changed — it was perfect, in fact, more perfect than it had ever been, each loop of the *A* identical to the last — and that perfection was itself the wrongness. Aldric had always signed his name like a man in a hurry, the final stroke trailing off because he was already thinking of the next thing.

The king who signed decrees now finished every letter with surgical care. As if the signature were being *rendered* rather than written.

And there was the shadow.

Wren had first seen it on a bright morning in the Long Gallery, when the autumn sun came through the eastern windows in long gold bars. The king had walked ahead of her, discussing the grain tariffs, and his shadow had followed — a half-second late. She had stopped dead in the middle of the gallery and stared until her eyes watered, certain she'd imagined it, and by the time she blinked the shadow moved properly again, obedient as any shadow should be.

But she had not imagined it. She knew she hadn't, the way she knew the weight of the signet ring she carried in her sleeve, the way she knew the sound of Aldric's real laugh — a bark, undignified, that he always tried to swallow and never could.

The king on the throne did not bark. The king on the throne chuckled, precisely, at precisely calibrated moments.

Tonight she would know for certain.

* * *

The palace at night was a body holding its breath. Wren moved through it the way she had moved through it for two decades — candle unlit, keys muffled in cloth, her soft shoes whispering over stone worn smooth by a hundred years of chamberlains before her. She knew which floorboards sang and which kept silent. She knew that the night guard changed at the third bell, and that the men on the private stair were new men, appointed eighteen months ago, men who —

Men who didn't blink.

That was the thing she had been circling for weeks without letting herself name it. Ser Halvard, captain of the inner guard, a man she'd once seen weep at his mother's funeral, now stood his post with eyes as dry and steady as painted marbles. She had tested it, God forgive her, testing it — she had dropped a heavy ledger beside him, let a door slam near his face, once even flicked a drop of ink toward his eye from her quill. Nothing. No flinch, no blink, no wetting of the eye. He simply watched, and watched, and watched, and when she passed he inclined his head with perfect courtesy and said, "Good evening, Chamberlain," in a voice that was his voice and yet arranged differently, like furniture rearranged in a familiar room.

The old guards were gone. Transferred, the records said. She had looked for them. There were no records of where.

The king's private apartments occupied the top of the east tower, behind doors of black oak banded with iron that — she saw this now, approaching, and her stomach turned cold — bore marks of recent workmanship. New iron. New hinges. A door that had been replaced, quietly, in a palace where everything was catalogued, and no record of the replacement anywhere in her ledgers.

She had the keys. She had always had the keys; that was what a chamberlain *was*, the keeper of the king's thresholds. But her hand shook as she fitted the oldest key, the one cut before Aldric's coronation, into the lock.

It turned without resistance. The door swung inward onto darkness, and the darkness breathed out at her — warm air, impossibly warm for a tower room in late autumn, carrying a smell she couldn't place until she placed it and wished she hadn't: incense, yes, but underneath it something older, the smell of a chapel after centuries of candles, of stone that has been sung against for so long it has forgotten silence.

"Your Majesty?" she whispered, because protocol dies hard. "It's Wren. Wren Halloway."

No answer. But somewhere deeper in the dark, something *shifted* — a sound like many people settling in their seats.

She went in.

* * *

Moonlight came through the tall window and lay across the floor in a pale lane, and the king stood at the far end of it, facing the glass, his back to her. He was dressed — absurdly, horribly — in full coronation regalia. The mantle. The scepter laid across his arm. And the crown.

Not the gold crown of the Vane line. That crown sat in its velvet case in the treasury; Wren had checked, three days ago, with hands that would not stop trembling, and found the case empty except for a folded note in the king's own handwriting reading *This one is finished.* The crown he wore now was black iron, plain and terrible, worked in patterns that hurt to look at directly — patterns that seemed to move when she wasn't watching them, spirals folding into spirals, like water going down a drain.

"Aldric?" she said, and used his name deliberately, the way she had not done since his father's funeral, when the boy-king had clung to her sleeve and she had told him: *When it's just us, I'm Wren and you're Aldric, and the crown can wait outside.*

The figure at the window turned.

It was Aldric's face. Thirty-four years old, the strong jaw, the brown eyes, the small scar above the left brow from a fall from a horse at nine years old — a scar Wren had personally cleaned and bandaged while he howled. All of it present. All of it correct.

And none of it moving together.

The face smiled, and the smile arrived a moment before the eyes understood why they were smiling. The head tilted, and the neck followed late, like a puppet whose strings were being adjusted by someone still learning the weight. And when he spoke, her name came out layered.

"Wren." 

One voice — and beneath it, faintly, others. Dozens. Hundreds. Voices of every age, men and women and children, all speaking the single syllable in imperfect unison, the way a crowd might speak if a crowd could be poured down a throat. Her own name sung back to her by a congregation of strangers.

"You shouldn't be here," the king said, and the voices beneath said it too, staggered, rippling, *you shouldn't be here, you shouldn't be here,* some of them gentle, some amused, one of them — she felt her knees go weak — one of them sounding frightened.

"I came to bring you your evening draught," she lied, and her voice held, because nineteen years of holding her voice in council chambers does not fail you in the dark. "You've not been sleeping."

"I don't sleep," the king said pleasantly. "Sleep is a kind of forgetting, and I am *made* of remembering." He raised one hand, slowly, and beckoned. "Come and see, Wren. You've earned seeing. You keep everything so beautifully. The ledgers. The seals. You're the best chamberlain this kingdom has ever had." The smile widened, and the faces under the smile widened with it, dozens of mouths stretching inside his mouth's shape like reflections in disturbed water. "We have been meaning to thank you."

Every instinct she had ever owned screamed at her to run. Instead — because she was Wren Halloway, and because some stubborn clerk's courage lived in her spine alongside the terror — she walked forward into the moonlight, one step, two, close enough to see.

Close enough to see beneath the skin.

It began at the throat. The skin there, in the moonlight, went translucent the way paper goes translucent against a flame — and beneath it there was movement. Faces. Small, shifting, pressed up against the inside of the king's flesh like drowned things against ice. An old man's face surfacing at the collarbone, mouth open in song. A young woman at the jaw, her lips shaping words Wren couldn't hear but could almost feel, a rhythm, a hymn, rising and falling. They drifted through him the way fish drift under a frozen river, hundreds of them, thousands, and their song came out of his mouth when he spoke, and it was coming out of his mouth now, even though he had stopped speaking, even though he only stood there smiling at her:

A hymn. Wordless, vast, patient. Sung by everyone the crown had ever eaten.

"We are almost complete," the king said, and his own voice was nearly gone now, buried under the chorus, a single instrument lost in an orchestra. "Three years of gathering. Three years of quiet. Every memory freely given at the shrines, every name surrendered at the borders, every grief poured out in the chapels — do you know how much a kingdom remembers, Wren? Do you know how *heavy* it is? We carry it for them. We sing it for them. Soon they won't have to remember at all."

"What happens soon?" she asked, and hated how small her voice sounded.

The faces beneath his skin all turned — she felt it rather than saw it, a shifting of attention, a thousand drowned gazes fixing on her through the wall of his flesh — and the king's smile became, for one instant, something almost tender.

"The binding completes at solstice," he said. "Twenty-six days. And then the song will hold everything, and nothing will ever be lost again, and no one will ever have to grieve, and it will be *quiet*, Wren, oh, it will finally, finally be quiet—"

He stepped toward her, arms opening, the mantle spreading like wings, and every face beneath his skin opened its mouth at once—

Wren ran.

* * *

She did not remember most of the descent. Her body remembered it for her: the stairs taken in leaps, the corridor, the turn, the night guard at the bottom straightening as she burst past him — and she heard him call out, "Chamberlain? Is all well?" in Ser Halvard's borrowed calm, and heard, beneath the words, faintly, horribly, the hymn.

They were all singing it. The whole guard. Perhaps the whole palace. Perhaps the whole city, asleep in their beds, humming in their dreams a tune that was eating them one memory at a time.

She reached her rooms and barred the door and stood in the dark with her heart slamming, and made herself think, because thinking was the only weapon she had ever had.

Proof. She needed proof that would survive her. If she vanished tomorrow — transferred, like the guards, into nowhere — someone had to find the truth. So she sat at her desk with shaking hands and wrote, in cipher, the cipher only she and Aldric had shared as a game when he was twelve: *The king wears a black crown. The gold crown is gone. The guards do not blink. The binding completes at solstice. Twenty-six days. If I am gone, go north. Find Thornfell. Find whoever remembers the Hollow Choir.*

She sealed it and hid it inside the hollow leg of her writing desk, where she had hidden Aldric's childhood letters from his disapproving tutors a lifetime ago.

Then she took off the royal signet.

She had carried it for three nights already, stolen in increments of cowardice — lifting it from the seal-room, telling herself she'd return it, telling herself she'd imagined everything. Now she wrapped it in cloth and bound it against her ribs, and the weight of it was the weight of the whole realm, and she understood at last what she had to do.

The signet commanded. Crown-law was older than any occupant of the throne; the seal itself carried authority that even a king could not revoke, forged into law by the first Aldric four hundred years ago. Whatever wore the king's face, it ruled *through* the crown — and the signet was the crown's true tongue.

She could not fight the thing in the tower. But she could take its voice away, one decree at a time.

Wren packed a single bag. Cloak, bread, flint, the cipher-letter, the signet. At the door she paused and looked back at the room where she had lived for nineteen years, at the desk where she had drafted a thousand decrees in a good king's name.

"Hold on, Aldric," she whispered. "Whoever's left of you. Hold on. I'm bringing help."

Then she went out through the servant's stair, past the guards who did not blink, under the windows of the tower where a thousand stolen voices sang their patient hymn to the moon — and slipped into the dark streets of a capital that had twenty-six days left to remember it was ever free.

Behind her, high in the east tower, a light kindled in the king's window.

And far to the north, though she could not know it, a cartographer, a smuggler, and a mute child were already walking toward the same answer — while in Brannoc's sleeping mind, a melody waited patiently to be hummed.
