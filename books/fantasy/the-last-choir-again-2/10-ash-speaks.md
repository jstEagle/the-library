## 10. Ash Speaks

Ash had been counting the storm's heartbeats.

That was not what a child was supposed to do — she knew that now, knew it from watching Brannoc swear at the tent poles and Silas press her palm flat against the ground to feel for water. Children counted sheep, or stars, or the days until some small festival. They did not count the intervals between lightning-flashes the way one counted the beats of a heart that might stop.

But Ash had never been a child, not exactly. She had been *made* into the shape of one, folded down and down until something vast fit inside something small, the way Silas folded her maps so that an entire kingdom could hide in a coat pocket. And the storm outside the tent — the first true storm of their climb into Thornfell's skirts — had a rhythm underneath its thunder that she recognized the way you recognize your own name spoken in another room.

*It knows I'm here,* she thought. *The Choir has finally noticed what it lost.*

The fire in the little iron brazier burned blue again. It had been doing that for three nights now, whenever Ash hummed, and she had stopped humming when Brannoc began to look at her sideways, his hand drifting toward the hatchet he kept beneath his bedroll even in sleep. She did not blame him. The blue light was beautiful and wrong, like frost blooming on a window in summer, and everything beautiful and wrong in this world belonged, in the end, to Thornfell.

She looked across the fire at Silas.

Silas was working, of course. She was always working — mending a torn seam of map-canvas by lamplight, her fingers quick and sure, her mouth set in that line of stubborn concentration that Ash had spent weeks learning to read the way other people read weather. There were things about Silas Nystrom that no lexicon could translate: the way she touched the child-drawn maps Ash left on the table as if they might bite; the way she said *Ash* — the name she had given her, out of nothing but kindness and convenience, ash like burnt wood, ash like what remains — and how, every time, something in Ash's borrowed chest ached.

*I chose her,* Ash reminded herself. *I walked out of the dark and knocked on her door because of what her brother wrote. But I stayed because—*

Because of moments like this one. Because Silas had cut the apple into thirds without being asked, though Brannoc pretended to be asleep. Because when Ash shivered in the night, Silas moved closer in her sleep, unconsciously, the way a hearth radiates heat without deciding to. Because Silas talked to her constantly — one-sided conversations, questions asked of silence — and had never once made her feel that her silence was a failure rather than a wound.

Tonight, Ash decided. Tonight the storm would shake the truth loose, or she would shake it loose herself.

The thunder arrived like a falling mountain. The tent canvas snapped. And Ash opened her mouth, and for the first time since she had crawled mud-soaked onto the doorstep in Vellum's Rest, she spoke the language of the living.

"Silas."

* * *

The word came out cracked and rusted, a hinge unused for years. It was barely a sound at all. But it crossed the space between them, and Silas's needle stopped mid-stitch.

She didn't look up at first. Ash watched her go very still in the particular way she did when something frightened her — not flinching, not pulling away, just becoming absolutely present, the way a deer becomes part of the forest. Then, slowly, she raised her eyes over the lamp flame.

"Ash?" Her voice was careful. Careful the way a bridge is careful over ice. "Did you—"

"Say it again," Ash whispered. "My name. Please. I need to hear it while I still remember why it matters."

Silas set down the needle and the canvas. She set down the lamp. She came around the fire on her knees, slow, giving the child time to flee if she needed to, and took Ash's cold hands in her warm ones, and said:

"Ash. Your name is Ash. You are safe, and you are here, and you are with me."

Something broke open inside her then — not painfully, not entirely, but the way river ice breaks in spring, in great groaning plates that reveal black water underneath. Ash heard herself make a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and both of them stared at each other across the firelight, two exiles from different countries meeting at last on shared ground.

"How long," Silas said. Her thumbs moved over Ash's knuckles, steadying herself as much as the child. "How long have you been able to speak?"

"I don't know how to answer that." Ash's voice strengthened word by word, like a muscle remembering itself. "Hollowtongue isn't a language you learn, Silas. It's a language you're *made of*. Every word I said to you in those first weeks — every word I've said since — cost me something. A piece of whatever I am. Hollowtongue spends the speaker." She smiled, and knew it must have been a strange smile, old and young at once. "You should have heard how much it hurt to say *river keeps moving*. That took almost everything I had left. I thought I'd have nothing for this."

"For what?" Silas asked. But Ash could see in her face that some part of her already knew; she was a cartographer, after all, and cartographers learn early that the shape of a coastline tells you where the sea has been.

"This," Ash said, and squeezed her hands. "The truth. All of it. You've earned all of it, and I've run out of reasons to be a coward about it."

Brannoc stirred in his corner, and Ash saw him surface from sleep the way he always did — instantly, silently, hand finding the hatchet — and then see the two of them kneeling together in the blue-lit tent, and settle back with the wary patience of a man who had learned that the strange child was the least dangerous thing on this road. He did not sleep again. He listened. Ash let him. He would need to know too.

"Three years ago," Ash began, "a king put on a crown that was waiting for him."

* * *

Outside, the storm climbed the mountain toward them. Inside, Ash told the story of her own birth, which was also a death.

"He wasn't a foolish king," she said. "Everyone will tell you Aldric was proud, and pride opens doors, but it wasn't that. His kingdom was grieving. Plague in the western valleys, a war he hadn't wanted, his queen dead two winters before. And Thornfell sang to him. That's what the Choir does — it doesn't attack, it *comforts*. It sings you the memory of every good day you've ever had, woven together, until you'd trade anything to live inside the song forever. And King Aldric, who could not sleep for grief, put on a crown of black iron because it promised him rest."

Silas's hands had gone still around hers. In the shadows, Brannoc sat up fully now, his scarred face lit from below.

"The crown ate his memories," Ash went on. "Not all at once. First his dreams — you can't dream when something else is dreaming for you. Then the small things. Names of servants. The taste of foods he loved. His daughter's face — he wakes some mornings now and looks at her and knows only that she is someone he must love, and cannot remember why, and that is a cruelty I don't have words for even in Hollowtongue." Her voice caught. She pushed through. "And as it ate, it grew. The Choir is old, Silas. Older than the kingdom. Older than the mountains pretending to hold it up. It has worn other crowns before this one — there are bones under Thornfell that used to rule. Each time, it eats a realm's memories until the realm forgets itself, and a country that forgets its own story is a country that stops resisting. It doesn't conquer. It makes conquest unnecessary. It makes everyone *agreeable*."

"The toll-shrine," Brannoc rumbled. "The memory we paid to pass. That was it leaking south."

"That was it reaching," Ash corrected softly. "Leaking implies accident. This is appetite. At the winter solstice — twenty-two days from now — the binding completes. The crown will stop wearing the king and start wearing the *realm*. Every mind from Vellum's Rest to the southern sea, singing one song, remembering one story — the Choir's story. Everyone alive will still walk and work and smile. They just won't be anyone anymore."

Silence, except the rain. Ash watched Silas absorb it — watched the cartographer's mind do what it always did, turning horror into terrain, marking distances, calculating routes. Twenty-two days. Two hundred miles of unmapped fold. One mute child.

Except not mute. Not a child.

"And you," Silas said quietly. "What are you, Ash? Truly?"

Ash took a breath. This was the hardest part, harder than the Choir, harder than the solstice. Because this was the part where she stopped being the mysterious messenger and became something smaller and more breakable: a fragment. A leftover. A thing that existed only because it had failed to be devoured.

"When the crown began to eat the king," she said, "there was one piece of him it couldn't swallow. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Because that piece had already given itself away." Ash's eyes stung, and she let them. "The deepest part of Aldric — the part that loved his daughter, the part that remembered his queen's laugh, the part that knew what he was for — that part felt the Choir coming, years ago, in dreams, before the crown ever touched his head. And it hid itself. It hid itself the only place a soul can hide from a thing that eats memories." She looked down at her own small, mud-stained hands. "It hid itself in a body that had no memories to steal. It made me. Or I made myself out of it. I'm not sure the difference matters. I am the piece of King Aldric that the Choir could not swallow — sent out of the palace three years ago in the shape of an orphan girl who speaks a dead language, to find the one person in the world who could read the old maps back into the world."

The fire popped. Blue light washed over all their faces.

"You knocked on my door," Silas said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone somewhere far away and very close at once. "Out of every cartographer in every town in the lowlands. You knocked on mine."

"Not every cartographer charts realms between the folds of the world," Ash said. "Not every cartographer was taught by Maerin Solvane, whose lexicons are the last living record of Hollowtongue. Not every cartographer—" She stopped. Steadied herself. "Your brother left me a message, Silas. That's why I found you. That's why I *chose* you."

* * *

The name landed in the tent like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples went out through all of them.

"Tomas." Silas said it the way people say names they have forbidden themselves to say aloud for ten years. Her hands withdrew from Ash's slowly, folding into her sleeves, into herself. "Tomas Nystrom. My brother walked into Thornfell a decade ago with a satchel of blank vellum and a theory about folding valleys, and he never walked out. I burned his letters. I sold his instruments. I built a shop on the far side of the border specifically so I would never have to look at those mountains." Her jaw tightened. "And you're telling me he left a message."

"In the only place I could carry it." Ash touched her own temple. "Memories pass through me strangely. I don't keep them the way you do — I *am* kept memories, all of me, so others can lodge in me like birds in a reed bed. When Tomas found the Hollow Court — and he found it, Silas, he got further than any living person — he understood what he'd discovered and understood he couldn't leave. So he caught me. I was passing through the folds near Thornfell, small and formless, barely anything yet. He poured everything into me that mattered. A warning. A method. A map — drawn not on paper, where the Choir reads everything, but in song, in memory, where only I could hold it."

"Why you?" Brannoc asked roughly. "Why not come out himself? Why not send word through some smuggler, some pilgrim—"

"Because anything with a mouth, the Choir can make sing," Ash said. "Anything with a past, the Choir can read. But a soul-fragment with no history, no name, no voice — I passed through its borders like light through glass. It couldn't see me. It still can't, quite. That's why the fire burns blue instead of black when I sing — it hears something, senses the hole in the world where I should belong, but it can't find the edges of me." She turned back to Silas. "He made me memorize one sentence above everything else. Before the map, before the method. One sentence, and he made me repeat it until it wore a groove in whatever I am. Do you want to hear it?"

Silas nodded. She could not speak.

Ash reached across the space between them and took Silas's hands again — and this time Silas let her — and she spoke the sentence in Common, slowly, in a dead man's cadence, a decade-old voice speaking through a child's mouth in a storm-lit tent:

"*If my sister comes — and she will come, because she is too stubborn to stay away — tell her I'm sorry, and tell her not to follow me. And then tell her where the cache is anyway, because she never once in her life did what I told her.*"

The sound Silas made was not crying and not laughter but some animal sound between them, ten years deep. She bowed forward until her forehead nearly touched their joined hands, and her shoulders shook, and Ash held on, and Brannoc turned his face tactfully toward the tent wall and pretended the rain required his full attention.

"He was right," Silas managed at last, wet and furious and tender all at once. "About the last part. He was right about the last part."

"He was right about all of it," Ash said gently. "He knew you'd come. He knew the Choir would try to use you — it already offered him the same bargain it will offer you, Silas. It offered him you, alive, if he stopped digging. He refused. That refusal is the reason he's still in there at all — still himself enough, in whatever cell they keep him, to have planned all this before he was taken. The counter-song. The disruption. He worked it out from inside the Court, and he hid it in me, and I have carried it for ten years looking for the stubborn sister he described."

She paused. The storm had begun its long retreat down the valley, thunder moving away like a herd of departing animals, and in the new quiet Ash felt something she had not felt since the palace: fear of a different kind. Not the fear of being found. The fear of what comes after being found.

"There's more," she said. "The last thing he gave me. I've saved it, because it isn't instructions. It isn't strategy." She swallowed. "It's a location. A cache, buried in the high fold below the Frozen Chapel — paper and ink and a finished map. He called it the map of the Hollow Court. Every corridor, every ward, every weakness he found. And a letter. He said—" Her voice wavered, and she let it. "He said, *give her the letter last, after she's decided whether to turn back. Because the letter is begging her to turn back, and I want her to read it knowing she chose.*"

Silas raised her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry now, and behind them Ash could see the cartographer working — the whole landscape of her grief suddenly gridded, surveyed, converted into a route with a beginning and an end. Twenty-two days. A cache below the chapel. A court of silence. A brother, alive, hollow-eyed, waiting.

"Then we don't have time to sit here listening to storms," Silas said hoarsely. She stood, already reaching for her satchel, already the woman who charted impossible places. "First light. We move for the chapel."

But as she bent to bank the fire, Ash caught her sleeve, and Silas stopped, and looked down, and waited.

"One more thing," Ash said. "The last thing he said to me, when he finished pouring everything in. He held my face — these face, this face — and he said: *she'll take one look at you and feed you first, before herself, before anyone. That's who she is. Make her promise not to.*" Ash's grip tightened. "So I'm asking you to promise, Silas. Whatever the Choir offers. Whatever the song costs. Promise me you won't pay with yourself."

For a long moment Silas said nothing. The firelight moved on her face, and Ash — who was made of memories, who could read a soul like contour lines — watched her consider the promise, and watched her understand that she could not honestly make it, and watched her decide to lie anyway.

"I promise," Silas said.

Ash smiled sadly, and let her believe it, and outside the retreating storm the first pale suggestion of dawn was gathering over the black teeth of Thornfell — and somewhere in the high folds below the Frozen Chapel, a dead man's map lay waiting in the dark, twenty hours' hard climbing away.
