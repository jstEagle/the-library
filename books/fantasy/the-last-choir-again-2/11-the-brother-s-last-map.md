## 11. The Brother's Last Map

The song led them north.

Ash walked at the front of the line with her small hand raised, palm open, as though she were reading something written in the air itself, and perhaps she was. Brannoc had stopped asking questions three days ago, which Silas took as a mark of either wisdom or surrender. The smuggler's road had long since given way to no road at all — a slope of scree and frost-bitten grass where the mountains of Thornfell shouldered up against the sky like old men leaning in to whisper.

The melody Ash hummed was not the Choir's tune. Silas had learned that much by now, the way you learn the difference between a snake and its shed skin. The Choir's music pulled; this one *pointed*. It moved in intervals that shouldn't have resolved but did, and each time Ash reached the end of a phrase she would pause, tilt her head, and turn slightly — always slightly north, always slightly upward — before beginning again.

"It's his," Silas said quietly, on the second morning. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "The melody. He used to whistle it while he inked coastlines."

Brannoc looked at her over the fire he was coaxing out of wet pine. "You said your brother vanished ten years ago."

"Ten years, four months, and eleven days." She hadn't meant to be precise about it either. Precision was just grief wearing a surveyor's coat. "He whistled when he worked. Drove our mentor half mad. Said maps made without music came out flat."

"And now a child who is possibly a piece of a king's soul is singing it back to you across a mountain range."

"Yes."

Brannoc nodded slowly, feeding another branch to the flames. "Right," he said, in the tone of a man deciding not to think about something until it killed him.

* * *

They found the cairn at noon on the third day, in a fold of the valley so narrow that the sun only touched it for an hour, and they arrived inside that hour as if the light had been waiting for them.

Ash stopped humming.

She stood very still before a heap of stones stacked against the cliff face — ordinary stones, gray and lichen-crusted, indistinguishable from a hundred others they'd passed. But Silas's eye, trained on the grammar of landscapes, caught what the casual gaze would miss: the stack was too regular. Stones don't fall into courses. Stones don't have corners.

She crossed the scree with her heart hammering somewhere up near her teeth and laid her hands on the topmost rock. It came away easily, deliberately, on a pivot some mason or mapmaker had ground smooth. Beneath it, set into a cavity lined with oilcloth, lay an iron box the size of a bread loaf, its lock green with age but its hinges freshly — impossibly — unworn.

Ten years in the mountain, and the hinges gleamed.

"Ash," Silas said. Her voice came out strange. "Did you know this was here?"

The child looked up at her with those eyes that held too much weather, and shook her head slowly. Then she pointed at the box, and at Silas, and pressed her small fist over her own heart.

*He left it for you,* the gesture said. Or maybe: *It waited.*

Silas knelt in the cold mud and broke the seal.

* * *

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen gone amber with time: a map.

Not a sketch. Not a working draft. A finished piece, executed in Tomas Nystrom's unmistakable hand — the fine confident linework, the marginal annotations in his cramped sideways script, the little flourishes he couldn't stop himself from adding even though their mentor had beaten the habit out of every other student. A compass rose drawn as a choir of tiny singing faces. A border of interlocking musical notation instead of latitude lines.

And at its center, rendered in ink that still seemed wet, still seemed to *breathe*: the Hollow Court.

Silas had spent her whole life charting places that didn't want to be found — realms folded between the folds, rivers that moved out of spite, islands that existed only on Tuesdays. She had thought herself beyond astonishment. She knelt there in the dirt with her brother's masterwork in her hands and discovered she had never once understood what fear was.

The palace was built of silence. He'd shown it that way — not drawn walls but drawn *absences*, negative space shaped like corridors, hatching that didn't mark shadow but marked sound dying mid-air. The throne room sat at the heart like a black pupil in a white eye. Around it, in concentric rings, he had mapped the Choir's reach: the chapel of stone singers, the toll-shrine, the frozen hymn-corridors, all the stations of a kingdom-sized throat swallowing downward.

And in the margins, in the cramped sideways script she knew better than her own handwriting, the notes:

*The crown is not worn. It wears.*

*The binding completes at solstice. I was nine days late. Forgive me.*

*Do not follow me. Whoever finds this — do NOT follow me. The map will show you the way because that is what maps do, and it is a liar, and so am I, because part of me wants to be found and that part is not me anymore.*

*Tell Silas—*

The sentence stopped there. There was no more room on that margin, and he had never turned over to continue it, and Silas sat in the freezing hour-light of a hidden valley holding ten unfinished words and felt something in her chest crack along a fault line she'd been pretending wasn't there for a decade.

*Tell Silas.*

Tell her what? That he was sorry? That he was alive? That he wasn't? Ten years she had built her whole careful life around the shape of that absence — the shop in Vellum's Rest chosen because it was far from Thornfell, the commissions refused that smelled of mountain work, the river she charted night after night because a moving river couldn't leave you the way people did.

Tell her *what*, Tomas.

* * *

Brannoc gave her space, which she appreciated, and then gave her soup, which she appreciated less but ate anyway. Night came down into the valley like a lid. Ash slept curled against Silas's side, breathing the small steady breaths of someone who had never learned to have nightmares, and Silas sat by the fire with the map spread across her knees and read her brother's hand until the letters swam.

The fury came first. It always did. Fury was easier — fury had directions, fury could be aimed. Ten years of silence and the man had left instructions. *Do not follow me.* As if he got to decide. As if he'd gotten to decide anything after walking into Thornfell with his beautiful maps and his whistling and his certainty that he could chart the one place that eats cartographers. He'd been arrogant. He'd been brilliant and arrogant and he'd left her a masterpiece and a commandment in the same breath, and both of them were lies, because the third thing in the box —

The third thing was the note.

A separate page, folded smaller, softer paper, written later. She could tell by the pen pressure — heavier, shakier, the script of a man writing fast in bad light with cold hands.

*I know you, little sister. You're reading this, which means you ignored everything above, which means you're already halfway up the mountain, which means I love you and I hate myself in roughly equal measure.*

*Here is what I couldn't put in the margins where anyone might read it. The Choir offered me a bargain on my ninth day. It offers everyone a bargain; that's how it feeds. Mine was this: walk away, live, forget — and it would leave Vellum's Rest unmapped. Unnoticed. Ungathered. Your streets, your customers, your stubborn face behind that ridiculous counter. All of it outside its appetite forever, if I never spoke of what I saw.*

*I signed. God help me, I signed. And then I stayed anyway, because I am a coward in the large ways and apparently a fool in the final ones. They caught me in the throne room trying to sing the counter-song alone. Alone, Silas. Write that on my grave: he tried to do it ALONE.*

*It needs many voices. Imperfect ones. That's the whole secret, the one I stole and am paying for now — the counter-song isn't a composition, it's a congregation. Every voice at once, wrong notes and all. The Choir is perfect unity, and perfection has exactly one note, and a single note cannot survive a chord.*

*I'm telling you this because if you've come this far, you'll go the rest of the way, and I would rather arm you than mourn you from whatever distance they leave me.*

*One more thing. If a child finds you — mute, dark-eyed, older than she looks — she's real. She's the piece of me they couldn't swallow. Not mine — the king's. Protect her. She is the only true map any of us will ever make.*

*Don't forgive me. Just finish it.*

*T.*

Silas read it twice. Then she did the thing she had not done since the day the search parties came back empty: she put her face in her hands and wept, silently and thoroughly, the way you drain a wound, while the fire burned blue at its edges because Ash, asleep, was dreaming of home.

* * *

Grief and fury are twins who hate each other, and they fought in her all night.

By dawn, fury had the advantage. Fury said: he signed the bargain. He traded your town for his life and then preened about his own courage in a private letter where no one could contradict him. Fury said: *do not follow me* — who does he think he is, issuing edicts from inside the enemy's mouth? Fury said, most dangerously: turn back. Take the child south. Let the kingdom keep its hollow king; the Choir never wanted Vellum's Rest anyway, thanks to Tomas's precious signature, and you could live out your days in the one safe street in the realm, charting your moving river, safe and small and spared.

It was a good argument. It was airtight. It had the elegant, terrible logic of every map she had ever refused to draw.

And underneath it, quiet as groundwater: *he tried to do it alone.* That was the sentence that undid her. Not the bargain, not the arrogance, not even *tell Silas* hanging there unfinished like a bridge to nowhere. That. Because she recognized it. It was her own handwriting. Ten years of solitary nights, ten years of refusing every traveling companion, every guild partnership, every offer of help — she had built her entire craft and her entire life into an instrument designed to be played by one hand, and she had told herself it was discipline, it was rigor, it was the price of precision, when all along it was just Tomas's lesson repeated: go alone, need no one, and lose beautifully.

Ash woke at first light, studied Silas's face with the unnerving directness of children and kings, and asked — in Hollowtongue, soft — a question Silas couldn't parse but understood anyway.

"I'm going on," Silas said. "If that's what you're asking."

The child nodded once, gravely, and then did something new: she reached out and touched the map where the black throne room pulsed at its center, and then touched Silas's sternum, directly over the fault line.

*In here too,* the gesture said. *We carry it in here too.*

"Sentimental," Silas muttered, wiping her face with her sleeve. "You're eight. You shouldn't know to do that."

* * *

They struck camp as the sun cleared the eastern ridge and lit the upper slopes gold. Brannoc rolled the rope in silence, glancing between Silas and the map with the tact of a man who had learned which questions cost too much.

"The throne room," he said finally. "His map shows a route in. Through the frozen chapel, then down the hymn-corridors."

"There's a flaw in it." Silas traced the line with one finger. "Tomas drew the approach as a single path. One road, one traveler. It's the same mistake in every version — his, and apparently mine." She began, carefully, rolling the map around a stave of ash wood. "But the counter-song needs many voices. So we're not going to take his road. We're going to make our own."

Brannoc snorted. "Fold space through an unmapped valley, you mean. Again."

"If necessary."

"You know what the fold showed me last time."

"I know," Silas said gently. "I know what it showed all of us."

Above them, the peaks of Thornfell stood white against a sky the color of unmarked parchment, and somewhere past them, in a palace built of silence, a man wearing a crown that wore him back was counting the days toward solstice.

Ash took the lead. Before she started humming, she turned and looked back at Silas, and for the first time since Vellum's Rest, she smiled — small, crooked, startlingly human.

Then she sang the next phrase of the memory-song, and it was not pointing north anymore.

It was pointing *down*.
