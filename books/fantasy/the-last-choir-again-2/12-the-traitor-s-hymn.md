## 12. The Traitor's Hymn

Brannoc had not sung since the shrine.

He knew this the way a man knows he has stopped bleeding — not by watching the wound, but by the strange quiet of it. For thirty years his voice had been his trade as much as his feet. He had sung rope-hauling songs on the switchbacks above Greywater, dirges at the graves of clients he'd failed to bring home, bawdy verses to keep terrified merchants from bolting on the icefields. His wife, Maren, used to say he hummed in his sleep like a kettle left on the banked coals. She said she always knew which season it was by what he hummed. In winter, the low ones. The old mountain tunes that had no names because they were older than names.

So when Silas shook him awake in the grey hour before dawn, her hand hard on his shoulder and her face white as the frost on the tent canvas, his first thought — absurd, unbidden — was *Maren knew I was humming again.*

"Brannoc." Her whisper came out ragged. "Wake up. Wake up, damn you."

He woke. And heard what she had heard.

It was coming out of his own mouth.

A melody, thin and sweet and wrong, rising from his throat while his jaw hung slack and his eyes — he felt them, he could feel them rolling beneath lids that would not obey — moved beneath closed skin. The tune was one he did not know, and yet his body knew it perfectly, every interval falling where it should, the way his boots knew a scree slope. It was the song Ash sometimes made without meaning to, the one that turned firelight blue. But where Ash's version sounded like a child picking out something remembered, this was fluent. This was *practiced.* This was a hymn sung by something that had been singing it for centuries and had merely borrowed his throat for a verse.

He tried to stop. That was the horror of it — he tried, and the song went on, and somewhere inside him a small cold voice observed with perfect calm: *you cannot stop breathing either.*

Then it ended. Not because he willed it. Because it was finished.

His eyes opened. Dawn light, grey and thin, filtered through the canvas. Silas crouched over him with a skinning knife held low against her thigh, not raised, not threatening, but *there* — and the fact that she'd thought to bring it told him everything about how long she'd been listening. Behind her shoulder, framed in the tent flap, Ash stood watching him with those enormous dark eyes, and the child's expression was not fear. It was grief. It was the look you give a house burning down that you grew up in.

"Say your name," Silas said quietly.

"What?"

"Say it. Now. Out loud."

"Brannoc Vael. Of the Thornfell side, of the Greywater guild, disgraced, indebted, and — " his voice cracked, and he hated it, hated how it shook — "and very much awake, thank you."

Silas exhaled through her nose. The knife did not go away, but it lowered. "You were singing," she said. "In your sleep. The whole night, near enough. Ash heard it first. Woke me an hour ago." A muscle worked in her jaw. "The toll-shrine. What memory did you give it?"

"I told you. My wedding day."

"You told me what you chose to tell me." She sat back on her heels, and he saw her make herself put the knife away — saw the deliberate motion, fingers stiff with cold or something colder. "Shrines like that don't take what you offer, Brannoc. They take what you *value*, and let you believe you chose. You gave it your wedding day. And it reached deeper and took the song under the wedding day. The song Maren loved. Every song you've ever carried." She looked away, out at the frost-hardened camp. "It didn't take them. It *copied* them. Left the shape behind and filled it with its own music. That's how it works — I found it in my mentor's notes last night, after Ash translated the chapel warning. Voices, not bodies. It hollows a voice and wears it."

The words landed in him like stones dropped into a well, each one striking deeper than the last. He lay there in his bedroll feeling suddenly like a borrowed coat. "So I'm — what? Its instrument now? It plays me whenever it likes?"

"I don't know." Honesty, at least. He'd give her that; she'd never once lied to soften a thing. "Ash?"

The child came forward into the tent and knelt beside him, close enough that he could see the faint tracery of pale scarring along her temple — the mark he'd noticed their second week together and never asked about, because some questions you save until you're sure you want the answers. She studied his face with that unnerving stillness of hers, then pressed two small fingers against his throat, just below the jaw, where a man's pulse lives.

She flinched. Drew her hand back as if the skin had burned her.

Then she spoke — in Hollowtongue, rapid and distressed, her hands moving too, sketching shapes in the air: a circle broken, a thread pulled taut, a door half-open.

"She says," Silas translated slowly, and her voice had gone very careful, "that there is a thread in you. Anchored. Running south and east, toward Thornfell, toward the Court. She says it isn't cut. She says — " Silas stopped. Started again. "She says the Choir can pull the thread whenever it wants. And when it pulls, you'll sing whether you're asleep or awake, whether you consent or not."

The silence in the tent was long enough to freeze.

*Well,* Brannoc thought, lying flat on his back staring at the sagging canvas overhead. *There it is.* Thirty years of guiding people through mountains where things lived that polite maps refused to acknowledge, and he had always believed — had built his whole shabby redemption on believing — that he was the one who knew the dangers. The one who stood between the soft folk and the dark. Now the dark had hold of him by the throat, literally, and he was lying in his own bedroll like cargo.

"So that's it," he said aloud. His voice sounded flat and far away to him. "You can't trust me on the mountain anymore. One bad night and I'm leading you off a cliff singing choir-music. Or worse — I walk us straight to its front door and call it a shortcut." He laughed, and the laugh came out ugly. "Gods. All those years I judged the guides who cracked. Who took the Choir's coin, who came back wrong. I used to say, *not me, I'd die first.*" He looked at Silas. "I'd like to revise that statement."

Silas didn't smile. But something in her face shifted — the wariness settling into something more like weight. "No one's deciding anything yet," she said. "Eat something. We'll talk when the sun's properly up."

She left the tent. Ash lingered a moment longer. Then, in a gesture that undid him more than anything else that morning, the child reached out and patted his hand twice, gently, the way you'd steady a frightened horse, and followed Silas out.

*

They gathered around the fire at midmorning, all three of them, and the discussion was exactly as brutal as Brannoc expected, because Silas Nystrom did not believe in kind discussions.

"The options are these." She counted on her fingers, brisk as a merchant tallying debt. "One: we bind him. There are rites in Tomas's notes — crude ones, but they'd seal his voice. Literally seal it. He couldn't speak above a whisper for weeks, maybe months. Maybe ever, if the binding scarred wrong."

"Muteness," Brannoc said. "For a guide whose job is shouting warnings."

"It wouldn't stop the thread," Ash signed, and Silas voiced it. "It would only mute the bell. The rope stays attached."

"Two," Silas continued, "we leave him here. Camp, supplies, wait for our return. Safe, but alone, in country that's already sprouting shrines."

"And three?" Brannoc asked, though he already knew, because he'd watched her face while she laid out the first two and seen how little conviction lived behind either.

"Three," Silas said, "we trust him. We accept that the enemy has a hand on him, we watch each other, and we use it. Because Ash says the thread runs both ways — and if it does, then whatever sings through Brannoc can be *listened through*. He becomes a wire into the Court's own house." Her eyes met his across the fire. "But that only works if he wants it to. If any part of him is willing, the thread can't steer him clean. Willingness is grit in the mechanism. Tomas wrote that. Before he — " She stopped. Everyone in that camp knew better than to finish sentences about Tomas.

Brannoc stared into the fire. Blue-edged, even now, even without Ash humming — he'd only just realized that. Since the chapel, their fires had never quite gone back to orange. He wondered if the world was doing that everywhere, or only around them.

"I want to say yes," he said finally. "That's the problem, isn't it? A smart traitor says exactly that. *Trust me, I want to be trusted.* The Choir's clever enough to make me say it. Clever enough to make me mean it." He rubbed his face, feeling the stubble and the frost-burn and the exhaustion of a man who had spent the night being played like a fiddle. "How do you decide? How does anyone decide, when the thing wearing your friend might be the thing asking you to untie it?"

"We don't decide," said a new voice, hoarse and cracking at the edges, from the treeline. "I do. I cast the deciding vote."

*

She came stumbling out of the pines like something the storm had chewed up and spat out — a woman wrapped in a fur-lined cloak crusted with ice, leading a blown horse that looked as though it had been ridden through hell at a trot. Her lips were split. Her hands, when she fumbled back her hood, were blackened at two fingertips with frostbite. But her eyes — dark, fierce, furious eyes — swept the camp once and fixed on Silas with the precision of a hawk that has flown three hundred miles to land on exactly one branch.

"Chamberlain Wren Halloway," the woman rasped, "formerly of the royal household, currently of nowhere at all. Which of you is Nystrom?"

Silas rose slowly. "Depends who's asking."

"Ash sent word. Through the dream-road, through the chapel statues, through whatever cursed method that child uses." Wren Halloway swayed, caught herself on her saddle, and kept talking as though her body were merely an inconvenience attending upon her will. "I have the royal signet. I have proof the king hasn't signed a true decree in three years. I have seen — " and here, for the first time, the iron in her bent, her voice dropping to nearly nothing — "I have seen what's underneath his skin. So before anyone votes on anything, understand: I watched a chorus of dead faces sing through my king's mouth four nights ago, and I ran, and I have been running since, and I am done running. Now." She peeled her gaze from Silas and turned it on Brannoc, taking him in — the bedroll, the way Ash stood close beside him, the guilt written on him plain as a brand. "This is the one with the thread in his throat. Yes. I can see it. Anyone trained in crown-lore can see it, once they know to look." She smiled, and it was a terrible, exhausted, magnificent thing. "Good. Keep it."

Every head in the camp turned.

"Keep it," Wren repeated, limping closer to the fire and lowering herself onto a stone with the groan of a woman whose bones had forgotten warmth. "You want to know why? Because I spent three years serving a thing that had no threads, no cracks, no seams — a perfect vessel, seamless as glass, and do you know what that means? It means nothing could get *in*. Nothing could reach it. No love, no doubt, no plea, no law. Perfection is just another word for sealed." She held her blackened fingertips to the light. "Your friend here is full of holes, Nystrom. Full of grief and debt and disgrace and a dead wife's favorite songs. The Choir thinks those holes are handles. It's wrong. They're doors. And doors open in both directions." She looked at Brancoc directly, and her voice gentled into something almost kind. "You're asking how they can trust you when you might be its creature. Wrong question. Ask instead: how can the Choir possibly keep a grip on a man who *chooses* his captors' company? Choice rots its ropes faster than any rite. Bind him, mute him, leave him — and the thread stays taut forever. Walk with him, feed him, argue with him, let him carry your packs and curse your cooking — and every hour he spends choosing you is an hour the thread frays."

"That's a theory," Silas said. "People die of theories."

"People died of caution in the capital too," Wren snapped. "Three years of cautious, loyal, blink-less guards, and where did it get them?" She drew something from her cloak and held it up: a heavy ring of dark gold, stamped with a sigil Brannoc recognized from proclamations nailed to every market post in the realm — the crowned stag of Aldric. "I stole this from a king who doesn't remember having a daughter. I rode eleven days through snow to find a cartographer and a child because a child sang to me in dreams and told me you were the ones who'd finish this. I did not ride eleven days to arrive at a committee." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "So here is my vote, and I am told it decides. Trust him. Trust him loudly, trust him messily, trust him where the Choir can hear you trusting him. Let that man's loyalty be so loud it drowns their hymn." She sagged slightly, catching herself, and added, quieter: "Besides. I've read the border reports. Whatever happens at solstice, none of us survives it politely. Best to spend our remaining days in decent company."

The wind moved through the pines. The blue fire popped. And Brannoc sat there with tears freezing in his beard before he quite understood he was crying, because no one had spoken of him like that in ten years — not as a liability, not as a debt, not as the guide who lost the Nystrom boy — but as a *door*, as a man whose broken places were worth more than his wholeness.

"Well," he managed, gruffly, scrubbing his face with his sleeve. "Someone hand the lady something hot before she dies on our doorstep. And someone teach her that speeches like that come after supper."

Ash crossed the camp and pressed a warm stone from the fire's edge into Wren's black-tipped fingers, and then — Brannoc watched it happen — the child took his hand in one small grip and Wren's in the other, and joined them, briefly, deliberately, like a knot being tied.

And deep in his throat, in the place where the thread anchored, Brannoc felt something shift. Not loosen — not yet — but strain. As if somewhere far south and east, something ancient leaned against its rope and found, for the first time in centuries, resistance.

That night, he slept without dreaming. But near dawn he woke once to the sound of voices — Silas and Wren, low by the embers, planning — and between their words he heard the mountains themselves, far off to the north, holding a single sustained note like a drawn breath.

Solstice was nine days away. And the Hollow Court had begun, very softly, to count.
