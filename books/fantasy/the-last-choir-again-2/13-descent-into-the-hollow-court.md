## 13. Descent into the Hollow Court

The map did not want to be read.

Silas had felt resistance in charts before — a river that squirmed under her pen, a coastline that redrew itself when no one watched — but Tomas's final map resisted like a living thing held down. The ink seemed to darken as she studied it, the lines of the Hollow Court's approach sinking into the vellum as though ashamed of what they described. She had spread it across her knees in the frozen chapel's lee, Brannoc and Wren huddled close against the wind, and for a long moment none of them spoke.

They didn't need to. Ash was already standing.

The child faced the mountain's northern face, where the rock split into a crevasse no wider than a coffin lid, and hummed. It was the melody from the campfire, the one that turned flame blue, but slower now, weighted, and Silas felt it settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well. The crevasse breathed out air that smelled of old stone and older silence.

"That's not on any survey," Wren said. Her breath came in white ribbons. "I've read every royal atlas twice. There is nothing north of Thornfell but blank vellum."

"Blank is a kind of answer," Silas said. She folded the map along Tomas's creases — his hands had made these folds, she realized, her brother's fingers had pressed these exact lines, and grief arrived on schedule, punctual as a debt collector — and tucked it inside her coat, against her heart, where paper could be kept warm. "My brother drew a door here. Doors don't care whether atlases believe in them."

Brannoc checked the rope at his waist with the grim economy of a man who had stopped believing he would survive things and started merely enduring them. Since the shrine, since the humming in his sleep, he had been quieter than the mountains themselves, and his eyes kept going to Ash with an expression Silas couldn't name — something between fear and apology.

"Rope won't help if what's down there eats sound," he said. "You can't call for help in a place that doesn't listen."

"Then we don't call," Silas said. She looked at Ash. "We follow."

* * *

The descent began as all descents do: with light behind and dark ahead, and the particular loneliness of leaving the sun.

The crevasse swallowed them single file — Brannoc first with his lantern, then Wren, then Silas with one hand on Ash's shoulder, feeling the small bones beneath the coat shift with each step like a bird deciding whether to stay perched. The walls narrowed, then widened into galleries of blue ice shot through with strata of black stone, and Silas, who could not help herself, who had never once in her life been able to look at terrain without reading it, began to understand what they were walking through.

This was not a cave. Caves are carved by water, by patience, by accident. This had been *built* — or grown, or sung — because the walls had rhythm. The black strata repeated at intervals too regular for geology, layer over layer like the staves of an impossible score, and when she pressed her bare fingers against the ice, she felt vibration. Faint. Patient. A note held so long it had become architecture.

"It's a song," she whispered. "The whole mountain is a—"

Her voice died.

Not faded. Not echoed away. *Died*, three feet from her lips, cut off with the clean finality of a blade coming down, and the silence where her words should have continued was worse than any sound she had ever heard. She tried again — said her own name, *Silas*, just to hear it — and watched the word fall out of the air like a bird struck mid-flight. Three feet. Then nothing. The silence didn't echo; it *absorbed*, drinking sound the way sand drinks rain, leaving nothing, not even the memory of having spoken.

Wren's mouth was moving. Silas saw the chamberlain say something urgent, saw Brannoc turn, saw both of them speaking, shouting perhaps — and heard only the mountain's held breath. Panic rose in her throat, the animal kind, the kind that makes people run blindly into dark, and she understood suddenly why the place was built this way. Not to keep intruders out. To keep them *alone*. A palace of silence where every voice dies at its owner's lips, where you cannot warn or comfort or even scream — where the self shrinks to the size of its own skull.

Then Ash spoke.

"*Come.*"

One word, in Common, cracked and small and eight years old — and it carried. It carried the way sunlight carries through cloud, effortlessly, touching everything. Silas heard it not with her ears alone but in her sternum, in her teeth, in the ice under her boots, which rang faintly like a struck bell. The girl's voice moved through the dead air as though the silence had been built around it, as though every wall of black stone had been raised for the sole purpose of giving these syllables somewhere to live.

Ash took Silas's hand. Her palm was warm — impossibly, unnaturally warm, warmer than any child's hand in a frozen mountain should be — and she led them downward, speaking now in the old tongue, Hollowtongue, each word falling through the silence like coins through deep water. Silas couldn't translate it all. But she caught fragments, and the fragments were enough to make her blood slow:

*...the hall of kneeling... ...he sings to them still... ...do not sing back...*

*Do not sing back.*

She looked at Brannoc, walking behind, his lips pressed white. She thought of the tune he'd hummed in his sleep, the toll-shrine's theft, and saw that he had understood the warning too — or feared he had. His hand drifted to his own mouth and stayed there, as if he could hold the music in by force.

* * *

The passage opened without warning, the way a held breath opens.

One moment they were in the narrow dark; the next, the dark simply gave up and became vastness, and Silas stood at the top of a stair that made her knees forget their purpose.

It descended in sweeping arcs into a hall the size of a cathedral's dream — no, larger, larger than reason, a space that should not have fit inside a mountain and yet did, because the Hollow Court obeyed no geometry but its own. Pillars rose from the depths like the trunks of petrified trees, each one carved — no, *grown* — from that same black singing stone, and high above, where a ceiling should have been, there was only darkness moving slowly, like the underside of a frozen sea. The air was warm here. That was the first obscenity. After days of cold that bit to the bone, the Hollow Court welcomed them with a warmth soft as a sickroom, gentle as a lie told kindly.

And it was silent. Perfectly, totally silent — except for the singing.

Silas heard it before she saw its source, and her first thought, shamefully, was *beautiful*. It was beautiful the way an avalanche is beautiful from a distance, the way fire is beautiful to the moth. A single voice, low and vast, layered with itself — one throat producing harmonies that should have required a hundred singers, a chord sustained so long it had become a feature of the air, like gravity or grief. The sound did not die in this place. The sound *was* this place. Every pillar hummed with it. The floor hummed. Silas's own bones hummed, resonating unwillingly, tuning themselves to the note like instruments abandoned by their player.

"There he is," Wren breathed beside her — and the words died at her lips, unheard, but Silas read them, and followed her gaze down.

The king sat upon a throne of black iron at the hall's far end.

Even at this distance, even diminished by scale, Silas knew him — not from portraits, though Vellum's Rest had those, the official likeness of a broad-shouldered man with a beard trimmed by committee — but from something deeper. From the shape of him. He sat the way her brother used to sit at his drafting table, spine curved forward, utterly absorbed, a man in love with his work. Except the king's work was the hall below him, and the hall was full.

Kneeling shadows filled the court from wall to wall.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They knelt in ranks so precise they might have been planted, figures of smoke and absence arranged in perfect rows upon the black floor, and every one of them faced the throne, and every one of them swayed — gently, in unison, in time — to the singing. They had no faces. Silas looked, and kept looking, and found smooth darkness where features should have been, and understood with a lurch that these were not creatures. These were *voices*. Voices that had been taken from throats somewhere in the world and given bodies of shadow to kneel in, gathered here like grain into a granary, stored, hoarded, *kept*.

She thought of the border shrine. Of Brannoc's wedding day, paid like a coin. Of the names leaking south into the lowlands, the memories going thin in villages, the king who couldn't recall his daughter's face. All of it flowed downhill, she realized. All of it flowed *here*.

And the king sang to them. Sang and sang, patient as erosion, his crowned head bowed over his harvest, and the crown —

The crown was wrong. Silas had seen drawings of Aldric's coronet, gold and laurel-work, a thing of celebration. This was black iron, plain and heavy and ancient, forged in a style no kingdom used, and it did not sit upon his head so much as *grow* from it, filaments of the same metal threading back into his hair, his scalp, down the line of his neck like roots into soil. As she watched, the filaments pulsed — faintly, in time with the song — and the shadows below pulsed with them, a tide of kneeling dark rising and falling to the rhythm of one man's stolen voice.

Beside her, Ash made a sound.

It was tiny. A catch of breath, a half-formed syllable, nothing — but in the Court's silence it rang like a dropped chandelier. The singing faltered. For a single heartbeat, the vast chord wavered, lost its footing, and in that gap Silas heard what lay underneath: hundreds of voices, faint and far, crying out from within the sound like drowning people under ice.

The king's head lifted.

Across the whole impossible distance of the hall, across ranks of faceless worshippers, his eyes found them — found *her* — and Silas felt the contact like a hand closing around her wrist. His eyes were clear. That was the horror of it. Not mad, not vacant, not the eyes of a puppet. Clear and brown and human and *pleading*, a man looking out from inside his own theft, and for one suspended moment Silas saw his mouth stop singing and shape something else, two syllables, desperate, aimed down the length of the hall at the four small figures on the stair—

Then the crown tightened. She saw it happen, saw the iron filaments flex like fingers, and the clarity drowned. The king's face smoothed into serenity, the mouth resumed its song, and the chord rolled out over the hall again, vast and beautiful and bottomless, and the shadows swayed, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

But Silas had seen it. And she knew — with the certainty she usually reserved for coastlines, for the true shapes of things beneath their fog — that whatever wore the crown, some part of Aldric was still in there. Still watching. Still begging.

Ash tugged her hand. The child's face was wet, though Silas hadn't heard her cry — of course she hadn't; nothing here was permitted to make a sound — and she pointed down into the hall, at the nearest rank of kneeling shadows, and then at herself, and then pressed her small fist against her chest.

Silas understood, and the understanding went through her like cold iron.

The shadows weren't strangers. Somewhere in those ranks, in that congregation of harvested voices, was a piece of this child. A piece of the king. The Choir had swallowed almost all of him — and what it couldn't swallow had walked out into the world to find a cartographer.

Ash looked up at her, and mouthed the words her voice was too small to carry, and Silas read them off her lips in the terrible quiet:

*He knows we're here.*

Below them, unhurried, the ranks of shadows began — gently, in perfect unison, without a single footfall — to rise.
