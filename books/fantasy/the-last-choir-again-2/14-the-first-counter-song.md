## 14. The First Counter-Song

The Hollow Court did not have doors so much as it had absences — places where the silence thinned and let you through. Silas had learned this in the hours since their capture: that the palace of the Choir was built the way she built maps, by leaving things out. Every corridor was a negative space. Every wall was a memory of a wall, held upright by the sheer insistence of the thing that sang at its heart.

They had put her in a cell of held breath.

That was what Brannoc called it, later, when his voice came back to him — a room where the air itself refused to move, where you could open your mouth and scream and feel the sound die against your own teeth like a moth against glass. She had been left there for a length of time she could not measure, because the Court did not permit clocks or heartbeats to keep honest count. When the absence finally opened, it was not a guard who stood there. It was Ash.

*Come,* the child said, in Hollowtongue, in that voice that was the only sound the place allowed to live. *He wants to hear you try.*

Silas's throat still ached from the first attempt — Tomas's plan, half-remembered from a note written in a hand gone shaky with fear, folded into a cache beneath a cairn of stones on the mountain road. *Sing the map,* her brother had written. *Not about it. The map itself. Every line as it was drawn. The Choir cannot hold what is true and sung at once.* And for nine seconds — she had counted them, nine seconds like nine heartbeats stolen from the world — King Aldric's eyes had cleared.

Nine seconds. Then the Choir had reached into her throat with fingers made of every voice it had ever swallowed, and taken the song out of her the way you'd take a splinter from a wound — quickly, expertly, without mercy — and she had been voiceless for a day while her companions were dragged into cells of held breath and she was left alone with the taste of her own failure.

Now they walked her back toward the throne hall, and Ash's small cold hand was wrapped around two of her fingers, and Silas thought: *I will not sing it alone this time.*

She could not say it aloud. Her voice had come back an hour ago, thin as thread, and she was hoarding it the way a miser hoards coin — but she squeezed the child's hand, and Ash looked up, and something passed between them that needed no language older than kingdoms to carry it.

* * *

The throne hall of the Hollow Court was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful — all its violence locked away beneath a surface you could see straight down through and never touch. Pillars of pale stone rose into darkness that swallowed their tops. The floor was polished to a mirror finish, and when Silas looked down she saw not her reflection but reflections of other people, standing where she stood, in clothes of a dozen centuries, all of them looking down at the same moment with the same expression: the look of someone who has just realized how far the fall is.

And on the throne, King Aldric sat crowned in black iron, singing.

It was not a song any throat should have been able to produce. It came out of him in layers — a bass note that seemed to rise from the foundations of the mountain, over which wove voices high and low, young and old, hundreds of them, thousands, each one distinct if you listened hard enough, each one a person who had once laughed or wept or called a child's name across a field. The kneeling shadows filled the hall before him, rank upon rank, swaying gently, like wheat, like drowned kelp, like a congregation asleep on its feet.

Wren Halloway knelt among them three rows deep, her jaw tight, her eyes moving — still moving, still hers, counting exits the way she counted ledgers. Beside her, Brannoc strained against the shadow-stuff that held his arms, his face gray, his lips moving around words that wouldn't come. The Choir had bound them where they stood when the guards — half paper, half song, tearing the court apart in Wren's forged-decree confusion — had finally been overwhelmed. Only Ash had been left loose, because the Choir could not bind what it could not swallow, and the sight of that small untouchable figure walking through the ranks of shadows had given Silas the only hope she carried now.

The singing faltered.

Not stopped — the Choir did not stop, would not stop until solstice or unbinding — but it bent, the way a river bends around a stone, and the king's head turned on the throne, and his eyes found her.

They were green. She hadn't expected them to be green. In the capital, in the rumors, in Wren's breathless account, the king's eyes had been described as flat, dark, absent — windows painted shut. But now, for a moment between verses, Aldric of House Merridan looked at her with eyes the color of moss after rain, and there was a man behind them, drowning, reaching up through thirty feet of black water.

His mouth kept singing. His eyes were screaming.

*I know,* Silas wanted to say. *I know. I'm going to try again.*

* * *

They gave her an hour. She suspected it was a test, or a cruelty, or both — the Choir had a cartographer's patience, oddly enough; it understood the value of letting a thing unfold so it could be harvested whole. They gave her an hour in a side chamber whose walls were lined with niches, and in each niche sat a bell made of some dark material, and none of the bells made any sound no matter how the air shivered against them. Bells that had given up. She tried not to think about what they'd once rung for.

Ash sat cross-legged on the floor, drawing in the dust with one finger. Not a map this time — figures. A tall woman with wild hair. A big man with a beard drawn as a scribble. A neat, narrow person holding what was unmistakably a piece of paper held aloft like a sword. And a small girl, standing in front of them all, drawn slightly larger than everyone else, though she was the smallest of them.

Silas crouched beside her. Her voice came out cracked, barely hers. "Tomas's notes. The second page. Read it to me again."

Ash didn't speak Common easily yet — the words came out round and strange, accented by a tongue that predated accents — but she recited from memory, because memory was the one thing the child had in abundance, being made of it:

*"The counter-song is not a weapon. It is a correction. The Choir sings the world as it wishes to remember it. You must sing the world as it is. Do not harmonize. Do not fight the melody. Simply tell the truth in rhythm, and the two songs cannot occupy the same space."*

"And then," Silas said, "it flays the song out of your throat."

Ash looked up. In the bell-light her eyes were old — not ancient, exactly, but *carried*, the way a stone carried in a pocket for forty years looks nothing like a stone picked up yesterday. She touched Silas's throat, gently, where the Choir's fingers had been.

*Yes,* she said in Hollowtongue. Then, laboriously, in Common: "It takes... the song. Not the voice. Not forever. My father—" she stopped, corrected herself with a small flinch that broke Silas's heart sideways, "—the king. He sang against them once. Before the crown finished closing. That is why there is me. The part that fought became me, because it could not stay in him."

Silas sat down on the cold floor beside her, robes and dignity and decade-long caution all abandoned. "So it can be done twice."

"It can be done *many* times." Ash's finger moved through the dust, drawing the throne hall from memory — pillars, ranks, throne — and then drawing lines radiating out from the throne like cracks in ice. "But alone, once, costs a day. Alone, twice, costs more than a day. Alone, three times..." She drew a small figure lying down, and did not finish.

"Then I won't be alone."

Ash looked at her.

"Wren can't sing worth a damn," Silas said, and heard her own voice steadying, finding its old cartographer's certainty, the certainty she used to feel over blank vellum before the first line went down. "Brannoc hums when he thinks no one's listening, off-key, always off-key, I've heard him on the road for three weeks. And your father's in there. Underneath. Nine seconds he looked at me, Ash. Nine seconds he was *home*. That's not nothing. That's a door."

Ash studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, the child took Silas's hand and pressed it flat against her own small chest, where a heartbeat lived that had no business living inside a fragment of a stolen soul.

*When you sing the truth,* she said, *I will hold the rhythm. I am made of what he lost. I am the metronome.*

* * *

They brought her back to the hall at what the Court insisted was dusk, though no sun had ever entered it. The shadows had not moved. The singing had not ceased. But Silas noticed, walking down the aisle between the kneeling ranks, that the shadows nearest her leaned *away* — minutely, the way grass leans from wind — and she chose to believe it was because truth had a smell, and they remembered it.

Wren's eyes tracked her. So did Brannoc's. Neither could speak, but Silas had spent a week learning these people — the chamberlain who fled a palace with a signet in her fist and terror in her spine and came out the far side as resolve; the guide who gave up his wedding day at a toll-shrine and hummed anyway. She mouthed, carefully, silently: *On my third verse. Follow anything. Anything at all.*

Wren's chin dipped, a fraction. Brannoc's fists clenched inside the shadow-binds, and his lips shaped a word she couldn't read but could guess: *bloody hell,* probably, or her name, or both.

She climbed the three steps to the dais. The Choir's song closed over her like water, and this time she did not flinch, because she had spent ten years avoiding Thornfell and she was done with flinching.

King Aldric watched her come. The green surfaced in his eyes and sank again, surfaced and sank, a man treading water in his own face.

Silas Nystrom had charted realms that existed only between the folds of the world. She had mapped rivers that moved, valleys that unfolded, roads that existed only on the second reading of a sentence. She knew — better than anyone alive — that a map was not the territory. A map was a *claim* about the territory. An act of stubborn declaration: *here is a ridge; here is a ford; here is where my brother last stood.*

The Choir sang the realm as it wished to remember it: smooth, emptied, obedient, every rough edge of grief and love sanded down into hymn.

So she opened her mouth, and she began to sing the map.

* * *

She started small. The border town of Vellum's Rest — its crooked bridge, the bakery on the corner that burned down twice and rebuilt twice because Marta Ostrow did not know how to surrender. The river Vell that kept moving, that she had charted eleven times in ten years, each version true and none final. She sang the ford at Miller's Knee and the false pass on the north road that looked like a shortcut and killed two mule trains before she inked the warning onto her master sheet in red.

The Choir's song bent toward her. Listening. Amused, almost — one small voice against an ocean.

Then she widened. The lowland villages, named and named: Harrow Fen, Cold Sallow, the twin towns of Ember and Emberside that hated each other over a mill race and shared a graveyard anyway because the dead had never cared about the feud. The shrine at the toll-crossing where Brannoc had laid his wedding day down like a coat over a puddle so others might cross dry. She sang the wedding — she hadn't seen it, but Brannoc had told her on the road, drunk on cheap wine and homesickness: a girl named Odile, rain, a borrowed ring, dancing in a barn until the lanterns ran dry — and somewhere in the third row of shadows, a shape flinched.

The song of the Choir stuttered.

Silas pushed harder. The capital. The palace kitchens where Wren Halloway had worked her way up from a clerk's stool, memorizing every ledger, every decree, every signature until she knew the king's hand better than the king did. She sang the real signature — the little hook Aldric made on the descender of his R, the way he dotted his i's slightly left of center — and above the throne, the king's hands twitched against the armrests.

She sang the princess. She didn't know her name — Wren had never dared say it aloud, superstitious — but she knew the shape of the loss in the chamberlain's face whenever the child came up, and grief, honestly sung, needs no proper noun. *A daughter with her mother's laugh, taken south to an aunt's keeping, and the king who woke one morning unable to call her picture to mind, and the horror of that, the specific horror, the horror of a man discovering erasure from the inside—*

The Choir screamed.

It was not a sound; it was a *subtraction*, a hole torn in the hall's fabric, and through the hole the true song poured in like light under a door. The kneeling shadows convulsed. Three of them simply stood up — stood, wavered, and collapsed into ordinary darkness, freed or destroyed, Silas couldn't tell and couldn't care. The bells in the distant niches rang, every one of them, a sound like a thousand years of held breath released.

And King Aldric looked at her, and his eyes were clear, wholly clear, green as spring, and his mouth stopped singing the Choir's verse and began, raggedly, terribly, beautifully, trying to sing hers.

"One," Silas counted, forcing the truth through her teeth. "Two. Three."

Beside the throne, Ash stood with her small fist pressed to her chest, humming the rhythm — the metronome, the fragment of soul, the part that fought. The shadows were rising all through the hall now, ranks breaking like ice going out in springtime.

"Four. Five. Six."

The king's mouth formed a word. It was hoarse, ruined, barely voiced. But Silas read his lips the way she read terrain, instantly, completely.

*Maren.* The princess's name. The name Wren never dared speak.

"Seven. Eight. Ni—"

The Choir flayed the song from her throat.

There was no gentleness in it this time. The first theft had been surgical; this was a *rending*. It reached down into her chest and pulled the counter-song out by the roots, and it took things with it that she hadn't agreed to give — she felt names go, felt them tear free like barbs: the smell of her mentor's lexicons, the weight of her first pen, the sound of Tomas laughing in the next room over some boyhood triumph. She clawed at her own throat and her fingers found nothing, no blood, no wound, only the terrible clean emptiness where a song had been.

The last thing she saw before the darkness took the edges of the world was Aldric — mortal-eyed, weeping, *his*, for nine full seconds — lurching forward against the black crown, one hand rising toward his daughter's name still hanging in the air.

Then the hall tilted, and Ash's voice — furious, ringing, in a tongue older than kingdoms — was the last thing she heard, shouting at the Choir in words that needed no translation:

*You cannot take what was freely given!*

* * *

Silas woke to firelight and the smell of pine smoke, which was impossible, and therefore the most wonderful thing she had ever smelled.

She was on bedroll in a chamber that was not a cell — Wren's doing, surely; the chamberlain sat nearby sharpening a letter opener against a boot heel with the grim industry of a woman who had decided the universe owed her interest. Brannoc dozed against the wall. And Ash sat cross-legged at Silas's side, watching her with those carried-old eyes, and when Silas opened her mouth to speak, nothing came out.

Not pain. Just — absence. A door boarded shut from the outside. She touched her throat. Ash caught her hand and shook her head, gentle.

"A day," the child said softly. "Maybe two. It keeps what it takes. But—" and here Ash smiled, and it was the smile of someone handing over a treasure, "—it did not take everything. Listen."

From the wall, without opening his eyes, without waking fully, Brannoc was humming.

Off-key. Always off-key. But it was the counter-song — the shape of it, the bones of it — the map of Vellum's Rest and the river that moves and the girl named Odile dancing in a lamplit barn. He had heard it once, and his traitor-touched, toll-scarred, loyal heart had kept it, the way stones keep the warmth of sun after sundown.

And Wren, sharpening her blade, was murmuring under her breath — the cadence of it legal, rhythmic, absurd and perfect — reciting clause and proviso and royal prerogative in the exact meter of Silas's stolen verse.

Silas lay still and listened to her broken, discordant, magnificent family sing her song badly, and felt the tears slide sideways into her hair, and thought: *nine seconds bought us this. Imagine what nine minutes could buy.*

Then the door opened, and a guard stood there — a real guard, blinking, human, terrified — and said, in a shaking voice, the words that turned her blood to winter:

"The king's broken free of the throne. He's asking for the woman who sang. And the Choir—" the guard swallowed— "the Choir has stopped singing altogether. It's gone quiet. All of it. Like it's *listening*."
