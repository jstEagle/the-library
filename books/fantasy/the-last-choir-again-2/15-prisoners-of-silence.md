## 15. Prisoners of Silence

The cell was not built. It was *held*.

Silas understood that the way she understood most impossible things—by mapping it. The walls had no mortar, no stone grain, no seams. They were simply *stopped*: air compressed into solidity by something that refused to exhale. When she pressed her palm against them, they gave like the skin of a drum, and somewhere beneath her fingers she felt a vibration too slow to be sound, a held note sustained for centuries by lungs that were not lungs at all.

They had taken everything. Her satchel, her compass, the knife in her boot, the folded scrap of Tomas's map she'd sewn into her collar. Brannoc was somewhere to her left—she could hear him breathing, or rather, hear him trying to breathe and failing to find enough air, because the cells drank breath itself. Each inhalation came back thinner than it went out. Wren was beyond him; Silas could tell by the chamberlain's careful, measured silence, the silence of a woman conserving herself the way she'd once conserved ink and wax and royal patience.

And Ash—

Ash was not in a cell at all.

They had hung her. That was the only word Silas's mind would permit, though she knew it was wrong the moment it arrived. The child floated in the corridor outside the cells, suspended a hand's width above the black floor, wrapped not in rope but in ribbons of absolute quiet. Her eyes were closed. Her small chest rose and fell with terrible slowness, one breath every minute, perhaps less, as if the Hollow Court were deciding whether such a fragment deserved to keep breathing at all.

"Ash," Silas said, and the word fell dead two feet from her lips. She watched it die—the Court did that, killed sound at birth, let you hear your own voice only as a ghost of pressure in your skull. Speaking here was like shouting underwater. Like praying to a god who'd stopped listening.

She sat down on the floor that was not stone, put her back against the wall that was held breath, and began, methodically, to map her way out.

It was what she did. It was all she had ever done. Give Silas Nystrom a blank space and she would fill it or die trying. So she catalogued: the cells ran along a corridor curved like the inside of a throat. The floor was warm in one place, cold in another—a vein of temperature that might be a passage below, might be nothing. The light had no source; it seeped from the walls themselves, grey as old parchment. And beneath everything, always, the Choir sang—not with ears but with bone, a harmony so vast and so patient that her teeth ached with it, her ribs hummed with it, and the scar tissue over her heart, where grief had knitted itself ten years ago, throbbed in time.

*We know you are counting,* the song said. Not in words. In certainty. *Counting is a kind of singing. We have room for counters.*

"Go to hell," Silas whispered, and felt even the whisper get eaten.

* * *

Time passed. Or didn't. In a place built from a single sustained moment, time was a rumor. Silas slept without meaning to, dreamed without images, woke to find Ash still floating, still breathing once per eternity, and Brannoc's breathing gone ragged and wrong in the cell beside hers.

"Brannoc." She pressed her mouth to the membrane between their cells. "Brannoc. Breathe shallow. Don't fight it—it takes more if you fight."

A long pause. Then his voice, muffled, hoarse, absurdly gentle: "Is this the part where you tell me about the river that keeps moving? Because I could use a boring story right about now, Nystrom."

"It's not boring. The Vellamir keeps moving because it never agreed to be named." She heard him huff something like a laugh. "I charted it eleven times. Eleven different rivers. My mentor said I was stubborn. I said the river was dishonest. We compromised: the river is honest, and I am stubborn, and maps are just arguments we lose gracefully."

"You're doing that now," he said. "Arguing with the walls."

"Someone has to. You're busy wheezing."

"And Wren?"

Silas listened. Beyond Brannoc's cell, the chamberlain's voice came thin and clear as a pin: "Still here. Still cataloguing. There are forty-one guards on the lower gallery, and nine of them blink out of rhythm, and I have been reciting the succession laws of Aldric's grandfather in my head because if I stop thinking in sentences, I will start screaming, and I refuse to give this place the satisfaction."

"There she is," Brannoc murmured. "The realm's last honest clerk."

"The realm's last clerk, full stop," Wren said. "Everyone else appears to have been eaten."

Silas laughed—or made the shape of laughter—and felt the Court drink that too.

* * *

When the Choir finally came for her, it did not come as footsteps. It came as an absence walking.

One moment the corridor was empty except for floating Ash; the next there was a figure standing before her cell, tall and robed in a color Silas's eyes kept sliding off—somewhere between the grey of unwritten paper and the black of a burned page. Where its face should have been there was a suggestion of many faces, layered like vellum laid on vellum, none of them quite opaque. And when it spoke, it used a voice she knew.

"Tomas?" The name tore out of her before sense could catch it.

*Not yet,* the thing said gently, in her brother's cadence, wearing her brother's vowels the way a thief wears a stolen coat. *But soon. If you wish.*

"I want to see him." Her hands were flat against the drum-skin wall. "Not a voice. Not a costume. Him."

*You may see him now,* said the Choir. *That is the offer.*

The wall dissolved.

There was no door, no hinge, no ceremony. The held breath simply exhaled in front of her and re-formed as a corridor, and the Choir-thing gestured with a hand that had too many knuckles, and Silas walked—because of course she walked. Ten years of avoidance, ten years of rerouted trade routes and uncharted margins, and all of it collapsed in a heartbeat at the words *you may see him now.* She hated herself for it. She walked anyway.

They led her down through the throat of the Court. Sound died around her in widening rings. The walls here were different—older—and carved, though *carved* was insufficient. The stone had been sung into shapes: arches like open mouths, pillars like throats, friezes of figures kneeling in rows that repeated until the eye gave up. And in each kneeling figure's chest was a hollow, and in each hollow was darkness, and the darkness moved.

*Do you know why we brought you here, cartographer?*

"Because I'm useful," she said flatly. "Everyone who captures me eventually figures that out."

*Because you read the world back to itself.* The many-faced thing drifted ahead of her, its robes making no sound at all. *The old maps were not drawings, Silas Nystrom. They were bindings. Your mentor knew. Your brother knew. The world holds its shape because someone once sang its names into it, and names wear out. We do not destroy the world. We remember it better than it remembers itself.*

"That's not remembering. That's taxidermy."

*It is mercy,* the Choir said, with such serene conviction that Silas's skin crawled. *Everything forgets. Every kingdom, every face, every love. We are the archive that does not rot. Your king forgot his daughter's face—we can hold it for him forever, perfect, unchanging. Is that not kinder than loss?*

Ahead, the corridor opened into a small chamber, and the Chamber of Offerings—that was what the friezes called it, she realized, the carvings were labeled in Hollowtongue, and Ash's lessons had taught her enough to read the repeated glyph: *gift, gift, gift*—the chamber held a table of black glass, and behind the table stood a man.

Tomas Nystrom.

He looked—gods, he looked *good*. Better than the day he'd left. His hair was combed. His cheeks had color. He wore clean clothes the grey of the Court, and when he saw her his whole face lit up with a smile so familiar that her knees nearly buckled, because she had spent a decade imagining this exact smile at the end of a rescue, and here it was, arriving ten years late and entirely wrong.

"Sila!" He said it the way he'd said it when they were children, when she'd fallen out of the rowan tree and he'd run to pick her up. "You came! I told them you'd come. I *knew* you would."

"Tomas." She crossed the room in four strides and grabbed his arms, and his arms were solid, warm, alive. She searched his face. "Are you hurt? Did they—"

"No, no. Nothing like that." He laughed, easy and bright. "Silas, it's fine. It's better than fine. I *work* here. Have you seen the archives? You would die. Whole valleys nobody's ever named. Rivers that haven't decided where to go yet. I've been helping them organize. Ten years, and I finally feel—" he paused, searching for the word, and found it with a smile of pure contentment, "—*useful*."

"Useful." The word tasted like ash. She shook him, gently, then less gently. "Tomas. Tomas, look at me. What happened at Thornfell? What happened the night you—"

"I followed the song," he said simply. "Like everyone does. And it was beautiful, Sila. That's the part nobody tells you. It doesn't drag you. You *want* to follow. All those years I thought I'd been taken, and really I just—" he beamed, "—came home."

She saw it then. Up close, in the good light with no shadows to hide in: his eyes were the problem. They were the right color, the right shape, and utterly empty behind—like windows of a house where every lamp had been carried out and the family photographed themselves waving from the porch before leaving forever. His smile reached his mouth and stopped. It never got near his eyes. Nothing ever would again.

"Oh," Silas said. Very quietly. The sound of a decade of hope dying was very quiet indeed.

*Tomas serves willingly,* the Choir said, drifting to stand beside him, its many faces softening into something almost tender. *As any soul serves once it understands. This is our offer, cartographer, and we make it only once.*

The black glass table rippled. An image rose from it: the throne room, far above—the king on his iron seat, the solstice sun beginning its long slide toward the horizon, and in the center of the hall, floating in ribbons of quiet, a small mud-brown child with her eyes still closed.

*Ash,* the Choir said. *The splinter. The flaw. The piece of Aldric we could not swallow. She is why the binding stalls. She is why your counter-song worked for nine seconds. While she exists, the realm keeps one door open, and through that door, everything we have gathered these three hundred years might leak away.*

"So kill her," Silas said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "If she's such a threat."

*We cannot. She must be surrendered. Freely given up, as all true bindings require.* The many faces turned to her, and every one of them smiled. *Give her to us, Silas Nystrom. Walk away from the child. Take your brother's hand. Return to Vellum's Rest, and he returns with you—restored, whole, himself. He will remember the rowan tree. He will remember your mother's lullabies. He will remember the day he vanished and apologize for every year of it. Everything the Court holds of him, we will pour back.*

The chamber was silent. The Court was always silent. Somewhere in her chest, Silas's heart performed a long, complicated act of treason—showing her the rowan tree, the lullaby, the apology, Tomas laughing in her kitchen, gray-haired and whole, saying *I'm sorry I'm late*—and she let it. She let herself feel all of it, because refusing to look at a trap had never once disarmed one.

"You'll pour him back," she said.

*Every drop.*

"And if I say no?"

The Choir did not threaten. That was the horror of it. It simply gestured, and the image on the black glass changed: Tomas as he truly was beneath the polish—a hollowed vessel, kneeling in some inner archive, mouth moving silently, sorting the memories of strangers into drawers for eternity. Smiling. Always smiling.

*Then he continues as he is,* the Choir said. *Content. Willing. Loved by us as much as we will ever love anything. No harm comes to him either way, cartographer. That is the cruelty you are looking for and will not find. We do not punish refusals. We simply keep what we have kept. Choose love or choose pride. Both cost you nothing but the other.*

Silas looked at her brother.

He was watching her with that warm, empty smile, waiting with the placid patience of the well-fed. And she understood—finally, fully, in the marrow—what the Choir was. Not a devourer. A *curator*. It did not eat souls; it convinced them to donate themselves, lovingly, willingly, one surrender at a time, and called the collection mercy. It offered power as love and love as power until the two were indistinguishable, and the people inside it smiled because smiling was the last shape their faces remembered.

Tomas had chosen this. That was the worst of it. Some night on Thornfell, tired and grieving and lost, he had chosen the warm dark over the cold light, and the Court had honored his choice forever.

"Sila?" he said. "What are you thinking?"

"I'm thinking," Silas said, "that when you taught me to read maps, you told me the first rule. Do you remember?"

His brow creased—a flicker, a ghost, a fish surfacing in deep water. "A map isn't the territory?"

"A map isn't the territory," she agreed. "And a smile isn't my brother."

The warmth drained from his face by degrees. What remained wasn't anger. It was worse: polite incomprehension, the look of a man being shown a puzzle whose solution he no longer had the tools to reach. "But I *am* happy, Sila. Why won't you be happy for me?"

"Because you don't remember enough to know if it's happiness." She stepped back from him. Her heart was a fist of broken glass, but her voice held. "You're a copy of my brother drawn from memory by something that never loved him. And I am not going to trade a living child for a beautiful forgery."

*Tomas,* the Choir said softly.

"Yes," he answered, already turning, already compliant, and Silas's whole body flinched at how fast he obeyed. But at the doorway he stopped—one half-second, one stutter in the machinery—and looked back over his shoulder. And for a moment, one impossible moment, the windows of his eyes lit from somewhere very far inside, faint as starlight through smoke, and his voice came out cracked and small and *his*:

"Sila. Don't sing the third verse. Whatever Tomas wrote down—don't sing the third verse."

Then the light went out. The smile returned, pleasant and vacant. "Goodbye, Sila," he said cheerfully, and followed the Choir away.

* * *

The wall re-formed around her. The held breath closed in. Silas sat down on the floor of her cell and allowed herself exactly sixty seconds of shaking—she counted them, because counting was a kind of singing, and the Choir had told her so itself, and tonight she intended to use its own arrogance against it.

Then she pressed her mouth to the membrane toward Brannoc's cell.

"Brannoc."

"Nngh. That was quick. Bargain go well?"

"They offered me my brother back if I handed over Ash."

A long silence from the next cell. Then, carefully: "And you said—"

"He's gone, Brannoc. Whatever's wearing him is happy, and it isn't him." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "He surfaced for half a second. Long enough to warn me about something. *Don't sing the third verse.* Tomas left a message inside their own archive, and it means he planned for this. It means some part of him has been fighting from the inside for ten years."

From beyond Brannoc's cell, Wren's voice, sharp as a paper edge: "Then the plan isn't dead. Silas—if your brother hid a flaw in the counter-song, and the Choir showed you its bargaining face, it thinks you've already lost. Arrogant captors make mistakes. And I stole something from the palace they may not realize I still have."

Silas leaned her head back against the drum-skin wall. Above her, through the ceiling of held breath, she felt it: the sun crawling toward solstice, the binding gathering itself, the great held note tightening toward its final consonant. One breath left in the world's lungs, maybe two.

In the corridor, Ash floated in her ribbons of silence—and her eyes opened.

She did not look at the guards. She did not look at the Choir's distant grandeur. She looked directly at Silas's cell, directly through the wall that was not a wall, and her small mouth shaped three words in Common, silent as prayer:

*Third verse. Now.*

Silas's blood went cold, then hot. Because Ash couldn't speak aloud here—but Silas could feel the pressure of sound in her own skull, and the Court killed noise two feet from the lips, and the cells were made of *held breath*—

Held breath. Breath that had never been released since the Court was sung into being.

Breath that had never once been *exhaled*.

Silas rose slowly to her feet, and began, very quietly, to hum.
