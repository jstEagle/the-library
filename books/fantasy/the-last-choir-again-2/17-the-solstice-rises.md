## 17. The Solstice Rises

Ash had never run before.

She had walked out of Thornfell in a body too small for the distance, had crossed rivers and roads and the long gray miles to Vellum's Rest, but running was different. Running was what bodies did when they were afraid, and Ash had never been afraid, because fear required something to lose, and she had already lost nearly everything there was to lose.

Now she ran, and she understood.

The corridors of the Hollow Court were singing.

Not with sound — sound died here, strangled a few feet from any mouth — but with the memory of sound, frozen into the walls like insects in amber. Every stone held a note. Every archway hummed a silence so dense it pressed against the ears like deep water. And as they fled down those corridors, hunted by things that had once been courtiers, the frozen hymn *moved*, note by note, verse by verse, keeping pace with them like a tide following the moon.

"They're herding us," Wren gasped, her breath smoking in air that had no right to be cold indoors. She clutched the stolen signet against her chest, wrapped in a scrap of cloth, as if it were a coal she could not let go out. "The corridors keep bending. We turned left twice and came back to the same fountain."

"It's not bending," Brannoc said. He had Tomas's map open across both forearms, running and reading at once, his boots striking the pale flagstones in a rhythm that Ash felt in her teeth. "It's *singing* us. The whole palace is one instrument. We're inside its throat."

Behind them, at the far end of the corridor, the guards had stopped pretending to be guards.

They still wore the king's livery, still carried the king's steel, but they no longer blinked, and they no longer breathed in any rhythm Ash recognized, and when they opened their mouths to shout commands, what came out was harmony. Four voices. Eight. Twelve. A chord wearing men like robes. The chord said, in a language older than kingdoms, in the tongue Ash had spoken since the day she was made:

*Come home. Come home. All voices come home.*

Ash did not look back. Looking back cost time, and time was the only thing she had left, and she could feel it going out of her like warmth from an open door.

* * *

She was fading. She had known this would happen — had known it since the storm, since the night she finally found her Common tongue and told Silas the truth: *I am the piece he could not swallow.* A fragment of a soul does not have infinite substance. She had spent herself getting here. Every mile, every word, every memory-song she had sung to lead them through the folds of the world had been a coin taken from a purse that did not refill.

Her hands were the worst. She kept looking at them because she could not stop looking at them. The light passed through them now, faintly, the way it passes through early morning fog. When she flexed her fingers she could see the flagstones through her palms, blurred, like fish seen through moving water.

Silas saw it too. Of course Silas saw it. Silas missed nothing; it was the cartographer in her, the woman who had spent her life noticing where the world bent. Every few minutes her eyes flicked down to Ash's hands, then away, quick, as if looking too long might make it true. Her jaw was set in the shape she made when she was refusing to cry. She could not speak — the Choir had flayed the counter-song from her throat nine days ago and her voice had never fully returned, only a rasp, only a ghost of a voice — but she reached back without breaking stride and caught Ash's hand in hers.

Her grip was warm. Solid. It was the realest thing in the Hollow Court.

*I'm here,* the grip said, because Silas's mouth could not.

Ash held on and ran.

* * *

The realm was forgetting itself, and Ash could feel every piece of it leaving.

This was the part no one had warned her about — not because they hadn't tried, but because there were no words for it in Common, or in Hollowtongue either. The binding had begun. Somewhere above them, beyond these buried halls, the sun was sliding toward the solstice line, and as it moved, the Choir drew breath — a breath three years long, three centuries long — and began to pull.

Ash felt it as a wind blowing inward. Not cold. Worse than cold. It blew *through* her, and everything loose went with it.

Far away, in Vellum's Rest, a baker was standing in his bakery unable to remember why the smell of bread made him want to weep. In the capital, a mother looked at her sleeping son and felt love arrive like news from a distant country — true, but secondhand. Old men forgot their wars. Young women forgot their first winters. The river near Silas's shop, the one that kept moving, forgot which way it had always flowed and began, very quietly, to flow uphill.

And in the mountains, in the lowlands, in every cradle and every grave between, the small unimportant memories went first — the name of a childhood dog, the tune a father whistled, the exact color of a door someone grew up behind — because the Choir ate from the edges inward. It had always eaten that way. That was how it had worn King Aldric for three years without anyone screaming: it took the garnish before the meal, the margins before the text, until a man could stand in the middle of his own life and find nothing but clean white space where himself used to be.

Ash knew, because she was made of the part he had refused to give up.

She stumbled. Brannoc caught her by the collar without slowing, hauled her upright, and kept running, and she understood that this — being caught, being carried forward by hands that owed her nothing — was the other thing she had been sent into the world to find. Not just a map-reader. These. This stumbling, swearing, half-frozen family of exiles who had followed a mute child into the throat of a god because she asked.

"Map says the throne hall is past the gallery," Brannoc shouted over the frozen hymn. "There's a door — Tomas marked it — a door that isn't a door."

"I see it," Wren said.

Ahead, the corridor opened into a gallery of portraits, and Ash's heart — the small borrowed organ in her chest, beating faster now than it had ever beaten — clenched.

The portraits were empty.

Not blank. Empty. Every frame held a face mid-vanish, paint peeling back from canvas like skin from bone, and beneath each portrait a brass plate bore a name, and the names were *fading*, the engraved letters smoothing themselves back into bare metal even as they watched. The Choir had hung its meals on the walls. Generations of the Hollow Court's dead, and now the living too, all of them becoming anonymous together.

One portrait still had eyes. A young woman in a green dress, half-gone, her mouth a smear of unfinished paint — but the eyes remained, fixed on Ash with an expression Ash recognized from the inside: *I was somebody. Wasn't I? Tell me I was somebody.*

"We can't save them," Wren whispered. She had stopped. She was staring at the portraits, and her hand around the signet had gone white-knuckled. "We can't — there are thousands of them, we can't—"

"Wren." Brannoc's voice was gravel and iron. "The sun doesn't wait for grief."

Wren tore her eyes away. But as they ran the length of the gallery, Ash heard her whispering under her breath, fast, desperate — reading the names off the plates before they finished fading, committing each one to memory as it died. *Alenna Corvane. Marris of the Low Ward. Tam Broadfoot. Ilse. Ilse. Ilse—*

That was Wren Halloway all over, Ash thought. The Choir stole memories, and Wren fought back the only way a chamberlain knew how: by keeping records. By refusing, on principle, to let anything go unwritten.

At the gallery's far end stood the door that wasn't a door.

It looked like wall. Pale stone, seamless, identical to every other stretch of pale stone. But Tomas had marked it on his map with a symbol Ash knew from the oldest lexicons: a circle with a line through it, the sign for *here the world forgets to be solid*. Silas pressed her palm flat against the stone, closed her eyes, and did the thing she did — the thing that had made her famous in towns that didn't officially exist, the gift of folding space along seams no one else could see.

For a moment nothing happened. Ash felt Silas strain against the wall like a woman pushing a locked gate, and felt, beneath her own fading skin, the terrible truth: the Court was fighting her. The hymn in the stones rose in pitch, a whine like a struck glass, and the wall *thickened*, and Silas made a small broken sound in her ruined throat—

Then Ash put her hand over Silas's and sang.

No words. No melody the living would recognize. Just the fragment of soul she was, humming the one note the Choir could never take, because it was the note Aldric had hidden inside her the day the black crown came down — the note of his refusal. The wall shuddered. The whine faltered. And in that gap, Silas *folded*.

The stone opened like a page turning.

* * *

Beyond the false door, the air changed.

The frozen hymn fell away behind them, and what replaced it was worse: a silence so complete it had texture, weight, presence — the silence of a held breath three hundred years long. They came out onto a high balcony of black marble, and below them, spread out like a wound in the world, lay the throne room of the Hollow Court.

Ash had seen it in dreams. Dreams were kind. This was not.

The hall was vast beyond reason, its ceiling lost in gloom, its pillars carved with the likenesses of every voice the Choir had ever swallowed — thousands of faces in the stone, mouths open, eyes shut, singing forever without sound. The floor was a single slab of obsidian polished to a mirror, and in it the party saw themselves reflected small and far away, five figures on a balcony, and Ash saw that in the reflection her outline was *soft*. Blurred at the edges. A chalk drawing someone had licked their thumb and smudged.

And at the center, on a dais of black iron thorns, sat the king.

Aldric — what was left of him — sat straight-backed and terrible, crowned in black iron that drank the light. His eyes were open and fixed on nothing, on everything, on the whole realm at once, and his mouth moved in constant silent articulation, shaping syllables that the pillars and the floor and the air itself obeyed. With every syllable, the pulling wind strengthened. With every syllable, somewhere in the world, another name smoothed off another brass plate.

Kneeling in the hall below, arranged in ranks like a congregation, were the shadows.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They had no faces anymore — faces were the first thing the Choir took — but their postures were human, and some of them, Ash saw with a horror that bypassed thought entirely, were *fresh*. Their outlines still trembled. Their knees still remembered how to ache. These were today's harvest, the people pulled out of the lowlands while the party fought its way through the galleries, delivered here to kneel and wait to be sung into the stone.

"There." Wren pointed with a shaking hand. "The dais — the steps — if we can reach him before the sun—"

High above, where the ceiling should have been, there was instead a vast circular opening, a mouth of sky, and through it Ash could see the winter sun hanging low and white over the peaks of Thornfell. It was descending toward the rim of the opening with the slow, indifferent certainty of all celestial things. When it touched the rim, the solstice would begin. When it cleared the far side, the binding would be complete, and every memory in the realm would belong to the thing wearing the crown, and the world would go on — fed, orderly, obedient, and empty — forever.

"How long?" Brannoc asked.

Ash did the arithmetic the way she did everything: wordlessly, instantly, painfully. She held up her hands, palms facing, and narrowed the space between them to show him. A hand's width of sky. Perhaps less.

"Then we go *now*," Brannoc said, and started for the stairs.

* * *

They almost made it.

The stairs down from the balcony were long and shallow and utterly exposed, and the Choir had known they were coming — perhaps had always known, had sung their arrival into its plan centuries before any of them were born. Halfway down, the shadows rose.

Not all of them. A rank of maybe thirty, peeling up off the obsidian floor like ink lifted by a magnet, flowing toward the stairs with a speed nothing faceless should possess. Brannoc roared — an honest, mortal, off-key roar, and Ash loved him for it — and met the first of them with his axe, and discovered what they had all feared to learn: you cannot cut a shadow. The blade passed through and the shadow closed around it like water, and Brannoc staggered, and where the shadow touched his arm, his sleeve went gray and his memories of that arm — learning to chop wood, learning to hold a bride — dimmed like coals raked over.

"*Don't let them touch you!*" Wren screamed. "*They take what they touch!*"

It was Wren who saved them, in the end, with the most Wren thing imaginable. She waded forward past Brannoc, held the royal signet high, and cried out in her clear chamberlain's voice the formula of crown-law, the old words every guard of the realm swore and every shadow here had once been bound by: *"In the king's name, HOLD!"*

The shadows hesitated.

Half of them. Half froze, bound by law older than the Choir's theft, because the signet was real and the words were real and some sliver of the men they had been still knelt inside the darkness. The other half surged on — and in that tearing hesitation, that civil war of the self, the party slipped through the gap like water through fingers.

Down the last steps. Across the mirror-floor, their reflections running beneath them, soft-edged and small. Past kneeling shadows whose hollow faces turned to follow them like sunflowers following a dying sun.

Silas kept hold of Ash's hand the entire way. Ash could no longer feel the pressure of it — her nerves were going, sensation draining out of her fingertips like color out of a sunset — but she could see the grip, white-knuckled, fierce, and she held on with everything that remained of her, which was less every second.

Because she was almost done. She could feel it now, unmistakably, the way you feel the last pages of a book approaching under your thumb. The Choir's pulling wind had found her — the loose thing, the unclaimed fragment, the piece of the king that had escaped the table — and it wanted her back. It wanted her *specifically*. The final binding required every piece of Aldric, and she was the last piece outside the crown, and the wind was drawing her apart thread by thread into the white air.

She looked down at herself and watched her feet grow translucent against the obsidian.

*Not yet,* she thought, in two languages at once. *Not yet. I brought them this far. Let me see it. Let me see him free. Let me hear my own name said by people who mean it.*

* * *

The gold line of solstice light lay across the floor like a drawn blade, and it was touching the first step of the dais.

Above, the sun's lower edge kissed the rim of the sky-mouth, and the whole hall inhaled — pillars, shadows, king, the very stone — a single vast indrawn breath that Ash felt pull at her like a riptide. The binding had begun in earnest. The Choir's silent hymn swelled to a pitch that cracked the frost on the far walls, and the carved faces in the pillars opened their stone eyes, all of them at once, and looked at the five small figures standing at the base of the dais with something that was not quite surprise and not quite hunger but was, horribly, both.

King Aldric turned his head.

For three years that head had not turned for anything living. It turned now, slowly, iron crown grinding against iron will, and the eyes that found Ash were not the Choir's eyes. For one suspended instant — one note's worth of time — they were a tired man's eyes, a father's eyes, eyes that had once looked at a daughter across a breakfast table and thought *there, that one, that one is mine, whatever else they take.*

His mouth shaped a word without sound. Ash read it off his lips the way she read everything, in the old tongue and the new:

*Run.*

"No," Ash said aloud.

Her voice came out thin and frayed and barely there, a thread of sound in a hall built to devour sound — but it carried. Only her voice ever carried here. It rang off the obsidian, off the pillars, off the thousand carved faces, and the Choir flinched, the whole cathedral-sized organism of it flinched, because that voice was the one thing in the world it had never managed to swallow.

Ash stepped forward into the light. Small. Unafraid. Fading at the edges like a photograph left in the sun.

And Silas Nystrom, voiceless, took a breath that seemed to come from the bottom of the world, opened her mouth —

— and the first note that came out was not hers alone.
