## 18. Every Voice at Once

The sun touched the solstice line and the world began to forget.

Silas felt it first as a loosening in her own skull — small things peeling away like paint from a sun-bleached door. The name of the street she'd grown up on. The smell of her mentor's pipe smoke. The color of Tomas's eyes, which had been brown, or maybe green, or maybe — no. *No.* She pressed her palms against her temples as if she could hold the contents of her head in place by force.

Around her, the Hollow Court drank.

The throne room was a cathedral of silence built from silence, its walls carved from compressed quiet, its pillars the petrified shapes of hymns that had been sung here before there were words to sing them with. And from the black iron crown on Aldric's head, the Choir poured outward — not light, not sound, but *taking*. Silas could see it now, could see it plainly for the first time: threads of silver rising from every living thing in the realm, from the guards along the walls, from Wren and Brannoc, from the distant sleeping villages beyond the mountains, all of them streaming toward the crown like rivers running backward into a wound.

And Ash was fading.

The child stood beside Silas, and where her outline met the light it frayed, unraveling into motes that drifted upward and did not come back down. Her small hand found Silas's sleeve and gripped it, and even that grip felt thinner than it had an hour ago, a memory of a grip, a promise of one.

*Hollowtongue*, Ash said, and her voice still carried where all other sound died, still rang clear through the dead air of the court. *Now. It has to be now.*

"I know," Silas said. "I know."

But her voice broke against the silence three feet from her lips, and the counter-song — Tomas's song, the map made music, the disruption he had planned and paid for — needed to cross this hall. Needed to reach the throne. Needed to reach the king inside the thing wearing him.

She opened her mouth and sang alone, and it was not enough.

* * *

Brannoc heard the song start and felt his own throat answer before his mind agreed to anything.

That was the trouble with the Choir's tune — the one the toll-shrine had buried in him, the one he'd been humming in his sleep like a man whistling past his own grave. It lived in him now. He could feel it coiled under his breastbone, patient as a tapeworm, singing along with everything. When the Choir pulled, it pulled the tune up through him like sap through a tree, and he had spent three days terrified that when the moment came, his mouth would open and what came out would belong to them.

But Silas was singing. And her song was not the Choir's song.

It was rougher. Wronger. It stumbled where the Choir's melody glided, hit intervals that hurt, resolved nothing. And Brannoc — who had carried cargo over passes that killed better men, who had buried a wife and surrendered the memory of marrying her to a hungry shrine and hated himself for how little it had seemed to cost at the time — Brannoc understood something in that moment that no scholar in Vellum's Rest could have taught him.

The Choir's song was perfect. That was why it could be broken.

He began to hum.

Off-key. Deliberately, gloriously off-key, the way he'd sung at his wedding, the way his wife had laughed and told him he sang like a landslide. The wrongness in his throat fought the Choir's coil for possession of it, and the coil — surprised, offended, unaccustomed to resistance — faltered. His hum was ugly. It was human. It threaded itself under Silas's melody and held it up the way a bad foundation holds up a good house.

Ash turned toward him, and her fading face did something it hadn't done in days.

It smiled.

*Yes,* she said, in the language older than kingdoms, and then, wonderingly, in Common: *Yes. Like that. Wrong like that.*

Wren Halloway, standing at Brannoc's shoulder with the royal signet burning a hole in her pocket and half the court's guards hunting her, looked from the humming guide to the singing cartographer to the dissolving child, and did the only thing she knew how to do.

She reached for law.

* * *

Wren had served the crown for nineteen years, and in nineteen years she had learned one truth above all others: that order was not the enemy of chaos but its antidote, and that the most dangerous thing in any kingdom was a rule nobody remembered the reason for.

The Choir dealt in forgetting. Very well.

"*By decree of Aldric, third of his name,*" she recited, voice cracking across the dead air and dying three feet out — but Ash caught it, caught the shape of it, and carried it, translated it into Hollowtongue, flung it ringing down the hall, "*sovereign of the Nine Valleys and the Thornfell Marches, protector of the low roads and the high: let it be known that the crown serves the realm, and not the realm the crown—*"

The words were old ones. Coronation oaths. Charter language. The boring, dusty, magnificent scaffolding of a kingdom built by people who had feared exactly this — a crown that forgot whom it belonged to. Wren had read them so many times she could have recited them drunk, bleeding, or both, and she did so now, pacing forward step by step, each phrase a stone thrown into the Choir's perfect pond.

She saw it work. She saw the silver threads nearest her stutter, snag on the old words like cloth on a nail. A guard along the eastern wall blinked — actually blinked, wetly, humanly — and looked down at his own spear as if he couldn't remember picking it up.

They were feeding Silas. Every imperfect voice, every cracked recitation, every off-key hum was another thread for the counter-song to weave itself from, and the Choir — which had spent centuries collecting voices precisely because voices were power — had never once imagined that the power might come back at it *discordant*.

Then, from the row of kneeling shadows near the dais, a new voice joined.

A voice Wren didn't know. But Silas did.

* * *

Tomas Nystrom had been hollow for eleven years.

He knelt among the shadows because kneeling was easier than standing, because the Choir poured its song through him and asked nothing except that he stop resisting, and resistance was heavy, and he was so tired. He had been tired for eleven years. He had walked into these mountains with a finished map in his pack and a warning in his heart and he had lost both, lost himself, become a pipe through which the stolen voices of a kingdom flowed.

But the counter-song was moving through the hall now, and it moved through the pipes too.

It found the verse he had stolen.

He had taken it years ago — a fragment of the binding hymn, the Choir's own central verse, copied out in Hollowtongue onto the back of his final map, hidden where only someone who read maps the way he read maps would ever find it. He had stolen it because some buried shard of himself had understood that a song this powerful must have a flaw, and the flaw would be written somewhere, and whoever found the flaw would need the verse itself to prove it.

He had forgotten he'd stolen it. He had forgotten nearly everything.

But when Silas's voice crossed the hall — his sister's voice, thinner than he remembered, raw with grief and fury and eleven years of waiting — the verse rose in him like water finding an old channel, and Tomas Nystrom stood up out of his kneeling shadow, and sang.

His voice was ruined. It scraped like a rusted hinge. Half the notes came out flat and the other half came out broken, and none of that mattered, because the verse was the Choir's own, sung back at it from inside its own throat, and the hall *shook*.

For the first time in centuries, the Choir faltered.

* * *

Silas heard it happen and could not believe it was happening.

The silver threads stopped flowing. All of them, all at once, the way a river stops when the dam breaks upstream — a shudder, a hesitation, a vast and ancient confusion rolling through the court like weather. The walls of compressed silence groaned. The pillars of petrified hymn cracked along lines no eye could see, and somewhere high above, dust that had hung motionless for three hundred years began, very slowly, to fall.

The king's eyes cleared.

Not all the way. Not for long. But for nine seconds — Silas counted them, she would count them for the rest of her life — Aldric's eyes were a man's eyes, terrified and drowning and *present*, and they found hers across the hall, and his mouth shaped two words around the Choir's gag:

*Don't stop.*

"We won't," she breathed, and meant it, and discovered in meaning it that they couldn't — not like this. Because the falter was already closing. The Choir was adapting, the way it had adapted for centuries, folding the discordance into itself, learning Brannoc's wrongness and Wren's law and even Tomas's stolen verse, digesting them, making room for them in its perfection. Perfection was a stomach with infinite capacity.

Unless you gave it more than it could hold.

Silas looked at her ragged, impossible choir — the disgraced guide humming like a landslide, the chamberlain reciting charter law through chattering teeth, the hollow-eyed brother singing theft back to its owner, the fading ghost-child conducting them all with hands that were more light than flesh now — and she understood.

It wasn't the song. It had never been the song. Tomas's map, her training, the counter-melody — those were the instrument. But an instrument needs fuel, and the fuel was not skill.

It was *everything else*.

Every voice at once. Every imperfect, mortal, contradictory voice, carrying everything imperfect and mortal and contradictory about the people behind them. The Choir could swallow a song. It could swallow ten songs. But it could not swallow a life given whole — could not digest a man's entire self offered freely, because everything it had ever taken, it had taken by force, and force leaves a residue, and freedom leaves none.

There was only one person in the hall whose entire self was made of exactly the right material.

A cartographer's mind is not a place. It is a *map* — every road she had ever charted, every river that kept moving, every fold between the folds of the world, every name in every lexicon, her mentor's pipe smoke, the color of Tomas's eyes (brown; it had been brown; she was almost sure), the weight of the door of her shop in Vellum's Rest, the knock that had started all of this.

"Silas." Wren had reached her side, had seen the look on her face, because chamberlains are trained to see the shape of decisions. "Silas, whatever you're thinking—"

"Ash." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "When I can't remember — tell me. Promise me you'll tell me."

Ash's fading hands took her face, and the child looked at her with eyes that held a king's grief and a child's ferocity, and said, in Common, in Hollowtongue, in both at once:

*I'll tell you everything. I'll tell you every day.*

"That's enough," Silas said. "That's a better map than any I ever drew."

She turned to face the throne. She filled her lungs with the dead air of the Hollow Court. And she began to give the song everything she had.

* * *

Brannoc felt her go and hummed louder.

He didn't understand the mechanics of it — didn't need to. You didn't need to understand a rope to pull it, and Silas had tied herself to all of them, and the only thing left to do was pull. His off-key drone wove under Wren's recitation, and Wren's law wove under Tomas's stolen verse, and Tomas's verse wove under Silas's pouring, endless, self-spending song, and Ash stood in the middle of them with her arms raised and her body coming apart into light, translating, carrying, holding the whole fragile architecture of their noise aloft and hurling it at the crown.

The Choir screamed.

It was not a sound so much as the absence of one made enormous — a tearing, a sundering, centuries of stolen harmony meeting four voices' worth of beautiful ruin and losing. The black iron crown grew hot and then white and then transparent, and inside it Silas could see them, all of them, the thousands upon thousands of voices the Choir had collected, pressing against the crack like prisoners hearing a key.

The crack spread.

The threads of silver snapped, one after another after another, whipping back toward the people they'd been drawn from, and guards along the walls gasped awake, and far away in the lowlands farmers paused mid-forgetting and remembered their children's names, and the pillars of the Hollow Court came down in slow, silent, cascading ruin—

—and the last thing Silas charted, the final entry in the great atlas of herself, was the sound of Ash's voice saying her name.

Then the map went blank.

* * *

The crown cracked open like an egg, and what fell out of it was a man.

Aldric collapsed, mortal and weeping, and Wren caught him — nineteen years of service catching him at last in the only way that had ever mattered. He was lighter than she expected. He was crying like a child, and saying names, dozens of names, his daughter's name over and over, testing each one in his mouth like a coin he'd feared was counterfeit, and Wren held him and said, "I know, Your Majesty. I know. They're all still here. You're all still here."

Tomas Nystrom stood in the ruins of the kneeling shadows, empty-handed, hollow-eyed, free and not knowing yet what to be free *for*. Brannoc sat down hard on a fallen pillar and realized the tune under his breastbone was gone, and wept without shame at the silence it left behind.

And Ash knelt beside Silas's body, solid again, whole again, holding the hand of a woman who no longer knew her own name — and did not let go.

Far below the shattered court, in the dark beneath the mountain, something scattered was still screaming. But it was screaming in a thousand separate voices now, and none of them belonged to the Choir anymore.

The solstice sun cleared the windows of the ruined hall, and began, very slowly, to warm the stones.
