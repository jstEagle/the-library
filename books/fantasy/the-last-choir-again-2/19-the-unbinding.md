## 19. The Unbinding

The sun touched the solstice line, and the Hollow Court began to sing.

Silas felt it before she heard it — a pressure behind her eyes, a tugging at the roots of her thoughts, as if someone had hooked fingers into her mind and begun to draw. Around her, the great hall of silence shuddered. The kneeling shadows along the walls rose to their feet in unison, ten thousand of them, and turned their faceless heads toward the throne.

On the black iron seat sat King Aldric, crowned in darkness, his mouth open in a song that made no sound but filled the world anyway.

Beside her, Ash flickered.

It was the worst thing Silas had ever seen. The child's edges went soft and translucent, like wet paper held to a flame. Her small hand — the one gripping Silas's sleeve — thinned until Silas could see the frozen hymn-corridors through her palm.

"Ash—"

*Hurry,* the girl's voice said, inside Silas's skull rather than the air, because the air belonged to the Choir now. *I can't hold much longer. It's pulling me back. It's pulling everything back.*

Brannoc was on his knees, both fists pressed to his temples. Wren had thrown herself against a pillar, teeth bared against the pull like a woman leaning into a gale. And further back, half-lucid, swaying between Brannoc's shoulder and the wall, Tomas Nystrom lifted his head with an expression Silas hadn't seen on his face in ten years: terror.

He remembered, in this moment, what he was.

The binding song rose. Silas could feel it doing its work — names peeling off things around her. The pillar Wren leaned against forgot it was granite. The torches forgot fire. Somewhere far south, she knew, whole villages were forgetting their own children's faces, rivers were wandering from their beds because no one remembered where they'd been taught to run. The Choir was drinking the realm down to its bones.

And there was no one left to stop it but her.

She had known this moment was coming since the frozen chapel. Perhaps since Vellum's Rest, since the knock at her door, since the first word of Hollowtongue fell out of a mud-caked child's mouth into her careful, ordered life. She had charted the road here line by line, and every mapmaker knows the oldest rule of the craft:

Every journey has a price at its end. You pay in what you carried.

Silas looked down at Ash, fading like breath off glass, and made her peace.

"Tomas," she said aloud, though sound barely survived three feet here. He turned anyway. Some things were louder than silence. "The counter-song. It needs fuel. A voice full enough to drown theirs."

His hollow eyes cleared for one heartbeat. *You can't,* he mouthed. *There isn't enough of you.*

"There is," Silas said. "You just never saw how much of me there was."

She closed her eyes.

A cartographer's mind is not a warehouse. It is a country. Ten years of it — no, a lifetime of it — unfolded behind her eyelids as she reached for the first thread.

The river Vessa, which moved a hand-span west every spring, and the way its water tasted of iron and willow-bark. The name of every ferryman who worked it: Odo, Maren, old Piet who sang flat. The road from Vellum's Rest to Thornfell's foot, all forty-one leagues, each milestone and mud-sink and shrine. Her mentor Aldous's lexicons, every page, every marginal note in his cramped hand — *check this, girl; doubt everything; the map is not the territory, thank the gods.* The smell of ink. The weight of her first compass, brass and cold. Her mother's voice reading survey reports by candlelight. Her father's laugh, low as distant thunder. The door of the shop, its specific creak, third hinge from the top. The color of dawn over the Vessa in autumn, which was not gold and not copper and had no name she had ever found, so she had invented one: *nystrom-orange*, after herself, because a cartographer is allowed one vanity.

She took hold of all of it.

And she began to sing.

The counter-song was Tomas's — stolen from the Choir decades ago, hidden in maps, smuggled out in ink. But a song is only a vessel. What fills it must come from somewhere, and the Choir sang with stolen voices, perfect and hollow. Silas sang with her own, and poured herself in.

The first thing to go was small: the name of the innkeeper in Vellum's Rest. She felt it leave like a tooth pulled — a little blood, a little ache, and then a smooth blank place. The song flared brighter. In the hall of silence, sound existed again, violently. The kneeling shadows staggered.

The Choir noticed her.

It came at her then, a wave of pressure, a thousand stolen mouths singing her name backwards, trying to unmake her the way it unmade kingdoms. *SILAS NYSTROM,* it sang. *DAUGHTER OF EDE AND MARTEN. SISTER OF TOMAS. KEEPER OF THE VELLA FOLDS.* It knew her completely. It had eaten people who knew her completely. It thought knowing was the same as owning.

She sang louder, and gave it the roads.

All of them. Every trade route and smuggler's path, every goat track and pilgrim trail, the whole silver web of the lowlands that she'd spent her life threading onto paper. She felt each one go — not destroyed, but *leaving*, pouring out of her like light out of a cracked lantern, feeding the song. Behind her eyes, the map of the realm went dark region by region, and where it went dark, the counter-song burned white.

Brannoc crawled to her side and hummed. Off-key, wavering, terrified — but humming, adding his rough thread. Wren pushed off her pillar and recited law, crown-law, border-law, the law of ferries and fences and funerals, her clear clerk's voice braiding through the melody. And Tomas — Tomas knelt beside his sister, tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face, and gave back the verse he had stolen, the original seed, his ruined voice cracking on every third note.

Discordant. Imperfect. Human.

The Choir faltered.

For the first time in centuries, the perfect unity stuttered. Because unity is only strength when everyone sings willingly, and the Choir's choir had been taken, not chosen, and something in the deep mathematics of song knows the difference. The stolen voices wavered. The shadows in the hall clutched at themselves like sleepers waking.

On the throne, King Aldric's eyes opened.

They were ordinary eyes. Brown, bloodshot, exhausted. For three years they had been windows into nothing; now, briefly, they were windows into a man watching his own rescue arrive at terrible cost.

"No," said the Choir, through his mouth, in a hundred harmonized voices. "No. The binding is *today*. It is *ours*. We have been patient. We have been—"

"You've been thieves," Wren snapped, still reciting, still standing, a small grey woman shouting down a god. "And crown-law has provisions for thieves."

Ash's fading hand found Silas's wrist.

*Now,* the child said. *Give it the rest. Everything. It's the only way to crack the crown.*

Silas knew. She had known since the chapel. The counter-song didn't want her roads or her craft or her cleverness. It wanted *her* — the whole interior country, every memory she had ever charted, because a cartographer's truest map was the one drawn on the inside of her own skull, and only a complete self had enough weight to pull a god apart.

She thought of Ash's face when she'd named her. She thought of the blue campfire, the toll-shrine, the fold in the valley where they'd shown each other their fears. She thought of her brother at nine years old, drawing maps in the dirt, saying *one day we'll chart somewhere nobody's ever been, Sil, somewhere new.*

Then she stopped thinking, and gave.

Her name went first. *Silas Nystrom* dissolved off her like frost off stone, and the song roared.

Then her mother's hands kneading bread. Then her father teaching her north from south by the stars. Then Aldous's funeral, and the rain on the umbrella. Then her brother's disappearance, the search, the grief, the decade of avoidance — even her sorrow, even that, spent, spent, spent, fueling a fire that filled the hall like the sun rising indoors.

The black crown cracked.

It began at the band and spidered upward, hairline fractures screaming open, and out of the cracks poured *voices* — hundreds, thousands, the stolen dead and the stolen living, spilling free like birds out of a burning granary. They scattered through the hall, through the walls, through the frozen corridors, shrieking and laughing and sobbing and calling their own names just to hear them, just to be sure.

The Choir unraveled.

It did not die quietly. The entity that had worn Aldric's crown for three years screamed with the voices of everyone it had ever swallowed, and the scream shook snow off mountains a hundred leagues away. Shadows collapsed into ordinary darkness. The kneeling figures crumbled into dust that smelled, impossibly, of morning. And the black iron crown split clean in two and rang on the flagstones like a dropped bell.

King Aldric fell.

Wren caught him. Of course she caught him — she had been catching the kingdom for three years without knowing it, and her arms were ready. He was lighter than a king should be, hollowed down to the mortal man beneath, and he was weeping, great ugly sobs into her shoulder, whispering a name over and over. His daughter's name. He had it back. He had it back and he would not let it go.

Silas watched from very far away.

That was the strange part. She had expected pain, and there was none — only a vast quieting, like snowfall, like the last page of a long book. She stood in the wreckage of the hall holding a fading child's hand, and tried to remember why the man on the floor mattered, and could not. Tried to remember her own name, and found the smooth blank place where it had been, vast now, a country with all its cities gone.

*Ash,* she started to say, and discovered she didn't know who Ash was either. Just a warm small hand in hers. Just a girl looking up at her with tears running freely down a dirty face, mouthing words Silas couldn't understand.

But she didn't let go.

Whatever the hand was, whatever the child was, some last unmapped fold of her held on, the way a coastline holds against the sea — and in the ringing silence after the Choir's death, while freed ghosts drifted up through the shattered roof toward a dawn that had almost not come, Silas Nystrom stood in the ruin of everything she had been, empty as fresh paper,

and waited, without knowing why, for someone to tell her story back to her.
