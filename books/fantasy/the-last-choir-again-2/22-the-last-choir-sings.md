## 22. The Last Choir Sings

The snow came down over Vellum's Rest like a slow forgiveness.

Silas stood at the shop window with a hammer and three nails held between her teeth, watching the town dress itself for the winter festival. Lanterns swung from the eaves of the chandler's across the lane. Children ran shrieking through the slush with orange peels frozen to their mittens. Someone had strung garlands of dried rowan berries along the well-house roof, and the crows kept landing on them, tugging, deciding against theft.

Behind her, the shop smelled of ink and cedar shavings and the particular warm dust of old paper — a smell she had been told was hers, once, though she could not remember learning it. She knew it now the way you know a friend's footsteps on the stairs. Not by history. By repetition.

"Higher," Ash said from the doorway. "Left side. It's crooked."

"It's not crooked."

"You're holding it crooked."

Silas took the nails out of her mouth. "You're eight years old. You don't get opinions about level."

"I'm also a piece of a king's soul," Ash said placidly, "so I get all the opinions." She had grown half an inch since the solstice, or perhaps she had simply stopped flinching, which made anyone seem taller. Her hair was clean for the first time since Silas had known her, braided by Wren's impatient fingers into something almost respectable. She wore a coat that had belonged to Tomas — too big in the shoulders, sleeves rolled twice — because when they'd opened his cache beneath the chapel floor, the coat had been folded on top of the maps, and Ash had claimed it before anyone else could speak.

The map in Silas's hands was Tomas's final work: the Hollow Court, rendered in his small fierce hand, every corridor of silence charted, every kneeling shadow noted in the margins with annotations that were part cartography and part prayer. *Here the sound dies. Here it dies faster. Here I stopped being able to hear my own name — turn back, whoever finds this.*

She had read that last line forty times. She would read it forty more.

Now she hung it in the front window of Nystrom's Maps & Charts, facing outward, so that the whole town could see what had been won and what it had cost. Brannoc had argued for burning it. Wren had argued for sealing it in the royal archive under triple lock. Ash had said nothing, only looked at Silas with those old-soul eyes until Silas understood that the map wanted light, not darkness. Things hidden fed the Choir. Things shown belonged to everyone.

She drove the third nail home and stepped back into the street to look.

The glass caught the lantern-light and threw it back doubled, so that the Hollow Court seemed to glow faintly from within, its silence lit like a reliquary. Passersby slowed. An old woman crossed herself. A boy asked his mother what it was, and the mother said, *that's where the king was kept*, in the hushed voice people used now for the whole affair — the hollowing, the binding, the nine days when no one in the realm could remember their own childhoods and then, all at once, mercifully, could again.

Most of them remembered. That was the miracle nobody had expected: when the Choir unraveled, it gave back what it had taken. Scattered voices, freed mid-flight, carrying memories home like birds returning to a burned roost. The king got back his daughter's face. The farmers got back their fathers' sayings. Brannoc got back his wedding day, and wept into his ale for an hour, and then laughed at himself for weeping, and then wept a little more.

Some things did not come back. Silas's decade of maps — every river she had chased, every coastline she had coaxed onto vellum, every name she had given to nameless folds of the world — those had gone into the counter-song as fuel, spent utterly. She was, by any record-keeper's accounting, the most accomplished blank page in the kingdom.

But records, she had learned, were not the only way of keeping things.

* * *

The festival began at dusk, as it always had, with the carol.

Silas did not remember learning the carol. That memory had gone into the song along with everything else. But when the townsfolk gathered in the square and the fiddler raised his bow and the first verse rose up — *O dark below us, O star above* — the words arrived in her mouth unbidden, worn smooth as river stones, and she understood that this was how memory worked when it lived in other people. You borrowed it. They lent it gladly. That was the whole architecture of love, maybe: an endless lending library where no one kept score.

Ash stood beside her in the crowd, gloved hand in hers, singing in two languages — the Common verse, and underneath it, barely audible, a thread of Hollowtongue that made the air feel briefly like a held breath. Since the unbinding, Ash's Hollowtongue had changed. It no longer sounded like a ritual language. It sounded like a lullaby. Whatever the Choir had built out of that tongue, the child was building something gentler out of its ruins, one syllable at a time, and Silas suspected that in fifty years scholars would trace half the realm's winter songs back to an eight-year-old girl humming in a map-shop.

The second verse rose. And then the third —

— and the third verse never quite caught. The fiddler faltered. The crowd's voices wavered, thinned, hung uncertain in the cold.

Because from the rooftops, someone else had begun to sing.

It started above the chandler's shop: a single voice, high and clear and slightly ragged, picking up the melody where the crowd had dropped it. Then another from the bakery roof, lower, rougher. Another from the bell tower. Another, another, another — dozens of them, scores, rising from every rooftop and gable and chimney-pot in Vellum's Rest, voices without bodies, singing the carol in harmonies no living throat had ever attempted, weaving through and around the human song like frost patterns threading through a windowpane.

The crowd went silent. Somewhere a mother pulled her children close. Brannoc's hand found the knife at his belt out of pure habit, and Wren — who had come up from the capital for the festival, still wearing her chamberlain's gray despite having resigned the post twice now — put her own hand over his and pushed the knife gently down.

"They're not the Choir," Wren murmured. "Look at them."

Silas looked.

The ghosts were visible tonight, the way freed voices sometimes were on cold clear evenings — faint shimmerings, like heat-haze in reverse, like breath made briefly into shape. And they were not kneeling. They were not arranged in ranks. They leaned over the eaves the way neighbors lean over fences. One of them sat cross-legged on the ridge of the tanner's roof with its shimmering chin propped on its hands. Another appeared to be conducting, badly, with enormous enthusiasm.

They were not perfect. That was the thing that undid Silas, standing in the square with her heart going hard against her ribs. The Choir had sung in perfect unity, one will through ten thousand throats, and its perfection had been the most terrible sound she had ever heard. These voices clashed. They dragged behind the beat. One ghost near the well-house was clearly singing a different carol entirely, some older regional variant, and instead of ruining the song it made the song larger, the way a river is larger than any of its tributaries.

Discordant. Human. Alive, in whatever way the dead are alive when they choose to join rather than be joined.

The crowd picked the melody back up. Slowly at first — the fiddler, bless him, simply started over — and then all together, living voices and dead ones braided into a single carol that rolled up off the square and out over the snowfields, and Silas sang too, and beside her Ash sang, and on her other side Brannoc hummed magnificently off-key, and Wren recited the legal charter of the winter festival under her breath because that was apparently how Wren participated in joy, and none of it matched, and all of it was true.

When the last verse ended, the ghosts did not vanish all at once. They faded the way lanterns do, one by one, each going out when its own song was finished. The badly-conducting one bowed to no one in particular before it went.

Ash squeezed Silas's hand. "*They stayed,*" she said in Hollowtongue, and then translated herself, as she always did now, out of courtesy: "They chose to stay. Some of them. To keep watch, maybe. Or just because they like the town."

"Can you blame them?" Brannoc said. "Vellum's Rest has the best ale within a hundred miles, and they can't even drink it. Cruel fate."

Wren snorted. The four of them stood a moment longer in the settling snow, and Silas thought: *this.* Not the throne room, not the counter-song, not even the moment the crown cracked. This — cold feet, bad harmony, a child's hand in hers. If identity was a map, then let it be drawn like this. Let someone chart her by the pressure of Ash's grip and the sound of Brannoc's terrible humming and the exact gray of Wren's stubbornness. Those lines would hold truer than any contour she'd ever inked.

* * *

Later, the shop full of mulled wine and leftover festival bread, Silas climbed the ladder to the loft where she slept and took down the box.

It was a small box. Cedar, iron-bound, unremarkable — except that inside it lay the only original of Tomas's handwriting she had left besides the map: the note. *Do not follow me.* Four words, and beneath them, smaller, added later in a shakier hand: *But if you do anyway — bring the blue ink. You always liked the blue.*

She had followed him anyway. She had brought the blue ink. And he had walked out of the Hollow Court beside her, partially restored, mostly himself, and then stood in the ruins of the frozen chapel on the way down the mountain and told her, in a voice like a man reading someone else's letter, that he could not come home.

"Not because I'm broken," he'd said. "Because I'm *half*-mended, and half-mended men make dangerous neighbors. I remember loving you. I remember the shop, the smell of it. But there are rooms in me the Choir emptied that haven't refilled, and I don't trust what moves in them yet. Give me the mountains. Give me a few years. Give me your forgiveness in advance, because I'm going to need it whether or not I've earned it."

So she had. All three. He sent word now and then through mountain folk — a carved token, a pressed sprig of pine, once a scrap of vellum with a single perfectly drawn contour line and no explanation, which was the most Tomas message imaginable. Last month the token had been a small wooden bird. She kept them all in the cedar box, and on nights when the blank spaces in her mind ached like old breaks in wet weather, she opened the box and let the tokens be proof: that grief could be survived without being solved; that exile and forgiveness could share a house after all, if the house was large enough.

She closed the box. Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear Ash reading aloud to Brannoc — a primer, working through Common letters, though everyone knew the girl could already read perfectly and simply enjoyed making the big man sound out words like *though*. Wren was arguing with someone at the door about whether chamberlains could retire or merely die at their posts. The fire ticked. Snow whispered against the glass, against the window where Tomas's map hung glowing in the lamplight, telling the whole town the truth about Thornfell.

Thornfell was closed. She felt it settle in her the way a bone settles after mending — not forgotten, but quiet. The book on the mountains, the choirs, the crown: shut. She had hung the last page in the window for everyone to read.

Which was why, when the feeling came, it took her a long moment to name it.

It came first as an itch behind the eyes — the old cartographer's itch, the one that had always told her when a map was wrong, when a river had moved, when a fold of the world didn't sit flat against its neighbors. She hadn't felt it since the unbinding. She had assumed it had gone into the song with everything else, spent like the rest of her.

But there it was. Faint. Insistent. Pointing north.

She set down the box and went to the shelf where she kept the works-in-progress — the few tentative charts she'd begun relearning her craft on, coastlines and market-towns, small honest things. And there, pinned to the corkboard among them, was the letter that had arrived that morning, which she had set aside unread because today was for the festival and nothing else.

A surveyor's report. From a mapping expedition in the far north, beyond the Graywater, beyond anything she had ever charted. Routine language, routine measurements — except for the final paragraph, which the surveyor had written in a hand that shook:

*...the valley does not appear on any previous survey, including our own from three years past. The passes around it refuse consistent measurement. Instruments disagree with instruments. On the second night, the men reported hearing singing from the direction of the valley — a great many voices, in unison. We withdrew. I enclose a sketch, for whatever it is worth. Perhaps someone in the lowlands has seen a fold like this before. Perhaps someone knows what sings.*

Silas stood a long time with the paper in her hands.

Behind her, Ash had stopped tormenting Brannoc. The reading had gone quiet. When Silas turned, the child was watching her from the fireside with those eyes that had been old before the world finished making them, and Ash said nothing at all — only tilted her head toward the letter, the way she had once tilted her head toward a door in Vellum's Rest, the way she had once tilted her head toward a mountain road, waiting, patient, certain, for the answer she already knew was coming.

Outside, high above the snowbound rooftops, one last freed voice — the one that sang slightly late, the one that bowed — began, very softly, to sing again. Not the carol. Something older. Something from the north.

Silas looked at the letter. At the window, and the map, and the closed book of Thornfell. At her family, gathered in the firelight, imperfect and unmapped and entirely hers.

"Well," she said, and reached for the blue ink.
