## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There were three ways to tell if a wine had been tampered with, and Mara Lockridge had learned all of them the hard way, which was to say: by nearly dying.

The first way was the nose. Henbane smelled of something gone sweet and wrong, like honey left too long in a warm room, and beneath that a faint bitterness, like a promise made carelessly. The second was the eye — henbane clouded a dark red wine only slightly, but it left a shimmer at the meniscus, a greasy rainbow thread that caught candlelight if you tilted the glass just so. The third way, and the one she trusted least because it trusted her most, was the tongue. A single drop on the tip. Wait. Count to ten. If your tongue went numb and your thoughts went soft and pleasant, you spat, drank the antidote in your sleeve-vial, and spent the evening being very charming to whoever had tried to kill you.

Mara tilted the king's wine toward the candelabra.

There it was. The rainbow thread, thin as a spider's opinion.

She did not flinch. Eight years at court had taught her that flinching was a luxury, like sleeping through the night or trusting a smiling man. Instead she swirled, sniffed, touched one drop to her tongue, counted — *one, two, three* — felt the first honeyed softness creep along her gums, and set the glass down with a small, professional click.

"Poison," she announced, to the room at large, in the tone other people used for remarking on weather. "Henbane. Enough to make His Majesty very relaxed and then very dead."

The great hall of Caer Alden went quiet in that particular way courts go quiet — everyone suddenly fascinated by their own hands. King Aldous the Third, who was sixty-one years old and had survived four wives, two rebellions, and one extremely ambitious cook, leaned back on his throne and sighed the sigh of a man whose dinner kept getting interrupted.

"Again?" he said. "Mara, I pay you a scandalous salary precisely so I am not interrupted at meals."

"You pay me a scandalous salary so that you are alive to complain about interruptions, Majesty. These are different services." She held up the glass to the light, letting the whole hall see the shimmer. "Someone dosed the southern vintage. The bottle came up from the cellars sealed, so either the cellar-master is careless, or someone with cellar keys is clever."

"Or," said a voice from the shadow of the pillar nearest the throne, "someone wished to test our taster's vigilance."

Lord Corvane, spymaster to the crown, stepped forward as if he had been standing there all along, which in a sense he had; Corvane had a gift for seeming to have always been wherever he currently was. He was a lean man going grey at the temples in a way that suggested distinguished exhaustion rather than age, and he smiled the way a knife smiles — all edge, no warmth.

"A test, Lord Corvane?" Mara asked pleasantly.

"A thought. Poison in the king's cup, on the very night we honor the mountain envoys? It strains belief. Unless, of course, one wanted to see whether the famous taster's tongue still worked." He spread his hands. "And here we find it does. Brava."

The mountain envoys — three clan-speakers from Thornfell, wool-cloaked and bearded and radiating the patient disapproval of men who had traveled nine days to discuss tolls — exchanged glances. The eldest, a broad man with a silver ring braided into his beard, said nothing, but his eyes went to Mara's face and stayed there a moment too long, the way a man looks at a door he remembers.

"The wine is withdrawn," Mara said. "I'll have the cellar keys collected. And Majesty, if I may — I'd suggest the envoys drink nothing tonight that hasn't been tasted by me personally. Clan throats are not my responsibility, but dead guests are terribly bad for trade negotiations."

"She has a point," the king said mournfully, looking at his empty hand where his cup should have been. "Very well. New wine. Tasted. And Mara—" he pointed a finger at her, half stern, half fond, "—find out who did this. That is an order, and also a request, and also I would simply sleep better."

"I live to serve, Majesty. Preferably for a long time."

Corvane's smile did not waver, but something behind it did — a shutter closing over a window. "Careful where you dig, Mistress Lockridge. Cellars are deep places. Things buried there stay buried for reasons."

"How poetic," Mara said. "Do you write those yourself, my lord, or does the crown supply them?"

A ripple of nervous laughter ran through the hall. Corvane bowed, gracefully, and withdrew into his shadows, and Mara felt his attention settle on her back between her shoulder blades like a cold coin dropped down her collar.

That was when the dread began. Not the sharp kind — she knew sharp dread intimately, the kind that arrived with numb tongues and rainbow threads. This was the slow kind, the kind that seeped. Because Corvane hadn't been surprised by the poison. Surprised men look surprised. Corvane had looked *interested*, the way a fisherman looks at water he knows holds the fish.

Which meant the poison hadn't really been meant for the king at all.

It had been meant for her. A test of her tongue — or a message: *I know what you know. Show me.*

And what did she know? Only this: three days ago, a courier had staggered into the palace gates half-dead, raving about roads and mountains and a fire that must not gutter, and the healers had pumped him full of poppy and parked him in the tasters' quarters because the infirmary was full of tourney-broken knights. Mara had fed him broth twice. He had gripped her wrist once, hard enough to bruise, and whispered a word she'd assumed was fever-talk.

*Aldenmarrow.* No. Not that. Another word, older, stranger.

*Emberwake.*

* * *

The tasters' quarters occupied a narrow stone wing behind the kitchens, chosen generations ago because it was close to the food and far from everything anyone cared about. Six narrow beds, six narrow windows, and a smell of bread and lamp-oil that Mara found more homelike than any place she'd ever owned.

She came back after midnight, having spent the evening pretending to investigate the cellar while actually watching who watched her. Two of Corvane's grey-cloaked birds had followed her; she had lost them in the laundry yards, which was less skill than it sounded, since nobody in the history of espionage had ever willingly entered a laundry yard.

The courier was in the third bed, where she'd left him. He was worse. Much worse. The poppy had stopped hiding it hours ago, apparently: he lay grey as ash, lips cracked, breath whistling through him in small torn pieces. And there was blood — a fresh dark bloom spreading across the blanket over his belly, though the healers had found no wound days ago.

"No," Mara breathed, kneeling. "No, no, don't you dare die in my quarters, I'll never get the smell out."

His eyes opened. They were clear, horribly clear, the clarity of a man who has spent his last strength arriving exactly where he needed to be.

"Taster," he rasped. "You're the taster. Good. Good — you're used to swallowing things that should kill you."

"That's the job description, yes. Hold still, I'll fetch—"

"No time. Listen." His hand shot out and clamped her wrist, same grip, same bruise. "They're coming for me. Corvane's men. Maybe already. So listen *fast*."

"Who sent you?"

"The mountain clans. The old covenant — the one the crown signed and shelved and forgot." A wet laugh cost him dearly. "Everyone forgets. That's rather the point of it. But the dragon doesn't forget. Emberwake. Say it."

"Emberwake," Mara said, feeling foolish and frightened in equal measure.

"The fire under the mountain keeps the peace. Keeps the wards strong, keeps the clans bound, keeps the crown honest — well. Attempts to. And the fire is failing, taster. Failing because the covenant was broken in every way but ink. And if it gutters out entirely—" his breath caught, rattled, "—the hoard wakes hungry, and the realms learn what the last hundred years of peace were actually built on."

"This is fever-talk. Beautiful fever-talk, but—"

He moved faster than a dying man should. From inside his shirt he dragged a cord, and on the cord hung a shard — black, veined with dull ember-red, warm-looking even in the lamplight. Dragon-scale, or something shaped like one, and etched across its surface in lines fine as hair: roads. Rivers. Mountain passes. A map.

"It shows the smuggler roads," he said. "Every forbidden path through Thornfell. You'll need them. You, specifically — I've seen your blood-work from the ward-gates. Don't ask how. The old marks are in you, taster. The clans will let you pass where they'd turn me to smoke." He pressed the shard into her palm and folded her fingers over it, and his grip was iron despite everything. "Take it to the hollow. Reforge it. Or watch the peace burn."

"Why me? I taste soup for a living!"

"Because you've survived more poisons than any soul alive," he whispered, "and the covenant wants a witness marked by poison survived. It's been waiting eight years for you to finish qualifying." A ghost of a grin. "Congratulations on your promotion."

Boots in the corridor. More than two sets. Moving fast, no pretense of stealth.

The courier's eyes went past her shoulder to the door, and all the urgency in him funneled into one final whisper: "Back window. Laundry yard. Go *now*, taster. And when they ask — you never spoke to me. Swallow it. Like everything else."

Mara looked at the shard in her hand, warm as a sleeping bird. She looked at the door. She thought of Corvane's smile, of the cold coin of his attention, of a rainbow thread in a glass of wine meant — somehow, absurdly — for *her*.

Dread finished its slow work and hardened into something else. Not courage. Courage was for people with choices. This was simpler: the arithmetic of survival. Stay, and be a loose end. Run, and be a problem.

She had always preferred being a problem.

"Swallow it," she muttered, closing her fist around the scale. "Right. One more thing that should have killed me."

The boots reached the door. Mara blew out the lamp, slipped through the back window into the dark and reeking sanctuary of the laundry yard, and began to run — south at first, like any sensible fugitive, and then west, where the mountains waited, and the roads she had sworn she'd forgotten.
