## 2. Roads She Swore She'd Forgotten

The smuggler roads of Thornfell were like old lovers: dangerous, half-forgotten, and liable to remember you at the worst possible moment.

Mara had sworn, eight years ago, that she would never walk them again. She'd been nineteen then, newly widowed of her old life and freshly apprenticed to the tasters' guild, and she had promised herself that the crown's kitchens—predictable, well-lit, occasionally poisoned but never *surprising*—would be enough. They had been. Right up until a dying man bled out on her floor with a dragon's scale in his fist.

Now she was three days into the Thornfell highlands with the spymaster's riders behind her and no plan worth the name, and she found, to her considerable irritation, that her feet remembered everything.

*Left at the split birch,* they said. *Down through the fern hollow where the rock looks like a sleeping boar. Mind the third stone—it rolls.*

Her feet were smug about it, too.

She paused on a ridge above the tree line to catch her breath and check her backtrail. Nothing moved in the grey morning light except mist and one extremely judgmental crow. But she could feel them—the riders. Corvane's men didn't gallop; they *seeped*, spreading through a landscape like ink through wet cloth, patient and total. She'd spotted their sign twice yesterday: a snapped twig cut clean by a cavalry saber, a fire ring doused with wine instead of water because someone had wanted to be sure it smelled of nothing.

That was Corvane all over. He didn't chase you. He made the world itself feel chased.

Mara pulled the scale-shard from inside her coat and turned it in the light. It was no bigger than her thumbnail, dull as an old coin, and yet when the sun caught it just so, faint lines woke across its surface—roads, she now understood. Not drawn *on* the shard but remembered *by* it, the way her own body remembered poisons: not in the mind, but somewhere deeper, somewhere that predated maps entirely.

And threaded through those roads, thin as veins of ore, ran marks she hadn't noticed until the mountains began testing her for them.

Because the mountains *were* testing her. She'd felt it last night—a pressure at the edge of sleep, like a hand pressed flat against her chest, asking a question in a language older than any she spoke. The wards. The clan wards that every smuggler learned to fear and every courtier denied existed. They should have turned her back, or worse. Instead they had touched whatever the courier's blood had left on her hands, whatever the covenant had already begun writing in her, and let her pass.

*You'll do,* the mountain seemed to say. *Barely.*

"Charming hospitality," Mara muttered, and walked on.

* * *

Sera Vane counted her father's killers the way other people counted coins: nightly, and always coming up short.

There was the king, who had sent him. There was the spymaster, who had worked him to death and called it service. And there was the woman—the taster—who had been the last to see him alive, whose name he'd whispered with his final breath while his daughter slept two doors down, dreaming of a homecoming that arrived instead as a body wrapped in sailcloth.

Four days Sera had tracked her. Four days of cold camps and stolen horses and learning, grudgingly, that the taster was *good*. She doubled back without doubling back. She crossed streams on stones no sane person would trust. Once, Sera had come upon a place where Mara had clearly slept, and found the ground arranged so that anything approaching from three directions would snap a twig or shift a pebble. The fourth direction was a cliff.

It was, Sera admitted privately, exactly the sort of thing her father would have liked.

That thought sat poorly in her chest, so she fed it to the fire and rode on.

* * *

By the fourth evening, the road Mara followed stopped pretending to be a road.

It became a seam—a crack in the hillside barely wide enough for her shoulders, walled with wet stone and hung with roots that grabbed at her hair like the mountain's opinion of trespassers. The scale-shard warmed against her sternum, and the vein-marks on its surface brightened until she could read them even in the dark, and she understood that she wasn't following the map anymore. The map was following her.

Then the light ahead changed, and she stepped out into a hollow that took her breath clean away.

A valley cupped in granite, floored with heather gone silver under the moon. A stream running loud over black stone. And rising from the far slope, half-swallowed by the mountain itself, a doorway—not built, *carved*, though no chisel had ever touched it, its edges smooth as river glass and tall as ten men. Old clan-work. Older than clans, maybe.

Wards hung across the entrance like heat-haze. She felt them before she saw them: that same pressing question, stronger now, almost impatient.

"All right," Mara said aloud, to the mountain, because eight years at court had taught her that power of any kind appreciated being addressed directly. "I don't know what you want from me. I have a dead man's map, a dragon's scale, and—" she patted her coat, "—half a wheel of cheese I'm not sharing."

The wards pressed closer. Cold slid along her skin, then beneath it, seeking.

She knew what it wanted. Blood. Wards always wanted blood; it was the one coin every creature carried. So she drew her belt knife, opened the pad of her thumb, and pressed it to the air before the door.

The haze drank the drop—and lit.

Not red, not warning-gold, but a deep ember-orange, and through it Mara saw the shape of what the ward had found in her: lines, faint and branching, curling through her blood like the roads on the shard. Covenant marks. Old ones. Her grandmother's people had come from these hills; she'd always assumed the stories her gran told were just stories. Apparently the mountain kept better records than she did.

The door unsealed with a sound like a long-held breath released, and Mara stood there bleeding mildly and feeling, absurdly, *welcomed*.

"You could have mentioned this sooner," she told the scale-shard, and went in.

* * *

Sera caught her at dawn, which was the traditional hour for vengeance and also, inconveniently, for ambushes to fail.

Mara woke because the birds stopped. That was all the warning she got—one heartbeat of unnatural silence—before she rolled left off her bedroll and something heavy buried itself in the earth where her ribs had been. She came up with her knife in her hand and found herself facing a young woman with storm-grey eyes, a crossbow, and the unmistakable jawline of the courier Aldric Vane, whom Mara had watched die holding a dragon's scale and saying a name like a prayer.

"You," the girl said.

"Me," Mara agreed. "And you're his daughter. You have his chin. He was very proud of it, if that helps."

"It doesn't." The crossbow didn't waver. "He died in your quarters. In *your* keeping. Why you? Why did he give it to *you*?"

"Because I was there," Mara said. "And because he was dying, and dying men don't shop around." She kept her voice level, her knife low. "I'm sorry. I mean that. I've watched a great many people die and I've never once enjoyed it."

"Liar."

"Yes," Mara said. "But not about that."

Something flickered across the girl's face—doubt, unwelcome as rain. And then, because grief makes people reckless in both directions, Sera did the reckless thing: she lowered the crossbow an inch and demanded, "Prove it. Prove you're not one of *them*. Prove why he chose you."

So Mara showed her. The scale-shard, waking under her blood-warmed fingers, roads blooming silver across its face. And Sera stared at it, and her breath went strange, because she recognized the markings—not from maps, but from the inside cover of her father's oldest book, drawn in his own hand, in ink he'd told her was "family business."

"He was clan-born," Sera said slowly. "He never—he said we were from the coast. He said—"

"People from Thornfell say a lot of things," Mara said gently. "I said them too, once. It keeps you safe right up until it doesn't."

Sera sat down abruptly on a rock, crossbow forgotten across her knees, and looked at the shard, and at Mara, and at the carved door looming behind them both with its ember-light still faintly glowing, as if the mountain were watching with the particular patience of something very old.

"He came back here once," she whispered. "When I was small. He came back with burns on his hands and wouldn't say how, and after that he started drinking on the anniversary of some day he never named. I thought—I thought it was the war."

"Maybe it was," Mara said. "Maybe it was this."

They sat in silence while the sun climbed. Then Sera looked up, eyes red-rimmed and hard as flint.

"I still don't trust you."

"Good," said Mara. "Trust is how Corvane gets people killed. Distrust me thoroughly, watch my back anyway, and we'll both live longer."

"That's not how any of this is supposed to work."

"No," Mara agreed, shouldering her pack and nodding toward the dark mouth of the ancient door, where the ember-light pulsed slow and deep, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating. "But I've tasted enough of how things are supposed to work to know I prefer how they actually do. Come on. Whatever's down there has been waiting a long time to be asked something."

"And if it asks *us*?"

Mara paused at the threshold, the warmth of old fire breathing against her face.

"Then," she said, "we'd better decide what we're willing to answer."
