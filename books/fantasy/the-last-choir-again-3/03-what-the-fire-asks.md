## 3. What the Fire Asks

The mountain did not want them.

Mara had known it from the first switchback, the way the paths seemed to unspool behind her and knit themselves shut ahead, as though Thornfell itself were a house closing its doors one room at a time. Sera walked half a step behind her, hand never far from her knife, and even she—the courier's daughter, raised on these roads in the way children are raised on lullabies they don't remember learning—kept glancing at the stone walls as if expecting them to lean in and listen.

"They're watching," Sera said, for the fourth time that morning.

"Of course they're watching." Mara picked her way over a shelf of ice-slick granite. "We're trespassing on the oldest road in the realms. I'd be more worried if they *weren't* watching."

"You're very calm for a woman being hunted by the king's own spymaster."

"I'm a food-taster, Sera. Calm is the whole of my profession. You learn to smile at a man while your tongue tells you his soup wants killing." She paused at a cleft in the rock where the wind funneled through with a low, organ-deep moan. "Besides, calm is cheaper than panic. Panic costs sleep."

The wards found them at dusk.

Mara felt it before she saw anything—a warmth in her veins, faint and insistent, like tea steeping too long. Then the cliff face ahead of them shimmered, and old carvings woke along it: spirals and claw-marks and lines of script in a tongue that predated the crown by a thousand years. The script began to glow, and the glow reached for her—not her eyes, not her hands, but her blood. She could feel it reading her, patient as a physician's fingers.

"What's happening?" Sera whispered.

"I think," Mara said slowly, "it's checking my references."

The light pulsed once, twice—and then settled into a steady amber, and the mountain opened. A passage yawned in the rock, breathing out air that smelled of sulfur and snow and something older underneath, something like hot iron left in the sun.

Sera stared at her. "Your blood has *marks* in it?"

"So it would seem." Mara flexed her fingers, unsettled. Eight years of swallowing death for a wage, and apparently her body had been keeping receipts. "My grandmother came from the high clans. I always assumed the stories about her were stories." She looked up into the dark throat of the passage. "Come on. If Emberwake dies before we reach him, none of this will have mattered, and I'd hate to have climbed all this for nothing."

* * *

Corvane rode through the foothills with the king's seal warm against his chest and three hundred crowns' worth of gold riding in his saddlebags, and he was, by his own considerable estimation, having an excellent week.

The courier was dead. The taster was running—running meant guilty, guilty meant predictable, and predictable meant cornered. And somewhere above him, in a hollow in the world's bones, a dragon sat dying on a hoard that would make the crown solvent for a century. All Corvane had to do was arrive before the fire went out, say the right words in the right order, and history would remember him as the man who ended the last inconvenient covenant in the realms.

He had said the right words in the right order his entire life. It was, frankly, his only talent, and he had made it sing.

At the last clan steading below the snowline, he stopped to be charming. He was very good at charming; it was the cousin of saying the right words. He drank their bitter heather ale, praised their grim hospitality, admired their ugly heirloom axes, and let slip—casually, so casually—that the crown might look kindly on clans who helped a royal envoy in his lawful business. He let slip the word *pardon*. He let slip the word *reward*. He watched the chieftain's brother count the gold coins Corvane let fall from his purse, one by one, like a man counting heartbeats.

Gold, Corvane had learned long ago, was simply poison with better manners. It killed slower, but it killed just as surely, and no one ever asked to taste it first.

By the time he rode on, two men in the hall were already looking at each other differently. That was all it ever took. Two men, one coin, and a little time.

* * *

Emberwake's hollow was a cathedral that grief had built.

Mara stood at its edge with Sera and could not speak. The cavern rose higher than any throne room, ribbed with stone the color of old embers, and at its center—coiled around a hoard that had gone dull as river pebbles—lay the dragon.

He was vast beyond sense, wings folded like a felled cathedral roof, scales the deep red of banked coals gone gray at their edges. His breathing came slow and far apart, each exhale a thread of smoke too thin to cast a shadow. Where once his fire must have lit the cavern like a second dawn, now a single guttering glow lived in his chest, pulsing. Fading.

"He's dying," Sera breathed.

"He's been dying for years." A voice from the shadows—an old woman, clan-marked, leaning on a staff of blackthorn. "Slowly. On purpose. A covenant holds only while both parties live, child, and Emberwake chose to burn low rather than burn out. Every year he gives less to the fire and keeps more for himself. But there is not much of himself left to keep."

"The courier," Mara said. "He came to find me. Why me?"

The old woman's eyes, pale as frost, studied her. "Because the covenant needs renewing, and renewal requires a witness. Not a signer. Not a king with ink and seals. A *witness*—one who has stood in death's doorway and walked back out, marked by poison survived, willing to stand there again. There are perhaps four such people alive." Her mouth twitched. "And only one of them was already carrying our map."

Mara laughed, short and without humor. "You mean my whole life—the tastings, the poisons, every hour I spent being the crown's expendable mouth—it was preparing me for this?"

"Nothing prepares anyone for anything," the old woman said. "But yes. Your blood remembers every poison you've survived. The covenant can read it. The question is whether you're willing." She turned toward the dim glow of the dragon's chest. "It will ask everything you've swallowed back up again, and ask you to hold it willingly, and offer it as proof. Most people who hear what that costs refuse. That's why the covenant is failing. Not malice. Refusal."

"And if I refuse?"

The old woman gestured at the dulling hoard, the graying scales, the thin thread of smoke. "Then the fire goes out within the season. And when the fire goes out, the peace written in it goes with it. The clans scatter. The crown takes the hoard. Wars start over less."

Mara looked at the dying dragon for a long moment. "I'd like to think about it," she said, "over something to eat. Old habit."

* * *

Corvane arrived at midnight, which was, he felt, the correct hour for destiny.

He came with six riders and the king's seal held high, and he made his offer standing in the great hollow with the dragon's fading glow painting him in shades of dying amber, and it was—Mara had to give him credit—a beautiful speech. He spoke of pardon. Of wealth beyond counting, a share of the hoard, lands, titles. He spoke of the future, of a realm unshackled from ancient bargains, of Mara Lockridge stepping out of the shadows at last into the light she deserved.

"You've spent eight years tasting death for people who'd feed you to it tomorrow," he said, and his voice was gentle, almost kind, and that was the most dangerous thing about him. "I'm offering you the opposite of poison, Mara. Everything you touch after today will nourish you. Say the word. Walk away rich. Walk away *safe*."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing she would not admit to anyone afterward: for three full breaths, she wanted it.

She wanted warmth that wasn't borrowed. A bed that wasn't guarded. To taste food for pleasure instead of profession. To stop running. Three breaths of wanting, sweet as honey with something bitter underneath.

Then she looked at the dragon. At the thread of smoke rising and falling, rising and falling, keeping faith with a bargain made before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath. Keeping faith badly, wearily, but keeping it.

"You're offering me everything," Mara said, "except the one thing I've never had."

"Which is?"

"A choice that doesn't cost someone else their life." She stepped forward. "No, Corvane. Find another witness."

Behind him, she heard steel leave scabbards—but not his riders'. Clan blades. Half the clan-folk in the hollow had drawn against the other half, gold already doing its quiet work, brothers sighting down their axes at cousins, the old woman's staff raised, her voice cracking across the stone: "*Hold!*"

Corvane smiled at Mara over the bristling chaos, unhurried, and drew a small vial from his coat—dark glass, wax-sealed, familiar in a way that turned her stomach cold.

"Then perhaps," he said pleasantly, "we should discuss what happens to witnesses who refuse. This arrived in my saddlebag two days ago, with a note suggesting you'd recognize it. Tell me, taster—can you identify it by smell alone? Because I'm told the dose in here would need to go into *something*."

He held the vial up to the dragon's failing light, and Mara's blood ran colder than the mountain around them.

She knew that seal. She knew that wax.

It was from the court kitchens. Someone back home had finally decided to return her eight years of service—with interest.
