## 4. The Last Choir

The hollow smelled of ash and old honey, the way a hearth smells long after the fire has gone to sleep but before it has quite decided to die.

Emberwake lay coiled in the deep of the mountain like a banked oven, scales gone the grey of cold cinders, one eye open and burning low—a coal wrapped in velvet. Mara stood at the edge of the covenant stone with the draught in her hands, and it was not a beautiful thing to hold. It looked like pond water that had made poor choices. It steamed faintly, though the hollow was cold enough to see her breath.

"Every poison you have ever tasted," Old Maren had said, when the clan mothers brewed it over three days of argument and one night of near-brawling. "Henbane. Wolfsbane. The king's little arsenic habit. That ghastly thing from the Estmark envoy—what was it, ground glass and spite?"

"Hemlock and powdered antimony," Mara said. "The spite was extra."

"That too." Maren had pressed the bowl into her hands with the gravity of a woman handing over her firstborn. "The covenant wants a witness who has died a hundred small deaths and walked away from every one. Your blood remembers them all. Let it speak."

Mara looked at the draught. She looked at Emberwake's dimming eye. And she looked—at last, because she had been avoiding it with the discipline of eight years at court—at Corvane.

The spymaster stood on the far side of the hollow where the clans had permitted him to stand, which was to say: surrounded by nine mountain men who were pretending very hard to be furniture. He had traded his riding leathers for court black, because Corvane believed presentation was half the battle, and he held the king's seal up in its lead case as though it were a lantern and everyone else moths.

"Mara Lockridge." His voice carried beautifully; he'd rehearsed. "You are a servant of the crown. You have been stolen from your post by smugglers and superstition. Return the seal's business to the crown's road, and you will be pardoned. Rewarded. There is a place for you at court—*honored*, this time. Not the taster's stool by the door. A chair. Land. A name."

Behind him, she could see what his gold had already done. Two of the Thornfell clansmen wouldn't meet their cousins' eyes. A purse changed hands at the edge of her vision with the furtive speed of a man swatting a fly he was ashamed of. Corruption, Mara thought, spreads like spilled oil—it finds every crack and makes the whole floor treacherous.

"A chair," she said.

"The king's own favor."

"I've watched what happens to people the king favors." She turned the bowl slowly in her hands. "They get chairs. Then they get shelves. Then they get graves with very nice inscriptions." She smiled at him, and it was the smile she used on suspicious soup. "I've tasted better offers than yours, my lord. Most of them killed me slower."

Corvane's jaw tightened. "You would throw away a pardon for *this*? For a dying lizard and a pack of hill-farmers?"

"She's not dying," said Sera, stepping forward out of the crowd, hand resting easy on her knife hilt. "She's *waiting*. There's a difference. My father taught me that." Her voice cracked only slightly on *father*, and Mara heard her catch it and carry on anyway, which took more courage than anything else that day.

Emberwake's eye flared—not much. A coal remembering it had once been a bonfire. The great head lifted, slow as a drawbridge, and the voice that came out was dry as pages turning.

"*Little taster.*"

Mara bowed. Eight years of practice; it came out graceful even with her heart hammering.

"You know what is asked," the dragon said. "A willing witness. Marked by poison survived. Blood freely given, freely bound. The old words do not bend, even for me."

"And if I refuse?" Mara asked, because she always asked, because hope was a habit she refused to break.

"Then I gutter out within the season, and the peace I hold dies with me, and the realms learn what the mountains look like without a keeper." The dragon's gaze slid past her to Corvane, and there was something almost amused in it. "And *that* one takes my hoard, and within ten years chokes on it, because gold is also a poison, and he has no tolerance built."

Mara laughed despite herself—a short, startled sound that echoed off the hollow walls. Then she stopped laughing, because the moment required it, and drank.

It tasted exactly as bad as it should have. Henbane first, sweet and wrong. Then the wolfsbane, numbing her tongue in rings. Then everything else arriving in a wave, every poison she had ever survived coming back at once like old debts called in on the same morning. Her vision went white at the edges. Her knees informed her they were resigning.

She did not fall. She knelt—deliberately, on the covenant stone—and opened her palm along the edge Maren had sharpened for her, and let her blood fall into the old grooves of the carving.

The words came back to her unbidden, the ones the courier had gasped with his last breath, the ones the wards had sung when they found the marks in her blood:

*What was sworn in fire is kept in blood. What is kept in blood outlives the crown.*

The hollow went silent. Even Corvane stopped talking, which for Corvane was a genuine miracle.

Then Emberwake breathed in.

Not the shallow sipping breath of a dying thing—the long, deep, *hungry* pull of something remembering its size. The grey scales flushed rose, then orange, then the deep molten gold of a forge at full song. Fire rolled down the hollow walls like a tide deciding to come home, and the heat of it should have killed her, should have cooked her where she knelt—

Instead it passed over her like a blessing, and Mara understood, dimly through the roaring in her ears, that her poisoned, scarred, stubborn blood had just been read aloud in the oldest language there was, and the fire knew her now. Knew every death she'd survived. Counted them, and found them sufficient.

Corvane ran.

Of course he ran. He made it eleven steps toward the tunnel mouth before the flame caught the king's seal in his raised hand—the lead case glowing, softening, the royal warrant inside curling into ash that swirled up and vanished. He stood staring at his empty, scorched palm with an expression Mara suspected he'd never worn before: honest astonishment.

The claim burned. The man did not. Emberwake's fire, it turned out, could tell the difference between a thing and the fool holding it.

* * *

Clan justice was faster than royal law and considerably more thorough.

The moot convened that same evening in the high meadow, nine clan heads on their stones, torches against the dark. The two clansmen who had taken Corvane's gold were not executed—they were *shamed*, which among the mountain folk was worse. Their names were struck from the roll for seven years. They would carry water for every hearth but their own, sit at no council, sing in no choir. One of them wept openly. The other looked, Mara thought, almost relieved; shame had a shape you could climb out of, eventually, if you were willing to haul water the whole way up.

Corvane himself they gave to the crown—alive, bound, and accompanied by a formal declaration that the clans renewed their covenant and would thank the king to send no further seals into the Thornfell unless he wanted them returned as ash. Sera insisted on drafting it herself. Her penmanship was atrocious and her phrasing was magnificent.

"He'll be tried in the capital," Sera said, watching the riders take him down the valley. "He'll talk his way out of most of it. They always do."

"Probably," Mara agreed. "But he'll never be warm again. Not really. Everyone at court will know what he reached for, and court runs on warmth the way a stove runs on wood." She shrugged. "For a spymaster, exile inside a palace is still exile."

Sera snorted. "You've spent too long around dragons. You're starting to sound like one."

"There are worse influences."

* * *

The king's letter came in spring, carried up the smuggler roads by a nervous courier who kept glancing at the sky.

It offered Mara everything. A title. Estates. A seat on the new Royal Council of Mountain Affairs, which Sera read aloud in a voice dripping with contempt ("they couldn't even think of a real name for it"). The king's gratitude, expressed in ink so fine it was nearly apologetic.

Mara read it twice by Emberwake's light, then folded it into careful quarters and fed it to the fire, which accepted it with a pleased rumble, the way a cat accepts a particularly good fish.

"You could have had a chair," Sera said, leaning in the mouth of the warden's cottage Mara had claimed—two rooms, one hearth, a view of the whole valley.

"I had a stool for eight years," Mara said. "Chairs are just stools that learned to betray you." She looked out at the evening, at the smoke rising straight from a dozen clan hearths, at the distant glitter of the dragon's flight-path against the first stars. "Out here, when someone poisons the stew, it's an accident, and they apologize, and you get extra bread. I've checked. Twice."

Sera laughed. "You're staying, then."

"I'm staying." It felt strange in her mouth—not heavy, exactly, but *settled*, like a bone knitting. "Someone has to keep the choir book. Someone has to taste the spring water before the clans drink, because apparently that's tradition now, and honestly? After eight years, it's the only prayer I know how to say."

Below them, the valley lights came on one by one, hearth by hearth, a scattered constellation answering the sky. And above the ridge, right on schedule, Emberwake crossed the dark in a ribbon of gold—the last dragon, no longer fading, keeping her rounds like a lighthouse that had finally been allowed to shine.

Mara watched until the glow passed behind the peak.

Then she went inside, hung the kettle, and began writing tomorrow's entry in the warden's log: *All roads quiet. All fires lit. One stranger expected from the south by week's end—a scholar, the clans say, asking questions about the old wards.*

She paused, pen hovering, and added one more line, because eight years at court had taught her that the quiet entries were always the ones worth reading twice:

*Taste everything before trusting anyone.*
