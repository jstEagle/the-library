## 1. The Weight of Small Numbers

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its books in a room with no windows, because windows let in weather and weather let in excuses. August Okafor had worked in that room for three years, and in that time she had learned the first law of ledgers: every number is a confession wearing a disguise.

She sat at her desk with a pot of cold tea, a stub of graphite, and the guild's quarterly accounts spread out before her like a patient awaiting diagnosis. The Ember Audit began at dawn. In eleven hours, auditors from the Concord of Faiths would walk through that door with their ink-stained fingers and their appetite for discrepancy, and everything August had balanced tonight would either hold or it wouldn't.

It would hold. It always held. That was the problem with being good at something nobody thanked you for.

"Column four," she murmured to herself, running her finger down the page. "You're lying to me."

The column was not, technically, lying. The numbers summed correctly. But the guild had received payment from the Widow Anselm's estate — two hundred crowns for the retrieval of a husband's shadow from a mirror he'd been foolish enough to die in front of — and the entry was dated three days before the widow had actually died. Someone had backdated it. Sloppily. With the kind of confidence that came from never having been audited by anyone who counted past their own fingers.

August corrected the date. She did not note who had made the error, because she already knew: Master Hesk, third chair, who believed that time was more of a suggestion than a system. She entered the correction under *clerical adjustment* and moved on.

This was the work. Not miracles. Miracles were what the guild sold; accounting was what the guild *was*. Every impossible thing they brokered — the shadows, the second chances, the love potions that actually worked and therefore ruined exactly as many lives as they saved — generated a debt somewhere, and debts were like cats. Ignore them and they multiplied.

The candle guttered. August trimmed the wick without looking up. Outside, the city of Vell was settling into its evening noises — cart wheels, drunk singing, the bell of Saint Ferren's tolling seven with its usual half-beat lag, which the priests insisted was piety and everyone else knew was a cracked bell. Somewhere out there, people were praying. Somewhere out there, gods were supposedly listening, though the gods hadn't answered anything in August's lifetime except with silence, and silence was free.

The guild's books said otherwise, but the guild's books said a lot of things.

She turned to the margins.

Here was the ritual no one knew about, not even the candle. In the bottom margin of each ledger page, in graphite so faint it might have been smudge, might have been the ghost of an erasure, August wrote small things. Her own name, sometimes. *A. Okafor, junior keeper.* Sometimes a single word: *tired*, or *cold*, or once, memorably, *the tea here is a war crime*. Things too small to be entries. Things that existed only because if she didn't write them somewhere, they would stop being true.

No auditor looked at margins. Margins were where arithmetic went to rest. In six years of Ember Audits, the Concord's men had examined every figure, every cross-reference, every seal and countersignature, and not one of them had ever glanced below the last ruled line. It was, August thought, the safest room in the world: the space between what was recorded and what was real.

*A. Okafor,* she wrote now, in letters the size of dust. *Balanced the books again. Nobody noticed. Again.*

Then, because honesty was a habit she couldn't break even in secret: *Nobody looked.*

* * *

The trouble with competence, August had learned, was that it made you furniture. Useful furniture, certainly — the guild would notice within a day if the ledgers stopped reconciling, the way you notice a missing chair — but furniture nonetheless. Nobody confided in furniture. Nobody invited furniture to drink after hours. When the nine members of the guild gathered in the meeting hall above, August carried the minutes in and carried them out, and between those two moments she was a pair of hands attached to a pen.

She told herself this was fine. She told herself it was even strategic. A junior ledger-keeper who was invisible could see everything, and seeing everything was worth more than being seen. This was true, and also it was the kind of truth you tell yourself at one in the morning over cold tea, which is to say: it was load-bearing and hollow.

Her desk drawer held the evidence of three years of almosts. A invitation to Master Corvane's midwinter dinner, addressed to *the accounting staff*, which had meant her, probably, but *probably* wasn't an address you could knock on. A note from Sefa — well, not yet, Sefa hadn't arrived yet, but there had been others, petitioners and debtors, who had lingered after their business was done and said things like *you're the only one here who looks at me like I'm a person* and then never come back, because the guild chewed through people and August watched from the margins, literally, writing it all down.

She flipped to the next account. The Candlemakers' Benevolent Fund, which was neither benevolent nor about candles, but was instead a laundering vehicle for the guild's contingency reserves, and which needed to look boring enough that the Concord's auditors would skim it. Boring was a craft. August had elevated it to an art.

The figures swam. She blinked, pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and saw the afterimage of columns burned into the dark — little ladders of numbers climbing nowhere.

Three years. Junior keeper. There were four ranks above her and she had climbed none of them, though the work she did was indistinguishable from the work of a second-rank keeper, and on bad nights, from the work of Corvane himself. She had raised this once, carefully, a year ago, framing it as a question about *responsibility structures*. Corvane had smiled his dry smile and said, "Responsibility follows trust, Miss Okafor, and trust follows time." Then he had given her a raise of four crowns a month, which was both generous and insulting, depending on how you held it up to the light.

She had decided to hold it up to the light exactly once, and then put it away.

* * *

At some point past midnight, her hand stopped moving.

Not from exhaustion, though exhaustion was there, folded into her shoulders like a letter into an envelope. Her hand stopped because she had reached the oldest section of the quarterly file — the carry-forward accounts, the ones that rolled from year to year like stones rolling downhill, gathering moss and mystery — and one of them was wrong.

Not arithmetically wrong. Arithmetically it was perfect, which was itself strange, because old accounts were never perfect; they accreted errors the way hulls accreted barnacles. This one was pristine. Every figure carried forward cleanly across forty years of audits. Every interest calculation was exact to the copper. It was the most beautiful piece of bookkeeping August had ever seen, and it gave her the same feeling as a smile from a stranger on a dark street.

The account had no debtor name. It had no creditor name either — just a designation in the oldest hand in the file, a script she'd seen only twice before, on documents the guild kept behind glass: *Account Zero.*

Interest accrued annually. Principal: never touched, never reduced, never discussed. In the notes column, in place of the usual annotations, was a single line, repeated identically every year for four decades:

*Held in trust. Do not audit.*

Except someone had written *do not audit* in a ledger, which was like writing *do not think of a blue door*, and every auditor who had ever passed over it had passed over it precisely because they were told to, and obedience was the most reliable lock in the world.

August stared at it for a long time. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. Her tea had achieved room temperature and moral ambiguity.

Tomorrow the Concord would come, and Account Zero would sit in the sealed section where sealed sections lived, and no one would open it, because no one ever had, and the guild's whole architecture rested on that never. She felt the way she imagined a locksmith feels finding a door with no keyhole: not locked, exactly. Just *finished*. Completed by whoever built it, in a way that suggested the builder hadn't wanted anyone deciding later whether to go through.

She wrote in the margin, smaller than ever, barely a breath of graphite:

*What are you?*

And underneath, because the ritual demanded her name and her name was the only currency she had that couldn't be audited:

*A. Okafor was here. Nobody knows.*

* * *

She finished the balancing at half past two. Everything reconciled. She initialed the master sheet, blotted it, squared the pages, and tied the file with the grey ribbon that meant *ready for inspection*, and she sat for a moment in the windowless quiet feeling the specific loneliness of a job well done, which is the loneliness of shouting into a well and hearing your own voice come back very slightly changed.

On her way out, she paused at the shelf where the historical ledgers slept under their dust cloths. Forty years of them. Behind them, older still, in a cabinet she had keys for and had never used, the founding records. She had inventoried that cabinet on her first week — it was part of orientation, a Corvane tradition, *know what we keep* — and remembered only that the oldest volume was heavier than it should have been, as if the paper inside had been compressed by more than time.

She didn't open it. She was tired, and tomorrow was the Audit, and curiosity was a debt too, and she had learned to keep her personal accounts clean.

But at the door, with her hand on the latch, she stopped. Because the thought arrived fully formed, the way the truest thoughts do, unwelcome and punctual:

*Forty years of interest on Account Zero, accruing without pause, against a principal nobody will name. Interest doesn't accrue forever unless someone intends to pay eventually. And nobody pays interest for forty years unless the principal is enormous.*

Or unless the payment isn't money at all.

August stood in the dark doorway a moment longer than she needed to, listening to the building settle around her — the tick of cooling pipes, the creak of the meeting-hall floor overhead where nine chairs waited for nine members, minus however many wouldn't come. Then she locked the ledger room, pocketed the key, and climbed the stairs toward her narrow bed in the keepers' wing, telling herself the familiar lie: *it's just an account. Accounts are just numbers.*

Behind her, in the margin of the quarterly file, in graphite the size of dust, her name dried in the dark where no one would ever look.

Dawn was five hours away. The Ember Audit was coming. And somewhere in the sealed vault, something old and perfectly balanced was waiting to be read.
