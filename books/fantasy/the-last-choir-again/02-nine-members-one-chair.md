## 2. Nine Members, One Chair

The Guild of Impossible Things had nine chairs and, on the morning of the Ember Audit's eve, only six bodies to fill them.

August counted twice, because counting was what she was for. Master Corvane at the head of the long table, in his charcoal coat with the silver thread fraying at the left cuff—the cuff he always turned away from the room, as if wear were a confession. Mistress Ivo of the Reconciliation Desk, who smelled of ink and vinegar and had not smiled since the Flood Ledger incident, which predated August's employment by eleven years. The twins, Pellam and Ostro Vane, who shared one vote between them by ancient charter and used it like a weapon they took turns holding. And old Brother Quill, retired three times, un-retired each time because no one else could read his own shorthand.

Six. Three chairs empty: the Chair of Foreign Obligations, vacant nine years; the Chair of Miraculous Claims, vacant since its last occupant ascended—literally, according to the minutes, though August suspected a clerical euphemism for *ran off to the coast*; and the ninth chair, which no one ever sat in, which was kept dusted and empty for reasons the charter described only as *customary*.

She stood at her station along the wall, ledger open, quill trimmed, and wrote: *Quorum insufficient. Meeting proceeds regardless.* That was the first impossible thing recorded that day, and it wasn't even noon.

"Read the roll," Corvane said.

"Master Corvane. Mistress Ivo. Messrs. Vane, jointly. Brother Quill." She paused. "The remaining seats are... unoccupied."

"They're never occupied," Ostro Vane said, without looking up from the letter he was folding into a shape that was either a bird or an insult.

"The seats are occupied," Corvane corrected mildly. "The occupants are elsewhere." He said it the way he said most things—as if the distinction mattered enormously and he alone could be bothered to hold it. "Proceed."

August proceeded. She read the quarter's balances aloud: the account of Deferred Rainfall, down four percent; the fund for Second Chances, technically insolvent but carried forward on the strength of a promissory note from a man who had promised to become someone else entirely and, remarkably, had; the Candle Reserve, stable. Her voice did the work her face refused to do—steady, dry, precise—and she felt the small cold satisfaction she always felt when numbers behaved. Numbers didn't look through you. Numbers didn't decide you were furniture.

"Ember Audit tomorrow," Corvane said when she finished. "The Concord of Faiths sends its assessors at dawn. Everything must balance. Everything must *appear* to balance, where appearance is all we have." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; it was how he made other people pick them up. "Which brings us to the vault."

August's quill stopped moving. She hadn't told it to.

"The sealed vault," Ivo said slowly, "has not been opened in—"

"Fifty-one years," Corvane said. "Yes. And the Concord's charter requires that every account held by this guild be available for inspection. Every account." He turned his head, and the light from the high windows caught the gray at his temples, and his eyes found August along the wall with the ease of a man who had known exactly where she was standing before he looked. "Okafor."

"Master."

"You will conduct the preliminary audit of the sealed vault tonight. Full inventory. Every entry verified against physical holdings. You'll present your findings to me before the assessors arrive."

The room did something complicated and quiet. Ivo's teacup paused halfway to her mouth. One of the twins—Pellam, August thought, though telling them apart was itself a minor miracle—actually looked up.

"That's a two-week job," Ivo said. "The vault inventory alone took Master Ellery a fortnight, and he had assistants."

"Ellery had assistants," Corvane agreed. "Ellery also had a habit of rounding. Okafor doesn't round." He said it like praise. It landed like a weight being tied to her ankle. "She balances to the copper. I've watched her do it for three years. If anyone can make fifty-one years of silence legible in one night, it's her."

*If anyone can.* August heard the sentence's second floor clearly, the one underneath: *and when you fail, it will prove the task was impossible, not that we neglected it.* She was being handed a test designed to be failed, dressed up as trust, and everyone at the table knew it except, apparently, the table.

"She's junior," Pellam Vane said. "Junior keepers don't touch the sealed accounts. Charter section—"

"I know what section," Corvane said, and the mildness in his voice acquired an edge so fine you could shave with it. "I am invoking the provision for exigent circumstances. The Ember Audit is exigent. Are we clear?"

Nobody argued with Corvane when he used that voice. Even the twins' shared vote stayed in its pocket.

"Tonight," he repeated, to August. "Alone. The fewer hands on old paper, the better. Paper remembers fingerprints."

It was meant as procedure. It came out sounding like a warning, or a gift, or both, and August spent the rest of the meeting trying to decide which.

* * *

The meeting limped through its remaining business—a dispute over whether a wish granted retroactively constituted income (the twins said yes, Ivo said the man had wished for it *before* it happened, which raised questions about time that no one wanted written into the minutes)—and then it broke apart the way meetings do, chairs scraping, voices resuming their ordinary registers, six people becoming six separate weather systems.

Corvane lingered.

He did it deliberately, August knew. He arranged his papers with the unhurried care of a man who wanted to be the last one out, and when the door had closed behind Brother Quill's slow shuffle, he spoke without looking up.

"You're angry."

"I'm not angry, Master."

"You've been re-inking the same line for two minutes. It's gone black as sin." He finally glanced at the ledger, then at her. "Sit."

There was only one spare chair—the ninth chair, the customary one, the one nobody sat in. August looked at it the way you'd look at a sleeping animal.

"It won't bite," Corvane said. "That's the whole point of it."

She sat. The chair was more comfortable than it had any right to be, which somehow made it worse.

"Do you know why the ninth chair exists?"

"No, Master."

"Neither does anyone else under seventy. Which is my point." He set down his pen. "When I was junior keeper—your age, give or take a bad winter—I asked the same question. Old Master Ferrow told me: the chair is for the member who isn't here yet. Every guild needs a seat saved for the person who will change it. Most years, that person never comes. The chair stays empty, and we dust it, and we go on."

"And this year?"

"This year I'm looking at her." He said it plainly, without warmth, which was the trick of it—Corvane never gave you warmth you could bank. "You count things nobody counts, Okafor. You noticed the Candle Reserve gained twelve candles last month that no requisition explains. You flagged a debt of gratitude owed by a woman who died owing it, and you asked—who inherits gratitude? Nobody asks that. Accountants don't ask that. You ask it because you can't help it."

"Thank you, Master."

"Don't thank me yet." He stood, buttoned his coat over the frayed cuff. "The vault audit is real work, and it's dangerous work—not physically, though the stairs are rotten—but dangerous the way old letters are dangerous. Fifty-one years of entries no one has reconciled. Whatever's in there has been compounding interest in the dark." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, back still to her. "One instruction above all the others. Read everything. Verify everything. But whatever you find—"

He turned his head just enough that she caught his profile, sharp against the hall's dimness.

"—bring it to me before you bring it to anyone. Including the page. Especially the page."

"What page?" August asked.

Corvane smiled, briefly, like a coin flashed in a market crowd, and was gone.

She sat in the ninth chair for a long time after, listening to the building settle around its secrets, and tried to name what she felt. Not pride—he'd praised her, but praise from Corvane was bait with excellent presentation. Not fear, exactly. Something adjacent. The feeling of being seen so precisely that it amounted to being handled.

Underestimated, she'd have called it yesterday. Underestimated was comfortable. Underestimated meant invisible, and invisible meant safe, and safety was the thing she'd built her whole quiet life out of, entry by entry, margin by margin.

But Corvane hadn't underestimated her. Corvane had *measured* her, to the copper, the way she measured everything else, and had decided what she was worth and what she was for.

That was different. That was worse. That was—

*Inventory,* said a thought she didn't invite, and she put it away and went to find the vault key.

* * *

The key lived in a drawer in Ivo's desk that required two keys to open, one of which hung around Ivo's neck and the other of which, per tradition, was kept inside the vault itself, which was either perfect security or perfect nonsense depending on your philosophy of locks.

Ivo retrieved it without comment, but she held it a moment before handing it over—an iron thing, longer than a thumb, cold enough that August wondered if it had been kept somewhere colder than drawers.

"Fifty-one years," Ivo said. "Master Ellery went in with a lamp and two assistants and came out with white hair. He was thirty."

"Was it the work?"

"He never said. He resigned by letter, from a town none of us could find on a map, and his letter was one sentence." Ivo's eyes, vinegar-pale, held hers. "*The books are honest; that is the problem.* Then nothing. For half a century."

"What did it mean?"

"If I knew, girl, I'd have burned this guild down myself years ago." She pressed the key into August's palm and closed her fingers over it, an unexpected touch, dry and brief. "Lamp oil is in the antechamber. Don't trust the third stair. And Okafor—" a hesitation, the first August had ever seen in her, "—if you find an account you don't understand, don't force it. Some debts explain themselves when they're ready. Some never do. Both kinds ruin people who rush them."

Then she went back to her vinegar and her ink, and August descended.

* * *

The stairs were exactly as advertised: forty-one steps, the third one soft as bread, spiraling down through stone that got older as it went. The guildhall above was three centuries proud, all carved lintels and charter-oak, but the stone down here was older than the guild, older than the city's walls, quarried by people whose names had rotted out of every record. August had worked in this building three years and never known it stood on bones this deep.

The antechamber smelled of lamp oil and dust and, faintly, of something else—cold ash, maybe, or the memory of smoke. She lit the lamp. Its flame stood up straight and small, and she noticed, distantly, that it burned slightly blue at the base, which lamps weren't supposed to do, and filed the observation away in the drawer of her mind marked *later.*

The vault door was iron banded with bronze, and across it, in script older than the guild's alphabet, someone had written a word she couldn't read. Below it, in newer paint, someone else had translated:

**PAID IN FULL — DO NOT REOPEN THE ACCOUNT**

Below that, in a third hand, smaller, almost apologetic:

*(it was not paid in full)*

August stood before three sentences written by three people across some gulf of years, disagreeing with each other in descending font sizes, and felt the specific vertigo of a ledger-keeper confronting evidence that the books had been lying.

She put the key in the lock. It turned too easily, as if it had been waiting.

Inside: shelves. Rows upon rows of them, receding past the lamplight, and on them ledgers—hundreds, thousands, bound in leather gone stiff and dark, spines labeled in fading ink. *Accounts Receivable: Prayers Unanswered, 1189–1204. Deferred Miracles, Third Register. The Book of Small Mercies. Debts of Gratitude, Inherited. Fire, Miscellaneous.*

Fire, Miscellaneous.

Her lamp flame leaned toward that shelf. Just slightly. Just enough that she noticed, and told herself it was a draft, in a sealed room, underground, with no draft anywhere to be found.

She began at the beginning, because that was the discipline, that was the whole of her: start at the first page, verify every entry, balance every column. She pulled the oldest ledger from the oldest shelf. It was heavier than it should have been. The cover bore no title, only a date older than the guild's founding by a full generation—which should have been impossible, because the guild's records began with the guild.

Unless the guild had begun before the guild. Unless someone had kept these books first, and the guild had merely moved in, the way a family moves into a house still warm from the last fire.

August opened it.

The first entry was written in a hand that made her breath catch—not because it was beautiful, though it was, but because it was *familiar*, the way a voice heard once through a wall is familiar. A single line, centered on the page like scripture:

*Received, in trust: one debt of fire, owed to VASKARATH, Last of the Burning Kind. Terms sealed. Guarantor named. Let no keeper balance this column until the fire comes due.*

And beneath it, in the margin, in ink so fresh it might have been written that morning—in handwriting that was not quite, not exactly, but terribly close to her own—

*It is coming due now.*

The lamp flame stood very still. Somewhere above her, muffled by forty-one stairs and three centuries of oak, the guildhall clock began to strike midnight, and August Okafor, junior ledger-keeper, alone in a room full of fifty-one silent years, reached out with a hand that was not entirely steady and turned to the next page.
