## 3. What the Vault Keeps

The sealed vault sat beneath the guildhall like a secret the building had swallowed and never digested.

August had worked in the Guild of Impossible Things for three years and had walked past the iron door at the bottom of the ledger stairs every one of those days without once seeing it open. It wasn't hidden. That was the strange part. It was simply there, banded in black iron, with a lock the size of a dinner plate and no handle on the inside—because nothing that went in was ever meant to come out wanting to.

Now she stood in front of it with a ring of six keys, a lantern, a slate for notes, and Corvane's parting words still warm in her ears: *Take your time, August. Thoroughness is its own reward.*

Which, from Master Corvane, meant either *I believe in you* or *I expect you to fail slowly enough that it doesn't embarrass me.* With him you rarely got to know which until afterward, and sometimes not even then.

The first key didn't fit. Neither did the second. The third turned halfway and stuck, and August spent four minutes persuading it with the particular patience of someone who had spent her life coaxing crooked numbers into straight columns. On the fourth key, the lock exhaled—a sound like a long-held breath finally released—and the door swung inward on hinges that hadn't screamed, which somehow made it worse. Well-oiled hinges meant someone came down here regularly.

The lantern light fell into a room that should not have existed under a guildhall of this size.

She'd imagined shelves. Dust, maybe, and the dry smell of old paper. What she got instead was a single reading desk in the center of a round stone chamber, and around the walls, floor to ceiling, ledgers. Not hundreds. Thousands. Spines of cracked leather, spines of vellum, spines of something dark and faintly iridescent that she decided not to think about. Some were chained shut. One, on a pedestal of its own near the back, was wrapped in a cloth so old it had gone the color of bone.

And the air—Gods, the air. It tasted the way a struck flint smells. Ozone and char and something underneath both, older than either. Like standing in a room where a fire had once been so large it had left a permanent memory of itself in the stones.

August set her lantern on the desk, took out her slate, and did what she always did when the world presented her with too much at once.

She started counting.

*Ledgers visible: approximately two thousand nine hundred. Chained: forty-one. Wrapped: one. Windows: none. Exits: one.* Her handwriting on the slate was small and neat, because neatness was armor, and armor was the only kind she owned.

There was no index. Of course there was no index. An index would imply someone wanted these found. She began at the nearest shelf instead, working left to right the way she read everything, pulling volumes at random and checking their dates against the guild's official founding—the year the Charter of Nine was signed, the year the first impossible thing was officially recorded as *managed.*

Every ledger predated it.

Not by years. By centuries.

Her hand paused on a spine stamped with a date four hundred years before the guild existed. Four hundred years before there was a Guild of Impossible Things, someone had been keeping accounts of impossible things. Which meant the guild hadn't invented the trade. It had inherited it. Like a debt.

August wrote that word on her slate before she could stop herself, then stared at it.

*Inherited.*

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Right."

* * *

She worked for two hours before she found it.

By then she'd developed a system—she always developed a system—and the system said: oldest shelf, lowest row, where the damp from the foundations crept in and the leather had gone soft as skin. She knelt, which put her at the level of the floor itself, and pulled out a volume that weighed far more than paper had any right to weigh.

It had no title on the spine. It had no title anywhere. But when she opened it to the first page, the parchment was not parchment. It was hide of some kind, thin and pale, and across the top, in ink that had gone brown with age but had not faded—not one letter, not one stroke—was written:

**Account of the Debt of Fire.**

Below it, smaller:

**Creditor: VASKARATH.**

August knew every name in the guild's histories. She'd memorized them the way other children memorized saints. Founders, masters, auditors, the nine signatures on the charter. There was no Vaskarath. There had never been a Vaskarath. The name meant nothing.

Except that it meant everything, because her hands had started shaking before her mind caught up, and her body had known something her head did not.

She set the book flat on the floor, knelt over it, and read.

The opening entries were transactional in a way that made her stomach turn, precisely because they were so calm. This was the language she lived in. Debits, credits, terms, interest. She'd expected—she didn't know what she'd expected. Screaming, maybe. Curses. Instead she got bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping was worse.

*Received: 1 (one) covenant of survival, binding upon the undersigned and their heirs in perpetuity.*

*Consideration rendered by creditor: 1 (one) act of fire, magnitude unrecorded, sufficient to the purpose.*

*Purpose: see annex.*

There was no annex.

There was, however, a second page, and the second page had numbers, and August Okafor had never in her life met a number she couldn't face. So she faced them.

Fire, it turned out, could be measured. In units she'd never heard of—*breaths*, the ledger called them, which was an absurd word for a unit until she remembered that fire breathed, that flames fed on air, that somewhere out there was a creature for whom flame was not a tool but a body. And against those breaths, columned neatly in a hand very different from the first, were withdrawals. Small ones. Regular ones. A few breaths here, a dozen there, taken at intervals of decades, each withdrawal annotated in the margin with a single word or phrase:

*Rain, city of Vel. Paid.*
*A harvest that did not fail. Paid.*
*A war ended early. Paid.*
*A prayer answered. Paid.*

Paid, paid, paid. Someone had been drawing down this account for four hundred years, spending fire on miracles, and every miracle had a return address, and every return address matched the kinds of petitions the guild logged every day in its front-facing books. The impossible things the guild *managed*—the droughts broken, the plagues halted, the storms turned—they weren't managed at all. They were purchased. From something called Vaskarath. Out of an account the guild kept locked in a basement and never spoke of.

August sat back on her heels. The cold of the stone floor came up through her skirts and she barely felt it.

"No," she said quietly. "No, that's not—" 

But it was. She ran the arithmetic the way she ran all arithmetic, ruthlessly, because mercy was for after the numbers were done. If the withdrawals averaged what she estimated, across the interval recorded, then the original deposit—the *act of fire, magnitude unrecorded*—had to have been enormous. Oceanic. The kind of fire that reshaped coastlines.

And the account was not infinite. No account was. She flipped forward through the pages, faster now, watching the balance column shrink entry by entry, century by century, the way she'd watched her mother's coin purse shrink in the last winter of her mother's life. Slowly at first. Then not slowly.

The most recent entry was dated eleven years ago.

Eleven years ago, someone had come down into this vault and recorded a withdrawal of three breaths of fire, and the annotation beside it read: *A girl in the Lowmarsh district recovered from fever. Paid.*

August had been fourteen. She had lived in the Lowmarsh district. She had burned with fever for nine days while her mother prayed at the bedside, and on the tenth morning the fever broke like a snapped rope, and the priests at the corner shrine had called it a blessing, and her mother had wept and thanked every god whose name she knew.

Three breaths of fire. Paid out of an account owed to something called Vaskarath.

August's hands were shaking so badly now that the pages rattled.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "That's not—I'm not—" But even as she said it, she was doing the math backward, matching dates, matching districts, matching the shape of a miracle to the shape of her own life, and the math was clean. The math was always clean. That was the cruelty of math. It didn't care what you wanted it to say.

She forced herself to keep reading. There was a final section, near the back, written in that same second hand—the hand of whoever maintained the account—and it was not a transaction. It was a note. Just a note, tucked between entries like a letter slipped into a drawer:

*The guarantor's line continues. The debt sleeps while the line lives. When the line ends, the account comes due in full.*

*Note to successor: do not let the line end.*

August read it three times.

*The guarantor's line.*

Someone had guaranteed this debt. Someone whose blood was collateral, whose descendants were the payment plan, whose continued existence was the only thing standing between the guild and whatever happened when a four-hundred-year account of dragon-fire came due all at once. And the guild had built itself on top of that guarantee, brick by brick, charter clause by charter clause, and never told anyone. Or told almost no one. Or—

Her grandmother's face rose unbidden, the way it always did: sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued, smelling of woodsmoke and dried herbs, telling little August stories about a world where gods still answered. *You have an old soul, girl,* she used to say, pressing a thumb hard against August's sternum, hard enough to leave a ghost of pressure that August could still feel sometimes, twenty years later. *Older than you know. Older than me.*

August had thought it was just something grandmothers said.

She closed the ledger. Her hands wouldn't fully obey her, but they closed it. She stayed kneeling on the cold floor for a long time, listening to the silence of the vault, which was not really silence at all—she understood that now. It was the quiet of a held breath. Four hundred years of held breath, waiting for someone to count it.

Above her, muffled through feet of stone, the guildhall went about its morning. Doors opened and closed. Somewhere, a clerk laughed at something. The Ember Audit was coming, and the members were polishing their ledgers and rehearsing their smiles, and none of them—surely none of them—knew that the entire institution rested on a debt of fire and a bloodline used as surety.

None of them except whoever had written that note to a successor.

*Do not let the line end.*

August looked down at the ledger in her lap, at the pale hide cover, at the brown ink that refused to fade, and felt the first crack go through the world she trusted. Not a loud crack. That was the terrible part. It sounded like nothing at all—like a hairline fracture in a teacup, discoverable only when the tea came through. Three years of belonging, of competence, of a desk of her own and a master who said *thoroughness is its own reward*—all of it still standing, still holding tea, and now permanently, invisibly split.

She had been hired three years ago. Out of dozens of applicants. A nobody from the Lowmarsh with good handwriting and no connections, given a junior post in the most selective guild in the city.

She had thought it was luck.

She picked up the ledger, wrapped it in her shawl against the vault's chill, and carried it toward the door. Then stopped. Carrying it out would be theft. Leaving it would be obedience. She stood in the doorway with the lantern guttering and the weight of the book pulling at her arms like a living thing, and she made the smallest, first, worst decision of her new life.

She left the ledger on its shelf—but she copied the guarantor clause onto her slate, and the last withdrawal, and the name. **VASKARATH**, in her own small neat hand, the letters steady now, because writing them down made them hers.

Then she climbed the stairs toward the daylight, toward the guildhall, toward Master Corvane's office, where she would report that the vault was in order, the records intact, nothing amiss.

She was halfway up when she realized her hands had stopped shaking.

That frightened her more than the shaking had.

At the top of the stairs, the duty clerk looked up from his desk. "Well? Corvane wants your report."

"The vault is in order," August heard herself say, smooth as a balanced column. "Nothing unusual."

The clerk nodded and looked back down at his work, and August walked past him into the ordinary morning light of the guildhall, carrying a name under her tongue like a coal she couldn't spit out.

*Vaskarath.*

Behind her, down in the dark, four hundred years of held breath waited to see what she would do next.
