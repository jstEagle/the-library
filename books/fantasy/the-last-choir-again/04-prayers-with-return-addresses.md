## 4. Prayers with Return Addresses

The impossible arrived on a Tuesday, because the impossible had never once had the decency to arrive at a convenient hour.

August was three days past the vault, and she had spent those days doing what she did best: pretending nothing had happened. She reconciled the tallow accounts. She chased a discrepancy of eleven coppers through the candlewrights' guild until it confessed to being a transposition error. She ate lunch at her desk, alone, the way she always did, and she did not once—except constantly, in every quiet moment—think about the sealed account at the bottom of the oldest drawer.

*Vaskarath.* The name sat under her tongue like a stone. She'd taken to carrying a peppermint so she could shift it from one cheek to the other whenever the name surfaced, as if sweetness were an argument against dread.

At half past two, the front bell rang.

Not the debtor's knock—that was a particular rhythm, three tired raps that every junior keeper learned to recognize before they learned the guild's seal. Not the merchant's ring either, which was quick and self-important. This was a single pull, held long, the kind of pull made by someone who wasn't sure they wanted whoever was inside to answer.

August looked up. The duty clerk had gone to lunch, which meant the door was hers.

She opened it onto a woman who looked like weather damage. Fifties, maybe older—the kind of age you couldn't pin down on someone who'd been rained on by life for decades. Gray threaded her close-cropped hair. Her coat was a priest's coat, sheared of its insignia, the shoulder seams still bearing pale ghosts where the sigils of the Concord of Faiths had been picked off stitch by stitch. A defrocked cleric, then. August had seen two or three come through the guild's doors over the years. They always carried themselves the same way: spine straight out of habit, eyes apologizing for it.

"I'm looking for the Guild of Impossible Things," the woman said.

"You've found it." August stepped aside. "Though I'll warn you, we're mostly ledgers."

"Good," the woman said, and came in like a tide coming into a harbor—slowly, inevitably, taking up more room than her body required. "Ledgers are honest. It's everything else about faith that lies."

"I'm August Okafor. Junior ledger-keeper."

"Sefa Amari. Formerly Sister Sefa Amari, Temple of the Nine Winds, before the Nine Winds stopped blowing and the Concord decided my temple was redundant rather than ruined." She looked around the entry hall with the flat appraisal of someone cataloguing exits. "I owe this guild money."

That got August's full attention. "You do?"

"Eleven years ago. A debt of one impossible thing, incurred when your guild arranged—" Sefa paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—an intervention. During the drought year. My temple's well had gone dry, and the Concord wouldn't fund a divining, so I came here."

August knew the drought year. Every ledger-keeper did; it filled two full volumes. The guild had done something—no one junior ever learned what—to bring water back to the eastern districts. The price had been scattered across forty debtors, each owing a single impossible thing, payable whenever the guild called.

"We've never called yours," August said.

"No." Sefa's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Which is why I'm here to pay early. Because last night, I prayed for rain. And this morning—" she reached into her coat, slowly, the way you reach for something you're afraid might bite, "—it answered."

She set a clay pot on August's desk. Small, unglazed, the kind sold for a copper at any market stall. Inside was ash.

August looked at it. Looked at Sefa. Looked back at the ash.

"That's not rain," she said.

"No," Sefa agreed. "It is not rain."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the city of Coriel, in the four-hundredth-and-somethingth year of the Guild's charter: it had not seen true weather in sixty years.

This was not a metaphor. The cloud-seas that once rolled in off the western hills had thinned, then frayed, then simply stopped arriving, and now the sky over Coriel was the same pale enamel every day, warm and blank as the inside of a bowl. Rain happened in other places. Fog was a rumor grandmothers told. The city drank from deep aquifers and paid water-tithe to the Concord of Faiths, who maintained—so they claimed—a covenant with heaven that kept the springs flowing.

The gods, everyone agreed, had gone quiet. Not dead. Quiet. Like parents in the next room. You didn't pray expecting answers anymore; you prayed the way you wrote letters to someone who'd moved away without leaving an address. It was good for the soul, everyone said. It kept the channels open.

Nobody had received a reply in living memory.

And yet Sefa Amari sat across from August with a pot of cold ash and said, "Tell me exactly what happened. From the beginning. Leave nothing out because it sounds foolish. Foolishness is often just detail wearing poor clothes."

August took out a fresh page. Her hand hovered. Then she wrote the date, the time, the names of the parties present, because procedure was a rope you could hold onto when the floor went strange.

"The beginning," Sefa said, "is that I have prayed every night for eleven years. Same prayer. I won't tell you what it is—not yet—but it has never been answered, not once, not in any way I could measure. Last night I prayed something different. I stood on my roof—it's flat, I sleep up there in summer—and I looked at that awful blank sky, and I thought: *I am tired of talking to no one. If anyone is listening, anything at all: send rain. Real rain. I don't care from whom.*"

"And?"

"And I went to sleep. And at some hour past midnight, I woke because I heard—falling. A soft falling, all around the house, like ten thousand small feet. I went to the window." Sefa's voice stayed level, but her hands, folded on the desk, had gone white-knuckled. "It was raining, ledger-keeper. Over my house only. A column of rain perhaps twenty feet wide, silver in the moonlight, falling from a clear sky. I put my hands out into it. It was cold. It smelled of—" she frowned, searching, "—of struck flint. Of fire just after it dies."

"How long?"

"Until I fell asleep again, I think. An hour? When I woke at dawn, the ground was dry. No puddles. No wet leaves. Nothing. Except—" she nodded at the pot, "—that. In the gutter, where the rain had run. Ash. Cold, gray, fine as flour. I gathered what I could."

August looked at the ash. Against every professional instinct she had, against the voice in her head that recited the guild's first maxim—*we record what people owe, not what people claim*—she dipped one finger in and touched it.

Cold. Not cool. Cold, like a thing that had been cold for a very long time and intended to stay that way. It clung to her fingertip with a faint static grip, and when she rubbed it between finger and thumb it made no grit at all, just a whisper of powder, and left behind a smell like a snuffed candle in a closed room.

Every ledger-keeper developed a nose for residue. Incense, saltpeter, alchemical salts, the ozone stink of storm-glass charms—August had catalogued them all during her training. This matched none of them. This matched *nothing*, and the matching-nothing was worse than any smell would have been.

She washed her hands at the basin without letting her face change, and then she wrote:

*Claim received: precipitation, localized, nocturnal, unsolicited. Residue collected. Residue matches no known substance in Guild reference volumes I–XIV. Claimant: Sefa Amari, defrocked, debtor (drought year, uncalled). Assessment:*

Her pen stopped.

Assessment was supposed to be the easy part. That was the whole architecture of the guild—you took the wild screaming chaos of what people experienced, and you reduced it to entries, categories, valuations. Miracles were priced. Hauntings were amortized. Curses were refinanced at punitive interest. Nothing entered these books without becoming a number, and nothing stayed frightening once it became a number.

But there was no category for this. There was no column for *the sky answered a private prayer in a city where the sky has forgotten how*. The forms didn't have boxes. The boxes were how August kept the world at arm's length, and this morning the world had leaned over her desk and breathed on the paperwork.

She wrote: *Assessment: pending.*

Cowardice, in ledger form. She knew it even as the ink dried.

* * *

They talked for another hour. August asked questions in the guild's idiom—duration, extent, witnesses, prior claims—and Sefa answered each one with a precision that kept snagging on August's attention. Defrocked priests weren't supposed to be precise. They were supposed to be bitter and vague, full of grievance instead of method. But Sefa gave measurements. Twenty feet wide, she estimated, maybe twenty-five. Beginning at roughly the third bell past midnight, ending before dawn. No other houses touched; her neighbor's washing, hung late, had stayed dry.

"You sound like an auditor," August said finally.

"I was my temple's registrar for nine years." Sefa almost smiled. "Before I was its priest. Faith was my second vocation. Records were my first." She tapped the desk with one finger, right beside August's open ledger. "So believe me when I tell you what I checked this morning, before I came here. I checked whether I dreamed it—I hadn't; there was ash in the gutter. I checked whether someone played a jest—no one in my street owns a rain-charm, and rain-charms don't work anyway, the aquifer wardens would have fined them. I checked whether I'd been drugged, whether I'd been hexed, whether the Temple of Small Waters had decided to make a point about the Concord's water monopoly. All negative." She leaned forward. "What remains, junior ledger-keeper, is that something heard me. Something with power over water, or the shape of it, answered a prayer in Coriel for the first time in sixty years. And it left ash instead of water on the ground. Does that suggest anything to you?"

August thought of a drawer at the bottom of a vault. Of an account older than the guild. Of a creditor named Vaskarath whose currency, if the marginalia could be trusted, was *fire*.

Fire that fell as cold rain and dried into ash.

"It suggests," August said carefully, "that you should leave that pot with us. As collateral against your debt. And that you should tell no one else. Not your neighbors, not old colleagues, certainly not anyone wearing Concord colors."

"Why?"

Because if the Concord auditors hear that prayers are getting return addresses, August didn't say, they will descend on this guild like gulls on a dropped fish, and somewhere beneath us is a sealed account that cannot survive daylight. Because three nights ago I read a name in the dark and my hands shook, and this morning the world started confirming my nightmares one detail at a time.

"Because," she said instead, "unanswered prayers are a private grief. Answered ones are a public commodity. The moment the wrong people believe the gods are listening again, your roof becomes the most valuable piece of real estate in Coriel, and you become either a relic or a hostage. Let the guild assess first. It's what we're for."

Sefa studied her for a long moment. August had the uncomfortable sensation of being read like a column of figures—of her careful face being summed and carried.

"You're frightened," Sefa said. Not accusing. Observing. "Good. So am I. Frightened is the correct devotional posture; we've simply forgotten." She rose. "Keep the ash. I'll come back tomorrow. And ledger-keeper—if your guild decides to call my debt while this is happening, I'd rather you told me straight. I've had enough of institutions managing me kindly."

She let herself out. The bell above the door gave its small bright note, and then August was alone with a pot of impossible weather.

* * *

She logged the interview properly. Three drafts. The first was too bare; the second admitted too much wonder; the third, finally, hit the register the guild demanded—flat, factual, faintly bored. *Claim assessed as unverifiable but non-fraudulent. Claimant sincere. Residue retained for analysis. Category: pending reclassification.*

Then she sat with her elbows on the desk and looked at the pot of ash, and let herself think the thoughts she'd been refusing for three days.

Because here was the thing she hadn't written anywhere, hadn't said aloud, barely allowed into the front rooms of her own mind: the sealed account had margins. Old accounts did—marginal notes by keepers centuries dead, little glosses in faded ink. And one margin, in a hand cramped with haste or fear, had annotated the founding entry's word *fire* with a single line:

*It does not burn upward. Ask what the temples burn.*

Ask what the temples burn.

The Concord of Faiths. The water-covenant. Sixty years of quiet gods and a city that paid tithe on the strength of a covenant nobody had tested. And now a god—or something wearing the office of one—had spent genuine power on a defrocked priest's rooftop prayer, and left behind cold ash, as if the miracle had been *bought* with something. As if fuel had been consumed and this was the exhaust.

Wonder and dread, August was discovering, were not opposites. They were the same sensation, itemized differently depending on who expected to pay the bill.

Someone had answered Sefa's prayer. Someone with fire to spend. Someone who had waited, possibly, a very long time for a prayer specific enough to be worth answering—and a prayer for rain, in a drought-hearted city, from a woman with a guild debt outstanding, was a prayer with a return address. With a thread attached. With a *name*.

August pulled a fresh sheet toward her and, in her smallest margin-hand—the hand she used for notes no auditor would bother reading—wrote a question she had no right to ask and no way to stop asking:

*If the gods are spending again, what are they buying it with—and who's been keeping their account?*

She stared at the words until the ink dried. Then she blotted the page, filed Sefa's claim in the pending drawer, and set the pot of cold ash on the corner of her desk where she would see it all day, because she was beginning to understand that she was going to need reminding.

Outside, the sky over Coriel did what it always did: nothing, beautifully, forever.

Tomorrow, she promised herself, she would go back down to the vault. Tomorrow she would read the founding entry again, properly, with Sefa's ash sitting in her memory like a footnote made flesh.

Tonight, the guild's bells rang the hour, and August noticed—for the first time in her career, with a lurch like a missed step—that she had no idea who they'd been ringing for.
