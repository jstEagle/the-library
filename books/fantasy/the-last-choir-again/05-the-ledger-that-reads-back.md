## 5. The Ledger That Reads Back

August Okafor did not sleep that night, which was normal, but she did not sleep *well*, which was not.

There is a particular insomnia reserved for ledger-keepers. It is not the insomnia of guilt or of love or of any of the grand causes the poets list. It is the insomnia of the unbalanced column. Somewhere in her head, a number refused to settle, the way a stone refuses to sit flat at the bottom of a pocket. She lay in her narrow bed above the counting-house and turned it over and over: *the debt of fire, owed to Vaskarath, opened in the year the guild was founded and never once touched since.*

Accounts did not simply sit for two hundred years. Accounts accrued. Accounts compounded. Even a dormant account gathered interest the way a shut room gathers dust. And this one had gathered nothing at all. No interest, no penalties, no adjustment for the collapse of the fire-standard, the three currency reforms, the burning of the southern exchange. It was as if the account had been *waiting*. As if it were less an entry than a held breath.

At the third bell past midnight she gave up, dressed in the dark, and went down.

The counting-house at night was a different animal. By day it was all shuffling clerks and the dry cough of quills and Master Corvane's footsteps, which everyone could track by sound alone, the way sailors track weather. At night it was just August and the ledgers and the great iron lamp that was never allowed to go out, because a guild that traded in impossibilities could not afford the one ordinary disaster of a fire among the books.

She lit the desk lamp anyway, a small one, and carried it to the restricted shelf.

The founding ledgers lived in a cage of blackened bronze, locked with a key that hung around the night-warden's neck, except that the night-warden had not actually existed in eleven years, his position having been quietly absorbed into the junior ledger-keeper's duties, which was how August had come to possess the key, which was how she had come to know about Vaskarath, which was how she had come to be standing here at the third bell with her heart going like a courier's boots.

She let herself in. The old ledger was where she'd left it, spine cracked, boards scarred, smelling of smoke that predated her grandmother's grandmother.

She opened it to the account.

*Debt of fire, owed to Vaskarath. Principal: one dragon's hoard, in flame. Status: dormant.*

The same words. The same faded iron-gall ink gone brown as old blood. She had checked the date, the reference marks, the little cross-reference to a contract stored in the sealed vault. Everything accounted for. Everything *dormant*.

Except.

August leaned closer. She told herself she was imagining it, and then she stopped telling herself that, because she had been a ledger-keeper since she was nine years old and the first thing they taught you—before penmanship, before arithmetic, before ethics—was that a ledger-keeper's eye does not imagine. A ledger-keeper's eye *records*.

The ink was wrong.

Not the color. The *age*. The entry had looked old yesterday. Two-hundred-years old, brown and sunk into the fibers of the page. Today it looked—she hunted for the word and disliked every candidate—*fresher*. Not new. Nothing about it was new. But it had the look of something that had been rained on and dried again, of ink that had moved after it was set. Ink that had *woken up*.

She sat down on the warden's stool, the one with the broken cane seat that bit you if you shifted wrong, and she did what she always did when the world misbehaved. She checked the margins.

August kept her own notes in the margins of everything, in pencil, in a shorthand of her own devising, because margins were the one country in the guild where no master had ever bothered to plant a flag. *Checked entry. Ink anomalous. Compare against vault copy.* She wrote it small, small enough that Corvane would need her own key to read it.

Then, because the night was long and her nerve was not infinite, she closed the ledger, locked the cage, and went back upstairs to lie awake in a new and worse way.

* * *

The next entry appeared two days later, on the morning of a rain that never fell.

She had come down early to prepare the day-books for the Ember Audit—three weeks out now, and the whole counting-house already smelling of panic and lamp oil—and she passed the bronze cage and did not stop, because she had decided, in the rational daylight part of her mind, that she had been tired and the light had been bad and ink is ink.

She made it four steps past the cage before her feet stopped on their own.

She went back. She unlocked the cage. She opened the ledger to the account.

Below *Status: dormant*, in a space that had been blank for two centuries, in a space she had personally verified as blank, there was a new line.

*Interest accrues.*

The ink was brown. Old-looking. Perfectly matched to the entry above it. Perfectly matched, in fact, to ink that had been dry since before the guild built its second wing.

August stood very still. Somewhere above her, clerks were arriving; she could hear the bar being lifted from the street door, the murmur of coats, the kettle being set on. The ordinary machinery of an ordinary morning. She looked down at a line of text that had not existed yesterday, written in ink that could not have dried yesterday, and she did the thing that had kept her alive and employed and invisible for twenty-two years.

She took out her pencil and wrote in the margin: *Noted. Do not touch.*

And then, because she was who she was, she touched it.

She ran her thumb along the line. The paper was smooth. No ridge of the pen, no pressure mark, no scratch of the nib. She held the page up to the lamp and tilted it, looking for the shadow of fresh ink, the faint sheen that even a hundred-year-old line betrays when the light rakes it. Nothing. The new line cast exactly the same shadow as the old ones, which is to say the shadow of something written when the paper was young.

Which meant one of two things. Either someone had forged the line with a skill and materials beyond anything the guild taught—using ink two centuries old, on a page locked in a cage, overnight, without breaking the lock, without disturbing the dust she had herself checked—and had done it to write two words of no obvious use to anyone.

Or the ledger had written it itself.

August put the ledger back, locked the cage, and went to her desk, and for six hours she entered tallow deliveries and quill requisitions and a dispute over a debt of one (1) remembered name, and her hand did not shake once, because her hand was a professional hand, and it was only when she was alone again at the evening bell that she allowed herself to notice that she had written the same date four times in the day-book and three of them were wrong.

* * *

The third entry came the night after Sefa arrived.

August had meant to think about Sefa—about the defrocked priest with rain in her testimony and no clouds in the sky, about the impossible thing August had entered into the day-book with a steadiness she had not felt—but the ledger would not let her. She dreamed of it. She dreamed of the bronze cage and woke with the certainty, the way you wake certain you've left the stove lit, that there was more.

There was more.

*Interest accrues. Interest compounds. The principal stirs.*

She read it three times by lamplight, alone, at the second bell, with the counting-house breathing around her. *The principal stirs.* She thought of the sealed vault, of the contract cross-referenced in the margin, of the word VASKARATH in a hand older than the guild. She thought of what stirs, after two hundred years, when it has been fed nothing and asked nothing and waited anyway.

And then she saw the handwriting.

She had been avoiding seeing it, the way you avoid looking directly at a figure at the end of a dark street, on the theory that what you don't examine can still be explained. But there was no one else in the room, and the lamp was bright, and she was, above all things, a professional.

She held the page close. She looked.

The hand was small and upright and slightly backward-leaning, with a distinctive closed loop on the letter *d* and a crossbar on the *t* that hooked left instead of right. It was a hand she knew better than any hand in the world, because she had spent twenty years studying it in the guild's copybooks, and then another twelve years erasing it from her own writing, stroke by stroke, because a junior ledger-keeper who wrote exactly like the masters got remembered, and being remembered was dangerous.

It was Master Corvane's hand.

No. She sat back. She made herself breathe. It was *close* to Corvane's hand. It was Corvane's hand the way a portrait is a face—accurate and yet wrong, the loop on the *d* a hair too tight, the crossbar a hair too long. A hand doing an impression of a hand.

Or a hand doing an impression of *her* impression of a hand.

August's mouth went dry. She thought: *I have spent my whole life writing like other people so that no one would know me. What if the ledger learned to write from me?*

She thought: *What if the ledger learned to write from my grandmother?*

She did not know yet why that thought arrived, or why it arrived wearing the specific cold weight of a key turning in a lock. She only knew that her own hand, her careful, invisible, professional hand, had a loop on the *d* that was a hair too tight, and a crossbar that hooked left, and that she had learned that hand at nine years old from the guild copybooks, which had been copied from the founding masters, one of whom had signed something in a sealed vault with a name she had not yet read.

She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal, and locked the cage, and stood in the dark with her hand flat on the bronze, feeling—she would not write this in any margin, not ever—feeling the faint, impossible warmth of the metal, like a body that had recently left the room.

* * *

The fourth entry she did not find. It found her.

She was at her desk at midday, entering the week's collections—nine debts of one impossible thing each, the guild's strange small stock-in-trade—and her pencil stopped. Not because of anything on the page. Because of a sound. Or not a sound. The memory of a sound, like a quill scratching, coming from a room where no one was writing.

The counting-house was empty; the masters were at their midday meal. The scratching had come from the bronze cage.

She crossed the room. The cage was locked. The ledger was shut. But through the bars, in the lamplight, she could see that the ledger was *breathing*—that is, the page-edges trembled very slightly, the way a book trembles when someone is turning toward a page they want.

August unlocked the cage. She was aware, distantly, that this was the moment a sensible person would go to Corvane. Corvane, who had been kind to her lately in his careful way; Corvane, who had taught her that the guild's true trade was not miracles but the management of what people owe. He would know what a stirring principal meant. He would know what to do about a ledger that wrote to its reader.

But even as she formed the thought, she knew she would not do it. Because the ledger was writing in a hand like hers. Because if the guild learned that the founding account had woken, the first question would be *who opened the cage*, and the second would be *who checked the dust*, and the third would be *why did the junior ledger-keeper visit a dormant account three nights running*, and August Okafor had spent her whole life ensuring that no one ever asked a fourth question about her.

She opened the ledger.

The new entry was longer.

*Interest compounds. The principal stirs. The guarantor's line is not extinguished.*

August read it twice. The words did not change. The guarantor's line is not extinguished. She did not know what the words meant, not precisely, not yet. But she knew what they meant the way you know a debt is yours before the collector says your name—some cold animal part of her standing up inside her chest and paying attention.

She looked at the handwriting. It was better now. Closer. The loop on the *d* was exactly right. The crossbar hooked exactly left. It was her hand. Not like her hand. *Her* hand, the one she used for her own private notes, the one no one had ever seen, the one she kept in the margins where no one looks.

The ledger had not learned to write from the copybooks.

The ledger had learned to write from *her*.

She thought of the margins. All the margins. Years of them, in every book she'd touched, her small pencil shorthand, her hidden self, the August who existed nowhere but in the white space at the edge of the guild's official truth. She had thought the margins were hers. She had thought they were empty.

The past had been reading over her shoulder the whole time.

August did the only thing she could think to do. She took out her pencil, and with a hand that shook for the first time in her professional life, she wrote in the margin of the founding ledger, small and neat, directly beneath the entry she had not made:

*Who are you?*

The graphite sat there, gray and modern and absurd against the brown ink. She waited. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, a clerk laughed at something; the sound fell through the floorboards and died.

Nothing happened.

She waited a full ten minutes, until her knees ached from standing at the cage. Nothing. The ledger lay open and innocent, an old book with a strange entry, and she felt the particular foolishness of a person who has asked a question of a piece of paper.

She closed it. Locked it. Went back to her desk.

It was at the evening bell, when the last clerk had gone and the masters' footsteps had faded up the stair, that she heard the scratching again—clear this time, unmistakable, coming from the cage she had locked an hour ago.

She crossed the room with her heart in her ears. She unlocked the cage with a key that suddenly felt less like a tool than a permission she should never have been given. She opened the ledger to the account.

Beneath her pencil line, in brown ink that matched the paper's age perfectly, in her own hidden hand, in her own private shorthand that no living person could read:

*You already know. Ask the right question. The vault keeps the signature. The signature keeps the name. Come and see what your blood bought, August Okafor.*

She stood there a long time. The lamp burned. The counting-house settled around her, beam by beam, the way old buildings do when they think no one is listening.

Her name. Her full name, in the margin, in her own secret hand, written by something that had been dead for two hundred years or waiting for two hundred years or both.

August looked at the last line for a long moment. Then she did the most dangerous thing a ledger-keeper can do with an entry she cannot explain.

She did not erase it. She did not report it. She took out her pencil and wrote, in her own hand, in the plain script she used for official work, where anyone might read it:

*I will. But I go alone, and I go after the Audit.*

The ink of the ledger seemed to dry a little darker, the way ink does when it is satisfied.

And far beneath the counting-house—so far beneath that no ledger-keeper has ever thought to draw it on any plan of the guild's foundations—something old turned over in its sleep, and began, with the patience of a creditor who has waited two centuries, to count.
