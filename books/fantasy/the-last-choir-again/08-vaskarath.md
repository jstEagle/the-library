## 8. Vaskarath

The vault did not want to be read.

August had understood that on her first night inside it, and she understood it more completely now, sitting cross-legged on the cold floor at three in the morning with a candle stub, a pot of ink-thinner, and a contract so old its vellum had gone the color of old teeth. The sealed account lay open across her knees. She had not slept. Sleep felt like a dereliction of duty, and duty was the only thing she had left that still made sense.

She had copied the first page four times before she trusted herself to say any of it out loud.

That was a ledger-keeper's habit, not superstition. You read an entry aloud when the numbers refused to sit still on the page—when your eyes kept sliding off them the way they slide off a word you've written wrong for years. Numbers that wouldn't stay put were numbers with something to hide. Master Corvane had taught her that in her second month, tapping her knuckles with his pen: *"The ledger is honest, August. It's the world that lies. When the two disagree, believe the paper."*

She believed the paper now. That was the problem.

"Account Zero," she said quietly, to the dark, because saying it made it realer than thinking it. "Founding instrument of the Guild of Impossible Things. Date of execution: one hundred and ninety-two years prior to current reckoning."

The date was wrong. Not wrong the way a clerk's error is wrong. Wrong the way a lie told by someone very powerful is wrong. The guild's own histories placed its founding at one hundred sixty years ago. Thirty-two years had been subtracted from the record somewhere between this contract and every other document in the building. Thirty-two years of something, excised like a bad tooth.

She kept reading.

"*Let it be set down that the undersigned parties, being the founders of the said Guild, do covenant as follows: that in exchange for the continuance of their persons, their charter, and their city against the hunger of the age, they shall deliver unto Vaskarath, last of the fire-drakes, one measure of flame per year, drawn from his own hoard, which hoard has been taken under the Guild's stewardship by force.*"

Her voice cracked on *by force*. She hadn't expected that. She'd read the phrase four times silently and it had sat there flat as any legal language sits. But aloud—in her own voice, in the voice she used for reciting balances to the Audit Board—it sounded like what it was. A confession.

*By force.* The guild hadn't been gifted dragon-fire. It had stolen it. And then it had written itself a contract in which the victim agreed, retroactively and on paper, to be robbed slowly, forever, and to call it stewardship.

August set down the contract on her knees and pressed both palms flat against the stone floor until the cold climbed her arms. Breathe. Count. One for the inhale, one for the exhale, the way Sefa had shown her outside the shrine of the drowned god, back when all of this was still only strange instead of unbearable.

Then she turned the page, because turning pages was the whole of her life and she did not know how to stop.

* * *

The middle sections were worse, if worse was a scale that went anywhere. Clause after clause of maintenance: how much flame might be drawn annually without killing the source; what portion would be diverted "to the maintenance of those powers which answer prayer within the city's bounds"; what penalties attached should the source perish prematurely. There it was, in clause fourteen, plain as a butcher's diagram—the gods. The gods who had begun answering prayers again, whose miracles were leaving cold ash in the streets, whose return Sefa had logged as impossible six nights ago. The guild wasn't just holding stolen fire. It was feeding it to heaven through a straw and billing the miracle-recipients nothing.

*Which powers shall be sustained thereby,* the clause ended, *until such time as the fuel be exhausted or the guarantor's line fail.*

August frowned. Read it again. *The guarantor's line.*

There was no guarantor defined anywhere in the preceding clauses. She checked. She checked the way she checked everything—finger under each line, twice through, cross-referencing the definitions section at the back. The word appeared seven times in the final third of the contract and zero times in any place that explained it. Whoever drafted this had assumed the reader already knew. Or had wanted the reader not to know.

Her hands were shaking again. She noticed them shaking the way you notice rain starting—not all at once, but as a fact that arrives and then belongs to the air.

The final leaf was heavier than the others. Not metaphorically; actually heavier, some densum treated into the vellum, some weight added deliberately, so that no wind could ever turn this page by accident. She had to use both hands.

It was a signature page.

At the top, in a hand so formal it looked printed: *Executed and bound by the following marks, in witness whereof the debt is created and cannot be discharged save by exhaustion, extinction, or substitution of the guarantor's line.*

Six signatures followed. Five of them meant nothing to her—names from the guild's oldest rolls, men and women rendered to dust and statuary long ago, their faces still hanging in the Charter Hall upstairs with gilt frames and clean biographies.

The sixth signature was at the bottom. Set slightly apart, as though the person signing it had insisted on standing alone while she did it. The ink had gone brown with age, but the strokes were deep, confident, made by someone who wrote quickly and beautifully and had never once doubted where the pen would land.

August knew that handwriting.

She knew it the way you know your own face in a shop window—suddenly, totally, with a lurch of recognition that arrives before thought and cannot be argued with afterward. She had grown up looking at that handwriting. It was on the flyleaf of the recipe book in her mother's kitchen. It was on the letters bundled in ribbon in the bottom drawer of the dresser her grandmother had left her. It was on the single sheet of paper August carried in the inner pocket of her coat at all times, the one that said, in that same quick beautiful hand:

*Counting is a kind of love. Be exact, little ember, and nothing can take what you've truly counted.*

Adaeze Okafor. Her grandmother. Dead eleven years. The woman who had taught her numbers before letters, who had held her fingers around a pencil and said *the ledger never blinks, so you must never flinch*, who had—August had always believed—loved the Guild of Impossible Things the way you love a stubborn child.

Had signed this.

Not witnessed it. Signed it. As party. As principle. As—

Her eyes went to the line beneath the signature, where signatories wrote their office or their stake. It was small. She had to lift the vellum to the candlelight to read it, and even then she read it three times, because the first two times her mind simply declined to assemble the words.

*Guarantor. Her line, pledged in lieu of flame, should the flame run short.*

The candle guttered. The vault breathed its long mineral breath around her. Somewhere above, through thirty feet of foundation stone, the city went on sleeping, dreaming its dreams, praying its small prayers to gods who burned on stolen light.

"No," August said. Out loud. To nobody. To the page. To her grandmother, dead eleven years, who could not defend herself and had left no letter explaining this, no margin note, no asterisk, nothing but the signature and the pledge and the phrase *little ember*, which August had always thought was just a thing old women called the children they loved.

*Be exact, little ember, and nothing can take what you've truly counted.*

Something had been taken. Something enormous had been taken, and it had been taken against a promise written in her grandmother's hand, and the promise had been about August. About her blood. About the line that ran from Adaeze Okafor's body down through her daughter and out the far end into a junior ledger-keeper who balanced accounts on the eve of audits and hid her own name in margins where no one looked.

They had known. Someone had known. Maybe everyone. Corvane hiring her—Corvane, who never hired anyone, who had personally interviewed a seventeen-year-old girl with good arithmetic and no connections and said *you'll do* in a voice she'd spent three years telling herself was kindness. Corvane assigning her the sealed vault as a test no one expected her to pass—or as a test he needed exactly her to pass, or to fail, or to find. She thought of his warm hand on her shoulder in the counting room. She thought of clause fourteen. She thought of the word *inventory* and made herself look at it directly, the way she made herself look at ugly sums.

If her bloodline was the guarantee, then she wasn't a member of the guild.

She was an asset on its books.

* * *

She did not cry. She wanted to note that, later, as a fact worth recording: she did not cry. What she did was worse and better, which was work. She pulled the fresh folio toward her and began, in her own careful clerk's hand, a full transcription of Account Zero, because whatever happened next—whatever this was, betrayal or inheritance or trap—the first rule of the ledger was that you preserved the record before you judged it. Judge the world, trust the paper. Copy first. Understand after. Grieve whenever the schedule allows.

She was halfway through clause nine when the door at the top of the vault stairs opened.

No footsteps announced it. No lamp preceded it. Just the door's soft iron groan, and then a voice she knew, low and roughened, pitched to carry down stone without echoing.

"You're still down here." Sefa came down the steps sideways, the way people did who had learned stairs in temples with armloads of vestments, one hand trailing the wall. She was carrying two bread rolls and a lantern she didn't need, and she stopped halfway down when she saw August's face. Whatever was on it made the ex-priest go still in the particular way of someone who had spent twenty years watching congregants receive news. "Ah," she said. "You found the end of it."

"How did you know I—" August started, and stopped, because the question didn't matter. "Did Corvane send you?"

"Corvane doesn't send anyone. He makes rooms available and lets people walk into them." Sefa reached the floor and sat down across from her without asking, folding her legs, setting the bread between them like an offering at a roadside shrine. "I came because you didn't come up. Three days in a vault is a novitiate, August. I've administered them. Eat something."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. You hide it badly. Eat."

August took a roll mostly to make Sefa stop looking at her like that. The bread tasted of nothing. Everything tasted of nothing tonight except ink and cold stone and the brown ghost of a signature.

Sefa waited. That was another thing about her—she could wait the way water waits, patient and total, and eventually the stone eroded. August heard herself talking before she decided to talk, her voice coming out level and clerkish, reciting the contract clause by clause in summary, the theft, the diversion, clause fourteen, the gods, and then the last page, and then—

—and there her voice failed, for the first time in her professional life, mid-entry, and she had to pick the contract up off her knees and turn it around and hold it out to Sefa with a hand that was not steady, and say only:

"The handwriting."

Sefa took the page. Held it near her lantern. August watched the ex-priest's face and saw the moment of recognition arrive there too—slower, because Sefa hadn't grown up under that hand, but arriving all the same, because the signature was large and the name beneath it was spelled out in full and there was no mistaking a name like Adaeze Okafor.

Sefa looked up. "Your grandmother."

"My grandmother signed the founding contract of the Guild of Impossible Things thirty-two years before the guild says it was founded, as guarantor, pledging her bloodline in lieu of flame." August heard her own voice do the thing it did when she read audit findings aloud—flatten, precise, each number wearing its little uniform. "Do you understand what that means? Do you understand what I am on these books?"

"A debtor," Sefa said quietly.

"Worse." August laughed, and hated the sound of it, a dry cracked coin of a laugh. "Debtors owe things. Look at the wording. *Substitution of the guarantor's line.* If the fire runs short—if Vaskarath dies before the account closes—then the guarantee comes due. My line. Me. I'm not a debtor, Sefa. I'm collateral."

The lantern hissed. Above them, faintly, through all that stone, a bell marked the fourth hour, and neither of them moved.

"I used to think," August said, and stopped, and tried again, because apparently tonight was the night every unsaid thing got said. "I used to think the guild took me in. Nobody else would have me—no family name worth anything since my grandmother died, no money, no connections, just arithmetic. And Corvane said *you'll do*, and I was so grateful I never asked why a guild of miracle-brokers needed one more junior ledger-keeper." She looked down at her hands, ink-stained, steady now, which felt like a separate betrayal. "He didn't take me in. He collected me. I walked in the door and some entry somewhere moved from column to column, and I've been balancing his books for three years while sitting in his inventory."

"August—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't comfort me yet. I haven't finished the arithmetic, and if I let you comfort me before the arithmetic is done, I'll stop doing it, and the arithmetic is the only thing keeping me upright."

So Sefa did the thing that made August love her—though she would not name it that for weeks yet—she nodded once and folded her hands and said, "Then finish it."

And August finished it. She read the rest aloud, every clause, her voice going raw on the legal repetitions, Sefa listening without interruption, and when it was done the vault was silent in the way that places are silent after thunder. August sat with the contract heavy on her knees and felt the shape of the next question rising in her the way floodwater rises in a cellar—slowly, then all at once.

One hundred ninety-two years. One measure of flame per year. A hoard taken by force, drained annually, diverted to heaven.

How much was left?

She didn't know. No one downstairs knew. The withdrawal records for Account Zero weren't in the vault—she'd searched—but they had to exist somewhere, because a debt this old would be serviced, tracked, amortized, because that was what ledgers *did*, because even crimes kept books.

And if she could find the withdrawal records, she could calculate the balance. And if she could calculate the balance, she would know exactly how many prayers the gods had left to buy, and exactly how close the guarantor's line had drifted to coming due.

"Little ember," she said softly, to the empty air, testing the endearment against her teeth like a coin bitten for gold. It didn't taste like love anymore. It tasted like a term of art. Like a unit of measurement.

Sefa leaned forward. "What is it? Your face changed."

August rolled up the transcription, tied it with cord, and stood, and her knees protested and she ignored them. "Clause fourteen says the gods are fed until the fuel runs short or the guarantor's line fails. Which means somebody, somewhere, has been counting the fuel." She slung her satchel over her shoulder. "And I intend to find that count, Sefa, because I am not going to spend one more day as a number in an equation I haven't read."

"And if the count is bad?" Sefa rose too, gathering her lantern. "If the fire's nearly gone?"

August paused at the foot of the stairs, one hand on the rail, and looked back at the sealed account lying open on the floor like a wound that had finally been unwrapped.

"Then I'll know," she said, "exactly how much I'm worth."
