## 9. Running the Numbers

The guild kept its worst arithmetic in the smallest room.

It was called the Counting Closet, though no one counted there anymore—it was too small for two people and a desk, and the senior members preferred the long tables of the Ledger Hall where their errors could breathe in company. August had claimed it years ago by simple right of being the only person who wanted it. One candle. One stool. One window the width of a brick, set so high you could see nothing of the city but a stripe of sky that went grey at dusk like an old man's beard going out.

She locked the door behind her anyway.

On the desk she laid out everything she'd taken from the vault's reading room—copies only, never originals; even now, with her heart hammering against her ribs like a debtor at the door, her hands did things correctly. That was the trouble with being made for this work. You could be falling apart and still rule your lines straight.

The founding contract sat in the center, weighted down with the inkwell because it kept trying to curl, as if paper could flinch. Beside it: three centuries of Ember Audit summaries, the annual reconciliations where the guild accounted for the fire it drew from the sealed account. And beneath those, her own working papers, blank except for the column heads she'd ruled at dawn:

*Remaining reserve.*
*Rate of withdrawal.*
*Prayers purchased to date.*

Simple questions. She had been asking simple questions since she was seven years old, when her mother first put a counting board in front of her and said, *numbers don't lie, August, which is why everyone hates them.*

She dipped her pen. She began.

* * *

The withdrawal entries ran back further than any living member's memory, further than the guild's charter, all the way to a date written in a script so old the numerals themselves looked like different animals. In the beginning, the draws were enormous. Whole conflagrations' worth. The early auditors had recorded them without shame—*Vaskarath account, disbursement: one burning of the northern fleet*; *disbursement: the plague-light of Vell, forty years sustained*; *disbursement: unlisted, marked only as THE FIRST PRAYER ANSWERED.* Whoever THE FIRST had been, the gods had paid for it dearly, or been paid for it dearly—August couldn't yet tell which direction the debt ran, and that ambiguity itched in her like a splinter under a nail.

But the amounts told their own story. Every century, the draws got smaller. Not because the gods wanted less. Because there was less.

She worked until the candle guttered, replaced it, worked on. The stripe of sky in the high window went from grey to black. Somewhere above her, the city settled into its sleep, ten thousand households banking their fires, none of them knowing what their prayers cost or who had pawned something to cover the bill.

By the third hour she had the curve.

That was the thing about numbers—they didn't argue. They just lay there in their columns, patient as corpses, letting you see exactly what they meant. And what they meant was this: the reserve was not a lake. It was not even a well. It was a puddle, and the shape of its shrinking was the shape of every dying thing—slow, then suddenly not slow.

August took a fresh sheet and did the calculation three ways, because when arithmetic tells you something terrible, the decent thing is to make it say it twice more.

First way: by volume. The remaining reserve, converted through the standard equivalence tables—the ones that translated dragon-fire into the currency of answered prayer—came to some four hundred thousand units. She stared at the number. It felt huge. Numbers always feel huge until you divide them.

Second way: by demand. Here she needed the prayer census, and the prayer census was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, because she had helped maintain it. Officially, the gods of the Concord received perhaps nine hundred petitions a year, each logged, each priced, each drawn modestly against the sealed account. But Sefa had come to their door talking about rain in a cloudless city, and impossible things were arriving faster than they could be filed. If the gods were answering freely again—if the taps were open all across the city in shrines and back rooms and desperate midnight whisperings—then the real draw wasn't nine hundred a year. It might be thousands. It might be tens of thousands, once word spread that heaven had reopened for business. Miracles were like debts that way: one forgiven, and everyone comes running.

Third way: by remainder. Four hundred thousand units, divided by even the official rate of consumption—

She did the division. Then she checked it, because her hands had gone cold and she assumed she'd erred.

She hadn't erred.

At current official draw: four hundred and forty years. A comfortable margin. A number you could put in a report and sleep behind.

At anything resembling the true rate—if the gods were answering at even one percent of their ancient capacity, if the city's hunger for miracles was waking up after three centuries of rationing—then the reserve would last eleven years.

Eleven.

And after that?

After that, either the gods starved—and she had seen enough starving things to know they do not starve quietly—or they found another source of fire. And there was, so far as the ledgers showed, exactly one other source left in the world.

She sat back on the stool. The little room seemed smaller than it had when she came in, the walls leaning in like interested parties at a will-reading.

*So that's the war,* she thought. *Not armies. Arithmetic.*

* * *

She should have stopped there. Any sensible ledger-keeper would have closed the book, gone up to her narrow bed above the counting floor, and let tomorrow's August carry the weight. That was the guild way: you recorded, you balanced, you did not *feel* the figures. Corvane said it often—*a number is a number is a number, Miss Okafor; grief is for the clients.*

But she was already turning pages, and the next section of the audit summaries was titled *Disbursement Detail: Recent Century*, and the recent century was where the handwriting changed.

She noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song—not immediately, but with a full-body flinch. For three hundred years, the withdrawals from the Vaskarath account had been recorded in a particular clerk's hand, formal and upright, generation after generation of different clerks all trained to write identically, because the guild believed continuity should look like one long unbroken sentence. But in the last fifty years, the entries had begun to drift. Looser. More hurried. As if whoever was drawing the fire had stopped bothering to pretend the account was ordinary.

And the purposes had drifted too.

Early entries: rain for harvests, healing for kings, the binding of a flood, the mending of a cracked bell. The bread-and-butter commerce of divinity.

Then, starting around the year her grandmother would have been young—she checked the dates twice, hating herself for checking, unable not to—the entries turned strange. *Disbursement: sustenance, dormant period.* Over and over. *Sustenance, dormant period. Sustenance, dormant period.* Dozens of them. The gods weren't buying miracles anymore. They were buying groceries. They were keeping themselves fed during the long fast between worshippers, drawing down the stolen hoard just to stay alive, the way a bankrupt lord sells the silver a spoonful at a time.

The gods were starving already. Had been starving for fifty years. And then—she flipped forward—three months ago, the pattern broke. New entries, small ones, frequent ones, clustered around the same weeks Sefa's rain had fallen out of an empty sky.

Someone had woken them up. Or promised them something. Or told them the account was finally coming due and they should spend while the spending was good.

August wrote a note in the margin, then crossed it out, then wrote it again because it was true and she had promised herself, back in the vault, that she would stop hiding true things in margins: *They know the fire is almost gone. They're spending like it.*

* * *

The knock came at the fourth hour, soft and doubled, the way Sefa knocked—two knuckles, apologetic, like an apology for existing near a door.

August swept the papers under a folio before she'd consciously decided to. Old habit. New necessity. "It's open."

Sefa filled the doorway the way she filled most spaces, big and rumpled, smelling of tallow and street dust, her defrocked priest's collar replaced by a plain wool scarf that she wore like an apology. She carried two cups of the guild's terrible chicory and the expression of a woman who hadn't slept, which matched August's own expression so precisely that they both laughed, briefly, without humor.

"You're awake," Sefa said.

"I'm calculating."

"Those aren't the same."

"No." August took the cup. "They are very much not the same."

Sefa lowered herself onto the floor—there was nowhere else—and drank, and watched August over the rim with those eyes that had spent thirty years watching people pray, and knew all the ways hope could be manufactured and all the ways it couldn't. "You found how much is left," she said. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"Because you've got the face. I saw it on the bishops when the famine tithe came in short. The face that says *I know the exact size of the disaster, down to the copper.*" She nodded at the folio hiding the papers. "How much?"

August hesitated. Telling anyone was a risk. Telling Sefa felt less like a risk than like breathing—Sefa had come to them with an impossible prayer and stayed out of stubbornness and grief, and somewhere in the past week August had stopped thinking of her as a client and started thinking of her as the closest thing the guild had to a conscience.

"Eleven years," August said. "If the answering stays small. Less if it doesn't."

Sefa closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was doing priest-math, August realized—the other kind of arithmetic, the kind done in congregations. "There are maybe sixty thousand people in the low city who prayed to anything last winter. Hungry people pray cheap and constant. If the gods start answering even the small ones—" 

"The draw triples. Maybe quadruples. Then we're not measuring in years."

"In months."

"In months." August turned her cup in her hands. "And here's the part I keep trying to make be about something else. The fire isn't theirs. It never was. It was taken—from Vaskarath, three hundred years ago, by the founders, with a contract my grandmother signed as guarantor. Every miracle this city has ever thanked heaven for was bought with stolen goods. We haven't been blessed. We've been *spending.*"

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. The candle fluttered in air neither of them had moved.

"Do you know what I did, the night the rain came?" she said finally. "I stood in the street with my arms out and I cried, because I thought—I thought someone up there remembered me. Thirty years of service and silence, and one answered prayer, and I forgave everything. All of it. The dead brother, the empty cathedral, the whole rotten hierarchy. One rainstorm and I was theirs again." Her laugh was rough. "And now you're telling me the rain was purchased. With stolen fire. On credit. Against a dying account."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me the gods are starving."

"Starving slowly. Spending fast. Which is worse—that's desperation math. Desperate debtors do unpredictable things." August heard her own voice flatten into guild-speak and hated it, the way competence becomes a wall you hide behind. "If the reserve runs out and they're still hungry, they'll need fuel. There's one source left."

"The dragon."

"The dragon." And me, she didn't say. My blood, the guarantee, whatever my grandmother pledged that I'm apparently the interest payments on. Some numbers you don't say aloud, because saying them makes them due.

Sefa set down her cup. She had the look of a woman rearranging her entire theology on short notice, and doing it the only way priests ever do it—by finding the question underneath the doctrine. "Then here's what I want to know, August Okafor. All your clever columns. Did any of them tell you what happens to *us?* Not the guild. Not the gods. The sixty thousand people in the low city whose prayers have finally started getting answered. What happens to them when the fire gutters?"

August looked down at her papers. The beautiful, damning, precise papers.

"No," she said. "I haven't run that number. There isn't a column for it."

"Make one." Sefa stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "You're the best counter I've ever met. So count the thing that matters."

She paused at the door, and added, softer: "My brother used to say God keeps books too. I used to find that comforting." Then she was gone, and the stair creaked away her weight, and August sat alone with the candle and the terrible tidy truth.

* * *

She ran the number.

Not the one Sefa asked for—she didn't have the instruments for that one, the human ones—but the one underneath it, the one that had been waiting all night for her to stop flinching. She ruled fresh columns, and her hand was steady, because her hands were always steady, God help her, even now.

*Reserve remaining:* 412,000 units.
*Current daily draw, observed:* rising, call it 90 units and climbing.
*Days to exhaustion:* she divided, re-divided, and got a number that fit inside a single year.

One year. Maybe less. The gods weren't spending like immortals anymore. They were spending like men told the ship was sinking—drinking the wine, lighting the lamps, answering every prayer that knocked because what was the point of saving?

Unless someone renegotiated.

Unless somebody walked into that dark with the accounts, and read the terms aloud, and offered the one thing no god expects from a debtor's descendant: a restructuring.

August laughed—a small, appalled sound in the tiny room. She was planning a negotiation with starving gods using double-entry bookkeeping. Her mother would have been proud. Her grandmother, the guarantor, the signature, the woman whose name had detonated August's whole life a week ago—her grandmother would have burned these papers rather than let them exist.

Or would she? August didn't actually know. Everything she'd believed about her family was ash now. Ash, she thought, like the cold residue the miracles left behind. Like what remained when fire passed through and took the warmth with it.

She squared the pages. She copied the critical figures into a single folded sheet and hid it where she now hid everything—in the lining of her sleeve, against the pulse point, where the guild's secrets rode along with her heartbeat. Then she snuffed the candle.

In the dark, one figure glowed in her mind, refusing to be un-seen: *one year.* Whatever she decided—expose the founders, save the dragon, warn the city—the clock was already running, and it did not care about her courage.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, she heard voices in the Ledger Hall. Late-night voices. Corvane's among them, low and level, and two others she didn't recognize.

At this hour, senior members didn't meet unless something was wrong.

Or unless someone had noticed a junior keeper withdrawing vault copies she had no authorization to read.

August pressed her palm flat against the folded sheet over her wrist and stood very still in the dark, listening to the arithmetic of footsteps overhead, counting—who was up there, which way the doors swung, whether the odds were running toward her or away.

For the first time in her life, she was afraid of a number she couldn't finish.
