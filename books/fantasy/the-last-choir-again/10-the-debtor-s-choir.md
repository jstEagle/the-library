## 10. The Debtor's Choir

The summons went out the way all guild business did: a slip of grey paper, folded twice, sealed with plain wax, delivered by someone who pretended to be delivering nothing at all.

August wrote eleven of them.

She told herself it was procedure. The Ember Audit was coming—the Concord of Faiths had moved its inspection up from autumn to midsummer, and every account had to be reconciled, including, eventually, the one she'd found in the vault and had not stopped thinking about since. Standard practice said that before any external audit, the guild's outstanding debtors should be called in for review. It was in the manual. She'd checked. Twice.

What the manual did not say was that she chose the debtors herself.

There were forty-one names on the active rolls. She picked eleven. She picked them at night, at her own desk, with the lamps turned low, working from memory more than ledger, because the ledgers recorded what people owed but not who they were, and August had spent three years learning the difference in the margins.

She picked Sefa first, obviously. Then she sat with the list for a long time, feeling like a woman picking pockets, and added ten more.

* * *

They came on the last Thursday of the month, which was the day the guild traditionally received no visitors, because the wards were refreshed then and the doors ran hot to the touch. August had booked the reading room under a routine pretext—debtor reconciliation, subcommittee of one—and had bribed the door-warden, a half-deaf old man named Pell, with the promise of a signed waiver on his own outstanding balance. He owed the guild an apology he'd never managed to deliver to his estranged daughter. The guild held it as collateral. The guild held everything as collateral. That was rather the point of the guild.

They began arriving at dusk.

The first through the door was Sefa, who came in like weather, arms full of a basket she thrust at August without explanation. Inside: bread still warm, a jar of honey, and six candles of the cheap tallow kind that smoked when they burned.

"For the table," Sefa said. "Nobody thinks straight under guild lighting."

Guild lighting was designed, August knew, to make people feel accountable. Every shadow in the reading room fell at an angle calculated to suggest someone standing behind you. She took the candles anyway.

Then came the others, in ones and twos, shuffling in with the particular posture of people summoned to a reckoning: shoulders braced, eyes down, hands empty or deliberately full so there would be nothing to fidget with.

Master Ivetta Crane, formerly the guild's finest miracle-appraiser, now owing nine years on a debt of *certainty*—she had once certified a false relic as true, and the penalty had been her confidence itself, held in trust until repaid. She walked into rooms now like a woman crossing ice.

Brother Anselm, defrocked twice over, once by his order and once by the Concord, owing a sermon he was forbidden to preach. He'd been trying to repay it for eleven years by writing sermons nobody would let him deliver. There were four hundred of them, bound in twine, stacked in his rented room.

The twins, Osk and Osla Vey, who between them owed a single debt split down the middle: they had witnessed something together, long ago, and the witness had been repossessed. Neither could remember what they'd seen. They only knew they kept waking at the same hour, holding hands across the space between their beds, both crying without knowing why.

Marrow, the bone-counter, who owed the guild a death—not anyone's death, just *a* death, entered into the books after a bargain gone soft, and who had spent fifteen years unable to die until it was paid. He was very tired. He joked about it constantly, in the way of people who have made peace with a thing by refusing to stop being angry about it.

Little Quill, who wasn't little and whose name wasn't Quill, a former guild scribe who owed a sentence—literally one sentence, taken out of her mouth during a contract dispute and never returned. She spoke around its absence the way you speak around a missing tooth.

And four more: a ferryman who owed a crossing; an herbalist who owed a season; a clockmaker who owed an hour, which sounded trivial until you learned it was the hour his daughter had been born, and he could not remember it, and neither could she.

August stood at the head of the table with her list and realized, with a lurch, that she had done something unprecedented.

Every person in this room owed the guild exactly one impossible thing. That was the old classification—the *Category Nine* accounts, the ones too strange to collect, too true to forgive. They were supposed to be scattered across the rolls, isolated, unacquainted, each debtor convinced they alone carried their particular absurdity. The guild had maintained this isolation for two hundred years. It was in the manual. Isolated debtors don't organize. Organized debtors ask questions.

She had just put eleven of them in a room with candles and warm bread.

"Right," she said, before her nerve failed. "You're wondering why you're here."

* * *

She gave them the official version first, because she was still, in some part of herself, a guild creature: reconciliation ahead of audit, signatures to confirm balances, administrative housekeeping. She watched their faces do the arithmetic of dread—*they're calling in the debts, they're finally collecting, I'm about to lose whatever scrap the guild left me*—and she watched the dread curdle into the flat resignation of people who had been waiting years for the axe.

Then Marrow said, "Bullshit," with great affection, and passed the bread.

It broke something open. Later August would try to reconstruct how it happened—the exact sequence of cracks—but she couldn't. It was less a decision than a collapse, a dam going all at once. Perhaps it was the candles. Perhaps it was the fact that Brother Anselm took one look at Little Quill's face and said, "You're the one who lost the sentence," in a voice of such recognition that she burst into tears into the honey jar.

Perhaps it was simply that none of them had ever been in a room with another Category Nine before, and the relief of that was structural, seismic, like water reaching a man who hadn't known he was dying of thirst.

Osla Vey described the hour she woke every night, weeping, holding her brother's hand across the dark, and Marrow nodded and said, "I know that hour. Grief keeps universal time." And the twins stared at him because no one had ever *described it back to them before.*

Ivetta Crane said, quietly, "Do any of you remember what certainty feels like?" and the whole room went silent, because they all did, and none of them had it anymore, and saying so out loud in front of witnesses was the closest any of them had come to having it back.

Sefa talked about rain in a city with no clouds. She'd been careful with the story before—with August, with everyone—but tonight, among the marked, she laid it all out: the answered prayer, the impossible wet, the cold ash afterward that matched nothing. "The gods are answering again," she said. "And I keep thinking: nobody answers for free. My order taught me that before it taught me anything else. So my question is—who's paying? Because it isn't me. I've checked my soul. It's not mortgaged. Yet."

"Yet is doing a lot of work in that sentence," Marrow observed.

"I'm a priest. Or was. 'Yet' is the whole job."

August listened from the head of the table and felt something she had no ledger category for. She had organized these people under a false pretext, intending—she told herself—to prepare them for the audit, to protect them from whatever fallout came when the sealed account surfaced. But sitting here now, watching Brother Anselm quietly move the candle so Little Quill could see her bread better, watching the ferryman refill Osk Vey's cup without being asked, she understood that she had done something else entirely.

She had assembled witnesses.

Because here was the truth she'd been carrying alone since the vault, and it had gotten heavy: the guild's founding account named a dragon. Named fire stolen and fire owed. Named a bloodline as guarantee. *Her* bloodline. Her grandmother's signature at the bottom of the founding sin. And the handwriting in the margins lately—the entries appearing that she never made, in script like her own—suggested the past wasn't content to sit in the vault anymore. It wanted collection.

She could not carry that alone. Not because she was weak, but because it was designed to be carried by no one. That was how the guild survived two hundred years: it made sure no single person ever held enough of the truth to be dangerous. Everyone knew a piece. No one knew the shape.

Eleven Category Nines in a room, though. Eleven people who already knew, in their bones, that the guild traded in things that could not be honestly owned. Eleven people with nothing left to lose that the guild hadn't already taken.

"You should know why you're really here," August heard herself say.

The room quieted. Even the candles seemed to lean in.

"The audit coming isn't routine. The Concord is moving early because something is moving early. The gods are answering prayers again—Sefa can testify to that—and answered prayers require fuel, and I have reason to believe the fuel is..." She hesitated on the cliff edge of it. "...not the guild's to give. There is an account older than the guild. It concerns fire. It concerns a debt that has just come due. And it concerns—" her voice did something she hated, wobbled, steadied— "it concerns me. Specifically. By name. By blood."

Anselm leaned forward. "Child, are you telling us you've read the sealed account?"

"I'm telling you I'm *in* the sealed account."

Silence, but not the hostile kind. The kind where twelve people recalibrate at once.

Then Ivetta Crane—nine years without certainty—Ivetta Crane said, in a clear, steady voice that seemed to surprise even her: "Well. Someone finally owes us an explanation instead of the other way round. Go on, girl. We're listening."

And August, junior ledger-keeper, three years hiding her name in the margins where no one looked, looked around the candlelit table at the sermonless priest, the certainty-less appraiser, the deathless bone-counter, the weeping twins, the sentence-less scribe, and thought: *these are the most dangerous people in the city,* because every one of them had already survived the guild taking everything, and were therefore the only people in it with nothing left to fear.

Her only allies. Assembled by accident, under a false pretext, on a night the doors ran too hot to touch.

She opened her mouth to begin—and that was when the knock came at the reading-room door. Three raps, precise, unhurried, in a rhythm every guild member knew by heart.

Corvane's knock.
