## 11. Interrogation by Candlelight

The summons came at the worst possible hour, which is to say it came exactly when I expected it and not one moment sooner.

A boy in guild gray tapped my elbow as I was reconciling the third-quarter tallow accounts—unglamorous work, deliberately so, the kind of ledger-keeping that made a person invisible. He handed me a folded card sealed with plain wax. No sigil, no flourish. Corvane didn't need flourishes. The card said only: *My office. After last bell. Bring nothing.*

I read *bring nothing* three times.

There are perhaps forty ways to interpret that phrase in the Guild of Impossible Things, and thirty-nine of them end with someone being erased from an account. The fortieth is that he wants you empty-handed because whatever he gives you, you'll have to carry out with your conscience instead of your pockets.

I finished the tallow accounts first. That matters, I think—or I want it to matter. Whatever else happened after last bell, the numbers would be true. It was the only currency I had left that hadn't been counterfeited by my own hands.

* * *

Corvane's office occupied the top of the Ledger Hall like a held breath. No portraits. No trophies from the guild's more theatrical contracts—the rain bought for Vellum Province, the ghost retired from the Marrow Street tenement. Just shelves of ledgers going back two hundred years, each spine numbered in his own hand, and one candle burning on the desk. One. In a guild that could afford a thousand lights, Master Corvane worked by a single flame, and everyone knew why: he said you could tell a lie faster in the dark, but you couldn't take it back by daylight.

"Sit," he said, without looking up.

He was writing something. I sat. The chair across from his desk was hard oak, no cushion, positioned so that whoever sat in it faced the window and the long drop into the courtyard below. I'd never noticed before that every visitor got the view and Corvane kept his back to it. A man who never looks down while everyone else must.

"The Ember Audit is in nine days," he said.

"Yes, Master."

"The Concord of Faiths sends three auditors this year. Last year, one. The year before, none." He turned the page he was writing. "Do you know what that acceleration means?"

"That they're watching us more closely."

"It means," he said, "that someone has been talking." Now he looked up. Candlelight did strange things to Corvane's face—it took the gray out of his beard and put iron in its place, made him look less like a tired administrator and more like something that had been waiting a very long time under a stone. "The Concord doesn't spend auditors on rumors. They spend them on leaks."

"I haven't spoken to anyone from the Concord, Master."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You haven't. That's not what I asked, though, is it?"

The candle between us made a small sound, wax settling. I kept my hands flat on my knees where he could see them. That was the first lesson he'd ever taught me, back when I was nineteen and terrified of the vault stairs: *keep your hands visible when questioned, August. Hidden hands confess things mouths haven't decided on yet.*

I wondered, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, whether he'd taught me that so he could watch me fail it.

* * *

Let me be honest about what I'd done, since honesty was about to become a performance.

Page 214 of the Sealed Account—the page describing the transfer of fire, the founding transaction, the thing older than the guild itself—had been in my satchel for six days. Not copied. *Taken.* I told myself I would return it. I told myself a great many tidy things while I sat in my rented room above the chandler's shop, reading it by the stub of a candle I couldn't really afford, learning my grandmother's signature the way you learn a dead relative's face from a single portrait.

Her name was Yewande Okafor. She had signed the founding contract as guarantor. Her bloodline—*my* bloodline—was written into the margin in ink that still hadn't fully dried after ninety years, as if the paper itself refused to accept it.

And then, yesterday, Corvane had sent a clerk to collect the Sealed Account for pre-audit review, and the clerk had come back pale, and now I was sitting in a hard chair watching a candle burn while the man who ran the Guild of Impossible Things decided what I was.

"You reviewed the Sealed Account eleven days ago," Corvane said. "Standard preparation. Your notes were exemplary, incidentally. Cross-referenced, dated, initialed. You initialed everything." He smiled without warmth. "You're the most thorough junior keeper we've employed in thirty years. Possibly ever."

"Thank you, Master."

"So imagine my confusion"—he turned a paper around and slid it toward me—"when the account arrives for review and page 214 is missing."

I looked down at the paper. It was a copy of the index for the Sealed Account, and someone—Corvane, presumably—had drawn a small precise circle around the entry for folio 214: *Terms of Fire; Guarantor Clause; Founding Transaction.*

"I can't explain it, Master."

"Can't or won't?"

"Can't." And here was the terrible thing, the thing I would turn over at three in the morning for weeks afterward: the word came out steady. My voice didn't shake. My hands stayed flat on my knees. Somewhere inside me a girl who had never told a lie larger than *yes, I've eaten* stood with her mouth open, watching her body conduct business without her.

"Hm." Corvane leaned back. "Who accessed the vault besides yourself in the past month?"

"No one. Vault access requires two keys. Yours and the Keeper of Record's. Neither key has left this floor—I check the log daily."

"And the log itself?"

"Is intact."

"Is it?" He let that sit. "Or does it merely say it's intact? You of all people know the difference, August. A ledger never lies. But a ledger-keeper—" He spread his hands. "A ledger-keeper is just a person with good handwriting."

The candle guttered slightly, recovered. Outside, the city was closing up for the night—the bell of the Chiming Quarter, the groan of the freight lifts going dark. Normal sounds. Sounds from a world where I was still a person with good handwriting and nothing worse.

"Master Corvane," I said, "may I ask a question?"

"That depends on whether you'll answer mine first." He picked up his pen, dipped it, and did not write anything. Just held it. "Where were you last night, between second bell and dawn?"

"With Sefa," I said. "Sefa Ilori. The priest-turned-debtor. We were tracing cold ash from answered prayers. She thinks the gods are buying miracles with something, and I thought—if there's a new commodity entering the city, it should appear somewhere in the ledgers, even obliquely. As freight. As tithe. As—" I stopped myself. Too much detail was its own confession. "We found nothing. I logged the null result. You can see it in my day-book."

"I have seen it." He set the pen down. "Your day-book is the only document in this building I trust absolutely, do you know that? Every entry in your hand, every date honest, no gaps, no cleverness. Thirty years I've waited for a keeper like you." Something moved behind his eyes—not warmth exactly, but the memory of warmth, like heat radiating off a wall long after the fire's gone. "Which is why this is difficult. Because either someone stole page 214 from a room only four living people can enter—and did it without disturbing the dust, the wards, or the log—or you removed it. And if you removed it, August, then the most trustworthy keeper I have ever trained has committed the oldest crime in our trade."

I said nothing. Silence is also an answer; I'd learned that watching debtors squirm through collection interviews. Silence says: *ask me again and I'll have to build a bigger lie.*

Corvane built it for me.

"There's a clause," he said quietly, "in the guarantor section. Page 214. Would you like me to tell you what it says?"

My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as dropping through a trapdoor. "If you wish, Master."

"Of course I remember it. I've memorized every page of that contract. Do you know what's on 214?" His voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from Corvane was more frightening than anger. "Fire. Terms of fire. The original price. And a name—a family name—written as surety. The Okafor line." He watched me. "Yewande Okafor. Your grandmother, I believe. Or should I say *our* former client's grandmother, since I don't yet know what you are."

The candle flame bent sideways though there was no draft. Or maybe there was; maybe the whole room had begun to lean.

"I don't know what you mean, Master."

"There it is," he said softly. "The first one."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about lying: it isn't hard. That's the horror of it. Nineteen years of scrupulous, painful, exhausting honesty—every entry balanced, every receipt filed, every confession volunteered before anyone had to drag it out of me—and when the moment came, the lie arrived fully formed, articulate, punctual, like a courier who'd been waiting outside the door my whole life.

"The Okafor name appears in many old records," I said. "It's not an uncommon name in the river districts."

"It's uncommon in *this* record, because it's written in blood-ink. Surety clauses always are. You can't forge blood-ink, August. Ask any forger worth the title." He rose and walked along his shelf of ledgers, trailing one finger across the spines. "When I hired you, I pulled your intake file. Your mother listed her mother's name on the family form, because mothers do that—they fill forms honestly even when their daughters are trying to disappear into competence." He stopped walking. "Yewande Okafor. Born Marrow District. Disappeared from all records in the year the guild was chartered. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I have known since the day you walked in here with ink on your fingers and fear in your eyes. I hired you anyway."

"Why?" The word escaped before I could weigh it, raw as a torn receipt.

"Because you were useful." He turned. "Because the guarantor clause requires monitoring. Because someday—soon, now, with the Concord sniffing around—I would need to know exactly how much you'd figured out, and exactly what kind of person you'd be once you figured it out. There are two kinds, you see. The ones who run to the Concord and sell the story. And the ones who come to me." He came back around the desk and leaned on it, close enough that I could smell the lamp-oil and old paper that was simply *him*, the way other men smelled of tobacco or horses. "So. Which are you?"

"I don't have the page."

"AUGUST." He slammed his palm flat on the desk and the candle jumped, and for one instant I saw the other Corvane, the one the debtors whispered about, the one who had personally closed three rival houses of impossible commerce and burned their charters in the courtyard below this very window. Then it passed, banked, gone, and he straightened his cuffs as if nothing had happened. "Forgive me. Let's try logic, since numbers are our shared language. Item one: the page is missing. Item two: you are the only person with unrestricted access short of myself and the Keeper of Record, both of whom have alibis I find acceptable. Item three: your day-book shows a two-hour gap on the night in question—two hours, unaccounted, the only gap in four years of otherwise immaculate records. Item four: you lied to me just now about the Okafor name, badly, which means you're protecting something rather than ignorant of it. Conclusion?" He opened his hands. "I don't actually need the page, child. I need you to stop insulting us both."

The gap. Two hours. I had spent them sitting in the vault stairwell with the page pressed against my chest like a hot coal, unable to go up and unable to go down, and I had thought—naively, arrogantly—that because I wrote no false entries during those hours, the hours themselves didn't count.

That's the thing about Corvane. He doesn't read ledgers. He reads the white space *around* ledgers.

"If I give it back," I said slowly, "unread by anyone but me—"

"Then we discuss terms like professionals." He sat down again, and the office returned to being an office, and I understood suddenly that the entire interrogation had been a negotiation conducted in the grammar of accusation. He'd known from the first sentence. Maybe from the first bell. This whole evening was him pricing my silence. "The audit is in nine days. If the Concord finds a missing page in our oldest sealed account, they don't fine us, August. They dissolve us. Charter revoked, assets seized, members scattered—and every soul on our debtor rolls gets collected by whoever holds their paper next, and I promise you the Concord's collections department makes me look sentimental. So think carefully about whose survival that stolen page is actually threatening."

Mine. Ours. The choir's. Sefa's. The arithmetic was real and I hated that it was real.

"Give me until after the audit," I said. "The page comes back. Nothing leaves this building. But I need those nine days, and I need you to log the folio as misfiled, not missing. Misfilings happen constantly before audits. Missing pages bring auditors."

He studied me for a long moment, and I braced for refusal—for the door, for the wardens, for the courtyard and its convenient height.

Instead, Corvane laughed. Quietly, mostly to himself, shaking his head like a man watching a colt clear a fence nobody believed it could jump.

"Misfiled," he repeated. "You realize you just negotiated with me. Blackmailed me, arguably, using the audit as leverage. Four years ago you apologized to a filing cabinet you bumped." He pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and began writing in his quick, economical hand. "Folio 214: misfiled during quarterly reorganization. Reported by J. Okafor. Corrective search ongoing. There. Done. You're now a co-author of a falsified guild record, which makes you either my accomplice or my problem, and I genuinely don't know which yet."

"Neither do I, Master."

"Honesty. At last." He signed the entry and blotted it, then looked up with an expression I couldn't catalogue—something old and tired underneath the iron. "One more thing, since we're trading truths tonight. The clause on page 214—the guarantor clause. Read the payment schedule before you return it. Not the amount. The *schedule*. When you understand what 'due' means in that context, come to me. Not before."

"What happens when it's due?"

"That, August, is the one question tonight I won't answer for free." He nodded at the door. "Last bell rang twenty minutes ago. Go home. Sleep badly. You've earned it."

* * *

I walked out through the Ledger Hall with my heart going like a stamping press. The long tables stood empty, moonlight striping the floor through the high windows, and my footsteps echoed the way footsteps only echo when you've done something that changes the acoustics of your own life.

I had lied. To Corvane. Twice—no, three times counting the negotiation, if a negotiated lie counts, and it counted, it counted enormously—and I had *won*. Not survived. Won. He'd wanted the page and the truth and my allegiance, and he'd gotten a forged log entry and a promise with a deadline, and he'd *signed it.*

In the stairwell I stopped, pressed my back against cold stone, and did something I hadn't done since childhood: I laughed. Alone, breathless, half-hysterical laughter at the sheer outrageous ease of it, the door in me I'd never known was there swinging open on the first touch. Guilt was there too, yes—a sour taste at the root of my tongue, the image of my grandmother's name in blood-ink—but beneath the guilt, undeniable as profit, ran a bright hot wire of *exhilaration*. I had been invisible my whole life by being perfect. Tonight I'd discovered that deception fit me like a glove tailored to my hand.

That frightened me more than Corvane did.

At the bottom of the stairs, pinned to the message board where the night clerks left routing slips, was a note in Sefa's round, unpracticed hand:

*Found another ash site. Cold as ever. But August—there's a mark beside it this time, pressed into the soot. Looks like writing. Come at dawn. Bring your books. Bring all of them.*

I took the note down and read it twice, feeling the stolen page shift in its hiding place against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Writing in ash. Ash that matched nothing in any ledger.

Whatever was answering prayers in this city had just started leaving signatures—and I was now, officially, a liar with a deadline and a forgery of my own on guild record, which meant there was no one left to report to except the people I chose.

I went home to sleep badly, exactly as instructed, and dreamed of a page that wrote back in handwriting like mine.
