## 13. The Serpent Under the City

The flame was smaller than a candle.

That was the first thing August understood, standing at the edge of the undercity shrine with ash drifting upward past her face like snow in reverse. All her life she had imagined dragons as furnaces—mountains of fire, wings like storm fronts, the kind of heat that turned cities to glass in an afternoon. Every ledger illustration, every guild fresco, every cautionary tale told to junior keepers agreed on this: dragons were excess incarnate. Fire beyond need.

The thing coiled in the hollow beneath the old cistern was not excess. It was a coal.

Vaskarath lay folded around his own heart the way a miser folds around a purse, scales gone the grey of banked embers, so still that for three long breaths August thought they had come all this way to find a corpse. The shrine around him—someone had built it a shrine, or perhaps he had grown it himself from the bones of the cistern—was a cathedral of small devotions. Candle stubs fused to stone. Ribbons bleached colorless. Coins worn featureless by centuries of hands that had come down here to ask something of a god who could no longer afford to answer.

"You came," said a voice, and it did not boom.

It scraped. It sounded like a page being turned very slowly, like the last inch of oil in a lamp deciding whether to go out. August's whole body wanted to run, and she stood her ground through pure professional habit: you do not flee an account before it is reconciled.

"You spoke," she managed.

"I have been speaking for some time." The great head lifted, and even lifting seemed to cost him. An eye opened—gold, but a gold so thinned it looked like brass left out in weather, like the gilt on a cheap reliquary. "In the only language left to me. Ash that falls up. Cold fire that leaves no entry." His gaze moved to Sefa, then back. "You read ledgers, little keeper. I have been writing in yours."

*The Ledger That Reads Back.* Her own handwriting, arriving unbidden in the margins of the oldest account in the guild's keeping. She had told herself it was a haunting. A trick. Something Corvane had arranged as a test within a test.

"Every answered prayer," August said slowly, working it aloud because working things aloud was how she kept from screaming. "The rain in a cloudless city. The cold ash at every miracle site. That's been you. You've been answering prayers."

"With what?" the dragon asked, and there was something terrible in the gentleness of it. "I have almost nothing left. A prayer costs more than I earn. But when someone prays *here*, in this place, close enough to my fire—I can spend a coal. One. And the ash falls upward because it is going home."

Sefa made a sound August had never heard from her—not the defrocked priest's dry wit, not the debtor's practiced bravado. Something younger. "My brother," she whispered. "When I prayed at the shrine above—the one with the ribbons—that was over your—"

"Yes." Vaskarath's eye closed briefly, as if even looking hurt. "Your brother's voice was not mine to give. I gave you the shape of him from your own memory and let the fire warm it. Forgive me. It was the closest thing to kindness I could still purchase."

* * *

August had rehearsed this moment on the long stair down. She had prepared questions the way she prepared audits: sequenced, cross-referenced, each one designed to corner the truth against its own contradictions. Account 001. The debt of fire. Vaskarath. Terms, principal, interest accrued across two hundred and eleven years.

What she had not prepared for was the way his voice made her want to apologize.

"The founding contract," she said instead, because apology was not in her skill set. "The guild's first account. I've read it. Or—I've read the parts that were left legible. There's a clause about a guarantor. A bloodline pledged as surety for—" She stopped. Started again. "You know whose name is signed there."

"I have known since before you were born." The coils shifted—a movement like dunes deciding to be mountains—and the heat coming off him, faint as it was, touched her face like a hand. "Come closer, August Okafor. Not too close. Closer than fear suggests. If we are to speak honestly, I would rather not shout. Shouting costs calories I do not have."

She came closer. Sefa stayed by the stair, arms crossed, watching everything with the flat vigilance of someone who had trusted institutions once and paid retail for it.

"Show me," August said. "Whatever you showed my grandmother's generation. Show me the theft."

Something moved behind the dragon's eye—something that might have been surprise, or grief, or the particular exhaustion of a creditor finally hearing the right question asked.

"Your grandmother did not ask to see," he said quietly. "Did you know that? Maren Okafor signed her name to a guarantee without ever once asking me what she was guaranteeing. She was nineteen. She believed the guild's arithmetic, because everyone believed the guild's arithmetic then. Everyone believed the guild was saving the world." A sound came out of him that took August a full five seconds to identify as laughter, because it was so dry and so far away. "They were, in a sense. They were saving their part of it. The rest was overhead."

"Show me anyway."

"Stand in the smoke," Vaskarath said, "and keep breathing."

He exhaled.

It was not fire. It was barely smoke—a thin grey ribbon that rose from his nostrils and thickened as it climbed, spreading along the vaulted ceiling of the dead cistern until the whole shrine was canopied in it. And then the smoke began to show things.

* * *

August had seen memory-magic before, or the guild's version of it: scrying pools, dream-ledgers, the expensive illusions the Concord of Faiths sold to wealthy penitents. Those were theater. This was accounting.

The smoke did not perform. It *itemized*.

She saw a city she half-recognized—Ashfall-on-the-Weep, older, whole, its rooftops unburnt—and above it, a sky with too many lights in it. Gods, she realized. Actual gods, walking openly, their bodies giving off light like open doors into furnaces. They were beautiful in the way that compound interest is beautiful if you are the one collecting it: radiant, inevitable, and feeding.

"They were starving even then," Vaskarath's voice said, somewhere inside the smoke. "Faith had thinned. Fewer prayers, thinner meals. The gods had ruled this valley since before your species kept written records, and they discovered—they *discovered*, the way a man discovers he cannot pay rent—that worship alone no longer sustained them. So they came to me. I was young. Two thousand years is young. I burned the way you imagine dragons burn. And I was proud, and stupid, and flattered that gods would negotiate with me at all."

The smoke showed a mountain. A hall carved into its heart, lit by the dragon's own fire—and figures in it, robed figures, human negotiators with ledgers under their arms. August recognized the seal on the oldest ledger instantly, because she stamped it onto documents six days a week. The Guild of Impossible Things, before it was a guild. Nine founders. One chair.

"The bargain was simple, which should have warned them. Simple bargains are the ones people sign fastest." The smoke sharpened. She saw the terms laid out on a stone table like cuts of meat. "I agreed to feed them. Voluntarily. A ration of my fire, given freely each season, enough to keep nine gods alive while faith recovered. In exchange, the guild swore protection—no hunter, no army, no rival power would touch me while I slept and healed. And as collateral for their promise—" the voice went very quiet— "they pledged a guarantor. One bloodline among their members, bound by oath, whose living fire would pass to me if the guild defaulted. Insurance. That is all a guarantor is. Insurance against the insurer."

"And they chose my grandmother's line," August said. Her voice sounded strange to her. Flat. Bookkeeper-flat.

"Maren Okafor volunteered. That is the detail that has kept me awake these two hundred years, keeper, if dragons sleep. She *volunteered*. She stood in this hall—young, laughing, certain the default would never come—and she signed, and the gods drank a toast of my fire, and everyone went home believing in each other." The smoke shuddered. "For sixty years, the bargain held. I fed them. They protected me. Faith recovered, slowly, the way bone knits. And then—"

The smoke went black.

Not dark—black, a hole in the vision, and when it cleared August was looking at the same mountain hall, but wrong. Scorch marks on walls that had been clean. The stone table split down the middle. And the guild's founders, all nine, standing in a ring around a sleeping dragon's hoard-side, doing something to the fire itself.

Not stealing it. That was the horror of it, the thing that made her stomach drop through the floor of the world. They weren't stealing anything. They were *re-routing*. She watched their hands move through the smoke with the sure, practiced gestures of people rerouting water or traffic or money—opening a channel from Vaskarath's burning heart directly into the sky, tapping the supply line, letting the gods drink straight from the source without the dragon's consent, without the ration, without the *accounting*.

"They found a way to take without asking," Vaskarath said. "Do you understand what that means, to a creature like me? It is not the loss. Loss I could survive. It is that they made me a well. Something you draw from. Something that does not get consulted." The eye beside her had gone narrow and bright, and for one flickering instant August saw the furnace underneath, the young dragon, the two-thousand-year-old pride, and she understood that his rage was not dead—it was only unpaid, deferred, accruing interest in the dark. "I woke fighting. Of course I woke fighting. I burned three of their number before they sealed me down here with bindings bought from the very gods I had been feeding. And the guild rewrote its own history overnight. The debt became mine. The theft became charity. The monster became the story they told children."

"But the guarantor clause survived," August whispered. "Because a contract needs its insurance even after everything else changes."

"It survived," Vaskarath agreed, "because your grandmother's signature survived, and signatures are the one thing your guild has never learned to forge cleanly. Everything else they rewrote. Her name they preserved like a relic. They have been spending her bloodline's interest for two hundred years, keeper, and none of them—not one guildmaster since—has ever had the courage to tell the guarantor's descendants what they were guarantors *of*."

* * *

The smoke settled. The shrine came back: candle stubs, colorless ribbons, the coal-grey mountain of the last dragon.

August found she was sitting. She had no memory of sitting. Sefa was beside her now, one hand hovering near her shoulder without landing, which was somehow more comforting than contact would have been.

"My mother," August said. "Her name wasn't in any guild record. I checked. When I got hired, I checked our whole family against the rolls, because—that's what you do, you check—" She heard herself spiraling and stopped. Breathed. "There's nothing. My mother doesn't exist on paper."

"No," Vaskarath said gently. "Because the guild deleted her. A guarantor's line stays cleaner if the guarantor doesn't know she exists. Your grandmother bore one daughter and hid her, and your mother bore you and hid you harder, and the guild let you both vanish from the books because a hidden guarantor is a safe guarantor." A pause. The upward-falling ash drifted between them like slow grey rain running the wrong way through time. "Until the debt came due. And then, suddenly, the guild needed to find you again. Tell me, August Okafor—how did a junior ledger-keeper with no connections come to be hired by the Guild of Impossible Things?"

The question landed like a coin dropped from a great height.

Corvane's kindness. The test no one expected her to pass. The sealed vault assigned to *her*, specifically, out of nine members and one chair. The warmth that had felt like mentorship and sat in her chest like belonging.

She had been hired the way one hires a key.

"Oh," she said. It was a very small word for the size of the thing collapsing inside her.

Sefa's hand landed on her shoulder after all. "Hey. Breathe. Whatever the arithmetic says, you're not a clause. I've known you four weeks and you're already the best person I know, and I used to work for gods, so my sample size is enormous."

August laughed—a cracked, involuntary sound—and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she lowered it, Vaskarath was watching her with an expression she recognized, absurdly, from mirrors: the look of someone doing sums they don't want to finish.

"There is more," the dragon said. "The reason the debt came due. The reason the gods are answering prayers again after a century of silence. You will want to understand it before you decide what you are."

"I'm not deciding anything tonight," August said. "Tonight I'm auditing. Tell me why the gods woke up."

Vaskarath drew a breath, and the whole shrine dimmed as he did, as if the room itself were lending him its light.

"Because the fire is running out," he said. "And when it does, they will not starve quietly. They will come for what they believe they are owed—and what they believe they are owed, little keeper, is everything your guild has, everything your city holds, and you most of all."

The ash fell upward between them, patient as interest accruing.

"So," said Vaskarath. "Shall I tell you the terms of the original sin?"
