## 14. Terms of the Original Sin

The flame was the size of a candle.

That was the first thing August could not stop looking at. She had descended past the shrine, past the cold ash that fell upward, down a throat of stairs carved by no human tool, and at the bottom of the world sat Vaskarath—last dragon of the age—and his fire was no bigger than a birthday candle guttering in a jar.

"You expected something larger," he said.

His voice did not echo so much as settle, like dust after an earthquake. He was coiled through the chamber the way a river is coiled through a valley: not arranged, simply *there*, scales the color of old iron gone green at the joints, eyes like two banked hearths that had learned to be careful with what they burned. He was smaller than the stories too. Everything about him was smaller than the stories. That, August understood with a sick lurch, was rather the point.

"Everything's larger when you owe it money," she said. "Or when it owes you."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been a smile. On a face built for rending, it looked more like a crack.

"A ledger-keeper," he said. "They sent me a ledger-keeper."

"No one sent me. I came on my own." Her voice held steadier than her knees. "Which your founding contract says is impossible. Clause nine. 'No heir of the Guarantor shall seek the Creditor.' My grandmother signed it, and apparently my whole life has been a violation of terms I never read."

"Your grandmother," said Vaskarath, "was the only one of them who ever apologized."

* * *

August had come down with questions arranged in order of urgency, the way she arranged everything: the debt, the amount, the interest schedule, the due date. She had rehearsed them on the stairs. She got exactly one out before the dragon spoke again.

"Sit."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit," he said, "or I will tell you everything standing, and standing truths go into people sideways and never come back out. Sit down among the ash, little keeper, and learn where your wages come from."

There were no chairs. There was ash, fine and grey and faintly warm, drifted against the walls in dunes. August sat on a low shelf of stone that might once have been part of an altar, which felt appropriate, because she was beginning to suspect she was in a temple, and that she had been in one all along—the Guild of Impossible Things was just its vestibule, and every ledger she had ever balanced was a prayer book written in numbers.

Vaskarath lowered his head until they were nearly level. Up close, his scales were not iron at all but glass, dark glass, and beneath the glass moved light. Very little light. A trickle. A thread.

"Do you know what prayer smells like?" he asked.

"Incense," August said. "Candles. Sweat, mostly. I grew up next to a cathedral."

"That is what prayer *wears*. What it *is*—" the great head tilted, considering how to make a thing simple without making it small, "—is heat. Attention burns. Hope burns. Every living creature that looks up at something bigger than itself and asks, produces warmth. The old gods fed on it the way your city feeds on grain: constantly, enormously, and without any thought for where the harvest grows. For ten thousand years, the world prayed, and the gods ate, and everyone called this natural law."

"And then?"

"And then we happened." His eyes closed. When they opened again there was something in them August recognized from the guild's oldest ledgers, from accounts marked in red ink that never closed: exhaustion wearing patience as a disguise. "Dragons are not made of fire the way your priests told you, to frighten children. We are fire's *custodians*. What burns in us cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be carried, tended, passed hand to hand. Before your guild, before your gods' hunger, the fire belonged to itself, and we kept it, and it was enough. Then the gods' congregations began to shrink. Fewer believers, less warmth, hungrier mouths. And the gods discovered that our hoard burned brighter than ten thousand prayers. Cleaner. Denser. One hour of true dragon-fire is worth a century of kneeling."

"So they stole it."

"They negotiated," Vaskarath said, and the word came out of him like a tooth being pulled. "That is what I want you to understand, ledger-keeper. No one kicked in the door. They sent clerks."

* * *

He showed her.

Not with words—with memory. The chamber dimmed, and the ash rose around them in slow spirals, and the spirals became pictures, and August stood inside a night that ended two hundred years ago.

She saw a hillside above a city she half-recognized, older, unwalled on its western side. She saw nine figures climb it carrying lanterns and documents in oilskin sleeves. She saw herself—or someone with her jaw, her stubborn line of shoulder, sixty years younger and walking with a limp—your grandmother, some part of August whispered, and her hands went cold in the vision's cold air. The nine figures spread their papers on the grass like merchants at a market stall.

And she heard the negotiation.

It was conducted, as Vaskarath had promised, in the language of clerks. The gods' emissary—a thing of gold light wearing the shape of a courteous woman—did not threaten. She *itemized*. She explained, with regret, that divine consumption currently exceeded divine income, that the shortfall would need to be met from alternative reserves, that certain parties possessed such reserves, and that the Concord of Faiths would prefer a contractual arrangement to the unpleasantness of requisition.

Requisition. August heard the word and understood, suddenly, why the guild's charter used it forty times. It was a borrowed word. They had learned to talk like this from the gods themselves.

Her grandmother's voice cut through the gold courtesy, young and furious: *"You're describing theft with a signature page."*

*"I am describing,"* the emissary replied, *"the alternative to theft. You will find, madam, that history remembers them very differently."*

And then the terms. August tried to hold each one as it passed, the way she held numbers, but memory-vision slipped like wet rope, and she caught only fragments:

—fire, drawn monthly, in quantities specified in a schedule annexed hereto;

—the guarantor's bloodline pledged as surety, unto the third generation and beyond, that the debtor shall not default, conceal, or flee;

—in exchange, the creditor's survival, and the survival of his kind's last vessel, guaranteed against seizure;

—and a final clause spoken softly, almost as an afterthought, that made the vision flicker and Vaskarath's presence around her tighten like a fist—

"—and there it is," the dragon said aloud, and the ash-picture collapsed. August found herself back on the stone shelf, heart slamming, hands pressed flat against the warm grit. "Clause eleven. The one they wrote quietest. Do you recall it?"

"The gods get the balance," she said. Her mouth tasted of copper and old paper. "When the account closes. Whatever fire remains when the last term expires—they collect it entire."

"When the account closes," Vaskarath agreed. "Or earlier. If the debtor defaults. If the guarantor's line fails. If the collateral attempts to withdraw itself from the agreement." His gaze rested on her with terrible gentleness. "You walked down here tonight in violation of four separate clauses. Do you understand now why no one stopped you? Why the doors opened? The contract *wants* you here. Your arrival is, technically, a collection event."

* * *

August made herself do arithmetic. It was the only prayer she knew.

"The Ember Audit," she said. "The account comes due at the audit. That's why Corvane sealed the vault. That's why the auditors from the Concord are coming early. It isn't a review—it's a repossession."

"It is a closing," said Vaskarath. "Two hundred years of monthly drafts. Do you know how much fire two hundred years of feeding takes, even rationed carefully, even taken from a dying man in spoonfuls?" He lifted one forelimb, and beneath the dark glass of his scales the light gathered, pooled, showed her plainly: a candle. A single candle's worth of divinity, hoarded in a body built to hold a sun. "This is what remains. I have been living on fumes and spite for twenty years. The gods have been living on me since before your mother was born. Every answered prayer in your city this season—every rain that falls from a cloudless sky—was paid for out of my account. Your guild has been running a distribution service and calling itself a guild of miracles."

"Sefa's rain," August whispered. "The cold ash. It's not ash at all, is it. It's dregs. It's what's left after the fire passes through."

"Burned-out grace. It matches nothing in your ledgers because your ledgers have no column for waste." He settled lower, coils shifting with a sound like a warehouse settling, like a mountain deciding to be patient. "So now, keeper, let me give you the full terms. Not the contract's terms. The real ones. Because your guild believes the account ends quietly, and your gods believe it too, and both of them are wrong in ways that will kill thousands."

She waited. Down here, waiting felt like a profession.

"When the fire runs out entirely—one way or another—the gods do not merely fade. They *starve*, and starving things of that size do not starve gracefully. Your cold cathedral downstairs—" August flinched; she hadn't told him about the cathedral, and his eye gleamed, amused, at having read her route like a ledger entry, "—holds proof that one god chose to die small and quiet, and even that death cracked the harbor and emptied a district of birds. Now multiply. Nine living gods. Nine starvations. Each one a slow detonation of everything it ever swallowed. Or—"

"Or," August said, because she could see the shape of it coming the way you see a wave coming, "they take it back by war."

"By war," Vaskarath confirmed. "Clause eleven gives them the right of collection. Collection against a debtor who cannot pay means collection against the collateral. Against the guild's assets. Against the guarantor's line. Against every soul your guild ever protected with its charter, which is, if you read the fine print—which no one does—a list of hostages." His voice dropped, and it did not drop far, because it had never needed volume; it was the voice of a thing that had watched empires file for bankruptcy. "They will come with light and call it justice. They will burn the ledger halls and call it audit. They will take the fire out of the world's last custodian, and out of the bloodline pledged beside him, and they will call the account settled. And your city will learn what a miracle costs when it is priced honestly."

August sat in the ash and felt the whole architecture of her life rearrange itself around a new load-bearing fact: that the guild had never been a guild. It was a payment plan. Every impossible thing it had ever done was a withdrawal. Every kindness it had ever recorded was a line item in a debt her grandmother had signed to keep a dragon alive and a city unburned—and the bill was coming due in days, addressed to a junior ledger-keeper who had spent her whole life hiding her own name in margins.

"Why tell me?" she asked finally. "You've had two hundred years to explain this to Corvane. To anyone. Why a junior clerk who broke clause nine?"

"Because Corvane already knows," Vaskarath said, "and has known for thirty years, and his answer was to seal the vault and hope the problem died of old age. Because your grandmother apologized, and her apology was the last honest transaction anyone made on that hillside, and apologies run in families like debts do. And because—" the great head lowered until his breath stirred her hair, breath that smelled of cold iron and banked things, "—because in two hundred years, little keeper, you are the first person who ever climbed down here to ask me a question instead of presenting me a bill. I wanted to see what you would do with the truth. I am still watching."

The candle-flame behind his scales flickered.

"One more term," he said. "The last one, and then you should sleep, though I doubt you'll manage it. The contract requires a guarantor. Not a name—a *function*. Someone of the bloodline, willing, aware, who stands surety for the account. Your grandmother filled it. After her, the office passed down whether anyone knew or not. It passed to you the day you were born, August Okafor, and every month since, a draft has been drawn against a fire that lives somewhere in your blood as surely as it lives in mine. You have been collateral your entire life. The only difference between yesterday and today is that today, you know the number."

August thought of the handwriting in the ledger that read back. Of entries in a script like her own that she had never written. Of the account that had begun, three nights ago, to write *to* her.

"I've seen it," she said quietly. "The ledger. It writes to me. In my hand."

Something ancient moved behind the hearth-light of his eyes—not surprise; recognition.

"Then it has begun choosing its next guarantor," Vaskarath said. "And you have very little time to decide whether you will sign—or whether you will be the first Okafor in two hundred years to refuse to pick up the pen."

Above them, muffled through two hundred feet of stone and the bones of the city, a bell began to ring. Not the audit bell. Not any bell August knew. It rang fast and ragged, alarm-pattern, and the cold ash on the floor began to tremble—and rise.

"They've felt it too," the dragon murmured. "Run home, keeper. Count fast. The gods just sent up a flare over the harbor, and flares are how creditors say *we know where you live*."
