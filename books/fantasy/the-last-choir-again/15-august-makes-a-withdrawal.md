## 15. August Makes a Withdrawal

The guild's money lived in three places, and August knew all of them the way she knew her own pulse.

There was the open ledger, which anyone could read and therefore almost no one did. There was the reserve ledger, which required two signatures to touch and one signature to forge, because Master Corvane had signed his name the same way for forty years—a loop on the C like a man waving goodbye—and she had watched him sign it eleven hundred times. And there was the contingency fund, which technically did not exist, which was kept in a strongbox under the floor of the records room beneath the third loose flagstone from the hearth, which Corvane thought only he knew about.

August had counted it once. Four hundred and sixty crowns in mixed coin, plus a small sack of old imperial gold that hadn't been legal tender since before the Concord of Faiths had a name. She'd counted it back when counting it was just diligence. Now it looked like a war chest.

She sat at her desk with the lamp turned low and the founding contract hidden inside the false spine of *Principles of Reconciliation*, and she made herself do the thing she always did first: the arithmetic.

Sefa needed lodging away from the debtor quarters where Corvane's informants drank. That was four crowns a week, paid in advance, untraceable if she paid it through the lamplighters' guild, who took a cut and asked nothing. The ash samples needed testing by an alchemist who wasn't guild-affiliated—Madam Oyelaran down on Tallow Street, who charged scandalously and worked discreetly. Ten crowns per assay, and August wanted three. Paper for forged travel documents, wax that matched the guild seal, a bottle of the cheap brandy Corvane kept in his office so she could match the stain pattern on the contingency receipts. Call it thirty crowns to start, and more after, because investigations, like debts, compounded.

Against that: her salary of nine crowns a month, of which seven went to her room and her meals, which left her a lifetime savings of exactly nothing.

She had never stolen anything in her life. She had never even rounded wrong. When she was twelve and a merchant had given her too much change, she had walked six streets in the rain to give it back, and her grandmother—her grandmother, whose name sat on the founding contract like a knife left standing in a table—had called her "the honest one" in a voice August had spent years trying not to think about now, because it hadn't been entirely pride in it. It had sounded, some nights, like relief. Like someone handing off a burden they were tired of carrying.

*The honest one.* Well. Grandmother. About that.

She opened the working ledger to the current page and smoothed it flat. This was the part no one understood about forgery, the part the ballads got wrong entirely. Forgery wasn't drawing. Anyone could draw. Forgery was *time*. A real entry was written by a tired person at a particular hour with a particular pen running out of ink, and every one of those facts bled into the strokes. If you wrote your false entry fresh and confident, at midnight, with a full reservoir, it would shine against its neighbors like a lie told too loudly.

So she waited. She copied entries for two honest hours first—coal deliveries, candle requisitions, the quarterly tithe to the Concord—until her hand ached in the specific way a clerk's hand ached at day's end. Then she dipped the pen and let it drain until the ink thinned, and then, only then, she wrote:

*Paid to H. Vell & Sons, stationers, 12 cr., sundries.*

H. Vell & Sons existed. She had checked. They were real, they sold sundries, and their proprietor was half-blind and delighted by any business. The receipt was already in her pocket; she'd bought actual paper there yesterday and paid for it twice, once in coin and once in the only currency that mattered here.

Twelve crowns. Her first theft.

Her hand didn't shake. That was the thing that frightened her most—not guilt, not yet, but the steadiness of it, the way the figures settled into the column as if they'd always lived there. She looked at the line for a long time and felt nothing but a cold professional satisfaction, and underneath the satisfaction something else, quieter and worse: recognition. She had read ten thousand fraudulent ledgers. She had testified, twice, against men who did exactly this, exactly this well. She knew what she looked like from the outside. She was the cautionary tale other junior keepers would someday be shown, the entry in someone else's margin.

*The ledgers always fear the ones who love them,* her training master used to say. *Because love is how you learn where the locks aren't.*

"Sorry, Master Ferren," she murmured to the empty room. "You were right."

* * *

Madam Oyelaran's shop smelled of sulfur and oranges, and Madam Oyelaran herself looked at the cold ash sample for exactly four seconds before saying, "Where did you get this?"

"A fireplace."

"No fireplace made this." She turned the little tin dish under her lens. The ash was grey-white and faintly blue at the edges, like frost on metal. "This isn't burned matter. This is *spent* matter. Something consumed itself to make heat and left nothing behind but the memory of burning. I can't match it to wood, coal, oil, tallow, bone, or any of the nineteen fuels in my reference library." She set down the lens and looked at August over it. "I can tell you what it costs to run the assays you'll want. Twenty-five crowns, and I don't write receipts."

"I need receipts."

"Then it's thirty, and my receipts will be beautiful." The old woman smiled with three gold teeth. "You're guild. You have the look. The look is: exhausted, competent, and lying about why you're here."

August put twenty crowns on the counter. "The rest Friday."

"And what should I call you?"

"Nobody. Nobody Okafor."

Madam Oyelaran laughed like a door hinge. "Ah. One of those. Fine, *Nobody*. Come Friday. Bring me more of the ash if you find it. Things that leave this behind are either dying or being murdered, and I've always wanted to meet one."

* * *

The lamplighters took their cut and asked nothing, exactly as advertised, which was how Sefa ended up with a room above a chandlery on Candlewick Row under the name Maren Doss, a widow of no particular history. August delivered the first week's rent personally, climbing the narrow stair with a wrapped loaf and a jar of pickles because spies brought paperwork and friends brought food, and she was trying, deliberately, to be the second thing.

Sefa had pinned the curtains shut and covered the walls with paper. Not maps—prayers. Dozens of them, transcribed in her round priest's hand, each annotated with dates and locations. The rain prayer answered in a cloudless city. A fever broken in Saltmarket. A lost ring returned to a woman who'd thrown it in the canal. A child's stutter gone overnight.

"They're spreading," Sefa said. She'd stopped sleeping; August could see it in the bruised skin under her eyes. "Two weeks ago it was one miracle a fortnight. Now it's one a day. They're answering faster."

"Faster is bad?"

"Faster is *hungry*." Sefa touched the wall of prayers without touching it. "In the seminary they taught us that gods answer at the speed of devotion. Slow answers meant weak faith. But that's not—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "That's not what this is. This is a merchant who's found buyers and is pushing stock. Every answer costs something, August. Every single one. And whatever it costs, they're paying it out of someone else's purse."

"The dragon," August said. It still felt strange to say aloud. *Vaskarath*, really, but the word dragon was easier in a small room.

"The dragon," Sefa agreed. "And here's what keeps me up at night. I did the numbers too, after you showed me the account. At the current rate of miracles—the rate that's *accelerating*—the fire runs out in months. Maybe a year. And then the gods don't gently fade, do they. Gods who are used to being fed don't fade. They come to the table."

"Which is why we need to know exactly how much fire is left and exactly how fast it's going." August unwrapped the bread. "Oyelaran can date the ash deposits. If we map every answered prayer against the burn residue, we can build the curve. Consumption over time. Then we extrapolate."

Sefa stared at her. "You're going to *account* the apocalypse."

"I'm going to *forecast* it. There's a difference. Forecasting has margins of error."

"You're impossible."

"Yes," August said, and heard, in her own voice, how much she'd changed—that she could say it lightly now, that the guild's old joke had become hers, a coat that fit. "It's why they hired me."

* * *

The contingency fund was the hard part, and she saved it for the deepest hour of the night, when even the building's ghosts seemed asleep.

She knelt on the cold flagstones with a pry bar and lifted the third stone from the hearth, and there was the strongbox, squat and smug in its little grave. The lock was good work. It was also a lock she'd studied for three evenings while dusting the records room, and its flaw was the same flaw as everything in the guild: it assumed honesty. The tumblers were arranged so that a key turned clockwise opened it—but so, if you knew the mechanism, did a thin blade turned counterclockwise with patience, because the maker had built a failsafe against keys breaking off inside, and failsafes were just doors you told people about in a quiet voice.

Four hundred and sixty crowns. She took one hundred and eighty. Not more—if the fund ever came up short, Corvane would tear the building apart stone by stone, and she couldn't survive that kind of search. But one hundred and eighty missing out of a box no one officially acknowledged? No one audited a ghost.

Then she did the thing she was proudest of and most ashamed of, and both feelings fit together so neatly it scared her. She went back upstairs, opened the reserve ledger, and wrote—in Corvane's own looping hand, practiced across two weeks of idle copying—a transfer authorization dated three days past:

*Contingency disbursement, 180 cr., per Master Corvane's instruction, re: sealed audit preparations. —M.C.*

Sealed audit preparations. Beautiful. Unfalsifiable. If questioned, the money had been moved for the very audit that might uncover its absence, and Corvane would have to either admit he'd authorized it or admit he hadn't, and either admission cost him. She had built a trap out of his own signature and baited it with his own suspicion of everyone else.

She blotted the page. She checked the ink age trick—one pass of the blotter, then leave it near the hearth's warmth for an hour, because fresh ink held a faint gloss that aged ink lost, and heat hurried the losing. She checked the alignment of the entry against the ruling lines. She read the whole ledger backward, entry by entry, hunting for the seam, and found nothing but a clean, boring, believable book.

Then she sat very still in the dark and let it happen: the grief.

Because this was the moment she'd known was coming since the night she'd found the founding contract, and knowing hadn't softened it. She had become a forger. A thief. A liar with excellent handwriting. Everything the Guild of Impossible Things existed to prevent—or rather, everything it existed to *manage*, because that was the trade Corvane had taught her, that was the guild's true scripture: the world ran on what people owed, and the guild stood between debtors and the fire. And now she stood on the other side of her own counter, moving invisible money, and the worst part—the part she'd take to whatever grave was coming for her—was how *good* she was at it. How natural. As though some talent had lain dormant in her all these years, patient as a seed, waiting only for permission.

Her grandmother's permission, maybe. Written into her blood along with the guarantee.

"Don't flatter yourself," she said aloud to the dark room. "Grandmothers don't plant larceny."

But the room didn't answer, and the ledger lay open beside her like a mouth, and she couldn't shake the feeling—irrational, unprofessional, true—that somewhere in the undercity, in the cold beneath the cold, something ancient had just felt its purse grow heavier and was pleased.

* * *

Friday she paid Oyelaran the balance and collected the first results, and the results were worse than she'd feared.

"Here." The alchemist spread three sheets across her cluttered bench. "Ash deposit one, from your church crypt—eleven days old. Deposit two, from the cobbler's chimney, six days. Deposit three, from wherever you got this last one"—she tapped the freshest sample, still faintly warm in its tin despite August having carried it across town in a cold box—"two days. Look at the residual density."

August looked. The numbers were small and getting smaller. Each deposit contained less than half the spent-fire residue of the one before it, even accounting for decay.

"That's not a linear decline," she said slowly. "If the draw rate were constant, the residues would scale with the miracles, not drop this fast. Unless—"

"Unless the source itself is guttering," Oyelaran finished. "Whatever's feeding your miracles is running dry faster than they're drinking. Like a lamp burning its own wick."

Like a serpent spending its own body, August thought. Fire drawn from flesh.

She walked back through the evening streets doing the arithmetic in her head, the terrible comfortable arithmetic, and by the time she reached the guild hall the number had assembled itself: at the current acceleration, accounting for the accelerating draw against a diminishing source, the fire would be exhausted within four months. Perhaps less. Perhaps much less, if the gods kept answering faster, chasing their own hunger downward into the dregs.

Four months. She'd come upstairs tonight to log the assay expenses—eighteen crowns, disguised as archive binding fees—and found the hall dim and empty, everyone gone home, the great ledger chained shut on its stand like a sleeping animal.

Except it wasn't quite empty.

A light burned in Corvane's office. Through the frosted glass of his door, his shadow moved once, unhurried, and settled. Stillness with weight in it. The stillness of a man sitting at his desk, in the dark, late, thinking.

Or the stillness of a man sitting at his desk, in the dark, late, *waiting*.

August stood in the doorway of her own records room, the assay reports crackling softly in her pocket, one hundred and eighty stolen crowns' worth of conspiracy folded against her ribs, and watched the shadow behind the glass.

Corvane had taught her the guild's true trade. He had said, that afternoon over tea, *we manage what people owe*, with such warmth, such genuine warmth—

He had signed the transfer authorization himself, in her hand, three days ago. If he compared dates. If he checked the fund. If he did any of the things she would do, in his place, because she had trained herself to be exactly the auditor he'd wanted and gotten precisely the enemy he'd deserved—

The shadow behind the glass shifted again. Rose. Moved toward the door.

August stepped back into the darkness of the records room, silent, heart hammering, and heard Corvane's latch click, and his footsteps cross the hall—unhurried, heavy, familiar—and stop just outside the records room doorway.

"Late night, Junior Keeper Okafor?" His voice was mild. Mild as milk. Mild as a man who has already counted everything in a room and is simply curious what you'll say about it.

She thought of the reserve ledger, open on her desk behind her, bearing his perfect forged signature. She thought of the strongbox, one hundred and eighty crowns lighter. She thought of Sefa's wall of prayers, of Oyelaran's three damning sheets, of the founding contract hidden in a false spine six feet from where he stood.

She thought: *lie well, or lose everything.*

"Always," August said, and stepped into the light.
