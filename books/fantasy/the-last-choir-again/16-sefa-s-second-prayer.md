## 16. Sefa's Second Prayer

The rain that wasn't still hung over the Lantern District three days later, and Sefa couldn't stop looking at it.

"It's not weather," she said. "I know it isn't. But it *feels* like weather."

August followed her gaze up through the guild-house window, past the soot-stained glass, to where a grey shimmer clung to the rooftops like breath on cold glass. It had no clouds behind it. No wind drove it. It simply hung there, a suggestion of rain, an apology from a sky that had forgotten how.

The city had lost its last real cloud eleven years ago, when the foundries of the Concord of Faiths burned so hot for so long that the atmosphere itself gave up trying. August had been nine. She remembered her mother pointing at the empty blue and saying, *Well. That's that,* in the tone she used for closed accounts.

Now the sky was remembering.

"Stop looking at it," August said. "You'll want it too much."

"I already want it too much." Sefa turned from the window. She had the look August was learning to dread—the one where her eyes went somewhere far away and didn't come back all the way. "That's the problem with answered prayers, August. You don't get to un-hear them."

* * *

They met in the counting room after hours, because Sefa refused to meet anywhere the gods might consider consecrated and August refused to meet anywhere the ledgers might consider unwatched. The counting room sat between those jurisdictions: candlelit, dust-furred, smelling of tallow and old ink and the faint mineral tang of the sealed vault two floors down, which August had begun to notice the way you notice a tooth that wants to ache.

Sefa spread her hands flat on the table. They were good hands, broad-knuckled, a stonemason's hands before the debts took the trade and gave her nothing back but the habit of shaping air.

"I'm going to pray again," she said.

"No."

"You don't even know what I—"

"I know exactly what." August kept her voice level. This was the trick of ledgers and of arguments: never let them see your entries wobble. "You're going to kneel down in some corner and ask whether they know your name, and something is going to answer, and then neither of us gets to pretend anymore that the first time was an accident."

"The first time was rain in a cloudless city. That's not an accident. That's an *invoice.*"

August flinched at the word. Sefa noticed. Of course she noticed; she'd spent six weeks in the debtor's pews of the guild hall learning to read people the way August read columns.

"That came out wrong," Sefa said.

"No. It came out right. That's what frightens me." August pulled the ledger toward her—the working ledger, not the old one—and turned to the page she'd titled, in small careful letters, *Anomalies, Atmospheric.* One entry. Rain without clouds. Cold ash without a source. A prayer with a return address. "Everything the guild does, we do by exchange. Nothing comes from nowhere. If the gods are answering prayers again, they're paying for it with something, and I need to know what, and I can't learn anything if you go kneeling in front of them asking for more."

"I'm not asking for more." Sefa's voice dropped. "I'm asking one question. Whether they know me. There's a difference between buying and being *recognized*, August. You wouldn't understand. You've never been nobody."

The words landed harder than Sefa probably intended, or exactly as hard as she intended—August genuinely couldn't tell, and that was its own small wound. She looked down at the ledger so she wouldn't have to look up.

"I keep accounts," she said quietly, "because numbers don't forget you exist."

Sefa reached across the table and put one mason's hand over August's ink-stained fingers. Her palm was rough as bark. "I know why you do it. That's why I'm telling you first. Before I do it. So someone knows where I was standing when I did."

* * *

She chose the ash-court behind the tanners' row, because it was ugly ground, unconsecrated, reeking of lime and rendered fat, and because—as she put it—if the gods were going to eavesdrop, let them do it somewhere honest. August stood at the court's edge with her ledger hugged to her chest like a shield, telling herself she was there to record data, not to protect anyone, and knowing both statements were lies.

The sun was going down the way it always did now: quickly, sullenly, the light going thin and coppery through the haze of the foundry districts. Sefa knelt in the ash. She didn't fold her hands. She just knelt, the way a woman kneels at a grave, and August understood suddenly that this was the shape the prayer would take—not petition but visitation.

"They answered once," Sefa had said on the walk over. "Do you understand what that means? For twenty years I prayed every morning. Twenty years, and the sky stayed empty, and my brother stayed dead, and I learned to pray the way you sweep a floor—because the sweeping has to happen whether or not anyone sees. And then one day the sky *moved*. Something heard me. And I have spent every night since wondering: was it listening all along? Was it there the whole time, behind the silence? Or did it only just arrive?" Her jaw worked. "Either answer is unbearable. So I'm going to ask."

She bowed her head. August opened the ledger. Wrote the date. Wrote the location. Wrote, in the column for expected outcomes, a dash, because she could not bring herself to write *none.*

"Sefa Adeyemi," Sefa said to the darkening air. Not loud. Just clear. The way you say a name into a doorway when you're not sure anyone's home. "You sent me rain. I never told you my name. So either you knew it, or you went and found it, or—" her breath caught and steadied, caught and steadied, "—or you're very good at pretending. I want to know which. That's all. That's the whole prayer. Who am I to you?"

Silence.

The ash stirred once, settled. Somewhere beyond the tannery wall, a dog began barking at nothing and thought better of it.

August felt her shoulders start to drop—the small, stupid relief of the skeptic whose skepticism has been confirmed—and then the temperature fell.

Not gradually. All at once, like a door opening onto winter. The copper light didn't dim so much as *thin*, and the shadows under the tanning vats deepened from grey to black, and August's breath came out white though the evening hadn't been cold.

And then a voice said:

"*Sefi.*"

August's pen stopped mid-stroke, leaving a blot.

It was a young man's voice. Warm, a little hoarse, with the particular cracked brightness of a boy whose voice had finished changing only weeks before it stopped forever. It came from everywhere and nowhere—from the ash, from the walls, from directly beside August's ear—and it said the name with such naked, unguarded delight that August's eyes stung before she could stop them, and she was a stranger to this dead brother. She could not imagine what it did to the woman kneeling five feet away.

Sefa made a sound August had never heard a person make. Not a sob. Something older than sobbing—a sound from before language, from the animal floor of grief.

"Tobias," she whispered.

"*You got taller,*" the voice said, laughing, disbelieving. "*Or I got smaller. Everything's smaller here. Sefi, Sefi—it's really me. It worked, the prayer worked, I've been trying to reach you for—*" The voice faltered, caught itself, brightened again with terrible effort. "*I don't know how long. Time's strange. But you called and I heard, I HEARD you, do you know what that's—I thought I'd be alone in the dark forever and then you PRAYED and the rain came and I thought, that's her, that's my sister, she always did find water where there wasn't any—*"

"Where are you?" Sefa's hands were pressed flat against the ash as if she could feel him through the ground. "Toby, where—are you all right?"

A pause. Just a hair too long. "*I'm fine. I'm fine. It's quiet here. It's cold, but I'm used to it. Sefi, listen—you have to keep praying. Don't stop. As long as you talk to me I can stay close to the surface, I can almost feel the sun—*"

August moved before she decided to move.

She stepped into the ash-court, into the circle of that impossible cold, and said, in her flattest ledger-voice: "What do you cost?"

The voice stopped.

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. August stood in it with her chin up and her knees soft and her whole body screaming at her to run, and she held her ground the way she held a column of figures—by refusing to blink first.

"*Who's that?*"

"A friend," Sefa said, and her voice was wrecked and grateful and furious all at once. "Toby, this is August. She keeps books. Toby—Toby, answer her question."

"*I don't know what she means.*"

"She means nothing's free." August crouched slowly, keeping her eyes on the space where the voice wasn't, and set the open ledger on the ash between them like a bargaining chip, like a dare. "Rain out of an empty sky costs something. A voice across the veil costs more. Somebody is paying for this call, Tobias Adeyemi, and I want to know who, and with what. Because I work for the Guild of Impossible Things, and I will find the entry. It's what I do."

Another silence. Longer.

When the voice came again, the warmth had gone out of it like a coal left uncovered. Not gone cold—*banked*. Careful. And underneath the carefulness, so faint August might have imagined it, something that sounded horribly like fear.

"*Tell her to stop counting,*" it said—not to August, but to Sefa, and the tenderness in it was worse than any threat. "*Sefi. Listen to me. Forget her. Forget the books. You don't need to know how the rain works to get wet. You just need to keep praying. Every morning, like you used to. Promise me. Promise me you won't stop, and I promise you'll never lose me twice.*"

"Oh, Toby." Sefa's composure broke clean through, and she was crying now, openly, ash-grey tears cutting tracks down her face. "I spent six years losing you. I'm not going to lose you because of *arithmetic*—"

"Arithmetic is how they get you!" August's voice cracked out sharp enough to make a tanner's dog start up again beyond the wall. She forced herself lower, quieter. "Sefa. Look at me. Look at me, not at the sky. Six years ago, when you prayed and nothing answered—did you love him less?"

"What?"

"When the sky was silent. When he never came. Did you love him any less than you love him right now?"

Sefa stared at her. The question went in like a splinter, and August watched it work.

"No," Sefa said finally. Bitterly. "No, of course not."

"Then whatever is talking to you doesn't hold your love. Your love holds *itself*. It existed for six years with no voice attached, and it will exist tomorrow with no voice attached, and anything that tells you otherwise—that says *keep paying or lose me*—" she jabbed a finger at the ash, at the cold, at the banked-warm silence, "—that is not your brother. That is a subscription wearing your brother's face."

"*Sefi,*" the voice said, and now it sounded hurt, wounded, betrayed, and it was a masterwork, it was flawless, August could hear the sixteen-year-old boy in it, the specific crack of his laugh, and she hated how much she wanted it to be real. "*Why is she saying these things? Why would you listen to—Sefi, please, it's cold here, I'm so cold, and you're the only warm thing I've ever—*"

"Stop," Sefa whispered.

"*Just one prayer a day. That's all I need. One—*"

"*STOP.*"

The word hit the ash-court like a dropped bell. The cold snapped off. The shadows sprang back to their proper grey. The copper evening light poured in again, ordinary and thin and real, and the silence afterward rang.

Sefa knelt in the ash with her fists pressed against her mouth, shaking. August knelt beside her—she didn't remember deciding to—and put an arm around the broader shoulders, and for a long time neither of them said anything. The dog beyond the wall had given up entirely.

"He sounded exactly like him," Sefa said at last, hollow. "Every detail. The way he said my name. The—there was this joke, about the well, when we were kids, and it—" She laughed, one broken note. "He used it. He used the well joke, August. Nobody alive knows the well joke."

August's arm tightened around her. Because here was the thing she could not say, the entry she could not post: *someone* knew the well joke. Dead men didn't keep secrets; graves weren't vaults. Whatever had worn Tobias's face had gotten the details from somewhere—from memory, from record, from some archive of the departed that the old gods apparently kept the way the guild kept ledgers.

Which meant the voice hadn't invented her brother.

It had *audited* him.

"I believe you loved him," August said carefully. "I believe everything it knew was true. That's what makes it dangerous. Lies are cheap, Sefa. Anyone can forge a lie. But a truth deployed at the exact moment of maximum—" she searched for the word and hated it when she found it, "—maximum *leverage*. That takes bookkeeping. Someone did research. Someone built this. Your brother is not a lure on a hook. But somebody read his file."

Sefa pulled back and looked at her, eyes red-rimmed and terribly steady. "You think they're fishing."

"I think the rain was free," August said. "The first taste is always free."

They gathered themselves in silence. August retrieved her ledger, brushed the ash from the open page—and stopped.

Below her blotting-pen entry, below the date and the location and the dashed line where outcomes should be, new writing had appeared. Small. Neat. In handwriting that made her stomach drop through four floors of guild-house, because she knew that hand intimately, had sharpened her own penmanship against it for years in front of a cracked mirror.

Her own hand.

The entry read:

*Received: one prayer, second instance. Paid: one voice, deceased male, adequate likeness. Note: subject resists conversion. Recommend escalation. Recommend the sister. Sisters always pay.*

August read it twice. Then she turned the page toward Sefa, whose face went through recognition, horror, and something colder and harder that August had never seen there before—the mason coming back into the mason's hands.

"Sisters always pay," Sefa repeated. Flat. Quiet. The way stone sounds just before it cracks.

"Yes," August said, closing the ledger. Her own handwriting stared up at her from beneath the cover, patient as a trap. "And I think it's time we asked ourselves who's been writing in my books."

* * *

They walked back through the dusk-lit streets without speaking, past shuttered stalls and guttering lamps and neighbors pulling doors closed against a night that had started arriving colder than it used to. At the corner where their routes divided, Sefa stopped.

"One prayer a day," she said. "That's what it wanted. Small number. Manageable."

"Small numbers are how every ruin starts," August said. "I balance books for a living. Nobody ever went bankrupt from one enormous loss. They go bankrupt from small numbers they agreed to."

Sefa nodded slowly. Then, at the edge of the lamplight, she smiled—a thin, fierce, terrible smile that August would remember for years, because it was the first time she'd ever seen Sefa look like a woman deciding something rather than enduring it.

"Good," Sefa said. "Because I lied to it."

August went still. "What?"

"Back there. When it asked me to promise I wouldn't stop praying." The smile widened a fraction. "I promised."

"Sefa—"

"It needed to think it had me." She turned up her collar against the cold and stepped backward into the dark of her street. "You can't audit a mark who walks away, August. So tomorrow, I pray. And you bring your ledger. And this time, when my brother's voice asks me for something—we make it *show us where the money comes from.*"

August stood alone at the corner long after Sefa's footsteps faded, hugging the ledger to her chest, feeling the new entry pressing against the leather cover like a heartbeat.

*Sisters always pay.*

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow they'd make the gods show their work.

She just wished she believed, even a little, that they were the ones setting the trap—and not walking, step by measured step, deeper into someone else's.
