## 17. The Audit Arrives Early

They came three days before the Ember Audit was scheduled to begin, which was the first thing wrong about them.

August knew it before anyone said a word. She had spent eleven years learning the guild's rhythms the way other people learned their own heartbeats—the creak of the east stair at dawn when Master Ivo took his walk, the particular silence that fell over the counting room an hour before any announcement, the way the ledgers themselves seemed to settle on their shelves like animals sensing weather. And on the morning the Concord arrived, every ledger in the building was awake before she was.

She came down to find the front doors already open and six strangers standing in the entrance hall, arranged with the casual precision of people who had rehearsed their arrival on the way there. Grey robes. Silver pins shaped like open hands. The Concord of Faiths did not believe in ostentation; they believed in being looked at anyway.

"Junior Ledger-Keeper," said the one in front. Not a question. A classification.

"August Okafor," she said, because her name was the only weapon she'd never been trained to use and therefore the only one she trusted.

"Auditor-Prime Vessal." The woman's face was smooth as an unsigned contract. "The Ember Audit has been advanced. Effective now."

"The audit begins on the first of Embermonth," August said. "By charter—"

"Charters are precisely what we've come to examine." Vessal smiled the way frost forms: gradually, and then all at once. "You may fetch your superiors. Or we may begin without them. The Concord is indifferent to which."

Behind her, August heard the slow tread of Master Corvane on the stairs, unhurried, deliberate, the tread of a man who had been awake for hours and dressed for exactly this. He descended into the hall wearing his formal grey-and-gold, the chain of office resting on his chest like something he'd been born holding up.

"Auditor-Prime." He bowed exactly deep enough. "We received no notice."

"Notices can be prepared for," Vessal said. "That is rather the point of them."

Corvane's smile didn't move but August, standing where the light from the door caught his eyes, saw something shift behind them. Not fear. Calculation running faster than it wanted to.

*He didn't know either,* she thought. *Or he knew and hoped he was wrong.*

*Which of those is worse?*

*Which of those is me?*

* * *

They convened in the Great Counting Room within the hour, because the Concord did not wait and the guild, whatever else it was, knew how to make waiting look like hospitality. August took her place at the junior bench with her ink, her blotter, and her hands folded so that no one could see them tremble—not with fear, though there was some of that, but with the specific terror of a person who knows exactly which drawer in the house is full of bones.

Nine members, one chair, six attendants. Corvane at the head. Masters Ivo, Delune, and the twins Ashkar and Oskar along the long table, plus old Mother Quill, who had not attended a full session in four years and had come today wearing her ceremonial spectacles, which meant she expected theater.

Vessal's five companions spread through the room like poured water. One stood by the ledger shelves. One examined the ceiling. Two flanked the doors. The last—a young man with ink-stained fingers and the flat gaze of someone who had read more documents than he had ever loved—sat down across from August without asking, unrolled a leather case of instruments, and began arranging calipers, magnifiers of graduated strength, and a small brass balance in the manner of a surgeon laying out knives.

"Ledger-keeper," he said. It wasn't a greeting.

"Okafor," August said. "And you?"

"Brammel. Concord Office of Provenance." He tested a magnifier against his eye, put it down. "I'll be reviewing your working ledgers. Daily entries, marginalia, correction practices. You'll provide access to everything you touch."

"I touch most things," August said.

"I know," Brammel said. "Your hand appears in eleven years of continuous entries. Very consistent. Very disciplined." He looked up, and for a moment the flatness in his gaze flickered into something almost like respect. "It will make forgery easy to detect. Consistent hands betray deviation beautifully."

August kept her face still while her stomach performed a slow, elegant revolution.

*I have forged three entries in the past two weeks,* she thought. *Withdrawals routed through the Sump accounts, disguised as depreciation on impossible inventory. Flawless. I checked them forty times.*

*Flawless against the guild's eyes.*

*Nobody here has guild eyes.*

"Of course," she said aloud. "Transparency is our founding principle."

Across the room, Corvane's head turned a degree, the way it always did when she quoted the charter back at him.

* * *

The first day of the advanced audit was, formally speaking, about nothing. Vessal requested the guild's statement of purpose, its membership rolls, its fee schedules, its charitable disbursements. She read each document at a pace that communicated she could have read it twice as fast and was choosing slowness as a form of pressure. The members answered questions. The members' answers were transcribed by one of the silent Concord scribes, whose pen moved without pause and whose face suggested he had transcribed confessions in nine languages and found none of them interesting.

August sat, recorded, and watched.

She watched Vessal ask Master Delune how many prayers the guild had brokered in the last fiscal year, and watch Delune answer smoothly, and watch Vessal write a single word in her own private notebook—one word, underlined once—and say nothing.

She watched the auditor at the ledger shelves pull down volume after volume, fan the pages with his thumb, and stop, twice, at gaps in the sequence. Both times he made a small mark on a slip of paper. Both times August's heart performed a maneuver she would have described, if asked to log it, as *asset loss, unrecoverable.*

She watched Corvane be Corvane: gracious, precise, generous with information that cost him nothing and stingy with everything that mattered. She had spent months believing she was learning to read him. Now she realized she was watching a man perform the same skill back at her, at a level she hadn't known existed, and the humiliation of that was almost clarifying.

And she watched Brammel work.

This was the thing she couldn't stop doing. He went through her working ledgers page by page, and his instruments were not for show. The calipers measured letter height and stroke width. The balance weighed pages—weighed *pages*, as if paper itself could confess—and twice he paused over a sheet, weighed it again, and set it aside into a growing stack.

At midday he spoke without looking up. "Your ink supplier."

"Quarrel & Sons, Tanner's Row."

"Batch numbers?"

"We don't record batch numbers."

"Hm," said Brammel, in the tone of a man adding a line to a very satisfying sum.

* * *

They broke at dusk, and the guild exhaled, and August did not.

She found Sefa in the courtyard behind the hall, sitting on the low wall where the rain barrels used to stand before the clouds stopped keeping appointments. The defrocked priest had taken to feeding the pigeons that no longer existed, scattering crumbs onto empty cobblestones with the serene persistence of true madness or true faith, depending on which sermon you'd last heard.

"They're early," Sefa said, without turning.

"You've seen?"

"I've *felt* them. Concord smells like cold iron and other people's sins. We used to joke about it in seminary." She scattered another handful of crumbs. "Early audits mean somebody upstream got nervous. Faiths don't move fast unless they're frightened, and faiths don't frighten unless something's answering prayers it shouldn't be able to afford."

August sat down beside her. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the silence between them had become, over these weeks of ash-tracing and ledger-hunting and one disastrous, wonderful night of comparing childhoods in a tavern that smelled of lamp oil, a silence August could actually rest in.

"The sealed vault," Sefa said quietly. "How long until they ask for it?"

"That's the question, isn't it." August turned her blotter over in her hands. She'd carried it out of habit and now it just sat there like an accusation with a cover. "Charter says the Ember Audit reviews all accounts. All accounts, Sefa. That language has been in there since the founding. It's very proud of itself. 'Nothing hidden from honest examination.'" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Whoever wrote that either never expected an honest examiner or never opened the bottom drawer."

"And what happens when they do?"

"Then they find an account older than the guild. A debt of fire owed to something called Vaskarath. A founding contract with a signature on the guarantee line that—" August stopped. Saying it aloud in daylight, in the guild's own courtyard, still felt like stepping off a roof. "A signature I share blood with."

Sefa finally turned. In the failing light her face was all planes and patience. "Your grandmother."

"My grandmother bought this guild its life with fire that was never ours to sell, and guaranteed it with a bloodline nobody told me I was carrying around like a coiled debt in my own veins." August's voice stayed level, which surprised her. "And now the Concord is here three days early, weighing our *paper*, and I keep thinking about what Vaskarath told me. When the fire runs out, the gods don't fade politely. They invoice. And the Concord of Faiths—" she gestured at the lit windows of the hall behind them, at the grey-robed silhouettes moving inside like patient moths, "—is the gods' collections department."

"So the audit isn't an audit."

"The audit is a *pre-invoice*. They're here to establish the value of the assets before the bill comes due." She looked down at her hands. "And I'm starting to suspect I'm listed among the assets."

Sefa reached over and closed August's fingers around the blotter, gently, the way you'd close a wound.

"Then we have three days," Sefa said, "to become worthless."

* * *

The second day, the Concord asked about the vault.

They did it casually, which was the frightening part. Vessal set down her tea, consulted a slip of paper she clearly did not need, and said, "Master Corvane. Our review of the charter notes that the Ember Audit encompasses *all accounts maintained by the guild*, including those designated sealed, restricted, or legacy. We'll want the sealed vault opened tomorrow at first light. Inventory complete. Every entry produced."

The room did not react, because the room was full of masters, and masters do not react. But August, watching from the junior bench, catalogued the non-reactions the way she catalogued entries: Ivo's pen pausing for exactly one heartbeat too long. Mother Quill adjusting her ceremonial spectacles though her eyes were fine. The twins exchanging a glance so fast it might have been a muscle spasm, except that twins who had survived forty years in the Guild of Impossible Things did not have muscle spasms.

Corvane set down his own cup.

"The sealed vault contains the founding records of the guild," he said. "Contracts of extraordinary age and sensitivity. Some of the signatories' descendants sit in this room."

"All the more reason for honesty," Vessal said pleasantly. "Unless the guild has something to hide."

"The guild has nothing to hide," Corvane said. "It has things that belong to *others*. There is a difference, Auditor-Prime, and my entire profession exists to explain it to people who confuse the two."

"Then explain it to us tomorrow." Vessal smiled her frost smile. "With the drawer open."

*Later,* August thought, *much later, I would try to remember whether Corvane looked at me then. Everyone swore afterward that he hadn't. I swear I felt it anyway—that half-second weight of a man checking his inventory.*

The session ended. The members filed out. August gathered her ink and blotter with hands that had finally, mercifully, stopped shaking sometime around noon, and she was nearly to the door when Brammel fell into step beside her.

"You weigh your corrections," he said.

It wasn't a question, but she treated it like one. "Standard practice. Corrected entries are re-verified by mass and impression."

"Mm. Your corrections are good." They walked together down the corridor, his instrument case swinging at his side like a pendulum. "Good corrections bother me more than bad ones, you know. Bad corrections are sloppiness. Good corrections are *intent*." He stopped at the junction where their paths divided. "Eleven years, and I count maybe two hundred corrections in your hand. Of those, perhaps fifteen deviate even slightly from your established stroke profile. Do you know what that suggests to me?"

"That I'm careful."

"That you're *practiced*." His flat gaze held hers, and there it was again, that flicker of something underneath—not suspicion exactly, not yet, but the space where suspicion would be installed if the measurements justified it. "Careful people make consistent mistakes, Ledger-Keeper. Practiced people make inconsistent successes. I'll let you decide which category you're in." He adjusted the strap of his case. "First light tomorrow. Wear something warm. Vaults hold their grudges at cellar temperature."

He walked away, and August stood in the corridor listening to the clock in the entrance hall tick, and for the first time in her life the sound of it made her feel counted rather than counting.

* * *

She did not go to her room. She went down.

Past the counting rooms, past the record halls with their sleeping thousands of volumes, down stairs that grew older as they descended, to the corridor outside the sealed vault where the air tasted of cold stone and older promises. The Concord had posted no guard—they were saving the vault for morning—but the door stood behind its triple seals like a held breath.

August pressed her palm flat against it.

Inside was the founding contract. Inside was Vaskarath's account, the debt of fire, the ledger that sometimes wrote back to her in handwriting like her own. Inside was her grandmother's name on a guarantee line, and beneath all of it, somewhere below the stone, a serpent the size of a cathedral slowly starving while gods above burned his stolen life to answer prayers about rain and love and safe journeys home.

Tomorrow at first light, strangers with calipers would open that door and measure every secret she had, and they would find the one thing she could not forge, hide, or correct: that the guarantor of the guild's oldest debt was standing in the corridor with her hand on the wood.

*Three days,* Sefa had said. *Become worthless.*

But August had run those numbers already, in the privacy of her own skull, the way she ran all numbers. Worthless was not available. She was the guarantor. Her blood was the collateral. There was no version of tomorrow in which the Concord opened that vault and did not, sooner or later, follow the signature trail straight to her name—unless the name stopped being there to find.

Her breath fogged on the cold door. Below her feet, faint as a pulse under bandages, something vast shifted in its sleep.

"Tomorrow they come for you," August whispered to the dark under the floor. "And the day after, they come for me. So I need you to hear me now, old serpent: I am done letting other people set my terms. Whatever they're owed—we're going to negotiate. All of us. Even the ones upstairs who think I'm furniture."

Far below, so faint she might have invented it, something that might have been laughter moved through the stone.

Above her, at the top of the stairs, the entrance hall clock began to strike midnight—and kept striking past twelve, past thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen strokes, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty, a count with no end in sight, while every candle in the corridor guttered blue, and August Okafor understood that the Concord had not come early for an audit at all.

Something had already gone wrong. And the clock downstairs was counting it.
