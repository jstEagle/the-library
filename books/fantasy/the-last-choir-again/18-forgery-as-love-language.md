## 18. Forgery as Love Language

The back room of the Lamplighter's Rest smelled of tallow smoke and cheap wine and, now, of ink.

August had pushed three tables together in the middle of the floor and covered them with everything she'd smuggled out of the guild over the past six days: nibs in four widths, two bottles of iron-gall ink, one of oak gall cut with soot for the older hands, a ream of ledger paper still bearing the guild's watermark if you held it to the light, blotting sand, a bone folder, and a razor for scraping mistakes off vellum without tearing it.

Nine people stood around the tables looking at the supplies the way you'd look at a snake someone had just set down among the cups.

"This is a crime," said Ovid Hask, the lamplighter who owed the guild one impossible thing—specifically, he owed them the debt of having once lit a lamp that never went out, which sounded like a blessing until you tried to explain it to the fire-wardens. He was sixty, with hands like tree roots and the expression of a man who had spent his whole life being careful. "I want to state for the record that this is a crime."

"Noted," said August. "For the record: everything we've done since I pulled you out of the audit queue is a crime. Sitting in this room is conspiracy. Drinking Wren's wine is conspiracy. Breathing near these tables is conspiracy. The Concord doesn't grade sins, Ovid. It counts them." She uncapped the ink. "So we may as well be good at it."

Wren, who ran the Lamplighter's Rest and owed the guild a debt so old she'd stopped remembering what it was for, snorted from behind her bar. "She's got you there, old man."

"I don't like it," Ovid said, but he sat down.

Nobody liked it. That was the thing August was learning about the debtor-choir, as she'd privately started calling them: they were all people whose entire lives had been built on not doing this. Debtor-priest Anselm Voss, defrocked for praying to a god who answered too specifically, kept his hands folded like he was waiting for absolution. Marisol Quen, the tide-watcher who owed an account of impossible weather, kept glancing at the door. Little Tam Broadstairs, who at nineteen owed the guild a miracle he'd been sold at fourteen and couldn't deliver, looked like he might be sick into the inkwell.

They were honest people. That was what made them useful, and what made them breakable, and what made August's chest hurt in a way she didn't have time to examine.

"Rule one," she said. "We never forge anything true."

That got their attention. Even Ovid looked up.

"Think about it," she said. "If the Concord finds a forged entry, they'll compare it against reality. Against witnesses, against weather records, against the grain of the truth. So we never counterfeit something that happened. We only ever forge *absence*. A page that was never written. A meeting that never occurred. A transfer that leaves no mark because officially nothing moved." She held up a sheet of ledger paper. "Lies are heavy. They have weight, and weight leaves tracks. But a hole? A hole weighs nothing. You can't arrest a hole."

"That's not how holes work," said Tam faintly.

"It's exactly how holes work," said August. "I've been making them in ledgers since I was twelve years old. My grandmother taught me." She hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out anyway, the way true things do when you're tired, and she watched it land on them—the small shift in the room, the way Anselm's folded hands loosened slightly. She had told none of them about her bloodline. About Vaskarath. About the founding contract currently hidden inside the false bottom of Wren's flour barrel, wrapped in oilcloth like a corpse.

Later, she promised herself. When they needed to know, they'd know. Not before. Telling people made them responsible, and responsibility was a weight, and she was trying very hard to keep her family light.

"Rule two," she said. "We practice until our lies are boring. Nobody gets suspicious of a boring lie. If you're going to fake a guild entry, make it dull. Make it about lamp-oil requisitions. Make it so tedious that even the auditor checking it wishes he were dead. Excitement is a confession."

"Rule three," said Sefa, from the corner where she'd been sitting with her boots up and a cup of wine she wasn't drinking.

August looked at her. Sefa had appointed herself rule-inventor sometime around midnight two nights ago, and August had let her, because Sefa was better at this than she wanted to admit—a defrocked priest knew more about institutional lying than any ledger-keeper alive.

"Rule three," Sefa said, swinging her boots down, "is that we lie for each other and never to each other. In here, this room is a confessional in reverse. Whatever you did outside, whatever you're asked outside, you bring it back here and you say it plain. The moment we start lying inside these walls, we're dead, because we'll be lying alone, and liars alone are easy to pick off one at a time."

Silence around the tables. Tam swallowed. Marisol nodded slowly, like a woman agreeing to weather.

"That's the real rule," August said quietly. "Everything else is technique."

* * *

They practiced for four hours.

August taught them the way her grandmother had taught her, which was to say cruelly and with love. She wrote a line in Master Corvane's hand—the tight, downward-slanting script of a man who pressed too hard because he believed pressure was character—and then made Ovid copy it forty times while she circled the failures in red.

"Your *r*s are too generous," she told him. "Corvane has never been generous with a letter in his life. Look—he strangles them. Every *r* is a little noose."

"He sounds charming," Ovid muttered, and bent over the page again.

To Marisol she taught dates. "Auditors check dates before they check anything else. Ink ages. Paper ages. If you write 'the third of Embermonth' tonight, the ink will be too fresh, too black, too wet-looking under magnification. So we age it." She showed them the trick with the warm oven and the candle-smoke and the careful, patient application of a tea wash, and Marisol—who turned out to have the steadiest hands in the room, hands that had spent thirty years reading tides—produced by the second hour a requisition slip so convincingly old that August held it up and felt a cold prickle of professional admiration.

"That," she said, "would fool me."

"Would it fool Corvane?"

"No," August admitted. "But Corvane isn't going to see it. That's the point of where we're putting things."

To Tam she taught the hardest lesson, which was handwriting under fear. She had Wren stand behind him and breathe on his neck while he copied entries, and when his letters shook she made him start over, and start over, and start over, until the boy was crying with frustration and his script had gone flat and calm, because terror, repeated enough times, becomes technique.

"You'll shake when it matters," she told him. "Everyone does. So we practice shaking until shaking looks like your normal hand."

"And after?" Tam asked. His eyes were red. "After the audit. After all this. What am I, then? What do you call someone who's good at this?"

August didn't have an answer that wasn't a lie, and rule three forbade lying in this room.

"A survivor," Sefa said instead, from across the table. "You call them a survivor. Sit down, Tam."

Anselm she saved for last, because Anselm frightened her a little. The defrocked priest took to forgery the way some men take to drink—with relief, with gratitude, with the terrible ease of a man discovering a talent he'd always suspected he had. Within an hour he could produce a guild seal impression in wax that would pass at arm's length. Within two, he'd improved it.

"The guild's seal presses unevenly on the lower left," he said, holding his attempt up to the candlelight. "Has for years. There's a flaw in the die. Whoever maintains it keeps meaning to fix it and never does. Institutional neglect leaves fingerprints, Miss Okafor. I spent twenty years forging the voice of a god. A wax stamp is restful."

"Is that supposed to reassure me?"

"No," said Anselm pleasantly. "It's supposed to frighten you. Frightened people check their work."

* * *

Near midnight, Wren called a halt and put food on the table—bread, hard cheese, pickled onions, the wine finally poured—and the debtor-choir ate like conspirators, which is to say quickly, guiltily, and with enormous appetite.

August sat apart with her cup and watched them and felt the thing she'd been refusing to feel all evening finally arrive and sit down beside her like an uninvited guest.

She was corrupting them.

There was no gentler word for it. Six days ago, every person at this table would have gone to the Concord, told the truth, sworn oaths, and been destroyed—politely, procedurally, with full honors. She had taken honest people and taught them to age paper and strangle their letters and press flawed seals, and she'd done it in the language of care, dressed it in the grammar of protection, and the worst part—the part that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone—was that she *meant* it. This was love. She was almost certain this was love. Her grandmother had loved her exactly like this: teaching her to hide her own name in the margins of her own life, frame by frame, margin by margin, until hiding was the only fluency she had.

And look how that had ended. Look at what her grandmother's signature had bought, and what it had cost, and what was sleeping under the city wearing the debt like a shroud.

Sefa dropped into the chair beside her. Up close, in the candlelight, August could see how tired she was—the priest's insomnia, the kind that prayer used to treat and now nothing did.

"You're doing it again," Sefa said.

"Doing what."

"The face. The one where you're counting everyone in the room and putting prices next to their names." Sefa took a pickle off August's plate without asking. "I know that face. My abbot wore it every budget season."

"I'm not—" August stopped. Rule three. She made herself say it plain. "I'm wondering when this stops being protection and starts being the crime itself. I'm wondering if I'm keeping them safe or just making them complicit so I'm not alone in it."

"Both," Sefa said. "Obviously both. Welcome to priesthood; the pay is terrible but the self-knowledge is free." She chewed thoughtfully. "Here's what I know about corruption, August, and I know it professionally. Nobody falls. People lean. You lean a little, and the ground turns out to be sloped, and you lean a little more, and one day you look up and the sky is in the wrong place and you can't remember choosing any of it." She pointed her fork at the tables, at Ovid carefully strangling his letters even now, practicing between bites, at Tam asleep with his cheek on a finished forgery, at Marisol and Anselm arguing softly about wax temperature. "You've got us leaning. That's real. But you keep stopping at the edge of the slope and telling us where it goes. You keep saying the ugly part out loud. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, maybe."

"For how long?"

"For as long as you keep doing it." Sefa smiled, tired and crooked. "The day you stop telling us the ugly part—when protecting us starts feeling cleaner than warning us—that's the day you've become Corvane. And I'll tell you, because I've met him twice and I've met a hundred of him: he tells himself he's protecting the guild. Same words. Same warmth. Same slope."

August looked across the room at the choir—at her strange, borrowed, accidental family of debtors and failures and one very old lamplighter—and made herself say the ugliest part out loud, right now, immediately, while it was still true that she could.

"If the vote goes wrong," she said, "if the guild decides to give me to the emissary—you need to let them. All of you. You burn nothing, you forge nothing, you risk nothing for me. You survive. That's the whole assignment. Survive, and remember what happened here, and write it down somewhere safe in your own boring, unbreakable hands."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment.

"No," she said.

"It wasn't a request."

"I know. No." She refilled August's cup. "Drink your wine. Tomorrow we teach Wren the oven trick, and then I'm going to show you something I found in the cathedral district that you're going to wish you hadn't seen."

"What is it?"

"Rule three," said Sefa, and went to bed.

* * *

August stayed late. She always stayed late now; sleep had become a country she visited briefly and without enthusiasm.

She sat with the master copy of the forged trail they were building—the false history that would show the sealed account's page having been misfiled decades ago, lost in a clerical error so mundane it insulted the imagination—and checked it line by line against the real ledgers in her memory. Dates. Hands. Ink weights. The little noosed *r*s. It was good work. It was the best work of her life, and it was all lies, and she had made it with her own hands and the hands of people who trusted her, and somewhere beneath the city an ancient serpent was starving on the truth of it.

Her pen hovered over the final entry. Then, in the margin—in the narrow white space where no auditor ever looked, where names went to be forgotten—she wrote, small and quick, before she could stop herself:

*Ovid. Wren. Anselm. Marisol. Tam. Sefa.*

Not debts. Not accounts. Just names, listed together, the way you'd list the contents of something precious.

Then she blotted the page, sanded it, closed the book, and sat in the dark listening to the building settle, and did not hear the soft tread on the stairs outside the Lamplighter's Rest, and did not see the shadow pause at the window, and did not know that in the morning, Master Corvane would ask her to come to his office, and close the door, and look at her with those kind, terrible eyes, and say:

*"Tell me about the missing page, August."*
