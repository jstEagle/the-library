## 19. Corvane Knew

"Tell me about the missing page, August."

Corvane's office smelled of tallow and old varnish, the way it always did, the way it had for eleven years of her life. The candle between them burned straight up, no draft to trouble it, because Corvane kept his windows sealed against the city the way he kept his accounts sealed against the world.

August stood in front of his desk with her hands folded behind her back so he couldn't see them shake.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

It was a lie. It was the second lie of her entire life—the first had been *I don't know what you mean* to Sefa, three nights ago, about the founding contract hidden in the lining of her satchel—and it came out smoother than she'd expected, which frightened her more than being caught would have.

Corvane didn't move. He was a thin man built like a ledger line, all straight rules and small figures, with grey in his beard that he refused to dye because, he liked to say, *a man who lies about his age will lie about anything.* His eyes were kind. They were always kind. August had spent years telling herself that kindness was the same as goodness, and now, standing on the far side of that desk, she understood for the first time that kindness was just a technique—something practiced, something deployed, something a man could aim at you the way an archer aims at a target he intends to hit.

"The Ember Audit is in nine days," Corvane said. "The Concord of Faiths sends its auditors. Every account must reconcile. Every entry must be present." He turned a sheet of paper toward her—she recognized the vault inventory, her own neat column of figures, the ones she'd copied out before returning everything but one page to its shelf. "Page forty-one of the founding folio is missing. The wax seal was broken and replaced. Replaced well—I'll grant whoever did it that much. But I have been replacing that seal myself for thirty years, August, and I know what my own work looks like when someone improves upon it."

Her heart was doing arithmetic without her permission. *Nine days. One witness. How many ways out of this room.*

"You think I took it," she said.

"I think," Corvane said, "that you are the only member of this guild who has entered the vault alone since the spring inventory. I think you are the only person here whose hand matches the marginalia on the ledger that has been writing entries nobody made." He said it flatly, without accusation, the way he read off interest rates. "And I think you lied to me just now, which interests me far more than the page."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no wind.

"Why does my lying interest you?" August asked.

"Because until this month, you never had." He rose from his chair—a slow motion, joints cracking like a book opening—and went to the window, though the shutters were closed and there was nothing to see. "Do you know why I hired you, August?"

"Because I balanced the fishmonger's guild ledger at fourteen. Because I caught the ferry-master skimming. Because—"

"Because your grandmother was Mirabel Okafor."

The room went very quiet. Not silent—the candle still burned, the building still settled, somewhere below a cart rattled over cobbles—but quiet in the way a held breath is quiet. August felt the floor tilt under her, gently, the way the world tilts when you realize the ground was never where you thought.

"My grandmother," she said carefully, "was a laundress in Salt Row."

"Your grandmother was a laundress in Salt Row," Corvane agreed, "because the Guild of Impossible Things paid her to be one. Because we moved her out of this building forty-one years ago, gave her a new name in the parish rolls, and watched her marry a cooper and raise children who never knew what their blood had signed away." He turned back to face her. "We paid her pension every month until she died. Did you ever wonder how your mother's family stayed solvent through two bad harvests? There's a line item, if you know where to look. *Household maintenance, legacy obligations.* You've passed it ten thousand times. You never once asked whose household we were maintaining."

August's mouth was dry. "You're lying."

"I never lie about numbers." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd ever seen him do, because it was gentle. "You taught me that, actually. Or rather—you inherited it. She had it too. Mirabel could look at any figure and know instantly whether it wanted to be true. That's not arithmetic, August. That's a gift. And gifts run in bloodlines."

*Bloodline.* The word landed like a coin dropped down a well—she heard it fall and fall and heard no bottom.

"So when a junior keeper applied to this guild with perfect references and a talent that shouldn't have existed outside our own founding families," Corvane continued, settling back into his chair, "I checked. Of course I checked. I check everything. And I found her granddaughter applying to audit the oldest debt the guild holds." He laced his fingers together on the desk. "You weren't hired despite your name, August. You were hired *for* it. You are the only living guarantor of the Vaskarath account. You have been walking around this guild for eleven years as the most valuable asset on our books, and you didn't know it, because I made sure the books said nothing."

She should have run. Some part of her—the part that had already hidden a page inside her satchel lining, the part that had learned to forge seals in a single night—knew exactly where the exits were and how long it would take Corvane to reach the bell that summoned the guild wardens. But another part of her, the older part, the lonely part, the girl who had hidden her own name in the margins of ledgers because margins were the only place anyone looked—
that part simply stood there and let the sentence finish falling.

"Inventory," August said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, in the way you're proud of a scar. "That's what I was. Not a keeper. An asset."

"Don't—" For one flickering moment something crossed Corvane's face that wasn't technique. Something tired. "Don't make it smaller than it was. I fed you. I taught you. I promoted you past four keepers with seniority because I wanted your hands near that vault. None of that was fake."

"It was all fake," August said. "It was *maintenance of a legacy obligation.* I'm a line item, Master Corvane. You taught me to read line items. Don't insult both of us by pretending I misread this one."

He looked at her for a long time. Outside, the city went on being a city—bells, gulls, someone shouting about the price of lamp oil—and inside the sealed office the candle burned on, patient as a creditor.

"The missing page," Corvane said at last. "The founding contract. Tell me you haven't shown it to anyone."

"And if I have?"

"Then in nine days the Concord of Faiths will read it aloud, learn that this guild bought its charter with stolen dragon-fire, and burn us to the ground—charter, members, and guarantor alike. And when they come for the guarantor, August, they will come for *you*, and there will be no guild left to protect you, because protecting assets is the only thing this guild has ever been good at." He leaned forward. "Give me the page. Let me handle the Concord. I have handled Concord auditors for thirty years; they are men and women who can be reconciled, like any other account. What you cannot survive is a war between gods and dragons fought over the top of your head."

"You mean," August said, "*you* cannot survive it."

"I mean the guild."

"The guild isn't people, Corvane. You taught me that too, though you didn't know you were teaching it. A guild is a set of obligations with a roof on. People are just—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the crack, hated that he saw it— "people are just collateral."

Something in the old man flinched. She catalogued the flinch the way she catalogued everything, automatically, professionally, and filed it under a heading she'd never used before: *things that cost him something.*

"Where is the page?" he asked quietly.

"You know I won't tell you."

"I know you won't. That's the problem." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment he looked less like a master of the guild and more like what he actually was: an old man in a room full of paper, keeping other people's secrets until the secrets killed him. "Eleven years, August. Eleven years of watching you balance impossible columns and thinking, *she doesn't know, thank every dead god, she doesn't know.* I told myself it was mercy. Keeping you ignorant. Letting you be a person instead of a guarantee." He dropped his hand. "And then you found it anyway, because of course you did, because you're hers, and now the mercy is over and I have to decide what kind of man I am with nine days left on the clock."

"What kind are you?"

"That depends entirely," Corvane said, "on whether the Concord asks me questions before or after the gods do."

August frowned. "What do the gods have to do with—"

Three knocks at the door. Precise. Spaced evenly, like entries in a ledger.

Corvane's head came up sharply. Nobody knocked like that. Nobody in the guild, nobody in the city, nobody—

"Master Corvane," said a voice through the oak, and it was a beautiful voice, resonant and warm, the kind of voice that belonged to a temple cantor or a beloved teacher, and it was coming from nowhere, because when Sefa wrenched the door open a heartbeat later there was no one in the corridor at all, only lamplight and dust motes and cold air moving against the warmth like a tide going out.

But the voice finished its sentence anyway, from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls and the floorboards and the space behind August's teeth:

*"The Concord of Faiths acknowledges receipt. The Concord of Faiths notes the guarantor is located. The Concord of Faiths will send a delegation tomorrow at dawn to discuss settlement of the fire-debt. Attendance is mandatory. Survival is negotiable."*

Then silence, real silence, the kind that has weight.

Sefa stood in the doorway, grey-faced, holding a candle stub that had gone out without her noticing. Behind her, in the stairwell, August could hear footsteps—Ovid, Wren, Marisol, drawn by the voice or by instinct, debtors drifting toward trouble the way water drifts downhill.

"Settlement," Corvane repeated. All the technique had drained out of him. What remained was an accountant hearing that the bank itself was insolvent. "They know. The gods *know* where she is. Which means the Concord knows, which means—"

"Which means," August said, and was surprised to find her hands had stopped shaking, surprised to find her voice steady and cold and clear as a balanced column, "someone finally ran the numbers honestly. Nine days just became one night, Master Corvane. So I'll ask you the question you've been avoiding all evening." She picked up her satchel—the satchel with the founding contract sewn into its lining—and slung it across her shoulder.

"When the delegation arrives at dawn and demands the guarantor," August said, "what will you tell them yes to?"

* * *

The choir convened within the hour in the Lamplighter's Rest, all six of them crammed around a table meant for four, while rain that Sefa had prayed for and immediately regretted ticked against the shutters like fingers counting.

"They'll trade you," Ovid said bluntly. He was a bricklayer who owed the guild an impossible bridge, and he had no patience for anything softer than brick. "One vote, maybe two against, the rest fold the moment the Concord mentions excommunication. We've seen the math, August. You've seen the math."

"Then we change the math," August said.

"How?" asked Tam, who was fifteen and owed an impossible apology.

August opened her satchel and drew out the founding contract, and laid it on the table among the cups and the candle stubs and the crumbs, and watched six faces change as they understood what they were looking at.

"We stop hiding it," she said, "and we start using it."
