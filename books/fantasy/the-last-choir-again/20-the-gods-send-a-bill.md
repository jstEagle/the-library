## 20. The Gods Send a Bill

The emissary arrived the way a bill arrives: without apology, without warmth, and with the assumption that you would pay.

August was in the ledger hall, third shelf of the sealed accounts, when the bells began. Not the guild's bells—the city's. All of them. Every tower, every chapel, every cracked little bell in the beggars' quarter that hadn't rung in years because no one had paid the ringer. They all went at once, and the sound came down through the roof slates like a held breath finally released.

She knew, before anyone told her. Her hands knew. They went still on the shelf's edge the way they went still when an entry refused to balance.

Sefa found her first, out of breath, her defrocked priest's collar gone gray at the seams. "It's in the square," she said. "It walked up the Processional Road and the crowd parted like it had a writ."

"What is it?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Sefa's laugh was thin. "It looks like a man. It isn't. I've stood in a hundred congregations and I know what wears a person like a coat."

Corvane was already in the square by the time they got there, which meant Corvane had known it was coming, which meant August added that to the growing ledger of things she would confront him about and could not yet afford to.

The emissary stood at the center of the Guild of Impossible Things' own square, on the mosaic of the first charter, and it was wearing Master Aldous Vey—no. It was wearing a man shaped like Master Vey, if Vey had been rendered by someone who had only ever had him described. The proportions were right. The gray beard was right. The guild seal on the lapel was right, except it was mirrored, the way a reflection is right.

The eyes were the problem. They were the color of banked coals, and they did not blink, and when they swept across the crowd, August felt them pause on her for exactly one heartbeat longer than on anyone else. A collector finding the account.

Corvane stood before it with his hands folded, and his voice, when he spoke, was the voice he used for difficult clients: warm, level, entirely without leverage. "The Guild of Impossible Things welcomes the Concord of Faiths—"

"I am not the Concord." The voice was Vey's voice pitched down into something geological. "The Concord sends auditors. I am sent by the parties to whom your guild owes a debt of fire. I am the collection."

The crowd made a sound like a wave going out over gravel. August watched the guild's nine members—six, really, ever since the vote that wasn't—arrange themselves at the edges of the square the way senior clerks arrange themselves when the news is bad and they want to be seen having heard it first.

"The guild acknowledges no such debt," Corvane said.

The emissary smiled. It was a terrible smile, not because it was cruel but because it was patient. It smiled the way a ledger balances.

"Of course not. That is what the sealed account is for."

*

They were given one hour to convene. The emissary did not sit, did not eat, did not accept the water Corvane's clerk offered with the trembling formality of a man feeding a bear. It stood in the guild's receiving hall, on the good rug, and left coal-colored scorch prints on the wool where it stood, faint rings like the ghost of a cup.

August stood at the back of the council chamber with the door open, listening, holding a ledger she had no intention of opening. She had learned that trick in her first year: carry a book and no one asks you what you're thinking.

"The debt is real," Corvane was saying. He stood at the head of the long table, and August noted—he noted it the way she noted everything now, compulsively, the way a gambler notes cards—that he did not deny the debt's existence. He denied its *enforceability*. "Whatever the founding contract says, it was signed under duress, by parties no longer living, against an entity the guild has never acknowledged in any charter."

"The entity acknowledges itself," the emissary said. It had followed them into the chamber without being invited, which was apparently a thing gods' servants could do. "It has been very patient. Patience is not the same as absence. Your guild has been spending its interest for two hundred years and calling the principal a legend."

Master Hesk, whose family had held a seat since the second generation, leaned forward. "What do you want? In plain terms. We're merchants. Speak in terms."

The emissary turned its head. The motion was smooth as poured wax.

"In plain terms. The fire is nearly spent. The gods who feed on it are starving, and starving gods do one of two things: they die quietly, which they will not, or they come down and take what they are owed, which will end your city. We are here to prevent the second thing." It paused, letting the silence do arithmetic. "There is a provision in the founding contract. A guarantor clause. The debt was guaranteed not by the guild's treasury but by a bloodline. The blood of the signer, and the blood of the signer's line, held in trust."

August's heart did something complicated and quiet, like a clerk discovering a page torn out.

"The guarantor's line has thinned," the emissary continued. "It is, in fact, nearly a single thread. One person. Surrender the guarantor, and the debt is settled in full. Peacefully. No war. No burning. The gods receive their fire and depart your affairs forever."

"And who," said Hesk, with the flatness of a man asking a question he already dreaded the answer to, "is the guarantor?"

The emissary's coal eyes moved. Not to Corvane. Not to the senior members.

To the back of the room. To the girl with the ledger she wasn't reading.

"The junior ledger-keeper," it said. "August Okafor. Granddaughter of Mirabel Okafor, who signed."

*

The chamber did not erupt. That was the worst part. August had imagined, in the three heartbeats it took for the words to land, shouting, denial, someone leaping to their feet. Instead there was a silence of a very particular guild-shaped quality: the silence of nine people—six people—checking their own exposure.

She had hidden her name in the margins for years. She had hidden the founding contract in a false-bottom drawer. She had hidden the truth from Corvane, from Sefa, from herself, in that order. And now a thing wearing a dead man's reflection had walked into the square and read her name off the sky.

"August," Corvane said, and his voice was gentle, and she hated how gentle, because gentle was what he used when he wanted something. "Is this true?"

She looked at him. She thought about chapter and verse, about the contract in her satchel, about the vote she hadn't heard yet and could already count. She thought about lying, and found, to her surprise, that the muscle for it existed now. Corvane had built it in her, candle by candle.

"I don't know what it's talking about," she said.

The emissary laughed. It was a short sound, dry as ash, and it did something to the candle flames—all of them leaned toward it, like flowers toward a bad sun.

"You lie like a ledger-keeper," it said. "Accurately. *I don't know what it's talking about*—and every word true, because you know exactly what it's talking about." It turned back to the table. "The offer stands until moonrise tomorrow. Surrender the guarantor, and the debt is closed. Refuse, and we proceed to collection. You have all read the founding contract. You know what collection means."

"We have not all read it," said Master Ivo, the abstainer, the one who had kept his hands down in every vote. "That's rather the point of a sealed account."

"Then I suggest you unseal it." The emissary moved toward the door, and the scorch rings followed it across the rug like a signature being written. At the threshold it paused, and its coal eyes found August again, and this time it addressed her directly, and the whole chamber turned to look where it looked.

"A message from my principal, for the guarantor. Vaskarath says: *They will vote. They always vote. Do not wait to learn the number.*"

And then it was gone—not out the door but out of the world, the way a debt leaves your mind the moment you pay it, and the candles stood back up, and the bells had stopped, and the rug had nine perfect rings of scorch that no clerk would ever quite scrub out.

*

Sefa caught up with her in the stairwell between the second and third floors, the narrow one with the rope handrail that smelled of tallow and old paper.

"August. August, stop."

She stopped. She didn't turn around. The stairwell was dark enough that she could let her face do whatever it wanted, which was apparently nothing, which was apparently the whole problem.

"You heard what it said," Sefa said. "They're going to vote on you. Tomorrow. Moonrise."

"I heard."

"Then why are you standing on a staircase like it's a normal Tuesday?"

August turned. In the dark she could just see Sefa's face, and the gray collar, and the place where her brother's voice had come out of the sky three nights ago and nearly taken her apart. "Because if I keep moving, I'll start running, and if I start running, I'll never stop, and I have one day, Sefa. One day to do arithmetic."

"Arithmetic."

"They said the guarantor's line. Singular. One thread." August heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used for balancing, the voice that had never once saved her from anything. "They think I'm the whole debt. But I've read the contract, Sefa. I've read it more times than I want to admit. And there's a clause they didn't mention, because clauses are how gods lie too."

Sefa leaned against the wall. "Tell me."

"The guarantee isn't my blood. It's my blood *freely given*. That's the word in the original. Freely. Which means they can't take me. They can only be *given* me. Which means the vote tomorrow isn't about whether they can have me." She felt something in her chest that wasn't fear, or wasn't only fear—something with edges, something like a blade being turned over in the hand. "It's about whether the people I've worked beside for four years will sell me. And whether, if they do, I go quietly."

"And if they don't?"

"Then the gods come down and take the city, and it's my fault for having a grandmother with a signature." August started up the stairs. "So I'm not going to wait for their vote. I'm going to give them a different bill to argue about."

*

She spent the evening in the ledger hall with the doors barred, which no one questioned, because August Okafor barring doors before an audit was as natural as rain being wet. She took out the founding contract and read the guarantor clause until the words blurred: *the blood of the signer and her line, held in trust, freely given, against the debt of fire.*

Freely given.

She took out a fresh page and began, in her smallest hand, the only kind of arithmetic that had ever frightened her: not what she owed, but what she was worth.

To the guild: a junior ledger-keeper's wage, twelve years of perfect entries, one forged page, one stolen contract. Replaceable. She wrote it down and the number was insultingly small.

To the gods: a bloodline's worth of dragon-fire. Enough, by her calculations from the vault accounts, to feed a starving pantheon for—she did the division twice, three times, hands steady, breath not—eleven years. Maybe twelve. She wrote *11* and looked at it. Eleven years bought by one person's everything, and then the hunger would begin again, because that was the nature of the arrangement: it didn't solve the debt, it just moved it onto a smaller ledger. Hers.

To Vaskarath: the granddaughter of the woman who signed him into a cage. She thought of the exhausted ancient under the city, the voice like a river remembering it had once been a glacier. *They will vote. They always vote. Do not wait to learn the number.*

"No," she said aloud to the empty hall. "I won't."

She heard the footsteps on the stair before the knock—two knocks, Corvane's knock, the one that meant *I know you're in there and I'm being polite about it.*

"August. The council convenes at dawn instead of moonrise. I moved it up."

Of course he had. A vote held at moonrise gave her a day. A vote at dawn gave her hours. She closed the contract, slid it into the false bottom, and said, in a voice she barely recognized as her own, "I'll be there."

A pause. Then, gently, terribly: "August. Whatever you're planning—there is a version of this where everyone survives. I've spent thirty years finding it. Let me help you find it."

She looked at the door. She thought about the word *inventory*. She thought about the choir of debtors in the low quarter, the ones who owed one impossible thing each, the ones who had never once voted on whether she was worth keeping.

"Master Corvane," she said, "when you find it, send it to the undercity. That's where I'll be."

She heard his footsteps retreat. She blew out the candle, took the contract, and went down the back stairs into the dark, while above her the guild slept the sleep of people who believed the morning would bring a vote instead of a fire.
