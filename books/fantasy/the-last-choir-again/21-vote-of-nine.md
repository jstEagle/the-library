## 21. Vote of Nine

She should not have gone back.

August knew this the way she knew the sum of any column: instantly, certainly, without needing to check the work. The smart move—the arithmetically correct move—was to stay in the undercity with Sefa and the debtors, to let the guild do what institutions do, which was grind forward regardless of who was crushed beneath it. Her worth had already been calculated. She'd seen the number in Corvane's eyes when he said *inventory*. There was nothing left to learn from watching it counted twice.

She went anyway.

Because there was a difference, she told herself, between being sold and knowing you were sold. Because a ledger-keeper understood that an unverified entry was worth nothing—that a crime recorded only in your own hand could always be dismissed as forgery, as hysteria, as the scribbling of a junior clerk who'd gotten above her station. She needed witnesses. She needed the vote itself, entered into the official record in the official ink by the official hands.

Or—and this was the truer reason, and she let herself hold it for exactly four seconds before putting it away—because nine people were going to decide whether she lived, and some small stubborn animal part of her needed to look each of them in the face while they did it.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things convened at dawn on the first day of the Ember Audit, in the Long Hall where the ceiling was painted with the nine founding trades and the floor was worn into a map of every debt the guild had ever forgiven. August knew the hall better than anyone alive. She knew that the third balcony stair creaked on its fourth step and that the acoustic shadow behind the eastern pillar swallowed sound whole—a flaw in the architect's arithmetic that she had discovered at sixteen, alone, during her first audit season, and had never mentioned to anyone because mentioning it would have meant explaining why she'd been hiding there.

Some habits kept you alive.

She climbed the balcony stairs in the grey hour before bells, took her position in the acoustic shadow with the founding contract pressed flat against her ribs beneath her coat, and waited for the guild to assemble below her like figures filling in a column.

Master Corvane arrived first. He came early even for him, before the light had properly committed, and stood at the head of the table with his hands flat on the wood, looking at the empty chairs the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself. He wore his full regalia—the chain of office, the silver quill at his collar—and August thought: *he dressed for this*. He had dressed for it the way you dress for a funeral you have personally arranged.

Then the others.

Master Ilenne of the Reconciliation Office, who had taught August double-entry and called her "the girl with the steady hand" exactly once, in a tone August had treasured for six years. Master Duvall, whose gout made him late to everything except, apparently, this. The twins Ferro and Ferro—who were not actually twins but had been hired the same week and had decided the joke was load-bearing. Old Mistress Anthea, ninety-one, who signed her name with a stamp because her hands shook too badly to trust a pen, and who had once told August over mulled wine that the guild's books were honest even when the guild was not, and that this distinction was the entire job.

And the last two, arriving together, heads bent together: Master Quillon of External Accounts and Master Yeva of the Sealed Vault—which meant Yeva already knew what August knew, or suspected it, or had been ordered by Corvane to suspect it.

Six attendees. Three seats empty. Nine members on the charter, and the charter required a majority of those present, which meant five hands could sell her, and everyone in the room could do that arithmetic, and everyone in the room did it in silence while the servants lit the lamps.

August watched from above, in the dark, with her heart going like a courier's boots.

* * *

Corvane opened the session the way he opened everything: with the numbers.

"The Ember Audit convenes tomorrow," he said. "The Concord of Faiths arrives with eleven auditors and two divine legates. They will demand production of every account, including—" and here his voice did something careful, a man stepping over ice— "including the sealed founding account. You have all been briefed on what that account contains. Some of you have known for years. One of you learned of it recently."

He did not look up at the balcony. August admired that. It was either discipline or certainty, and she couldn't tell which, and both frightened her.

"You will also have received the visitation," Corvane continued. "Yesterday evening. The emissary of the Concord."

"I received it," Master Quillon said. His voice had the particular smoothness of a man who has rehearsed. "As did we all. A god's own herald, standing in our courtyard, asking us to surrender one junior ledger-keeper in exchange for peace." He spread his hands. "I confess I spent the night trying to find the flaw in the offer. There isn't one. We give them the guarantor's bloodline, the fire-debt resolves, the gods stand down, the audit dissolves, and the guild survives another thousand years. Against that, we weigh—" he consulted a card he did not need to consult— "one employee. Six years of service. No family. No faction. No vote bloc within these walls."

"No vote bloc," August repeated silently, from the dark. *You'd be surprised.*

"She is in the room," said Master Yeva.

That got everyone's attention. Even Corvane's head came up.

Yeva of the Sealed Vault was a narrow woman with ink-stained knuckles and the patient expression of someone who had spent thirty years guarding documents nobody was allowed to read and had formed opinions about all of them. She turned slowly in her chair until she was facing the eastern pillar, the acoustic shadow, the exact patch of darkness where August stood.

"She's been up there since before I arrived," Yeva said. "Fourth stair creaks. Always has." She turned back to the table. "If we're going to count her, Masters, we should do her the courtesy of letting her hear it to her face."

August's first instinct was to run. Her second was more interesting, and more damning: she stayed. Because Yeva hadn't said *remove her*. Yeva had said *let her hear it*. Whatever that was—it wasn't cruelty, or it wasn't only cruelty—and August found she needed to know which.

She stepped out of the acoustic shadow to the balcony rail, where the lamplight could find her, and looked down at six faces turning upward.

"Good morning, Masters," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'll take notes, if it helps. I'm told I have a steady hand."

* * *

What followed was the most obscene hour of her life, and August Okafor had grown up reading the guild's charity ledgers, so she knew obscenity when she saw it conducted in proper format.

They debated her. That was the only word for it, and even that word flattered the thing, because debates have sides and this had only weights. They spoke of her in the third person, in the passive voice, in the language of exposure and precedent and fiduciary obligation, and every word of it was technically true, and the truth of it was the worst part. She was a liability on the books. She *was* the guarantor's bloodline. Her existence *did* place the entire charter at risk. A ledger-keeper could not argue with the entries; she could only argue with the interpretation, and they had closed the interpretation before she reached the stairs.

Duvall spoke of the guild's obligations to its pensioners. Ilenne spoke—Ilena, who taught her double-entry—of systemic risk, and used the phrase "contained loss" twice, and did not look at the balcony either time. The Ferros spoke of the Concord's armies, of what happened in Vell to the charter-house that refused a divine demand, of the children in the guild's foundling school. Everything they said was a real number. That was the trap of it. Cruelty done properly is never made of lies. It is made of accurate figures summed in the wrong direction.

Only three voices broke pattern.

Old Mistress Anthea spoke fourth-to-last, and she did not rise—she was past rising—but she turned her stamped signature toward the table like a weapon laid flat.

"I've buried four masters of this guild," she said. "I signed the condolence accounts myself, all four times, because my hands were the steadiest. And I will tell you what none of their eulogies said, which is that every one of them died owing the guild less than the guild owed them." She fixed the room with eyes gone pale as old paper. "This guild exists because a woman named Zhou signed away her bloodline to buy our survival. Now we propose to complete the transaction. To pay out the final clause. To deliver the last of her blood to the fire because the alternative is *inconvenience*." She tapped the table once, a dry knock like a closing door. "I have read the founding contract. I read it in the year of the Ash Flood, when Corvane thought I was too old to matter. There is a clause none of you have quoted today. Ask him why."

August went cold on the balcony. Clause fourteen. *The guarantor may not be surrendered while any voluntary alternative remains unexhausted.* Anthea knew. Anthea had known for decades and had stamped her name under it in silence.

Corvane's jaw tightened, and he moved the session along with the skill of a man who had spent his life moving sessions along.

Sefa wasn't there—of course she wasn't, Sefa had never held a chair—but August felt her absence like a missing column, and then, strangely, felt her presence, because when the vote came August heard in her memory the debtor-priest's voice saying *gods keep receipts, August, and so should we*, and it steadied her hands on the rail.

Master Yeva voted third from last. She stood to do it, which no one else had done.

"I keep the sealed vault," she said. "For thirty years I have guarded a document I was forbidden to read, on the theory that ignorance was protection. Last night I read it." She looked around the table. "We are not voting on whether to sacrifice an employee. We are voting on whether the founding crime worked. Whether you can steal fire from a living creature, sign the theft in someone's blood, and then finish the theft by delivering the rest of that blood when the bill comes due. If we say yes, we are not survivors, Masters. We are the second half of the original sin." She lifted her hand. "No. And I note for the record that the emissary's terms specified surrender of 'the guarantor' — and that clause fourteen defines the guarantor as the bloodline entire, not one woman. Handing them August settles nothing. Read your contracts. Vote."

Three hands rose against. Anthea. Yeva. And Master Ilenne—last, slowest, her face gray, her mouth a hard line, and when she caught August's eye across the hall she did not soften it, did not apologize with her expression, simply nodded once like a woman signing her name to something she could never unsign.

Four hands rose for. Duvall. Both Ferros. Quillon.

Two abstained. Corvane—and August watched his hand hover, watched the man who had taught her that kindness was a management technique fail to commit it even now—and Ferro-the-second, who raised his hand halfway and then, visibly, could not finish either motion, and put it down, and stared at the table.

"Four for," said the recorder, whose pen had gone very dry-sounding in the quiet. "Three against. Two abstentions. Abstentions count as—" he glanced at Corvane— "as non-votes. Motion carries on votes cast. Five to three."

*Motion carries.* August heard the phrase land in her chest like a coin dropped down a well. Motion carries. The same phrase that approved new roofs and pension increases and the annual festival candles. Her death, filed under ordinary procedure.

Corvane rose. When he spoke, his voice was the warm one, the mentor one, the one that had once made her believe she belonged somewhere.

"The motion carries," he said. "The guild will notify the Concord's emissary at noon. Miss Okafor—if you are still present, and I believe you are—" he looked up at the balcony at last, and his eyes found hers, and there was something in them that was almost grief, or was wearing grief's clothing very convincingly— "you have until noon. I am instructed by the motion to say that resistance will be treated as breach of charter. I am permitted, as Master, to add privately that the side door by the scriptorium is unlocked, and that the watch rotation changes at the ninth bell, and that I have never once in twenty years failed to notice a discrepancy in my own accounts."

He sat down. Around him the guild began gathering its papers, its pens, its dignity, the machinery of a body that had just voted and now wished to be elsewhere. Anthea was the last to rise. She paused at the door and looked back up at the balcony, and mouthed something August couldn't parse at distance—*clause fourteen*, maybe, or *run, girl*—and then the hall emptied, and the lamps burned down, and August stood alone above the long table where her worth had been counted in raised hands and found to be four.

* * *

She did not cry. Crying was a private expense, and she had learned young to keep private expenses off the books.

Instead she did what she always did with unbearable totals: she broke them into parts. Four yes. Three no. Two abstentions—one of them a coward's, one of them, possibly, Corvane's terrible sideways mercy. Five votes cast against her life; three cast for it. In any other ledger, three out of eight would be a minority position. Here it was the entire population of people willing to raise a hand against the machine that fed them, and she wrote the number down in her mind the way she wrote everything down, because numbers kept, and people didn't, and someday—she did not yet let herself name the day—someday those three names would go in a ledger of a different kind entirely.

Anthea, who buried the dead and kept their true accounts.

Yeva, who guarded the vault and finally read it.

Ilenne, who taught her double-entry and had just taught her the third kind: the entry where you debit yourself to balance someone else's survival.

August came down the balcony stairs—avoiding the fourth step, out of habit, out of spite—and walked the length of the hall past the empty chairs. At Corvane's seat she stopped. His copy of the motion lay face-up, the recorder's notation still drying: *Motion carries, 5–3, abstentions noted.*

Beneath it, in Corvane's precise hand, a marginal figure she recognized because she had invented it: a small calculation of the ninth-bell watch change, and beside it, the words *side door, scriptorium,* and after that, struck through but still legible, three letters that had started life as *inv* and been crossed out and rewritten as *Aug*.

He had corrected himself. Too late to mean anything. But he had corrected himself.

August picked up the page, folded it into her coat next to the founding contract, and walked out through the side door by the scriptorium, which was, as promised, unlocked.

Outside, the bells began ringing the hour—two hours early, wrong, ragged, the alarm pattern rather than the time—and down the avenue she saw them coming through the morning mist: the Concord's auditors, eleven strong, in their gray-and-gold, walking with the unhurried confidence of creditors who had heard the vote results before the debtor had.

The audit hadn't been scheduled for tomorrow.

It had been moved up. Someone had sold the schedule along with everything else.

August pulled her collar up, turned south toward the undercity, and ran.
