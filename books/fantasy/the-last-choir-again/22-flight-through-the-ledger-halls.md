## 22. Flight Through the Ledger Halls

The vote was still ringing in her ears when August started running.

Three noes. Four yeses. Two abstentions. She had counted them the way she counted everything, because counting was the only prayer she'd ever been taught, and now the numbers had a taste: copper and smoke. *Four hands raised to hand you over.* Four people she had passed in corridors for three years, four people whose tea she had poured, whose columns she had balanced without error, and every one of them had looked at her the way you look at a line item that has finally, mercifully, come due.

She didn't take the stairs. Stairs were where people waited.

Instead she went through the scriptorium, past the sleeping copy-clerk, and pulled the founding contract from its hiding place inside the false spine of *Principles of Reconciliation, Volume IX*—the one book in the guild nobody had ever opened. It was warm against her chest, warmer than paper had any right to be, as if Vaskarath's fire still lived in the ink of her grandmother's signature.

Then she did the thing she had rehearsed a hundred times in her head since the night she first read the sealed account, back when it was only a nightmare and not a plan.

She opened every ledger on the open shelves.

Not randomly. August Okafor did nothing randomly, even with her heart slamming against the contract like a fist against a door. She opened them to specific pages—the ones she had flagged months ago with the tiny marginal marks that meant *discrepancy, unresolved*. Let Corvane's allies spend their first frantic hour chasing errors that were real but harmless. Let them drown in honest arithmetic while she became an dishonest number herself, slipping out of the sum entirely.

Behind her, in the dark of the scriptorium, someone began to hum.

She froze, one hand still on a ledger. The humming resolved into words—an old debtor's hymn, the kind sung at collections, off-key and unbothered—and then Marisol Vey stepped out from between the shelving with a lamp in one hand and a look on her face that August had never seen there before. Marisol owed the guild an impossible thing; they all did, the choir of them, the debtors who had gathered in the lower hall like moths around a candle nobody would admit was lit. Marisol's impossible thing was a door that opened onto anywhere. She'd never been able to pay it. Tonight, apparently, she'd found another way.

"You're going for the east stairs," Marisol said.

"I'm not."

"Good. Because there are six of Corvane's people on the east stairs." She set the lamp down on the reading desk, deliberately, the way you set down a piece in a game. "I told them I saw you go west. Toward the record annex."

"I'm not going west either."

Marisol smiled, slow and crooked. "I know. That's what makes it such a good lie. Come on. The choir's been waiting all evening for something to do."

* * *

What the Guild of Impossible Things kept in its halls, more than gold or relics or contracts, was paper. Miles of it. Aisles of it. The Ledger Halls ran beneath the guild like veins under skin, and August knew them the way a thief knows a house—not because she'd ever intended to steal anything, but because competence is its own kind of trespass. She had memorized the fire codes. She knew which aisles were lined with flame-retardant slate and which were lined with vellum old enough to burn like a scream.

That distinction mattered very much in the next ten minutes.

Because at the mouth of the western corridor, exactly where the floor plan said the night-watch brazier stood, there was now a fire.

It was not a real fire. August saw that immediately—saw the absence of heat, the wrongness of the light, the way the flames licked upward without consuming so much as a dust mote. But it *looked* real, looked real enough to make the two watchmen shout and reach for sand-buckets, and behind it, crouched in the shadow of the brazier with his sleeves rolled up and ash already smudging his jaw, was Odji Tam, the debt-ridden lamplighter, grinning like a man watching his life's work come together at last.

His impossible thing, owed to the guild these eleven years, had been a fire that burned without fuel.

He couldn't do it. He'd said so himself, a hundred times, at every collection day. And yet here it was—a fire without fuel, small and bright and utterly fraudulent, and Odji feeding it scraps of treated paper from a pouch at his belt, paper he'd soaked in something alchemical and forbidden, paper he must have spent weeks preparing.

"You're paying your debt," August said, skidding to a stop.

"I'm *renegotiating*," Odji said. "There's a difference. Go. West annex is clear for another ninety seconds. Sefa's buying us more."

"Sefa's—"

But Marisol was already pulling her onward, through the smoke-smell and the shouting, and August ran.

* * *

Sefa had been a priest before she'd been a debtor, and it showed in the strangest ways. She prayed like other people filed reports: thoroughly, punctually, with footnotes. So when August burst out of the stairwell into the annex antechamber and found Sefa standing calmly before the guild's master roster—open on its stand, quill in her hand—August's first thought was that she'd been caught.

Her second thought was that Sefa was *writing in it*.

"What are you doing?"

"Lying," Sefa said pleasantly. "It turns out I have a talent. Who knew." She was adding entries to the duty roster—entries dated three days from now, in three different hands, recording the movements of three different couriers carrying three different sealed documents to three different districts of the city. Any auditor following the paper trail would find a thicket. "Corvane taught me the principle himself, actually, though he'd be furious to hear it. You don't hide a transaction. You bury it in season. Nobody questions paperwork that looks like the time of year it belongs to."

"That's—" August stared. "That's genuinely sophisticated forgery."

"I served a god of harvests for nineteen years, August. I know how things ripen." Sefa capped the ink, wiped her fingers on her cassock, and looked at her with those tired, kind eyes that had once belonged to a woman who believed the heavens were listening. "The east gate has a Concord man on it. Has since sundown. They came early—they came *early*, August, someone told them to expect this tonight, which means we have a problem bigger than the vote. But the coal chute in the north wall comes out behind the tannery, and tanneries don't ask questions."

"You've mapped my escape route."

"We've been mapping it since the vote was called. You think you're the only one who can run numbers?" Sefa took her by the shoulders, and her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "Listen to me. Whatever happens—whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten—the contract doesn't leave your body. Not into a bag. Not into someone else's hands. Your grandmother signed it with her bloodline. As far as the magic of the thing is concerned, *you are the signature*. If it leaves you, it stops being yours to burn. Do you understand?"

August understood. It was the same principle that governed every debt in the guild: obligation clung to flesh. She nodded.

"Good." Sefa's voice cracked, just slightly, just enough to show the seam. "Now run. And August—when you see him, tell the old serpent that some of us down here remember what his fire looked like before they put it in cages."

* * *

The coal chute was exactly as narrow and humiliating as advertised.

August descended feet-first in the dark, the contract pressed flat between her shirt and her sternum, breathing in tallow and tanning-lye and the mineral cold of old stone. Above her, muffled through layers of brick, she heard the guild's alarm bells begin—one bell, then a second, then the ragged syncopation of panic spreading through the halls like ink through water.

They'd found the fires. Plural.

She permitted herself, in the blackness, one moment of fierce and terrible pride. Odji's false blaze in the west. Marisol's misdirection toward the east stairs. And somewhere above—she heard it even now, a crackle and a glow leaking down through a ventilation shaft—a third fire, in the Hall of Records itself, where the guild kept nine hundred years of accounts receivable. That one she hadn't ordered. That one was Pellam Wray, the debt-choir's oldest member, a former guild archivist struck from the rolls for asking too many questions about the sealed vault, standing in front of the one archive he loved more than anything alive and setting it alight with his own hands, with real flame this time, because some messages could only be sent loudly.

*Burn the margins,* the choir had decided. *Burn everything except her.*

The chute spat her out behind the tannery, into an alley that smelled like a wound, and August lay in the filth for three full seconds doing what she did best: taking inventory. Contract: present, warm, hers. Body: bruised, functional. Pursuit: none visible. Assets: one debt-choir, scattered across the guild like lit fuses, each of them spending their one impossible thing not on themselves but on her.

Nobody pays a debt for someone else. It was practically the first law of accounting. It was certainly the first law of the guild.

And yet.

She got up and ran north, toward the undercity, toward the shrine built around a dying flame, toward the exhausted ancient serpent who had every right to hate her name—and behind her, above her, the Ledger Halls burned in three places and lied in forty, and every lie was signed with love.

* * *

She made six streets before the sky noticed her.

It happened the way weather happens—no warning, just a change in pressure. The lamps along Tanner's Row guttered. Her breath frosted though the night was mild. And then, drifting down through the air like snow that had forgotten how to fall, came ash.

Cold ash. The same gray flakes she and Sefa had traced across half the city, the residue of answered prayers, the exhaust of stolen dragon-fire. It settled on her shoulders and did not melt.

A voice spoke from everywhere, the way divine voices always do—beneath hearing rather than above it, felt in the teeth:

**GUARANTOR.**

August stopped running. There wasn't much point. When a god addresses you by your function, distance becomes a technicality.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said aloud, to the empty street, because gods appreciate protocol—it was one of the few useful things Sefa had taught her. "You know my lineage. I don't know your name."

**WE HAVE NOT YET DECIDED WHAT YOU ARE,** the voice said. **THAT IS THE DISADVANTAGE.**

"Catch up quickly, then, because the auditors will be here within the hour, and after them the Concord, and none of you coordinate well." She was talking to buy time, walking again as she talked, angling toward the storm-drain mouth two alleys down that Sefa had marked on the scrap of map tucked in her sleeve. "You want the guarantor. Fine. Here's what the guarantor wants: terms. Rations. A schedule. Fire given freely instead of taken by war. There's precedent—you'll find it if your clerks check the cathedral of Veluneth, the one who starved quietly rather than burn a city. He left records."

Silence. Long enough that she thought the connection had failed.

Then, softer, almost puzzled, almost human: **NO ONE HAS EVER SENT US AN INVOICE BEFORE.**

"No," August said. "Someone should have, generations ago. It was negligence. My family's, and the guild's, and frankly yours." She reached the drain mouth, hooked her fingers into the iron grate. "I'm going below now, to speak with your creditor. When you've read the precedent, send word. In writing. I keep accounts, and I'm told I'm good."

She pulled the grate aside and dropped into the dark before the sky could decide whether to be amused or offended.

* * *

The way down was long, and in the long places, the mind does arithmetic whether you want it to or not.

Four votes yes. Three noes. Two abstentions. Nine members, one chair, and August had sat outside that chamber door and listened to her own worth being calculated in raised hands, and the terrible thing—the thing she would carry longer than the bruises—was that the math had almost worked. Four to three. One more hand and she'd have been delivered to the emissary like a defaulted bond, and the guild would have called it survival, and the guild would have been right, by its own lights. That was the horror of Corvane's world: it was internally consistent. Every cruelty balanced.

Except it didn't. Not anymore. Because downstairs, in the halls where the numbers lived, nine debtors had just broken the first law of accounting. They had paid each other's debts. They had set their own impossible things on fire to buy her ninety seconds at a time.

You can't book that. There's no column for it. It doesn't balance, and it doesn't need to, and standing in the dripping dark with a burning contract against her heart, August Okafor understood that this was the entire discovery of her life: the ledgers were wrong about what a person is worth. They had always been wrong. Worth was not what someone would pay for you.

Worth was who came when the bells rang.

The tunnel opened. Warm air rose to meet her, smelling of sulfur and old sleep, and ahead, in the vast ribbed dark of the undercity shrine, a light was waiting—the last true fire in the city, guttering, patient, older than the guild, older than the gods' theft, older than her grandmother's signature.

Vaskarath's eye opened. It filled the cavern like a moon filling a window.

**LITTLE LEDGER,** the dragon said. His voice was gravel and embers and something underneath both that might have been relief. **YOU RUN FAST FOR SOMEONE CARRYING YOUR WHOLE FAMILY ON YOUR CHEST.**

"They voted to give me to the gods." August walked forward into the firelight, ash in her hair, soot to the elbow, the contract in her hands. "Four to three. I thought you'd want to know the current market price of an Okafor."

Something moved in the deep of the shrine—coils shifting, scales whispering against stone like pages turning. When Vaskarath spoke again, the amusement in his voice had edges on it.

**AND WHAT DID YOU DO WITH THEIR VERDICT?**

"Appealed." She held up the founding contract, and her grandmother's signature caught the dragon-light and blazed. "To a higher court. Yours."

For the first time in the whole long night—for the first time, perhaps, in two hundred years—the last dragon laughed, and the sound came up through the floor of the world like spring.

Then the laughter stopped, because far above them, faint but unmistakable, the shrine began to shake—and through the cracks in the ceiling sifted not cold ash, but light. Divine light, descending in columns, and with it a sound like a thousand voices clearing their throats at once.

The gods had read the precedent. And they had come down to negotiate—or to collect.

Vaskarath drew himself up against the dark, and every scale of him caught fire.

**STAY BEHIND ME, LITTLE LEDGER,** he rumbled. **THE AUDIT HAS ARRIVED EARLY.**
