## 23. What Sefa Chose

The safehouse was a defrocked counting-house three streets from the river, the kind of place the Guild had seized for unpaid debts so long ago that no one remembered who'd originally owed what. The shelves still bore the ghost-rings of a thousand ink pots. August sat on a desk that wasn't hers, in a building that wasn't hers, holding a founding contract she'd stolen from the only institution that had ever pretended to want her, and she thought: *this is what it feels like to be an entry someone else wrote.*

She had not slept. Neither had anyone.

The debtor-choir had scattered at dawn into defensible smallness — Marlow to his sister's tannery with a forged bill of sale, the twins Ivet and Rone down to the docks where names meant nothing, old Brother Cassian back to the temple steps to beg in earnest for the first time in thirty years, which he claimed was a kind of camouflage. That left August and Sefa and the contract, wrapped in oilcloth like a corpse, tucked behind a loose brick that had once hidden a moneylender's private vice.

Sefa was at the window. She had been at the window for an hour, doing nothing that could be called watching, because watching would have been useful and this was something else. She stood the way people stand at graves.

August noticed things like that. It was her trade. You couldn't keep ledgers without learning that people were entries too, and entries always told on themselves eventually — a smudged figure, a date in the wrong hand, a friend who wouldn't turn around.

"Sefa."

"Mm."

"Come away from the glass."

"I'm fine."

"You've said 'fine' four times since midnight. It's becoming a pattern, and patterns are how I catch people." August kept her voice light. Lightness was a tool; she'd learned that much from Corvane, if nothing else worth keeping. "Come sit. Eat something. We have half a loaf and a jar of pickled eels that I refuse to believe anyone paid money for."

Sefa turned then, and August saw her face properly for the first time since the flight from the Ledger Halls — really saw it, the way you see a page under candlelight after reading it all day by grey window-glow, and every error stands up and screams.

Sefa had been crying. Not recently. Recently she'd been dry-eyed and rigid, the way people are when the crying has stopped being a relief and become a symptom. And beneath that, worn smooth as an old coin, was guilt. August knew guilt's texture intimately. She'd spent two weeks carrying her own around like a stone sewn into her coat lining.

"Sefa," August said, and the lightness fell out of her voice entirely. "What did you do?"

* * *

Here is what Sefa did, and she told it standing, because sitting felt like permission, and she did not want permission. She wanted sentence.

"After we ran," she said. "After the choir covered you, after the fires in the west stacks — I went out. I told you I was going to check on Brother Cassian's route. That was a lie. I went to the shrine of Ilvane-of-the-Second-Rain, the little one, the one behind the chandler's. The god my brother prayed to. The god who answered me first, back when the rain came to a city with no clouds."

August's hands went very still in her lap. Stillness was another tool. She was discovering she had more tools than she'd ever wanted.

"There's an offering box there," Sefa went on. "Old style. You write your petition and you burn it, and the ash falls up instead of down, and you know it's been received. Except now the gods are starving, and their boxes are hungry differently. This one didn't take paper anymore." She laughed, and it was the worst sound August had ever heard her make. "It took promises. I put my hand in and I said: tell me where they're keeping her, and let me hear him again. Just once. Just his voice. That was the whole price. My location for my brother. One prayer, one address, one dead boy saying good morning."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere below, a barge horn moaned on the river, long and indifferent.

"You gave them the safehouse," August said.

"I gave them the counting-house. They already knew about this one by then — your friend Corvane keeps better secrets than he thinks but worse ones than he hopes. No. I gave them the tannery." Sefa's voice cracked down the middle. "Marlow's sister's tannery, August. I told them you'd send Marlow there, because I know you, I know how you think, you hide people with family because family is the last thing anyone checks. And they went. An hour ago. Two of the Concord's auditors and something wearing a divine envoy's coat that walked wrong, like its legs had been described to it rather than grown."

August was on her feet before she knew she'd moved. "Is Marlow—"

"Gone. He smelled them coming — tanneries smell everything, that's the whole trade — and he ran. He's alive, he sent word by a dock-boy, he's moving, he's angry, he'll live. But they found the trail, August. They found the thread I handed them, and they're pulling it, and every yard of it ends at you." Sefa finally sat, abruptly, like a puppet with cut strings, on the edge of the oldest desk. "I'm not telling you this because I'm brave. I'm telling you because I can't carry it alone and it's going to kill me either way, and I'd rather die honest than live the way Corvane lives."

August looked at her. Really looked, the way she'd looked at the vault ledger, the way she'd looked at the founding contract: taking inventory of damage, assessing what could be salvaged, running the numbers on a loss.

And here is the thing about August Okafor, junior ledger-keeper, liar of exactly one day's standing: the first thing she felt was not rage. It was recognition.

Because hadn't she done the same arithmetic? Hadn't she forged guild funds — chapter by chapter, entry by entry, coin by coin — telling herself it was for everyone, knowing in the small dishonest hours that some part of it was for the version of herself that finally mattered? Hadn't she corrupted the choir gently, teaching them forgery and misdirection, dressing theft up as care? Everyone in this ragged, impossible family was paying for love with something they'd sworn they'd never spend.

Sefa had just spent louder than most.

"You heard him," August said quietly. "Your brother. When you prayed."

Sefa flinched as if struck. "Yes."

"What did he say?"

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "He said good morning. He used to say it that way, dragging the syllables, *gooood morrrning*, like morning was a country he was reluctant to enter. He said it exactly right. Exactly. Do you understand? Dead eleven years and the voice was perfect, because they keep our dead the way banks keep deeds. Perfect and filed and *not ours anymore*." Her hands were shaking. "That's the trap. That's the whole filthy genius of it. They don't offer you power or gold. They offer you the sound of someone you loved, and they let you believe that hearing them again means having them back. It doesn't. It means renting them. It means every time you want to hear that voice, you pay again — and the price is always someone else."

August came around the desk slowly. "You burned the message."

Sefa looked up. "What?"

"The message. From the offering box. Whatever they sent back — instructions, confirmation, a summons. You said you went to the shrine an hour ago. You've been standing at that window for longer than an hour, and you came back here, to the person you sold, instead of running. Which means whatever they gave you, you still have it, and you haven't used it." August held out her hand. "Give it to me, or burn it yourself. Those are the only two merciful options, and I think you already know which one you want."

For a long moment Sefa didn't move. Then she reached into her coat and drew out a slip of paper, folded small, soft-edged with handling — she'd opened and refolded it many times, August saw, many times, reading it over and over like a letter from a lover.

"I haven't read all of it," Sefa whispered. "That's a lie. I've read all of it. Forty times. It says: *Bring her to the Ember Square at moonrise and the voice will be returned to you permanently. Not rented. Returned. We will rebuild him from the fire and he will never have died.*" Her breath hitched. "*Permanent*, August. Not an echo. Him. Whole. Alive. All I have to do is walk you into a square."

"And you brought it here instead."

"I brought it here because—" Sefa's face did something complicated, grief and fury wrestling for the same chair. "Because when I imagined handing you over, I saw your face, and it looked like mine looks right now. Because you burned your whole life down for a dragon you'd never met, and I sold my only friend for a boy I can't even remember the smell of anymore. Because somebody has to be the person who doesn't. Don't you see? Somebody in this story has to be the person who *doesn't*. If it's not me, if it's not even me, then there's no version of any of this worth surviving."

August took the paper from her.

It was warm. That was the horror of it — the gods' messages stayed body-warm forever, like something freshly dead. She read it once. The handwriting was beautiful. Everything the gods made was beautiful; that had always been the problem.

Then she held it to the candle stub on the desk.

Sefa made a sound — a small, involuntary wound of a sound — and half-reached out, and then stopped herself, and watched her brother burn a second time.

"Wait," she said, as the flame ate the word *permanent*. "Wait, wait—"

"I know," August said.

"*Wait*—"

"I know." August held the burning slip steady until the fire reached her fingers, then dropped the last black scrap into the empty ink-pot ring on the shelf, where it curled and went out. "I know exactly what I just burned. And I need you to hear this, so listen: I am not forgiving you because it cost me nothing. It doesn't cost nothing. Marlow ran for his life today because of you, and tomorrow the Concord will be one step closer because of you, and I may die in a square because of you. That's real. That stays real. Forgiveness isn't forgetting the number, Sefa. I'm a ledger-keeper. We don't forget numbers." She looked up, eyes stinging, exhausted past tears into something drier and harder and warmer underneath. "We decide what to do with them. And I'm deciding that the woman who stood at a window for an hour rather than run — the woman who carried the temptation here instead of cashing it — that woman is worth more than her worst entry. Every book I've ever kept says people are sums. I'm choosing to believe we're not."

Sefa broke. There was no other word for it. Eleven years of a dead brother's absence and three days of unbearable choice came out of her all at once, and August held her while she shook, among the ghost-rings of ink pots, in a stolen building, with a stolen contract, both of them owing everything and owned by no one — and it occurred to August, distantly, absurdly, that this was the first time since her grandmother died that anyone had cried against her shoulder, and the first time ever that she hadn't tallied the cost.

When Sefa finally pulled back, scrubbing her face with her sleeve, she laughed wetly. "Pickled eels?"

"Pickled eels."

They ate them. They were terrible. It was the best meal August had ever had.

* * *

Moonrise was six hours off, and the Ember Square would be crawling with divine envoys waiting for a traitor who was no longer coming. Which meant, August said, mouth full, thinking aloud the way she did when the numbers were running hot: the gods had budgeted for her capture tonight. Auditors staged. Envoy positioned. Choir-hunters reassigned.

Which meant everywhere else in the city had just gone thin.

Sefa stared at her. "You're smiling. Why are you smiling? Only terrible people smile like that."

"Because I've been asking the wrong question." August pushed the loaf aside and pulled the oilcloth bundle from behind the brick, unwrapping the founding contract across the desk, her fingers finding the margins automatically, the way they always did — where no one looks, where the truth hides. "I've been asking how to hide. Wrong question. The right question is: what do starving gods do when the meal doesn't show up?" She tapped the contract, tap tap tap, the rhythm of columns balancing. "They get desperate. Desperate entities renegotiate. And renegotiation requires a counterparty, which means tonight, while they're all staring at an empty square, one of them might finally be willing to *talk*."

"To talk about what?"

"About rations," August said. "About fading gently instead of waging war. About a third path that nobody's ever offered, because nobody's ever been mad enough to sit down with hunger and open negotiations." She was gathering the contract, the candle, the terrible eels, the map of the undercity she'd memorized from a debt-map in Corvane's own office. "But first I need proof it's possible. Proof that a god can starve quietly and choose to. And I know where to find it — there's a cathedral under the third district, a god who died first, before the theft, before the bargain. If any god ever faded without a war..."

Sefa stood, wiping her hands, reaching for her coat. "If any god ever chose to die rather than fight," she said slowly, "then dying peacefully is a thing a god can choose."

"Exactly. Precedent. Even the divine honors precedent — they're older than law, and anything older than law is just accounting with incense." August blew out the candle, and in the sudden dark her voice was steady and certain and terrified, all three at once. "Get your coat. We go down where the ash falls up. And Sefa — when we find what's down there—"

"Yes?"

"If I write myself into that ledger as sole debtor, someone has to be willing to read the terms aloud afterward. Someone who knows what it costs to burn the thing you love most." August smiled in the dark, and this time it wasn't terrible at all. "Consider it your penance. Paid in full."

Above them, far away, a bell began to ring the hour — and then rang a second time, and a third, though no bell in the city rang three times except for one reason:

Fire. Somewhere above, the night was catching.
