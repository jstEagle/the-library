## 24. The Cold Cathedral

They came down into the cathedral through a crack in the world that Sefa swore she'd known about since seminary, and which August was fairly sure had not existed until Sefa needed it to.

"That's not how architecture works," August said.

"That's how *faith* works," Sefa said. "The building is only where the belief used to live. Nobody believes in Heshem anymore. So the walls stopped insisting on themselves." She shouldered through the gap in what looked like solid stone and it gave way like wet paper. "Come on. Mind the threshold. It bites."

August followed her into the dark and felt the bite—a cold snap across her ankle, like a ledger closing on a finger. Then she was inside, and her breath went out of her in a white plume, because the cathedral of Heshem the First-Fed was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

It was cold the way an empty account is cold.

* * *

She'd seen abandoned churches before. Every city kept a few, bought up by merchants or converted to granaries or left to pigeons. They had a particular smell—dust and birdlime and old incense gone sour. This place didn't smell like anything. That was the horror of it. A space so completely emptied of its purpose that even decay declined to move in.

The nave stretched away from them, vast and black, and August's lantern showed her pews still ranked in their rows, an altar still dressed in cloth gone grey as ash, windows still standing in their frames—windows that should have thrown colored light and threw nothing at all, because the glass had forgotten color along with everything else.

"Heshem," Sefa said quietly. She walked forward like a woman approaching a grave she'd helped dig. "He was the first of them to answer prayers, you know. First to be worshipped, first to be fed. And when the fire started running out—" she made a small, terrible gesture, "—first to starve."

"You knew him?"

"I knew *of* him. Every priest did. He was our cautionary tale." Sefa touched the back of a pew and her fingers came away clean; there was no dust for them to find. "They told us he grew proud. They told us he demanded too much worship and the people turned away and he faded, and that was the wages of arrogance." Her voice caught. "I believed that story for thirty years. It has such a tidy moral."

"And now?"

"Now I've heard a starving dragon under my feet, and I know what hunger actually sounds like." She turned to August, and in the lantern light her face was older than it had been a week ago. Older than it had been twenty years ago. "Heshem didn't grow proud, August. He grew quiet. There's a difference. Pride makes noise. Hunger just... waits."

August set the lantern on the altar and began, because she could not help herself, to take inventory.

It was the guild in her, the junior ledger-keeper who balanced columns in her sleep. Racks of candles, all burned down to stubs—or no. She looked closer. Not burned. *Unburned.* The wax had never melted; it had simply given up holding its shape over decades, slumped like tired soldiers. Offerings on the side tables: fruit gone to leather, coins gone green, a child's carved wooden bird with its wings worn smooth by hands long dead. Everything here had been left mid-sentence. A congregation that walked out one morning and never came back, or a god who faded so gently that no one noticed the service had stopped being answered until years had passed.

"Look at this," Sefa called from the north transept. "August. Look at this."

There was a door behind the altar, low and plain, the kind of door built for work rather than worship. Beyond it lay a scriptorium—a small room, a desk, shelves of records, a window of ordinary glass that let in nothing but the grey light of the undercity's phosphor moss. And covering every surface, floor to ceiling, shelf to shelf, were books.

Not scriptures. August knew scriptures; the guild audited temples often enough. These were something else. Columns. Dates. Figures.

"They're accounts," she said, and her voice came out strange.

"I can see they're accounts. Why does a god keep accounts?"

"Because someone has to track what comes in." August pulled the nearest volume toward her and opened it, and the cold of the room seemed to gather around her shoulders like a coat. "And what goes out."

* * *

The handwriting changed over the years. That was the first thing she noticed, flipping forward through the earliest volume. At the start the script was enormous, exuberant, letters tall as her hand—the writing of a being for whom paper was a courtesy, not a constraint. Prayers received: four thousand, two hundred and eleven. Gratitude rendered: ample. Fire expended in answer: negligible. Balance: overflowing.

Then the numbers began to shrink.

Not quickly. That was what undid her, turning page after page by lantern light while Sefa stood silent at her shoulder. Not quickly. Slowly, the way a fire dies when everyone swears there's still heat in the coals. Four thousand prayers became three thousand became nine hundred became forty. The answers got shorter too. Where once a prayer for rain had been met with actual rain—*expended: trivial, joyfully given*—later entries read like a clerk apologizing to a customer. *Prayer for healing of the boy Tomas. Answered in part. He will walk again but not run. Expended: considerable. Balance: low.*

"He rationed himself," August said. She heard her own voice go flat, which was what it did instead of breaking. "He didn't hoard. He rationed. Look—he starts refusing the big miracles first. Weather. Plague-turning. Resurrections. Keeps the small ones going as long as he can. A blessing on bread. Comfort in grief." She turned another page. "He's doing triage. He's doing exactly what I would have done."

"August."

"No, look at this entry—" She jabbed the page with her finger. "*Balance insufficient for rain over the whole valley. Will give rain over the widow Chen's field only. She has children.* He prioritized. He did arithmetic with mercy. This is—" She stopped.

"This is what?" Sefa said softly.

"This is good bookkeeping," August said, "for a god who is dying of it anyway."

* * *

The last volumes were thin. The handwriting had shrunk until August had to hold the pages close to the lantern, squinting at letters small as insect tracks. The final book covered eleven years. Eleven years in perhaps forty pages. Prayers received: none. Prayers received: none. Prayers received: one, and beside it, in those tiny fading characters, an answer that took up half a page—the god spending, apparently, nearly the last of himself on a single response:

*To the child who prayed that her father come home from the war: I cannot bring him home. But I can make the waiting lighter. I have sent you a dog. Love him and be loved. This is the whole of what I have left. It is enough. It must be enough.*

Below that, one more line, in a hand so faint it might have been imagined:

*Let no one say I raged. Let the record show I went out like a lamp, not like a fire.*

August sat down on the scriptorium floor among ten thousand entries and put her face in her hands.

* * *

Sefa let her sit for a while. That was one of the things August had come to love about her, though she hadn't said the word yet, not even in the privacy of her own margins: Sefa knew when silence was the correct entry.

But eventually Sefa crouched beside her and said, "You've realized it. I can see you've realized it."

"Realized what?"

"What these books are." Sefa picked up the last thin volume and held it as if it were a relic, which, August thought, it was. "Every temple keeps records like this somewhere. Sealed archives, private ledgers, the gods' own books. We called them mystery texts in seminary. Forbidden. But they weren't forbidden because they were holy." She laughed, and it was not a happy sound. "They were forbidden because they were *evidence*. Because any priest who read them would learn what you just learned."

"That a god can starve quietly."

"Yes."

"And that—" August reached for it, the shape of the thought, huge and terrible. "And that the ones answering prayers now, the ones burning stolen dragon-fire—they don't *have* to do what they're doing. They don't have to fight Vaskarath. They don't have to take the fire back by war. If the fire runs out, they can simply..." She looked at the little book in Sefa's hands. At the god who had spent his last ember on a dog for a lonely child. "They can fade. Gently. Like he did. It's been done before. There's precedent. There's *paperwork*."

"There's paperwork," Sefa agreed. "Gods respect paperwork more than they respect commandments, in my experience. It's the one law none of them ever broke."

"But it costs them everything. That's the terrible cost of it. Heshem didn't survive. Fading isn't survival—it's just dying without making anyone else die first." August stared at the rows of ledgers. "No wonder they chose war. No wonder the Concord is marching. Who chooses to go out like a lamp when there's fuel to burn and someone else's city to burn it in?"

"Someone very tired," Sefa said. "Or someone very brave. In my experience the two are difficult to tell apart at this range."

August pulled the final volume gently from Sefa's grip and wrapped it in the spare cloth from her satchel, the way the guild taught you to handle documents that were worth more than the person carrying them. Once, that training had served the guild. Tonight it served a dead god's testament.

"We take these back," she said. "We take them to Vaskarath. Because right now he thinks the choice is war or extinction—and honestly, so did I. But if the gods can be shown that fading is possible, that it's *been done*, that there's a record of a god who did it without cursing the world on his way out—" She stood, legs stiff, knees cracking in the cold. "Then maybe nobody has to burn."

"And if the gods don't want to be shown? If they'd rather fight than fade?"

August thought of the emissary's voice saying *the guarantor*. Of four raised hands in a room where she had worked for six years. Of Corvane's face when he'd admitted what she was to him: inventory.

"Then we'll know we offered," she said. "That has to count for something. Somewhere. In some ledger." She hefted her satchel. "Come on. We need to get above ground before dawn. The choir will be worried, and Vaskarath needs to see this tonight, while I still believe it can work."

They were almost to the cracked threshold when Sefa stopped so abruptly that August walked into her back.

"Sefa?"

"The window," Sefa whispered. "In the transept. The one that forgot color." She wasn't moving. She wasn't breathing properly. "August. It's lit."

August turned.

Far down the black nave, in a window that had held nothing but grey glass and greyer memory for a hundred years, there was light. Faint. Gold. Moving, the way candlelight moves, or the way something behind the glass moves when it is looking out.

And threaded through the cold air of the cathedral, so soft it might have been the building remembering rather than speaking, came a sound neither of them could mistake:

Someone, somewhere in the dark, was praying.

* * *

August's hand found Sefa's in the dark. The satchel of a dead god's testimony pressed against her hip. Behind them, the little gold light in the colorless window flickered—waiting, patient, hungry—and ahead of them the crack in the wall breathed cold air down from a city where gods were waking and an old serpent waited for news he had waited three hundred years to hear.

"Tell me you feel that," Sefa whispered.

"I feel it."

"It's coming *from* the window. Something is listening on the other side." Her grip tightened. "August—that prayer. Listen to whose voice it's in."

August listened. And the blood in her veins went cold as the cathedral, because the voice drifting down the nave, small and hopeful and asking for help, was her own.
