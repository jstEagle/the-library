## 25. Negotiating With Hunger

The idea came to her the way most of her ideas did: as an arithmetic error.

August was lying on the cold cathedral floor with the dead god's records spread around her like a fallen deck of cards, and she had been staring at the same column for an hour when she finally saw what was wrong with it. Not with the numbers—with the question behind them.

Every account in the undercity, every scrap of divine bookkeeping the starved god's priests had left behind, asked the same question in a different font: *How much is left?* How much fire remained in the hoard. How many years at current consumption. How long until the gods burned through their inheritance and turned, hungry and enormous, toward the only fuel source remaining.

The guild's ledgers asked it too. The Concord's auditors would ask it. Vaskarath, coiled in his dark below, was living inside that question like a man trapped in a burning house.

*How much is left.*

And it was wrong, because it assumed the fire was being spent at one rate—the rate of appetite—and would end at one time—the moment of emptiness—and between now and then there was only one road, and that road ended in war.

But August Okafor had spent six years managing debts, and there was one thing every junior ledger-keeper learned before they learned anything else, before the seals and the cipher-work and the secret margins:

*A debt ends differently if you change the payments.*

Nobody had ever renegotiated with the gods. Nobody had ever *asked*. Because asking required believing the gods could be reasoned with, and because the arithmetic of terror made negotiation look like weakness, and because—she sat up so fast her elbow cracked against the stone—it had never once occurred to anyone that the party holding the asset might get a say in how it was sold.

The gods were spending dragon-fire at the rate of hunger. Fine. Hunger was not a law of nature. Hunger was a *budget line*.

"Sefa," she said. Her voice came out strange, too loud in the dead god's silence. "Sefa, wake up."

Sefa was asleep sitting up against the altar, which took a particular talent August didn't have. She opened one eye. "If you've found more depressing columns, I don't want them."

"I've found a way out."

That got both eyes. Sefa leaned forward, and in the grey light of the cathedral her face looked carved, exhausted, beautiful in the way worn things are beautiful. "Out of what? Name it precisely. I'm a priestess, or I was. Precision matters."

"Out of the war." August pulled the records toward her, fanned them out. "Look. The gods don't burn the fire all at once. They ration it already—they just ration it by appetite instead of by plan. A prayer for rain costs this much." She tapped a column. "A prayer answered in a brother's voice costs this much more, because it has to be *shaped*, it has to carry memory. The expensive miracles are the ones that touch grief. The cheap ones are weather."

"You've been costing my prayers," Sefa said. There was no accusation in it. That was the thing about Sefa; even after everything, even after the message she'd burned, she watched August work the way sailors watch a horizon.

"Everything gets costed eventually. That's not the point. The point is—" August's hands were moving faster than her words, which happened when she was frightened or inspired, and right now she couldn't tell which. "The point is the fire doesn't have to run out in nine years. It could run out in forty. In sixty. Slow enough that the gods fade gently. Slow enough that they have time to become something else, or stop, peacefully, the way the god whose church this was did. He starved quietly. It's *documented*. The Concord has the precedent in writing and they've buried it under three feet of theology because it's inconvenient."

"And the dragon?"

August stopped. Her hands went still on the paper.

"That," she said, "is the part I haven't figured out how to say."

* * *

They went down in the last hour before dawn, because August had decided that if she was going to attempt the most audacious negotiation in the history of debt, she wanted to do it while she was too tired to be properly afraid.

The stair into the deep was longer than she remembered, or perhaps fear had been shortening it before. Cold ash drifted upward past them as they descended, falling in reverse, and Sefa kept glancing at it with her lips moving—not praying, August realized, but counting. Old habit. Priests counted everything too; they just called it liturgy.

At the bottom, the shrine opened around the dying flame like a wound around a splinter, and the flame itself guttered low and gold, and beneath and around and through it all lay Vaskarath.

He was larger than her mind insisted on remembering. Every time she came down here some animal part of her recalculated him from scratch and arrived at *impossible*, and every time her ledger-keeper's mind overruled it and wrote him down as simply *very large*, the way you write *miscellaneous* for a number too strange to categorize. His scales were the grey of bank-vault doors. One eye opened, amber, ancient, and fixed on her with the weary attention of someone who has been interrupted ten thousand times and expects nothing better.

"The little accountant returns," he said. His voice moved the ash on the floor. "Come to count my ribs again?"

"I've come to make you an offer."

Something shifted in the vast dark. Not alarm. Interest, maybe—or the memory of interest, rusted but still turning. "An offer. From the Guild of Impossible Things. Let me guess the terms. You will give me my fire back, all of it, the moment your masters find a way to survive without it. You will apologize in writing. You will name a shrine after me." The eye narrowed. "I have heard offers before, child. Your grandmother's generation made several. All of them were lies wearing formal dress."

"This isn't from the guild."

That got her the second eye.

"I burned my bridge coming down those stairs," August said, and heard her own steadiness with a kind of distant pride. "Corvane knows where I am. The auditors know about the sealed vault. By tomorrow nightfall, probably sooner, this whole city is going to be full of people who want either the fire or the guarantor, and most of them want both. I'm not here representing anyone. I'm here because I ran the numbers, and every path except one ends with everyone starving or everyone burning, and I am *tired* of paths that end badly. So here it is."

She unrolled the papers she'd carried down against her chest, and her voice took on the cadence it always took when she read accounts aloud—the flat, clear, merciless cadence of a woman reciting truth into a room that would rather not hear it.

"Rationed fire. Freely given. Not seized, not stolen, not taken in war—*offered*, on a schedule, at a rate the gods can live on but cannot grow fat on. Enough to keep their altars warm. Not enough to fund another miracle like the rain, or the voices. A maintenance budget. And as the fire declines—as it must decline, because there is a bottom to every hoard, even yours—the rations decline with it, slowly, predictably, over decades. Long enough for the gods to learn what your predecessor learned." She gestured upward, at the cathedral of the god who had died first. "Long enough to starve gently. Long enough to *choose* it."

Silence. The dying flame popped. Somewhere far above, water dripped onto stone with the patience of a clock.

Then Vaskarath laughed.

It was not a kind sound. It was also not a cruel one, exactly—it was the laugh of something that had gone a very long time without laughing and found the muscles atrophied and strange. The ash jumped in waves. Sefa grabbed August's arm, and August held still, because she had learned something about large creatures in debt: they do not eat the person presenting terms. It's bad form.

"A choice," the dragon said, when the laughter had ground itself out. "You are offering me a *choice*."

"I'm offering you a role in the negotiation, yes."

"No one has ever—" The great head shifted, and scales scraped like millstones. "Do you understand what I am, little ledger-keeper? I am not a beast that men failed to tame. I am a *counterparty* who was never permitted to negotiate. Your guild's founders came down here with a contract already written. They did not ask what I wanted. They asked what I would surrender to avoid extinction, and then they took it, and they wrote the taking down in beautiful script and filed it in a vault, and for two hundred years every entry in that accursed book has treated my fire as *inventory*. Mine. My fire. Inventory." The amber eyes were very bright now. "And a starving girl walks down my stairs and asks whether I would prefer to be *consulted*."

"Yes," August said. "That's the offer. Consulted. Terms drawn jointly. You hold the asset; the gods hold the need; I hold the paperwork. Nobody signs anything until all three parties have read every clause. I know that sounds naive. I've read the founding contract. I know what happened the last time someone said *trust me* in this room." She swallowed. "I'm not them. But I'm carrying their signature, and I'd like to spend the rest of my life paying that down."

Vaskarath was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Sefa's grip on her arm loosened, then tightened again. Long enough that August became aware of her own heartbeat, her own breath, the small hot economy of being alive in front of something that had watched empires open accounts and close them.

"Why," the dragon said finally, "would the gods accept? They can take. Taking has worked for them for two centuries. Why would hunger accept a diet?"

"Because taking has *stopped* working, and they're too proud to notice yet." August crouched, spreading the dead god's records on the stone where the amber eye could see them. "Look. Their consumption is rising—the prayers are getting bigger, more desperate, more expensive, because people are starting to feel that something's wrong and the gods are buying faith with fire they don't have. That curve ends one way. In nine years, maybe eight, they hit empty, and then their options are: die, or come down here and take the rest from you, and taking the rest means a war that cracks this city open like an egg. Even if they win—even if they take everything—they've won *nine more years of appetite*. That's it. That's the prize. Nine years, and then the same cliff, closer." She looked up, directly into the eye that was the size of a chapel window. "Whereas if they ration—if they accept a declining budget—they get forty. Fifty. Time to shrink gracefully. Time to decide what a god is when it stops being a furnace. Some of them will refuse. I know that. Some of them would rather burn the world than go on a diet. But the eldest, the tired ones, the ones like the god whose church we slept in—they'll take it. Hunger negotiates, Vaskarath. Hunger has always negotiated. It's pride that refuses to come to the table, and pride is the one expense the gods can't actually afford anymore."

Another silence, but a different quality of silence. August knew silences the way musicians knew intervals. This one was thinking.

"And what," Vaskarath said at last, very softly, "do *you* want out of it, August Okafor? Granddaughter of Ilsette. Guarantor. Debtor-in-chief. What does the little accountant charge for brokering peace between heaven and the last dragon?"

And there it was, the trapdoor in the middle of the conversation, and August stood at its edge and considered lying, because lying had gotten easier lately and that frightened her more than the dragon did.

"I want it on record," she said, "that my family's debt is paid. Not forgiven—*paid*. I want the founding contract voided and the guild's charter dissolved and every protection and every prison those pages built torn down at once. I want the choir to walk away owing nothing to anybody. And I want—" her voice caught, and she let it, because she was done hiding things in margins, "—I want to stop being an entry in someone else's book. That's my price. It's not a number. I know that makes it a bad term. I'm offering it anyway."

Vaskarath regarded her for a long moment. Then, with the slow care of a mountain changing its mind, he lowered his head until his snout rested on the stone an arm's length from where she stood, close enough that his breath stirred her hair—breath that smelled, strangely, of cold hearths and old winters and something underneath it all like sun-warmed rock.

"My grandmother," the dragon said, "once told a human king that dragons do not forgive. She was right. We are too long-lived for forgiveness; forgiveness is for creatures who can outlive their grievances." The eye, near now, was less like a window and more like a lamp behind horn. "But we remember accurately. And accurate memory is worth more than forgiveness, though no one ever trades for it." A slow exhalation. "You have brought me accurate memory twice now, little accountant. Once when you read the crime aloud to yourself. Once tonight, when you walked past every easy lie on your way down those stairs. So: yes. Bring me your ration schedule. Bring me your clauses. Bring me these starving gods and their pride and their empty altars, and we will see whether compassion survives contact with an actual counterparty." The great head lifted slightly. "But understand what you are proposing. You are asking gods to practice the one discipline they have never once attempted in the entire history of worship."

"What's that?"

"Living within their means."

August laughed—a startled, exhausted bark of a laugh—and beside her Sefa laughed too, high and disbelieving, and even the dying flame seemed to steady for a moment, as if the idea of a budget had warmed it.

They worked through the hours before dawn, the three of them—dragon, priestess, ledger-keeper—drafting terms in the ash with fingers and claw-tip, and it was nearly morning when August climbed back up into the grey light of the undercity with the schedule folded against her heart and the wild, terrified feeling of a person who has bet everything on a single entry.

Which lasted exactly as long as it took to reach the top of the stairs.

Sefa saw it first, because Sefa had spent thirty years reading crowds and could read smoke the same way. She caught August's sleeve. "Look."

Above them, rising through the shafts and grates of the undercity, the morning air was moving wrong. It moved *inward*—every draft in every tunnel bending the same direction, toward the surface, toward the guild hall, the way air bends toward a fire. Or toward a vacuum.

Somewhere above, something vast had begun to draw breath.

"They're here," Sefa whispered. "The Concord. Early."

August put her hand flat against the wall, felt the pull of it, the city inhaling, and thought: *We're out of time. We were always out of time. The only question left is what we spend.*
