## 26. The Concord Closes In

The first forgery to fail was a small one, which is how August knew they were in trouble.

It was a receipt from a chandler's shop on Tanner's Row, dated eleven days ago, recording the purchase of three pounds of tallow and a bundle of wicks. Sefa had written it. August had aged it with tea and candle smoke. It should have been perfect.

The auditor who found it held it up to the light for a long time, then said, mildly, to no one in particular: "Tanner's Row flooded on the ninth. The chandler closed his shop. He sold nothing to anyone."

That was the thing about the Concord of Faiths that August hadn't understood until now. She had imagined them as priests with ledgers—pompous, slow, buried under their own bureaucracy. She had imagined she could out-paper them.

She had not imagined they would simply *ask people*.

* * *

By dawn of the third day after the audit began, the Concord had stopped looking at documents and started looking at streets.

August watched it happen from the window of the defrocked chapel the choir had claimed as their workshop, high enough to see over the rooftops of the undercity but low enough not to be seen herself. Below her, the market that never slept—the one where debtors sold their debts, where miracles were pawned, where the ash fell upward on bad nights—was emptying. Not quickly. Not all at once. The way water empties from a basin when someone lifts the stopper: quietly, and then all at once.

Vendors rolled up their awnings before noon. The pawnbroker who kept a shrine to Saint Ferrous behind his counter drew its curtain. Two children who usually played dice against the chapel steps weren't there, and neither was anyone scolding them for being there.

"They're posting watchers at every stair down," Pell said beside her. He was the oldest of the debtors, a former guild courier whose impossible thing—a debt of one honest answer, owed to a man he'd lied to thirty years ago—had made him expert at recognizing when questions were coming. "I counted six new faces at the Gullet Stair alone. They stand wrong. Guild men lean when they wait. These ones don't lean."

"Who are they?"

"Concord marshals, maybe. Or worse." Pell turned his hat in his hands. "There's a kind of agent the faiths keep for special work. They say they can smell a lie the way you smell rain coming."

"Can they?"

"I don't know," Pell said. "Nobody who's met one has ever successfully lied to me about it."

August almost laughed. Then she looked back down at the emptying street and didn't.

* * *

The choir gathered at dusk, because dusk was when they'd always gathered, and because stopping would have felt like admitting something.

They sat in the pews of the dead god's chapel—Saint Vellum's, a god of record-keeping who had starved two centuries ago, which August still found darkly funny and no one else did. Nine of them tonight. Ten counting her. The candles were stubs; wax was expensive when you were funding an investigation out of forged withdrawals.

"The chandler's receipt," August began. "Tell me what happened."

Sefa spoke first. "A marshal came to the shop. Asked him if he'd sold tallow on the fourth. The chandler said he'd been closed since the flood. The marshal showed him the receipt." She paused. "He said it wasn't his handwriting."

"It wasn't," August said. "It was yours."

"He doesn't know that. But he knows it isn't *his*." Sefa's hands were folded very tightly in her lap. "And then the marshal asked him who else had come asking about the shop lately. And the chandler—" Her voice caught on something. "The chandler used to pray at my old temple. Before I was defrocked. He recognized me from the description."

"You ran?"

"I walked," Sefa said. "Slowly. Like a woman with nowhere to be. And then I took the long way home and changed my shawl twice." She looked up. "August. They're not auditing anymore. Auditors check books. These people are asking *neighbors*."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone has realized the same thing and no one wants to say it.

It was Wren who said it. Wren, who was nineteen and owed the guild one impossible apology and had the fastest hands August had ever seen. "Every forgery we planted is a thread," she said. "We planted forty-one."

"Thirty-eight," August corrected. "Three were never placed."

"Thirty-eight threads, and every single one leads back to this room." Wren's voice didn't shake, which somehow made it worse. "We thought we were building cover stories. We built a map. To us."

* * *

They spent the night pulling threads.

It was miserable, meticulous work, and August hated every minute of it, and she was good at it, which she hated more. A forged bill of sale for a mule that could be traced to a stable that didn't exist—burned in the chapel stove, the ashes mixed with real hearth-ash so even a fire-reader would find nothing. A false confession letter written in Corvane's hand, meant to suggest internal guild politics—retrieved from the drop-box by a boy they'd paid in bread, unopened, thank every dead god. A ledger page slipped into the records of a shipping house—too late, already found, already copied, already being traced.

"We lose that one," August said grimly. "Assume they have it. Assume they know the hand that wrote it."

"Is it your hand?" Pell asked.

"It's *a* hand. It's close to mine the way my handwriting is close to my grandmother's." She rubbed her eyes. "Which means if they've seen the founding contract—and they haven't, it's sealed—but if they ever do, they'll see the family resemblance. And they'll start asking why a junior ledger-keeper writes like a woman who died sixty years ago."

"Will they ask that?"

"They're the Concord of Faiths," August said. "Asking impossible questions is the only thing they've ever been good at."

* * *

On the fifth day, the divine agents arrived, and everything got worse in a way that was hard to describe and impossible to ignore.

Pell saw the first one at the Gullet Stair and came back white as tallow himself. "It stood like a person," he reported. "Dressed like a person. But it didn't blink. I watched it for ten minutes. It didn't blink once, and it didn't shift its weight, and when a cart nearly hit it, it didn't flinch. It just turned its head, exactly as far as it needed to, and turned back."

"What was it watching?"

"The stair. Just the stair. Like a doorpost that had opinions."

August knew what it was, or at least what it probably was. An emissary's herald—one of the minor divine functionaries the gods deployed when they wanted somewhere watched without spending actual attention on it. She'd read about them in the guild's older files, back when she'd still believed the files were historical curiosities. Heralds didn't think. They observed and reported, and whatever they reported to received everything, perfectly, without distortion or mercy.

You couldn't bribe a herald. You couldn't forge anything past a herald. You couldn't even lie near a herald, because while it couldn't tell truth from falsehood, it recorded *everything*, and eventually someone who could tell the difference would read the transcript.

"There are four of them now," Pell said. "One at each stair down. One at the well. They don't move. They don't eat. They just—" He made a small helpless gesture. "*Stand* there, being witnessed."

Sefa had gone very still. "Then we can't go down the stairs."

"We live down here," Wren pointed out. "Where exactly are we going instead?"

"That's not what I mean." Sefa's voice had the careful flatness she got when she was frightened and managing it. "I mean the dragon. The shrine. If they post a herald at every entrance to the undercity, then sooner or later one of them follows the cold ash. And the cold ash leads to Vaskarath."

No one said anything for a moment. The candles guttered. Somewhere above them, faintly, through stone and earth and the bones of the city, they could all feel it—not hear, *feel*—the slow breathing of the last dragon, asleep beneath a city that had stolen his fire and forgotten his name.

"They won't find him," August said. "The ash falls up. It disperses. There's no trail."

"August." Sefa reached across the pew and took her hand. "They posted a herald at the well. The *well*. Who watches a well? Nobody watches a well unless they're watching what comes out of it. And what comes out of our wells?" She let go. "Water that tastes of smoke. Water that's warm in winter. Every drop of it has passed through the aquifers around him."

* * *

The sixth day, the Concord issued a proclamation, and August got a copy within the hour because the boy they paid in bread had a friend who swept the proclamator's floor.

It was short. That was the frightening part. Concord proclamations were traditionally florid things, dense with scripture and precedent. This one was four sentences.

*By order of the Concord of Faiths: the Guild of Impossible Things is suspended pending full audit. All accounts, sealed and otherwise, are to be surrendered within three days. Any person harboring guild officers, guild records, or persons of interest named herein is subject to seizure and questioning. The guarantor of the founding covenant is sought for peaceful interview.*

August read it four times.

*Peaceful interview.* The Concord had never in its history used the phrase "peaceful interview" except immediately before something that was neither.

But it was the last line that made her sit down on the chapel floor with her back against a pew and breathe carefully for a while. *The guarantor of the founding covenant.* Not "the junior ledger-keeper." Not "August Okafor." They hadn't named her. Which meant either they didn't know who she was—

—or they knew exactly who she was, and saying her name aloud would reveal how much they knew, and the Concord never revealed its hand before the game was over.

"Pell," she said. "How many copies of that proclamation went up?"

"Hundreds. Every district. Every stair."

"And how many people in this city know what 'the guarantor of the founding covenant' means?"

Pell thought about it honestly, which was Pell's tragedy and his gift. "Maybe nine. Counting us."

"Counting Corvane," August said. "Counting whoever told the Concord the phrase existed." She stared at the paper. "Someone talked."

* * *

She confronted that suspicion at midnight, alone, in the worst possible place: her own memory.

Because there was only one person outside the choir who knew the term. Only one person she'd spoken to since the audit began, only one meeting, one candlelit conversation in which she had asked—she remembered asking, she could feel the shape of her own words in her mouth—whether the Concord's audit would extend to the sealed vault.

Corvane had said no. Corvane had said the sealed accounts predated the Concord's jurisdiction, that they were protected by clauses older than any living faith.

Corvane had also known about her bloodline since the day he hired her. Corvane had voted yes. Corvane had taught her, with such warmth, that the guild's true trade was the management of what people owe—and she had learned the lesson too well, because here was the arithmetic she couldn't avoid:

If the Concord found the vault, they found the contract. If they found the contract, they found the term *guarantor*. If they found the term, they needed someone to explain it, and the only someone available was the man who had run the guild for thirty years.

Or he had given it to them first. Preemptively. A trade: the guild's cooperation, the guarantor's name, in exchange for the institution's survival. It was exactly what she would have done, in his position. That was the worst part. She could construct the whole negotiation in her head, clause by clause, and it was flawless, and it was merciful, and it ended with her walking into a "peaceful interview" so that eight other members could keep their chairs.

She had spent her whole life believing that competence was a kind of safety. That if you understood the numbers, the numbers would protect you.

The numbers were pointing at her, and they were balanced, and they were right.

* * *

Day seven. The forgeries began dying in earnest.

It happened fast, the way a fever breaks fast. The Concord's document examiners—real ones, not marshals, pale ink-stained people who worked in shifts—went through the guild's auxiliary records like a flame through dry grass. The shipping-house page: matched to the hand of "one A. Okafor, junior ledger-keeper" by an examiner who noted, in the margin, that the letterforms resembled "an older hand present in certain restricted materials." The chandler's receipt: confirmed counterfeit. The mule bill of sale: confirmed counterfeit, and the stable master questioned, and the stable master's daughter questioned, and the daughter mentioned, helpfully, that a young woman with ink-stained fingers had asked directions to the undercity stairs three weeks ago.

By noon, the word *Okafor* appeared in the Concord's working notes. By evening, it appeared on a list. By nightfall, Pell came pounding up the chapel steps to say that a herald had taken up position at the mouth of Cooper's Alley, fifty feet from the chapel door, and that it was facing the door.

Not the street. The door.

"They know," Wren said. She had her satchel packed. She'd had it packed for two days; August pretended not to have noticed. "They don't know where exactly, but they know it's this neighborhood, and they're going to knock on doors until someone sells us. Someone always sells us. Someone always has."

"Not everyone," Sefa said.

"No," Wren agreed. "Just enough. It only takes one."

August stood at the window again, looking down at the herald in Cooper's Alley. It was exactly as Pell had described: motionless, unblinking, patient as a nail. Its face was smooth and slightly wrong, like a face remembered by someone who hadn't looked closely. Around it, the alley cats gave it a wide berth. Cats always knew.

She thought about Vaskarath, sleeping under the weight of the city, dreaming whatever dragons dream when they dream of debts. She thought about the cold cathedral and the starving god who had chosen to fade rather than fight, and the proof it offered that surrender wasn't the only alternative to war. She thought about the plan—the mad, half-formed, rationed-fire plan that lived in the back of her notebook between an inventory of candle stubs and a list of names.

None of it mattered if they came through that door tomorrow.

"All right," she said, turning around. "Listen to me. All of you."

They listened. That was the miracle she never logged, the one she'd have entered in a free ledger if she kept one: that nine debtors, each owing one impossible thing, each with every rational reason to scatter, listened.

"In three days, the guild surrenders everything, including the sealed vault. When they open it, they'll find the founding contract, and they'll find the name on it, and whether or not they already know, they'll *confirm*, and then they'll come for me. Not arrest me. Interview me. Peacefully." She let them hear the quotation marks. "So we have three days to finish what we started, and after that, none of this—" she gestured at the chapel, the pews, the candle stubs, the nine tired faces— "can look like what it is. The forgeries that survive need to point somewhere that isn't here. The ones that can't survive need to burn tonight. And everyone in this room needs a story about why they know me that isn't true."

"August," Pell said gently, "you're asking a room full of debtors to perjure themselves before the Concord of Faiths."

"I'm asking a room full of people who owe impossible things," August said, "to do one more."

Silence. The candles burned. Down in the deep places, something vast exhaled.

Wren slung her satchel over her shoulder. "I'll get the stove," she said. "Somebody decide what we're burning first."

* * *

They burned until nearly dawn. Documents fed the stove in handfuls, and August kept a running tally in her head—what evidence died, what evidence lived, what evidence would be found and misread, which was sometimes better than either. Sefa sang under her breath while she worked, some old temple hymn with the holy words swapped out for nonsense, and the nonsense was somehow more comforting than the original.

Near the end, when the stove glowed orange and the air tasted of ink and cinder, Pell paused at the window on his way to dump the ashes and said, quietly:

"August. Come look at this."

She came. She looked.

The herald in Cooper's Alley was gone. In its place, at the mouth of the alley, stood a figure in Concord grey with a human face and a human posture and a human impatience—it shifted its weight, it blinked, it consulted a watch. Behind it, faintly, from the direction of the Gullet Stair, came a sound August had never heard in the undercity before.

Boots. Many boots. Descending in step.

"They're not waiting for the deadline," Pell said. "They're not even waiting for morning."

August's hand found the notebook in her pocket—the one with the rationed-fire plan, the list of names, the arithmetic of mercy. Three days had become one night. The net was closing, thread by thread, and every thread led down.

Down to the ash that fell upward. Down to the shrine around a dying flame. Down to the oldest debtor of all, who had never once been offered a choice.

"Sefa," she said. "Get your shawl. We're going to see the dragon."

Above them, far above, the bells of the Concord began to ring.
