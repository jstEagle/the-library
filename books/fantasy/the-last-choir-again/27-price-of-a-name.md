## 27. Price of a Name

The undercity had its own weather.

August felt it as they descended—the air thickening, cooling, carrying that faint mineral sweetness she'd first noticed at the shrine, like rain remembered rather than rain fallen. Sefa walked beside her with the shawl pulled tight, her prayer beads clicking in a rhythm August had learned to read: fast for fear, slow for thought. Right now they were slow.

"You've counted your steps," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. "You always count when you're afraid."

"I'm not afraid."

"Your steps say otherwise."

August didn't answer, because the answer was yes, and because the number was ninety-two, and because counting was the only prayer she'd ever been taught. Ninety-two steps from the mouth of the cistern stair to the place where the ash began to fall upward. She'd measured it twice. The second time it had been ninety-three, and she'd lain awake half the night wondering whether the city itself was settling around the dragon's weight, or whether the distance between the world above and the world below was simply no longer fixed.

The guild had rules about distances. The guild had rules about everything. That was the whole point of a ledger—to make the world hold still long enough to be written down.

She was beginning to suspect nothing held still.

* * *

Vaskarath was smaller than she remembered.

That wasn't true, of course. Serpents the size of cathedral naves did not shrink between visits. But the flame he coiled around had guttered low since their last descent—a hand's height now where it had been an arm's—and in its diminished light the great body looked less like a mountain and more like a dune, wind-worn, patient, already becoming the shape of what would be left after.

"You brought the priest again," the dragon said. His voice came up through the floor, through the stone, through the soles of August's boots, as if the city were his throat.

"She brought me," Sefa said. "I've stopped pretending otherwise."

"Good. Lying is expensive." A sound moved through him that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be made of gravel settling. "Though I see you've both been practicing."

August opened her mouth to deny it and closed it again. There was no lying to a creature who had watched a guild learn arithmetic over three hundred years. She took out the notebook instead—the ration plan, the schedules, the columns she'd worked until dawn—and set it on the stone between them.

"We found a precedent," she said. "A god who starved quietly. In the cold cathedral, there were records—offerings that dwindled by choice, a congregation that gave less each year and the god let them, let himself fade rather than take. If the Concord's gods can be shown that fading gently is possible, if we can guarantee them rations of fire freely given—"

"Child."

Just that. Her name's opposite. Every word she'd rehearsed on the stairs went out of her like heat through a cracked window.

"You've done the sums," Vaskarath said. "How much fire remains in me?"

"By my figures—" August's voice caught; she made herself finish, because finishing was the one thing she had never failed at. "Fourteen years. Twenty if the rationing holds. Less than ten if the gods keep drawing at the current rate."

"And how many prayers is that?"

"Thousands. Tens of thousands. Enough to buy them time enough to—"

"To what?" The flame bent sideways, though there was no draft. "To become small? To lie down as I am lying down and agree to be forgotten?" The great head shifted, and one eye came level with her—an eye like a well, deep and dark and old past all decency. "Read me the terms of the debt, little keeper. Not your terms. The Concord's. You have their emissary's demand memorized. You recite it in your sleep—I can hear it through the stone."

August's hands went cold. Because she did know it. She'd copied it out three times, once for the file, once for the choir, once for herself, and somewhere in the third copying the words had stopped being words and started being a weight she carried against her ribs.

"'The Guild of Impossible Things will surrender the guarantor of the founding covenant,'" she said quietly, "'in full settlement of the fire-debt, that the account may be closed and the matter concluded.'"

"In full settlement," Vaskarath repeated. "Closed. Concluded." The eye did not blink. Snakes, she remembered from some ledger or other, did not blink. "Now tell me what you know of closing accounts with gods."

* * *

Sefa sat down first. She simply folded onto the cold stone, shawl and all, the way she did when a prayer was going to take a while, and August understood the gesture for what it was: whatever came next, they would hear it sitting, because standing people ran away.

"The gods do not want you," Vaskarath said. "I am sorry to tell it plainly, but plainness is the last courtesy I have left. They never wanted you. Your blood is a key, and keys are not loved—they are used and then thrown in the drawer."

"But the emissary said—"

"The emissary said what settles the *account*. Accounts are paper. Paper is your guild's religion, not theirs." Firelight crawled along the scales of his jaw, and August saw, up close, how thin those scales had grown—how the light showed through them in places, gold gone translucent as old vellum. "Ask yourself the question you have been too frightened to ask. If they take you, take your bloodline's fire, close the covenant—what evidence remains that the gods' power was ever stolen?"

The question landed in her chest like a dropped coin.

"Nothing," she whispered. "If the covenant is settled and sealed, the founding crime is... resolved. Paid. No longer a crime. Just history."

"Just history," the dragon agreed. "And who else knows the history?"

And there it was. The whole terrible shape of it, unfolding in her mind the way debts unfold—each term revealing the next, each next worse than the last.

"Everyone who's read the sealed account. The choir. Pell, Marisol, the Tessic twins. Sefa. Corvane—" Her voice thinned. "Corvane has known for thirty years. He told me. He said he voted to hand me over because sacrifice was the guild's purpose."

"Yes. And the guild's charter is the document that proves the theft. Every clause, every seal, every entry in your beautiful books—the whole edifice exists to administer a stolen inheritance. The gods cannot erase what happened in my cave three hundred years ago. But they can erase everyone who remembers."

Sefa's beads had stopped moving entirely.

"They don't want a guarantor," August said. The words tasted like copper. "They want witnesses. They want—" She made herself say it. Made herself run the sum all the way to its bottom line, because running sums to their bottoms was the only courage she owned. "Nine members. A choir of debtors. One defrocked priest. And the last dragon, who is the evidence itself."

"Now you are doing arithmetic," Vaskarath said, and there was something in his voice that was not kindness and not cruelty but the flat respect of one accountant for another. "Tell me the total."

"No."

"August." Sefa's hand found hers in the dark.

"*No.*" Louder than she meant. The sound went up through the water-stained vaults of the undercity and came back changed. "I refuse to total it. That's—that's the oldest trick in the book, making the victim do the accounting of her own funeral. I've seen Corvane do it to debtors a hundred times. Get them to name their own price so the guilt of naming sits on them instead of us. I won't. I won't carry that column."

The silence that followed was long enough to have a texture. Dust drifted upward past the dying flame, unhurried, obedient to some older law.

"You sound like her," Vaskarath said at last.

August went very still. "Like who."

"Ysolde." The name moved through the stone like water through cracks. "Your grandmother. When they brought her to me—young, furious, chains of office she hadn't earned yet—she refused too. Refused to sign at first. Do you know why the contract names her bloodline and not her life? Because she argued them down. They wanted a hostage. She gave them a *lineage*—something abstract, something she could believe might never come due. She signed to save the others in that room, and she spent the rest of her life believing the abstraction would hold." A slow exhalation stirred the ash. "It held for sixty years. Abstractions always hold, right up until someone decides they don't."

"She never told anyone," August said. "My mother never knew. I never knew. We grew up thinking she'd just—retired early. Moved to the coast. Died ordinary."

"Guilt keeps its own books. Hers were very well hidden." The great eye closed, then opened. "But she left you one thing, little keeper. Do you know what it was?"

"The fire," August said. "In my blood."

"No. Anyone can inherit fire. Gods have fire. I have fire, and look at me." The flame guttered, flared. "She left you the habit of refusal. The moment in the signing room where she said *no* before she said *yes*. I have waited three hundred years to see if it bred true." The eye regarded her, well-dark, bottomless. "It appears it has."

* * *

They climbed in silence for a while. Then Sefa said, without preamble: "He's right, you know. About the witnesses."

"Don't."

"Marisol can't read the old script but she's heard you read it aloud. The twins helped forge the diversion entries. Pell carried the notebook to the shrine. I—" Her beads clicked once, twice, resumed. "I prayed to them, August. Before any of this. They know my voice. If they're keeping lists of who remembers, mine is a name with a return address."

August stopped on the stair. Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the Concord's bells were still ringing—not the deadline peal anymore but something faster, arrhythmic, the sound of an institution clearing its throat.

"Then we don't run the numbers their way," she said. "We change the terms."

"With what leverage? We have a dying dragon, a bag of forged seals, and a choir of people who owe the guild one impossible thing each."

"That's not nothing." August heard her own voice go quiet and hard, the voice she used at collections, except she was collecting from the universe now. "Impossible things are the only currency the gods respect. They trade in answered prayers—we've seen the receipts. And every person in that choir owes a debt that has never once been called in. Debts that old accrue interest, Sefa. Three hundred years of interest on nine impossible things." She started climbing again, faster. "Nobody's ever asked the debtors to pay. Nobody's ever asked what happens when they all pay at once."

Sefa caught up, breathing hard. "That's either brilliant or blasphemous."

"In my experience those are frequently the same entry."

* * *

The cistern stair let them out behind a tannery near the Lamb's Quarter, into streets gone strange with curfew. Concord lanterns hung at every corner—white flames that burned without warmth, and August made herself look at them properly this time, made herself read the light the way she read ink. Cold fire. Dragon-fire, filtered through divine plumbing, burning in the streets of the city that stole it.

Every lantern was a receipt. The whole city was a ledger of the crime, lit from within.

"There," Sefa said.

Pell stood in the tannery doorway, and even at thirty paces August could read him wrong—shoulders wrong, hands wrong, the particular stillness of a man holding news the way you hold a hot pan.

"Pell?"

"It's Corvane." He swallowed. "He's cleared out his office. Took nothing but one box—small, locked, old. Left this on the master's desk." He held out a folded paper, guild-sealed, and August's fingers knew the weight of it before her eyes finished reading the superscription:

*For the junior keeper. Alone.*

"He's asking for me."

"He's asking for you at the old counting-house on Candle Lane. Midnight." Pell hesitated. "August—it's midnight in twenty minutes. And there are Concord agents watching the lane already. Two of them. I counted."

Of course he did, she thought. Of course he counted.

Sefa touched her elbow. "It could be a trap. He voted yes. He admitted he's known about your bloodline since the day he hired you. Every reason you have to trust him is a reason he's given you *on purpose*."

August unfolded the note. One line, in the master's precise, upright hand—the handwriting of a man who had never once rounded a number in his life:

*You deserve the whole truth before they take the rest. Come alone. Bring nothing you cannot burn.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it, and held its corner to the white glow of a Concord lantern, and watched Corvane's thirty years of careful penmanship curl and blacken and drift upward with the ash.

"Burn everything," she said, "except the truth. That's what he taught me." She tucked the last unburned scrap into her pocket—just enough of the seal to prove the summons real—and turned toward Candle Lane. "Come on. Both of you. He said alone."

"And?" Pell asked.

"And I've spent my whole life following the guild's rules." The bells above were ringing faster now, and beneath her feet, far down, she thought she felt something vast shift in its sleep. "Time someone audited them."

Midnight was twelve minutes away, and the men in the lane were already counting doors.
