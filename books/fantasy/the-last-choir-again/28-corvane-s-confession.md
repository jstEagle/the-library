## 28. Corvane's Confession

The Concord's auditors had taken the east wing at dusk—boots on marble, wax seals cracking against doors that hadn't opened in a century—and the guildhall had gone quiet in the particular way of a house where something is about to be decided.

August found Corvane where she knew he would be: the old counting room, the one with the leaded windows and the scale that weighed nothing anymore because no one trusted weights. He sat behind the long desk with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, and for once he did not look like Master Corvane of the Guild of Impossible Things. He looked like an old man doing sums he already knew the answer to.

"You shouldn't be here," he said without looking up.

"Neither should you."

"I'm always here." He set down his pen. "That's rather the problem, isn't it."

She had come armed. She'd rehearsed it on the stairs—the accusations, ordered by weight, the way she'd order columns: *You hired me knowing. You put me in the vault. You let me find my grandmother's name. You voted yes.* She had imagined his face going through stages, denial to anger to collapse, like a building coming down floor by floor.

Instead he said, "Sit down, August," and pulled out the chair across from him with his foot, the way he used to when she was new and still afraid of the furniture.

She didn't sit. "I want to know why."

"Which why? There are so many." He rubbed his eyes. When he took his hands away, he looked older than he had that morning, older than he had last week, as if the vote had cost him years the way a bad entry costs a ledger its balance. "Why I hired you. Why I taught you. Why I voted the way I did. Pick one and I'll answer it honestly. It will probably be the only honest hour this guild has left."

"Why did you vote yes." Her voice came out flat. Good. Flat was safe. "You raised your hand. Four hands went up and mine wasn't one of them and yours was, and I have spent two days trying to make the numbers work, Corvane, and they don't. You taught me everything I know about making numbers work, and they don't."

"They do," he said quietly. "You just don't like what they add up to."

"Then show me."

He stood. That surprised her; she'd expected him to stay behind the desk, behind the wood, behind the institution. But he came around it slowly, joints complaining, and crossed to the wall where the guild's founding charters hung in frames under glass—nine of them, one for each seat, each signed by a founder whose name was now a title. He stopped in front of the first one.

"Do you know what the Guild of Impossible Things was, before it was this?" He tapped the glass. "It was nine people in a cellar during the Ash Famine, keeping a list. That's all. A list of things people needed that couldn't be bought, begged, or prayed into being. A widow needed her husband's ghost to stop following her. A boy needed rain for a field that had been salted. Impossible things. And the nine of them—grandmother's generation, your grandmother among them—they couldn't *do* any of it. They were clerks. They were nobody. So they made themselves useful the only way nobodies can."

"Bookkeeping."

"The management of what people owe," he said. "Yes. And it worked. For sixty years it worked beautifully, and then the gods got hungry, and the founders were faced with a choice that has sat in that sealed vault ever since like a stone in a shoe." He turned to look at her. "They could let the city burn while the gods fought over the last of the dragon-fire. Or they could pay."

"Pay with Vaskarath's fire. Stolen."

"Bought," Corvane said. "Paid for. There is a difference, August, and the difference is the whole history of civilization. They didn't sneak in and take it. They drew up terms. They negotiated. Your grandmother sat across from a creature older than the mountains and haggled, and what she bought with the bargain was time. Two hundred years of time. Every prayer answered since, every miracle logged in our books, every impossible thing this guild has ever made possible—it ran on fire we purchased with a promise."

"A promise someone else would keep."

"Yes." He didn't flinch from it. "A promise written in blood, guaranteed by a bloodline, payable by a person who wouldn't be born for another hundred years. Your grandmother signed it knowing that. Knowing exactly that. I've read her marginalia. She wrote, in her own hand, next to the guarantee clause: *Let it fall to whoever comes after. They will be stronger than us, because they will know.*"

"She thought she was protecting them?"

"She thought she was *arming* them." Corvane's voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word. "August. Sit down. Please."

This time she sat. Not because he asked—because her legs had opinions of their own.

He lowered himself back into his chair and folded his hands, and for a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the shutters and, far below, the muffled industry of the Concord's auditors cataloguing a century of secrets.

"When you were six months in," he said, "you found a rounding error in the Meridian account. Fourteen coppers, misallocated across eleven years. Nobody would ever have caught it. Nobody would ever have cared. You stayed three nights running until you'd traced every copper to its origin, and then you brought it to me at seven in the morning with ink on your fingers and this—" he gestured vaguely at her entire face "—this expression, like you'd personally rescued a child from a well. Do you remember what I told you?"

"You said accuracy was a form of mercy."

"I said that because it's true, and because I wanted you to stay." He exhaled. "I hired you off a list of candidates forty names long. I interviewed twelve. I chose the girl with no family, no connections, no patron, and a gift for numbers that bordered on the pathological, and everyone assumed it was because she was cheap and grateful. And I let them assume it. Because the truth was that I'd read the sealed account, all of it, including the guarantee clause, and I'd done what you do—I'd run the arithmetic backward from the debt to the debtor—and the arithmetic pointed at a surname. Okafor. And then I waited eleven years for an Okafor to walk through my door looking for work."

The room tilted. August gripped the edge of the desk. "Eleven years."

"I could have come to you. I could have knocked on your landlady's door and said, good news, you're collateral. What do you think would have happened? You'd have run. Or worse—you'd have stayed, and hated the guild, and poisoned every ledger you touched out of spite, and the debt would have come due anyway with no one willing to manage it. No. The only way to keep you was to make you love this place first. Make the guild your family before you learned it was your cage." His mouth twisted. "I'm not proud of it. I'm telling you how it was done, not defending how it was done."

"You put me in the vault."

"I put you in the vault because the Ember Audit was coming and the Concord was sniffing around and I needed to know if the account was intact. And because—" he paused, and she watched him decide, deliberately, to stop lying, "—because part of me hoped you'd find it. Hoped you'd read your grandmother's name and understand that she wasn't a victim. She was an architect. That the family you never met left you the most important job in the world, and all I had to do was hand you the key and get out of the way."

"And the vote." Her voice was very steady. She was distantly proud of that. "Explain the vote."

And here Corvane finally looked tired—not old, tired, the tiredness of a man who has carried a number too heavy for too long—and he said:

"The vote was arithmetic. Nothing else. Listen to me, because this is the part you've refused to calculate. The Concord arrives tomorrow. Behind the Concord stand the gods, and the gods are starving, and a starving god does not negotiate, it *consumes*. If we surrender the guarantor—if we give them you—the debt is settled peacefully. The fire keeps flowing. The city keeps standing. Nine hundred thousand people go on waking up, buying bread, complaining about the weather, living lives they don't know were purchased. One life against nine hundred thousand. You've balanced harder ledgers than that, August. Don't tell me the math is wrong. Tell me you hate the math. Those are different things."

"It's wrong," she said.

"It isn't."

"It's wrong because of what it counts." She stood up now, and the chair scraped back, and she heard her own voice rise and didn't care. "Your sum has one number on one side and a big number on the other side, and you think that makes it clean. But the big number isn't real, Corvane. It's an abstraction. Nine hundred thousand people is not a thing anyone can hold. It's a column. And you've spent your whole life learning to respect columns more than faces, because faces are messy and columns balance. But I've read the cold cathedral records. I know the gods can starve quietly. I know there's a third path your arithmetic doesn't have a column for, because you never thought to look for one. You voted yes because surrendering me was the *cheapest* option, and cheap is not the same as right, and a guild built on managing debts should know better than anyone that the cheapest payment is almost never the honest one."

Corvane was silent.

"The founders bought time," she went on, quieter now, because the fury was burning down to something steadier underneath. "Two hundred years of it. And what did this guild do with the time? It hid. It filed. It kept the secret in a vault and trained bookkeepers and called itself necessary. It had two hundred years to find another way—to ask Vaskarath, to study the gods, to prepare for the day the bill came due—and instead it spent the inheritance hoping the day would never come. My grandmother didn't arm me, whatever her margins say. She was abandoned by everyone who came after her. Including you. Especially you. You knew for eleven years, and you spent those eleven years teaching me rounding errors."

"I taught you everything else too," Corvane said. "Everything I had."

"And it nearly worked." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "That's the worst part. It nearly worked. I would have walked into that square tomorrow and offered myself up if I hadn't found Sefa, if I hadn't found the choir, if I hadn't learned that there are people in this city worth more than the whole balance sheet. You built my loyalty to the institution because you needed it. Well, congratulations, Master Corvane. It transferred."

"To the dragon," he said.

"To the people," she said. "Vaskarath included. He's owed two hundred years of interest, and unlike you, I intend to pay it."

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she had never seen him do in eleven years. He reached beneath the blotter on the desk and drew out a single sheet of paper, folded twice, worn soft at the creases, and laid it between them without unfolding it.

"What is that?"

"My resignation," he said. "Written the night of the vote. After I raised my hand. I sat here until dawn trying to make the arithmetic feel clean, and it wouldn't, and I realized that a man who votes yes and then can't sleep has answered his own question and is simply refusing to read it." He pushed it an inch toward her. "I haven't submitted it. Cowardice has excellent posture; it looks just like prudence. But I want you to know it exists. And I want to know what you're going to do, because the auditors finish the east wing by morning, and when they reach the sealed vault—and they will reach the sealed vault—everything ends one way or another."

August looked at the folded paper. At the man who had been her map of how the world worked, revealed as a man who had known the coordinates of her cage before she was born and called it kindness.

"You could help," she said. "There's a third path. Rationed fire, freely given. Vaskarath has agreed to consider it. The cold cathedral proves the gods can fade gently. All of it falls apart tomorrow unless someone who knows the guild's charter law inside and out stands up in front of the Concord and argues the legal case—which no one alive knows better than you."

"You're asking me to burn down the thing I gave my life to."

"I'm asking you to do what the founders should have done with their two hundred years." She picked up the resignation letter and held it out to him. "Choose. But choose tonight. Because tomorrow I open the sealed account in front of the Concord, and I read my grandmother's crime and this guild's crime aloud, and I would rather do it with you beside me than over your objection. Either way, Corvane, it happens."

He took the letter. His hand shook—she had never seen his hand shake—and he looked at it as though it were an entry in a ledger he'd been avoiding for years.

Then, from somewhere above them, came a sound that made both their heads turn: the great front bell of the guildhall, ringing once, twice, three times—the emergency pattern, unused in forty years—and beneath it, footsteps on the stairs, many boots, moving fast, and Sefa's voice calling her name with a crack of panic in it that August had never heard before.

"The youngest god," Sefa shouted through the door, before anyone could knock. "It's not waiting for the audit. It's already here."
