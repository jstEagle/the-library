## 29. The Ledger Burns Both Ways

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time August understood what she was holding.

She sat in the cold cathedral's vestry with the founding contract open across her knees, and she did what she always did when the world became too large: she ran the numbers. It was the only prayer she'd ever been taught, and unlike the ones Sefa whispered, hers had never once gone unanswered.

*Item one,* she wrote in the margin of her own notebook. *The debt of fire owed to Vaskarath is secured against this contract. The contract is the debt. Not a record of it — the thing itself. Burn the paper, void the obligation.*

That part was simple. That part was almost beautiful. She could see it already: the contract curling in a brazier, the ink lifting off the page like a flock of black birds, two hundred years of stolen fire suddenly belonging to no one, claimed by no one. The gods would have no claim. The Concord would have no evidence. Vaskarath would be free of them all.

*Item two.*

Her pen hovered.

She made herself write it anyway, because arithmetic that stops at the answer you want isn't arithmetic. It's just wishing with better posture.

*Item two: the founding contract is also the guild's charter.*

She stared at the words until they stopped looking like words.

Of course it was. She'd known it the way you know the load-bearing wall in your own house — without ever thinking about it, because thinking about it meant imagining the house coming down. The Guild of Impossible Things had no other founding document. Every charter, every license, every writ of protection the guild enjoyed flowed from that single sheet of vellum, signed by nine founders and guaranteed in blood by one of them. Her grandmother. The Concord of Faiths recognized the guild because the charter recognized the guild. The city recognized it. The banks, the courts, the wardens who looked the other way when debtors walked free after paying their impossible things — all of it, every thread of safety, anchored to that page.

Burn the contract, and the debt died.

Burn the contract, and so did everything else.

August set down her pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations.

* * *

Sefa found her there an hour later, sitting exactly where she'd been, the contract untouched, the candle dead, the vestry lit only by the grey light leaking through a broken window shaped like a saint nobody remembered.

"You've figured it out," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. She lowered herself onto the pew across from August, her defrocked priest's robes rustling like dry leaves. "You have the face. The face my brother used to make when he balanced the temple accounts and realized the tithe wasn't going to cover winter."

"The charter," August said. Her voice came out flat as a ledger line. "It's the same page. The crime and the protection. They're on the same piece of vellum, Sefa. Two hundred years ago someone wrote 'we stole a dragon's fire' and 'this guild shall be protected by law and faith' on the same sheet, and everyone since has been too afraid or too complicit to notice that you can't destroy one without destroying the other."

"And if you burn it?"

"Then the debt dies. The gods lose their legal claim — their *any* claim. Vaskarath goes free." She swallowed. "And the guild dissolves. Instantly. Completely. Every debtor currently under our protection becomes an ordinary person owing ordinary debts to ordinary creditors. The choir — Marta, the twins, old Pellam with his forty years of compounded interest — they lose the walls around them. The Concord won't just audit us anymore. Without a charter we're not an institution. We're *evidence*. Loose evidence, with names."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Outside, somewhere in the drowned dark of the undercity, water dripped its slow count.

"So," she said finally. "One dragon, freed. Nine hundred debtors, exposed. One guild, ended." She looked up. "You're doing the math where you come out the villain either way."

"I'm doing the math where there *is* no way out. Those are different."

"No." Sefa leaned forward, and in the grey light her face was fierce and tired and kind all at once. "Different math says: some people survive this and some don't, and you get to choose which. Same math says there's no version where nobody bleeds. You keep pretending those are the same sum because if they're the same, you don't have to choose. Choosing is the part that costs."

August wanted to argue. She was very good at arguing with ledgers; ledgers couldn't look at you like that.

Instead she said, "Corvane would hand me over. He said so with four raised hands behind him. And he'd be right by his own accounting — sacrifice the guarantor, preserve the institution, save nine hundred people from the Concord's reprisal. That's the arithmetic the guild has run for two centuries. One bloodline against everyone else. My grandmother did the same sum in reverse, didn't she? She put herself forward as collateral so the others could live. Maybe it runs in the family. Maybe the only honest thing I can do is finish the entry she started."

"That's not finishing her entry. That's letting Corvane write yours."

"It's *arithmetic*, Sefa."

"It's fear wearing arithmetic's coat." Sefa stood, restless, pacing the width of the vestry in three steps and back. "You know what I did before I was defrocked? I kept the prayer rolls. Hundreds of names, hundreds of requests, and I decided which ones got read aloud and which went in the drawer. I told myself it was triage. God can't hear everything; someone must ration the listening." She stopped. "Then my brother died, and I prayed the drawer-prayers, the ones I'd never read aloud, and no one answered, and I understood that I hadn't been rationing anything. I'd been deciding who mattered. Same as Corvane. Same as the Concord. Same as every institution that ever called a person a line item."

August looked down at the contract. At her grandmother's signature — *Ilse Okafor*, small and precise, the letters of a woman who had also once believed she was doing arithmetic.

"If I burn it," August said slowly, "I need the choir ready before the flame catches. Because the moment the charter dies, the Concord will know, and the wardens will know, and every creditor in this city will know. There'll be maybe an hour. Perhaps less. An hour where nine hundred people learn they're unprotected."

"Then we spend the hour well."

"And there's something else." August turned back to the first page, to the clause she'd read a dozen times and only now truly heard. "'This charter stands while the debt stands. When the debt is discharged — by payment, forgiveness, or destruction of instrument — the fellowship formed thereby is dissolved, and its members are released unto the common law.'" She tapped the words. "*Released unto the common law.* Do you understand? It's not just punishment waiting outside. It's also — " she hesitated, embarrassed by the hope even as she said it — "it means no one is bound. Not the debtors. Not me. If the guild dies, then Corvane can't vote on me again. No one can. The whole machinery that priced me last week simply stops existing."

Sefa smiled, slow and crooked. "So burning it doesn't just take away your protections. It takes away their permission."

"Yes."

"And you're only realizing this now?"

"I'm a ledger-keeper," August said. "We find the answers. We're rarely told what they mean."

* * *

They brought the news to the choir that evening, in the flooded crypt where the debtors gathered now, because the guildhall belonged to watchers and the cathedral belonged to a dead god and the crypt, at least, belonged to no one.

There were eleven of them. Marta the baker, whose impossible thing was a loaf that never staled, owed and unpaid these six years. The twins Osk and Ossa, who between them owed a perfect memory. Old Pellam, bent double under forty years of interest. The widow Cett, who owed a song she'd promised to a river. And the rest — quiet people, mostly, people the city had priced long before the guild ever wrote their names down.

August stood on the crypt's flooded step, the contract wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and told them plainly. All of it. The theft. The charter. The choice. She had learned that much from the Ember Audit: these people had been lied to by institutions their whole lives, and the least she owed them was the unrounded number.

"So that's the sum," she finished. "I burn this, and the debt dies, and the dragon lives, and the gods lose their claim. And the guild ends. Your protections end. Within the hour, the Concord comes for all of us, and there is no charter, no guildhall, no Corvane to negotiate terms. You'd be released unto the common law. Which is another way of saying: released to the wolves, together, with nothing between you and them but each other."

Silence. Water dripped. Somewhere above, the city went about its evening, unaware that its oldest institution was standing in a crypt asking permission to die.

Marta spoke first. "What happens to the loaf?" she asked. "My impossible thing. If the guild ends, does my debt transfer? Does some bank inherit it?"

"No. The debts aren't held by the guild — they're held *by the charter*. When it burns, they don't transfer. They end. Unpaid, unforgiven, just... over. Like a note sung into a room after everyone's left."

Osk frowned. "Is that legal?"

"Nothing about us has been legal since 1741," said Ossa.

Pellam raised his head. He was the oldest of them, older than Corvane, older perhaps than the dust, and when he spoke his voice was like paper. "I've owed my impossible thing for forty years. Forty years I've walked under the guild's roof, safe and small and owned. You're asking do I mind losing the roof." He laughed, a dry rustle. "Girl, I've been waiting my whole life for someone to ask me to leave it."

"But the Concord—" began the widow Cett.

"The Concord hunted me when I still *had* the charter," said Sefa. "Protection is a room. It keeps out weather and it keeps in prisoners. I've lived both ways. Give me the wolves and my own two feet."

August felt something loosen in her chest — not relief, nothing so clean, but the specific loosening of a knot pulled to its final turn. She had expected persuasion. Bargaining. Fear. Instead she got Pellam laughing and the twins finishing each other's objections and Marta already asking practical questions about where nine hundred debtors would sleep.

They were not choosing her side. She understood that with sudden clarity. They were choosing the version of the world where debts could simply *end* — where a person was not a running balance. She was just the one holding the match.

"One more thing," she said. "Once it burns, it can't be undone. No draft, no copy, no reconstruction. I checked. The contract is unique — the vault fire of '09 saw to that. What I'm proposing is the single most irreversible entry in the history of accounting."

"Good," said Pellam.

"*Good?*"

"Every protection I ever had," the old man said, "came with a chain attached. Burn the whole ledger, girl. Protections, chains, and all. Let's find out what we are when we owe nothing to anyone."

* * *

She almost did it that night.

She stood alone in the cold cathedral afterward, the others gone, the contract unwrapped on the altar of the god who had starved rather than fight, and she held the candle, and her hand did not shake, and that frightened her more than any trembling could have.

Because here was the truth she hadn't said aloud in the crypt: she wanted to burn it. Had wanted it since the vault, since the handwriting that looked like her own, since the moment she learned her name was collateral. Burning it would be clean. It would make her righteous instead of ruined, decisive instead of cornered. Every terrible thing that followed — the Concord's hounds, the creditors, the scattering of nine hundred frightened people — would be the *price of principle*, and prices absolve. She knew that trick. She'd seen Corvane use it. Sacrifice laundered into meaning.

And that was exactly why she waited.

Because the choice wasn't real yet. Not while it was still only hers to make, made in a dead god's house with no one watching. A choice crystallized, her grandmother's ghost seemed to say from the margin of her memory, only when it costs you something you cannot spin.

There was also the matter of Vaskarath. The contract wasn't only a cage for the guild — it was the last proof of what had been done to him. Burn it, and the crime itself became unspeakable, unverifiable, a story with no receipt. The dragon would be free, yes. But free of the record too. Free to be disbelieved forever.

She needed to ask him. Not permission — she was done asking permission of anyone's hunger, divine or otherwise. But the debt was his. The fire was his. If the instrument of his theft was going to become smoke, he deserved to stand in the room when it happened. Or to stop it. She genuinely did not know which he would choose, and that not-knowing felt, for the first time in weeks, like respect.

At dawn she went down to the shrine beneath the city, where the dying flame guttered in its cradle of stone, and the great serpent lifted his head, ancient and exhausted, eyes like banked coals.

"You've done the sums," Vaskarath said. It was not a question. Dragons, she was learning, could smell arithmetic the way sharks smelled blood.

"I've done the sums."

"And?"

"Burning the contract voids the debt. It also kills the charter. Everything your theft paid for, everything built on the price you were never paid — gone in the same flame. The guild. The protections. The record of the crime itself." She met his coal-dark eyes. "I can give you freedom, or I can give you justice. This page is the only place in the world where both exist, and it can't be split."

The old serpent was silent a long time. The flame between them guttered low, rose, guttered again, keeping its stubborn time.

"When your grandmother signed," he said at last, "she wept. Did you know that? She signed anyway, and she wept, and I have hated her gently for two hundred years, because the alternative was hating her the way I hate the gods, and she was the only one of them who ever cried." His head lowered until his breath stirred August's hair, warm as a hearth. "Burn it, little keeper. Burn the debt and the charter and the shame together. Justice is a story the victors keep receipts for. I have had enough of stories. I would rather have the morning."

August closed her eyes. When she opened them, the choice was no longer a fork in the road. It was a door, and she was already walking through it, and behind her she could hear, faint and far above, the bells of the Concord beginning to ring.

They had found the crypt empty.

The hour was spent. The wolves were moving.

She turned back toward the stairs, the contract tight against her chest, and started to climb — toward the light, toward the choir, toward the square where in three days the Ember Audit would convene and she would burn two hundred years of sin in front of everyone the city had ever taught to kneel.

If the choir could get her there.
