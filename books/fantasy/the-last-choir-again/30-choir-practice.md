## 30. Choir Practice

The cold cathedral had no pews left worth sitting on, so the debtors sat on the floor, on broken altar steps, on the collapsed ribs of an organ that hadn't spoken in two hundred years. Someone had found candles. Of course someone had found candles; every person August had collected over the past weeks was the kind of person who could find candles in a building that had been stripped to its bones.

Nine of them. She counted twice, because counting was the one prayer she trusted.

Nine debtors, each owing the Guild of Impossible Things exactly one impossible thing, each of them now technically harboring a fugitive from that same guild, which meant by morning they would owe considerably more.

"Before anyone says anything," August began, "I want to be clear about what this is."

"It's a choir practice," said Sefa, cross-legged on the altar step, tearing bread she'd charmed out of a baker with a smile and a lie about a funeral. "You called it that. We're keeping it."

"I called it that as a joke."

"You don't joke," Sefa said. "You itemize. So when you call something a joke, it's load-bearing."

A few of them laughed. The sound bounced strangely off the dead god's ceiling—this cathedral belonged to a deity who had starved first, quietly, before anyone thought to feed the others, and the architecture still had the acoustics of absence. August felt the laugh pass through her like coin through a counting house: weighed, logged, kept.

She laid the founding contract on the floor between them. The old vellum caught the candlelight and threw it back wrong, the way old debts do.

"This is the guild's founding crime," she said. "This is the reason the gods are coming. And if we burn it—and I mean *we*, because I can't do it alone—the charter dissolves. Every protection the guild grants goes with it. Sanctuary status. Debt forgiveness clauses. The legal fiction that makes you people untouchable." She looked around at their faces. "You'd be free of your debts because there would be nothing left to owe them *to*. But you'd also be free of everything else."

Silence. The good kind, the kind where people were actually thinking instead of waiting to talk.

Then Pellam, the forger with the burned hands, said: "What protections?"

August blinked. "The—I just listed them."

"No, I heard you list them." Pellam turned his scarred palms up. "I've been a guild debtor for eleven years. In eleven years, the sanctuary clause has protected me from exactly nothing. The Concord raided my shop twice. The city watch took my tools once. The guild sent me a letter both times explaining that my complaint had been *logged*." He smiled without warmth. "You're asking us to trade away a shield made of paper. I've been reading paper my whole life. Paper doesn't stop anything. People do."

*People do.* August wrote it down in her head, in the margin where she hid her own name.

* * *

They went around the circle, because that was how choirs worked—one voice at a time, until the parts learned each other.

Sefa went first, because Sefa always went first; defrocked priests made excellent first voices. "My impossible thing," she said, "was praying for rain in a city with no clouds. That's what I owed the guild—a miracle I couldn't perform, a debt they held over me since the day they defrocked me for trying." She touched the little brass rain-charm at her throat, the one her brother had given her, the one whose voice the gods had stolen and worn. "What I pay toward this: I know how the divine bureaucracy talks. When the Concord's auditors come, I can speak liturgy at them until their eyes glaze. It's a real skill. It's also the only weapon I have left that isn't grief."

Pellam: "Forgery. Obviously. Give me three days and I'll give the Concord a version of the guild's books so beautiful they'll weep. Give me one day and I'll give them something merely convincing." He paused. "One day, then. We don't have three."

Marrow, the ex-tollkeeper who owed the guild one bridge that had never been built: "I know every tunnel under this district better than the rats do. When the net tightens—and it will tighten, I've watched nets tighten my whole career—I move people. All of you. Out through ways the auditors haven't mapped because the maps were sold to them by men who never walked the tunnels at night."

Old Cassia, who owed one resurrection: "I have nothing practical." She said it plainly, without apology. "I'm seventy-one years old and my impossible thing is a boy who died forty years ago, and I have stopped expecting to pay it. But I can stand in a square and be seen standing. Old women who refuse to move are very hard to arrest. There's always a crowd within a minute, and crowds make the Concord nervous. Nervous institutions make mistakes. I am, professionally speaking, a mistake-generator."

The twins, Fen and Fennec, who jointly owed one prophecy: "We can't see the future," Fen said.

"We can *perform* seeing the future," Fennec said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that ours come true more often."

"And what we see," Fen finished, together, which was unsettling even after weeks of practice, "is fire in the ledger halls. Tomorrow night or the night after. We saw it before you told us about the burning. We're telling you now so you know: when it happens, it was already going to happen. You're not choosing destruction. You're choosing to be on time."

August's hands wanted to shake. She kept them flat on her knees, ledger-keeper's discipline, and moved to the next voice.

* * *

It took until well past midnight, and what struck her—not all at once but in slow accumulating weight, the way snowload breaks a roof—was that none of them asked what was in it for themselves.

She kept waiting for it. Eleven years in the guild had taught her that every account balanced, that every kindness carried an interest rate, that Master Corvane's warmth was a down payment on obedience. She had built her whole self on the arithmetic of exchange. And here sat nine people—ten, counting her—in a dead god's house, offering arson, forgery, smuggling, civil disobedience, and false prophecy toward a plan whose only guaranteed outcome was that they would all lose their legal existence by week's end.

"Why?" she finally asked. She hated how small it came out. "Why are you doing this? None of you knew Vaskarath. Most of you didn't know me a month ago. This plan costs you everything and pays you—"

"Pay," Sefa interrupted gently. "There it is. You can't help it, can you? Every kindness comes in and you immediately look for the invoice."

"Because there's always an invoice."

"Not with family," said Marrow.

The word landed in the cathedral and rang like the organ used to ring. August stared at him.

"I'm not—" she started.

"You are," said Cassia. "You gathered us. Badly, grudgingly, with a great deal of accounting language, but you gathered us. Do you know what the guild did with us debtors? It filed us. Alphabetically. You're the first person in eleven years who learned all our names and then used them in a sentence that wasn't about money."

"The first person," Pellam corrected, "who used our names in a sentence that wasn't about money *and then risked her life*."

"That's a lower bar than you think," said Fen.

"It's the only bar that matters," said Fennec.

August looked down at the founding contract, at her grandmother's signature dried into the vellum like a scar, and understood suddenly why her hands had been shaking since the vault. Not fear. Not quite. It was the tremor of a scale rebalancing—of a woman who had spent her whole life as a line item discovering she was a signatory.

"My grandmother signed this," she said. "She sold dragon-fire to buy the guild's survival, and my bloodline was the guarantee. Everything I am, on paper, is collateral." She looked up. "Tomorrow—or soon, before the Concord finishes chewing through the choir's forgeries—I'm going to stand in front of the Ember Audit and read this aloud. All of it. And then I'm going to write myself into the ledger as sole debtor and offer the gods rations instead of war. And some of them will say no. The youngest already has. So I need you to understand the shape of tomorrow: truth first, then fire, then a fight we might lose in front of the entire Concord of Faiths."

"Cheerful," said Marrow.

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Accuracy matters more than comfort."

"Say the ask," Sefa said. "You keep describing the weather. Say the ask."

August breathed. "Stand with me. Publicly. Where the auditors can see you. When I read the crime aloud, I need it to be a chorus, not a confession. One voice saying 'the guild stole from a dragon' is a madwoman. Nine voices is testimony. Ten—" she swallowed— "ten is a verdict."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Cassia stood up. It took her a while, and nobody helped her, because she clearly preferred it that way, and when she was upright she looked around the circle with the imperiousness of a woman who had once run a household of fourteen on a widow's pension.

"Well?" she said. "Are we a choir or an audience?"

Pellam stood. Then Marrow. Then the twins, rising in unison, which was worse somehow. Then the rest, one by one, until the only ones seated were August and Sefa, and Sefa put a hand on August's shoulder and pushed her gently upward too.

"There," Cassia said. "Motion carries. Unanimous. See how easy democracy is when everyone's already lost everything?"

* * *

They practiced.

That was the absurd, holy heart of it: they practiced. Sefa arranged them in the nave like sections—testimony here, witness there, the twins at the back for atmosphere. They rehearsed the reading. August read the founding contract's worst passages aloud until her voice stopped catching on her grandmother's name, and the choir learned their lines: *We heard it. We witnessed. We stand as guarantors of this truth.*

Pellam drilled them on what to do when the Concord's agents moved—where to look, what to hold, how to drop pamphlets of forged ledgers into six pockets in under a minute. Marrow walked them through the tunnel exits twice, tapping walls that sounded identical and weren't. Cassia taught them the old woman's art of looking immovable, which turned out to involve feet planted shoulder-width and an expression of profound personal disappointment.

And somewhere past the second hour, August realized she was laughing. Actually laughing, at something Fennec had said about the youngest god's hair, and the sound startled her so badly she stopped, and Sefa looked over and grinned and said, "Log it," and she laughed again.

Later, when the others had curled up among the fallen masonry to sleep—choirs need rest before performances—Sefa stayed awake beside her at the cold altar, sharing the last of the bread.

"You know what I paid tonight?" Sefa said quietly. "Toward my impossible thing."

"What?"

"When I prayed the second time, and the gods answered with my brother's voice—I spent part of it. Every time I use that voice, there's less of him left in whatever's wearing it. Tonight I gave the choir his laugh. Just the laugh. I figured if we're going to burn everything down, we should do it to the sound of something that was actually happy once." She chewed, swallowed. "He always laughed at bad plans. He'd have loved this one."

August didn't have words for that, so she did the only honest thing she knew: she opened her pocket ledger, the small one, the private one, and wrote it down. *Sefa. Paid: one laugh, her brother's. Received: everything.*

"What are you writing?" Sefa asked.

"The new kind of account," August said. "The kind we're going to keep after."

"After what?"

"After tomorrow. If there is one."

Sefa bumped her shoulder. "There'll be one. Might be shorter than advertised, but there'll be one."

* * *

Near dawn, the twins woke screaming in unison, and the whole cathedral came upright with knives and candlesticks and in Cassia's case a hatpin of frankly alarming length.

Fen and Fennec knelt on the stone floor, hands pressed flat, eyes white to the rim, speaking in the doubled voice they only used when the future leaned close:

*"The audit moves. Not tomorrow. Today. Dawn bell. They found the forged trail—they found it hours ago—Corvane showed them where to look—"*

The vision broke. The twins sagged, caught by Marrow, breathing hard.

August was already moving, gathering the contract, the ledgers, the cold-cathedral records proving gods could starve gently. Her hands were steady now. Whatever happened next, the trembling was done; a scale, once settled, does not shake.

"Dawn bell," she said. "How long?"

Outside, far above the undercity, through grates and gutters and a hundred feet of buried stone, the first bell of morning began to toll.

"Now," said Sefa. "It's now."

August looked around at her choir—her impossible, indebted, magnificent choir—and raised her voice the way she imagined her grandmother must have raised hers, once, before the signing.

"Places, everyone. We sing today."
