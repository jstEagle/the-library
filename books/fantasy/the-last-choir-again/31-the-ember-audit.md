## 31. The Ember Audit

They came at dawn, because institutions always do.

August stood at the guild's great window and watched the Concord of Faiths arrive in the square below—nine carriages, black-lacquered, each drawn by horses that had been blessed so many times they no longer spooked at anything, which August had always thought was a kind of cruelty. Auditors stepped down in their grey robes, their ledgers chained to their belts, their faces arranged into expressions of perfect procedural neutrality. Behind them, worse, walked the divine agents: six figures in robes the color of banked coals, faces veiled in gauze that shimmered faintly, like heat off a stove.

They were starving. She could see it even from three floors up, the way the veils fluttered inward, as if the air itself was being pulled into their mouths.

"August." Corvane's voice behind her, flat and careful. "The hall is prepared. Nine chairs. Six of us, as it happens."

"Two abstentions last week," she said. "I suppose they couldn't face counting me again."

"Master Ellery is ill. Master Doss has resigned." He paused. "You should know that I intend to speak on your behalf."

"I know." She turned from the window. "That's what frightens me. Your advocacy has a way of making things worse."

For a moment something moved behind his eyes—hurt, or its well-rehearsed imitation—and then it was gone, folded away like a page in a ledger. "The auditors will call the roll. Then they will demand the accounts. All of them." He let the word sit between them. *All.*

"I know what I'm doing, Corvane."

"That," he said, "is what I told them about you, eleven years ago, when I hired a girl with ink on her fingers and a name I recognized. So I suppose one of us is consistent."

He left her alone with the window and the square and the nine black carriages, and August did the last arithmetic of her old life: one founding contract, folded against her ribs beneath her coat. One choir scattered through the building, each of them carrying their forged papers and their true loyalties. One dragon under the city, old and hungry and, as of last night, awake.

One chance. She had never liked odds that came in single digits. She'd spent her whole career making sure the guild's never had to.

She went down to the hall.

* * *

The Ember Audit was held, by ancient charter, in the guild's counting hall—a room built to intimidate, all black oak and brass fittings, with the Guild of Impossible Things' motto carved over the hearth: *EVERYTHING IS OWED.* The Concord's auditors sat at a long table that had been carried in for the occasion, and they made the room feel smaller, the way a scale makes a room feel smaller, everything in it suddenly subject to weighing.

The senior auditor was a woman named Preceptor Halloway, and she was not what August expected. No cruelty, no zealotry. Just a tired, precise face and hands that moved through documents the way a surgeon's move through a body—without sentiment, because sentiment killed patients.

"Roll of the guild," Halloway said. "State your names and offices for the record."

"Master Corvane, First Chair."

"Master Ives, Keeper of Seals."

"Master Quillon, Assessor of Miracles."

"Master Fen, Warden of Debts."

"Apprentice Sefa, formerly of the Order of the Weeping Saint." Sefa's voice only shook a little. August, standing at the end of the row, felt the tremor travel down the line like a current and steadied her own spine against it.

"Junior Ledger-Keeper August Okafor."

Halloway's pen stopped. "Okafor." She looked up for the first time. "The name appears in our preliminary filings."

"Many names do," August said. "It's a large city."

Something almost like respect crossed the auditor's face, there and gone. "Let us begin. The Concord has received testimony—anonymous, but corroborated—that the Guild of Impossible Things maintains a sealed account predating its charter. That it has concealed this account from every audit in the guild's history. That the account concerns a debt of fire." She folded her hands. "The Concord does not care about your debts, Master Corvane. The Concord cares about fire. As I'm sure you understand, fire has been... in the news."

The coal-robed agents did not move. But the veils fluttered.

"The guild," Corvane said, "cooperates fully with the Concord. We have produced every account in our keeping."

"Every account *in your keeping*," Halloway repeated. "Yes. That's the phrase that brought us here."

The silence stretched. August watched Corvane's hands, folded on the table, and saw the knuckles whiten by a single degree—the smallest entry, the one no one but a ledger-keeper would ever read.

It was time.

* * *

"Preceptor." August stepped forward, out of the row. "If I may. The guild's charter requires that a sealed account be opened only by the officer who holds the key and in the presence of—" she let herself smile, small and procedural, "—a quorum. We have a quorum. We have the key. I hold it."

"You?" Corvane said, and it was beautifully done, the shock in his voice, the betrayal, the wounded institutional dignity. He had voted to hand her to the gods six days ago and he still managed to sound like a man watching a trusted apprentice steal from the till. August had to admire it even as it curdled her stomach. "August, that account is not—"

"Is not mine to withhold," she said. "You taught me that. Everything is owed. Isn't that what it says over the hearth?"

She walked to the vault door at the back of the hall. The choir had done their work overnight: the lock's wards re-keyed, the paper trail rewritten in six hands so that the sealed account's custody had passed, legally, impeccably, from Corvane to her. Fen had wept while forging the transfer, and then laughed, and then wept again. *This is the most impossible thing I'll ever do,* he'd said, *and I owe one, so it counts.*

The vault breathed cold when it opened. Inside, on its shelf, the founding contract waited—wrapped in oilcloth, older than the guild, older than the city's walls. August took it in both hands and carried it to the center of the hall, and the coal-robed agents turned their veiled faces to follow it the way sunflowers follow the sun.

"What is that," said one of them. Not a question. A recognition. A voice like ash settling.

"The account," August said, "you've come to audit."

She unwrapped the oilcloth. The contract unfurled across the table, and the room changed. She felt it change—felt the air pressure shift, the candle flames stand up straight, the auditors lean back as if the paper were hot. Which, in a sense, it was. It had been drinking heat from the world for two hundred years. That was what the fire-debt was. That was what it had always been: a slow sip, taken by gods, from a dragon, with the guild as the straw.

"Read it," said the ash voice. "Read it aloud. We have waited two hundred years to hear it in a human voice."

"Preceptor," Corvane started, "this document is—"

"Two hundred years," the agent said, and now the veil turned toward Corvane, and every candle in the room guttered. "Sit down, straw."

Corvane sat.

August put her finger at the top of the page. Her hands were steady. She had rehearsed this in the cold cathedral, alone, at three in the morning, until the words lost their power to shake her—and then, this morning, in the privy, where she'd been quietly sick, and the words had gotten their power back, and she'd decided that was fine. Fear was just the body's way of entering a figure twice, checking it against itself.

"Contract of Foundation," she read, "between the undersigned, being the founders of the Guild of Impossible Things, and Vaskarath, Last of the Fireborn, hereinafter the Source."

The name went through the room like a struck bell. One of the auditors made a small involuntary sound.

"Article One. The Source grants unto the founders one measure of dragon-fire, freely drawn, sufficient to kindle the divine and sustain the gods of this city against the Long Dark."

"Article Two. In exchange, the founders covenant that the fire shall be replenished—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the next words, and she let it catch, because the room needed to hear a human voice catch, "—from the Source's own body, by consent given under duress, the said duress being the siege of the city and the death of all the Source's kin."

"Article Three." Here it was. The page her grandmother had signed. "As surety for the covenant, the founders pledge a bloodline. The descendants of—" and she read the name. Her grandmother's name. Her own name was in it, two hundred years downstream, a figure in a margin, accruing.

She looked up.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," she said, "was founded on a theft. We did not buy our survival. We stole it. We took fire from a starving dragon while her family burned, and when she asked for fair terms we gave her a contract instead, and when she asked for a guarantee we gave her my grandmother, and when my grandmother died we kept the arrangement and called it accounting, and we hid it in a sealed vault, and we audited everyone else's sins for two hundred years while never once entering our own." She turned the contract so the auditors could see the signature. "This is the founding crime of this guild. I am the collateral. And the gods you serve, Preceptor—the gods whose agents stand behind you in their nice red robes—have been feeding on stolen fire ever since. Every answered prayer in this city for two centuries has been paid for out of the body of the last dragon alive. That is the account. That is the audit. Everything is owed. Read it over the door on your way out."

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. August stood in the middle of it, chest heaving, and watched truth do what truth does when you finally stop managing it: it made the room honest.

Preceptor Halloway looked at the signature for a long time. Then at Corvane. Then at August.

"You understand," she said quietly, "what you've just done. This document, authenticated, dissolves the guild's charter. Every protection, every license, every debt we're empowered to forgive—it all burns with it. You've read your own institution out of existence."

"I know."

"And the divine claim on the guarantor—"

"Is now public knowledge," August said. "Which means it can be negotiated in public. That was the point. You can't disappear a girl quietly when nine auditors and a preceptor have watched her read her own price aloud. I've entered myself into the record, Preceptor. I can't be quietly written out."

Halloway's mouth twitched—almost a smile, filed away as soon as it appeared. "You're using procedure as a weapon."

"I'm a ledger-keeper," August said. "It's the only weapon I was issued."

One of the coal-robed agents laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry and vast, like a fire dying in an empty house, and all six veils turned toward August at once, and the temperature of the hall dropped so fast that frost bloomed on the brass fittings.

"You read it well," the ash voice said. "Two hundred years, and the straw finally speaks. But hear us, guarantor. We have heard the terms. We have counted the fire that remains." The veils inclined, together, toward the floor—as if listening to something far below, something under the city, something vast. "And it is not enough. It was never going to be enough. The eldest of us has starved already; did you know that? The cathedral on Vellum Hill was his. He went quietly, as you hoped we all would go. And it bought him nothing but being forgotten." The voice gathered itself, and the candles all bent toward the agents like grass in wind. "We do not want your rations, little clerk. We want what was promised. We want the debt paid in full. And the debt, by the terms you have just read aloud—"

The lead agent raised one hand, and the gauze veil burned away, and beneath it was not a face but a hunger, and it looked at August with two hundred years of starvation in it.

"—is her."

The frost crept across the table. The auditors were on their feet. Somewhere above them, faintly, through the floor, through the foundations, through two hundred feet of stone and clay and buried history, August felt a tremor begin—slow, deep, patient, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and opening one eye.

Vaskarath had heard her read the contract.

Vaskarath had heard the word *consent given under duress.*

And the last dragon in the world was coming up to collect.

"*August,*" Sefa breathed beside her, grabbing her sleeve, "what did you do—"

"Exactly what I said I'd do," August said, as the floor began to glow. "I opened the books."

"Now the fire answers."
