## 32. Fire Answers

The Ember Audit was held in the Guildhall square, because the Concord of Faiths insisted on witnesses and the Guild of Impossible Things had nowhere else to put them.

They came in their hundreds. Auditors in grey wool with iron styluses. Priests of nine surviving faiths in vestments the color of old bruises. Guild debtors who had no business being there and came anyway, because August had asked, and because a woman who teaches you to forge your own name tends to own a piece of you afterward. The square's cobbles had been scrubbed of the cold ash twice. It had come back both times, fine as flour, drifting up out of the seams in the stones like the city itself was exhaling something it had swallowed a long time ago.

August stood on the guild's steps with the founding contract in a leather tube against her spine, and her hands did not shake, which surprised her. She had expected them to. She had spent her whole life believing that terror lived in the hands. It turned out it lived higher up, behind the sternum, in the small cold room where she kept her arithmetic.

*Three hundred and eleven years of stolen fire,* that arithmetic said. *One dragon. Nine gods. And a girl with a ledger.*

Master Corvane stood to her left, hands folded, wearing his audit face—the one that said he had already counted everything in the square, including the square. He had not looked at her since the vote. Four raised hands. She could still feel them, four small earthquakes in the room.

The eldest auditor, a woman named Prelate Ossen with a voice like a drawer closing, struck her iron stylus against the reading lectern three times.

"The Concord of Faiths convenes the Ember Audit of the Guild of Impossible Things," she said. "All accounts to be produced. All debts to be read aloud. All parties bound by the Concord's covenant to answer truthfully under the eyes of the nine."

*The nine.* August watched the sky as she said it. The city had no clouds left—hadn't for a generation, not since the rains stopped meaning anything—but the sky over the square had begun to do something worse than cloud. It had begun to *wait*. The light coming down through it had gone the color of the inside of a held breath.

"Then let's not waste the nine's patience," August said, and her voice carried further than she intended, the way voices do in squares, the way they do when something underneath the world has stopped pretending to be stone. "I'm August Okafor, junior ledger-keeper, and I have an account here older than this guild. Older than the Concord. Older than every faith represented on this square except the one you all stopped praying to."

Murmur, spreading outward like a dropped glass. Corvane's hands tightened, very slightly, around each other.

"Junior ledger-keeper," Ossen said, "the sealed vault account was not within the audit's scope—"

"It is now," August said, and broke the seal.

She had practiced the reading eleven times in the cold cathedral, Sefa timing her with a chipped pocket watch, the debtor-choir hissing at her to slow down, to speed up, to *say the name louder*. She did not need the practice. She had read the contract so many times the words had worn grooves in her, and now she simply fell into them.

"In the year of the Long Smoke," she read, "the undersigned, being the founding nine of the Guild of Impossible Things, do covenant as follows: that the entity known as Vaskarath, last of the fire-drakes, being besieged and dying, shall surrender its fire in perpetuity; and the guild shall hold and distribute that fire to the powers called gods, who cannot otherwise survive the age of iron; and in guarantee of this covenant the guild shall pledge a bloodline—"

She stopped. Not for effect. Because the ground moved.

It was not an earthquake. Earthquakes shake things. This was more like the square taking a breath, a slow swell under the cobbles, and every pigeon in the eaves went up at once, a grey detonation of wings, and the crowd's murmur cut off into the particular silence that people make when their bodies understand something before their minds do.

August kept reading. Her voice only shook a little.

"—pledge a bloodline, to serve as living guarantor of the debt, generation unto generation, until the fire is spent or the covenant broken. Signed: the nine founders. And sealed in blood: *Maren Okafor, guarantor.*"

She said her grandmother's name into the square, and the square said it back in a hundred whispers, and then the ground said something else entirely.

The cobbles at the center of the square rose. Not cracked—*rose*, a whole raft of them, tilting like a raft on a swell, and through the gap came a smell August had only ever met in one place: the undercity shrine, the dying flame, hot stone and old smoke and something underneath both that her hindbrain identified as *dragon* and her heart identified, absurdly, as *home*. Cold ash leapt up out of the seams in a fine grey fountain. People screamed. The grey wool of the auditors parted like water around a rock.

And Vaskarath came up into the daylight for the first time in three hundred and eleven years.

She had seen him in the dark, in the shrine, by the light of his own guttering. She had thought she was prepared. She was not prepared for scale. He came up slowly, the way a mountain would come up if a mountain were tired, and the square's fountain—some ancestor of the guild had built it over the exact center of the vault, out of arrogance or forgetfulness—toppled and rolled away in pieces. His head alone was the size of the guildhall door. His scales were the grey of banked coals, of ledgers closed for the night, and between them ran seams of light, dull orange, flickering like the last lamp in a dying street. One eye found her on the steps, ancient and gold and unbearably patient, and the eye said, without words, *you read it aloud. Good. Now they all have to hear.*

"Impossible," said Prelate Ossen, and for the first time the drawer-voice wobbled. "Dragons are extinct. The Concord holds records—"

"The Concord holds records," August said, "because the guild *sold* you the lie that they were extinct. We were never in the miracle business, Prelate. We were in the *inventory* business. And he—" she gestured, and her arm did not shake, and she was proud of that for the rest of her life, "—he is the inventory."

The sky answered.

It did not tear open. It *condensed*. The waiting light thickened, and shapes stood up in it, the way shapes stand up in heat-haze, except these had edges and intention. August had spent weeks reading answered prayers across the city—Sefa's rain, the baker's bread that never staled, the widow's dead husband's voice in the chimney—and every miracle had left cold ash, and every cold ash had led down. Now she saw where it all came from. The gods were *thin*. That was the horror of them, the thing no hymn had ever mentioned. They were outlines of glory, drawn in light with nothing filling the middle, nine starving figures standing on the air above the square, and every one of them was looking down at Vaskarath the way a starving man looks through a bakery window.

The youngest one—the one August privately thought of as the loud one, all edges and hunger, the god whose emissary had come to the guild with a bill—spoke first. Its voice did not come through the air. It arrived fully formed inside every skull on the square at once, which August felt as a pressure behind the eyes, a handprint on the inside of her forehead.

*"THE GUARANTOR,"* it said. *"SURRENDER THE GUARANTOR AND THE COVENANT STANDS. THE DEBT IS SETTLED PEACEFULLY. WE DEPART IN GRACE."*

"There is no covenant," August called up. "You're standing on a theft. That fire was taken, not given—read the contract, it's right here, the guild bought its survival with his life and pledged my grandmother's blood as the *collateral*—"

*"THE BLOODLINE IS PRESENT,"* the young god said, and August felt its attention land on her like a physical weight, like being priced. *"THE ARITHMETIC IS SIMPLE. ONE MORTAL AGAINST NINE DIVINITIES. EVEN YOUR GUILD WOULD CALL THAT A FAIR TRADE."*

"Actually," said Master Corvane, stepping forward, and August's stomach dropped, because she knew that voice, the lecture voice, the *this is how the guild's true trade is done* voice, "the arithmetic is not simple at all. May I?" And without waiting for permission from gods or auditors, he turned to the crowd and became, horribly, a teacher. "The Concord should understand what it is witnessing. The guild has managed this debt for three centuries. We know the burn rate. The fire in that creature's body is not infinite—it is, in fact, nearly spent. Nine gods feeding on the dregs of one dragon. When it's gone, it's gone, and everything you've built your miracles on goes with it. The guarantor's bloodline extends the covenant's *terms*, not the fuel. Killing her buys you nothing but time and a war you cannot win against a dying dragon with nothing left to lose." He spread his hands. "I voted to surrender her because sacrifice is what this guild is *for*. But sacrifice should purchase something. So: I propose the Concord accept the original terms. The guarantor serves. The fire is rationed. The gods fade gently, as the covenant always intended."

August stared at him. *He knew.* He had always known. And now, with the square burning and the sky full of hungry light, he was doing the only thing he knew how to do—managing the debt, protecting the guild, spending her like a currency he'd been holding in reserve since the day he hired her.

"That's not rationing," she said. "That's a slow execution with paperwork."

"It's *survival*, August. It's the only trade on the table."

"It isn't," she said. "It never was. You just never learned to count past the guild's own ledger."

And Vaskarath laughed.

It was not a roar. It was a laugh, low and vast and cracked with three centuries of disuse, and it came up through the soles of everyone's boots, and it shook loose a fine rain of ash from the eaves of every building on the square. The gold eye rolled toward Corvane with something that was almost fondness.

*"Three hundred years,"* the dragon said, and his voice was the shrine-voice, the exhausted ancient, the voice that had told her *no one has ever offered me a choice*, *"and your guild still thinks the fire belongs to whoever holds the ledger."*

The seams between his scales brightened.

It happened the way a banked coal happens—slowly, and then all at once. Light ran along his spine like script being written, orange to gold to a white so deep it hurt, and the cold ash on the square began to rise, every fleck of it, three hundred years of it, spiraling up around him in a slow grey galaxy. The heat came off him in a wave that August felt as a kindness, which frightened her more than the heat would have. The crowd recoiled to the square's edges. The auditors' iron styluses glowed dull red in their hands. Prelate Ossen's drawer-voice was gone entirely; she had backed against the lectern and was holding it like a railing on a ship.

*"HE BURNS,"* the young god shrieked, and the other eight shapes flared, hungry, reaching. *"THE FUEL IS MOVING—TAKE IT NOW—"*

They came down.

August had imagined many endings for this day. She had not imagined the gods as fast. They fell on the square like comets with intentions, and where they struck, the cobbles did not shatter—they *whitened*, flash-fired, and the cold ash that had drifted up around Vaskarath was dragged toward them in streams, and where the ash touched their light it flared and fed them and made them briefly, horribly, more solid. The youngest god landed nearest the guildhall steps, and up close its thinness was worse—a silhouette of a god, a god-shaped absence with light poured around it, and it turned its absence toward the steps, toward the tube in August's hands, toward her.

Sefa hit her from the side.

They went down behind the lectern as the god's attention swept past, and the stone where August had been standing crazed with heat. Sefa was on top of her, grey-robed, smelling of ink and temple incense and fear, and her face was two inches away and furious.

"You *idiot*," Sefa said. "You beautiful, ledger-brained idiot. You had a plan, and the plan was *stand on the steps and read*?"

"The plan was the reading," August gasped. "The reading puts the crime on the record. The Concord can't un-hear it. Everything after this is just—" the square lit white, a god's strike, a scream somewhere in the crowd, "—everything after this is just the part where nobody's listening anymore."

"Then make them listen!"

The debtor-choir was already doing it. August saw them through the smoke—Maret the baker hauling a handcart of flour that he burst open against the cobbles in a great blinding cloud; the twins who owed the guild one impossible apology setting fire to their own forged ledgers and waving them like torches, shouting that the vault was breached, the vault was breached, drawing auditors and guild guards toward a fire that existed only on paper; old Ives, defrocked, deaf in one ear, standing in the open with his arms out singing the Litany of Accounts at the top of a voice that had no business being that loud. They were paying their impossible things, one after another, right there in the ash. They were buying her time with the only currency they had.

Vaskarath turned his head and *breathed*.

Not fire. Not at first. A wind, a hot exhale that flattened the flour cloud sideways and sent two of the thinner gods staggering back into the air like leaves, and then the fire came, a long low river of it, and it did not burn the cobbles or the people—it burned *upward*, a wall between the gods and the guildhall steps, a wall of light with the whole square's shadow dancing behind it. The heat of it reached August and Sefa behind the lectern like a hand laid gently on their shoulders.

*"THE CHOIR STAYS WHOLE,"* Vaskarath said, to the gods, to the square, to the sky. *"I HAVE EATEN ENOUGH OF THIS CITY. I WILL NOT EAT ITS PEOPLE. COME AND TAKE THE FIRE IF YOU CAN CARRY IT."*

The youngest god came. Of course it did. It came down through the fire wall with its absence outstretched, and where its light touched the dragon's seams the gold dimmed, and Vaskarath made a sound that August would hear for the rest of her life—a sound like a ledger being torn, like three hundred years of patience reaching its final page—and his tail came around and caught the god across the square and the god *burst*, scattering into a thousand embers that drifted and died, and the crowd made a sound that had no name, awe and terror in one throat.

But the other eight kept coming, and the dragon's seams were dimming where they touched him, guttering like the shrine flame, like the last lamp, and August understood with a cold clarity that this was the arithmetic she had run in the vault, made flesh: there was not enough of him left to fight them. There had never been enough. Every minute of this battle was a minute of the fire being spent, and when it was spent the gods would either die with him or turn on the city, and either way the ledger closed on all of them.

"August." Sefa had her by the collar, ash in her hair, her eyes wide and certain. "The tube. Tell me you have the tube."

August had the tube. The founding contract, against her spine, warm from her own body.

"Burning it voids the debt," Sefa said. "Your words. The charter dies, the claim dies, the whole rotten covenant dies. *Burn it.*"

"Not yet." August was watching the fire wall, watching the dragon hold it, watching the seams dim. "If I burn it now, the gods' claim evaporates—but so does his protection, so does the choir's, so does everything. The debt dies and the war doesn't. I need them *listening*. I need the Concord on the record. I need—"

The eldest god spoke then, and it spoke not to the square but to her, its voice arriving quiet, almost gentle, the voice of something that had been starving so long it had gone past hunger into a kind of terrible calm.

*"LITTLE GUARANTOR,"* it said. *"WE KNOW WHAT YOU CARRY. WE KNOW WHAT YOU INTEND. YOUR GRANDMOTHER STOOD WHERE YOU STAND, ONCE, AND CHOSE THE PAPER. CHOOSE FASTER."*

Above the square, the fire wall flickered. Below the square, in the seams of Vaskarath's scales, the light guttered once, twice—

—and went out along his left flank, a whole continent of him going dark, and the dragon staggered, and the youngest god's scattered embers were drifting back together in the sky, reassembling, hungry, patient as interest.

August stood up from behind the lectern, in full view of gods and auditors and the burning square, and pulled the founding contract from the tube.

* * *

The paper felt different than it had that morning. Lighter. As if some of the ink had already begun to leave it.

She walked toward the fire wall, because it was the only place on the square where the gods could not follow, and because the reading lectern had burned, and because if she was going to end a three-hundred-year crime she was going to do it in the light. The heat parted around her—Vaskarath bending his exhale, she realized, shaping the fire around her the way you'd cup a hand around a match—and behind her she heard Sefa shouting her name, and the choir shouting, and Corvane shouting something that was probably arithmetic.

*"GIRL,"* the eldest god said, warning or plea.

"You want the fire," August said, and her voice cracked down the middle and she kept going anyway. "It was never yours. It was never the guild's. It was *his*, and you took it, and my grandmother's name is on the theft, and I am done being the collateral on a crime I didn't commit. So here is my final entry, and all nine of you are my witnesses, and the Concord is on the record, and there is no power in this square that can un-write what happens next—"

She struck a match. Sefa's, from her pocket, the one she'd carried since the cold cathedral, saved for exactly this.

The fire wall roared. The gods flared. The dragon's gold eye found hers across the smoke, ancient and exhausted and, impossibly, *trusting*.

August touched the flame to the corner of the founding contract, and the paper caught, and the fire ran up the margin toward her grandmother's name—

—and the sky went white.
