## 33. August's Entry

The white did not fade so much as *peel*.

It came off the sky in sheets, like burning vellum curling away from a frame, and behind it the square resolved again—cobblestones, the guildhall's cracked facade, the choir pressed against its doors—and standing in the middle of everything, where a moment ago there had only been smoke and shouting, were the gods.

There were nine of them. Of course there were nine. The Concord of Faiths recognized nine living gods, and here they all were, arrived like creditors at a debtor's door, arranged in a loose crescent around the burning contract in August's hands.

They were smaller than she had imagined. That was the first obscenity of it. She had expected mountains, storms, figures tall enough to blot the sun. Instead they stood the height of ordinary people, flickering at the edges like candles in a draft, and every one of them was *thin*. Not thin the way a starving dog is thin. Thin the way a fire is thin when it has been fed on damp wood too long—guttering, sullen, hoarding what light remained.

The eldest stood nearest to her. It wore no face she could read, only a smoothness like heat-shimmer over stone, and when it spoke, its voice came from everywhere at once, including, horribly, from inside her own chest.

*Stop.*

The word landed on the square like a dropped anvil. Fires bent flat. Sefa, three paces away with her arms spread wide in front of the younger debtors, staggered as though shoved.

August looked down at the founding contract.

The match-flame had died. Not gone out—*died*, snuffed along a line as clean as a ruled margin, stopping a finger's width short of her grandmother's signature. The paper smoked gently in her hands. The guild's founding crime sat there half-eaten, half-intact, like a sentence interrupted mid-confession.

She understood then, with the cold clarity that arithmetic always gave her, why the sky had gone white.

The gods hadn't come to stop the burning out of mercy for the guild.

They'd come because the contract was collateral. Because the debt—the fire-debt, the theft, the whole rotten architecture of the last ninety years—was written into that page, and if it burned unstructured, unconverted, the claim didn't vanish. It *chaosed*. It scattered into every direction at once, and some of those directions led through the gods themselves.

They could not let her destroy it blindly.

But they also could not stop her without admitting, in front of two hundred witnesses, what the page said.

August Okafor, junior ledger-keeper, stood in the ash-fall holding the oldest account in the city, and realized she was, for the first time in her life, holding the better position at the table.

Her hands were shaking. She let them. Let everyone see them shake. There was a kind of power in a trembling hand that still doesn't put the pen down.

"Good evening," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she kept going anyway. "My name is August Okafor. I'm the junior ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. And I am holding the founding contract of my guild, which states—" she raised it, turned it outward, let the smoke curl up past her wrist— "in language any auditor can parse, that this guild purchased its charter with stolen dragon-fire, guaranteed against the bloodline of one Adaora Okafor. My grandmother."

The square made a sound like the sea drawing back from a shore.

"You've come to collect," August said to the gods. "I know. I've run the numbers. I know exactly how much fire is left in him—" she nodded toward the ground, toward the deep places where a gold eye had found hers through smoke— "and I know exactly how many prayers you can buy with it before it gutters out. I know you're not monsters. I know you're *starving*. And I know that if you take me—if you take the guarantor—you don't actually solve your problem. You just silence the evidence."

The eldest god's shimmer tightened. Around the crescent, the other eight had gone very still, the way debtors go still when someone starts reading their accounts aloud in public.

"So before anyone collects anything," August said, "I'd like to propose an amendment to the terms."

*

Later—much later, in the ruined years after, when people asked her how she had found the courage—August would tell them the truth, which was that she hadn't. Courage had nothing to do with it. What she felt, standing in front of nine dying gods with a smoking contract in her hands, was the specific vertigo of a ledger-keeper who has finally found the entry that explains every other entry, and understands that the next mark she makes will be permanent.

What she felt was the weight of small numbers. Her own number, this time.

Sefa reached her first, crossing the square at a dead run, defrocked priest's robes flapping, and planted herself at August's shoulder like a wall built of stubbornness. Behind her came the others—Marrow the chimney-debt man with his burned hands, the twins Odile and Odette who owed a door that opened both ways, old Fenwick who had once borrowed a Tuesday and never given it back, and the rest, the ragged choir of them, debtors all, fanning out into a half-moon of their own.

"You don't have to do this alone," Sefa said quietly.

"I'm not," August said. "That's rather the point."

Corvane was there too. He had come out of the guildhall when the sky went white, and he stood apart from everyone, hands clasped behind his back, watching her with an expression she had spent three years learning to read and still couldn't—not pride, not grief, something older than either. He had voted yes. Four hands had risen to sell her, and his had been the first. But he had also taught her that the guild's true trade was not miracles but the management of what people owe, and right now, whether he liked it or not, she was managing.

"The amendment," said the eldest god, and its voice scraped like a whetstone. *Speak it.*

August took out her pen.

It was a plain thing, guild-issue, black lacquer worn to bare wood at the grip where her fingers had lived for three years. She had balanced ten thousand entries with it. She had hidden her own name in margins with it. She had forged withdrawals with it, taught a choir of debtors to lie with it, and now she uncapped it in front of gods and auditors and the last dragon's memory, and knelt, right there on the cobblestones, and laid the founding contract flat on the ground with the blank verso facing up.

Every ledger has a verso. Every account has space beneath the final line. No one ever looks there.

No one had ever looked there.

She wrote:

*Entry the Last. Whereas the Guild of Impossible Things contracted in the year of Ash a debt of fire against the bloodline of Adaora Okafor, and whereas said bloodline persists in the person of August Okafor, junior ledger-keeper; let the record show that the undersigned assumes the whole of the debt, sole and entire, as sole debtor and sole guarantor. The fire of her line, freely offered, freely given, term by term, ration by ration—*

Her pen stopped.

Here was the hinge. Here was the whole negotiation compressed into a single clause, and she knew—if she got this wrong, if she wrote it proud instead of precise, generous instead of exact—they would take her literally, and literally was the one way this ended with her emptied to nothing in a year while the gods burned hot and brief and the dragon died anyway and the city learned nothing.

Ledger-keepers do not write proud. Ledger-keepers write *exact*.

*—ration by ration, in quantities sufficient to sustain, not to glut: that the Nine may fade as fires fade, gently, over long years, rather than war for what remains; and that Vaskarath, last of his kind, shall receive from each ration his equal share, that he may live out his natural span unmolested. In exchange, the undersigned asks no worship, no temple, no title. Only that the taking be by consent, at set times, in set measure, witnessed and recorded. Signed—*

She signed it. Her name, full and large, the way she had never once written it in any margin anywhere. Not hidden. Not small.

*August Okafor.*

The ink was barely dry when the square reacted.

*

The youngest god moved first.

It had been flickering hardest of the nine, thinnest, most desperate—a thing like a boy's silhouette filled with ember-light, all hunger and no patience—and it came forward with a sound like a struck bell, reaching for the page, for her, for the whole offered line of her at once, wanting it *now*, all of it, the way the starving want.

Sefa stepped into its path.

"No," she said. Just that. A defrocked priest with nothing left to lose, standing between a god and a girl, saying the one word her whole broken faith had been built on.

The god stopped—not from obedience. From confusion. It looked at her the way a drowning man looks at a rope he doesn't trust.

"She offers *everything*," it said, and its voice was young and awful. "Why would we take *sips*?"

"Because," August said, still kneeling, still calm, still keeping her voice level the way you keep a scale level, "sips are what's left after war. You can fight the dragon for the dregs and win, maybe, and spend most of what you win winning it. Or you can sign, and eat for a hundred years. I've done the arithmetic for both. Would you like to see my work?"

She held up the running-numbers sheet—she had carried it since the undercity, since the night horror had looked like arithmetic—folded soft with handling, columns dense as rain.

"I am not brave," she told the youngest god, and meant it, and the meaning was somehow more convincing than bravery would have been. "I am an accountant. This is simply the entry that balances."

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight, pressing down on the square until the torches leaned.

And then the eldest god did something none of them expected.

It laughed.

Not cruelly. Wearyingly—the laugh of something ancient hearing, after eons of debtors begging and bargaining and lying, the first honest invoice ever slid across its table. The sound rolled through the cobblestones and up through August's knees and into her teeth.

*Ninety years,* the eldest said. *Ninety years we have sent emissaries and demands and threats, and every guildmaster groveled or lied or hid behind charters. And now a junior ledger-keeper kneels in the street and offers us* terms. *With* witnesses. *With* shares allocated to the creature we stole from.*

It drifted closer. The shimmer of it bent over the page, reading—actually reading, line by line, the way auditors read, and August watched a god audit her offer and understood that whatever happened next, she had already won the only contest that mattered: she had made them treat her as a counterparty.

"The share to the serpent," the eldest said. *Explain it. He is your enemy by blood-debt. His fire was taken. Why feed him?*

"Because the debt isn't only yours," August said. "It's owed *to* him. If I settle with you and leave him unpaid, I haven't closed the account. I've just changed the creditor." She looked up, and let the eldest see her eyes, red-rimmed, exhausted, certain. "I balance books. All of them. Or I balance none."

Behind her, she heard Marrow breathe out slowly, and Odile whisper to Odette, *"she balances none,"* in the tone of someone memorizing a phrase they intend to keep forever.

The youngest god surged again, ember-light flaring. *We are DYING. You speak of FAIRNESS—*

"We are all dying," Sefa said, and her voice broke open, and out of it fell everything she'd been carrying since the cold cathedral. "I prayed for rain and my brother answered and he was DEAD, do you understand? Your hunger wears the faces of the people we loved. I nearly gave away the only friend I have left for one more minute of his voice. So yes. You are dying. So is he—" she pointed at the ground, at the deep places, at the exhausted gold eye August could still feel watching through the stones— "and so is every soul in this square, eventually, and the only question any of us ever get to answer is *how*. Gently or greedy. That's the whole liturgy. Everything else is commentary."

The youngest god stared at her.

And wavered.

August saw it—the flicker stuttering, the hunger meeting something it had no category for. The gods had been fed on stolen fire for ninety years; they had forgotten, maybe, that fire could also be *given*. That consent changed the taste of a thing.

The eldest extended what might have been a hand—a suggestion of a hand, heat-shimmer shaped by intention—and hovered it above the verso of the contract, above her entry, above her name written large for the first time in her life.

"Terms accepted?" August asked. Her voice only shook a little. "Rations. Consent. Witnessed. Equal share to Vaskarath. Say it plainly, for the record. I have two hundred witnesses and a Concord of Faiths full of auditors who will want the wording exact."

A ripple went through the crescent of gods—outrage, relief, terror, the whole quarrel of a family at a deathbed—and the eldest began, gravely, terribly, to answer—

—and the ground shook.

Not the gods. Not the dragon rising. Something else. From the direction of the Concord's barricades at the mouth of the square, a horn blew—one long note, then two short—and through the smoke came marching the external auditors of the Concord of Faiths, forty strong, ledgers chained to their belts, and at their head walked the Chief Auditor herself, silver seals swinging, her voice carrying flat and cold across the square:

"By authority of the Concord, ALL accounts are hereby seized. Including—" her eyes found August kneeling over the founding contract, found the half-burned page, found the gods bending over it like moths— "*especially* that one. Nobody signs anything."

The eldest god's shimmer turned, slow as a lighthouse beam, toward the newcomers.

And August, still kneeling, pen still in her hand, felt the terrible arithmetic shift beneath her all over again—because there was one party at this table she had forgotten to price, and it was the one that had spent three hundred years believing that no one should ever, under any circumstances, settle an account without paying *them* first.

The Chief Auditor smiled.

"Junior Okafor," she said. "You have something that belongs to the Concord."

August closed her hand around the pen, and the contract, and did not move.
