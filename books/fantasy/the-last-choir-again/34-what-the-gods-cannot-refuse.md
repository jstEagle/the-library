## 34. What the Gods Cannot Refuse

The square had stopped being a square.

Ash hung in the air like snow that had forgotten which way to fall, and above us the gods stood in the smoke—not descending so much as *arriving*, the way a headache arrives, the way debt arrives. Three of them. I counted them the way I counted everything now: the eldest, tall and grey as a winter horizon, his face worn smooth like a coin passed through too many hands; a middle one whose outline kept shifting, all elbows and hunger; and the youngest, who burned.

That was the only word for it. The youngest god burned from the inside out, light leaking through the seams of him, and every time he moved, the air around him made a sound like a page tearing.

Vaskarath's fire lit the square from below, gold and patient, rising through the cracks in the cobblestones where the old serpent's breath came up from the undercity like heat from a forge. Between the two lights—his below, theirs above—we small creatures stood in a band of ordinary dusk, holding papers.

It was, I want it recorded somewhere, the strangest audit in the history of audits.

"The precedent," I said again, because my voice was the only thing I had that worked reliably. "The god of the eastern fenlands. He starved. Quietly. Over two hundred years. And no one went to war over it."

The eldest god looked at me. Looking at a god is not like being looked at by a person. A person's gaze lands on you. A god's gaze *weighs* you, and I felt myself weighed—ledgered, appraised, my whole life reduced to a line item—and found, to my enormous surprise, not worthless. Merely small. There is a difference, and the difference mattered.

"Show me," the eldest said.

His voice was not thunder. It was tiredness given grammar. It was the voice of someone who has been awake for a very long time and would like, just once, to be allowed to sleep without the world ending because of it.

I lifted the records I'd carried out of the cold cathedral—Sefa's arms around the heavier volumes, because I'd been honest with her about how much I could carry and she'd taken the difference without comment. The pages were damp with undercity air and smelled of stone and old incense gone sour. I opened them on the broken plinth at the center of the square, the one that had probably held a statue of somebody once, before somebody else decided statues were an expense.

"Here." My finger shook. I let it shake. Some things should be seen shaking. "Tithes received: nothing, for sixty-one years. Prayers answered: none—the record says he *chose* not to answer, but look at the handwriting. It gets worse every year. That's not choice. That's a god writing with hands he can barely lift." I turned the page. "And here. His last entry. He writes down what he did with the last of himself."

I read it aloud. I had read it eleven times in the cathedral and it still caught in my throat, so I read it slowly, letting the catch be part of it:

*"I gave the remainder to the river ice, so the spring thaw would come gentle and the drowning year would spare the village downstream. They will not know why the water was kind. Let that be enough. It was always supposed to be enough."*

Silence. Even Vaskarath's fire seemed to lower its voice.

"He starved," I said, "and his last act was a kindness no one ever billed anyone for. No war. No theft. No dragon-fire stolen to keep the lights on. He dimmed. He went out. And the world continued, and the village downstream never knew they'd been loved by something dying."

The middle god made a sound. I couldn't tell if it was hunger or grief. With gods, I was learning, the distinction may not exist.

"You ask us," the eldest said carefully, "to do that. To choose the fen-god's road."

"I'm asking you to be allowed to do it," I corrected. "There's a difference. He didn't have a choice either—he starved because there was nothing left to steal. I'm offering you a third column. Rationed fire. Freely given. Enough to fade slow instead of fast. Enough to spend your endings on kindness instead of conquest." I spread my hands. "You've been doing arithmetic since before my city had a name. So do this sum. War against Vaskarath costs you everything—you know it, he knows it, and even if you win, you win ash. The ration costs you your eternity. But eternity—" and here I did something I had never done in any ledger, I editorialized, "—eternity was always a lie you told yourselves. Nothing keeps. You of all beings should know that. Everything goes out eventually. The only question anyone ever gets to answer is *what burns for while it lasts.*"

The youngest god laughed.

It was not a nice sound. It was the sound a fire makes when it finds something it wasn't supposed to burn.

"Listen to the little clerk," he said. His voice was young and bright and wrong, like a bell rung by someone who hated bells. "She stands in her little square with her little papers and offers us a *pension*. A dignified decline. A managed portfolio of our own deaths." Light poured off him when he spoke, and where the light touched the cobblestones, the stones went grey. Not scorched. *Drained.* I understood then what I was looking at: he wasn't burning bright. He was burning *hungry*, consuming whatever was nearest because the dragon-fire ration he'd been living on stolen for three hundred years had taught him appetite and nothing else.

"You don't understand what we are," the youngest said, drifting closer. The crowd of debtors behind me shifted; I heard Pell whisper a prayer and then, catching himself, apologize to no one. "We are not fires waiting politely to go out. We are *answers*. Every prayer ever prayed built a piece of us, and every piece expects to keep existing. You want me to fade? Then unbuild the ten thousand years of kneeling that made me. Go on. Unbuild it."

"I can't," I admitted. "But neither can you take it back by war. That's the whole point of the arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." He said the word like an insult, and maybe to him it was. Maybe to a god, arithmetic is blasphemy—the insistence that even infinity must balance. "My siblings may remember being hungry before they were worshipped. I don't. I was born *owed to*. And I will not be lectured on restraint by a woman whose guild stole the fire that fed me and then hid the receipt for three centuries."

"That's fair," I said, and meant it, and watched surprise flicker across his burning face. "The guild committed the crime. I've read the contract. I've read it aloud in front of witnesses. But the crime was theft, not creation—we didn't make you dependent, we made you *comfortable*, and those are different sins with different cures. Comfort ends. Dependence doesn't have to end in blood."

He came closer still. Close enough that I could feel my own edges starting to fray, close enough that Sefa grabbed my sleeve and pulled, and I let her, taking one step back while keeping my eyes up because I had learned—at terrible tuition—that looking down is how you lose.

"Here is what cannot be refused," the youngest said softly. "Not your ration. Not your precedent. *Me.* I am what the prayers made, and the prayers wanted a god who answers, and I will answer every prayer ever prayed by taking everything there is to take. Your grandmother's generation lit me with stolen fire. Her granddaughter will finish the transaction."

"There it is," said a voice beneath us, vast and dry and old as sediment. Vaskarath's head had risen through the square's central fissure, scales like roof slates the size of doors, one eye—a gold, ancient, exhausted eye—fixed on the youngest god with something worse than hatred. Something like recognition. "There is always a youngest. In every family, in every pantheon, in every ledger. The one who arrived after the lessons were learned and inherited only the appetite." The dragon's breath stirred the ash into spirals. "I knew your eldest when he was small enough to hide from storms. I have watched your kind be born and burn out and be born again, and every cycle, the youngest makes the same offer and calls it destiny."

"It *is* destiny—"

"It is a *budget*," Vaskarath said, "with delusions of grandeur."

And the eldest god—grey, tired, smooth-faced—began, very quietly, to laugh.

* * *

It was not mocking laughter. That was the frightening part. It was the laughter of a man watching his own reflection finally say something true.

"You see it, don't you, old serpent," the eldest said. "Three hundred years I have eaten stolen fire and told myself the theft was temporary. An interim measure. A bridge loan." He turned his worn face toward me. "Your guild's founders used those words. Bridge loan. I signed nothing—I didn't need to sign; silence is also a signature—but I accepted, and acceptance compounds."

"Yes," I said, because apparently I was the kind of person now who prompted gods during confessions.

"My middle sibling cannot choose otherwise. Hunger is all that's left of them; the worship that shaped them was desperate, and desperation set their shape." The eldest gestured, and the shifting thing beside him stilled for a moment into something almost pitiable—a silhouette of open hands, forever reaching. "But I was worshipped first with gratitude. Harvest festivals. Children's songs. There is still, buried under three centuries of compounding appetite, a self that remembers how to receive a gift." He looked at the records on the plinth, at the fen-god's last entry, and his voice dropped to nearly human quiet. "*Let that be enough. It was always supposed to be enough.*"

"Yes," I said again. My heart was going like a hammer on a nail.

"If the ration is real. If the fire comes freely, not extracted, not invoiced—" He paused, and I watched a god do the hardest thing I have ever watched anyone do, harder than burning or fighting or dying. He hesitated. He doubted himself in public. "If it is freely given, then accepting it does not make me a thief. And fading slowly, spending my ending on gentleness—" his gaze went to the fen-god's page again, "—that is not defeat. That is the original job description. We were never supposed to be eternal. We were supposed to be *useful*, and then gracious about leaving."

"Sister," the youngest snarled—it took me a moment to realize he meant me, that in his taxonomy I had been promoted or demoted into family, "you're letting the clerk write your obituary."

"No," the eldest said. "I'm letting her write my *last entry*. There's a difference, little brother, and the difference is everything your appetite never let you learn." He descended—not landing, gods don't land, but lowering himself until his grey nearness stood level with me, and the pressure of his attention eased from crushing to merely immense. "Ledger-keeper. What are your terms?"

Terms. He was asking for terms. Somewhere behind me, Pell made a sound like a sob and a cheer colliding.

"Fire from Vaskarath, voluntarily offered, apportioned monthly," I said, and my voice only cracked once. "Administered by—well, by us. By the choir. By people with no charter and nothing left to steal for. Every offering recorded openly. Any god who takes more than their share forfeits the next month's ration. And when the fire runs low—as it will, everything runs low—the rations shrink gently, on a published schedule, so no one starves by surprise. You get to plan your sunset. That's more than mortals get."

"And the guild?" the eldest asked. "The debt? The guarantor?"

"Burned," I said. "Or burning soon. The founding contract dies tonight, one way or another. Nobody's bloodline is collateral anymore. Mine least of all."

The eldest god extended a hand—grey, smooth, worn like sea glass—and waited.

I understood the gesture. He would not take. Taking was the old way, the stolen way, the way that had rotted three centuries of heaven. If there was to be a new arrangement, it would begin with a god *receiving*, openly, in front of witnesses, for the first time in living memory.

I looked at Vaskarath.

The old serpent's eye met mine, and in it I saw the question he was too proud—or perhaps too hopeful—to ask aloud. *Do you mean it, little clerk? Will you give away the fire that could buy your guild its survival back?*

I thought of chapter thirty-three of my own life, the entry I'd already written: *August Okafor, sole debtor, offers her bloodline's fire willingly.* I had written it expecting to lose everything. I hadn't expected the losing to feel like this—like setting down a load I'd forgotten I was carrying.

"Give him the first ration," I said.

Vaskarath breathed.

I cannot describe it properly. Fire came up out of the earth like a spring comes out of a hillside—not hurled, not weaponized, just *offered*, a column of warm gold rising between the eldest god and the sky, and the eldest god opened his worn hands and received it, and the grey of him flushed, briefly, with color. With memory. For one moment the square smelled not of ash but of harvest, of bread, of children's songs sung badly in summer, and half the debtors behind me burst into tears without knowing why.

"Accepted," the eldest said, and his voice had changed—less tired, or tired in a better way, the tiredness of a day's work rather than a century's. "Witnessed. Recorded. Little clerk, enter it in whatever book you carry. Enter it *large*."

I was reaching for my satchel when the youngest god screamed.

* * *

It was not a scream of rage. Rage I could have handled. It was a scream of *terror*—the raw, uncomprehending terror of a creature watching the rules of its existence get rewritten in real time. Because that was what the eldest's acceptance was: not a treaty but a proof. Proof that a god could receive instead of take. Proof that the hunger was a habit, not a nature. And habits, once exposed as habits, can be broken—which meant the youngest's entire self-justification, the destiny he'd wrapped around his appetite like a flag, had just died in front of him.

"If they can fade," he shrieked, light tearing out of him in ragged sheets, "then fading is *possible*, and if it's possible then it's *expected*, and if it's expected then I am not eternal, I was never eternal, I am just the newest flame in a long line of flames that everyone *let believe*—"

"Yes," said Vaskarath, with terrible gentleness. "Welcome to mortality, hatchling. We've been waiting for you a long time."

The youngest god attacked.

Not the eldest. Not Vaskarath, though Vaskarath rose to meet him, scales grinding like a mountain range deciding to move. Not even me, though I was nearest and smallest and my name was on every page that had wounded him.

He attacked the *ration*.

He dove into the column of offered fire—the first freely given gift in three hundred years of divine theft—and began to drink it, gulping it down in great starving swallows, converting gift back into plunder, because plunder was the only shape his soul knew how to hold. The golden light guttered. The smell of harvest curdled into the smell of a house fire. Behind me, Sefa shouted something; Pell grabbed the children among the debtors and pulled them back; the middle god, all reaching hands, drifted toward the chaos with an expression that might have been longing or might have been the beginning of its own choice, impossible to say.

"Stop him!" someone cried, uselessly. You don't stop a god by shouting at it.

But Vaskarath was already moving, and the eldest was already turning, and I was already doing the only thing I have ever known how to do with a catastrophe.

I opened the ledger.

Because here is what the youngest god had forgotten, in his three centuries of comfortable theft, and what I would never forget as long as I drew breath: *everything is accounted for.* Every gift has a record now. Every swallow he stole from the ration was a withdrawal against a fund that belonged—to the extent anything belonged to anyone—to all of them, jointly, on terms witnessed. He wasn't defying us.

He was embezzling.

And I had spent my whole life learning what happens to embezzlers.

"Enter it," I said to Sefa, shoving the ledger into her hands, my pen already moving across the fresh page, my handwriting steadier than it had any right to be. "Every draught he takes. Timestamp it. Witness it. Get Pell to countersign—he's got the cleanest hand in the choir. If he wants to steal, then by every law of heaven and accounting both, he steals *on the record*."

The youngest god felt the pen move. I swear he felt it. He turned from the guttering ration-column, fire streaming off him like torn banners, and fixed his burning eyes on me, on the small mortal woman writing his crimes down in a book that suddenly, horribly, mattered more than armies.

"What," he said, "are you *doing*."

"Bookkeeping," I said.

And above the square, the eldest god raised his newly-fed hands, and Vaskarath coiled beneath us like a storm deciding, and the middle god reached out with all ten thousand of its desperate fingers toward a choice it had never been offered before—

—and the youngest came for my ledger.
