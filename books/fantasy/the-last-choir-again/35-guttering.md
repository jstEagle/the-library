## 35. Guttering

The square had become a place where light went to argue.

August crouched behind the overturned ledger-cart with Sefa pressed against her shoulder, and watched the sky do things the sky was not built to do. The youngest god hung above the Guildhall like a wound in the air—a shape of white fire that kept trying to be a person and kept failing, all elbows and hunger and too many mouths. Below it, Vaskarath had risen until his head cleared the rooftops, and his body was a mountain that had decided, after four hundred years, to move.

He was losing.

She could see it in the way his fire guttered. Not the great theatrical flares of a dragon at war—she'd read about those, imagined them her whole life in the margins of ledgers—but a stuttering, rationed burn, like a lamp with an inch of oil left and a long night ahead. Every time he breathed flame at the young god, the flame came out thinner. Dimmer. The color wrong: more amber than gold now, the color of a hearth at the end of winter when you're burning the furniture.

"He's spending himself," August said. Her voice came out flat, which was how it always went when she was most afraid. Numbers voice. "Look at him. He's not fighting, he's *paying*."

"Then we should stop letting him," Sefa said.

The choir was scattered across the square behind barricades of their own making—staged fires still smoking where they'd bought August her escape from the Ledger Halls, forged trails still fooling half the Concord's auditors into searching empty rooms. Maren the baker-debtor had dragged three of the smaller ones behind a fountain. Old Tull, who owed the guild one impossible apology, was standing in the open with his arms out like he thought he could catch falling gods. He couldn't. Nobody could. But he stood there anyway, because that was what the choir did now—they stood where it made no sense to stand, together.

The young god struck.

It didn't descend so much as *arrive*, all its hunger focused into a single column of white heat that came down at the fountain, at Maren, at the children of debtors who had never owed anyone anything except love. And Vaskarath moved.

Four hundred years under the city. Four hundred years of cold stone and colder silence, of being the guild's oldest account, its secret, its shame—and he moved faster than anything that size had any right to move, folding his body over the fountain like a hand closing around a candleflame.

The white fire hit his back and the sound was not a sound. It was the absence of every other sound, a hole in the world shaped like pain. August felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of her teeth, in the bones behind her eyes. When her vision cleared, Vaskarath was still there, coiled around the fountain, and the people inside the coil were alive, and his scales along the spine were—

Gone. Not burned away. *Spent.* A swath of his back had gone dull and grey as old ash, scales cracked like fired clay, and through the cracks she could see something that wasn't flesh underneath. Something dimmer than embers.

"No," Sefa whispered beside her. "No, no—he can't—"

"He can," August said. "That's the arithmetic. He told us himself. He has maybe a tenth of what he was. He's been running on fumes since before I was born, and he just spent a year's worth in nine seconds." She was doing the math without meaning to, the terrible guild math, survival-as-accounting, and the answer kept coming out the same. "At this rate he has minutes."

"Then we have minutes," Sefa said. "What do we need?"

August looked at her. Rain-singer. Defrocked priest. Debtor of one impossible thing—the impossible thing, the prayer that had started all of this, the rain that fell in a city with no clouds. And then the second prayer, the test, the thing that had answered with her dead brother's voice. Sefa hadn't used it since. Hadn't spent it. She'd told August once, in the undercity dark, holding the memory of that voice like a coal cupped in bare hands: *"It's the last of him I have. Once I say it back into the world, it's gone for good."*

"Sefa." August turned fully toward her. "I need you to know that I'm going to ask you for something, and I need you to know that I hate myself for asking, and if you say no I will find another way even if there isn't one."

Sefa looked at her with those rain-grey eyes and almost smiled. Almost. "The young god is made of stolen fire. Fire needs fuel, but—" she frowned, working it, "—it also fears water. Real water. Not ash-rain, not the cold stuff the miracles leave behind. *Real* water. Living water. The kind that falls because the sky grieves, not because something burned."

"The kind you called down the first time."

"The kind I called down the first time." Sefa's hands were shaking. August took them. They were cold. "When I prayed for rain, I didn't know what answered. I didn't know it was him—Vaskarath—or his fire, or the gods wearing it. I just knew the sky opened. And when I prayed again to see if they knew my name, my brother answered." Her breath caught. "He sounded so *tired*, August. Even dead, even borrowed, he sounded tired. Like whatever wears his voice is running low too."

"It is. It's all running low. That's the whole war—that's why they're here. They're starving and they've come to eat the last meal in the house." August squeezed her hands. "If you spend it—if you call real rain onto this square—it won't kill the young god. But it will cool him. Slow him. Buy Vaskarath time, buy the choir time, buy *me* time to get to the founding contract and end this the only way it ends."

"And my brother's voice goes with it."

"Yes."

Sefa closed her eyes. Above them, Vaskarath roared—not the roar of a monster, August understood now, but the roar of an exhausted ancient doing the last hard work of a very long life, protecting people whose grandparents had robbed him. Another column of white fire came down and he caught it on his ruined back and the grey spread another arm's length and August heard Tull scream somewhere in the smoke and the math got worse.

"I used to think faith meant believing you'd get an answer," Sefa said quietly. "Then I learned it means believing the answer might cost everything, and praying anyway." She opened her eyes. "My brother died owing money. Stupid money, dice money, the kind of debt that eats a man. The last thing he ever asked me was whether I thought he was good. I said yes. It was true. And now the only piece of him left in the world is a voice in a starving god's mouth, and I am going to spend it buying time for a dragon and a bookkeeper and a choir of debtors, and I think—" her voice broke and reassembled itself, "—I think he would have loved that. I think he would have thought it was the first honest transaction of his life."

"Sefa—"

"Don't apologize. Apologizing wastes seconds. Tell me where to stand."

* * *

They ran.

Across the square, through ash that fell upward near the shrine-entrance and downward everywhere else, past the Concord auditors frozen at the edges like men afraid to touch a live wire, past Corvane—who stood in the Guildhall doorway watching the dragon bleed light, and whose face, for the first time since August had known him, held no calculation at all. Only grief. She had no time to decide what to do with that.

Sefa climbed onto the fountain's rim, inside the shelter of Vaskarath's coil, and the dragon's head swung down to look at her—one vast eye, amber going grey at the rim, ancient and gentle and dying by installments.

"Little priest," he rumbled. His voice shook the cobblestones and her ribs alike. "You are the one who prayed for rain."

"I'm the one who prayed for lots of things," Sefa said. "Most of them were selfish. This one isn't."

"Nothing given freely is selfish." The old eye blinked, slow as a season turning. "I have waited four hundred years for someone to give me something freely. It appears it will be weather."

"Hold still," Sefa said. "This is going to hurt me more than it hurts you."

And she raised her face to the broken sky, and she prayed.

Not in the guild's language. Not in the Concord's liturgy. In the small plain words of a woman from a fishing town that had drowned when she was nineteen, words her brother had taught her on the deck of a boat that no longer existed:

*"Brother. If any of you is still in there—I don't want a miracle. I want rain. Real rain. Cold and heavy and ordinary, the kind that ruins picnics and saves crops. Fall on this square. Fall on the burning man. Fall on everything we love and put out what can still be put out. I'll pay with the only coin I have left. Take the voice. Take him back. Just let it be water when it lands."*

For one heartbeat, nothing.

Then the young god screamed.

It was not anger. August knew anger; she'd been cataloguing it all day. This was something worse—this was *recognition*. Somewhere inside that white furnace of stolen fire, something that wore her brother's voice and a hundred thousand other borrowed voices understood exactly what she was offering, and grieved it, and could not refuse it, because a freely given gift binds deeper than any contract the guild ever wrote. August had spent her life among contracts. She knew the law of them in her marrow: what is taken can be fought. What is *given* cannot.

The sky opened.

Not parted—opened, like a hand unclenching, and the rain that came down was black at first, all the soot of the burning city wrung out at once, and then it was clean, and then it was *cold*, gloriously, viciously cold, the kind of rain that had weight and opinion and no interest whatsoever in anyone's war. It hit the white fire of the young god and the fire *hissed*, shrieked, contracted, its column of heat bending sideways like a candle in wind. Steam rolled off Vaskarath's wounded back and the old dragon groaned—not pain, or not only pain. Relief. Coolth on burns.

And in the middle of the rain, for less than a second, Sefa's brother's voice spoke one last time, out of the air, out of the god, out of wherever borrowed voices go:

*"Good throw, little sister."*

Then it was gone. August saw it leave—saw Sefa feel it leave—saw the woman's knees buckle and her hands press flat against the wet stone of the fountain rim and her mouth open around a sound that wasn't crying, exactly, and wasn't anything else either. The rain fell on her upturned face and mixed with whatever was coming out of her, and August knelt beside her and put her arms around her and held on, because that was the only entry she could make in any ledger right now, and it was the only one that mattered.

"You spent it," August said into her hair. "Oh, Sefa. You spent it."

"It was never mine," Sefa said. "It was a loan. I just finally paid it where it would do some good." She laughed, wet and ragged. "First honest transaction of his life. I told you."

Above them, the young god flickered.

The rain was doing to it what cold truth does to a lie. Its white fire thinned, guttered, and beneath the fire—for the first time—August could see its actual shape: not a god at all, not really. A *hunger*. A vast, starving, terrified hunger wearing the shapes of everyone whose prayers had fed it, everyone whose voices it had borrowed, everyone whose dead it had worn like clothing. It looked at Sefa with her brother's eyes for one awful moment, and the eyes were grateful, and then the hunger closed over the gratitude and it struck again at Vaskarath, weaker now, much weaker, a blow with a fraction of its former weight.

The old dragon caught it anyway. Caught it on the ruin of his back, and the grey spread to his shoulders, and his forelegs buckled, and he went down onto his belly in the flooded square with the fountain and the choir and the children still sheltered in the coil of him, and his breathing came slower, and his fire—

His fire went out.

Not all of it. One ember. August could see it through the rain, deep in his chest, behind the cracked scales: a single point of amber light, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart, the last unspent coin of four hundred years of theft. Everything else was ash and dark and the drumming mercy of the rain.

"Vaskarath!" She scrambled up the wet coils, slipping, swearing, finding handholds in seams of scale, until she reached the great head lying in six inches of floodwater. His eye found her. Still aware. Still *him*.

"You're dying," she said. No guild polish. No ledger voice. Just the truth, naked, terrible.

"I am resting," he corrected, in a whisper that still rattled her teeth. "There is a difference. There has always been a difference. Your guild never learned it." The ember pulsed. "One flame left, little keeper. Enough for one act. Choose it well. I am too tired to choose for myself, and I have carried your grandmother's choice for four hundred years, and I will not carry yours."

August looked out over the square.

The young god, rain-cooled, flickering, half its borrowed faces gone dark. The Concord auditors at the edges, some kneeling, some fleeing, one—an old woman with ink-stained fingers—actually weeping. The choir, soaked and alive and gathering, climbing out from behind their barricades, moving toward the fallen dragon with no plan and no weapons, carrying nothing but themselves. Tull hauling Maren upright. The children. The staged fires drowning. The Guildhall doors standing open behind Corvane, and beyond them, up three flights and through two sealed doors, the vault, and in the vault, the founding contract, and written on the founding contract in her grandmother's careful hand, the signature that had started everything.

The contract. The debt. The whole rotten architecture of it, four hundred years tall, built on one stolen fire and one signed-away bloodline.

Burn it, and the debt dies. Burn it, and the charter dies, and every protection the choir had ever leaned on dies with it. Burn it, and the gods' claim evaporates, because you cannot collect on a contract that no longer exists.

But the contract was upstairs, and she was here, and between her and the vault stood a flickering god and a failing dragon and forty feet of open, rain-drowned ground.

"Vaskarath," she said. "Your last act. I'm asking for it."

The old eye narrowed, amused despite everything. "Name it."

"Not yet." She looked at the Guildhall doors, measuring distance, counting seconds, doing the only math she had ever trusted. "First I have to get upstairs. First I have to get the paper. Then—" her throat tightened, and she made herself finish, "—then I'm going to need you to burn something. One last time. With your very last coin."

The dragon was silent a long moment. Rain ran off his brow in rivers. Deep in his chest, the final ember pulsed, patient as an unpaid account.

"Ah," he said at last, softly, and there was something in the softness that August had never heard from him before—something that sounded almost like hope. "So that is what you brought me up out of the dark for."

Behind her, Sefa got to her feet, wiped her face, and picked up a length of broken barricade like a quarterstaff.

"Well?" she said. "What are we waiting for? We're a choir. Let's sing him upstairs."
