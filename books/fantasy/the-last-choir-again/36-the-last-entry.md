## 36. The Last Entry

They came up out of the undercity like water finding a crack in a wall.

Vaskarath went first—not because he was fastest but because the city had been built around him for three hundred years and knew the shape of him the way a glove knows a hand. Streets that had never been streets opened. Walls that had always been walls leaned aside. August ran in the river of his wake with Sefa at her shoulder and eleven debtors strung out behind them like beads on a fraying cord, and above them the sky was doing something the sky was not supposed to do.

It was full of gods.

Not one god. Not even the handful she'd imagined. The heavens over Ember Square had gone white and crowded, layer on layer of luminous pressure, shapes that were less bodies than hungers wearing the idea of bodies. They had come to collect. The emissary's voice still hung in the air where it had spoken an hour ago—*surrender the guarantor and the account closes peacefully*—and the word *peacefully* had been the most frightening thing August had ever heard, because it meant they had already priced the alternative.

The square itself was a battlefield of light and ash. The Concord's auditors had formed their ranks along the north colonnade, gray-robed, ledger-bound, instruments out, trying to audit an apocalypse in real time. The guild's surviving members stood scattered near the old charter hall—Corvane among them, hands empty, face unreadable. And between all of them, on the flagstones where August had once balanced the guild's books as a girl too young to know what balancing meant, lay the founding contract.

She had carried it up from the vault herself, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart.

"August." Corvane's voice cut across the chaos, thin and precise. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"You voted yes," she said, not slowing. "I read the tally. Four raised hands. I counted."

"I voted to save nine hundred years of—"

"You voted to save the books," she said. "I'm going to close them."

Then Vaskarath reached the center of the square and rose, and everything else stopped mattering for a moment because a dragon the size of a cathedral uncoiling into the open air is not a thing you look past.

* * *

He was dying. She could see it now in the daylight, in a way the undercity's dark had let her half-forget. His scales had gone the color of old coals, gray-rimmed, flaking. When he breathed, the breath came out cold. Three hundred years of theft, siphoned prayer by prayer through the guild's sealed accounts, and what remained of him fit in a fist-sized ember behind his ribs—the last entry in the oldest account in the world.

The gods saw it too. The light above the square bent toward him like iron filings toward a magnet, and August felt the pull in her own chest, in the blood her grandmother had signed away, the guarantor's fire answering its kin across whatever distance gods used for measuring.

*"THE FUEL,"* said the eldest god, and its voice was famine given grammar. *"RETURN WHAT WAS TAKEN."*

"It wasn't taken," August shouted, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she didn't care. "It was *bought*. That's worse. That's the whole point. You didn't steal it—you bought it, which means there's a contract, which means there are *terms*, and I am the junior ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things and I am telling you the terms have been met."

The light rippled. Something like attention settled on her, vast and cold and interested.

*"SPEAK, GUARANTOR."*

She unwrapped the oilcloth.

The founding contract lay in her arms, heavier than paper had any right to be, older than the guild, older than the Concord, its ink the brown of dried blood because some of it was. Nine signatures at the bottom. Her grandmother's name sat in the middle of them, written in a hand August recognized from recipe cards and birthday letters, and beside it, in smaller script, the words she had read aloud only once before:

*In consideration of survival, the Guild conveys unto the undersigned powers all fire of Vaskarath, drawn at the rate of need, until the well runs dry; and the bloodline of the signatory stands as surety, that when the well runs dry, the debt may be collected in kind.*

Collected in kind. In fire. In her.

"The account," August said, and her voice steadied because this, at least, was the one thing she had ever been good at, "is closed."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about burning a three-hundred-year-old contract in front of nine starving gods: it takes a long time.

She got the corner lit on the third strike of Sefa's flint. The oilcloth helped. The old vellum caught grudgingly, curling at the edge, and the flame crawled up the margin slower than any fire she had ever watched, as if the paper were arguing with it, as if every clause were fighting for its life clause by clause.

And the gods screamed.

Not with mouths. With weather. The light above the square went from white to gold to a bruised, furious violet, and the pressure of it drove half the auditors to their knees. Somewhere in the colonnade glass burst outward in a long glittering sheet. The youngest god—the one who had refused the ration, the one made of answered prayers and borrowed thunder—came down out of the sky like a verdict.

Vaskarath moved.

She had never seen him move fast. In the dark beneath the city he had been all patience, all sediment, an ancient thing conserving its final calories. But when the youngest god struck—a lance of white fire aimed not at August but at the burning contract, at the evidence—Vaskarath put himself between them and took it across the whole breadth of his chest.

The sound was not a sound. It was the absence of one, a hole punched in the world's hearing, and then the roar arrived late and enormous and August felt it in her teeth, in her sternum, in the soles of her feet.

"KEEP READING," Vaskarath bellowed down at her, smoke pouring from the wound across his breast. "OUT LOUD. THEY HAVE TO HEAR IT CLOSE."

So she read.

"*Clause the first: the conveyance ends with the document.*" The fire ate the preamble. Above her, one of the smaller lights guttered like a candle in a draft and did not come back. "*Clause the second: no power may claim what has not been conveyed.*" Another page curled to black. A god shaped like a column of rain vanished mid-scream, and where it had been there was nothing, just ordinary evening air rushing in to fill the space, and August thought, wildly, *so that's what it feels like when a debt dies.*

"They're going hungry," Sefa said beside her. She had planted her quarterstaff and was watching the sky with the flat professional calm of a woman who had spent thirty years taking confessions. "Look at them. They're not angry, August. They're *starving*, and they just realized the pantry burned down."

"Good," said Pip, the youngest debtor, who was seventeen and had owed the guild one impossible apology for six years. Her hands were shaking. She held the flint out anyway. "Let them learn what it's like."

"No," August said, striking the flint again, feeding the flame another clause. "That's the old arithmetic. Corvane's arithmetic. Somebody pays, somebody burns, the books balance." The corner of the signature page caught, and her grandmother's name began to blacken letter by letter, and August found she was crying without having decided to. "We're done with that arithmetic. That's why we wrote the new one."

* * *

The youngest god did not accept the new arithmetic.

It came down again while the contract was half ash, and this time it did not aim at the paper. It aimed at the choir.

Later, August would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory gave her fragments: Pip screaming and standing her ground anyway. Old Maren the defrocked priest throwing her body over two younger debtors like a mother hen made of stubbornness. Sefa's quarterstaff shattering against something that had no business being touchable. And Vaskarath, moving through it all like a collapsing building that had chosen where to fall, putting his failing body between the choir and the divine, again and again, spending fire he did not have to buy seconds he could not afford.

"DOWN!" he roared, and folded himself over them like a hand closing, and the youngest god's fire broke across his back in a white cataract.

When he lifted his head, half his left wing was gone. Not burned—*gone*, unmade, the way debts were unmade, and August understood with a lurch that the god hadn't missed. It had been collecting. Taking its claim directly, clause by clause, from the source.

The contract in her arms was down to the final leaf. One clause left. She could see it through the flame:

*And upon dissolution of this instrument, all obligations herein, in both directions, are void.*

Both directions. She had read that line a hundred times in the vault without understanding it. Not just the guild's debt to the gods. The gods' claim on the fire. On Vaskarath. On her bloodline. Everything the contract had ever bound, freed at the moment of its burning—including the dragon, including the ember in his chest, including the guarantor's surety that had hung over her name since before she was born.

"Burn it," Sefa said. "August. *Burn it.*"

But the flint had skittered away in the chaos, and the wind off the gods' descending light kept snuffing the flame, and the last leaf of the founding contract fluttered in August's grip like a living thing trying to escape.

Above her, the eldest god spoke one more time, and its voice was quieter now, and somehow that was worse.

*"GUARANTOR. THE WELL IS DRY. THE DEBT STANDS. GIVE US THE FIRE IN YOUR BLOOD AND WE WILL LET THE REST OF THEM WALK AWAY."*

It was a fair offer. That was the horror of it. It was exactly the kind of offer the guild would have taken, the kind of trade the ledgers loved—one life against many, cleanly entered, neatly balanced. Corvane would have signed it. Her grandmother, maybe, would have signed it, if signing it had saved the people she loved.

August looked at the last leaf. Then at the choir, huddled in the dragon's shadow, alive because a dying serpent had decided they were worth his last warmth. Then at Vaskarath, whose ember pulsed behind his ribs like a patient heart, waiting three hundred years for someone to ask him what *he* wanted.

Nobody had ever asked him what he wanted.

"Hey," she said to the eldest god, conversationally, the way you'd address a difficult client. "You want to hear the funniest thing in your own contract?"

The light did not answer. She told it anyway.

"'All obligations herein, in both directions, are void.'" She smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and she was dimly aware it was her grandmother's smile. "Void means void. Not renegotiated. Not transferred. *Void.* When this burns, you don't get my bloodline. You don't get his ember. You don't get anything, because there will be no instrument left in the world that says you're owed anything at all." She lifted the last leaf high. "You've been feeding on paperwork for three centuries, and I'm the auditor, and I'm finding *no supporting documentation.*"

She struck the flint against her belt buckle, the way Sefa had shown her an hour and a lifetime ago, and the spark caught, and the last clause of the founding contract of the Guild of Impossible Things began to burn.

* * *

The gods' claim evaporated like fog in morning sun.

There was no other way to describe it. One moment the sky was a wall of hunger pressing down on Ember Square, and the next the wall simply wasn't there, and what rushed in behind it was so ordinary it made August laugh out loud through her tears: clouds. Actual clouds, gray and heavy, the first ones the city had seen since the rains stopped. The youngest god, mid-lunge, came apart into a shower of warm rain and did not reassemble. The eldest lingered longest—a tall pale absence looking down at the burning scrap of vellum with what August chose, in memory, to call confusion—and then it too thinned, and faded, and the evening sky closed over the place where it had been like water over a stone.

The charter died more quietly.

She felt it go as a loosening, everywhere at once. The guild's protections had been woven into the same founding document—survival purchased wholesale, three hundred years of immunity and authority and the right to be believed—and as the last corner of vellum crumbled between her fingers, August felt nine hundred years of institutional weight lift off the city like a hand removed from a throat. Somewhere in the colonnade, the Concord's auditors looked up from their instruments, which had all gone blank at once. Somewhere in the crowd, Corvane sat down slowly on the steps of the charter hall, an old man in an empty building, holding nothing.

The ash of the contract drifted up out of her hands and scattered on the rising wind, and every fleck of it glowed briefly gold before it went dark, and then it was just ash, falling on the just and unjust alike, owing nothing to anyone.

"Is it done?" Pip whispered. "Is it actually—"

"It's done," August said.

And then Vaskarath made a sound.

Not a roar. Not words. A sound like a bell the size of a mountain being struck once, softly, deep underground—the sound of an account closing after three hundred years of accruing interest. He reared up against the rain with his ruined wing and his coal-gray scales and the little ember behind his ribs, and the ember flared—brighter than it had been in the undercity, brighter than it had any right to be—and August understood.

The theft had been a draw against him. Every prayer the gods had ever answered on stolen fire had been billed to his account. And now the account was void, and the withdrawals stopped, and what remained was *his*—not fuel, not collateral, not the guarantor's surety. Just fire. Just his.

It was not much. It was not enough to make him young again, or whole, or anything but old and scarred and wing-broken. But it was his, and it burned, and as the rain came down proper and steady across Ember Square, the last dragon in the world breathed out a single thread of true flame into the wet air, and the flame did not go out.

"Ledger-keeper," he rumbled, looking down at her, and his old eyes had rain in them or possibly not rain. "Your grandmother signed that document to keep people safe. It kept no one safe. It only kept the *books* safe." He lowered his great head until his brow nearly touched the ground before her. "Burn the rest of them too. Every page. Start over."

Around them, the choir picked itself up out of the mud—eleven debtors, soggy, singed, alive, staring at the sky where gods had been and weren't. Sefa put a hand on August's shoulder. Pip was crying and pretending not to. Down in the colonnade, Corvane rose to his feet and walked, alone, out of the story of the guild forever.

August stood in the rain in the middle of the ashes of everything she had trusted, holding nothing, belonging to nothing, protected by nothing.

She had never felt lighter in her life.

"Sefa," she said. "Do we still have that blank ledger from the cold cathedral?"

The ex-priest grinned through the rain. "Never left my bag. Why?"

"Because," August Okafor said, turning toward the ruined hall where her strange, impossible, unpaid family was gathering, "somebody has to write down what happens next—and for the first time in nine hundred years, nobody gets to tell us what it says."
