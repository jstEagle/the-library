## 38. A New Kind of Account

The Guild of Impossible Things had been dead for eleven days, and nobody had come to bury it.

August noticed this on a gray morning while standing in the ruined counting hall, holding a broom she had no official authority to use. There was no official authority anymore. That was rather the point. The charter was ash in the Ember Square, the Concord's auditors had withdrawn with their seals and their silence, and the building itself stood like a gutted whale—grand, hollow, smelling faintly of old paper and older money.

She swept anyway. It gave her hands something to do that wasn't shaking.

The hall had survived the battle better than the square outside. Scorch marks fanned up the walls where Vaskarath's fire had licked through the windows. The great ledgers—the living ones, the ones that had written back—were gone entirely, burned by her own hand or dissolved when the charter died, depending on which theory Sefa preferred. What remained were the ordinary records: decades of debt tallies, interest schedules, collateral inventories. A mountain of paper describing everything people owed and nothing they had.

August looked at that mountain for a long time.

Then she did something no junior ledger-keeper had ever done in the history of the guild.

She started a second pile.

* * *

By noon, the debtors' choir had found her, because they always found her now. It was less a decision than a gravitational fact. Wenna the rope-maker arrived first, carrying her daughter and a sack of bread rolls that were technically payment on a debt the guild no longer had the legal existence to collect. Then came the twins Osk and Brannic, defrocked priests both, expelled from two different orders for two different heresies they insisted on describing as "early." Old Tam with his burned lungs. Marisol, who had owed an impossible thing for nineteen years and had never once been told what it was. And Sefa, last as always, slipping in through the broken door with the expression of someone arriving late to her own funeral and finding it improved.

"You're sorting paper," Sefa said.

"I'm reading it."

"Same thing, with you." She dropped onto a bench that had survived three audits and one dragon and helped herself to a bread roll. "What are we reading for?"

"Names." August held up a page. "Every entry in this ledger is a debt. But underneath most of them—" she tapped the margin, the place where she had hidden her own name for years, the place no one looks— "there's other writing. Notes. Corrections. Times someone paid early, or paid too much, or paid for a stranger. The guild recorded all of it and counted none of it."

Wenna shifted her daughter to her other hip. "Counted none of it how?"

"The ledger only ever ran one direction. What you owe. What you've failed to pay. What can be taken from you if you don't." August set the page down carefully, the way she'd once handled the founding contract. "There's another column hiding in these margins. What people have given. Freely. When nothing required them to."

The hall went quiet in the particular way rooms do when an idea arrives and everyone pretends they haven't heard it yet.

"That's not accounting," said Brannic.

"No," said August. "It's better than accounting."

Osk, the more skeptical twin—or possibly the less skeptical; even August had stopped trying to tell them apart—leaned over the table. "You want to start a new ledger."

"I want to start an honest one."

"With whose seal?" Brannic pressed. "A ledger without a seal is just a diary. Who enforces it? Who collects? Who comes to your door when you default on kindness?"

"No one," August said. "That's the whole design. You can't default on a gift. It isn't structured that way."

"And what happens," said Osk slowly, "when people realize there's no punishment attached? When they take the fire and give nothing back?"

"Then we've learned something true about them," said August. "The old guild never wanted to learn anything true about anybody. That's why it kept secrets instead of records."

Sefa was watching her with an expression August had learned to read during the long weeks underground: not doubt, but measurement. Weighing whether the person she'd chosen was still the person she'd chosen.

"And you?" Sefa asked quietly. "First name in the book?"

August had thought about this. She'd thought about very little else since the ashes cooled.

"Vaskarath," she said. "He gave everything he had left to shield us in the square. He's owed a debt so large the old system couldn't have contained it—and under the old system, that would have made him our master. Under this one, it makes him..." She searched for the word and didn't find it, so she made one. "...our elder. First signatory. Nothing more than anyone else, except in history."

"Your grandmother signed a contract," said Wenna. "Now you sign a different kind. You sure the blood doesn't tell?"

"It told before," August said. "This is me telling it back."

* * *

They spent four days arguing about what to call themselves, which August privately considered the healthiest institutional activity she had ever witnessed. The old guild had named itself in an afternoon, drunk on its own ambition, and the name had been a lie within a generation. These people took their time.

They rejected *The Free Ledger*—too proud. They rejected *The Choir*—too tied to what they'd been, a chorus assembled by what they owed. They rejected, after a long and surprisingly heated debate, *The Cooperative*, on the grounds that Marisol hated the word and Marisol had earned a veto through sheer endurance.

In the end it was Tam, wheezing, half-deaf, who settled it.

"We give fire," he rasped. "That's the trade. Dragon-fire, freely rationed, so the gods fade gentle instead of fighting. So call it what it is. The Firegivers."

Nobody loved it. Everybody could live with it. August wrote it at the top of the first page and found, to her surprise, that her hand didn't shake.

The mechanics took longer, because August refused to let sentiment rot into sloppiness. Rationed fire meant measuring, and measuring meant trust, and trust meant records—and records, she knew better than anyone alive, could become chains if you weren't careful.

So she built the new ledger differently. Three rules, read aloud at every gathering until they wore grooves in everyone's memory:

*A gift entered is a gift ended.* Once you gave something, the book recorded it and released it. No interest accrued. No repayment expected. No collector would ever come.

*A debt confessed is a debt shared.* Anyone could bring their burdens to the Firegivers—not to have them paid, but to have them witnessed. Half the crushing weight of owing, August had learned, was owing alone.

*No one signs twice.* Membership wasn't a status. You joined by doing one unrequired kindness, recorded by someone other than yourself, and after that you simply... were. There was nothing to renew, nothing to lapse, nothing to lose.

Brannic called it naive for six days straight. On the seventh day, he quietly paid a stranger's toll at the river gate and asked Osk to enter it, and August pretended not to notice that his hands were trembling slightly, the way hers had over the vault ledger a lifetime ago. Some people needed to be taught that giving wouldn't kill them.

* * *

The first distribution of fire happened on a cold evening at the mouth of the undercity stairs, where the ash had finally stopped falling upward and the air smelled, faintly and impossibly, of rain.

Vaskarath came up to meet them.

He was thinner. Everyone could see it and no one said it. The gods' rations were working—the eldest had accepted August's terms in the square, and the youngest had been buried under the cathedral rubble by the rain Sefa's final prayer had called down—but thinness sat strangely on a creature built to hold centuries. His scales had gone from banked-coal red to something like dusk, purple-gray shot through with embers that pulsed slow as sleeping breath.

"You look terrible," Sefa told him, because she was Sefa.

"I am dying," he agreed pleasantly. "More slowly now. It's remarkable how motivating a choice can be. For nine hundred years I had two options: burn out alone, or be devoured in war. Now I have a third option, which is to die gradually among people who bring me soup." He lowered his great head to regard the line of city folk gathered on the steps, each holding a small clay vessel. "I find I prefer it. Though the soup remains unnecessary. I don't actually eat."

"It's not for eating," said Wenna. "It's for the ritual."

"What ritual?"

"The one we invented Tuesday," said Sefa.

And it was true—they had. August had watched it happen with the fascination of someone studying a foreign currency. The choir had developed, without any charter or committee, a small ceremony around each giving: a word of thanks spoken by the giver, a word of witness spoken by a neighbor, the flame passed hand to hand down the line so it touched everyone before it went to its purpose. Lighting a widow's hearth. Warming a fever-house. Feeding the shrine-lamps of the god who died first, in the cold cathedral, so that his memory stayed lit even though his worshipers were gone.

It was inefficient. It was unmeasurable. August had tried, twice, to calculate its value in ledger terms and had produced figures so absurd she'd burned the scratch paper.

That evening she carried the last vessel herself, down to the small hearth in the undercity where Vaskarath rested, and fed the flame in while speaking the witness words: *"Given freely, seen truly, owed nothing."*

The old serpent watched the fire settle into his chest, brightening him briefly from within, like a window at sunset.

"Your grandmother," he said, "would have wanted the ledgers burned a hundred years ago. She knew. That was her punishment, really—to know exactly what she'd signed and to spend her whole life keeping the books balanced so no one else would ever have to read them." A plume of smoke, almost wistful. "You read them in a week."

"She raised me to count."

"She raised you to count *for* people. There's a difference, and it turns out to be the whole world." He closed his eyes. "Go home, August Okafor. Whatever that is now. Go to it."

* * *

Her room above the ruined hall had become, without discussion, something else. People left things there. Not payments—offerings, maybe, or just leavings. A jar of pickled plums from Wenna. A blanket from Marisol, who claimed she'd woven it badly and couldn't bear to look at it. A candle stub from Tam, which was nearly a joke between them now, Tam and his candles.

August sat at the table by lamplight with the new ledger open, entering the day. It was small work. A rope mended for the ferryman, unasked. Bread left on a sill. Two hours of Brannic's time spent teaching a child letters, which he would deny under oath. The kind of entries the old guild would have thrown away.

She was halfway down the page when Sefa let herself in without knocking, which was also tradition now.

"You've got ink on your face."

"It's my face now. Permanent feature."

Sefa dropped into the chair across from her and spun the ledger to look. "Three weeks. You've kept this thing going three weeks and nobody's cheated, nobody's defaulted, and the gods are fading polite as guests at the end of a party." She tapped the page. "Do you know what Corvane would say?"

"He'd say it can't last."

"He'd say it can't *be*. A ledger with no enforcement. An institution with no charter. He spent his whole life believing people only behave when they're priced." Sefa turned the book back around. "What do you say?"

August considered. Outside, the city was quieter than it had been in generations—the prayers answered gently now, the gods dimming like coals at evening instead of roaring. Somewhere below, an ancient serpent slept on gifts. Somewhere in the ashes of the Ember Square, the Concord's agents were still asking questions their masters hadn't authorized, and the answers were getting harder to contain.

"I say it lasts exactly as long as we keep choosing it," she said. "Which is terrifying. Which is also the only reason it counts."

Sefa grinned. "Good answer. Now close the book and eat your plums. Tomorrow's work."

But tomorrow's work arrived early, the way important things do.

It came past midnight, a knock at the hall's broken door—three knocks, formal, spaced like a summons. August opened it to find a young woman in traveling clothes soaked to the bone by real rain, mud to her knees, holding out a letter sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"For whoever keeps accounts here now," the woman said. "From the hill temples. They've heard the fire-giving has no price. They want to know—" she glanced at the paper in August's hands, then up at her face— "they want to know what happens when everyone in the world decides to come."

August looked down at the letter. Looked at the long road behind the messenger, dark and muddy and full, she suspected, of more roads behind it.

Then she stepped aside to let the rain in, and reached for a fresh page.
