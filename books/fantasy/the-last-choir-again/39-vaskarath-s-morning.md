## 39. Vaskarath's Morning

The way down to Vaskarath had changed.

Before the Audit, before the burning and the battle and the week of ash that followed, the route to his shrine had been a secret — Sefa leading August through flooded stairwells and behind the cisterns, both of them moving like thieves. Now there was a line.

August stood at the mouth of the undercity at dawn and looked at it: a queue of people winding down the old merchant stairs, holding candles, jars, bundles of kindling, loaves of bread, letters. A woman near the front carried a birdcage with nothing in it. A boy held a shoe. When August asked him about it, he shrugged with the terrible gravity of the very young and said, "It's what I have."

"What is this?" she asked Sefa, who had appeared at her elbow with two cups of chicory and the expression of someone who has been waiting to be asked.

"Offerings." Sefa handed her a cup. "Voluntary ones. Word got out after the battle — you know how word gets out in this city, which is to say sideways and overnight. People come down and give him something. Not because they owe him." She said *owe* the way a reformed smoker says the name of their old brand. "Because they want to."

"And he takes them?"

"He takes some of them. He refused my bread for three days running until I brought the kind he actually likes, which apparently is burnt on purpose, which I refuse to find charming." Sefa sipped her chicory. "There's a system now. There are *shifts*, August. Debtor's choir members run the queue. Marta keeps a list."

"A list?"

"A ledger," Sefa said, and watched her face when she said it, because Sefa always watched her face when she said that word, and August always pretended not to notice. "Of kindnesses given. Not debts owed. We talked about the difference for approximately four hours one night, and then we wrote it down so we'd stop arguing."

The queue moved slowly. Nobody complained. That was the strangest part — the undercity had never been patient in August's experience; patience was a currency and nobody down here had any. But they waited, shuffling forward by inches, each carrying whatever small impossible thing they'd decided to give away, and the waiting itself looked like part of the giving.

"You could go ahead," Sefa said. "You're — well. You know."

"I'm what?"

"The woman who burned the founding contract and unmade a nine-hundred-year-old charter and negotiated a starving pantheon down to rations. He calls you 'the little auditor.' With what I can only describe as fondness."

"I'm going to stand in the queue," August said, "like everyone else."

Sefa grinned and did not argue, which was its own small mercy.

* * *

The descent took an hour. August used it the way she used all empty time now — doing sums in her head, though the sums had changed. Before, she had counted what the guild owed and what the world would cost. Now she counted offerings. The rationing schedule she'd negotiated with the eldest god required a baseline of freely given fire — not stolen, not extracted, but offered — and the arithmetic of that was delicate in a way that kept her up at night. Too little, and the young gods' faction would start whispering about war again. Too much, and the givers would begin to feel the taking, and freely given things that start to feel taken stop being free. She had built a model. She had rebuilt it twice. Corvane, before he resigned, had looked at it for a long time and said only, "You've made compassion into a solvency problem," and she still didn't know if he'd meant it as praise.

She suspected he had. It was the worst thing about him.

The shrine opened around her the way it always did — gradually, then all at once. First the warmth, rising through the stone like memory through an old house. Then the light, amber and low, pooled in the cavern the way lamplight pools in a bowl. And then Vaskarath.

He was bigger than she ever remembered and smaller than she feared, which she suspected was the point of him. The dragon lay coiled in the deep hollow where the guild had once kept him as an asset, and the difference was visible everywhere: no chains, no wards, no brass instruments bolted to the rock to measure his output. Just an enormous ancient body, dull gold gone grey at the edges, wings folded like a cathedral deciding whether to be a ruin. Around him, arranged with a carelessness that must have been deliberate, were the offerings. Candles by the hundred. Jars of honey. A child's drawing of a dragon that was mostly teeth. The shoe. Someone had left a full set of ledgers, blank ones, good paper, stacked in a pyramid, and August felt her chest do something complicated about that.

He knew she was there before she cleared her throat. Of course he did.

"Little auditor." His voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always had — less a sound than a settling, tectonic, patient. "You're standing in the queue like everyone else."

"Sefa told you."

"Sefa tells me everything. She believes I am a good listener." One vast eye opened, amber ringed with grey, and regarded her with something that had taken August months to correctly identify, because she had never had a frame for it. It was affection. Unhurried, undemanding, entirely without agenda. "She is right. Come here. Mind the candle pyramid; the boy who built it will know if it falls, and he is very serious."

August picked her way through the offerings and sat down against a warm ridge of scale, in the spot that had become hers without either of them discussing it. The heat soaked into her back. Above them, filtered through a hundred feet of stone, the city was waking up — carts, bells, the first arguments of the morning. Down here it was quiet in the way a bank vault is quiet, except that everything in it was alive.

"You look tired," Vaskarath said.

"I'm always tired."

"Yes. But lately you wear it differently. Before, it sat on you like debt. Now it sits on you like work." The eye blinked, slow as a season turning. "This is an improvement. Work ends. Debt only changes hands."

August pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. She had rehearsed this conversation, insofar as she rehearsed anything anymore; the choir teased her about drafting talking points for casual dinners. But rehearsals dissolved the moment she was actually here, in the heat and the hush, with the last dragon breathing slow enough to measure seasons by.

"I found something," she said. "In the ruins of the hall. Going through what was left of the archives — most of it burned, obviously, but fire is not thorough the way people think. Fire skips. It leaves things."

"It does," Vaskarath said. "Fire has always been selective in its affections. What did it leave you?"

"A letter." She took it out of her coat. She had been carrying it for six days without opening it, which even she recognized as a pattern. "It's addressed to the Guild of Impossible Things. Care of the Ledger-Keeper. It's old — the wax seal is the pre-charter seal, the kind they used before the founding contract. And the handwriting—" She stopped. Started again. "I've seen it before. In the margins of the oldest accounts. In the founding contract, above the signature. Vaskarath. It's my grandmother's handwriting. It's almost *my* handwriting. And I can't open it, and I've opened every hard thing in my life like it was nothing, and I've been sitting outside your door for six days working up to asking you—"

"Whether you should read it."

"Whether she would have wanted me to."

The silence that followed was long even by dragon standards. August counted his breaths to keep herself steady — she had learned that trick during the negotiations, when terror needed converting into numbers to be survivable. Four breaths in. Sixteen out. A creature built for patience.

"Your grandmother," Vaskarath said at last, "was named Yewande Okafor."

August went still. "You never said her name. Not once. Not in the whole—"

"No. Because names are weight, and you were already carrying too much." The great head shifted, lowering until one eye was level with her, close enough that she could see herself reflected in it, small and doubled. "I will say it now, because you have earned the weight. Yewande Okafor came down here herself, once. Did you know that?"

"She signed the contract. I read it. I read it aloud in front of the entire Concord while the sky was on fire. I know exactly what she signed."

"You know what she *agreed* to. Those are different documents." Vaskarath's breath stirred her hair, warm and smelling faintly of woodsmoke and, strangely, of honey — of the offerings. "She came to me the night before the signing. Alone. No guild escort, no witnesses. She was young. Younger than you are now. And she sat where you are sitting, in nearly the same place, and she asked me a question."

August's hands had gone cold despite the heat. "What question?"

"She asked me if there was another way. Any other way. She had seen the terms — the guild's survival purchased with my fire, her bloodline posted as surety, generation after generation of Okafors held as collateral against a debt they never chose. She saw all of it, and she came to ask the creature being robbed whether he knew of an escape clause." A sound moved through him that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be geological. "And do you know what I told her? I told her the truth, which was: yes. Burn the contract. Refuse the bargain. Let the guild die unborn, let the gods starve or fight or fade, and let the world take its chances honestly. I told her the truth, and she thanked me, and she went up those stairs, and the next morning she signed anyway."

"Why?" August's voice cracked on it. "Why would she—"

"Because of the letter you are holding." The eye did not blink. "Because she had already written it, the night before, sealed it, and left instructions that it reach the ledger-keeper of her line when the debt was done — whenever that was, however that was. Because she was not signing to save the guild, child. She was signing to buy time. Time for the world to grow into something that could survive the truth. Time for someone — she did not know who, she hoped, but did not dare presume — to come down those stairs one day and finish the thought." His voice softened, which for a creature his size was like watching a mountain kneel. "She told me, the last thing she said to me at that door: *If my blood ever comes back here, tell them I'm sorry it took so long.*"

August did not remember deciding to cry. She was simply crying, quietly, the way she did everything, and Vaskarath did not comment, merely curled slightly closer so that his warmth surrounded her more completely, and waited, because he was very good at waiting.

When she could speak again, she wiped her face with her sleeve and looked at the letter in her hands. The wax seal caught the amber light. Her grandmother's hand, pressed into wax nine hundred years ago, waiting.

"She wanted them burned," August said. "The ledgers. You said—"

"Open it," Vaskarath said gently. "Then you won't need me to say it."

* * *

The seal broke easier than it should have, as if the wax had been waiting.

Inside, one page, written in a hand that was August's hand aged and steadied, and it began:

*To whoever of my blood reads this —*

*I have just come from the serpent. He told me the truth, which is more than anyone in that marble room upstairs has done, and I find I cannot stop thinking about the shape of it. They will call this a bargain tomorrow. It is not a bargain. A bargain requires two parties who might walk away. I am signing because the guild has a knife at the city's throat disguised as a charter, and because I am the only person in that room who bothered to count what the knife would cost.*

*But I want one thing written down, and I am writing it here because this letter is the only honest document I will ever produce: the ledgers should burn.*

*Not today. Today they hold the city together, such as it is. But someday — someday there will be someone who can do what needs doing without the charter propping them up, and on that day, every account we keep, every debt we track, every impossible thing we have priced and filed and owned — burn it. All of it. Start over with blank pages and better rules. Debt is how they caught us. Do not let them catch anyone with it again.*

*I am sorry. I am sorry I am choosing the safe coward's path tonight, and I am sorry that the cost of my safety will land on you. If you are reading this, you paid for my life with yours, and I never met you, and I love you, and I hope you found people worth the price.*

*Burn them. Burn them all, and write your own name large on the first free page.*

*Yewande Okafor*

August read it twice. Then she folded it along its original creases with the precise movements of someone whose hands needed a task, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where she suspected it had been traveling toward since before she was born.

"Well," she said, and her voice came out wrecked and steady at the same time, which she decided was allowed. "You could have told me any of this. At any point. During the war, during the negotiations, literally any of the forty-seven moments I was spiraling about whether my bloodline made me complicit—"

"You would not have believed me." Vaskarath settled his chin onto the stone, bringing his face level with hers, and his expression was the one she had catalogued during chapter after chapter of their strange acquaintance: the exhausted tenderness of something very old watching something very young insist on learning things the hard way. "Some truths must be excavated, not delivered. You are a ledger-keeper, August. You know this. An entry means nothing unless the reader arrives at it themselves."

"That is not how accounting works."

"It is exactly how accounting works. Why do you think audits exist?"

August laughed — wet, surprised, real. Above them the city bells rang the hour, muffled to a heartbeat by all that stone between. The offering-candles flickered in the draft of his breath, hundreds of small flames, freely given.

"There's a meeting tonight," she said. "The cooperative. We're voting on the charter question — whether we write one at all, or stay unchartered forever. Half the choir wants structure. Marta wants bylaws. Bylaws, Vaskarath. The woman once forged an archbishop's signature as a joke and she wants *bylaws*." She leaned her head back against his scales. "I keep thinking about what to say to them. About what we owe each other, if the answer isn't debt. I've spent my whole life fluent in one language and I'm trying to learn another one with no dictionary."

"Tell them about the queue upstairs," Vaskarath said. "Tell them about the boy with the shoe. Tell them that yesterday a man gave me a debt he'd been carrying for thirty years — a real one, owed to his dead father, written on paper — and asked me to eat it, and I did, and he walked out lighter than any miracle I have ever performed." The amber eye closed halfway. "Tell them that the first entry in the new kind of account was written by someone who had nothing else to give. That is your dictionary, little auditor. You have been reading it all week. You simply haven't realized it's in your language yet."

August sat with that, in the warmth, in the hush, with her grandmother's letter pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Outside the shrine she could hear the queue shifting, the murmur of people waiting to give things away, and somewhere in it Sefa's voice laughing at something, and somewhere beyond that the whole ragged unfinished choir of her life, assembling itself one kindness at a time.

"Vaskarath," she said. "Her name. Yewande. Would you say it again? Once. I want to hear how it sounds in your voice."

He said it. It sounded like the closing of a very long account, balanced at last.

Then, as she rose to go — as she straightened her coat and touched the letter one more time and turned toward the stairs and the queue and the day — the old dragon spoke again, quieter, and the words stopped her with one foot on the first step.

"August. Before you go up. There is something you should know." The amber eye opened fully now, and in it she saw reflected not the shrine but something farther off, deeper in, the dark places under the dark places where even dragons do not like to look. "The youngest god sent an emissary last night. While the city slept. He did not come to the queue. He did not bring an offering." A slow exhale, smoke-grey in the candlelight. "He came to make me a private offer. Fire enough to live fat forever, he said, if I would help him persuade the others that the rationing is a cage." The eye held hers, and held, and did not blink. "I refused. But he left something behind, little auditor. A message, addressed to you. And August — it is written on a page torn from a ledger I have never seen, in a hand that knows your grandmother's script perfectly, as if it learned it from the inside."

August stood very still on the cold stone step, her grandmother's letter warm against her heart, and heard herself ask the only question that mattered:

"When can I see it?"
