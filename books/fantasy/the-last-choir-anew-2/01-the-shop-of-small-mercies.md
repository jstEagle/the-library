## 1. The Shop of Small Mercies

There are three rules to selling charms in a kingdom where magic is outlawed, and I wrote all three myself, which is the only reason I've survived twenty years of following them.

Rule one: never sell anything that works.

Rule two: never let the customer suspect rule one.

Rule three — and this is the important one, the load-bearing wall of my entire gray little life — *never go into the cellar when someone might be watching.*

I broke rule three this morning, but we'll get to that.

The shop is called the Shop of Small Mercies, a name I chose because it sounds pious without being religious, comforting without being specific, and faintly apologetic in a way that discourages haggling. It sits at the crooked end of Candlemakers' Row in Vellharrow, wedged between a chandler who smells of tallow and grief and a notary whose sign has read NOTARY & SONS for thirty years despite his having no sons, only a cat named Litigation. My window displays what I like to think of as an honest dishonesty: dried lavender bundles against nightmares (they smell nice; nightmares are mostly boredom anyway), iron nails from the old cathedral demolition "for protection" (iron is iron), knotted red thread for luck in love (red thread is cheap, love is rare, and hope will pay for either).

And hanging in the window, catching the light like a promise nobody should keep: glass beads I call *tear-catchers*. You wear one over your heart, I tell customers, and it drinks your sorrow before it can settle. What actually happens is nothing, which in Vellharrow is a miracle of restraint on everyone's part.

My first customer of the day was Mistress Halloway, who buys a luck-knot every Tuesday for her son's fishing boat and every Wednesday for her other son's fishing boat, because she cannot remember which son drowned and has decided to cover both possibilities. She paid me four coppers, told me her hip was acting up, and asked — as she asks every week — whether I had anything for joints.

"Lavender," I said.

"You said that last week."

"And it worked, didn't it? You're walking."

"I'm limping."

"Walking *with character*." I handed her the bundle. "No charge for the flattery."

She left smiling, which meant I'd done my job, which meant I'd done no magic whatsoever, which meant everything was exactly as it should be. Twenty years of Tuesdays like this one. Twenty years of harmless thread and honest lies and the slow, comfortable erosion of whatever I used to be before I became a man who sold lavender to widows.

If you'd told me at twenty-five that this would be my life — that I would come to treasure it, even, the way you treasure a coat too small but warm — I'd have laughed. Or wept. At twenty-five I still had enough of myself to do either.

But I'm getting ahead of things, and a shopkeeper knows better than to spend inventory he hasn't stocked.

* * *

The Ministry came at half past eleven, which was rude of them, since half past eleven is when I take my tea and I have strong feelings about interruptions to tea. Not that I shared those feelings with Inspector Quillon's clerk, who arrived first and announced the inspection by rapping twice and letting himself in, because Ministry clerks believe doors are suggestions made to lesser people.

He was a narrow young man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular posture of someone whose spine has been issued to him by the state. Behind him, unhurried, came Inspector Quillon herself.

Now, I had met inspectors before. They come around twice a year, sometimes three if there's been trouble — a hedge-witch burned in the Low Quarter, a child found speaking backwards, some poor soul whose fever dreams got reported as visions. The inspections follow a script older than most of the buildings on this street. They look at my wares. They sniff at my lavender. They ask if I understand that the sale of enchanted goods carries penalties up to and including the fire, and I say yes, inspector, I understand, and they leave satisfied that Candlemakers' Row contains nothing more dangerous than bad poetry.

This one was different, and I knew it before she said a word, because she looked at the *shelves* instead of the *window*.

Sera Quillon — I learned her name later, though at the time she was just the woman in the slate-gray coat with the Ministry's brass pin set slightly crooked, as if she'd fastened it in the dark or didn't care — was perhaps forty, with a face built for disapproval and hair going silver at the temples in a way that suggested the silver had been earned rather than inherited. Her eyes did something I hadn't seen an inspector's eyes do in years.

They counted.

Not the trinkets. The room. The doorframes. The floorboards near the back where the grain runs wrong. Me.

"Mister...?" She consulted a ledger. "Winterbourne. Isandro Winterbourne." She pronounced it carefully, the way you pronounce a word you intend to test later. "Proprietor."

"That's what the sign says," I agreed. "Though I've considered 'Emporium.' Feels ambitious. I don't want people expecting more than thread."

She did not smile. Inspectors are trained out of smiling somewhere around their second year, I assume; it's probably a module. "Routine inspection under the Statute of Order, section twelve. All goods subject to examination. All premises."

"*All* premises," I repeated pleasantly. "Including the cellar?"

Something flickered behind her eyes — quick, gone, like a fish turning under ice. "Is there a cellar?"

"There's a cellar," said the clerk, already consulting his papers. "Registered. Storage use. Last inspected..." He squinted. "Four years ago."

"Cellars flood," I said. "Damp gets into everything. Ruins the thread. You'd be wasting the Ministry's valuable time on mold."

Inspector Quillon looked at me for a long moment, and I want to be clear about what happened in that moment, because I have replayed it enough times since to know it mattered: she looked at me the way a person looks at a lock they suspect is newer than the door it's set into.

"Then we won't waste time on the cellar," she said. "We'll waste it properly, on the shop."

And she did. Thoroughly, cheerfully, terribly. She picked up my tear-catchers and held them to the light. She unknotted a luck-thread and examined the knot-work with the focus of a woman reading a confession. She smelled the lavender and said, "Cut too late in the season. Loses its oils." Which was true, and annoyed me, because it meant she knew plants, and a Ministry officer who knows plants is a Ministry officer who knows what *belladonna* looks like drying above a hearth.

"No wards?" she asked, setting down a nail from the cathedral lot. "Charmsellers usually claim their goods are warded. Even the fraudulent ones. It's good theater."

"Theater costs extra," I said. "I deal in honesty. It's cheaper and less likely to get anyone burned."

"Honesty." She turned the word over like a coin of doubtful minting. "That's rare in your trade, Mister Winterbourne."

"My trade *is* rare, Inspector. That's rather the point."

The clerk snorted. She didn't. She finished her circuit, wrote three lines in her ledger — I saw *compliant*, which stung oddly, like being called adequate by someone whose opinion you'd secretly wanted — and paused at the door with her hand on the frame.

"One thing," she said. "Reports come out of this district now and again. Old reports. Resonance readings, they used to call them, before the instruments were decommissioned. Nothing actionable. But Candlemakers' Row sits close to the mountain's root, and the Ministry likes to know what's stored where, near the roots." Her eyes went, briefly, to the floorboards. To the exact spot, I realized much later, where the stairs begin. "Keep your cellar dry, Mister Winterbourne."

"I always do," I said.

It was the second lie I'd told her. The first was my smile.

* * *

They left. I drank my tea cold, standing at the window, watching the slate-gray coat recede down Candlemakers' Row like a cloud deciding whether to rain.

Here is what I did not tell her. Here is what I have not told anyone in twenty years, and what I am telling you now only because a story has to start somewhere and mine starts, as most ruinous stories do, with a door.

Beneath my shop there is a gate. Not a door to a cellar — the cellar is real, it holds barrels and damp and spiders of frankly aristocratic bearing — but beneath the cellar's far wall, set into the living rock of the mountain itself, there is a door of black wood banded in green bronze, and it does not open onto any space that architects would recognize. It opens onto the Undercourt. The realm below. The country under the country, where the fae keep their courts and their bargains and their long, patient appetites.

Twenty years ago I sealed it. That was my work — not charm-selling, not lavender, but *sealing*, the great labor of my youth, done with rites I no longer fully remember in a language I no longer fully dare speak. And the price of that sealing was my true name, paid out of me syllable by syllable into the keeping of the Undercourt's archivist, so that no working could ever call me back to undo what I'd done. A perfect lock, keyed to a man who could never again prove he existed.

Or so I believed for two decades, in the way you believe a roof holds: not joyfully, but daily, until the day it leaks.

Which brings me back to this morning. To the crack in twenty years of silence. To rule three, and how I broke it.

Quillon's clerk had barely cleared the corner before I was moving, down the cellar steps with a lamp, past the barrels, to the black door — because when a Ministry inspector says *resonance* in the tone she said it, a man with my particular history doesn't wait for a second invitation to check his foundations.

The wards were intact. That was the first thing I saw, and it should have been a comfort: the old sigils along the doorframe still glowing their dim, patient green, unchanged, faithful, dumb as stones.

Except.

At the bottom edge of the door, low where the shadow pools, where no one would ever look unless they were kneeling in the dark with a lamp and twenty years of dread — there was a new mark. Small. Fresh. The edges of it still sharp, the way a brand is sharp before time blunts it.

A contract-seal. I knew the shape the way you know a scar in a mirror. Someone below had signed something against this door. Something had been *promised* through it.

And the wards, which had not flared in twenty years — which had not so much as shimmered when the Ministry walked this street, when the city rang its bells, when the world above did all its loud indifferent living —

the wards pulsed once, softly, like a heart remembering it had one.

I stood there a long while, an old man in a lamplight cellar, listening to a silence that had just stopped being silent.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself seals shift, stone settles, mountains dream. I climbed the stairs, put away the lamp, straightened the lavender, and prepared to continue being Isandro Winterbourne, seller of harmless things, keeper of a perfectly quiet life.

That night, just at dusk, someone knocked on my shop door.

Small knuckles. Twice. And when I opened it — thinking lost child, thinking beggar, thinking Ministry trick — there stood a mud-streaked girl no older than eight or nine, looking up at me with eyes like struck flint, and she said:

*"Veth-annur seli. Doru keth vael."*

*I know what sleeps beneath your floor, keeper. Open the small door, or the big one opens for you.*

In the First Tongue. The language of the gate.

The language I had buried with my name.
