## 4. The Girl Who Sold Herself

The trouble with being nobody is that everybody assumes you'll do anything for bread.

I learned that early. Ashmarket orphans learn it the way other children learn their letters — by heart, and usually from someone who's about to take advantage of them.

My name was Wren before any of this happened. I want to be clear about that. Whatever they took out of me, whatever's missing when I look in a window, the name stayed. Names are like burrs; they stick to the inside of you where nobody thinks to look. Everything else was fair game.

* * *

Here is how the Ashmarket worked: there were four kinds of people in it. The ones who sold things, the ones who stole things, the ones who had nothing left to sell or steal, and the ones who watched. I was mostly category three with ambitions of category two, though I'd been known to dabble in watching when the pickings were thin.

I'd been on my own since I was seven, which sounds tragic until you meet my mother, at which point it sounds like a sensible career move. She'd gone off one morning to "find work" and found something else instead — a man with a boat, according to the gossip, though knowing her it might have been a man with a hat. Either way, she never came back, and after the first hungry month I stopped expecting her to. Grief, in the Ashmarket, is a luxury item. You can't eat it. You can't sell it for much.

What I could sell was small errands. Running messages between the fishwives and the fence behind the tannery. Holding places in queue. Standing outside shops looking pathetic enough that soft-hearted grandmothers bought me rolls, which I then split with the other gutter-mice because that's the law — you share food, always, because next week the roll-buyers might love someone else better.

That was my whole world, right up until the autumn I turned ten, when the veiled lady came to the market.

* * *

She didn't belong there. That was the first thing. Ashmarket has a smell — smoke, fish guts, wet wool, the sweet rot of overripe pears nobody can afford — and she moved through it like the smell couldn't touch her. Long grey cloak, hood up, and beneath the hood a veil of silver gauze so fine you could almost see through it, except that every time I tried, my eyes slid off her face the way rain slides off oiled canvas.

The second thing was that everyone else's eyes slid off her too. She walked the whole length of the market row and not one vendor called out to her, not one cutpurse marked her purse, and the dogs — who will bark at a leaf if the leaf looks suspicious — lay down flat as she passed.

I should have run. I want that on the record. Ten-year-old Wren, future legend of the gutter, should have taken one look at the wrongness of her and bolted.

But she stopped in front of me. And she said, in a voice like warm honey poured over cold stone:

"You're hungry."

Not *are you hungry*. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"I'm fine," I said, which in Ashmarket means *I am dying of hunger but I'll be hanged before I let you know it.*

"You're fine," she agreed, and there was something in the way she said it — a tilt, almost amused — that made me feel like we were both agreeing on a fiction for politeness' sake. Then she held out her hand, and in it was a bun. Not just any bun. A bun from Marisol's stall, the good ones, still steaming, studded with currants, worth more than I usually made in two days.

Marisol was twenty feet away, selling her last dozen. I counted. There were eleven.

I took the bun. Obviously. I'm an orphan, not a philosopher.

"Eat," said the veiled lady. "Then we'll talk."

* * *

For six days, she came back.

Every day: a bun, sometimes cheese, once a whole meat pie that I ate so fast I burned my tongue and would have fought anyone who tried to take the rest. Every day she sat with me on the steps of the shuttered dyehouse while I ate, and she talked. Not about herself — I never got so much as a name — but about me. Where I slept. Who looked after me. Whether the cold got into my chest in winter, whether I coughed, whether I'd ever been truly ill.

At the time, I thought: kind. Strange, but kind. In the Ashmarket, kindness is like weather — you don't ask where it comes from, you just enjoy it while it lasts.

Now I look back and count the questions and think: inventory. She wasn't asking after me. She was appraising the vessel.

On the seventh day, she said, "Would you like to come away with me?"

And I — fool, fool, starving little fool — said yes before she finished the sentence.

Because here's what she offered, and here's why it worked: not gold. Not sweets. She offered *enough*. A bed with a blanket on it. Meals every day, not just days when luck was feeling generous. "You'll have a room of your own," she said, "and no one will ever make you hungry again."

Do you understand what that's worth to a child who has slept in a coal cellar and eaten candle wax in a bad week? Not luxury. Luxury means nothing to someone who's never had the baseline. She offered the baseline. She offered *enough*, and enough is the most beautiful word in any language if you've never had it.

"There's only one thing," she said. "A formality. A paper to sign, saying you're mine to care for. It's how it's done. Guardianship."

"A paper," I repeated.

"Just ink and a name." She produced it from her cloak — a single sheet, pale as skimmed milk, covered in writing that made my head hurt to look at, all loops and hooks like bird tracks. "You can't read it, I know. But I'll tell you what it says: I promise you bread and safety and a home. And you promise—" a pause, delicate as a needle finding fabric "—to be mine."

"Yours," I said. "Like a servant?"

"Like a daughter," she said, and under the silver veil I swear her voice smiled. "Better than a servant. Servants can leave."

Well. When you put it like that.

She gave me a little pen, thin and black-feathered, heavier than it looked. She told me where to write my name — on a line near the bottom, under a block of text that squirmed when I looked at it directly, like flies under ice.

I wrote *Wren*. Just Wren. No family name; I never had one. Four letters, crooked as a broken fence.

The ink sank into the page like water into drought-parched earth. All of it. Every drop, gone, so fast my hand jerked back. For half a heartbeat the paper glowed faintly, warm as fresh bread, and I remember thinking — this is the last coherent, sensible thought I had for quite some time — *that's odd.*

Then the veiled lady folded the paper, tucked it away, and said, "There. Now you're safe forever."

Reader, I believed her. That's the part that still burns. Not that she tricked me — trickery I could respect, trickery is a trade like any other — but that I wanted so badly to believe her that I did the tricking for her. She didn't even have to lie. She just had to let me hope.

* * *

I don't remember walking to wherever we went.

That's another thing I should say plainly: there are holes in me now. Not memories exactly — memories you can miss, the way you miss a tooth. These holes are stranger. There's a stretch of that night that simply isn't there, and when I probe at its edges, my mind slides away like it's been greased. One moment I was on the dyehouse steps, licking currant stickiness off my thumb. The next—

The next I was waking up.

* * *

I woke in a narrow room with whitewashed walls and a ceiling I couldn't see, because above the reach of the single candle the dark just went on, patient and total, like the inside of a bell.

I knew immediately that something was wrong, and it took me a long, cold minute to work out what, and when I worked it out I wished I hadn't.

There was no mirror in the room. Of course there wasn't; poor orphans don't get mirrors. But there was a basin on the washstand, full of clean water, and clean water shows you your face same as any glass.

It showed me the ceiling. The candle flame. The wall behind me.

It did not show me me.

I leaned over that basin until my nose nearly touched the water. Ripples spread. Candlelight shivered across the surface. And where my reflection should have been — where I had spent ten years making faces at myself in puddles and polished spoons and shop windows, usually rude ones — there was nothing. Just the room, seen empty, as if I weren't there at all.

I did what any reasonable person would do. I screamed. Then I threw the water on the floor, in case it was the water's fault. Then I checked the candle flame, which showed the room without me. Then the blade of the little knife someone had left by the bread — bread, real bread, sitting on the table as promised — which also declined to acknowledge my existence.

Somewhere in the middle of all this, I noticed the hum.

It started low, in the center of my chest, behind the breastbone. A single sustained note, very quiet, like the memory of a bell that had been struck somewhere far away and long ago. Not painful. Worse than painful. *Present.* As if someone had hung a tiny bell inside my ribs, and it rang whenever my heart beat against it, and it would go on ringing, patiently, forever.

When I pressed my palm flat against my sternum, the hum answered. Faintly. Politely. Like something acknowledging my knock.

"That's yours," said a voice from the doorway. "Or rather — you're its."

The veiled lady stood there, silver gauze catching the candlelight. Up close, through the weave, I caught a glimpse of what lay beneath, just for a second — something pale and smooth and wrong, a face like an egg that had been told about human faces secondhand — and then my eyes slid away again, mercifully.

"What did you do to me?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"Nothing was done *to* you, dearling. Something was done *by* you. There's a difference, and the Court is very firm about the difference." She glided into the room and set something on the table beside the bread: a small brass bell, no bigger than my thumb, with a crack running down its side. "You signed. Freely. I watched you do it. Ink and intent. The oldest contract-shape there is."

"I signed a guardianship. You said—"

"I said you'd have bread and safety and a home." She tilted her head. "All true. Look around you. Is there not bread? Are you not safe? Is this not a home?" A pause, and that needle-tilt of amusement again. "I never said whose home. Or for how long. Or what 'safety' would cost you. You didn't ask. Nobody asks. Nobody ever asks. Do you know why?"

"Why," I whispered.

"Because the hungry only ever hear the first half of any bargain." She reached out — I flinched, but her fingers, when they brushed my hair, were cool and light as moth wings — and she smoothed it back from my forehead almost tenderly. "Eat your bread, Wren-of-no-surname. Grow strong and quiet. You'll be collected soon enough, and delivered below, and there you'll learn what your voice is actually for."

"Collected? Below? Below *where*?"

She was already turning away. At the door she paused, and though I couldn't see her face, I felt her look back at me the way you feel sun through a window.

"The mountain eats its debts," she said. "Yours comes due at the Turning of the Year. Eleven days, give or take. Spend them well — they're the last ones you'll own."

The door closed. The hum in my chest rose, just slightly, like a held breath.

* * *

Eleven days. I've done arithmetic under worse pressure — I once calculated how many days of stolen bread equaled a beating from the tannery foreman (answer: three) — but never like this. Eleven days to figure out what I'd signed, what I'd lost, and whether either could be gotten back.

I spent day one crying, because I'm honest with myself about these things and pretending otherwise helps nobody. Day two I spent testing the room, which was a cell, whichever way you dressed it up — locked, though oddly the lock grew bored of holding me by evening, and I found the corridor beyond it empty and dim and smelling of stone and old singing. Day three I spent learning the rules of my new condition, which were as follows:

One: mirrors, water, polished metal — none of them knew me anymore.

Two: the hum in my chest changed pitch around certain doors. Some doors made it sink low and sour. Others — doors that breathed cold, doors that hummed *back* — made it rise toward something like eagerness. I hated those doors. I hated most that part of me that didn't hate them.

Three: bells. Anywhere within hearing of a bell — the great cathedral bells of Vellharrow, distant and muffled through however many layers of stone I was buried under — the hum in my chest turned over in its sleep and listened. And once, just once, when the cathedral rang noon, I felt something answer from deep below, a vast slow tone rolling up through the mountain's bones, and every hair on my body stood up, because that answering note had sounded, unmistakably, like my own name.

Four — and this is the one that mattered — the holes in me had edges, and the edges could be followed.

* * *

I escaped on the sixth night, through a door that hummed welcome, down stairs that weren't entirely convinced of their own existence, and out through a seam in the world that opened onto — I checked twice, pinched myself, checked again — the mouth of Tanner's Alley in the Ashmarket, at dusk, eleven hundred miles and one impossible depth from where I'd woken.

Don't ask me how. I've been asked. The honest answer is that the First Tongue lives in my chest now along with the bell, and sometimes it opens things for me the way a well-trained dog fetches sticks, and I don't question it because questioning it feels like pulling a thread that holds up something load-bearing.

What matters is that I was free, and free meant one thing: find help. And there was exactly one place in Vellharrow where a girl with a hollow chest and no reflection might find someone who understood the words for what she was.

I knew this because on the fifth night, pacing my cell, I'd heard the walls whisper. Old walls do that down there — murmur in the First Tongue, gossip older than the city. Most of it slid past me. But one phrase kept circling back, patient as tide:

*The doorkeeper keeps a shop. The doorkeeper remembers. The gatekeeper of the gray door sells small mercies in the street called Lantern Row, and he alone below knows what was sealed and what was paid.*

So: Lantern Row. A shop of small mercies. A man who remembered.

By then I'd worked out what I was, more or less. Not a daughter. Not a servant. An *account*. A debt with legs, walking around in borrowed skin, waiting to be collected. And the thing that had been taken from me — the shining, weightless, essential thing that used to live behind my eyes and show itself in mirrors — was sitting in a ledger somewhere under the mountain, filed and shelved and accruing interest.

They'd given me bread. They'd given me safety. They'd given me a home.

And they'd taken everything that made me someone to give those things *to*.

I came up out of Tanner's Alley with ash in my hair and a plan the size and shape of a prayer: find the doorkeeper, tell him everything, and pray to whatever gods bother listening to gutter-mice that he was kinder than the veiled lady, and braver, and that mercy — small or otherwise — was actually for sale.

The shop, when I found it, was shuttered against the evening. Through the window: trays of trinkets, ribbons, copper charms gone green at the edges. Nothing magical. Nothing dangerous. Nothing, I thought bitterly, worth the walk.

But underneath my feet, beneath the floorboards, beneath the cellar I could smell through the cracks — something hummed. A ward-line, old and gray and tired, wrapped around a door I couldn't see. And the bell in my chest, my unwelcome passenger, lifted its head like a hound scenting home.

I raised my hand and knocked.

And because I am what I am now, the words that came out of my mouth were not "please" or "help" or even "hello."

They were First Tongue. Old words. Gate words. Words I had never learned, arriving fully formed, in a child's cracked voice, at the door of the only man alive who would understand exactly how much trouble they meant.

The lights along his doorstep flared like struck matches.

Inside, I heard something fall over.
