## 5. The Ministry Comes Calling

The Ministry of Order had a way of arriving that was less like a knock and more like weather. You felt the pressure change before anything happened. The birds went quiet on my roof — I kept a family of starlings up there for exactly this reason; they were better sentries than any ward I could legally own — and the street outside the Shop of Small Mercies developed a sudden interest in its own boots.

I was grinding dried feverfew when it started. Through the window I watched two gray-coated clerics take up positions at either end of Copperlane like bookends, and a third begin an unhurried inspection of my neighbor's shutters, which were not his business and never had been. Then came the fourth figure, walking straight down the middle of the lane with the particular stride of someone who has never once needed to hurry.

Inspector Sera Quillon.

She'd been in the shop eleven days ago for the routine quarterly inspection — the one where she'd counted my inventory twice, read my license three times, and looked at me once in a way I still hadn't managed to stop thinking about. Routine inspections ended with a nod and a stamp. Hers had ended with her standing in my doorway for a full ten seconds after she should have left, saying nothing, before walking out into the rain without her hood up.

Now she pushed open my door and brought the cold in with her.

"Charm-seller." She said my trade the way other people said *suspect*.

"Inspector." I set down the mortar. "Back so soon? I'm flattered. Most of your colleagues only visit when something's on fire."

"Something is always on fire in Vellharrow." She hung her coat on the hook by the door — uninvited, unhurried, the gesture of a woman claiming territory — and surveyed my shelves. "You've rearranged."

"I rearrange seasonally. It's good for sales. People trust a man whose stock moves."

"People trust a man whose stock doesn't move," she corrected mildly. "Movement suggests turnover. Turnover suggests customers. Customers suggest belief. Belief, in this district, suggests trouble." She picked up a luck-knot from the display by the window, turned it over in her fingers. It was braided horsehair and river clay, worth perhaps two coppers, and entirely innocent, which was why I sold forty of them a month. "This one's new."

"You have an excellent memory for knots."

"It's not the knot I remember." She put it back precisely where it had been, which was somehow more unsettling than if she'd thrown it. "It's the dust."

Ah, I thought. There it is.

* * *

Let me explain about the dust, because the dust was going to be a problem.

A charm-shop lives or dies by its dust. Real dust — honest, accumulated, undisturbed — says *nothing happens here*. It says *this man is boring and his wares are worse*. The Ministry's inspectors are trained to read dust the way sailors read clouds, and for twenty years I had curated mine like a garden: a fine gray velvet on the top shelves, a respectable fuzz behind the counter, a careful scatter across the ledger no one ever opened.

But Wren had been living in my cellar for six days now, and children are dust's natural enemy. She moved through the back rooms like a small storm system. She'd swept the stairs. She'd wiped down the workbench. Two days ago she'd actually *dusted*, the little savage, humming to herself in that language I couldn't stop hearing even when she wasn't speaking it aloud — a bell-tone under the skin of every silence.

So when Inspector Quillon trailed one gloved finger along the shelf above my ledger and examined the fingertip, what she found was clean wood.

"No dust," she observed.

"Dry winter. I oil the shelves."

"In winter?"

"Wood contracts. Oiling prevents cracking. Cracking prevents complaints. Complaints prevent sales." I was aware, even as I said it, that I was constructing an explanation the way a bad liar builds a wall: brick by brick, each one load-bearing, all of them ugly. "It's in my supplier's manual."

"You have a supplier's manual."

"I have several. Would you like to borrow one? Very dull. Excellent for insomnia."

For a moment something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, filed away somewhere behind professional restraint. Then it was gone, and she was all business again, which was worse.

"The missing child," she said. "Wren. Nine years old, Ashmarket orphan, last seen at the Turning-tide markets. The Ministry has a register of foundlings, and hers went quiet six days ago." She let that number sit between us like a coin on a table. "Six days ago there were also reports of resonance on Copperlane. A hum in the stones. Dogs howling at nothing. A lamplighter who swore his flame burned blue for the length of your street."

"Lamplighters swear their flames burn blue every winter. It's the coal."

"This was in summer's ledger, then misfiled." She watched me. "Or the ledger lies. Ledgers do that, when people write them carelessly. Or frightenedly."

"I wouldn't know about frightened ledgers. Mine's very brave."

That got me closer to the smile than anything else I'd tried. It also got me a long look — the kind of look that takes inventory not of shelves but of a man, of the shadows under his eyes and the steadiness of his hands and the exact pitch of his breathing. I had spent twenty years being unremarkable. I had made a profession of it, a craft, almost an art. And here was this woman reading me like a page with too-large print.

"Your cellar," she said.

"What about it?"

"I didn't finish inspecting it last time. The quarterly form requires it. I noted the omission myself, which was irregular of me, but you seemed eager to move things along, and I was—" she paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—tired."

"I seem eager to move things along generally. It's called commerce."

"Is the child in your cellar?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass. Neither of us moved to pick it up.

And here is the thing I will say plainly, because this account deserves honesty even if I didn't deserve her: I am a good liar. Twenty years of practice. I have lied to archivists who could taste falsehood the way you taste copper, lied with my pulse slow and my eyes level and my whole gray boring life arranged around the lie like scaffolding. If Sera Quillon had asked me about the child on day one, I would have lied beautifully and she would have believed me and walked out into the rain none the wiser.

But six days of Wren had done something to me. Six days of a child eating bread at my table and asking questions in the First Tongue and flinching at bells — six days of that had rusted something loose in the machinery, and when the moment came, what came out of my mouth was:

"Why would a child be in my cellar?"

Not *no*. Not *what child?* Not even a well-crafted deflection about vagrants and city ordinances. Just a question shaped exactly wrong, defensive where it should have been puzzled, guilty where it should have been bland.

Sera Quillon looked at me for a long time. Somewhere outside, one of my starlings gave a single alarmed chirp and then thought better of it.

"That," she said quietly, "was the worst lie I have heard in nine years of service. And I once interviewed a man who claimed he'd been robbed by his own reflection."

* * *

She didn't press it. That was the strange part. A Ministry inspector with probable cause — and she had probable cause, she must have known she had probable cause, the missing resonance reports alone would have gotten her a writ by nightfall — simply stood there in my shop and let the worst lie of my career hang in the air between us, unexamined, like laundry neither of us wanted to fold.

Instead she walked the shop. Slowly. She read spines. She lifted jars and set them down in the same spots, always the same spots, a precision that told me she'd been trained in search procedure and was performing it as a courtesy rather than a threat. And while she walked, she talked.

"The Court of Bells," she said, apropos of nothing. "You've heard the name."

"It's a story mothers tell. Keep your soul close or the Bells will ring for it."

"Mm. And do you believe the stories?"

"I believe mothers tell them true enough to keep children indoors at night. That's what stories are *for*, Inspector."

"Is it?" She took down a jar of moth-wing charms — legal, licensed, useless — and held it to the light. "When I was eight, my mother told me a story about a door under a hill. She said her own mother told it to her, and her grandmother before that, all the way back to a time when the hill was real and the door was real and people went through it singing." She set the jar down. "Then the Order came to our village and explained that hills don't have doors, songs aren't magic, and grandmothers who say otherwise are confused old women, and the confusion is a public hazard."

I kept my face still. Under my apron, my hands did not stay still, but hands are easier to hide than faces.

"What happened to your mother?" I asked. Carefully. The way you step onto ice.

"She stopped telling stories." Quillon turned to face me fully, and for the first time since she'd entered, the inspector's armor slipped — not off, just aside, a crack the width of a hair. "She became very good at agreeing with the Ministry. She agreed herself into an early grave, as far as I can tell. And I grew up, and joined the thing she'd surrendered to, and spent nine years learning its procedures so thoroughly that I can now perform them in my sleep." Her voice stayed level. Her eyes didn't. "Which is why I know exactly how much of what I do is procedure, and how much is theater, and how much is fear wearing procedure's coat."

"Inspector—"

"There were resonance reports on your street, charm-seller. Real ones. Blue flames and howling dogs and a hum in the stones that four independent witnesses described the same way, which in Vellharrow constitutes a miracle of consensus. My superiors want it filed as coal gas and closed by Friday. Coal gas does not explain blue flames in summer. Coal gas does not explain why the hum, when the lamplighter whistled back at it, whistled *back*." She folded her arms. "I am telling you this because I am supposed to be interrogating you, and instead I appear to be confessing, and I would very much like to know what it is about your shop that makes me do that."

I could have answered honestly. *It's the door in my cellar. It's twenty years of silence pressing on me like water. It's that you're the first person in two decades to look at me like a man instead of a license.*

I said: "Good lighting. People confess in good lighting. It's documented."

"There," she said, "is another terrible lie. You're getting worse, not better. It's remarkable. Like watching a swimmer drown in the shallow end."

* * *

She found the stairs eventually. Of course she did. Shops like mine have cellars the way wounds have scars, and inspectors like her find cellars the way blood finds bandages.

She stood at the top of the steps with her hand on the rail and did not descend. I stood behind her doing arithmetic: the wards on the gate below were keyed to fae-touch and First Tongue, not to Ministry-issue boots, but Wren was down there, and Wren was nine, and nine-year-olds sneeze.

"Form 11-C requires a visual inspection of storage areas," she said. "Perishables, contraband, structural hazards."

"My cellar contains turnips and a broken chair."

"A broken chair is a structural hazard."

"The chair is *resting*."

She laughed. Actually laughed — short, surprised, quickly strangled, but real, and it did something to the air in the shop, loosened it, the way a draft loosens smoke. For half a second we were not inspector and suspect but two tired people in a warm room, and I understood with a lurch that this was far more dangerous than the alternative.

Then she descended three steps, and stopped, and went quiet in a way that had weight to it.

The cellar was dark. I hadn't lit the lamp down there since Wren arrived — light drew attention, and attention was the enemy — and the darkness pooled at the bottom of the stairs like standing water. From somewhere in it, faintly, came a sound. A hum. Small, tuneless, the absent-minded sound of a child amusing herself, except that the note it hummed was not a note any throat in Vellharrow should have been able to hold. It was the First Tongue's lowest register, the tone the gate itself made when it dreamed, and it rose up the stairwell and passed through us both like weather.

Quillon's hand tightened on the rail until the knuckles paled.

"You hear that?" she asked, and her voice had changed. Stripped. Younger. The inspector was gone and someone else was standing on my stairs — someone eight years old, in a village that no longer existed, listening to her mother tell a story about a door under a hill.

"Hear what?" I said, and for once I wasn't lying. I heard it constantly now. It had become the sound of my house, the way creaking is the sound of other houses. I'd forgotten it was audible to anyone else.

She listened a moment longer. Whatever war was happening inside her, it ended the way sieges often do — not with victory but with withdrawal. She climbed back up the stairs past me without meeting my eyes, retrieved her coat from the hook, and let herself out into the gray afternoon.

At the threshold she stopped. She didn't turn all the way around — just enough that I caught her profile against the light.

"Friday," she said. "My report is due Friday. Coal gas closes the file. But files, charm-seller, have a way of reopening, and inspectors have a way of being reassigned to whatever reopens them." She pulled her collar up against the cold. "Whatever is humming in your cellar — and I am not saying anything is humming in your cellar, because officially nothing is — you have until Friday to decide what you're going to tell me when I come back. Think carefully. Practice your lies on someone. Because the ones you're using now would get a confession out of a saint."

She stepped into the street. One of the gray-coated clerks fell in behind her, and the other peeled away from the corner, and the pressure over Copperlane eased like a storm moving off.

I stood in my doorway and watched her go, and I noticed — because noticing was suddenly my whole occupation — that she walked past the end of the lane before she put her hood up. Same as last time. As if she liked the rain on her face. As if it reminded her of something.

Behind me, down the cellar stairs, the hum faltered and stopped.

"Wren," I called softly. "They're gone."

She appeared at the bottom of the steps, wide-eyed, clutching the lantern I'd given her for the dark hours. "Was that the Ministry?"

"That was the Ministry."

"Are they coming back?"

"Friday."

She considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troop movements. Then she said, in the First Tongue, a word I knew from the gate itself — the word for *door*, though it carried other meanings too, older ones, meanings like *threshold* and *promise* and *the space between*.

"Yes," I agreed, before I could stop myself. "That's exactly what she is."

And Friday, I thought, closing the shop early for the first time in twenty years, was five days away — and I had five days to decide whether Inspector Sera Quillon was the most dangerous woman in Vellharrow, or the only one I might not have to lie to.

Knowing my luck, I suspected she intended to be both.
