## 6. Bells Beneath the Mountain

The trouble with being nobody is that everybody thinks you're theirs.

I'd learned that early in the Ashmarket. The cook-wives at the tannery row figured a girl who slept in their woodshed owed them first pickings of the slops. The rag-man figured I owed him a cut of anything I found worth selling. Even the pigeons had opinions about what was mine. Nobody in Vellharrow asks if you *belong* to yourself. They just assume the answer is no, and act accordingly.

I'd made my peace with it. Mostly. You make peace with a lot when your other options are hunger and weather.

But there's making peace with being nobody, and then there's what happened on the fourth night after the paper, when the veiled merchant came back for her property — and I understood, with the particular clarity of a person standing on ice that has just gone quiet, that I had signed away the only thing I'd ever actually owned.

Let me back up. Because Wren tells things backwards when she's scared, and I was scared enough to tell this whole story from the end if you let me.

* * *

The paper. Right.

Her name was Mistress Sallow — or that's what she said, though I'd have believed Mistress Turnip by that point, honestly, because kindness makes you stupid and she'd been kind for three whole days. She'd appeared at the soup cart near the tannery like winter appearing: gradually, then all at once, and suddenly everything smelled different. Veiled, gray-gloved, voice like warm milk. She bought me a bowl with actual meat in it. She asked my name and — here's the stupid part — I told her. Nobody in the Ashmarket gives out their real name. It's rule one, carved somewhere into every orphan's skull before they lose their first tooth. Your name is yours; give it away and you've given away the handle.

But she had meat in her soup, and she didn't grab, and she listened like listening was something she did professionally, and by the third day I'd have handed her my teeth if she'd asked nicely.

She asked less nicely than that, in the end. Just a paper, she said. A formality. A signature so the foundling house could claim its allowance — because she wanted, she said, to take me somewhere safe. Somewhere warm, under the mountain, where orphans were fed and taught and *kept*. Kept. She used that word twice, and I was so busy tasting the warmth of it that I never once thought to ask what happens to things that get kept.

She held the paper flat against a wall for me. She guided my hand — gently, kindly, the way you steady a child learning letters — and I made my mark, which wasn't even a mark, really, just a squiggle, because I can't write. Can't read either. And a paper you can't read, signed in a place where no one will read it *to* you, isn't a promise. It's a door closing that you agreed to walk through blindfolded.

I felt it happen. That's the part I don't tell anyone, the part I barely admit to myself. The moment the quill left the paper, something went out of me — not dramatically, not with thunder, but like a candle carried out of a room behind you. One breath I was lit from inside. The next I was just a girl-shaped shadow in an alley, and the world had gone slightly flatter, like a song heard through a wall.

And when I looked up at the tanner's window beside us, the glass showed me the alley, the crates, the cat — and not me. No girl. No reflection at all, just a gap in the world shaped exactly like the space where I wasn't.

Mistress Sallow looked at the empty glass too, and nodded once, satisfied, the way a merchant nods at a correctly weighed scale.

"Eleven days," she said. "Rest, little bird. Eat well. Grow sweet. We collect at the Turning."

Then the veil turned, and the gray gloves folded away into the crowd, and I stood in the Ashmarket holding a soup bowl that had gone cold in my hands, humming — actually *humming*, a faint bell-tone in my chest that wouldn't stop, wouldn't hush, like a church bell someone had swallowed.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about losing your soul: you keep all your memories. That's the cruel joke of it. You remember everything, you feel everything — fear, cold, the specific humiliation of discovering your own absence in a windowpane — but it all comes to you secondhand. Like watching another girl's life through fogged glass. Like hearing music through the wall of a house you've been locked out of.

I sat that night in the woodshed behind the tannery row — my woodshed, my territory since I was seven — and I inventoried myself like the rag-man inventorying a dead man's coat. Hands: mine. Fear: mine, and abundant. Name: still Wren, thank all the indifferent gods, because apparently the paper hadn't wanted that part yet. But underneath everything, where the *me* of me used to live, there was just this hum. This small silver ringing. And when I pressed my hand flat against my sternum, the hum answered it, one clear note, like a bell struck once in an empty cathedral.

Somewhere under the mountain, I understood without knowing how I understood, something had been taken out of me and hung up. Filed. Shelved. And it was *ringing*, faintly, in time with whatever lived in my chest now — two bells, me and mine, calling across the dark to each other.

Which meant, I reasoned, lying very still in the straw while the hum throbbed, that my soul was still in one piece. Still hanging somewhere. Things that are filed can be unfiled. Debts can be disputed. I'd watched the rag-man talk a bailiff down from seizing his cart three separate times, using nothing but a bad limp and a detailed knowledge of debt law he'd learned purely to abuse it.

If a soul could be sold, a soul could be *bought back*.

I just had to find out who held the paper now.

That was the night terror turned into something else. Not courage — let's be honest, I'm not brave, I'm simply too stubborn to lie down. But terror and resolve turn out to be made of the same stuff, the way a knife and a plowshare start as the same iron. It just depends which way you bend it.

* * *

They came on the fourth night, earlier than promised, and I only survived because the pigeons hated me.

I was crossing the tannery yard when the whole flock went up off the chapel roof at once, screaming — pigeons scream, whatever anyone tells you about cooing — and I'd spent enough years reading the market's moods through its animals to know that birds only do that for two reasons: a hawk, or a hunter. I ducked behind the rendering vats and there she was, coming up Tallow Lane with two companions this time, tall ones, wrong ones, moving with that gliding smoothness that says *these legs are borrowed*. The veil caught the lamplight. The gray gloves flexed.

"Wren," Mistress Sallow called, warm as fresh bread, and my own traitorous chest *hummed toward her*, one clear note, answering like a dog hearing its name. That's the worst thing I learned that night: the contract pulls. Whatever they took, they left a hook in the hole it came out of, and the hook wants to go home to them.

I ran.

Not bravely. Not cleverly, at first — pure animal panic, elbows and knees and the kind of sprinting you learn when the watch-whistles mean a beating rather than rescue. Through the tannery yard, over the fence with the nails (took some sleeve, worth it), down into the warren of the Ashmarket where the streets forget their own names and the lamps burn tallow-thin. Behind me the gliding footsteps didn't hurry. That was worse than running. They walked. They knew the hook would do their hurrying for them.

Except it didn't. And that — that was the discovery that saved my life, and changed everything after.

Every time the pull in my chest yanked me toward them, toward surrender, toward that warm milk voice, I did the opposite of what it wanted. And the hum *changed*. When I fought it, the note in my chest rang sharper, brighter, angrier — and the gliding steps behind me faltered, stumbled, like men walking against a wind they couldn't see. My stolen soul, ringing in its drawer under the mountain, was pulling the *other* way. Or maybe it was just me, whatever was left of me, refusing. Either way: the bell in my chest was a weapon, and the people who forged it had forgotten to ask whose hand would hold it.

So I ran *at* things instead of away from them. Through the fish-market, slapping the great salt-crusted tarps loose so they billowed behind me like ghosts. Through the chandlers' row, shouldering a rack of dipped candles into the lane — wax everywhere, the hunters' borrowed feet skidding. Down the stair behind the shrine of Saint Ovel, the one with the broken bell, and here's the strange grace-note of the whole night: as I passed that cracked bronze ruin, the hum in my chest leapt toward it, sang to it, and the broken bell — dead for sixty years, everyone knows it — gave one thin, wavering tone back.

Everything rings for me now, I thought wildly, taking the stairs three at a time. Everything under the sky knows what I am.

The problem, of course, is that everything under the mountain does too.

* * *

I lost them eventually — or they lost interest, which I doubted — in the deep lanes below the aqueduct, where I crouched behind a shrine to some saint whose name had worn off, breathing like a bellows, listening to my own chest ring.

"You're loud," said a voice above me.

I nearly died of fright on the spot, which would have solved several problems for various parties. A boy crouched on the aqueduct wall — older than me by a few years, rat-thin, quick-eyed, with the particular grime of the ash-works under his nails. I knew him slightly: Tam. He scavenged the same markets I did. We had the unspoken treaty of all Ashmarket children: don't touch my corners, and I'll pretend not to see yours.

"They took your paper," Tam said. Not a question.

"How—"

"Girl, half the market heard the chapel bell wake up." He dropped down beside me, unhurried, like we were sharing a pie instead of hiding from things that glide. "My gran worked the ash-works her whole life, and her gran before her, and they both told me the same story, so listen, because I'm only saying it once and it costs me to say it." He glanced up the lane, checking. "Long ago, before the Ministry, before the ban, there was a court under the mountain. Fae folk. They kept the old bargains — harvests for songs, luck for names, that sort of trade. Then the kingdom sealed them down there, and the trade didn't stop. It just got *quiet*. They buy what the city won't miss." His eyes came around to me, and for once there was no mockery in them. "Souls of nobodies. That's the coin they like best. Nobody misses a nobody."

"The Court," I said. The word rose in me like water in a well, and I knew I hadn't learned it — I *remembered* it, from wherever my self was stored, some corner of my own soul leaking knowledge back through the hook. "The Court of Bells."

Tam blinked. "That's the name, right enough. How'd you—"

"What do they pay? What's the price?" Because debts have terms; the rag-man taught me that much law.

"Due at the Turning of the Year," Tam said quietly. "When the veil thins and the doors open honest for one night. Eleven days, Wren. Whatever they wrote you down for, it comes due on the longest night, and they come collect in person."

Eleven days. I put my hand against my sternum, and the hum rang against my palm, small and silver and furious, and I thought: *eleven days to steal myself back.*

"Where's the door," I said. "Their door. The way down."

"That's the wrong question." Tam stood, brushing ash off his knees, and gave me a look I'd remember later, though I didn't know why yet — a look like a man deciding whether to point a stranger at a fire or lead them around it. "The right question is: who keeps the door? Because there's a man in the low quarter sells charms that don't work, and there's a cellar under his shop, and my gran said — she said it laughing, so maybe it's nothing — she said the door under that shop is the *only* door, and the man who keeps it paid more for it than any of us will ever understand."

He started up the lane, then paused.

"Shop's called the Small Mercies," he said. "Charm-seller. Gray fellow, careful as Sunday. If you knock, knock quiet."

* * *

I want it noted, for whoever ends up telling this story properly, that I did not sleep that night, and that I spent six of the remaining hours doing sums.

Because here's what the sums said. The Court holds my soul. The Court collects at the Turning, eleven days out, in person, with gliding feet and warm-milk voices. Running is a fool's sum — the hook pulls, the veil finds you, and nobodies have nowhere far enough to run. Staying is a dead man's arithmetic. Which leaves only the sum nobody sane attempts: go down before they come up. Steal the debt before the debtor's due. Break into the bank of me.

It was a terrible plan. It was the worst plan ever planned. I lay in the woodshed straw with my hand over my ringing chest and grinned at the roof beams like a lunatic, because it was *my* terrible plan, the first thing in four days that was entirely, unmistakably mine.

At dawn I climbed out, dusted the straw off, and set out through the waking Ashmarket — past the soup carts and the rag-man and the pigeons with their opinions — toward the low quarter, and a gray shop with a careful man in it, and a door underneath that my chest was already humming toward like a compass finding north.

The sign said THE SHOP OF SMALL MERCIES. The paint was faded. The shutters were closed, though it was past opening time.

I raised my hand to knock — quiet, as instructed — and stopped, because through the wood, faint but unmistakable, something was answering my hum. Note for note. Like a bell recognizing its twin.

Whoever kept this door, I thought, had secrets the size of mountains. Good. So did I.

I knocked.
