## 8. Thieves' Vigil

### Isandro

There is a particular art to asking a favor of a criminal, and it is this: never call it that.

I practiced the sentence the whole way down Gutterlane, past the shuttered dye-works and the shrine with its saint's face chiseled off by the Ministry's tidy hammers. *Oskit, I need your help.* No. Too much like begging. *Oskit, I have a proposition.* Worse — propositions were what got fences hanged. In the end I settled on knocking twice, pausing, then once more, which in Oskit's private grammar meant *business, and not the kind you can sell.*

The door of his pawnshop opened a hand's width. One eye appeared in the gap, deep-set and gray as slag iron, beneath a brow like a shelf built for holding grudges.

"You're dead," Oskit said. "Or you will be. Which is it?"

"Neither yet."

"Then come in before both are true." He shut the door behind me and shot three bolts, spun the lock twice, and muttered something into the keyhole that I chose not to ask about. His shop was a cathedral of other people's misfortunes: cracked mirrors, wedding rings, a taxidermied owl that had seen better centuries. He cleared a space among it all with one sweep of his arm and put down two cups and a bottle of plum brandy that was older than the Ministry and considerably more honest.

I told him everything. Not because he'd earned it — because lying to Oskit was like lying to a ledger; the numbers wouldn't balance and we'd both know it. The gate. Wren. The contract signed in the First Tongue. The debt due at the Turning of the Year, eleven days out.

He listened without interrupting, which frightened me more than shouting would have. When I finished, he turned his cup slowly on the counter, around and around, like a man trying to unscrew something invisible.

"Undercourt," he said at last. "You're telling me Undercourt. After twenty years of you selling me charms that don't charm and swearing the cellar was just damp."

"The charms work," I said. "They work very slightly. That's the point of them."

"And now there's a girl whose soul is *payable*." He said the word like he was spitting out a pit. "Payable where?"

"The Court of Bells."

Oskit went still. Dwarves go still the way mountains do — completely, and with the sense that if they move again, something will have been decided. "My brother traded with them once," he said quietly. "A debt of craft. They took his hands' memory. He could hold a hammer after, but it didn't know him anymore. He sat by the fire turning it over like a stranger's tool until he died." He looked up at me. "You want to walk into that place."

"I want to steal from it."

Something happened to his face then — not a smile exactly. More like ice cracking along an old fault line. "Well," he said. "It's about time somebody did. Count me in, Winterbourne. But I'm keeping my grievance. I've been saving it."

"For what?"

"For spending," said Oskit, "where it'll buy the most."

* * *

### Oskit

I'll tell you what nobody tells you about being old: you stop fearing death and start fearing *waste*. A life spent badly. A grudge carried to the grave unspent. My brother died with his grief still folded up neat in a drawer, and I swore I wouldn't.

So when Winterbourne came asking — not asking, really; standing there looking like a man reading his own eulogy — I said yes before my sense caught up with my mouth. Sense has always traveled slower than my mouth. It's why I'm rich.

The priestess was harder. Maren kept a room above the tannery, of all places, where the smell kept the pious away and the rents were honest. She answered the door with a knife held low and easy, saw it was me, and put it away the way you'd put away a spoon.

"Fence," she said.

"Priestess."

"Ex-priestess. The Ministry was very clear."

"The Ministry," I said, "is about to be very busy. Can we talk inside, or does your god object?"

"My god," she said, stepping back, "hasn't objected to anything since they defrocked me. It's one of the few comforts left."

Her room was small and fierce with cleanliness. Candles burned at proper intervals. On the wall hung a bell — no clapper, just the shell of one, green with age — and when I looked at it too long she moved her body between me and it, casual as shifting a chair.

"Bell of the old rite," I said.

"You know bells?"

"I know who rings them under the mountain," I said, "and what they ring us for."

She went white as candle-wax. And I'll grant her this: she didn't laugh, didn't call me mad, didn't reach for the knife. She sat down slowly on her one good chair and said, "Tell me everything, and then tell me why you came to *me*, of all the broken people in Vellharrow."

"Because you remember the rites," I said. "The ones from before. And because Winterbourne needs someone who knows how souls are sung — and I need someone who remembers my brother's name properly. The way the old prayers say it. Not the way the registers do."

Maren looked at the clapperless bell a long moment. Then she reached up and touched it, once, gently, like touching a sleeping thing you weren't sure you wanted awake.

"All right," she said. "But I want it understood: I'm not coming back to the faith. I'm coming back to the *work*. There's a difference, and I intend to keep it."

"Keep whatever you like," I said. "Just bring the difference. We may need every kind of holy we can get."

### Maren

They say a defrocked priestess keeps her vestments in a box under the bed, and they are right, though in mine the box also holds a bottle of communion wine I was supposed to pour out eleven years ago and did not. I am not proud of everything I've saved. I am only honest about it.

When Oskit left, I sat with my hands flat on the table until they stopped shaking, and then I took out the box. Not the wine. The stole — gray wool, embroidered with the sevenfold knot, frayed at the ends where I used to worry it during long sermons. The Ministry's men had taken the silver ones. Wool they'd let me keep. Wool, they said, was harmless.

Wool, I thought, buttoning the stole on under my coat where no inspector would see it. You poor fools.

The girl was already at Winterbourne's shop when I arrived that evening — small, sharp-elbowed, sitting cross-legged on the counter among the charm-bottles like a cat claiming new territory. She watched me come in with eyes too old for the face, and when I spoke the greeting of the old rite — *the road rise to meet you, the bell answer you kindly* — she flinched and answered in a language I had never heard but somehow, bone-deep, understood.

First Tongue. Spoken by a child in a city where even the word *magic* was contraband.

"She does that," Isandro said dryly, from the doorway to the cellar stairs. "Don't encourage her. Or do. She encourages herself."

The girl — Wren — slid off the counter and circled me once, the way you'd inspect a horse you were thinking of trusting. "You're the priestess," she said. "The fence said you'd come."

"The fence talks too much."

"He said you'd say that too." Something almost like a grin crossed her face, quick as a fish. Then it sank again. "Do you really know songs? For souls? Because mine's in a jar down there somewhere, and the man who has it —" she stopped, started again, smaller. "I heard it humming. In my chest, where it should be. Like a bell with no clapper."

I knew that phrase. I had a bell like that on my wall. I put my hand on her head, briefly, the old blessing, half-remembered and mostly instinct.

"Then we'll get it back," I said, "and hang it where it belongs."

Down in the cellar, Isandro had laid out what passed for a war map: the city plan, the mountain, and beneath it, drawn in chalk that shimmered faintly, lines only he seemed able to see — the borders of the Undercourt as the gate remembered them. I looked at those lines and felt the stole against my ribs grow warm, or imagined it did, and thought: *eleven days. Eleven days to make priests out of a fence, a liar, and a lawwoman.*

Assuming the lawwoman came.

### Sera

Inspector Sera Quillon had arrested forty-one people for magical practice in her nine years of service, and had never once believed any of them guilty of anything except being poor and strange. She kept this arithmetic in a locked drawer of herself, next to other things that did not bear daylight: her mother's lullabies, sung in a tongue the registers called *unlicensed dialect*; the warrant she'd once refused to sign; the memory of her own grandmother's hands making signs over a cradle, quick as birds, gone before the neighbors could see.

So when the summons came — *Quillon, report to the Winterbourne premises, resonance complaints, missing child* — she read it twice and understood it perfectly. Her superiors had finally done their sums. And she had perhaps a day, maybe two, before someone less thoughtful came to do the arresting.

She went alone. Unarmed, technically. The knife in her boot was ceremonial. Everything in the Ministry was ceremonial, if you squinted.

Isandro opened the door before she knocked. Of course he did. Behind him, she glimpsed the dwarf and the defrocked priestess and the child, arranged around the little shop like conspirators in a bad play, all pretending to examine inventory.

"Inspector," Isandro said. "What an unexpected —"

"Don't," Sera said. "Please. I've heard four bad lies today already and yours won't survive contact either. May I come in, or shall we do this on the step where the neighbors can count us?"

He stepped aside.

Inside, she laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only currency she had left. "The Ministry knows something resonates on this street. They know a child went missing from Ashmarket and surfaced here. By tomorrow they'll have a full writ, and the officer serving it will be Halvorsen, who burned a hedge-witch's cottage in Corrier's Field with the family still arguing inside it. So." She set her badge on the counter between them — her actual badge, the tin heart of nine careful years. "I'm not here to arrest anyone. I'm here to negotiate."

"With what?" Oskit asked, arms crossed. "Your charm and wit?"

"With falsified registers," Sera said. "Passage through the Rite seals on the Turning night. A blind eye that doesn't blink. And in exchange —" she looked at Wren, small and watchful on the counter, and something in her chest that had been locked since childhood turned over in its sleep — "in exchange, you take me with you."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"You don't know what's down there," Isandro said carefully.

"No," Sera agreed. "But I know what's up here. I've served it nine years. I'd like to see the competition."

"It's not a joke, Inspector."

"No," she said, and heard her own voice drop into the register her mother had used for prayers. "It's the first thing that hasn't been."

Isandro studied her a long moment — the same look, she realized, he'd probably given every soul-contract that ever crossed his gate. Weighing value against cost. Then he glanced at Wren, who gave a single nod, imperious as an empress.

"The Rite seals at moonrise," Isandro said. "Eleven nights from tonight. If your registers fail —"

"They won't fail. I've been failing them correctly for years. Nobody's noticed yet, which is either incompetence or a miracle, and I've stopped believing in the second." She picked her badge back up and, after a moment, dropped it into Wren's small hands instead. "Hold that. Collateral."

The girl turned the tin over, frowning. "It's not worth anything."

"It's worth everything I have," Sera said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

### Isandro

After she left, we stood in the shop in the lamplight — a fence polishing his spectacles, a priestess relearning her own hands, a child wearing an inspector's badge on a string like a talisman — and I felt the strangest sensation, familiar from twenty years ago and nothing since: the sense of a door opening onto a lit room full of voices.

A company. Gods help us all.

"Rounds," I said, before the feeling could embarrass me. "We keep rounds. Oskit provisions us. Maren teaches us what rites she remembers, and Wren translates what the First Tongue shows her. Sera holds off the Ministry until moonrise on Turning night. And I —"

"What do you do?" Wren asked.

Fair question. "I open the door," I said. "And I close it behind us. And I pay whatever it asks."

"That's a terrible plan," said Oskit.

"It's the only one I have."

"Good," said Maren quietly. "Terrible plans need faith. Sensible ones only need competence, and look where competence got the Ministry."

Outside, far off toward the mountain, a bell rang the hour — and every one of us stopped, each for our own reason, and listened to it fade. Eleven days. Somewhere below, something was counting too.

Wren broke the silence first, holding up Sera's badge so the lamplight caught it. "So we're really doing this," she said. "All of us."

"We're really doing this," I said.

And under the floorboards, so faint that only I heard it — or told myself only I did — the old gate hummed one note, low and patient, like a throat clearing before a song.
