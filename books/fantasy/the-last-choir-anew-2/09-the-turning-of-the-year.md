## 9. The Turning of the Year

The Ministry teaches you three things in your first year at the Academy, and only one of them is true.

The true one is how to write a legible report. The other two are that the Ministry protects the people, and that the people are worth protecting. I believed all three for eleven years, which I think says more about my upbringing than my intelligence.

By my twelfth year, I had learned to read reports the way a gambler reads faces — not for what they say, but for what they're careful not to say. And the report on my desk that morning, three days before the Turning, said nothing very loudly indeed.

*Resonance anomalies, Candlemakers' Row and adjacent streets. Six incidents in nine days. Cause undetermined. Recommend continued observation.*

Six incidents in nine days is not *continued observation*. Six incidents in nine days is a man with a bell in his cellar, or worse — a crack in something old. But someone upstairs had stamped it OBSERVE, which meant either they didn't believe their own surveyors, or they did, and wanted no paper trail leading anywhere near the answer.

I had spent four days trying very hard not to think about Isandro Winterbourne's cellar.

I was failing beautifully.

* * *

The Rite of Order turned Vellharrow into a locked box every year on the longest night, and every year the city pretended to find this comforting.

Officially: as the veil between worlds thinned — the one night, the Ministry maintained, when such thinning occurred at all — the gates were barred, the wards were sung fresh by the Order-choirs, and every soul in Vellharrow slept safe behind stone and scripture. Unofficially: the rich paid for private blessings, the poor paid for candles, and the streets emptied by curfew filled instead with the kind of quiet that made your own heartbeat sound like an accusation.

My job on Turning night was simple. I sat in the Registry annex off Gildersgate with two junior clerks and a ledger, and I accounted for every registered movement through the city's sealed doors. Pilgrims to the Rite-cathedral. Physicians on emergency writs. Watchmen on rotation. That was the whole permitted world, written in iron-gall ink in a hand so neat it looked printed.

It was also, if one were inclined toward such things, a lock with exactly one key that didn't appear on any inventory: the registers themselves. A name entered, a purpose recorded, a stamp applied — and a person could walk through Gildersgate at midnight without existing, officially, at all.

I had known this for eleven years. I had never once used it. Using it would have been forgery, sedition, and — the charge that actually got people hanged — *resonance abetment*, under the Act of 311, subsection whatever-it-was. I could recite the subsection in my sleep. I had helped draft the citation format.

So naturally, on the afternoon before the longest night, I found myself walking down Candlemakers' Row with my collar up and my conscience doing arithmetic it couldn't finish.

* * *

Isandro's shop smelled the same as it always had — beeswax, dried lavender, and the faint metallic ghost of something he'd swear wasn't there. He was behind the counter wrapping a luck-charm for a woman who clearly believed in it completely, and he was doing that thing with his face where he smiled like a man being photographed.

The woman left. The smile left. He looked at me the way a man looks at weather he'd been told was coming but hoped would change its mind.

"Inspector Quillon."

"Master Winterbourne." I set my hat on his counter, which was either friendly or territorial depending on how you read it. I meant it as both. "Busy night ahead?"

"Every night's busy. People are frightened of the dark. Frightened people buy charms." He tied off a string with unnecessary precision. "You've come about the resonance reports."

Straight to it, then. Good. I hated the dance almost as much as he did. "Six incidents on your street in nine days. Surveyors logged harmonic readings out of the Bellwright range. You know what that means?"

"It means your instruments are expensive."

"It means," I said, "that whatever's down there under your shop isn't behaving like dead magic anymore. Dead magic doesn't *change*. And it means that in"— I checked the clock on his wall, because cruelty should be punctual —"about thirty hours, the entire city seals itself, the choirs sing the wards down to bedrock, and if there's anything living in that cellar of yours that objects to the Rite of Order, it will object very loudly, in front of witnesses, and you and I will both be standing in the ash afterward wondering whose fault it was."

He was quiet long enough that I heard the shop's little clock tick twice.

"You already know what's down there," he said finally. Not a question.

"I know what the archives say was down there. Twenty-two years ago. Before your time at that address." I let him chew on that. "And I know a girl went missing from Ashmarket last month, and her description matches a child who was seen entering your shop, and I have not entered that girl into the missing-persons register, which, Master Winterbourne, is the single most illegal thing I have ever done in my career, and I once watched my captain take a bribe from a slumlord over dinner."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt exactly. Guilt's cousin, the one who shows up at funerals. "She's here," he said. "She's safe. She's—"

"If you say *fine*, I'll arrest you on principle."

"She's *complicated*," he amended, and then, quieter, in the tone of a man stepping off a ledge: "Would you like to see?"

I would not have said yes. I want that on the record, wherever records go after they burn. I would not have said yes, except that he was already lifting the counter hatch, and I had spent eleven years learning that the moment you stop following a thread, the thread starts following you.

* * *

I am not going to describe the door. Other people in this story will do it better than I could — people who grew up with the First Tongue in their ears or twenty years of ward-songs in their hands. What I will say is this: I stood in front of it, and every hymn I had ever sung at the Rite of Order rearranged itself in my memory into something older and less comfortable, and I understood, with the particular clarity of a woman realizing she has been lied to by her own voice, that the Ministry's version of the Turning was a fence built around a truth too large to fence.

There was a child sitting against the wall beside the door. Mud-streaked, sharp-eyed, holding a lantern with something silver moving inside it like a moth made of moonlight.

"Sera Quillon," I said, because she was staring at me with the frank appraisal children reserve for new adults, and it seemed polite to surrender my name before she took it.

"I know," Wren said. "You smell like ink and the cathedral."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me this year."

"The bells don't like you," she added, conversationally. "But they don't hate you either. They're waiting to see."

Behind me, I heard Isandro exhale through his nose — the laugh of a man who has stopped being surprised. I decided not to ask. There are questions you ask, and there are questions you survive longer by writing down and saving for later, and I had a whole drawer of the second kind.

"How bad is it?" I asked Isandro instead. "On Turning night. If we do nothing."

"We do nothing," he said, "and at midnight the Rite hits whatever's stirring down there like a boot on a hornet's nest. Only the hornets are on the other side of the nest wall, and the wall is me." His hand rested flat on the door, casual as a man leaning on a fence, and I saw the tendons stand up white. "Or we do something else."

"What something?"

"Down. Through. Before midnight, while the veil's thin enough to make the passage survivable and the Court's attention is on its own celebrations." He said it the way men say things they've rehearsed saying casually. "Wren's soul is held below. We mean to go and get it back."

"*We*," I repeated.

"Oskit's coming. Maren's coming." He met my eyes. "We could use someone who knows which registers get checked."

And there it was. The whole trap laid out in one sentence, and the terrible thing, the thing I will be honest about even now, is that it hadn't been a trap at all until he said it — it had been a shape in the fog, and I had walked toward it on my own feet, four days ago, when I pulled the Candlemakers' Row report off the pile and didn't pass it up.

"I can lose my commission for this," I said. "Not my post. My *commission*. There's a difference. A post you can be transferred out of. A commission they strike your name from the rolls for, and striking names is something this city takes seriously."

"Yes," said Wren, from the floor, with the serene brutality of children. "They took mine."

The lantern-light moved. Nobody spoke. Somewhere above us, muffled by floors and years, a cart went by on Candlemakers' Row, and I thought: *in thirty hours this street will be empty, and every door on it will be sealed by law, and the only people who will know what happens beneath it are the ones in this room.*

Eleven years of believing the Ministry protected people. One month of evidence to the contrary, filed neatly in my own handwriting.

"When do you need the gate open?" I asked.

* * *

Forging a register entry is easier than you'd think and harder than you'd hope. The easy part is the penmanship; I learned clerk-hand before I learned to ride, and my pilgrim entries were famous at the Academy — Instructor Vael used mine as exemplars, which in retrospect should have warned me about myself. The hard part is the waiting. Every forged line is innocent until someone looks at it, and then it's treason forever.

I worked through the evening of the Turning in the Registry annex, with the two junior clerks dozing over their tea and the cathedral bells beginning their long warm-up drone across the city. Entry forty-one: *Maren Ostreva, lay-healer, pilgrimage to the Rite, return by matins.* Entry forty-two: *Oskit Brannagh, licensed broker, delivery of votive goods to the cathedral precincts.* Both true enough to survive a glance. Neither mentioned a cellar, a gate, or a descent into the dark under the mountain, because registers are for the world's surface, and the surface is all the Ministry has ever believed in.

Entry forty-three was mine.

*Sera Quillon, Inspector, Third Rank, assigned observation duty, Gildersgate post, duration of Rite.*

I wrote it in exemplar clerk-hand. I stamped it with the annex seal. And then I sat with the ledger open in front of me and felt the strangest sensation of my professional life, which was not fear, exactly, and not guilt either, but something drier and more permanent: the sound of a door closing behind me that I could hear but not see.

"Inspector?" One of the clerks, blinking at me. "You've been on that page a while."

"Cross-checking," I said, and turned the page, and my heart kept its steady traitor's rhythm, and outside the window the first star of the longest night came up over the mountain like a struck match.

* * *

Gildersgate at half past eleven was a study in organized dread. The watch stood in pairs along the barricades, breath steaming, halberds polished for an enemy none of them had ever seen. Above the gate, the Order-choir had begun the Rite proper — twelve voices climbing the old scale, each note a stitch in the net they believed they were weaving over the whole city. Below them, the crowd of sanctioned travelers shuffled through my checkpoint: pilgrims with candle-boxes, physicians with writs, Oskit with a handcart of "votive goods" that clinked in a manner votive goods generally shouldn't.

He stopped at my table. Up close, under the lantern, his dwarven scowl was doing magnificent work concealing what I suspected was terror.

"Papers," I said.

He produced them. I examined them at length. Somewhere in that examination I stamped them, and somewhere in that stamping, a woman named Sera Quillon ceased, in any way that mattered on paper, to belong entirely to the Ministry of Order.

"Broker Brannagh," I said loudly, for the benefit of the watch pair loitering nearby, "your manifest lists forty votive tallow-lights. This cart sounds more substantial."

"It's the packaging, Inspector. Reverence adds weight."

"Move along. And Broker—" I lowered my voice under the choir's next swell. "If the packaging breaks, I was never at this table."

His scowl cracked, just at one corner. "Aye," he said. "Funny. That's just what we were going to say to you."

Then Maren, hooded, murmuring her pilgrim's blessing at me with a warmth that made the watchman beside me visibly relax — priests are good for something, apparently — and then the gate, and then the cold open dark beyond the walls, where the mountain waited with its buried country and its court of bells.

I stayed at my post until midnight. Procedure. Habit. Spite, maybe — if I was going to commit treason, I was going to commit it thoroughly, with a clean checkpoint log and a full accounting of every soul who passed legally through Gildersgate on the longest night of the year.

At midnight the choir hit the high note. The whole city's bells answered it, every tower at once, and the sound rolled down the streets and into the ground, and I felt it come up through the soles of my boots and through the table and through the ledger, through the neat forged lines that were now the only proof I'd ever existed as the person I had been that morning.

And beneath the ringing — under it, inside it, the way a wrong note hides inside a chord — I heard something else answer.

Faint. Far. Patient. Like a bell in a cellar deciding, after twenty years, to try its voice.

The junior clerk shivered and pulled his cloak tighter. "Cold one tonight, Inspector."

"The coldest," I agreed.

I closed the ledger. I picked up my lantern. And I walked, alone and unregistered, away from the gate and toward Candlemakers' Row, toward a shop that sold small mercies and kept a large one in the cellar — knowing that whatever waited for us down the stair that wasn't there had already heard the choir, and had already begun, in its patient bell-hearted way, to count.

It knew we were coming.

I could only pray it hadn't counted how few we were.
