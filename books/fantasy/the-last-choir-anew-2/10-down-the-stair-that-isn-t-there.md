## 10. Down the Stair That Isn't There

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before you do the stupidest thing of your life. I'd heard it twice before — once at my wedding that never happened, and once the night I signed my name away to a creature with too many joints. Both times, the silence had been the world holding its breath, waiting to see if I'd come to my senses.

I hadn't then. I didn't now.

The cellar door stood open behind me, and for the first time in twenty years, so did what lay beyond it.

"Isandro." Maren's voice came down the stair, low and steady, the voice of a woman who had spent years learning not to flinch during other people's rituals. "The wards are singing."

"They're not singing," I called back. "They're screaming politely. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"In Vellharrow, yes. Everything screams politely or it gets arrested."

I heard Oskit snort somewhere above me. The dwarf had insisted on coming down first, on the grounds that if anything went wrong he wanted to be eaten before the humans, as a matter of principle. He'd also packed three knives, two flasks, and a grudge so old it had gone vintage.

Wren came down the stairs last, carrying her lantern with both hands, the way children carry things they've been told are precious and have decided are sacred. Inside the glass, her soul burned small and silver, like a moth that had decided to be a star instead. She'd refused to leave it home. I understood. I'd spent twenty years refusing to leave myself home too, though what was left of me fit in a smaller box.

The gate itself was nothing to look at, which had always been its genius. A door of black oak, iron-banded, unremarkable, set into the cellar wall where any inspector would assume it led to coal. The Ministry had looked at it four times in twenty years and seen nothing but a door. Doors are the most honest lies in the world. Everyone believes them.

But tonight the iron bands were frosted with light, and the wood breathed warmth like something sleeping, and when Wren touched the handle the whole frame shivered the way a dog shivers when it hears its name in a stranger's mouth.

"It says hurry," Wren said.

"You can read it?"

"I can *hear* it." She frowned, listening to something beneath the floor of the world. "It's been waiting so long it forgot how doors work. It remembers being a threshold. It doesn't remember being shut."

I put my hand over hers on the handle. The wood was warm as skin. Twenty years of pretending this thing didn't exist, twenty years of dusting around it and lying about it and waking at night convinced I could hear breathing through the grain — and all that time it had just been waiting for someone who could talk back.

"I need to say some words," I told the others. "Old ones. If the door asks you a question, don't answer. It only asks one question, and the answer is always a trap."

"What question?" Sera asked from the top of the stairs.

"'What did you bring?'" I said. "Everyone who ever crossed brought something they didn't mean to. Grief. Guilt. Hope. The door takes stock. Don't let it take yours."

Sera nodded slowly, the way she nodded at suspects she intended to see again. She'd falsified three registers tonight to get us out of a sealed city on the longest night of the year, and she carried her treason the way she carried everything: neatly, close to the body, where no one could count it. I'd have felt worse about dragging her into this if she hadn't volunteered with the particular briskness of a woman settling an old debt.

Maren lit the candles we'd set in the corners — seven of them, old rite, wax mixed with grave-dirt and honey. "Ready when you are," she said.

I wasn't. I said the words anyway.

They weren't in trade-tongue. They weren't in anything the Ministry taught or the temples kept. They were in the First Tongue, the language under all languages, and my accent was terrible — twenty years of disuse will do that, especially when using the language at all is technically a hanging offense. But the door didn't care about my accent. The door cared about the grammar of the thing, the shape of permission, and when I finished, every candle flame bent toward the black oak like grass toward sun.

The door opened inward. It had never done that before. For twenty years it had opened outward onto a brick wall that wasn't a wall, a sealing I had built out of my own stolen name. Now the wall was simply gone, and beyond the doorframe there was —

Well. That was the problem. There was no beyond. There was a doorway-shaped absence, dark and deep and smelling of rain on stone, and my lantern-light fell into it and kept falling, further than physics allows.

"The stair," Wren whispered.

"What stair?" asked Oskit.

"That's the trouble," I said. "There isn't one until you commit."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stepping off the edge of the world: the falling is optional, but only if you believe that. Maren believed it immediately; she stepped through like a woman entering a bath she'd paid for. Sera hesitated exactly one heartbeat — long enough for me to see the fear and the decision to be ashamed of it later — and followed. Oskit swore in three languages and stepped through with his eyes open, because he was the sort of man who refused to blink at his own funerals.

Wren went through holding my hand.

And the stair assembled itself beneath our feet, step by step, out of whatever stairs are made of when nobody is looking — stone, mostly, but stone that remembered being riverbed, and root, and once, briefly, a choir loft. We descended for what felt like an hour and what my knees insisted was three days. The light of Vellharrow faded above us until it was a coin-sized memory, and still we went down, past shelves of rock that held fossils of things that had never lived, past a seam of mineral that glowed faintly and hummed a tune I almost knew.

Then the air changed.

You know how you can feel weather coming before it comes? This was that, but for a whole country. The stair ended without announcing itself — one moment stone, next moment moss — and we stood in a forest that grew downward.

I want to be precise about that, because my companions' faces told me I wasn't mad and neither were they. The trees rooted in the air overhead, their branches reaching *down* toward the ground like a crowd leaning in to eavesdrop. Their leaves were silver-gray, and they shed constantly, drifting up instead of down, so the whole sky — the wrong sky, the under-sky, a vast dark canopy shot through with veins of pale light — was full of slow-rising snow.

"Leaves fall up here," Maren said. Her voice had gone soft, priestess-soft, the voice she probably used on altars before anyone defrocked her. "Or — rise. Whatever the verb is."

"Up," said Oskit flatly. "The verb is up. I hate it here already."

But he said it the way men say such things when their eyes are wet, and I pretended not to notice, because that's the courtesy dwarves prefer.

We walked. There wasn't really a path, but there was something better: a reluctance in the undergrowth, places where the inverted trees simply declined to grow, as though the forest itself had agreed generations ago that travelers would come through here someday and should be accommodated. Wren walked ahead without hesitation, lantern swinging, soul-light silvering the moss.

"How does she know where she's going?" Sera murmured to me.

"She doesn't. The First Tongue does. Watch." I raised my voice. "Wren. Ask the ground which way to the river of songs."

Wren gave me a look that said *I'm eleven, not stupid*, and then crouched and pressed her palm flat against the moss and said something in the First Tongue — liquid, layered, a sentence that seemed to happen underneath itself. The moss answered. I felt it answer, a tremor in the soles of my feet, a word made of pressure and patience.

"This way," Wren said, standing, pointing left. "Also the ground wants to know why the tall one smells of bells."

Sera's hand went to her coat. "I don't smell of bells."

"You do now," I said grimly. "Since the Court took your name into their registers, everything you touch down here rings faintly. It'll fade. Probably."

"Wonderful," said Sera, with the tone of a woman adding a line to a very long list.

* * *

We reached the river at dusk, except dusk down here is a matter of opinion, since the under-sky doesn't so much darken as *concentrate*. The river ran wide and slow through a valley of upside-down willows, and it was not water.

I knelt at the bank and cupped a handful and listened.

A song came out of my hands. Not recorded — *remembered*. A lullaby in a dialect dead eight hundred years, sung by a mother whose face I could almost reconstruct from the tune alone. Then another song, overlapping, a marching song, a wedding lament, something bawdy and something holy, hundreds of songs braided together, flowing past us toward wherever forgotten music goes when the world stops humming it.

"Every song anyone ever stopped singing," Maren whispered. She had both hands pressed to her sternum like a woman keeping something in. "It all drains here. I used to think rites were lost when the last priest died. They're not lost. They're *here*."

"Don't drink," I said.

"Why not?"

"Because songs get stuck. Drink enough of this and you'll spend the rest of your life humming things that never happened to you. I've met travelers who drowned in it — walking, talking, but every word they spoke came out rhymed and none of it theirs."

Oskit filled both flasks anyway. "For throwing at things," he explained, when we stared at him.

We made camp in the lee of an uprooted tree — rooted in the air, crown buried in the earth, providing shade by growing downward over us like a green cathedral roof. Maren built a fire that burned slightly sideways, and we ate hard bread and harder cheese, and for a while nobody talked, because the country itself was doing the talking, in creaks and drips and the distant chiming of something we chose not to investigate.

It was Wren who broke the quiet. She was sitting closest to the river, hugging her knees, lantern between her feet.

"It sounds like home," she said.

Nobody answered right away, because none of us had a good answer, and because all of us were thinking the same terrible thing: that an orphan of the Ashmarket, sold by the only kindness she'd known, was homesick for the country that had taken her soul.

"Not the Court," she said quickly, hearing our silence. "Not them. Never them. Just — the *sounds*. The river knows everyone's songs. Even mine. Listen." She cupped her hands, dipped, lifted. And out of her small palms came a scrap of melody, thin and street-worn — a skipping rhyme from the Ashmarket, half nonsense, sung by ten thousand dirty-throated children across two hundred years, and the river remembered every version, including hers.

She laughed. It was the first real laugh I'd heard from her since the night she knocked on my door, and it cracked something in me that twenty years of careful gray living had calloused over.

"That's the trick of this place," I said. "It's beautiful in the way that makes you forget it ate you."

"You talk like it ate you personally," Oskit said.

I fed the fire instead of answering. Some confessions keep. Mine had cost enough the night I gave it.

Sera stood watch first. Somewhere past midnight by her pocket-watch, which had begun running backward down here, she woke me without a word and pointed.

Out in the dark, the river had gone quiet. All of it, all at once, along its whole visible length — thousands of years of songs holding their breath. And standing on the far bank, at the exact center of the silence, was a figure. Tall. Veiled. Holding a lantern of its own, in which burned a flame the color of a signature.

It didn't approach. It didn't speak. It simply raised one hand — too many fingers, moving too smoothly — and pointed downstream.

Toward the mountain. Toward the Court of Bells.

Then the river started singing again, every song at once, loud enough to hurt, as though it had only paused to make sure we were listening.

Wren was awake beside me, gripping her lantern, her face pale but her jaw set in a way I recognized with a lurch, because I'd worn the same expression twenty years ago in front of a very similar veiled creature.

"They know we're coming," she said.

"Yes," I said.

"Good." She turned back to the fire, chin high. "Let's not disappoint them."

Downstream, far off, a single bell rang — once — and every inverted leaf in the forest rose toward the sound like iron filings finding their north.

We had nine days, and the map was a language, and the language belonged to a child.

We broke camp before the echo died.
