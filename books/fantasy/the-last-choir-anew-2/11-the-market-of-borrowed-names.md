## 11. The Market of Borrowed Names

The first thing you should know about the Undercourt is that it smells like a bakery.

That's the trap, right there. Everything down here smells like something you want. The inverted forests drip honey-sap that tastes like your grandmother's kitchen even if you never had a grandmother or a kitchen. The rivers of remembered songs hum in keys that make your chest ache for a home you've never seen. And the markets — the markets smell like fresh bread and rain on hot stone and every good thing that ever almost happened to you.

I'd been down long enough to stop trusting my nose. Mostly.

"Stay close," Isandro said, which was funny, because he hadn't let me more than three steps from his elbow since we came down the stair that wasn't there. "And whatever they offer you —"

"I know. Don't sign anything."

"Don't *speak* anything," he corrected. "Signing is how they get you. Speaking is how they *find* you."

We stood at the mouth of the Market of Borrowed Names, which is not what the fae call it — they call it something in the First Tongue that translates roughly to *the place where what you carry becomes what you owe* — but Oskit had named it on sight with the flat authority of a man who has fenced stolen goods in four kingdoms, and the name stuck.

It was built inside the ribcage of something enormous. I don't know what. A whale, maybe, if whales grew silver bones the length of cathedrals and died standing up. Stalls hung between the ribs like lanterns, each one lit by a small captive glow — a firefly's worth of light, except when I looked closer I saw they weren't fireflies. They were memories. Little bottled moments, burning themselves out for somebody else's benefit.

"That one's a wedding," Maren murmured beside me. She'd gone pale under her travel-stains, and her hands kept finding each other, priestess-fashion, though there'd been no altar to press them against in eleven years. "A real one. You can tell because it flickers when it's happy."

"How can you tell?"

"Because mine did." She said it lightly, the way people say the worst things about themselves, and then she walked forward into the market like a woman walking into cold water all at once instead of inch by inch.

The rules of the market were simple, and Oskit laid them out while we walked, in the tone he probably used to explain why the watch shouldn't look too closely at a crate of "spoons."

"Everything costs memory here. Coin's just pretty metal — nobody wants it. You want passage, directions, a night's safe sleep, you pay with something true. A name. A face. A grievance. A doubt. The stall-keepers will tell you it doesn't hurt." He snorted. "They're lying. It hurts exactly as much as the thing was worth."

"And if you don't pay?" Sera asked. She'd been quiet since the borderlands started — quieter than usual, which for Sera meant she'd gone from speaking in complete sentences to speaking in verdicts.

"Then you don't trade, and you don't eat, and you don't sleep, and the things that live in the dark between the ribs start wondering what you're carrying that you won't spend." Oskit shrugged. "Everybody pays eventually. Question is whether you pay at a stall or pay in an alley."

I looked at Isandro. He was watching the stalls with an expression I was starting to learn — the one where his face went very still and very kind at the same time, like a door being held shut by someone standing behind it.

"You've been here before," I said.

"A lifetime ago." His voice did the thing where it went smooth and distant. "Stay near me. If anyone asks your name, give them Wren. Just Wren. Not where you got it, not who gave it to you."

"Nobody gave it to me. I picked it off a gutter sparrow."

Something moved through his face then — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Even better," he said. "Names you choose yourself are the only kind they can't take without asking twice."

* * *

The first stall that wanted us belonged to a creature made mostly of elbows and courtesy. It had a face like a kindly moth and six hands, five of which were busy sorting little glass vials, and the sixth of which gestured us in with the warmth of a grandmother offering soup.

"*Travelers!*" it said, in the First Tongue, and then again in Common so thickly accented it sounded like it was chewing the words. "Travelers, walkers, wanderers-from-above! You have the look of those who need a road. And roads, dear things, roads are what I sell."

"We need to reach the Court of Bells," Isandro said. "Quietly. Before the Turning."

The moth-creature's five busy hands stopped moving. All at once, like birds going silent before a storm.

"The Court," it repeated. Its sixth hand withdrew the soup-warmth and folded itself away somewhere. "Now that is a road with a toll at both ends. Passage to the Court's antechambers, unremarked, unwatched — that is expensive. That is *very* expensive."

"How much?" Sera asked.

The creature looked at her, then at each of us in turn, the way a butcher looks at a herd. "Not coin," it said gently. "Never coin. Memories. Names. True things. One piece apiece, freely given, and I will walk you to the Court's back stairs myself."

"Freely given," Maren said. "Meaning what, exactly? Meaning we choose which piece?"

"Meaning you must say *I give this*, and mean it, and it must be yours to give. Stolen memories curdle. Borrowed names come home. The market knows." The creature tapped its temple with one elbow-hand. "The market always knows."

There was a silence. Somewhere above us, in the ribs, a bottled wedding flickered happily to itself.

"I'll go first," Oskit said.

He stepped up to the stall like a dwarf stepping into a bar fight he'd already decided to win. "What'll you give me for a grievance?"

The moth-creature's whole body rippled with interest. "A grievance! Oh, old ones are best. Fresh ones leak. What do you carry?"

Oskit reached into his coat — I thought he was going for a knife, honestly, it was that kind of reach — and pulled out nothing at all. Just held his empty fist over the counter.

"My brother," he said. "Dorik. Worked the deep seams under Karag Vhol until the Undercourt's debt-collectors came through the thin places buying souls for bell-metal. He signed because they had our mother's name on the paper. They rang him, understand? They *rang* him. Turned what was left into a chime in some lordling's hallway, and I have spent forty years hating them so hard it's the only warm thing I own." His fist didn't shake. I watched it not shake, and understood that not-shaking was costing him everything. "That's the grievance. Forty years of it. Take it."

"*Oskit*—" Maren started.

"It's dead weight anyway," he said, which was the biggest lie I'd heard since the veiled merchant told me bread came free. "Weighs nothing. Never did me a day's good."

The creature extended its sixth hand, palm up. "Say the words."

Oskit said them. "*I give this.*"

I wish I could describe what it looked like when they took it. It wasn't blood, it wasn't light, it wasn't anything so honest. It was more like watching someone set down a pack they'd carried so long their spine had grown around it — and then watching them stand up straighter, and stagger, all at once. Oskit swayed. Caught himself on the counter. When he straightened, his face was the same face, but the lines around his eyes had changed their direction, like a river rerouted.

"Huh," he said. He sounded lost in his own skull. "It's quiet in there. Forty years, and it's just — quiet." He turned to us, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Don't any of you dare tell me I'll regret it."

"Passage for four," the creature said briskly, already sealing Oskit's grievance into a vial where it glowed dull red, like a coal. "One payment received. Who's next?"

* * *

Maren took longer.

She stood before the stall for the better part of ten minutes, hands finding each other, letting go, finding each other again. The market waited. That was the horrible thing about it — the market never hurried you. It knew you'd pay. Patience was just another way of holding the knife.

"What will you take?" she finally asked. "What would you *want* from me?"

The creature studied her with eyes like oil slicks. "You are defrocked," it said. Not cruelly. The way you'd note weather. "I can see the shape of the office that was cut out of you. There is a hole there, and holes collect things. What has collected in yours, little priestess?"

Maren laughed — one short, cracked note. "You want the inventory? Fine. Doubt. One doubt, aged eleven years, kept in a dry place. The doubt being: *maybe they were right to strip me.* Maybe the rites were always just theater, maybe the gods were always just silence, maybe the whole holy architecture was scaffolding around nothing, and everyone who ever knelt beside me was kneeling to their own echo." Her voice shook on the last word and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "That's the doubt. It goes everywhere with me. It sits at every meal. It sleeps in my bed. Take it."

"Wren." Isandro's hand touched my shoulder. "Look away if you need to."

I didn't look away. I wanted to see. Because I had a hole too — a soul-shaped one, humming in my chest like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing — and I needed to know what it looked like when someone handed over the thing living in their hole.

"*I give this,*" Maren said.

The taking was gentler with her, or she made it look gentler. She exhaled, long and slow, like blowing out a candle she'd guarded through a long night. Then she stood there blinking, touching her own sternum, looking faintly scandalized.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's *appalling*. I feel *fine*. I feel light as a lie. How do people live feeling like this?" She turned to Isandro, and there was something new in her face, something almost frightened by its own freedom. "Isandro. If the doubt was wrong — if the rites were real — I just sold the one thing keeping me honest enough to find out."

"Then buy it back someday," Isandro said quietly. "Markets always resell."

The creature was already wrapping three vials — red, gray, and one that shimmered like heat-haze — and pushing a folded map across the counter, drawn on skin I decided not to think about. "Two payments," it said. "Two remain. The road to the Court's back stairs opens at the ninth rib. Follow the sound of water that remembers being sung."

"And them?" It nodded its moth-face at me and Isandro. "Do the last two pay, or do the last two walk the alleys?"

* * *

Sera paid fast and ugly.

"One memory," she said. "The day I passed my inspector's oath. All of it. The hall, the oath-words, the way it felt to mean them." She said it like reading charges. "*I give this.*"

I grabbed her arm afterward. "Why *that* one?"

"Because I've been carrying two versions of that day for years," she said. Her voice was steady but her sleeve was twisted tight in her fist. "The one where I meant it, and the one where I watched myself say the words and felt nothing. I kept the empty one. Let the Ministry keep the other. Between us, we'll call it balanced." She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a joke I'd ever heard her make, and it scared me more than the Hollow stories had.

Which left Isandro.

He approached the counter slowly, and I saw him doing arithmetic behind his eyes — the same careful, gray arithmetic he did in the shop, pricing charms at amounts that were honest but never memorable. A man who had spent twenty years making sure nothing about him was worth stealing.

"What will you give me," the creature asked, "for the last seat on the road?"

"Directions are all I need," Isandro said. "The others can rest. I'll pay for a map alone. Name your price."

"Oh no." The moth-creature's wings fluttered — delight, I realized. Delight and something hungrier underneath. "No, no. You don't get to be modest here. Do you know what you are, gatekeeper? Do you know what walks into my market wearing a charm-seller's coat and a dead man's posture?" Its six hands had all gone still. "You are the most expensive thing to enter these ribs in a century. Every broker below the mountain has your description. Every stall-keeper has orders. And you stand here offering to buy a *map*."

"We're leaving," Isandro said.

But it was too late, because that was when I heard the bells.

Small ones. Not the Court's great throbbing peal — these were hand-bells, sweet and clear, coming up the market lane behind us. A procession of servants in gray livery, faces smooth as eggs, carrying trays of little vials to restock the stalls. Bell-servants. I'd seen their like in the borderlands, hollow-eyed and humming, and I'd learned to step out of their way.

Most of them passed us.

One stopped.

It was shorter than the others, and its livery had a silver cord at the collar, and when it turned its face toward Isandro I saw that its eyes weren't hollow at all. They were full — brimming, overfull, like cups poured past the rim — and everything in them was fixed on him with an attention so total it made the air feel thin.

It leaned close. Close enough that I could hear the hum in its chest, the same note that lived in mine, and it opened its mouth and whispered:

"*Isandro Vael.*"

The name went through the market like a crack through glass.

Every stall-light flickered. The bottled wedding guttered. Somewhere in the ribs above, something vast shifted its weight. And Isandro — Isandro, who had lied to inspectors, lied to lords, lied twenty years to his own reflection — went white to the lips and did not move, because you cannot move when someone says your true name; it holds you the way a bell holds its note.

"That's not possible," he breathed. "I paid. I paid *everything*. It's not in me anymore, it's not —"

"Not in you," the servant agreed. Its voice was soft and terrible, a child's voice with a cathedral behind it. "In *us*. In the archive. In the ledger. We ring it every morning, gatekeeper, when the archive opens. We ring it every night when the books close. *Isandro Vael, Isandro Vael* — do you know how many times your name has been spoken beneath the mountain while you sold lucky rabbits upstairs?" It smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing I had ever seen. "Twenty years of mornings. We know you better than you do."

"Step back," Sera said, hand on her baton, but the servant wasn't threatening. That was the worst part. It was *reverent*.

"He is the linchpin," the servant said, to the market, to the ribs, to whoever was listening — and things were listening; the shadows between the bones had grown crowded and attentive. "He is the name the Great Bell lacks. And he has walked into the market of his own free will, eleven days before the Turning." It drew back, cupping its hands around something invisible, cradling it. "I am only a servant. I cannot keep such news. Forgive me. Forgive me. But they will already know."

The bell-servant bowed — deep, mournful, final — and walked away into the gray procession, and its little hand-bell rang all the way down the lane, and every ring of it sounded like my name, like *Wren, Wren*, like the debt coming due.

Isandro found his voice somewhere down near his boots. "New plan," he said hoarsely. "We don't go to the Court's back stairs."

"Then where?" Oskit asked.

"Somewhere they won't look for a man running *toward* them." He was already moving, pulling me by the wrist, the four of us folding into the market crowd, past stalls whose keepers had begun, very quietly, to close their shutters. "Because in eleven days, everything they have is going to be pointed at that bell. And the only place to hide from a hunt is inside the thing being hunted."

Behind us, at the ninth rib, the road we'd paid for opened like a throat.

Ahead, in the dark between the bones, the things that lived there had stopped wondering what we were carrying.

They'd started following.
