## 12. Chorus of the Hollow

**Isandro**

I have spent twenty years learning the exact weight of silence. The silence of a shop at dawn before the wards wake. The silence of a cellar door that has not spoken in two decades. I had thought myself an expert.

The Undercourt taught me otherwise. Its silence has texture. It leans against you like a large animal deciding whether you're worth eating.

We had been walking since what I generously called morning — the light here doesn't rise so much as seep, gray-gold through a canopy of inverted trees whose roots clawed at a sky the color of old pewter. The rivers of remembered songs ran alongside us, sometimes audibly, sometimes only as a feeling, like a tune you can't place and can't stop humming. Wren had been humming one for an hour. She didn't seem to know she was doing it. The First Tongue lived in her now the way a splinter lives under skin.

"Tell me again why we're walking," Oskit said. He said it the way he said everything, as though the universe had personally disappointed him and he intended to file a complaint.

"Because the stair brought us here," Maren said.

"And the stair couldn't have brought us *closer*?"

"The stair goes where the stair goes," Maren said, with the serene infuriating patience of a woman who had spent thirty years telling parishioners that God's ways were mysterious and privately suspecting God of winging it.

I let them bicker. It was good for them. Fear, unspent, curdles; bickering was our fermentation. Sera walked at the rear, hand never far from her knife, watching our backtrail with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Wren walked in the middle, where we had all, without discussing it, arranged her.

It was Wren who stopped first.

She didn't say anything. She just stopped, mid-hum, and the absence of the hum was louder than any alarm.

"What is it, little wren?" I asked.

She turned her head slowly, the way a bird does, one eye finding the treeline. "They're singing," she said. "But nobody's home."

I heard it then. Under the river's remembered melodies, under the gray hush of the inverted forest, there was a second song. Thin. Repetitive. The same phrase, over and over, like a man knocking on a door where he knows no one lives.

Maren went white. I had seen her face the Court of Bells' antechambers without flinching, and she went white.

"Off the road," she said. "Off the road, *now*, and whatever you hear, do not answer it."

* * *

**Wren**

Maren grabbed my sleeve and pulled me off the song-road into the root-shadow of the inverted trees, and I didn't argue, because I knew the song. That was the horrible part. I knew it the way you know a lullaby someone sang over your cradle — not with your ears but with your chest, in the place where my soul used to live and where the bell-hum still echoed.

It was the song the veiled merchant had hummed in the Ashmarket, the day she bought my name.

"They're coming," Maren whispered. She had chalk in her hand already — she always had chalk, the way Oskit always had a knife and Sera always had a grievance — and she was drawing a circle around our little huddle, quick and sure, muttering the old rites under her breath. The words were in the First Tongue. I understood them. That was new. That was getting less strange and more frightening every day.

*Stand fast, stand whole, stand witnessed.*

The trees began to move.

Not the trees. The *things in* the trees. They came down out of the root-branches the way ash comes down a cold chimney — slowly, all at once, wrong. They had been people once. You could see it in what was left of them: the shape of a shawl, the ghost of a soldier's coat, one that still wore a wedding ring on a finger gone gray as candlewax. Their faces were smooth. Not blank — *smooth*, like wax that had been held near a fire until every line of living was melted away.

And they sang. The same phrase, over and over, in voices like bells with the clappers stolen out.

I knew what they were before Maren said it. I felt it in my chest, in the hollow where my soul had been, a horrible kinship, a *this is the shelf they took me down from.*

"Hollow," Maren breathed. "Souls sold and spent. The Court rings them dry and hangs them back up."

One of them turned its smooth face toward our circle. Toward *me.*

And it stopped singing.

They all stopped. A hundred wax faces, turning, all at once, like flowers finding a sun that wasn't there. The silence was worse than the song. Then the one in the wedding ring opened its mouth — a mouth that hadn't been there a moment ago — and said, in a voice like my own voice heard underwater:

*"Half-empty. Half-empty one. Come back to the shelf."*

My legs wanted to. That's what nobody tells you. My legs wanted to walk out of that circle and join them, and it was only Maren's hand clamped on my shoulder, and the chalk-circle burning white under her words, that kept me standing.

"You are *witnessed*," Maren said, louder, to them, to me, maybe to herself. "You are *whole*. Stand fast."

* * *

**Oskit**

I'd fought dwarven clan-feuds, customs men, one very determined badger, and my own brother over a disputed pie. I knew what a bad situation smelled like, and this one smelled like wet stone and old candles.

The wax-faces couldn't cross the chalk. That was the good news. Maren's circle held, glowing like a hearthstone, and every time one of those things touched the light it flinched back with a sound I'll not repeat in polite company — which, granted, I was not in, but the principle stands.

The bad news was that there were more of them than there were of us, and they were patient. Dead things usually are. They didn't charge. They *circled*, slow as a debt accruing, and they sang that one phrase, and I could see it wearing on the girl. Wren had gone gray as her own namesake bird, and every repetition of that song pulled at her like a hook in the chest.

"They can't get in," Isandro said. He had his hand on Wren's other shoulder. "They're trying to wear the circle down. Maren, how long can you hold it?"

"As long as you need," Maren said, which was a priestess's answer, meaning *until I can't.*

I did the arithmetic I do. Four of us, one circle, a hundred-odd wax-faces, and no exit I could see. The road was behind them. The trees were full of them. Above us the inverted roots went up and up into the pewter dark, thick as a dwarven hold's ceiling beams.

Thick enough to climb.

"Right," I said, and started unbuckling my pack. "New plan. We go up."

"Up," Sera said flatly.

"Roots are hand-over-hand all the way to the crown, and wax-faces don't strike me as climbers. We go up, over, and around, and we're back on the road past them before Maren's chalk gives out." I slung the pack off and dug in it for the coil of rope I'd traded two honest knives for at the Market of Borrowed Names, because I'd had a feeling, and my feelings about bad situations are the only thing I've never sold.

Isandro looked at me a moment too long. I know that look. It's the look a man gives a door he's not sure he can close again.

"Oskit—"

"Don't 'Oskit' me. I'm the heaviest and the strongest and the only one of us who's climbed a chimney for a living. I go first, I set the rope, girl comes second, you third, priestess and the law-woman after. Simple. Even a Ministry man could follow it."

Sera, to her credit, didn't rise to it. She was watching the treeline. "They've stopped circling," she said.

They had. All of them, at once, had gone still, faces tilted up toward the roots. Toward *us*. And the song changed. New phrase. Still bell-flat, still clapperless, but different, and it took me three repetitions to translate it, because I'd been picking up the girl's tongue out of self-defense.

*Give back the half-empty one. Give her back and the rest may pass.*

"They want Wren," Isandro said quietly.

"They can't have her," I said. "That's the whole point of this miserable expedition, or have I misread the itinerary?"

"It's not a negotiation," Maren said. Her chalk-hand was shaking. The circle's light had dimmed, just a shade. "It's a *term*. They're reading us a term. If we refuse—"

The wax-faces moved.

Not all of them. Not a charge. Just six, peeling off the circle, moving with that awful ash-slow smoothness toward the base of the nearest root-tree, and I understood, with the special clarity that comes to a dwarf at the worst possible moment, that they didn't need to cross the chalk.

They only needed to get between us and the tree we'd chosen.

* * *

**Isandro**

There is a moment in every fight — I remember it from the old days, from before the shop, from the life I paid away — when the plan dies and what's left is just people deciding, in real time, who they are. I had hoped, in twenty gray years of selling luck-charms that were only wood and wire, never to stand in that moment again.

The Hollow split our escape. Maren's circle was dimming. Wren was swaying on her feet, that borrowed song pulling at the hollow in her chest. And Oskit — stubborn, grudge-bearing, generous Oskit — was already moving, because in his arithmetic the plan was still alive and he was the part of it that made it work.

He went over the circle.

I'll say that plainly, because I've turned it over ten thousand times since: Maren did not fail. The Hollow did not break through. Oskit looked at the wax-faces sliding toward our tree, looked at the rope in his hands, and stepped over the glowing line of the circle into the dark, bellowing in the First Tongue — badly, with a dwarven accent you could hang a door on — the only words he'd learned:

*"Not this one! NOT THIS ONE!"*

They turned to him the way iron filings turn to a lodestone. Of course they did. He was loud, and alive, and *outside*. And for the length of ten heartbeats it worked — they flowed toward him, away from the tree, away from us, and Sera had Wren by the hand and Maren was scrubbing a gap in the chalk with her heel, and we were moving, we were *through*, we were at the roots with the rope going up—

I looked back. I have regretted looking back every day since, and I would look back again every time.

Oskit had gotten them bunched against a root-arch, and he was laughing — actually laughing, that terrible dwarven cackle — backing toward a second tree, drawing them off our line entirely. "Go on, you candle-wax horrors!" he shouted. "I've been chased by better! I've *been* better!"

"Oskit!" Wren's voice cracked like a whip. "OSKIT!"

He glanced up at us, just a flicker, just long enough to grin. And in that flicker, one of the Hollow — the one in the wedding ring, the one who had spoken — slid behind him with the smoothness of poured wax and put its gray hands on either side of his head.

It didn't strike him. That was the worst of it. It only *sang*, into him, that clapperless phrase, up close, at the hollow places a person keeps even when they're whole.

Oskit's grin went out like a lamp.

He didn't scream. He sighed — a long, emptied sigh, the sigh of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was there — and his knees folded, and the Hollow caught him, and the circle of them closed around him like wax closing over a wick.

"OSKIT!" Wren was fighting Sera, fighting me, and the chalk was dying under Maren's feet, and I made the choice that I will be making over again on my deathbed, whatever name I die under: I wrapped my arms around the girl and I *ran*.

We ran. Maren's circle shattered behind us with a sound like a bell dying. The song rose up around us, triumphant and hollow, and threaded through it — fainter, fading, already going flat — I heard one voice that was not wax and not bell, one voice with a dwarven grudge still in it, singing the phrase back at them, wrong, defiant, *his*:

*"Not. This. One."*

Then the river of remembered songs swallowed everything, and there was only the four of us, running through the pewter dark with one rope and no fence, and Wren crying in my arms not for herself, for the first time since I'd known her, but for a man who'd sold a grievance at a fae market and kept his heart after all.

* * *

**Maren**

I light no candle for the dead. That is the first rite I ever learned and the last one I still believe: you do not light a candle for the dead, you light it for the living, so they can see each other's faces in the dark.

So when we finally stopped — hours on, in the lee of a standing stone that hummed faintly with old boundary-law — I did not light a candle for Oskit. I lit it because Wren's hands were shaking and Sera's face was stone and Isandro was staring into the flame like a man reading a bill he couldn't pay.

"He's not dead," I said.

They all looked at me.

"The Hollow don't kill. They *empty*. And what's emptied can be—" I stopped. Refilled. Started again. "The Court took him to the Court. Souls go to the Court of Bells. That's the whole architecture of this cursed country. If Oskit's soul is anywhere, it's in an archive, in a ledger, on a shelf." I looked at Wren, at the lantern-soul she'd have carried if we'd gotten that far, at the hollow in her chest she'd learned to live around. "On a shelf he can be taken *from*. That's not a funeral, child. That's a change of itinerary."

"It's a trap," Sera said quietly. She had not stopped watching the dark since we ran. "They didn't take him despite our escape. They took him *because* of it. The Court knows where we're going. It knows what we're after. And now it holds one of ours." Her jaw worked. "They'll want to trade."

"Then we'll want to refuse," Isandro said.

"Will we?" Sera looked at him, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen there before — not the Ministry's flatness, not the officer's arithmetic. Grief. Raw, unregistered, off the books. "You said it yourself at the market. Everything down here costs. What do you suppose a dwarf costs?"

Nobody answered. The candle burned. Somewhere behind us, far off under the pewter dark, a bell rang once — not the Great Bell, not yet, but a small one, a counting-house bell, the sound of a ledger being opened.

And in the flame, for just a moment, I saw the wick bend against the draft, as if something had sighed across it from a long way off — a long, emptied sigh, with a grudge still in it.

Wren stood up.

"They're taking him where they took my soul," she said, and her voice had stopped shaking, which frightened me more than the crying had. "So we go where we were going anyway. And we take him back too." She picked up the candle and held it out, and the light caught all our faces, the living ones. "All of us. That's the whole point. Isn't it?"

It was. God help me, it was.
