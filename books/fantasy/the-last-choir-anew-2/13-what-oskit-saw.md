## 13. What Oskit Saw

I have been robbed by professionals. I have been robbed by amateurs so bad it was practically charity work on their part. I once had a cousin in Kharvask who stole my own boots off my feet while I was wearing them, at my own wedding, and I respected him for it right up until the divorce.

But I have never in two hundred years been *processed* the way the Court of Bells processes a prisoner.

They didn't chain me. They didn't even touch me after the Hollow finished carrying me through those singing corridors — carried, mind you, like a sack of turnips, which I found insulting on top of terrifying, and I made sure to tell them so, in case any of them still had ears left to be insulted with. They set me down in a small stone room with no door that I could find, though there was very obviously a doorway, because things kept coming out of it. A table appeared when I needed to sit. A cup of something warm appeared when I'd gone long enough without. Once, a blanket.

That's the thing about the Undercourt nobody warns you about, because nobody comes back: they're not cruel down here. Not exactly. Cruelty is a passion, and passions are expensive, and the Undercourt is nothing if not economical. What they are is *thorough*. Everything down here is accounted for. Everything has a place in a ledger, a shelf in an archive, a bell assigned to its name. Including me, apparently. Including, if I didn't do something about it soon, everything I had ever been.

The room hummed. That was the worst part. There's a bell somewhere under this mountain — the Great one, the one the whole rotten cathedral seemed built around — and even muffled behind walls of stone it never quite stopped. You don't hear it so much as feel it in your back teeth. Dwarves live our whole lives underground; we know what rock is supposed to sound like when it's holding its shape. This rock wasn't. This rock was being *played*.

"Right," I said to the empty room, on what I judged to be the second day, because time down here moved like honey uphill and I'd slept twice and eaten four times and dwarves keep regular habits even in hell. "Let's see what you've got."

The door — the non-door — opened.

* * *

She was tall the way winter is tall, all vertical and gray and no warmth in it anywhere, and she wore robes the color of ledgers, which is to say the color of every bad decision ever written down. Her face was smooth as an unwritten page. She did not introduce herself, because things down here don't bother with names unless a name is going to change hands.

"You are Oskit Broadbeam," she said. Not a question. A filing.

"Fence," I said. "Broker of pre-owned goods. Occasionally a finder, when the fee's right." I looked her up and down with the professional appraisal I give everything, because habit is armor. "You lot have excellent taste in stonework and terrible taste in hospitality. Who do I speak to about the turnips incident?"

"You were taken by the Hollow because you resisted," she said, in the tone of someone reading terms and conditions aloud. "Resistance is noted. It will be weighed."

"Weighed by whom?"

"The Archivist."

And there it was. The name Isandro had said like a man spitting out a bone he'd swallowed twenty years ago. Vess.

"Well then," I said, and sat down on the chair that hadn't existed a moment before, mostly to show I wasn't impressed, partly because my knees had opinions. "Fetch your Archivist, love. I've a grievance to file, and I'm told you people are *very* good with paperwork."

Something crossed her blank face. I want to say it was amusement. With these creatures you can never tell if you've amused them or simply been added to a category.

"He will come to you," she said, "when your weight is determined."

"And how long does that take?"

"As long as it takes."

She left. The non-door closed behind her, which is to say the wall stopped pretending. I picked up the warm cup, sniffed it — honey and something floral and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the smell of memory, that old-paper-and-summer smell that follows you around the Market of Borrowed Names — and set it down untouched. I'd sold enough of other people's memories to know a hook when it was baited.

Then I got to work.

Because here's the thing about me, and about every fence and thief and grifter who's ever lasted past their first decade in the trade: hope isn't something we believe in. Hope is for priests and children and men who sell charms out of shops with cheerful signs. What we believe in is *leverage*. Somewhere in every cage there is a loose bar, and somewhere in every jailer there is a price, and the only real question is whether you're clever enough or patient enough or stubborn enough to find them. I had spent two hundred years finding loose bars in places that didn't know they had them.

So while the Undercourt waited for my weight to be determined, I determined theirs.

* * *

It took three more sleeps, maybe four. I counted by the meals, which came with metronome regularity, which told me the kitchen ran on bells, which told me the bells ran everything, which was the first piece of the map.

Piece the second: the antechambers weren't empty. Between my cell and whatever lay deeper, there were corridors where I could hear things moving — servants, or the fae equivalent, bell-servants with faces like polished brass and movements like pendulums. And they talked. Not to me. To each other, in that chiming speech of theirs, half language and half actual ringing. But I'm a dwarf who's spent his life in markets where six languages get shouted over a single barrel of disputed eels. You learn to catch the shapes of words you don't own. And Wren's First Tongue had soaked into my ears on the road down — the girl chattered in it in her sleep, poor mite, like a kettle that couldn't stop whistling — and the shapes of it matched the shapes here, older and colder but the same bones.

Piece the third, and this was the one that mattered: they were *nervous*.

Fae nervousness doesn't look like ours. No sweating, no pacing. It looks like precision getting sharper. The servants' pendulum-movements started arriving early. Meals came a hair faster. Twice I heard, through the stone, the sound of many small bells being counted — counted again, counted a third time — like a treasurer who suspects a shortfall and daren't say so.

Something was coming. Something with a date on it.

On the fifth sleep, the wall opened and instead of the ledger-woman, it was him.

Vess did not look like a monster. He looked like a librarian, which is worse, because librarians decide what gets remembered and monsters only decide what gets eaten. He was thin and neat and gray-eyed, with ink stains on his fingers that I'd have bet my last coin went down to the bone, and he looked at me the way I look at an item that's come across my counter with provenance papers that don't quite add up.

"Oskit Broadbeam," he said. "You knew my gatekeeper."

"Your gatekeeper," I said. "That's one way to describe a man you robbed blind and left holding a door for twenty years."

"He was paid."

"He was *paid*," I agreed. "In the oldest coin there is. So let's skip the part where you tell me what a fair transaction it all was. I run a shop too. I know what we call it when the customer doesn't understand the contract." I leaned back. "We call it Tuesday."

Something flickered behind those gray eyes. Interest, maybe. Or the archive equivalent: accession.

"You are angry," Vess said. "Good. Anger has weight. Weight can be entered into the record."

"Everything's weight down here, is it? Souls, grudges, names?"

"Everything is weight," Vess said, with the mild pride of a craftsman describing his trade, "and the Great Bell weighs it all. That is what the Turning is for." He folded his hands. And then — and this is how they get you, remember this, this is how they've always gotten everyone — he *told* me. Freely. Because down here, knowledge isn't a secret. Knowledge is inventory, and inventory is shown to serious buyers.

"In eleven days," he said, "the Turning comes, and the veil between your world and mine wears thin as old cloth. For one night, the border forgets itself. For one night, the Bell may be rung loud enough to write upon it." His voice stayed level, pleasant, the way water stays level right up until it goes over the edge. "A border, Master Broadbeam, is just a line both sides agree to remember. We are going to help your world forget."

"Forget what?"

"Its edges." He smiled, and it was the politest horror I have ever witnessed. "The Bell requires a chorus. Not musicians — souls. Willing ones, ideally; unwilling ones ring flat, and flat notes make crooked borders, and crooked borders tear. We have been gathering a chorus for longer than your city has had its name. Every debtor. Every signed page. Every child whose guardian read the wrong paper too fast." He tilted his head. "Including your friend's young companion. Including, in a sense, myself. We all serve the ledger, Broadbeam. Even the archivists. *Especially* the archivists."

"And when this Bell rings?"

"When it rings, the chorus sings what we have written, and the writing becomes true, and the border moves." He said it the way a surveyor says *the river changes course in spring*. Weather. Fact. "Vellharrow sits directly above us, built — your masons never knew why it stood so well — on our foundations. It will be the first payment. Its streets, its stones, its living thousands, folded into the Undercourt as territory, as tithe, as song. And in exchange—" he spread his ink-stained hands— "we take the rest of the mountain, and the deep veins, and the old dark places your kind loves so well. Your people will have the sunlit lands entire. Fair, wouldn't you say? A trade. Everyone keeps something."

"And the thousands of souls in the chorus?"

"Are instruments," said Vess. "Instruments are not kept. They are used."

* * *

He left me alone with that for a long time.

I want to be honest, since honesty costs me nothing anymore: part of me did the arithmetic. That's the curse of my trade — I can't hear an offer without pricing it. Sunlit lands entire. The deep places given up, yes, but my people have always been better at building upward than anyone gives us credit for. A whole city traded for a whole country. On paper, in a certain light, squinting—

Except.

Except I'd watched Wren sleep talking in a dead tongue. Except I'd seen Maren's face when she spoke the old rites, like a woman greeting ghosts she'd mourned. Except Isandro Winterbourne had given up his own *name*, the root and floorboard of himself, to shut a door, and had spent twenty years selling lucky buttons rather than touch magic again, and the moment a mud-streaked child knocked he'd picked up a candle and said *all of us*.

And except for the word Vess had used without noticing what it would do to me. *Willing.* The Bell needs willing souls, he said. Unwilling ones ring flat.

Which means, you see, that willingness matters. Which means it's a load-bearing wall in their whole cathedral of a plan. Which means it can be — what's the word Maren would use? — *revoked*.

I'm a fence. I deal in goods with cloudy titles. And I know the oldest truth in my trade: ownership is a story everybody agrees to, and stories can be retold, and there is always, *always*, somebody willing to buy the retelling.

So there in that humming stone room, with eleven days ticking down in my teeth, I made the choice that would have gotten me laughed out of every den in Ashmarket. I chose hope. Not the soft kind — the working kind. The kind that's just leverage wearing its good coat.

I got off the chair and put my palm flat against the stone wall, and I listened the way dwarves listen, which is with the whole skeleton.

The Great Bell hummed beneath me, vast and patient and full of stolen voices. But underneath the hum — faint, thread-thin, easy to miss if you hadn't spent two centuries hearing what merchandise really wants to say — underneath it, some of those voices were *flat*. Off-key. Ringing against the grain.

Souls that had never agreed to any of this.

"Right," I told the wall, conversationally. "Here's what happens now. In a day or three, someone brings me food, and I'll ask them — politely, mind — exactly how willing a soul has to be to count. And whether a signature taken from a child who couldn't read counts as willing, or whether it counts as *theft*, which is my area of expertise, and let me tell you, love, I have never once lost track of stolen goods."

The wall, naturally, said nothing.

But the hum shifted. Just slightly. Just enough.

Somewhere below, in the great bronze throat of the thing that meant to swallow a city, a hundred stolen voices leaned toward me like a crowd leaning toward a seller who's just named a suspiciously honest price — and I swear on my mother's forge, some of them rang *sharp*.

Eleven days. A loose bar in every cage.

Now I only had to survive long enough to tell the others where it was.
